Bilobalide increases SKCa current via 5-HT2BR-PLC–PKC pathway, causing hyperpolarization and enhanced EET production in human coronary artery endothelial cells
Authors: Katesirin Ruamyod, Patcharaporn Taokum, Neennara Wongsatan, Sompol Tapechum, Wattana B. Watanapa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGinkgo biloba extract and its active component bilobalide induce endothelium-dependent vasodilation in coronary and other vascular beds. This effect is associated with increased Ca2+-activated K+ channel activity in endothelial cells. We aimed to identify ion channels targeted by bilobalide in human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs) and to explore the underlying mechanisms.MethodsWhole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on cultured HCAECs. Epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs) and prostacyclin were quantified with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays; nitric oxide and hydrogen peroxide were assessed with commercial kits.ResultsBilobalide dose-dependently increased whole-cell current through activation of the small-conductance Ca2+-activated K+ (SKCa) channel, but not through Cl−, transient receptor potential, or other K+ channels. The current increase persisted when all channels except SKCa were blocked and was abolished by apamin, a selective SKCa blocker; additionally, apamin prevented bilobalide-stimulated current. Bilobalide also induced HCAEC hyperpolarization, which was inhibited by apamin. The bilobalide-induced current increase was blocked by antagonists of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)1/2 and 5-HT2B receptors, phospholipase C, and protein kinase C, but not by inhibitors of 5-HT1B receptors, cytochrome P450, or Gi/o proteins. Furthermore, bilobalide stimulated EET production but not nitric oxide, prostacyclin, or hydrogen peroxide production; this effect was also apamin-sensitive.ConclusionsBilobalide increases HCAEC current and induces hyperpolarization by activating SKCa via the 5-HT2B receptor–phospholipase C–protein kinase C signaling pathway, leading to EET generation. These findings provide insights into bilobalide’s endothelial mechanisms and highlight its potential as an alternative therapy for coronary artery disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05418-y.

Background

Coronary artery endothelial cells regulate coronary vascular tone via vasodilator release, a process significantly modulated by endothelial ion channels [1]. In human coronary artery endothelial cells (HCAECs), these channels include the small-, intermediate-, and large-conductance Ca2+-activated K+ channels (SKCa, IKCa, and BKCa, respectively); the inward rectifier K+ channel (Kir); the ATP-sensitive K+ channel (KATP); the voltage-gated K+ channel (Kv); the transient receptor potential (TRP) channel; and the Cl− channel [2, 3]. In particular, SKCa and IKCa channels are essential for endothelial hyperpolarization and vasodilator production in human and rodent vessels [4, 5]. Moreover, apamin, an SKCa blocker, has been shown to decrease membrane hyperpolarization in coronary arteries [6] and to inhibit substance P–induced aortic vasodilation [7].

Ginkgo biloba extract and its constituent bilobalide cause vasodilation in some vascular beds [8]. A study in healthy elderly adults found that G. biloba extract improved endothelium-dependent vasodilatory capacity in coronary arteries [9], and G. biloba extract also activated endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation in rabbit aortic rings [10]. In aortic endothelial cells, G. biloba extract dose-dependently activated KCa channels [8, 11]; Li et al. [8, 11] identified bilobalide as the active ingredient in G. biloba extract that elicited vasoactive responses. In addition, studies have demonstrated that bilobalide exerts anti-ischemic effects in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes [12] and decreases infarct volume after middle cerebral artery occlusion [13]. Thus, bilobalide may affect KCa in various cell types and exert anti-ischemic effects.

To date, no specific bilobalide receptor has been reported. In Caenorhabditis elegans, bilobalide significantly inhibited 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT, or serotonin)–controlled egg-laying behavior by blocking 5-HT receptor (5-HTR) homologs [14]. A study in HCAECs demonstrated that 5-HT–induced nitric oxide (NO) production was mediated by both the 5-HT1B receptor (5-HT1BR) and the 5-HT2B receptor (5-HT2BR) [15]. Moreover, 5-HT–stimulated endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation was mediated by the 5-HT2BR in rat jugular vein and in pig pulmonary and cerebral arteries [16, 17]. We therefore hypothesized that bilobalide may affect HCAEC KCa channels via a 5-HTR.

In this report, we identified the SKCa channel as the main contributor to bilobalide-induced current and showed that bilobalide acted via the 5-HT2BR–phospholipase C (PLC)–protein kinase B pathway. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the SKCa channel was involved in bilobalide-induced hyperpolarization and the production of epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), but not NO, prostacyclin (PGI2), or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), in this cell type.

Methods

Cell culture

HCAECs were purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MD, USA) and grown in endothelial cell growth medium (EGM-MV BulletKit, Lonza) at 37 °C in 5% CO2 humidified air. Cells at passages 4–8 were used. Three to four hours before an experiment, cells were trypsinized and plated on petri dishes containing poly-l-lysine–coated coverslips to facilitate attachment.

Whole-cell patch clamping

Whole-cell currents were recorded with the following solutions (modified from Ledoux et al. [33]). External solution contained (in mmol/L): 140 NaCl, 5.6 KCl, 1.2 MgCl2, 2.6 CaCl2, 10 d-glucose, and 10 HEPES–NaOH (pH 7.3, adjusted with NaOH; 320–325 mOsm/kg, adjusted with d-mannitol). Internal solution contained (in mmol/L): 140 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 4 CaCl2, 5 HEPES, 7 EGTA, and 3 ATP (pH 7.2, adjusted with KOH; 290–300 mOsm/kg); estimated free Ca2+ concentration was 223 nmol/L (calculated with the WEBMAXC Extended online program [34]), a low activating concentration for KCa channels. To study primarily the SKCa channel, we used “SKCa solution,” which contained external solution plus blockers of most known HCAEC channels except SKCa. Blockers included were 1 mmol/L TEA, 10 μmol/L clotrimazole, 10 μmol/L La3+, 100 μmol/L Ba2+, and 250 μmol/L DIDS, to inhibit BKCa, IKCa, TRP, Kir, and Cl− channels, respectively.

To examine the involvement of 5-HTR and intracellular signaling pathway, HCAECs were first preincubated for at least 30 min with a culture medium containing an antagonist of 5-HTR or pathway components, followed by electrophysiological recording in external solution containing the same preincubating antagonists.

All patch-clamp studies were performed at 25 °C to 26 °C. Cells were allowed to equilibrate for 3 min in a new solution before recording. In a voltage-clamp experiment, currents were recorded in response to a voltage ramp protocol consisting of a continuous depolarization from − 110 to + 100 mV within 200 ms (Fig. 1A, inset). The holding potential was − 40 mV, which is close to reported mean values for HCAEC resting membrane potential [35]. Cells with a total current of < 25 pA at + 80 mV and the seal resistance < 0.8 GΩ were excluded. For current clamping, cell membrane potential was recorded at zero current. Vasodilator assays

To assess vasodilator production, HCAECs were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. Cells were preincubated for 30 min with 500 nM apamin, followed by a 10-min exposure to 100 μmol/L bilobalide. The culture medium was centrifuged at 14 000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for NO, PGI2, or H2O2 assays. For EET assay, cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 14 000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Results were compared among HCAECs in basal conditions, HCAECs treated with bilobalide alone, and HCAECs pretreated with apamin before bilobalide exposure.

NO assay

NO was quantified by measuring its products, nitrate and nitrite, in the supernatant using a commercial NO assay kit (ab65328, Abcam). Briefly, nitrate was first converted to nitrite with nitrate reductase, and then nitrite reacted with the Griess reagent to form a deep purple azo compound. The optical density of the azochromophore was detected at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Total protein concentration was determined with the micro bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). Values were normalized to protein concentration in the supernatant.

PGI2 assay

Because PGI2 undergoes rapid metabolism, its stable metabolite, 6-keto-PGF1α, was detected instead in the supernatant using competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; 6-keto-PGF1α ELISA kit, 515211, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The optical density of 6-keto-PGF1α was read at 405 nm. Values were normalized to protein concentration in the supernatant.

EET assay

EET levels in whole-cell lysates were quantified by measuring the stable metabolite 14,15-dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid (14,15-DHET) using a competitive 14,15-EET/DHET ELISA kit (ab175812, Abcam). Briefly, 14,15-DHET in the lysate competed with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated 14,15-DHET for binding to a precoated antibody. Optical density was read at 450 nm. Data were normalized to whole-cell protein concentration to account for variations in cell number.

H2O2 assay

H2O2 concentration in the supernatant was measured using an H2O2 assay kit (ab102500, Abcam) based on a horseradish peroxidase–catalyzed reaction that produces a colorimetric product detectable at 570 nm. H2O2 levels were normalized to whole-cell protein concentration.

For comparison, all induced vasodilator generation was expressed as the percentage of control (i.e., production in the absence of an agonist).

Data analysis

Initial analyses were performed with the pClamp 10.6 program (Axon Instruments) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Data were discarded if seal resistance was less than 1 GΩ, maximum voltage error was greater than 3 mV, or initial cell current amplitude (+ 80 mV) in the external solution was less than 25 pA. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism program (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). All data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired data were compared using paired t test, Wilcoxon signed rank test, or repeated measures ANOVA with Greenhouse–Geisser correction followed by Tukey post hoc test, as appropriate. %Control or %Change data were compared to 100 or zero, respectively, using one-sample t test. Between-group differences were analyzed according to data type using unpaired t test or Mann–Whitney U test. Multiple comparison was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc test, for if the assumption of equal variance was met (using Brown-Forsythe test); otherwise Kruskal–Wallis test with post hoc Dunn multiple comparison test was employed. All data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was accepted at P < 0.05.

The bilobalide concentration–response curve was fitted to a dose–response equation using GraphPad Prism to obtain the EC50 and maximum response.

Results

Bilobalide-enhanced whole-cell current primarily goes through the SKCa channel

Figure 1A shows an increase in outward whole-cell current after an HCAEC was exposed to 100 μmol/L bilobalide. The change could be seen within five minutes after starting the bilobalide-containing external solution. The corresponding change in average current density–voltage (I–V) curves derived from 34 cells is shown in Fig. 1B. This bilobalide-induced current increase was concentration-dependent (Fig. 1C and supplementary Table S1). A statistically significant increase was observed with 10, 30, 100, and 300 µmol/L bilobalide (%increase [mean ± SEM] was 33.19% ± 7.53% [n = 6], 42.40% ± 4.97% [n = 6], 39.61% ± 4.55% [n = 17], and 42.76% ± 10.36% [n = 5], respectively; P < 0.01), whereas the changes with 1 and 3 µmol/L bilobalide were not statistically significant (− 2.14% ± 1.64% [n = 5] and 5.80% ± 4.44% [n = 6]). The solvent for bilobalide (0.33% DMSO maximum concentration used) by itself did not affect HCAEC current (− 2.26% ± 7.80% [n = 4]). The EC50 and maximum response were 5.31 ± 1.51 μmol/L and 43.51% ± 9.43%, respectively. Bilobalide at 100 µmol/L was chosen for use in the remaining experiments because this concentration could elicit a readily distinguishable vasodilation in rat aortic ring experiments [8, 36].

As a screening test for ion channels involved, bilobalide-induced current was exposed to a blocker of a suspected channel. The results are shown in Fig. 1D (details in supplementary Table S2). Exposure to 500 nmol/L apamin (an SKCa blocker), 1 mmol/L TEA (a BKCa blocker), or 250 µmol/L DIDS (a Cl− channel blocker) significantly reduced 100 μmol/L bilobalide-induced HCAEC current by 23.26% ± 2.20% (n = 6), 18.68% ± 4.60% (n = 7), and 23.91% ± 6.12% (n = 6), respectively (all P < 0.05). In contrast, a statistically significant difference in current amplitude was not obtained with 10 µmol/L clotrimazole (an IKCa blocker), 1 mmol/L 4-aminopyridine (a Kv blocker), 10 µmol/L La3+ (a nonspecific TRP channel blocker), 10 μmol/L glibenclamide (a KATP blocker), or 100 µmol/L Ba2+ (a Kir blocker) (Currents were reduced by 3.85% ± 1.18% [n = 6], 3.61% ± 1.17% [n = 5], 7.83% ± 2.65% [n = 5], 7.09% ± 6.93% [n = 6], and 3.23% ± 4.32% [n = 5], respectively). Therefore, the contribution of IKCa, Kv, TRP, KATP, or Kir channels in mediating the bilobalide’s action could be ruled out. However, it was still uncertain whether SKCa, BKCa, and Cl− channels were involved because the respective channel blocker could have inhibited channels already open before the bilobalide exposure.

To resolve this, we conducted experiments where a blocker of a candidate channel was applied to inhibit basal channel opening before adding bilobalide (Fig. 2). Whole-cell currents were recorded in the presence of 500 nmol/L apamin, 100 nmol/L iberiotoxin ([19], a more selective BKCa blocker than 1 mmol/L TEA), or 250 μmol/L DIDS, followed by 100 µmol/L bilobalide. Only apamin was able to prevent bilobalide-induced current, whereas iberiotoxin or DIDS could not. The percentages of HCAEC current increase by bilobalide without prior exposure to any blocker vs with exposure to apamin, iberiotoxin, and DIDS were 38.42% ± 5.01% (n = 7) vs − 0.47% ± 2.42% (n = 5), 26.32% ± 4.07% (n = 5), and 31.37 ± 4.06 (n = 6), respectively (Fig. 2A-G, details in supplementary Table S3). Bilobalide acts via the 5-HT2B receptor

Because a 5-HTR can bind to bilobalide [37, 38] or mediate bilobalide actions in some tissues [14], we studied bilobalide-induced current in HCAECs pretreated with a blocker of the 5-HTR subtypes found in this cell. Cells were preincubated for 30 min in culture medium containing 1 µmol/L methiothepin (nonselective 5-HT1/2R antagonist), 10 μmol/L SB204741 (5-HT2BR antagonist), or 1 μmol/L SB224289 (5-HT1BR antagonist). Basal current (i.e., before bilobalide exposure) was not statistically significantly different between control cells (preincubated without antagonists) and antagonist-preincubated cells (supplementary Tale S5). Addition of 100 μmol/L bilobalide in the continued presence of the same antagonist concentration increased HCAEC current in SB224289-preincubated cells (Fig. 4C) but not in cells preincubated with methiothepin or SB204741 (Fig. 4B and D). The %increase of current by bilobalide after methiothepin, SB224289, and SB204741 preincubation was − 7.81% ± 9.88% (n = 5; P > 0.05), 20.77% ± 5.55% (n = 5; P < 0.05), and − 4.31% ± 3.35% (n = 6; P > 0.05), respectively (Fig. 4E; details in supplementary Tale S6.) Bilobalide effect is mediated by the PLC–protein kinase C pathway

Activated 5-HT2BR mainly exerts its effects through either the PLC–protein kinase C (PLC–PKC) [39] or arachidonic acid pathways [40]. In addition, EETs, which are cytochrome P450 products and an endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factor, can modulate KCa channels [41]. Moreover, the platelet-activating factor (PAF) receptor can mediate bilobalide or G. biloba extract effects in some tissues [12], and PAF effects are usually mediated via Gi/o protein [42]. Gi/o protein also mediates 5-HT1BR activation in endothelial cells and others [43–45].

To evaluate the intracellular pathways involved in the bilobalide effect, groups of HCAECs were incubated for 30 min in culture medium containing 2 μmol/L U73122 (selective PLC antagonist), 200 nmol/L Gö−6983 (selective PKC antagonist), or 10 μmol/L MS-PPOH (selective cytochrome P450 antagonist), or for 90 min with 1 μg/mL pertussis toxin (PTX, a selective Gi/o protein inhibitor). As in the previous section, preincubated antagonists were correspondingly present throughout the experiment, and no difference in basal current was observed between control and antagonist-preincubated groups (supplementary Table S4).

Addition of 100 μmol/L bilobalide increased HCAEC current in MS-PPOH– or pertussis toxin–preincubated cells (Fig. 5D and E) but not in cells preincubated with U73122 or Gö−6983 (Figs. 5B and C). The percentage current increase in bilobalide at + 80 mV was − 23.16% ± 6.88% (n = 6; P < 0.01), − 38.78% ± 5.19% (n = 5; P < 0.01), 43.94% ± 11.17% (n = 6; P < 0.05), and 34.86% ± 10.29% (n = 6; P < 0.05) with U73122, Gö−6983, MS-PPOH, and PTX preincubation, respectively (Fig. 5F; details in supplementary Tale S6.) These results suggest that the PLC–PKC pathway may mediate the bilobalide-induced current increase. If bilobalide opens the SKCa channel, causing K+ efflux, hyperpolarization should be expected. However, the apparent reversal potential when bilobalide successfully induced a current increase (Figs. 1A, 2C & E) did not show a distinct hyperpolarization shift. This may be due to the small current in our cells causing low signal-to-noise ratio. Moreover, in a voltage ramp protocol the membrane current and voltage may not be in the steady state [46]. We therefore studied HCAEC membrane potential with current clamp experiments. The results (Fig. 6) demonstrated that bilobalide hyperpolarized HCAECs, with an average control membrane potential of − 22.59 ± 5.74 mV in external solution vs − 27.43 ± 5.73 mV during exposure to 100 µmol/L bilobalide (n = 7; P < 0.05). Addition of 500 nmol/L apamin virtually abolished bilobalide-induced hyperpolarization, reducing the membrane potential to − 19.35 ± 5.4 mV (n = 7; P < 0.01 compared with bilobalide; P > 0.05 compared with control). Discussion

This study showed that bilobalide dose-dependently increased HCAEC whole-cell current with a maximum response of 43.51% ± 9.43%. The EC50 of 5.31 ± 1.51 µmol/L is in the same range as the bilobalide concentrations eliciting vasodilation in rat aorta [8]. The increased current primarily consisted of SKCa current, whereas other K+ channels (BKCa, IKCa, Kir, KATP, and Kv), Cl− channels, and Ca2+-conducting TRP channels were excluded (Figs. 1 and 2). Moreover, bilobalide significantly increased HCAEC current when most channels except SKCa were blocked (Fig. 3), and apamin virtually eliminated or prevented the bilobalide-induced current (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). These data clearly demonstrate that the SKCa channel is a target of bilobalide. Our findings are consistent with studies in aortic endothelial cells reporting that G. biloba extract or bilobalide can activate a KCa channel [8, 11]. Of note, endothelial SKCa is also a target of other vasodilatory substances and hormones [47, 48].

Although the in vivo bilobalide effect was expected to occur at the resting membrane potential (RMP), we could only monitor currents at high positive potentials because the net membrane current at rest was zero and our HCAEC currents were often tiny. However, for the non-voltage-gated channels (SKCa, IKCa, Cl−, and TRP), currents at positive potentials actually reflected channel opening at any potentials. Therefore, if bilobalide’s target was a non-voltage-gated channel (as was the case in our investigation), its effects at + 60 or + 80 mV would indicate what occurred at the RMP and were physiologically relevant. (Voltage-sensitive channels could still have physiological role at rest, depending on the actual mechanism and conditions; currents at high positive or negative potentials just signified whether bilobalide affected them.)

In our screening experiments (Fig. 1D), bilobalide was applied first, followed by a channel blocker. The results that apamin, 1 mmol/L TEA, or DIDS decreased the current exposed to bilobalide could be interpreted as bilobalide raising the SKCa, BKCa, or Cl− current, or the blockers reducing existing currents (not caused by bilobalide). Therefore, in Fig. 2, apamin, iberiotoxin, or DIDS was applied first to block existing open channels followed by bilobalide. Only apamin could prevent the bilobalide’s action, and thus SKCa was the most probable bilobalide target. Note that although 1 mmol/L TEA was reported to block mainly BKCa [18], higher TEA concentrations may block other K+ channels. Therefore, the more specific BKCa channel blocker iberiotoxin was employed in Fig. 2, proving the non-participation of BKCa. Besides, some studies have suggested that BKCa is present only in culture and not in native cells [49]. Notice also that HCAECs seemed to have a high Cl− conductance (Fig. 1 D, DIDS) which may serve to resist changes in resting membrane potential (ECl was ~ −0.5 mV). Nonetheless, the fact that bilobalide could still hyperpolarize the cells in Fig. 6 is another indication that Cl− channel was not affected by bilobalide.

Our findings provide direct evidence that bilobalide causes endothelial hyperpolarization and increases EETs, which are known endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factors (EDHF). Importantly, both effects were completely neutralized by apamin (Figs. 6 and 7). Our results strongly indicate that the SKCa channel is principally responsible for hyperpolarization and EET production. Previously, it was reported that G. biloba extracts, including bilobalide and ginkgolides, can induce cytochrome P450 enzyme activity [50]. By extension, this may in turn enhance EET production, as found in our study. Interestingly, we found that bilobalide did not affect endothelial NO or PGI2 production. This is consistent with a previous study in human umbilical vein endothelial cells [51]. However, it has been reported that bilobalide can induce relaxation through NO production in rat thoracic aorta [8]. Moreover, Li et al. reported that G. biloba extract activated a KCa channel and nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity in porcine aortic endothelial cells [11]. The discrepancy may be explained by the difference in vessel type: it is known that NO is dominant in large vessels, whereas EDHFs predominate in small vessels [52]. We demonstrated in HCAECs that bilobalide induces only the generation of EETs, not other vasodilators (Fig. 7), and apamin virtually eliminated bilobalide-induced EET production.

Our data may support the SKCa channel as a mediator of bilobalide's known cardioprotective effects on coronary flow [53] and infarct size [12]. Interestingly, down-regulation of endothelial SKCa channels was found in pathological circumstances like hypertension [54], diabetes [55], and cardioplegic-hypoxia [56, 57], causing impaired vasodilatory response. Moreover, treatment with SKCa activator improved SKCa function and restored the vasorelaxation in the cardioplegic-hypoxia scenario [57]. These findings call for further investigation into bilobalide's potential to alleviate these conditions.

This research also shows that the effect of bilobalide in endothelial cells is mediated by the 5-HT2BR. Both the 5-HT1BR and the 5-HT2BR are expressed in endothelial cells, including HCAECs [17]. Moreover, activation of both 5-HTR subtypes causes endothelium-dependent vasodilation in these cells [15]. There has been no previous report associating bilobalide with the 5-HT2BR. Only Brown and Luo [14] found that bilobalide could modulate C. elegans metabotropic SER-4 receptors, a mammalian 5-HT1/2R homolog. On the other hand, bilobalide can inhibit 5-HT3R, a neurotransmitter-gated ion channel not found in endothelial cells [58, 59], with an IC50 of 470 μmol/L or higher [38]. We did not test other 5-HTR subtypes because the selective 5-HT2BR antagonist SB204741 completely inhibited the action of bilobalide (Figs. 4D and E).

The 5-HT2BR is a Gq/11 protein–coupled receptor that typically sends a downstream signal via PLC and PKC activation [39]. Our data are consistent with this conventional signal transduction pathway. The PKC isoforms found in HCAECs are the Ca2+-dependent α and β1 and the Ca2+-independent δ, ɛ, and ι isoforms [60]. It should be noted that the intracellular (pipette) Ca2+ concentration in our whole-cell patch-clamp experiments (223 nmol/L, see Methods) was sufficient for a low PKC activation [61, 62]. Nevertheless, the available literature does not provide evidence that PKC can directly phosphorylate SKCa channel subunits. On the other hand, a recent study convincingly showed that inhibition of HCAEC SKCa currents by NAD was mediated by PKCβ, with experiments using the PKC activator phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate or PKC inhibition (both subtype-specific and nonspecific) [63]. This suggests the possibility that bilobalide-induced PKC activation could be compartmentalized, probably involving one of the other PKC subtypes in these cells [60]. Finally, it is intriguing that currents in cells preincubation with and in the presence of U73122 or Gö−6983 was actually reduced by bilobalide (Fig. 5). This may suggest a parallel inhibitory effect of bilobalide that was unmasked when the stimulatory pathway (PLC and PKC) was blocked.

SKCa channel, Gq/11 protein, and PLC are known to colocalize in endothelial caveolae [64], the same location as NO synthase, which catalyzes NO synthesis [65]. Hence, the finding that bilobalide elicited the generation of EETs and not NO may suggest the presence of SKCa in another compartment. Therefore, our results imply that the 5-HT2BR and the associated SKCa channel are probably not localized in caveolae. In addition, because the intracellular Ca2+ concentration was kept constant in our experiments, the mechanism of channel modulation may involve increased Ca2+ sensitivity of the channels.

Bilobalide elicits vasodilation in various tissues, acting on both vascular smooth muscle and endothelial cells [8, 66]. Our data provide a plausible explanation for the responsible mechanism in endothelial cells: Bilobalide binds to the 5-HT2BR and activates endothelial SKCa channels via the PLC–PKC pathway, causing hyperpolarization and thus increasing the electrochemical force for Ca2+ entry [1]. In intact cells (where Ca2+ was not kept constant as in our whole-cell patch clamping experiments), intracellular Ca2+ concentration will become higher, which can then stimulate phospholipase A2 to convert phospholipids to arachidonic acid [67], a substrate of cytochrome P450 enzyme with EETs as products, leading to smooth muscle relaxation and vasodilation [68]. Concurrently, membrane hyperpolarization induced by SKCa channel opening can spread passively via myoendothelial gap junctions to hyperpolarize smooth muscle cells [1]. Studies have shown that SKCa channel activation is involved in EDHF-like hyperpolarization in endothelial cells and the underlying smooth muscles [69]. Furthermore, in intact cells the increased Ca2+ entry can activate more SKCa channels, providing a positive feedback loop for sustaining intracellular Ca2+ increase [4]. This is a likely chain of events that collectively leads to bilobalide-induced vasodilation in human coronary arteries (Fig. 8). G. biloba extract and its constituents have long been used in traditional medicine and are widely available today as dietary supplements, particularly for patients with cardiovascular and neurovascular conditions [70, 71]. G. biloba extract contains primary active compounds—bilobalide, flavonoids, and ginkgolides—that have demonstrated antihypertensive, antiplatelet, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties [72–74]. Our findings further support bilobalide as a promising therapeutic candidate for cardiovascular disorders, with the potential to transition from complementary use into mainstream clinical practice.

Limitations

There were certain limitations to the whole-cell patch clamp method. In this configuration, much of the intracellular content had been dialyzed, so only the membrane-delimited signal cascade could be studied. Additionally, our experiments could only reveal a Ca2+-independent pathway because our internal solution contained 7 mmol/L EGTA Ca2+ buffering. However, this does not rule out the existence of parallel cytosolic pathways in intact cells. Nonetheless, our findings showed that the SKCa-mediated route may be a principal process for bilobalide-induced EET production: in experiments with intact cells, apamin virtually eliminated EET production (Fig. 7C). Whole-cell configuration also allows a relatively short experimental time window. To minimize rundowns, we included ATP in the intracellular solution and discarded cells when the seal resistance was < 0.8 GΩ. Further, we limited our experiments within 10–15 min, during which HCAEC whole-cell currents were usually steady with little decay. However, we were not able to obtain wash-out currents for bilobalide within the experimental time frame, probably attributed to its dependence on intracellular signaling cascade.

Another limitation was that we did not unravel the mechanism leading from PKC activation to increased SKCa currents, nor did we investigate the specific PKC isoforms involved. This gap warrants further investigation. In addition, although SB204741 is a well-validated selective 5-HT2BR antagonist [27], off-target effects cannot be fully excluded. Future studies demonstrating bilobalide binding on the 5-HT2BR site and/or using receptor-specific knockdown approaches would strengthen these findings. Note also that in the experiments of Fig. 4 and 5, preincubation with vehicle was not tested. However, as shown in supplementary Table S5, the basal current of control group (preincubated with culture media without a blocker) was not different from basal current of other groups. Lastly, demonstrating the bilobalide action on coronary artery functions and SKCa involvement would be essential for the applicability of our findings, however this was beyond the scope of this study.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that bilobalide acts via the 5-HT2BR, PLC, and PKC to enhance SKCa currents in HCAECs, resulting in increased whole-cell current, hyperpolarization, and EET generation. Bilobalide did not affect NO or PGI2 production. The mechanism described herein may explain, at least partially, bilobalide-induced coronary vasodilation.
