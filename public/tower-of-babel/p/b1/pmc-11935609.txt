Association Between Genetically Determined Serum Corin and the Risk of Stroke in Chinese Adults: A Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Yang Liu, Linan Chen, Guozhe Sun, Hao Zhang, Wenqing Geng, Xinwei Li, Qiu Zhang, Yibing Jin, Jialing Yao, Xiangdong Yang, Wenxiu Fan, Jiexiang Jing, Shuyao Wang, Hao Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSerum corin has been associated with stroke in observational studies, but the underlying causality is uncertain. This study examined the causal association between corin and stroke through Mendelian randomization study.Methods and ResultsIn the Gusu cohort, serum corin was assayed at baseline, and stroke incidents were prospectively obtained during 10 years of follow‐up. Single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in CORIN were genotyped by MassArray for 2310 participants (mean age, 53 years; 39% men). Seventeen SNPs passed the Hardy–Weinberg test and were considered the potential instruments. Only 1 SNP (rs2271037) determined variability of serum corin was significantly associated with stroke even after adjusting for conventional risk factors (hazard ratio [HR], 1.36 [95% CI, 1.00–1.85]). The weighted genetic risk score generated from the SNP‐corin associations was significantly associated with stroke (HR, 2.01 [95% CI, 1.15–3.51]). Using this genetic risk score as the instrument, 1‐sample Mendelian randomization analysis found a significant HR of stroke per‐SD higher log2‐transformed corin (HR, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.07–1.76]). The inverse variance–weighted analysis based on the SNP‐corin and SNP‐stroke associations found that the HR of stroke pre‐SD higher log2‐transformed corin was 5.92 (95% CI, 2.23–15.72). The effect estimates stayed consistent regardless of an individual SNP being removed from the instruments. An almost identical effect estimate was also confirmed by multiple other 2‐sample Mendelian randomization methods.ConclusionsGenetically determined variations of serum corin concentration were significantly associated with the risk of stroke in Chinese adults. Elevated serum corin may be a risk factor for stroke.

Human corin, a type II transmembrane serine protease secreted primarily by cardiomyocytes, 1 is the main activator of ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide) and BNP (B‐type natriuretic peptide), 2 important components of the natriuretic peptide system, which maintain blood pressure (BP) homeostasis through natriuresis, diuresis, and vasodilatation. 2 Circulating levels of ANP and BNP have been associated with the risk of stroke in varied populations. 3 , 4 , 5 Corin protein may also participate in the development of stroke, at least through its effect on the natriuretic peptide system. Indeed, the potential role of corin in the cardiovascular system has been reported in basic and population studies. For example, in vascular endothelial cells, corin expression was upregulated in response to stimulation of oxidative stress, which is the major injury factor in atherosclerosis pathology. 6 Animal studies found that overexpression of corin reduced cardiac cell apoptosis and infarct size and improved heart function in mice with myocardial infarction. 7 Corin deficiency caused by genetic knockout resulted in the development of hypertension and cardiac hypertrophy in mice. 8 , 9 In humans, polymorphisms of the CORIN gene that encodes corin protein were associated with susceptibility to hypertension, 10 heart failure, 11 and cardiac hypertrophy. 12 Circulating levels of corin protein have also been associated with cardiovascular disorders, including stroke, in several population studies. For instance, case–control studies found that serum corin levels were reduced in patients with heart failure, 13 , 14 myocardial infarction, 15 and stroke. 16 Cross‐sectional studies found that serum corin was associated with metabolic factors of stroke such as hypertension, 17 diabetes, 18 dyslipidemia, 19 and obesity. 20 The association between serum corin and stroke has also been found in our previous prospective study. 21 However, residual confounding and reverse causation make it difficult to make robust causal inferences in observational studies. Therefore, the causal relationship between corin and stroke needs further investigation and is critical for related clinical translation. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an efficient approach to assess the causal effect of lifelong exposure on diseases. 22 In brief, MR takes genetic variants as instrumental variables, the random allocation and nonmodifiable nature of the human genome, to avoid residual confounding and reverse causation in the observational studies. 23 In this study, an MR analysis was performed to evaluate the causal effect of serum corin levels on the risk of incident stroke.

Methods

The data used in this manuscript are accessible by reasonable request from the corresponding author at penghao@suda.edu.cn.

Study Participants

The Gusu cohort is a community‐based prospective study of stroke and its risk factors in middle‐aged and older Chinese adults. The study design, survey methods, and laboratory techniques have been described previously. 17 In brief, as illustrated by Figure 1, a total of 2498 community members residing in the Gusu district of Suzhou city, aged over >30 years and free of overt cardiovascular disease, completed the baseline examination in 2010. All the participants were followed up by either phone calls or face‐to‐face visits by staff from the local community health service institutes every 2 years until 2020. The staff reviewed the hospital records and completed a standard event form when a new event was identified during follow‐up. Based on the event form, an end point review committee made the final diagnosis. The date of each event was ascertained from either the initial point of diagnosis or a death certificate. During 10 years of follow‐up through 2020, 88 participants developed stroke, 71 participants died from causes other than stroke, and 214 participants were lost. The protocols were approved by the Soochow University Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. After excluding 188 participants for lack of genotype information, 2310 participants were included in this analysis.

Flowchart illustrating the study design and selection of participants.

Leveraging the Gusu cohort, a 1‐sample Mendelian randomization study was designed to test the causal relationship between corin and stroke, with serum corin as the E, SNPs at the coding gene of corin as the IV, and stroke as the O. Calculated GRS was also considered as the IV. GRS indicates genetic risk score; E, exposure, IV, instrumental variable; O, outcome; and SNP, single‐nucleotide polymorphism.

Assessment of Risk Factors

Demographic data, including age, sex, and education level, were obtained by questionnaires administered by trained staff. Current smoking was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in the entire life, having smoked cigarettes regularly, and smoking currently. Alcohol consumption was classified as current drinkers or not. Current drinkers were those who had consumed any alcoholic beverage ≥12 times during the past year. Three BP measurements were performed after the participants had been resting for at least 5 minutes in a relaxed, sitting position by trained staff using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer and a cuff of appropriate size, according to a common protocol. 24 The first and fifth Korotkoff sounds were recorded as systolic BP and diastolic BP, respectively. The means of the 3 measurements were used for statistical analyses. Participants with a systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg or under antihypertensive treatment in the past 2 weeks were diagnosed with hypertension, according to the Chinese guidelines for the management of hypertension. 25 Body weight (kg) and height (cm) were measured when participants wore light clothes and no shoes by trained staff. Body mass index was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Fasting glucose and blood lipids, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, were measured by standard laboratory methods. 17

Measurement of Serum Corin

Blood samples were obtained through venipuncture after an overnight fast for all participants and then stored at −80 °C. We used a quantikine human corin immunoassay (R&D Systems, Inc, Minneapolis, MN; Catalog: DCRN00;) to test soluble corin levels in serum. All the samples were processed in a duplicate assay at the Central Laboratory of School of Public Health in Soochow University, by trained staff who were blinded to the clinical characteristics and follow‐up outcomes of the study participants. Intra‐ and interassay coefficients of variation were <2.7% and 6.3%, respectively.

Selection and Genotyping of Single Nucleotide Polymorphism

Genomic DNA was extracted from venous blood using a QIAamp DNA Blood kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) and was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA). For the candidate gene CORIN, a set of tag single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) was initially selected using the Chinese (CHB and CHS) population from 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 with Tagger using a minimum minor allele frequency of 0.10 and an r 2 threshold of 0.8. Due to the limited sample size, lower frequent variants were not considered to avoid insufficient statistical efficiency. SNPs that had been reported in previous studies in association with cardiovascular phenotypes were preferred, and those that failed initial assay design were replaced with equivalent tagSNPs. Finally, a total of 19 SNPs in the CORIN were genotyped using the Agena MassArray Analyzer 4 system (Agena Bioscience, San Diego, CA). We assumed an additive genetic model for SNPs with scores of 0, 1, or 2 for genotyping containing 0, 1, or 2 effect alleles, respectively. The effect allele was considered to be related to a higher level of serum corin.

Statistical Analysis

Two‐stage MR analysis was used to examine the causal role of corin in the development of stroke. After Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium tests, SNPs with Bonferroni‐corrected P values ≥0.05 in 19 (19 SNPs were tested) were included in the final analyses. No multiple testing correction was done for the other analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); a 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

SNP‐Corin Association Analysis

Log2 transformation was applied to improve the normality of data distribution of serum corin, and the generated values (log2‐transformed corin) were used in downstream analyses. Three linear regression models were constructed to examine the association between each SNP and serum corin using the R package “SNPassoc.” In model 1, log2‐transformed corin (per‐SD increment) was the dependent variable, and each SNP (additive effect allele) was the independent variable. In model 2, model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. In model 3, model 2 was additionally adjusted for education level, smoking, drinking, body mass index, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic BP, diastolic BP, fasting glucose, and antihypertensive and hypoglycemic medication.

SNP‐Stroke Association Analysis

To examine the association between each SNP and the risk of incident stroke, we similarly constructed 3 Cox proportional hazards models. In these models, time (in years) to stroke was the dependent variable, each SNP was the independent variable, and death from nonstroke causes was the competing event.

Genetic Risk Score–Based MR Analysis

Three kinds of genetic risk scores (GRSs)—GRS1, GRS2, and GRS3—were calculated on the basis of the unadjusted, age‐ and sex‐adjusted, and multivariate‐adjusted SNP‐corin associations, respectively. GRS was calculated using the following formula: GRS=∑i=1nβiXi. where n is the number of SNPs, X i is the number of risk alleles (0, 1, 2) carried by the ith SNP, and β i is the corresponding coefficient for ith SNP. Then, the association between each GRS and the risk of incident stroke was similarly examined.

Statistics‐Based MR Analysis

To carefully test the causal relationship between corin and stroke, both 1‐ and 2‐sample MR analyses were conducted. For 1‐sample MR analysis, we constructed a 2‐stage least‐squares model in which a linear regression of log2‐transformed corin on the instrumental variable (IV) was first modeled to obtain a fitted value, followed by a similar Cox proportional hazards model with the fitted value as the independent variable to obtain the IV estimator. For 2‐sample MR analysis, based on the aforementioned estimates for SNP‐corin and SNP‐stroke associations, the inverse variance–weighted (IVW) MR method was applied to estimate the IV estimator using the R package “MendelianRandomization.” The heterogeneity of the instrumental SNPs was tested using the Cochran Q test, and a significance threshold for evidence of heterogeneity was set at P<0.05. If heterogeneity existed, random‐effect IVW models were used. Otherwise, fixed‐effect IVW models were used. Potential pleiotropy in the instrumental SNPs was examined by MR Egger. The leave‐one‐out approach was applied to test whether the causal estimate was biased by individual instrumental SNP. Various MR methods were additionally used to evaluate the robustness of the IVW findings, such as simple median, weighted median, MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier, and robust adjusted profile score methods. We additionally performed IVW MR analysis on the basis of external summarized estimates for SNP‐stroke associations, in combination with our original SNP‐corin association statistics. Summary statistics about stroke and its subtypes were obtained from GWAS launched by the International Stroke Genetics Consortium (MEGASTROKE [Multiancestry Genome‐Wide Association Study of Stroke Consortium Mendelian Randomization]). 26

Association Between CORIN Gene SNPs and the Risk of Stroke

The Cox proportional hazards model was used to examine whether the 17 potential IVs were associated with the risk of stroke (Table S2). None of them were significantly associated with stroke (all P>0.05) after adjusting for conventional risk factors.

Association Between CORIN Gene–Related GRS and the Risk of Stroke

The Cox proportional hazards model found that all 3 GRSs were positively associated with the risk of stroke (Table 3). Results of Statistics‐Based MR Analysis

The 2‐stage least‐squares MR analysis using single SNP as the IV found that rs2271037 determined variability of serum corin was significantly associated with the risk of stroke before (HR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.03–1.89]) and after (HR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.00–1.85]) adjusting for conventional risk factors (Table 4). We failed to reveal a statistically significant association between corin variability and stroke using other single SNPs as the IV (all P>0.05). However, using the 3 GRSs as the IV individually found a robust significant causal association between serum corin and stroke. After adjusting for conventional risk factors, the HR of stroke per‐SD higher serum corin concentration (log2‐transformed corin) was 1.40 (95% CI, 1.10–1.78), 1.37 (95% CI, 1.07–1.76), and 1.37 (95% CI, 1.07–1.76) by using GRS1, GRS2, and GRS3, respectively, as the IV (Table 4).

The IVW analysis found that no individual SNP produced a significant estimate that would be consistent with a causal effect (Figure 2). Nevertheless, there was no heterogeneity among the 17 chosen SNPs as suggested by the Cochran Q test (P>0.05). MR Egger showed that any potential pleiotropy in the instruments was balanced and unlikely to bias the results of the analysis (all P>0.05). The fixed‐effect IVW analysis based on the unadjusted, age‐ and sex‐adjusted, and multivariate‐adjusted SNP‐corin and SNP‐stroke associations found that the HRs of stroke pre‐SD higher log2‐transformed corin were 8.40 (95% CI, 2.88–24.48), 6.38 (95% CI, 2.45–16.57), and 5.92 (95% CI, 2.23–15.72), respectively (Table 5). The effect estimates stayed consistent regardless of individual SNP being removed from the instruments (Figure 3). Notably, even leaving the SNP with the largest effects on serum corin concentration and stroke risks (rs11934749) out of the instruments did not dramatically alter the effect estimates.

The causal association between corin and stroke was also confirmed by other 2‐sample MR analyses. As shown in Table 5, an almost identical effect estimate to the one with the IVW method was also yielded by various MR methods, such as simple median, weighted median, maximum likelihood, MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier, and robust adjusted profile score (all P<0.05). Discussion

Our group previously reported for the first time a significant observational association between serum corin and stroke by case–control 16 and prospective cohort 21 study designs. Considering its role as a switching regulator of the natriuretic peptide system, corin could be a potential therapeutic target for stroke. However, the causality between them has yet to be studied. The present MR study is the first to investigate the causal association between genetically determined serum corin variation and the risk of stroke in populations. An almost identical effect estimate of genetically determined serum corin on the risk of stroke was confirmed by multiple MR analyses. Our findings suggest that elevated serum corin may be a risk factor for stroke.

In line with our study, the association between the circulating level of corin and stroke has been reported by many observational studies. For example, our previous case–control study including 597 patients with stroke and 2498 healthy controls found that serum corin was significantly decreased in patients with stroke compared with their healthy counterparts. 16 In the Gusu cohort, our group previously found that serum corin was not only associated with metabolic risk factors for stroke (eg, hypertension, 17 diabetes, 18 dyslipidemia, 19 and obesity 20 ) but also predicted future risk of stroke. 21 In addition to stroke per se, some clinical studies found that circulating levels of corin were also associated with other cardiovascular disorders such as heart failure, 13 atrial fibrillation, 27 and myocardial infarction. 15 Prospective cohort studies found that plasma corin at admission was associated with future risk of major adverse cardiac events in patients with chronic heart failure 28 , 29 and myocardial infarction. 30 We can conclude that there is indeed a significant association between circulating corin level and stroke, as well as other cardiovascular phenotypes. The underlying causality is warranted to be inferred because it is critical for the clinical translation of corin. However, observational studies cannot elucidate the causal inference due to unknown confounders and reverse causality. 22 These limitations can be overcome by the MR approach. 31 Genes are randomly combined during transmission from parents to their offspring, and the distribution of risk alleles in the population is not influenced by individual behavior or environmental factors. 32 Therefore, the use of genetic variants in MR studies as instrumental variables can make a credible causal inference to avoid confounding factors and reverse causation in observational studies. Here, based on a prospective cohort study, the Gusu cohort, we designed an MR study to examine the causal association between corin and stroke with serum corin concentration at baseline as the exposure variable, stroke incidents during 10‐year follow‐up as the outcome variable, and variants at the corin coding gene as the instrument variable. The significant association between genetically determined serum corin variation and the risk of stroke identified by the present study provides not only a piece of causal evidence but also a theoretical basis for targeting corin as the therapeutic target for stroke prevention.

The mechanisms underlying the effects of corin on the risk of stroke are not very clear, but the results of several studies suggested potential pathways on which corin could act. For example, as an important physiological activator of the natriuretic peptide system, corin may influence the risk of stroke by regulating the activation of ANP and BNP. 33 , 34 Besides, in a cell‐based study, cultured vascular endothelial cells stimulated by oxidized low‐density lipoprotein, which is a major risk factor of atherosclerosis, showed higher levels of corin. 6 Overexpression of corin could also alleviate the cell injury stimulated by H2O2 by increasing the cell viability and migration and decreasing apoptosis in the myocytes. 35 These biological functions of corin protein supported our finding that elevated corin may be a risk factor for stroke. In contrast, an animal study showed that corin overexpression in mice with dilated cardiomyopathy decreased the development of myocardial fibrosis and heart failure; simultaneously, it enhanced heart contractile function and ejection fraction. 36 Mice with the CORIN gene being knocked out exhibited cardiac hypertrophy and reduced cardiac function. 8 The reason why these findings from animal‐based studies are contradictory to those from population‐based studies is still unknown at present. The probable explanation is that increased BP, retention of sodium, and volume overload of the heart in individuals at a high risk of stroke stimulate secretion of corin in feedback to facilitate conversion of pro‐ANP to active natriuretic peptide. Like the phenomenon that natriuretic peptides are beneficial for cardiovascular health but their circulating levels indicate higher cardiovascular disease risks in populations. In humans, CORIN gene polymorphisms have been associated with cardiovascular disorders, such as heart failure, 11 cardiac hypertrophy, 12 and hypertension, 10 suggesting that the factors regulating the function of the CORIN gene may be the underlying molecular mechanism of the association between corin protein and cardiovascular disease.

There are several strengths in our study. First, in contrast to traditional observational studies, the MR study using genetic variants as instruments to make rational causal inferences could exclude reverse causality, residual confounding, and other biases. Second, multiple analytical methods were applied to carefully examine the assumptions of instrument variables and estimate the causal effect of corin on the risk of stroke. However, we need to acknowledge that there are also several limitations in our study. First, our participants only included Chinese adults; therefore, the generalizability of our results to other populations with different ethnic backgrounds is uncertain. Second, the validity of the MR study was dependent on the chosen genetic instruments. 37 Although no evidence of violations was observed in our study, we cannot exclude that weak associations between individual SNPs and phenotypes might be overlooked. Third, our MR analysis was based on common variants, which was considered to explain only a limited degree of variation in serum corin levels, and we were not able to analyze the potential contribution of rare variants in a not very large population.

In conclusion, through a 1‐sample MR analysis in the Gusu cohort, we found that a genetically determined variation of serum corin concentration was significantly associated with the risk of stroke in Chinese adults. Our results provided a piece of evidence for the causal role of corin in the development of stroke. This finding suggested that elevated serum corin may be a risk factor for stroke and corin could be considered a potential therapeutic target for stroke prevention.
