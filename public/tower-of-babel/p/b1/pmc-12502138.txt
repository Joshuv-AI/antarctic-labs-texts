Prevalence and associated factors of anemia among newborns in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mengistu Abebe Messelu, Baye Tsegaye Amlak, Tadesse Yirga Akalu, Bekele Getenet Tiruneh, Mulualem Biazen Adugna, Abiyie Zeleke, Makda Fekadie Tewelgne, Aster Tadesse Shibabaw, Getnet Nibret, Salelign Amlaku, Temesgen Ayenew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemia in newborns is a complex health problem posing immediate life-threatening and long-term complications, and it is considered a serious global public health problem, particularly in African countries. Due to significant geographical variations in anemia prevalence across different African countries, inconsistencies between study findings were notably increased. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the pooled prevalence and its associated factors of anemia among newborns in Africa.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024608059) and conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A comprehensive and structured search was carried out in PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, ScienceDirect, HINARI, and Web of Science, covering all literature available up to July 20, 2025. A meta-analysis using a random-effects model was performed. The heterogeneity of studies was assessed using I2 statistics, and to identify the possible causes of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis and meta-regression were used. Egger’s test and funnel plots were used to evaluate a publication bias. STATA version 17.0 software was used for data analysis, and a p-value < 0.05 was used to declare a statistical significance.ResultsFourteen articles with 4488 newborns were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. This review found that the prevalence of anemia among newborns in Africa was found to be 30.52% (95% CI: 22.92, 38.11) (I2 = 96.94%). The subgroup analysis using the regions of the continent showed that the lowest prevalence was observed in East Africa (23.48%; 95% CI: 19.01, 27.96) (I2 = 84.82%), followed by West Africa (39.46%; 95% CI: 14.58, 64.34) (I2 = 99.16%). Insufficient vegetable consumption (OR: 3.22, 95% CI: 1.93, 5.37), maternal anemia (OR: 2.94, 95% CI: 1.55, 5.58), lack of iron supplementation during pregnancy (OR: 2.66, 95% CI: 1.35, 5.23), and having a history of antepartum hemorrhage (APH) (OR: 2.26, 95% CI: 1.39, 3.66) were factors associated with anemia among newborns in Africa.Conclusion and recommendationsAnemia remains a significant public health concern in Africa, with approximately one-third of newborns affected. Maternal anemia, insufficient vegetable consumption, lack of iron supplementation during pregnancy, and a history of antepartum hemorrhage were significantly associated with anemia among newborns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06103-8.

Introduction

Anemia is a condition in which the number of Red Blood Cells (RBCs) or the hemoglobin concentration within them is lower than normal [1]. The optimal hemoglobin level required to meet physiological needs varies by age, sex, residence, smoking habits, and pregnancy status [2].

The global prevalence of anemia in children under five was 39.8%, equivalent to 269 million children with anemia [3]. The prevalence of anemia among newborns was between 50 and 80% in developing countries, and the highest prevalence was observed in the African region, ranging from 23.4% to 66% [3, 4].

The cause of anemia in newborns is multifactorial, including nutritional deficiencies, hemorrhage, hemolysis, failure of red cell production, hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease, and maternal and neonatal infections [5, 6]. In Africa, anemia in neonates is often associated with maternal anemia, inadequate nutritional status, and various infectious diseases, particularly malaria [1, 7, 8]. Moreover, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency is a genetic disorder that affects red blood cells and is a significant cause of mild to severe anemia in newborns [6, 9]. Anti-D alloantibodies are the most common cause of alloimmunization to Rh blood group antigens in newborn anemia with fetal erythroblastosis [9, 10].

The severity of anemia in neonates is divided into three categories based on hemoglobin value as follows: 10 to 13.9 g/dL for mild anemia, 7 to 9.9 g/dL for moderate anemia, and < 7 g/dL is severe anemia [1, 11]. If left untreated, it poses impairment in brain maturation and development, tissue hypoxia, stunted growth, and then arrested growth [12]. Furthermore, anemia in newborns leads to irreversible long-term complications such as bone diseases, heart failure, multi-organ failure, liver and spleen enlargement, growth disorders, decreased motor activity, social inattention, delay in brain maturation, tissue hypoxia, stunted growth, and severe cognitive impairments [13].

Despite several advancements in anemia prevention and control strategies, including maternal prophylaxis with iron [14], it has been the major public health problem in the world, specifically in African countries [15]. Due to significant geographical variations in anemia prevalence across different regions of Africa, heterogeneity among the included studies was markedly increased. These regional differences contribute to inconsistencies in the findings, underscoring the need for subgroup analysis to better understand the distribution and determinants of anemia on the continent [15–17]. Moreover, there is no systematic review or meta-analysis study that reveals the pooled prevalence of anemia among newborns in Africa. Therefore, this review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with anemia among newborns in Africa.

The findings would hold significant implications for public health, which will provide information for the development of policies and strategies aimed at preventing anemia in newborns in Africa. Additionally, policymakers can utilize these findings to allocate resources and plan effective prevention strategies for anemia among newborns.

Methods

Study registration and reporting protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis is registered on ResearchRegistry.com under the unique identification number (UIN): review registry CRD42024608059. This study has been reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [18].

Searching strategy

Following the PRISMA guideline, studies were searched via different databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Scopus, ScienceDirect, HINARI, and Google Scholar without publication year restriction. We have also done manual searching of the reference lists of already identified relevant articles so as to retrieve more eligible studies. In addition, we searched Google Scholar up to July 20, 2025, to search for more relevant articles; all published literature were retrieved, critically evaluated, and assessed for inclusion in this study. Search terms used to access studies using PECO strategies, including CoCoPop (Condition, Context, and Population), are the following: (Condition: “anemia OR anaemia OR anemias OR hemoglobin level”) AND (Context: “Sub-Saharan African countries OR Africa OR Developing countries OR African countries”) AND (Population: “neonates OR newborns OR newborn OR babies OR neonatal period OR up to 28 days OR infants”) for prevalence studies and PEO (Population, Exposure, Outcome) (Population: “neonates OR newborns OR newborn OR babies OR neonatal period OR up to 28 days OR infants”) AND (Exposure: “determinants OR associated factors OR risk factors OR causes”) AND (Outcome: “anemia OR anaemia OR anemias in Africa”) to identify determinants of anemia. These search strategies were developed using “AND” and/or “OR” Boolean operators (Supplementary file 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of anemia among newborns in Africa, which is expressed as a percentage and frequency in articles. The secondary outcome was factors associated with anemia. Possible factors associated with anemia among newborns are maternal age, neonatal age, sex, gestational age (GA), iron supplementation, maternal anemia, parity, mode of delivery, gender, residence, comorbidities, subgaleal hemorrhage, having hyperbilirubinemia, Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH), income, and vegetable consumption. Nevertheless, to increase the validity of this study, factors reported in at least two studies were considered for pooled analysis.

Data extraction

Three independent reviewers (M.A., T.A., and B.T.) screened the articles based on their titles and abstracts. A full-text review was performed by the independent reviewers (M.A., T.Y., A.Z., M.F., M.B., A.T., and B.G.) to ensure the eligibility of studies for the final analysis. The disagreement was handled based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the third reviewer (B.T.) was involved in the final decision.

Data, including the author name, publication year, country of the study, prevalence of anemia, sample size, study design, and the severity of anemia, were extracted using Microsoft Excel 2019. Firstly, two independent reviewers (M.A. and T.A.) extracted and cross-checked the extracted against the inclusion criteria. Secondly, if there was a discrepancy, it was handled by re-evaluating the full text. Finally, to ensure accuracy and identify any errors, a senior researcher (B.T.) examined the extracted data.

Assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias

The authors (M.A. and T.A.), who performed data extraction, assessed the quality of included studies. The quality of these studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment tool [22]. It is a widely recognized and validated tool for evaluating observational studies, including cross-sectional and cohort designs, providing a comprehensive and standardized approach to assessing methodological rigor. The criteria were:1) sample representativeness; 2) inclusion criteria; 3) objective and standard criteria used; 4) sample size adequacy; 5) using valid and reliable measurements; 6) using appropriate statistical analysis methods; 7) strategies to identify and handle confounders; and 8) response rates. The overall quality scores were categorized as low quality (high risk of bias), moderate quality (moderate risk of bias), and high quality (low risk of bias) for scores of < 50, 50–75, and > 75%, respectively [23].

Data analysis

STATA version 17 was used for statistical analysis. The funnel plot [24] and Egger’s test [25] were used to check the potential publication bias. The I2 statistics were used to assess the heterogeneity of articles [26], and the scores 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered as low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively. For the test results that exhibited heterogeneity, the DerSimonian and Laird’s random effects model was used as a method of analysis. Subgroup analysis was done using countries and severity of anemia. The sensitivity test was done to check the effect of individual studies on the pooled estimate.

Factors associated with anemia among newborns

Insufficient vegetable consumption

Two studies were reported on the association between the prevalence of anemia among newborns and vegetable consumption in Africa. This meta-analysis study revealed that those who didn’t consume vegetables per week had 3.2 times higher odds of having anemia as compared to their counterparts (OR: 3.22, 95% CI: 1.93, 5.37). There was no observed heterogeneity, i2 = 0% (Fig. 5).Fig. 5The association between vegetable consumption and anemia among newborns in Africa

The association between vegetable consumption and anemia among newborns in Africa

Maternal anemia

This meta-analysis study revealed that those newborns whose mothers had anemia had 3.2 times higher odds of being anemic as compared with those who hadn’t (OR: 2.94, 95% CI: 1.55, 5.58) (I2 = 75.2%) (Fig. 6).Fig. 6The association between newborn anemia and maternal anemia

The association between newborn anemia and maternal anemia

Lack of iron supplementation during pregnancy

According to this meta-analysis, iron supplementation during pregnancy was another determinant factor associated with anemia among newborns in Africa. Those newborns whose mothers didn’t take iron supplementation during their pregnancy had 2.66 times higher odds of being anemic compared with their counterparts (OR: 2.66, 95% CI: 1.35, 5.23) (I2 = 79.4%) (Fig. 7).Fig. 7The association between iron supplementation during pregnancy and anemia among newborns

The association between iron supplementation during pregnancy and anemia among newborns

History of APH

Furthermore, this meta-analysis found that the odds of anemia among newborns delivered from mothers who had a history of APH was 2.26 times higher compared with those who hadn’t (OR: 2.26, 95% CI: 1.39, 3.66) (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 8).Fig. 8The association between history of APH and anemia among newborns

Discussion

Anemia among newborns is a critical public health issue, given its potential long-term effects on infant growth, development, and overall health. The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive insight into the prevalence and factors associated with anemia among newborns in Africa.

The overall pooled prevalence of anemia among newborns in Africa was found to be 30.52%. This suggested that approximately one-third of newborns in Africa are born with anemia, highlighting the significant burden of the problem on the continent. This finding was in line with the study conducted in Afghanistan [38], which reported that the prevalence of anemia among newborns was 26.8%. However, it is much higher than previous studies done in the USA (14%) [39], Nepal (5.7%) [40], and Iran (11.7%) [41]. This discrepancy might be attributed to nutritional deficiencies, poor maternal health, various socioeconomic factors, and differences in the clinical status of the study subjects [42]. Moreover, the methodological differences, such as variations in study design and sample size, might also have contributed to the observed variation [33]. This high prevalence of anemia observed across different countries in Africa points to systemic public health challenges, including limited access to adequate nutrition, maternal care, and health services in many regions.

Subgroup analysis revealed that the lowest prevalence was observed in East Africa (23.48%), followed by West Africa (39.46%). These variations might be attributed to differences in local health policies, healthcare infrastructure, socio-economic conditions, and the availability of resources for maternal and child health programs [43]. Furthermore, better utilization of antenatal care services in East Africa, leads to higher rates of iron and folic acid supplementation, malaria prevention, and early identification of maternal anemia [44]. On the other hand, the highest prevalence of anemia among newborns was observed in Benin (61.1%) and Gabon (52.0%), which indicated regional variability across the continent. The possible reason for the high prevalence in Benin could be due to the inclusion of premature newborns, and most of the newborns were delivered from malaria-infected mothers [45]. These findings underscore the need for country-specific strategies to combat anemia, considering local health challenges and the burden of diseases.

The study revealed that newborns whose mothers did not consume vegetables regularly were 3.2 times more likely to develop anemia compared to those whose mothers consumed vegetables. This finding was supported by the study done in Qatar [46]. This might be due to the fact that vegetables are an important source of vitamins and minerals, particularly folate and non-heme iron, which are essential for RBC synthesis and preventing anemia [47]. This finding highlights the importance of maternal nutrition during pregnancy in preventing anemia among newborns.

This meta-analysis found that there was a significant statistical association between maternal anemia and newborn anemia. Newborns whose mothers had anemia were 2.9 times more likely to have anemia. This was consistent with previously conducted studies in [48, 49]. This could be due to the fetus obtaining iron from maternal iron stores, and when the maternal iron stores are depleted, the fetus cannot get adequate iron, which leads to decreased iron stores among newborns [50]. In addition, anemia in pregnant women, often due to nutritional deficiencies (such as iron and folate), can lead to inadequate oxygen supply to the fetus, increasing the risk of anemia at birth [51]. Thus, the WHO guideline recommends that a daily iron and folic acid supplementation starting from the first trimester and continuing until delivery to prevent anemia among mothers and newborns [52]. This implies the need for improved maternal healthcare, including prenatal nutritional screening and supplementation programs aimed at reducing maternal anemia.

Moreover, newborns whose mothers did not receive iron supplementation during pregnancy had 2.7 times higher odds of developing anemia compared to those whose mothers did take the iron supplements. This finding was supported by the study done in Afghanistan [38]. A systematic review of randomized and quasi-randomized trials also reported a similar finding, which revealed that daily iron supplementation of pregnant women resulted in a 67% reduction in iron deficiency anemia in term newborns [53]. This could be due to the fact that iron supplementation during pregnancy increases maternal iron stores, which in turn increases the amount of iron transfer to the fetus because the main source of iron among newborns is their iron store during their intrauterine life [54]. In addition, newborns are prone to iron deficiency due to increased iron requirements to support rapid growth and an earlier onset of erythropoiesis [55]. During pregnancy, a maternal iron demand increases significantly, particularly in the second and third trimesters, due to increased blood volume, the growing fetus, and placental development [56]. Without adequate iron supplementation, the fetus may not store sufficient iron, increasing the risk of neonatal anemia [57]. Therefore, iron supplementation during pregnancy and a diet rich in micronutrients that may facilitate iron absorption and utilization, such as vitamin C, vitamin A, vitamin B12, carotenoids, and folate, are recommended [58, 59]. However, consuming foods high in phytic acid, which may reduce iron absorption, should be discouraged to reduce the burden of anemia among newborns.

According to the current meta-analysis, the odds of anemia among newborns delivered from mothers who had a history of APH were 2.3 times higher compared with those who didn’t a history of APH. Similar findings were reported from Afghanistan [38] and Canada [60], revealing that a history of APH was a determinant factor for anemia among newborns. This could be due to the reason that bleeding during pregnancy leads to a compromised fetal iron transfer, limiting the development of adequate fetal iron stores and increasing the risk of neonatal anemia [61, 62]. Furthermore, APH can result in maternal blood loss, leading to maternal anemia, which in turn reduces oxygen and nutrient supply to the fetus [63]. Another reason could be blood loss during pregnancy may result in placental insufficiency, affecting nutrient and oxygen delivery, thereby impairing fetal hematopoiesis [64]. Therefore, a pregnant woman who had a history of APH should receive intensive follow-up, including iron supplementation, treatment of anemia, and possibly early neonatal screening for anemia.

Implications for public health and policy

The high prevalence of anemia among newborns in Africa calls for urgent public health interventions focused on improving maternal nutrition, particularly ensuring that pregnant women have adequate access to iron, folate, and other micronutrients. Effective strategies may include expanding antenatal care services, increasing the distribution of iron and folic acid supplements, and improving access to nutritious foods. Additionally, there is a need for public health campaigns to raise awareness about the importance of balanced diets during pregnancy, including the consumption of vegetables and other nutrient-dense foods.

Addressing maternal anemia is critical not only for improving newborn health outcomes but also for reducing the long-term impact of anemia on child development. Given the strong association between maternal and neonatal anemia, interventions targeting pregnant women should be prioritized.

Moreover, the variability in prevalence rates across different African countries suggests that context-specific interventions are necessary. Factors such as healthcare infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and access to nutrition should guide the design and implementation of anemia prevention programs in different regions.

Limitations of the study

Our study has limitations. First, all included studies were observational, which may involve residual confounding that cannot be completely eliminated in observational studies. Second, the meta-regression analysis showed no significant heterogeneity related to sample size or publication year. This suggests that the differences observed in prevalence rates across studies are likely due to other factors not captured in the analysis. Third, the limited number of studies included in certain analyses (as reflected by the I2 value of 0%) may have reduced the power to detect true heterogeneity and impacted the robustness of the pooled estimate.

Conclusion

Anemia remains a significant public health concern in Africa, affecting approximately one-third of newborns. Maternal anemia, insufficient vegetable consumption, lack of iron supplementation during pregnancy, and a history of APH were significantly associated with the prevalence of anemia among newborns. Therefore, improving vegetable consumption, enhancing iron supplementation during antenatal care, and preventing and promptly managing maternal anemia can help to reduce the burden of anemia among newborns. Furthermore, given the regional differences observed, tailored approaches should be adopted to ensure effective anemia prevention and management across the continent.
