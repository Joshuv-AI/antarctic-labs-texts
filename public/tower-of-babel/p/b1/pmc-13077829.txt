Supporting paramedic sleep and fatigue: a qualitative exploration of education considerations from the early-career paramedic perspective
Authors: Sian E. Wanstall, Anjum Naweed, Brandon W. J. Brown, Meagan E. Crowther, Robert J. Adams, Amy C. Reynolds
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor sleep and fatigue are common among paramedics, with negative implications for the safety and well-being of paramedics and the community. Targeted education can support sleep and fatigue outcomes, however knowledge relating to the optimal content, delivery methods, and timing of education delivery, is limited. This study explores the experiences of sleep, fatigue, and shift work among early-career paramedics to inform sleep and fatigue education specifically for the paramedic workforce, with considerations for implementation across the paramedic career span.MethodsWe used a qualitative approach underpinned by a critical realist paradigm. Semi-structured interviews with early-career paramedics (n = 30) were conducted, and data analysed inductively using conventional content analysis.ResultsThree categories were constructed: (1) Learning to sleep well as a paramedic, (2) Sleep knowledge and experiences of paramedics, and (3) Sleep and fatigue education preferences for paramedics. As shift workers, paramedics learned to sleep well experientially and through informal knowledge sharing, rather than meaningful education or preparation. Feeling unprepared for shift work was common, as was a desire for evidence-based education and systematic support throughout the career. Co-designed, contextually relevant, adaptive and embedded educational approaches were preferred. Results also reflected a critical gap in knowledge and awareness surrounding sleep disorders, suggesting a need for meaningful education and support pathways.ConclusionsFindings underscored a need for evidence-based education about sleep, fatigue, and sleep disorders within paramedic tertiary education and organisational systems. Co-designed approaches combining expert knowledge and lived experience with clearer help-seeking pathways are essential for improving paramedic and community well-being, safety, and workforce sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14278-8.

Background

Sleep is essential for human physiological function, and regular, healthy sleep is a fundamental aspect of overall health and well-being [1]. Paramedics face various challenges in obtaining sufficient sleep, likely due to the demands of their occupation, both in terms of role, and when navigating disrupted timing of sleep opportunities due to work schedules [2, 3]. Reports of poor sleep quality and excessive daytime sleepiness among paramedics are not only common [4], they are often reported at higher rates than the Australian general population [5–7]. In addition, sleep disorders such as insomnia, obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), restless legs syndrome, and shift work disorder (SWD), are common in this cohort [5, 8], with prevalence rates equal or higher to those observed in the general young (20%) [9, 10] and middle-aged (42%) [9] adult population. Poor sleep and fatigue in paramedics is associated with cognitive impairment, injury, and poor mental well-being [5, 6, 11]. Further, the safety critical nature of the paramedic role means that insufficient sleep and fatigue can impact the broader community through safety compromising behaviours, increased risk for accidents and errors, and unsafe driving [7, 11, 12].

While paramedics work across a variety of contexts [13], shift work is a common part of the job. Shift work (i.e., non-standard work hours, typically beyond 8am-6pm) [14] is a well-known, independent contributor to poor health and well-being outcomes [15]. Insufficient sleep due to circadian disruption, as well as reduced sleep opportunity and quality, are key factors in this relationship. The amount of time spent on task, break regularity, task type, meal timing, and end of shift can vary significantly, impacting sleep and recovery. Moreover, the nature of paramedic work involves exposure to potentially traumatising events and a requirement to perform emotional labour [16, 17] which may contribute to delayed sleep onset or poor sleep quality, and thus, insufficient sleep.

One underexplored approach to supporting sleep in paramedics is the provision of targeted sleep health and fatigue education. Although varying widely in delivery and application, this strategy invariably incorporates a range of information aimed at promoting sleep knowledge and behaviours (e.g., basic sleep science, sleep hygiene, sleep disorders) [18]. Nevertheless, in shift working populations, sleep and fatigue education has been shown to have a positive impact on sleep outcomes [19]. In nursing and emergency medical service (EMS) groups, sleep and fatigue education has been found to enhance sleep quality and duration and reduce fatigue-related risks [20, 21]. Additionally, sleep and fatigue education is an important element of other existing approaches to support sleep health in shift workers [18, 22, 23]. Paramedics exhibit limited knowledge and engagement with healthy sleep practices, and explicitly report a desire and need for sleep and fatigue management education [24, 25]. These findings, together with commonly reported poor sleep outcomes, mean there is a clear imperative to develop robust education interventions in this workgroup.

To date, research examining sleep and fatigue education interventions in paramedics is limited [26], and co-designed approaches that address the requirements of this profession are not evident. However, results from similar occupational groups (firefighters, nurses) suggest that this approach may hold promise for paramedics [20, 21, 27, 28]. For example, a meta-analysis of fatigue training (including sleep) reported a beneficial impact on fatigue, sleep quality, and sleep quantity in emergency medical service personnel and other shift workers [21]. Further, a scoping review reported improved trends in sleep outcomes in nurses who had received sleep education and training across various studies [20]. A consistent feature of existing studies is that the optimal delivery methods for education, as well as optimal timing relative to the commencement of shift work roles, remain unclear [20, 21, 24, 29]. Incorporating the perspectives of the end-user (i.e. paramedics) through collaborative research approaches may address this uncertainty.

In a co-designed study with young shift workers, Shriane et al. (2025) reported that collaboratively developing sleep health resources led to materials that reflected the lived experience of shiftwork, and were user-friendly, valuable, and informative [30, 31]. Further, much like other professions, paramedics have been found to prefer and engage better with sleep education tailored specifically to their occupational needs [32], which can also be addressed through underpinning education with paramedic experiences and perspectives.

The purpose of the current study was thus, to establish a base of knowledge to inform sleep and fatigue education specifically for the paramedic workforce, with considerations for systematic implementation across the paramedic career span. We aimed to explore the experiences of sleep, fatigue, and shift work among early-career paramedics (defined here as 2–15 years’ experience) as a key step towards co-designed sleep and fatigue education resources and approaches. The following research questions guided the study:

RQ1

What knowledge and experiences do paramedics have regarding sleep disorders and their identification and management?

RQ2

Based on their training and early-career experiences, what do paramedics perceive they need to learn about sleep, fatigue, and shift work?

RQ3

What content, timing, and delivery methods do paramedics prefer for education about sleep, fatigue, and shift work?

Methods

Study design

A qualitative research design was employed, underpinned by a critical realist ontology and a constructionist epistemology. Critical realism theorises that a reality exists beyond human perception, which is shaped by underlying social contexts (including unobservable structures and mechanisms), and thus ever negotiable [33]. This paradigm allowed for the examination of paramedic experiences and perspectives related to sleep, fatigue, and shift work, whilst also considering the influence of the complex social settings in which paramedics exist. Using a constructionist epistemology recognised reality as a social and historical construction which through interaction, discourse, and shared experience [34], allowed for a socially shaped interpretation and narrative of paramedic experiences and perspectives of educational needs. We conducted one-to-one interviews to facilitate in-depth exploration of experiences and examination of underlying influences.

Participants and recruitment

Recruitment occurred in May 2022, facilitated by a research participation poster disseminated via social media and paramedic contacts, as well as by the Australasian College of Paramedicine to their members. Inclusion criteria were two-fold: registration as a paramedic within Australia, and 2-to-15 years’ experience in a paramedic role. Prospective participants (n = 68) provided voluntary, informed consent within REDCap [35, 36] (an online, secure data-capture platform hosted at Flinders University) which was accessed through a QR code located on the research poster. Demographic information and eligibility criteria were collected and purposively sampled to capture a diverse range of Australian early-career paramedics (n = 30). Two researchers conducted semi-structured interviews with the selected participants between June and August 2022, which were audio-recorded. Characteristics of the sample have been described previously [37]. Participants were, on average, 30 years old with 5.5 years of experience as a paramedic, and just over half (53%) were male. Most of the sample was engaged in shift work (88%) and working full time (83%) in metropolitan regions.

Procedure

Both in-person (n = 3) and virtual (n = 27) interviews were conducted according to participant preference, lasting between 45 and 142 min. Interviews commenced with rapport building, including questions such as “how did you come to be a paramedic?” and “what are the best/hardest parts about being a paramedic?”. Participants were asked about their experiences with sleep as an early-career paramedic and the strategies they and their peers used to manage sleep and fatigue while working shifts. How these strategies were learned and applied during the early-career phase was also explored. Additionally, participants were asked about their knowledge of sleep disorders, including identifying common disorders, approaches to management, and their broader experiences with these conditions.

Following these questions, participants completed a qualitative scenario-based task for understanding systems thinking processes (that is, how individuals consider complex interactions within their work environment), using challenging stories specific to their occupational context. This approach has been used previously in similar safety-critical contexts [38–43] and findings from this component of the interview will be reported elsewhere. After the scenario task, participant education experiences related to sleep, fatigue and shift work, and associated perspectives on the educational needs for early-career paramedics were explored.

The semi-structured interview protocol was designed specifically for the overarching study to answer a series of sleep questions in early-career paramedics. The protocol was informed by insights derived from prior studies utilising similar methodology [38–43]. Interviews concluded with an offer for the participant to review their transcript, and information regarding receipt of a $50 gift card for remuneration of their time. Relative to the hourly rate for paramedic employment in Australia, this was considered a modest token of appreciation and unlikely to have influenced a desire to participate. Interviews were transcribed by a professional third-party service, and de-identified by the research team. Human Research Ethics Committee approval was obtained from Flinders University (Project ID 4668).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using conventional content analysis [44] with an inductive approach (i.e. no preformulated codes or categories). The intent of the study was not to develop theory, but rather, describe and explore a large dataset, therefore suited to content analysis [45]. Consistent with the flexible yet structured nature of conventional content analysis, the analysis was primarily interpretive, while allowing for the supplementary use of frequency counting to illustrate prominence of particular concepts within the data. The analytic process involved data familiarisation, open coding in NVIVO 14 [46] to identify recurring patterns across the dataset, iterative refinement of codes, and construction of categories that captured and meaningfully interpreted participant discourse. Several closed-end questions were individually summarised and descriptively reported using frequencies. This approach aligns with conventional content analysis and has been adopted in prior research [44, 47]. Where closed questions were not asked by an interviewer, missing cases were documented and number of respondents transparently reported, with frequency counts based only on available responses. Analysis was conducted solely by lead author and critically refined through reflexive discussion with the broader research team.

Positionality and trustworthiness

To ensure trustworthiness of findings, we considered key tenets for content analysis proposed by Graneheim and Lundman [48]. The lead author is a registered paramedic and brought an understanding of the lived experience of sleep, fatigue and shift work in paramedicine. Similarly, other members of the research team had lived experiences in shiftwork within healthcare and research environments. This ‘insider researcher’ status facilitated rapport and trust throughout the interview process, and in data interpretation, enhancing credibility and dependability of the findings [49]. The broader research team comprised expertise in qualitative methodology, sleep and shift work research, and clinical sleep health, ensuring broader interpretation and co-construction of findings. To reduce the influence of individual bias and ensure interpretations remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researcher assumptions, reflexivity was embedded throughout the research process. The lead author engaged in ongoing critical reflection to identify and account for personal beliefs and assumptions through regular in-depth discussions among team members, further promoting credibility of the findings.

In addition to ongoing reflexive dialogue, credibility was supported through purposive sampling to ensure representation of diverse experiences and perspectives. Further, systematic coding techniques were undertaken in collaboration with members of the research team, with analytic decisions carefully documented and presented visually to ensure transparency and dependability. Participant quotes were used extensively to preserve and illustrate the core meaning of participant accounts, enhancing both credibility and confirmability. Participants were also invited to review their transcripts to confirm accuracy; one participant accepted this offer. Dependability and transferability were supported through transparent reporting of participant characteristics and the research process including recruitment processes, and data collection and analysis.

Results

Three broad categories, incorporating nine subcategories, were constructed from participant accounts. These included: (1) Learning to sleep well as a paramedic, describing early-career experiences of learning to sleep well and experiences surrounding sleep and fatigue education; (2) Sleep knowledge and experiences of paramedics, highlighting paramedic experiences and knowledge related to sleep and sleep disorders; and (3) Sleep and fatigue education preferences for paramedics, outlining preferred sleep and fatigue education content and modalities from the paramedic perspective. Learning to sleep well as a paramedic

Learning the hard way

The early-career phase for paramedics was reported to involve a “trial and error” (P. 2) period of learning to achieve healthy sleep, as well as how to manage shift work more broadly. Typically, this involved trying out various strategies over time to “see what works” (P. 9) and involved an acquired understanding that “…what works for some people doesn’t work for others” (P. 10). It was also considered an ongoing process as there may be a need to “…change it up as the years go by with how your circumstances change” (P. 9). While finding practical strategies were seen as key, so too was learning the importance of valuing sleep itself. Ultimately, most had learned that to sleep well, one needs to take responsibility and make sleep a priority: “…sleep and fatigue management is actually a really important part of life and of your job. And you will actually be a better person and a better paramedic if you get good sleep” (P. 12). Unfortunately, this was often “learned the hard way” (P. 3), by virtue of being a young shift worker with insufficient knowledge:I think it was probably a bit of naivety about how invincible you are. I wouldn’t say I was - I wasn’t arrogant. I wouldn’t say I was stupid. But I guess you’re not really that aware of consequences. So, it’s not until as you get older you go ‘ooh oh I shouldn’t have done that, probably not the healthiest thing to do' (P. 14).

So, it’s not until as you get older you go ‘ooh oh I shouldn’t have done that, probably not the healthiest thing to do' (P. 14).

Learning to sleep well began for some during initial shift work exposure on ambulance placements, however for most it was during initial employment experiences: “…that first set of four [shifts] that you do is the first time that you really start to understand and start to work out how you’re going to handle [shift work] (P. 19)”. Some shared they “found it really hard to adjust” (P. 8), with one participant stating “…you don’t really know what you’re getting yourself into until you start doing it and realise how hard it is” (P. 20). When asked if they were prepared for shift work, nearly all participants (n = 26, 87%) felt that with the benefit of hindsight they were not prepared, and that largely, early-career paramedics “are vastly unprepared for it” (P. 3). Informal education as the norm

Feeling unprepared was connected to experiences and perceptions of limited education and support regarding sleep, fatigue, and shift work, both prior to and during the transition into shift work or employment. By and large, participants shared that they learned what they know through “…word of mouth and shared experience” (P. 13). A culture of knowledge sharing was frequently discussed and highly valued across all participants, extending beyond the context of sleep. However, the discourse around sleep was nuanced relative to occupational experience. Many shared that while “…sleep gets discussed in the context of helping new people” (P. 21), beyond the transition into shift work, conversations become more casual: “…at the start of night shifts, people would be ‘oh did you have a good sleep today?’ kind of thing, just like conversational sort of stuff” (P. 7). These conversations were reported to seldom involve practical advice (“no one is really like ‘Oh, well have you tried this?’ or ‘Oh, I do this to sleep’” P.10) but rather act as a mechanism for coping and camaraderie.

Participants often sought to independently learn more about managing their sleep and fatigue as a shift worker: “…I kind of took my own initiative because I knew I kind of wasn’t the best sleeper anyway, so I knew it was probably going to be a bit more difficult” (P. 8). This was, however, often undertaken in an informal manner: “…you just sort of Google stuff” (P. 9). Seeking knowledge was also reported to be common when supporting student paramedics: “…when I have students or like fresh interns come along to work with me, often they will ask like ‘oh what do you do around night shifts and what do you do with this and that and how do you manage it?’” (P. 7). Due to challenging experiences, the vast majority (n = 26, 87%) of participants reported that they would have liked to receive more formal education about sleep and fatigue prior to commencing shift work. Additionally, participants expressed that education about sleep, fatigue and shiftwork management should be available throughout the paramedic career-span to support a healthy transition into shift work and sustainable careers:…these uni[versity] students they literally just have no idea what they’re walking into… they’re going to finish their third year this year and go straight into full time shift work without adequate preparation and education around it. Like it’s going to make it a lot harder to transition and to form healthy habits early. (P. 28)

Formal education experiences

The majority of participants reported receiving limited, or no, formal education related to sleep, fatigue, or shift work management across their career span. Figure 1 shows self-reported sleep and fatigue education experiences. Ten (33%) participants believed they may have received education at one time point but were not able to recall it, and eight (27%) reported receiving no education at all. Nine (30%) recalled receiving brief or limited education at either the university or organisational level. While most participants reported receiving no education as part of their tertiary studies, for a few, education was embedded into their undergraduate curriculum. This education, however, was largely felt to be insufficient, delivered too early relative to commencing employment, or lacking information tailored specifically to managing sleep in the context of shift work: “…we had one lifestyle management topic in first year and that just touched on gym and meditation and I think it’s message was really important, but I think it was almost lost on us as 18-year-olds…” (P. 29). Overall, 87% of participants would have liked to have had more sleep and fatigue education prior to or during the transition into shift work.

Many participants reported there “wasn’t a whole lot” (P.10) of education within organisation through their transition period as an early-career paramedic. Some described both formal and informal well-being sessions delivered during induction phases, however, these were also felt broadly to be lacking in appropriate focus or information, specifically “in terms of sleep management” (P. 20). It was reported that the most common education delivered in the transition phase involved only information regarding organisational fatigue management processes. Yet, knowledge of existing organisational processes, or where to find relevant resources (e.g., policies, fatigue scoring tools), was typically limited. Indeed, some participants had “no idea” (P. 23) if their organisation had a formal fatigue management processs, and others believed their organisation offered no formal fatigue management process at all: “It’s disappointing when you actually think about it and speak about it, there is nothing offered at all” (P. 22). Additionally, this education was typically delivered via mandatory learning packages, a modality which participants felt was largely ineffective, and that overall, key information was lacking: “You have the like… fatigue policy and stuff, which talks about how you should make sure you get enough [sleep], blah, blah, blah. But it doesn’t say how you get enough” (P. 9).

There were only three participants (10%) who described memorable and impactful education related to sleep. These experiences included a university-held session about shift worker health and an organisational online information package regarding sleep for shift workers. One participant also shared their positive experience with an organisational well-being course incorporating sleep education:…we had a wellness week…they had a nutritionist come in and talk to us about food management and things like using coffee and health snacks to get through nightshifts and everything. But then also the effects of caffeine too close to when you need to sleep and stuff… I think we had some exercise physios come in as well and talk about exercise relating to sleep as well which was really good… (P. 8).

Sleep knowledge and experiences of paramedics

General sleep knowledge and experiences

Knowledge of sleep and fatigue information beyond the need to get sufficient sleep was uncommon. Concepts that were occasionally discussed in interviews were circadian rhythms and light, optimal sleep duration, sleep stages (specifically REM and deep sleep), and sleep debt, however knowledge typically reflected a surface level of awareness. One participant, who broadly displayed good knowledge of sleep basics, discussed the concepts of sleep regularity and sleep pressure: “…after you’ve been out (working) for a certain number of hours, like the sleep pressure is really high… no matter what you’re doing, your body’s going to put you to sleep…” (P. 5). Participants commonly referenced ‘sleep hygiene’ of which they had a good understanding based on learned practices. Certain strategies were reportedly commonly used to create a healthy sleeping environment, including controlling light, noise, and temperature. This included use of blackout blinds, eye masks, ear plugs, and air conditioning, limiting screen time, and maintaining a solitary sleep environment. Pre-bed routines were also important, incorporating showering, fresh clothing, reading, meditating, listening to music/sleep sounds, and downtime to facilitate “unwinding” (P. 30).

Exercise and nutrition were commonly discussed in terms of sleep and fatigue management, including meal planning, hydration, and timing of food intake, reflecting that participants are engaged with general sleep hygiene advice. Caffeine and alcohol were considered carefully, with strategic caffeine use to manage fatigue while avoiding impacts on sleep; however, there were varied perspectives, with one participant reporting: “…a lot of people will knock off and have a beer to go to bed basically. They will, you know, have a couple of drinks to help them wind down to then be able to go to bed to sleep” (P. 28). Some participants reported using sleep medications (most commonly melatonin), as well as other sedatives, although many emphasised that they knew medication should be an adjunct rather than a primary strategy due to concerns about reliance. Overall, planning ahead, maintaining consistent routines, and drawing on support from family or social networks were considered central to achieving good sleep.

Despite good knowledge of practical strategies to achieve good sleep, regularly achieving good sleep varied considerably across participants. Unsurprisingly, shift work and having children were both intrinsically linked to variable sleep quality. Having young children at home or engaging in on-call work schedules were key factors for those reporting highly inconsistent sleep quality and fewer episodes of ‘good sleep’. For participants who reported having children after commencing shift work, changes to their sleep through these periods were a significant aspect of their sleep experience and relationship with sleep overall. Concerningly, a third of participants reported having good sleep only “a couple times a week” (P. 23) or “rarely to never” (P. 24). A few also qualified themselves as “not a good sleeper” (P. 13) or a “light sleeper” (P. 22) and attributed this to infrequent good sleep. While there were a few who described consistently good sleep, generally others reported they slept well “most of the time” (P. 25) with poor sleep episodes common around shifts (particularly prior to fist day shift or between nights).

Sleep disorder knowledge and experiences

Knowledge of common sleep disorders was limited; only a small number could share awareness of the difference between a sleep problem and a sleep disorder which met diagnostic criteria. Figure 2 shows awareness of and personal experience with sleep disorders. The majority made an educated guess when asked if they knew what a sleep disorder was: “Sleep disorder? I’m not going to fumble my way through a sleep disorder, but I can only assume it’s something that is affecting your sleep in a negative way, but couldn’t tell you definitions, hours, time” (P. 2). Providing an example of a sleep disorder was less challenging, with insomnia (n = 23), OSA (n = 17), and narcolepsy (n = 9) commonly identified, and all participants able to identify at least one sleep disorder. Restless legs syndrome, shift work disorder, and hypersomnia were also each recognised once, and night terrors/sleep paralysis twice. There were a few occasions (n = 3) where participants incorrectly attributed a chronic health disorder (chronic fatigue, post-traumatic stress disorder) as a sleep disorder. Knowledge of prevalence and risk factors was also limited, although there was an intrinsic awareness of the link between sleep disorders and both mental well-being and engagement in shift work and emergency services roles: “I know it’s pretty common amongst those with mental illness. Also shift workers, I’d imagine, from experience” (P. 3). Despite limited knowledge, most participants (n = 25, 83%) shared experiences related to sleep problems or sleep disorders (Fig. 2).

Interacting with patients with a diagnosed sleep disorder was a common occurrence in paramedic work and the primary source of sleep disorder awareness for some (“I come across patients all the time that have obstructive sleep apnoea” P. 18). Having general awareness that family members, friends, or colleagues were experiencing sleep disorders was apparent, “I just know people with CPAP [continuous positive airway pressure therapy], like sleep apnoea” (P. 9), while others shared detail around specific diagnoses, treatments, and related challenges –usually experienced by their loved ones (n = 17). Some reported that they do not “know of anyone that has a diagnosed [sleep] disorder” (P. 7), several still sharing however, that they “know people who really struggle with sleep” (P. 21). Some reported living with a diagnosed sleep disorder (n = 4), including OSA, insomnia, and sleep paralysis and several shared experiences of nightmares or night terrors (n = 4), and ongoing symptoms suggestive of an underlying sleep disorder (n = 7): “my [spouse has] noticed lately – oh for the last 12 months that I’ve started having periods where I’ve stopped sort of breathing for a little bit” (P. 16).

Knowing how to get help for sleep

When asked about how sleep disorders may be managed, participants thought there would ‘likely’ be a “multidisciplinary” approach, which may include psychologists, psychiatrists, pharmacists, or breathing or sleep specialists, requiring general practitioner (GP) referral for access. While many used the term ‘sleep specialist’, most did not know who these practitioners are or what they do: “I’m sure there’s sleep specialists. Exactly what their training and role, where they fit, I don’t know particularly” (P. 19). Predominantly, the GP was considered the primary point of call for any sleep problems. In relation to diagnostic and treatment processes for a sleep disorder, many suggested that screening and ‘sleep studies’ may be involved, and some discussed the use of CPAP for OSA and psychological therapies for “non-physical” (P. 6) sleep disorders (e.g., insomnia). Broadly, however, participants were unaware of available treatment modalities or where to seek them.

At odds with limited knowledge and experience when asked about sleep problems and disorders, participants largely reported feeling confident to support those around them who may be experiencing a sleep problem: “[I have] tons [of confidence] … obviously my knowledge in it is quite minimal, but I could offer my personal experience, be an ear to listen to, and only just assume, go to your GP, and go from there” (P. 2). Perceived confidence to support others coincided with paramedics highly valuing knowledge sharing and feeling they had enough experience to share “general advice” (P. 13) relative to their own trial and error period of learning to sleep well. Broadly, participants reported they would first seek to understand the problem (“why are they having trouble sleeping? What’s going on? What can I do to help?” P. 16), then share their knowledge about healthy sleep practices and the importance of prioritising sleep: “I’d go through like, this is like what works best for me… so making sure you’re setting your environment up right, making sure your like pre-sleep routine is good” (P. 20). Beyond general advice, participants shared they would advise others to seek help from a GP where re-addressing healthy sleep practices had been unsuccessful, or in the context of significant sleep problems. Finally, some reported that they would advise a peer to seek within organisation support:…Have a chat to [the well-being team]. I mean these guys deal with people doing shift work and might not be their primary response or role dealing with people with issues surrounding jobs or even home life but they’re still going to have a bit of an idea and probably be helpful. (P. 19)

Sleep and fatigue education preferences for paramedics

Desired sleep education content

Informed by their own experiences, participants provided clear and consistent recommendations surrounding education needs for early-career paramedics, as well as important considerations for the broader content and context. Regarding educational content, participants primarily discussed healthy sleep practices as a crucial component. As demonstrated by their own habits, participants felt this should include a wide range of holistic health strategies, encompassing the idea that there is no one-size-fits-all approach: “…some people might benefit more from the nutrition side, and some people might benefit from the exercise physio or meditation, because not everyone likes meditation and not everyone likes coffee” (P. 8). It was suggested that strategies should be clear and actionable, as opposed to general guidelines, and could incorporate “example management plans” (P. 25). A structured template may allow paramedics to have “somewhere to start from” (P. 25) in their inevitable trial and error process of learning to sleep well.

Participants also felt that theoretical knowledge surrounding basic sleep science and fatigue concepts (sleep stages, optimal sleep duration, circadian rhythms, fatigue definitions) should be delivered alongside practical advice, connecting strategies to their underlying principles. A desire for more theoretical knowledge also extended to a need for paramedics to understand and learn the “importance of good sleep” (P. 29) as early as possible. Specifically, it was felt that to best manage sleep loss and fatigue, one must know “…how much sleep you’re supposed to get, and… all the ways you can have bad sleep… and what bad sleep is” (P. 9). It was thought that better knowledge on the signs of sleep loss and fatigue could lead to improved individual awareness, enabling paramedics to implement support strategies, and seek support when needed. Participants emphasised that educational content should be “evidence based” (P. 25), and tailored to the paramedic context:So, I feel like any intervention or like any assistance with sleep and sleep health and shift work and stuff, if that’s going to be presented to paramedic students it needs to be tailored for the paramedic experience, because it’s so unlike anything else in health (P. 28)

So, I feel like any intervention or like any assistance with sleep and sleep health and shift work and stuff, if that’s going to be presented to paramedic students it needs to be tailored for the paramedic experience, because it’s so unlike anything else in health (P. 28)

In extension to this, tailoring sleep education to life stages was also discussed, where different stages of life such as starting a family, were felt to come with emerging challenges that may not be relevant at a younger age: “…I think [managing sleep]changes to…the different places you work or parts of your life, like what worked for me 10 years ago is not going to work for me now…I can’t just go to the gym. Can’t just leave the kids” (P. 9).

Information highlighting the consequences of sleep loss and fatigue was also considered a possibly valuable component of education. Participants described a range of impacts they had experienced related to sleep loss and fatigue as a shift worker. There was particular concern for individual health effects (“…the weight gain, the circadian rhythm disturbance, all your health conditions you get. And then obviously the reduced life expectancy…” P. 6), as well as work performance detriments and the “risks associated with working fatigued” (P. 24). Impacts to family and personal relationships were also highlighted frequently: “…shift work obviously affects family life dramatically” (P. 18). Touching again on a need to recognise the importance of sleep, participants felt that younger paramedics having a better awareness of the consequences of sleep loss and fatigue could play a role. This perspective coincided with the idea that: “…for something as critical as sleep, fatigue, work life balance, all those factors… it needs to be ugly. It needs to be up in your face” (P. 6).

Recognising the importance of sleep also went hand-in-hand with learning to prioritise sleep and have realistic expectations as a shift worker. Sleep prioritisation (which sometimes had social consequences) was seen to be necessary: “You’ve just got to be a bit selfish, and you’ve got to get home, and sleep is your only priority” (P. 21). Participants felt that a lack of preparedness for shift work among early-career paramedics often arose from limited awareness of “the sacrifices we have to make to make sure we have enough sleep” (P. 9). Recognising and validating the realities of shift work was considered an important part of the broader conversation:…shift work is an anomaly to the rest of society, you’re not a superhero for doing it, you’re just a normal human, and doing shift work for most people will make navigating life a little bit harder and that’s normal and that’s to be expected and that’s okay… (P. 17).

…shift work is an anomaly to the rest of society, you’re not a superhero for doing it, you’re just a normal human, and doing shift work for most people will make navigating life a little bit harder and that’s normal and that’s to be expected and that’s okay… (P. 17).

However, it was also felt that these conversations should be underpinned by a “recognition and acknowledgement of how hard shift work is” (P. 20). While prioritising sleep was at times framed as an act of selfishness, others felt that it was a practice of self-compassion and being “kind to yourself” (P. 13), which was considered an important skill for young paramedics to learn as a shift worker in a challenging occupation.

It is important to note that a few participants felt that education may not be the key to supporting sleep and fatigue in paramedics, though this was generally contextualised in terms of the influence carried by job demands and workload: “Like, yeah, we could educate people, but I think there’s a real limit to that. I think the real change is going to come in like shorter, like better rostering… that’s the only real solution I can see…” (P. 5). Therefore, acknowledging the uncontrollable aspects of broader systems constraints, were also felt to be an important consideration in educational content and it’s delivery:“…I think people would struggle to receive it if the challenges that are unique to ambulance weren’t acknowledged. people need to be seen before they can be receptive to information…” (P. 17).

Who should deliver sleep education and when?

When considering who should be involved in supporting and educating paramedics about sleep, fatigue and shift work, participants suggested a “team effort” (P. 29). A system-wide approach involving tertiary institutions, organisations, unions and governing bodies was felt to be the best approach to ensure support across the paramedic career-span, and to capture a wide audience (i.e., varied age, life, and career stages/pathways): “I think they all kind of have to take a bit of a unified approach because…depending on paramedic attitudes or engagement, they’ll engage with one and not another” (P. 15). In the first instance, universities were thought to be well-situated to provide an initial base of knowledge about basic sleep science and fatigue concepts, as well as broader health and well-being education. There were considerations regarding timing of this education, specifically, some felt that education delivered too early (i.e., in the first year of tertiary education), may not be impactful. One participant recalled thinking “What are you talking about? I’ve got loads of energy. I don’t need a sleep routine. I’m good. I’m young. I’m fit and I’m healthy” (P. 29) during education delivered early in their undergraduate degree. Many believed that education situated in the final year of tertiary education was the best approach:…as soon as you hit third year, all you can think about is a job, so they’ll actually take a lot of it in, and go okay, this is what I need to do to prepare, so when they come into their internship, hopefully they have some knowledge. (P. 30)

…as soon as you hit third year, all you can think about is a job, so they’ll actually take a lot of it in, and go okay, this is what I need to do to prepare, so when they come into their internship, hopefully they have some knowledge. (P. 30)

Organisations providing formal, meaningful education during the transition into shift work was considered the next step. Whilst this period was felt to be particularly important, participants also reported a need for ongoing education within organisations. This was a particularly important consideration for those who experienced having children as a shift worker:…it [education] has to be ongoing because as the nature of what we do and how we work changes, and as the nature of individual’s roles change, how you manage and how you deal with fatigue is going to be different. I mean even for me… how I managed fatigue pre-parenthood and post parenthood is like massively different. Like hugely different. So, …there needs to be a constant conversation about not only how should we, but how do we, manage. (P. 23)

…it [education] has to be ongoing because as the nature of what we do and how we work changes, and as the nature of individual’s roles change, how you manage and how you deal with fatigue is going to be different. I mean even for me… how I managed fatigue pre-parenthood and post parenthood is like massively different. Like hugely different. So, …there needs to be a constant conversation about not only how should we, but how do we, manage. (P. 23)

In addition to changing needs across the career-span, ongoing education was also thought to ensure paramedics have the most up-to-date knowledge, and to address the fact that paramedics may not recall early-career education or weren’t receptive to it at a younger age. A few participants highlighted that unions and governing bodies could also play a role by way of offering accessible information and resources to paramedics at all stages of their career: “…stuff that was put out by like our industrial associations…our union. [that] said, ‘hey look, in the interests of staff wellbeing, this is something we want to let you know about…Here’s the support that we can offer’” (P. 26).

Beyond institutions, two specific voices were considered an essential part of the educational process: subject matter experts and experienced paramedics. An appreciation for evidence-based knowledge delivered by experts was evident among participants and extended to who they thought best to deliver education: “…they’re the people that know the field the best” (P. 12). Of utmost importance, however, was paramedics themselves, with participants highly valuing peer-to-peer knowledge sharing: “you definitely need that lived experience, like the perspective of having done shift work because it needs to be relatable” (P. 29). This was conceptualised by most as having paramedics share “personal experiences” (P. 30) in an open forum type setting. The power of sharing knowledge and role-modelling, particularly for early-career paramedics, was exemplified by one participant:

I was a uni[versity] student, and I was constantly overcommitting to things and trying to be in 101 places at once, [I heard a colleague asked] if he could come in for this and he went, ‘No. That’s between my nights. It doesn’t happen.’ I just remember being like, ‘Oh. Wow. Is that not up for negotiation?’ and he said, ‘No. That is my time.’ That really resonated with me, and I went, ‘If this [senior clinical role], this very experienced paramedic is telling me that he, not can’t manage, but refuses to manage or try and squeeze in something between his nights, maybe that’s telling me something. (P. 28)

A desire for education to be delivered by experts and peers emphasised the need for trust when it came to conversations about individual health. While it was felt that organisations would be an essential part of the process, some felt that paramedics lacked trust in their organisations, and in some instances, organisational well-being teams and unions, thus, collaboration between all parties an ideal approach.

Sleep education approaches

Similar to the need for various timepoints and voices to capture a diverse audience, participants described a range of preferred education modalities. With reference to the who and when, participants spoke of a need for “in-built” (P. 2) education systems for sleep and fatigue. At the tertiary education stage, this was often conceptualised as a specified topic, or formal sessions dedicated to sleep and health more broadly as a shift worker. Similarly, within organisation, many thought that devoted time and sessions to sleep and fatigue are essential in the transition period (e.g. as part of an induction process). One participant considered this in light of significant work and study responsibilities for early-career paramedics during this time: “…a full day where… you don’t have to worry about the exam coming up and you can sit there for the whole day and go through it and not be distracted by other things. That’s the right time to do it” (P. 18).

There was, however, nuanced discussion surrounding in-built education beyond the transition period. While mandatory education was considered essential by many (“I don’t think people would go voluntarily” P. 8), the approach was thought to require careful consideration. Some held the view that mandatory online learning packages did not foster good learning outcomes (“people don’t pay any attention to it; they just quickly get it done” P. 24), or that, in the case of fatigue, they may not be well-intended: “Sometimes education packages can be seen as, you know, ‘we want you to tick this box so we can come and whack you over the head with it when you don’t do it right’. And if it’s seen as that, it wouldn’t go right” (P. 26). Conversely, voluntary education was also considered problematic for engagement, where paramedics may lack time or motivation: “‘I’ll get to it at another time.’ Or ‘yeah, I’ll do that at home.’ Or ‘I’ll do that when I’ll get some spare time on my shift’, which is never…” (P. 6). A possible solution to address these factors was education delivered “face-to-face on the company’s time” (P. 6). Many described ideal on-going organisational education as incorporating a formal session (often preferred as a full day), which could be incorporated into scheduled training days or a standalone organisation-led forum. An open-forum type session which could allow for inclusion of lived experience perspectives, and “honest conversation in a safe environment” (P.17) was considered important: “…one day a year event where you just talk about it and have a cup of coffee and a few muffins and discuss what’s working for people and what’s not…” (P. 22).

Preference for dedicated education sessions also reflected a desire for accessible information. Some current organisational approaches were thought to be difficult to access (“you have to really search for information” P. 4) or disseminated in inefficient ways: “emails they send out with the things that they do provide – if other people are like me generally that just gets deleted” (P. 18). However, having access to resources was still considered important: “…there should be a huge, whole page document which everyone could refer to, that’s got all these different bits in it, like recommendations from paramedics, and ways to better your sleep, and different numbers” (P. 30). Including families in education sessions was also felt to hold value:There needs to be a bit more of a holistic approach to understanding how shift work affects your life generally… previously a lot of the shift work education that I’ve had, a lot of responsibility falls to the learner or in this case the paramedic… having those resources where, you know, it’s not incumbent on me as the person who’s already a bit stressed and a bit concerned about, you know, how is this going to affect my relationships. To be able to provide that opportunity for family members as well who, maybe they’re not understanding, maybe they’re frustrated, to have somewhere for them to go to read about it and maybe a bit more detail and understanding about what that person is going through, I think is quite important. (P. 15)

There was an opinion from some that having an opportunity to experience shift work prior to employment is also a critical component of the learning process: “You can’t expect someone to finish a degree in an area where 50 per cent of their job is going to be nightshift and not have given them any access to actually working that roster or learning what that’s like” (P. 29). Participants reported that there is a “…lack of understanding about what it really is to do a night shift, because students don’t do night shifts anymore” (P. 28), therefore opportunities for placement involving shift work are important: “I think there should be – before they get a job is, if possible, to do at least a couple of weeks rotating roster – even a month – just to see what it’s like working shift work” (P. 16). Another suggested approach was simulated night shifts or learning activities: “…like IV access on a mannequin with beer goggles or something like, I think that would be fun to sort of understand like the level of fatigue that you’re facing” (P. 4). An interactive component to placement or simulated learning was also a consideration: “having guided practice sessions of night shift… you know, get into bed at roughly this time, do this, do that, like these are the recommendations, try them out, see how you feel, and then we’ll come in, we’ll do some work” (P. 15). An interactive approach was thought to be well-situated in the tertiary setting, but also during the transition, where learning strategies could be supported peer-to-peer by mentors.

Mentorship was perceived as a highly valued aspect of the transition into paramedic work broadly. Therefore, it was suggested that educating paramedics in mentorship roles on sleep and fatigue could hold value: “if you’ve got educated educators working with those interns, that’s when it becomes more of a mentor relationship…it becomes - yes a clinical one but also sometimes can be a personal mentor thing as well” (P. 28). Finally, the need for education to co-exist with systematic supports for paramedics experiencing sleep problems was also considered, which was conceptualised as clear and accessible referral pathways to clinical sleep support: “…you can also get referrals to… psychologists [within organisation] for the mental health side of things but there’s nothing in there about sleep and I think it’s much more prevalent and much wider impacting than people give it credit for” (P. 12). Discussion

The current study explored the perceived sleep health, sleep disorder, fatigue, and shift work education needs for paramedics based on their early career experiences. For paramedics, learning to sleep well as a shift worker occurred primarily through experiential learning and informal knowledge sharing, rather than through meaningful education or preparation. A key finding was that many had limited awareness of the distinction between a sleep problem and a diagnosable sleep disorder and were unclear on appropriate management pathways. Overall, paramedics largely felt unprepared for the realities of shift work, and reported a clear desire for in-built, evidence-based sleep and fatigue education, as well as systematic support throughout the career-span to improve sleep, and support sleep disorder management.

Early-career paramedics described learning to sleep well as a challenging, trial and error process, typically only occurring after commencing full time shift work as a novice paramedic. In this study, few paramedics recalled receiving any meaningful education regarding sleep or fatigue management as a shift worker, instead developing self-taught strategies over time. This experience has been observed in other shift-working healthcare professions, where adjustments to, or prioritisation of, sleep commonly occurs only after negative consequences are experienced [24]. The reactive learning process described by paramedics reflected an absence of systematic preparation, such as education, sleep health evaluation, or pre-employment exposure to shift work. While a level of iterative learning is to be expected, reliance on self-taught strategies alone may be problematic, and sleep and fatigue may remain unmanaged in the interim.

Limited understanding of how to achieve healthy sleep and manage fatigue may be particularly problematic for early-career paramedics, who are simultaneously learning to perform a challenging new role within a safety-critical work context [50]. In the absence of formal training or education, paramedics also relied on informal, peer-to-peer knowledge sharing, leveraging shared experiences. While this approach holds value, limited underlying sleep-related knowledge beyond learned healthy sleep practices, which was evident in this study, may inadvertently reinforce misinformation (particularly in differentiating sleep disruption and a possible sleep disorder) or maladaptive coping strategies [51]. Additionally, while sleep hygiene advice may provide benefit more broadly, this approach is considered ineffective in isolation when managing sleep disorders such as insomnia or shift work disorder [52]. Such informal processes, however, highlight an opportunity to address a modifiable gap in current structured learning opportunities within tertiary education and organisational systems. Despite the limitations to how paramedics described learning and continuing to manage their sleep, they also demonstrated resourcefulness and a strong value for collective knowledge sharing. Ensuring that this motivation is supported with accurate, evidence-based education may strengthen both individual and organisational approaches to sleep and fatigue management.

Key sleep and fatigue education considerations

Paramedics exhibited a strong desire for structured, credible, and contextually relevant sleep and fatigue education that integrates both theoretical knowledge and practical application. While there are very few existing studies which have explored this topic in paramedics specifically, our findings align with Booker et al. (2024) who reported that their sample of paramedics, nurses, and midwives highlighted a need for more education on how to better manage sleep and fatigue as a shiftworker [24]. Actionable healthy sleep practices were considered highly important in terms of educational content; however underpinning practical knowledge with an understanding of foundational sleep science was essential, aligning with recent consensus-based healthy sleep guidelines for shift workers [53]. However, participants in our study highlighted the importance paramedics place on education that is co-led by experts and paramedics themselves. This suggests that while broad or general advice may provide some benefit, there is also a need for field-specific input and delivery. Bringing together theoretical understanding of sleep and the lived experiences of learning to sleep well was thought to be important for engagement in education approaches. Engagement was further seen as contingent upon its relevance to both the broader work context, and alignment with career or life stages. Specifically, education that acknowledges the challenges of paramedic work (e.g., unpredictable schedules, involuntary overtime) was considered essential, ensuring content recognises how these uncontrollable occupational factors influence the ability to achieve adequate sleep. This perspective also recognises the concept of shared responsibility between workers and organisational structures (i.e. organisational requirements in addition to individual preparation) when it comes to managing sleep and fatigue [54].

Tailoring education to different life and career stages was also considered vital to maintaining relevancy and thus engagement. This highlights the importance of delivering the right message at the right time, ensuring that educational content aligns with current contexts and challenges for paramedics. For example, education in an academic setting may focus primarily on building base knowledge around healthy sleep practices and identifying sleep disorders which require treatment, whereas experienced paramedics may benefit more from education which centres how to manage sleep as a shift worker through different life stages (e.g., parenthood, aging). Embedding ongoing education into educational and work systems, which was a key preference for paramedics, is a practical approach to addressing paramedic sleep education needs. Further, in-built education delivered in-person is a meaningful way to also address low help-seeking behaviours commonly reported in shift working and paramedic populations [55, 56]. Increasing awareness of the health, performance, and safety consequences of poor sleep and fatigue may further enhance engagement with sleep education, echoing findings of prior research in similar occupations [27]. Paramedics report that early awareness of consequences may foster prioritisation of, and engagement with, healthy sleep practices. Given that paramedics in this study largely felt unprepared for shift work, this also highlights the importance of providing education prior to, or at the point of, transition into shift work, when awareness of fatigue-related risks is likely limited. Ultimately, considerations from the paramedic perspective point to the need for co-designed, contextually relevant, embedded educational approaches that evolve with the workforce and recognise sleep and fatigue management as integral to career sustainability.

Sleep disorders – a critical knowledge gap

An important and novel finding of the current study was the limited awareness and knowledge surrounding sleep disorders among paramedics. While paramedics in this study demonstrated good understanding of the principles of healthy sleep practices, many still reported consistently poor sleep and symptoms which can overlap with common sleep disorders. This is unsurprising, given the prevalence of both poor sleep and sleep disorders reported in paramedic populations to date [4, 8]. The ability to recognise when poor sleep may reflect an underlying sleep disorder, or to know when and how to seek professional assessment for a sleep problem, was also limited. This finding may reflect a broader challenge within shift-working populations. Reynolds et al. (2024) identified that among Australian shift workers with diagnosed sleep disorders, recognition and diagnosis were often delayed for several years, with workers (and even healthcare providers) attributing symptoms to the demands of shift work itself rather than to a treatable condition [57].

Misattribution of sleep disorder symptoms as solely related to shift schedules may normalise persistently poor sleep and fatigue, which is common among paramedics [37], and undermine timely help-seeking [58]. In the context of shift work and a safety-critical work environment, this may further compound the risk for adverse health and safety outcomes, including on the commute home [59]. These findings highlight an important knowledge gap: that while paramedics may know how to optimise sleep, they are insufficiently equipped with the appropriate knowledge to discern when poor sleep and fatigue signify a clinical problem requiring intervention. Addressing this gap necessitates sleep and fatigue education that includes targeted information on sleep disorder recognition and where and how to seek help; ideally as early as possible [60]. This also highlights a broader educational shortfall within undergraduate and early-career preparation, and the need to ensure paramedics enter the workforce with the clinical literacy required to recognise sleep disorders and navigate assessment and care. Beyond education alone, and consistent with paramedic preferences for systemic solutions, integrating clear and accessible pathways for sleep-related concerns alongside educational approaches is also essential. Given that the nature of shift work can create barriers for engagement with healthcare [57], this may be a feasible approach to improving paramedic sleep outcomes, and thus improved individual well-being and workplace safety. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of addressing sleep and fatigue not only as an individual responsibility, but as a systemic workforce issue central to long-term health, safety, and career sustainability in paramedicine.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the in-depth exploration of paramedic perspectives on sleep and fatigue education needs within a shift work context. By centring paramedic voices, the study provides occupation-specific insight into key knowledge gaps, and education preferences for meaningful engagement. This is an important step toward co-designed sleep education and support in a profession which is currently lacking meaningful education and workplace interventions for sleep and fatigue [26]. A further strength lies in the identification of a critical knowledge gap surrounding sleep disorder recognition and management. This has significant implications in a safety-critical workforce. This is particularly relevant in the current context, where the limited existing literature has focused on fatigue and sleep hygiene education, rather than sleep disorder literacy. The study also provides practical translational value. Through highlighting paramedic preferences for co-designed education which spans working life, the findings offer clear direction for the development of targeted interventions that align with the realities of paramedic work.

Several limitations should also be considered. Interestingly, our findings do not align with prior research reporting limited knowledge related to health sleep practices among paramedics [25]. This discrepancy may represent a selection bias in our study, where those who prioritise sleep may have been more inclined to participate. This limitation, however, was managed with purposive sampling. Second, the study relied on self-reported perceptions and experiences related to sleep disorders, and did not include objective sleep measures or validated sleep disorder screening tools. Some participants described symptoms consistent with sleep disorders, but we intentionally did not explore quantitative screening tools due to the focus on qualitative experiences. Further, while the inclusion of participants from ambulance services across multiple Australian jurisdictions strengthened the breadth of perspectives captured, the transferability of findings may be limited in settings where paramedic educational or work settings differ substantially. Finally, we defined 2–15 years as ‘early career’ for recruitment in our study. It is possible that some participants may not be considered ‘early career’ by some definitions. However, the sample who responded reported a range of 2–10 years of experience (with a median of 5.5 years), and most were aged in their late 20s and early 30s and broadly could be considered to be in early adulthood.

Future directions

Leveraging the current findings, future research could prioritise the development and evaluation of co-designed sleep and fatigue education interventions tailored specifically to paramedic work and education contexts. Co-designed education, and early access to sleep disorder treatment options [60], may enhance relevance and engagement, increasing the likelihood of meaningful improvements in sleep and fatigue outcomes, which have not been observed in prior research utilising other education approaches [29]. Importantly, this research could also consider mental health indicators, particularly given the known associations between poor sleep, sleep disorders and mental health in tertiary paramedic students [61], and in young adults working shifts more broadly [10]. Given the vulnerability of the transition into practice period described in this study, research should also examine the timing of intervention delivery, including pre-employment education, integration into university programs, and longitudinal education across career stages. Evaluating whether early sleep education influences long-term sleep behaviours and help seeking would be of particular value. It is important to note that education alone will be insufficient in managing paramedic sleep and fatigue, and should be delivered in conjunction with other systemic solutions such as organisational fatigue risk management systems. Another key system-level consideration for supporting paramedic sleep is pre-employment sleep disorder screening, which is common in other safety-critical industries, and ongoing organisation-facilitated access to sleep health services [57, 58].

Conclusion

Paramedics felt largely unprepared for the demands of shift work in terms of managing their sleep, reporting limited experiences of meaningful sleep and fatigue education and preparation. To reduce reliance on self- and peer-taught strategies, evidence-based and contextually relevant education is needed. Such education should be embedded within both tertiary education and organisational systems and co-led by experts and paramedics, bridging scientific knowledge and lived experience. Furthermore, knowledge surrounding sleep disorders among paramedics is limited, despite frequent reports of poor sleep and symptoms suggestive of a possible sleep disorder. Addressing this gap requires targeted education surrounding sleep disorders, which should co-exist alongside systemic support pathways to reduce barriers to help-seeking.
