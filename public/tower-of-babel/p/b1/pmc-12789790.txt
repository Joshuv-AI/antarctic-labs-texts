Left main coronary artery compression (LMCo) by a dilated pulmonary artery (PA) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), and may lead to angina, syncope, or myocardial infarction. The diagnosis is often challenging because the lesion is extrinsic and may not be apparent on standard coronary angiography. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) can provide critical insights into the underlying mechanism. Herein, we present a fatal case of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction caused by left main trunk (LMT) compression from a giant PA aneurysm, in which IVUS enabled definitive diagnosis and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) achieved only temporary hemodynamic stabilization.

History of Presentation

An octogenarian man with bronchial asthma and known pulmonary hypertension presented to the emergency department with dyspnea. He developed pulseless ventricular tachycardia and required defibrillation. Electrocardiogram revealed ST-segment elevations in leads I, aVL, and V4 to V6.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of bronchial asthma and PAH.

Investigations

Coronary angiography demonstrated 99% stenosis of the proximal LMT (Figure 1A). IVUS revealed a slit-like compressed lumen without plaque or thrombus, suggesting extrinsic compression (Figures 2A to 2D, Video 1). The IVUS frames (Figures 2A to 2D) correspond to pullback positions marked by yellow lines on the angiogram.

Subsequent computed tomography imaging revealed a 73-mm dilated PA abutting the LMT, with a PA-to-aorta (PA/Ao) ratio of 2.28, confirming LMCo (Figures 3A and 3B). Axial computed tomography imaging also demonstrated the maximal transverse diameter of the main PA (Figure 3C). The yellow arrow in Figure 3B indicates the stented segment of the LMT compressed by the aneurysm.

Computed Tomographic Images of PA Aneurysm Compressing the LMT

(A) Three-dimensional reconstruction showing a markedly dilated main PA (diameter: 73 mm) adjacent to the left main coronary artery. (B) Sagittal view revealing the spatial relationship between the PA aneurysm and the stented left main trunk. The yellow arrow indicates the location of the stent placed in the compressed segment of the LMT. (C) Axial view demonstrating the maximal diameter of the PA, measured at 73 mm (blue arrow), confirming the presence of a giant PA aneurysm. Ao = aorta; PA = pulmonary artery; LMT = left main trunk.

Management

A 3.5- × 15-mm drug-eluting stent (Resolute Onyx) was implanted, restoring TIMI flow grade 3 (Figure 1B, Figures 4A to 4D). Poststent IVUS confirmed full expansion and apposition without dissection (Video 2). The patient was managed with intra-aortic balloon pump and continuous hemodiafiltration for circulatory and renal support.

IVUS After Stent Implantation in the Left Main Trunk

The left panel shows poststenting coronary angiography with yellow lines (A to D) indicating IVUS pullback positions. A to D correspond to those positions and demonstrate a well-expanded, circular lumen with full stent apposition and no evidence of dissection or underexpansion. IVUS = intravascular ultrasound; PA = pulmonary artery.

Follow-Up and Outcomes

Despite initial stabilization, the patient's condition progressively worsened because of biventricular failure. Despite technically successful PCI and transient hemodynamic improvement, he died on hospital day 15. In addition to left ventricular dysfunction from acute myocardial infarction, preexisting severe pulmonary hypertension likely contributed to refractory right heart failure, ultimately leading to in-hospital death.

Discussion

LMCo because of a dilated PA or aneurysm is a rare but potentially fatal complication, most commonly observed in patients with PAH.1 Diagnosis can be challenging with angiography alone because the external compressive nature of the lesion may not be fully appreciated. IVUS plays a critical role in identifying the extrinsic nature of such stenosis, as demonstrated in our case where IVUS revealed a slit-like narrowing of the LMT without calcification.1,2

IVUS has proven useful in previous reports of LMCo. Seabra et al2 described a PAH patient with dynamic compression of the LMT, successfully diagnosed and treated with IVUS-guided stenting. Similarly, Saia et al1 showed that IVUS can effectively delineate extrinsic compression and guide intervention; however, long-term outcomes remain suboptimal because of persistent pulmonary pathology.

A meta-analysis by Badea et al3 found that LMCo may occur in up to 6% of PAH patients, with key predictive factors being a PA diameter ≥40 mm PA/Ao ratio ≥1.21. In our patient, the PA measured 73 mm with a PA/Ao ratio of 2.28, meeting these criteria (Figure 3C). This markedly exceeds the predictive threshold, further supporting the diagnosis of LMCo. Lee et al4 also showed that such anatomic findings are associated with an increased risk of sudden cardiac death in PAH.

Although stenting of the LMT in LMCo may improve symptoms in the short term, it is largely a palliative measure. Underlying PAH, right heart strain, and progressive vascular remodeling often remain unaddressed. In our case, despite achieving TIMI flow grade 3 with stenting and initial stabilization, the patient died on hospital day 15 from progressive heart failure. This reflects the limitation of PCI alone in treating LMCo.

Finally, LMCo may contribute to the risk of sudden cardiac death in PAH patients because fatal arrhythmias are common in this population.5 This underscores the importance of multimodal imaging, particularly IVUS, for diagnosis, and a comprehensive multidisciplinary strategy that addresses both coronary and pulmonary pathology for long-term management.
