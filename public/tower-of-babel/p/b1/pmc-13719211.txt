An Effective Case of Radiofrequency Ablation with the Combination of Durvalumab and Tremelimumab Treatment for Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Koji Rinka, Kiyohide Kioka, Rika Shioji, Maiko Adachi, Takayuki Kashima, Tetsuya Ikeda, Tsubasa Yamaguchi, Nanako Fukui, Yuhei Sakata, Tadashi Ochiai, Hiroaki Okuda, Yuga Amano, Naoto Hirata, Satoshi Sugimori, Tomoaki Yamasaki, Takashi Nakai, Yuta Akahane, Naomi Ishii, Takeshi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved the survival of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, the therapeutic efficacy is influenced by the tumor immune phenotype. We report the case of a 77-year-old man with advanced HCC who was treated with durvalumab plus tremelimumab. Although radiologic imaging showed tumor shrinkage and normalized serum tumor marker levels, contrast-enhanced ultrasound identified residual hypervascular lesions. A biopsy revealed scarce intratumoral lymphocytes, suggesting a cold tumor phenotype. Sequential radiofrequency ablation (RFA) was performed, resulting in a complete response according to the modified RECIST. This case highlights that cold tumors may persist despite an apparent regression, and suggests that combining RFA with ICIs may enhance antitumor immunity and improve treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Systemic therapy for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has markedly advanced with the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The combination of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Ate/Bev) and durvalumab plus tremelimumab (Dur/Tre) have demonstrated survival benefits and are now established as first-line therapies for advanced-stage HCC (1,2). However, not all patients derive comparable benefits, as therapeutic efficacy is strongly influenced by the tumor immune microenvironment. Tumors are broadly classified into three major immunophenotypes: immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, and immune-desert (3). Immune-inflamed, or “hot,” tumors are characterized by dense CD8+ T-cell infiltration and typically respond favorably to ICIs (3-9). In contrast, immune-excluded and immune-desert tumors, collectively termed “cold tumors,” frequently exhibit resistance to immunotherapy (3-10).

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has been shown to augment tumor-specific immune responses by releasing tumor-associated antigens and promoting immune cell infiltration. Accordingly, combining RFA with ICIs has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance the antitumor efficacy (11-13). In this report, we present a case of HCC that was successfully managed with sequential Dur/Tre immunotherapy followed by RFA. This case underscores the clinical relevance of immune phenotyping and highlights the potential synergy between ICIs and locoregional ablation.

Case Report

A 77-year-old man was referred to the gastroenterology department after the detection of multiple hepatic lesions on abdominal ultrasonography. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, primary aldosteronism, and effort angina pectoris for which he had previously undergone percutaneous coronary intervention. He only drank socially. He reported an unintended weight loss of 5 kg over two months, anorexia, and elevated liver enzyme levels. He had undergone a liver biopsy for hepatic dysfunction approximately six years earlier. The pathological findings at that time led to a diagnosis of metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis. The NAS score was 3 (steatosis, 2; lobular inflammation, 1; and hepatocyte ballooning, 1). In addition, antinuclear antibodies were negative and both IgM and IgG levels were normal. The patient also had a documented allergy to iodinated contrast agents and initially declined contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Non-contrast MRI revealed a large hepatic mass measuring 105 mm in maximal diameter, with mildly increased signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences (Fig. 1a, b, d, e) and restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (Fig. 1c, f). Laboratory evaluations showed elevated α-fetoprotein (75.8 ng/mL) and des-γ-carboxy prothrombin (21,398 mAU/mL) (Table). A diagnosis of HCC was established and classified as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B. The patient was deemed unsuitable for transarterial chemoembolization due to the tumor burden (14). Given his preserved liver function (Child-Pugh A) and adequate hematologic and organ functions, systemic therapy with Dur/Tre was initiated. After two treatment cycles, he developed grade 1 hepatotoxicity (aspartate aminotransferase, 158 U/L; alanine aminotransferase (ALT), 146 U/L), but continued therapy without interruption and received supportive treatment with Stronger Neo-Minophagen C. Liver function gradually improved. By the fourth cycle of Dur/Tre therapy, non-contrast MRI demonstrated tumor shrinkage (Fig. 3), and serum tumor marker levels had normalized. Due to ongoing concerns regarding contrast media, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) with Sonazoid was performed, revealing hypervascularity in a 40 mm lesion in segment 5 (Fig. 4). RFA and a biopsy were performed for this lesion after the fifth cycle of Dur/Tre therapy. The liver tumor biopsy was performed by targeting the central area that showed early phase hyperenhancement on CEUS with Sonazoid (Fig. 4b). The purpose of the biopsy was to determine whether the lesion represented viable HCC and to obtain tissue that could potentially be used for future genetic panel testing. Biopsy specimens were obtained from the tumor in segment 5 and surrounding liver tissue to confirm the pathological status of the tumor (Fig. 5).

After the eleventh cycle of Dur/Tre therapy, the patient consented to undergo gadolinium-ethoxybenzyl-diethylenetriamine-pentaacetic acid MRI (EOB-MRI), which revealed a suspicious lesion in segment 4 (Fig. 6a-c). Subsequent CEUS confirmed arterial hyperenhancement (Fig. 7b), and a second RFA was performed on the lesion in segment 4, because it was difficult to exclude HCC. Following this procedure, no early phase hyperenhancement was observed on EOB-MRI and a complete response was achieved according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria.

Discussion

The IMbrave150 trial established the combination of Ate/Bev, which targets programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and vascular endothelial growth factor respectively, over sorafenib for unresectable HCC, demonstrating a median overall survival (OS) of 19.2 months (1). Similarly, the HIMALAYA trial confirmed the efficacy of the Dur/Tre combination, which targets PD-L1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4), with a median OS of 16.4 months, an overall response rate of 20.1%, and a disease control rate of 60.1% according to RECIST 1.1 (2). On this basis, both regimens are now recommended as first-line therapies for advanced HCC in the updated BCLC guidelines (15). Mechanistically, CTLA-4 blockade enhances antigen-specific T-cell priming and activation while also reducing regulatory T-cell activity within the tumor microenvironment, whereas the inhibition of PD-L1/PD-1 restores effector T-cell cytotoxicity against cancer cells (16).

Despite these advances, the clinical responses to ICIs remain heterogeneous and are strongly influenced by the tumor immune microenvironment. Tumors can be broadly categorized into three major immunophenotypes: immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, and immune-desert (3). Immune-inflamed or “hot,” tumors are characterized by dense CD8+ T-cell infiltration within the tumor mass or at the invasive margin (3-9). These tumors often display high levels of T-cell infiltration, increased PD-L1 expression, and elevated tumor mutational burden, features that render them more responsive to ICIs (3-9).

In contrast, immune-excluded tumors exhibit T cell accumulation restricted to the tumor margins without penetration into the parenchyma, a pattern thought to arise from chemokine dysregulation, vascular barriers, or stromal-mediated suppression (3). Immune desert tumors lack T-cell infiltration in both the tumor and stromal compartments, often due to inadequate antigen presentation, impaired T-cell priming, or absence of recruitment signals. These “cold” tumors, comprising both immune-excluded and immune-desert phenotypes, pose a substantial therapeutic challenge because they fail to mount or sustain adaptive immune responses and rarely respond to ICI monotherapy (3-10).

In this case, a biopsy performed at the time of RFA for a residual HCC lesion revealed striking discordance between the tumor and peritumoral tissue. While CD8+ T-cell infiltration and CD68+ macrophages were evident in the adjacent non-tumorous liver, lymphocytes and macrophages were largely absent within the tumor parenchyma (Fig. 5), consistent with an immune-excluded phenotype. Recent evidence indicates that dynamic vascular events within the tumor immune microenvironment also influence anti-tumor immunity and treatment responses. The infiltration of CD8+ T cells and CD68+ macrophages suggests an inflamed immune phenotype. Additional markers, including PD-L1 expression and FoxP3+ regulatory T cells, are required to characterize the tumor immune phenotype further (17). However, we did not investigate PD-L1 expression and FoxP3+ regulatory T cells in our patients because PD-L1 and FoxP3+ immunostaining data were not available. Further studies of this potential marker are required. This observation underscores that “cold” tumors may persist even when radiologic regression and normalization of tumor markers suggest an overall treatment response.

The combination of local ablation and systemic immunotherapy is biologically rational, as ablation can enhance antitumor immunity. In HCC, RFA induces coagulative necrosis, releasing tumor debris that functions as both an antigen source and immune stimulant. These antigens are subsequently processed by antigen-presenting cells, facilitating dendritic cell maturation, monocyte activation, and the initiation of tumor-specific T-cell responses (18,19). Therefore, RFA not only provides local tumor control, but it also contributes to systemic immune priming.

Although RFA alone has been shown to augment tumor-associated antigen-specific T cell responses and improve the recurrence-free survival, emerging evidence suggests that combining RFA with ICIs may exert synergistic effects, leading to superior tumor control and potentially enhanced survival in patients with HCC (11-13). In the present case, sequential RFA following Dur/Tre immunotherapy resulted in continued tumor shrinkage and the absence of enhancement on EOB-MRI, culminating in a complete response. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that the integration of RFA with ICIs can amplify the antitumor immune responses and may achieve durable remission in selected patients.

Conclusion

This case underscores the heterogeneous responses to ICIs between immune-hot and immune-cold HCCs to ICIs. Tumor biopsy, particularly when performed in conjunction with local therapies, such as RFA, provides valuable insights into the tumor immune microenvironment. Our findings highlight that cold tumors may persist despite apparent radiologic regression and normalization of serological markers, emphasizing the importance of histologic evaluation. The integration of RFA with ICIs has the potential to augment antitumor immune responses and improve clinical outcomes. In this patient, sequential RFA following Dur/Tre therapy was instrumental in achieving a complete response.

Limitations

The present study was limited by its single-patient design, which restricts its generalizability. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate the therapeutic synergy between RFA and ICIs and to further delineate the optimal treatment strategies for cold tumor phenotypes.
