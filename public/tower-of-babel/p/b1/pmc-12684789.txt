Trends in Bicuspid Aortic Valve–Attributable Death in the United States From 2010 to 2020
Authors: Marco Zuin, Umberto Campia, Kim I De la Cruz, Michael Singh, Gregory Piazza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation‐based data on bicuspid aortic valve (BAV)‐attributable mortality trends in the United States are limited. This study assessed trends in BAV‐attributable death in the United States from 2010 to 2020, evaluating differences by sex, ethnicity, urbanization, and census region.MethodsData were extracted from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Wide‐Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research system. Trends in age‐adjusted mortality rates per 100 000 population were analyzed using JoinPoint regression to calculate average annual percentage change and annual percentage change, with corresponding 95% CIs.ResultsFrom 2010 and 2020, 3537 deaths were attributable to BAV, corresponding to 11.7 deaths per 100 000 population. Age‐adjusted mortality rates increased linearly, with an average annual percentage change of +4.6% (95% CI, 2.8–6.5; P<0.001). Women experienced a steeper increase (average annual percentage change, +8.0% [95% CI, 3.3–12.9]; P<0.001) compared with men (average annual percentage change, +5.3% [95% CI, 3.6–7.0]; P<0.001). Mortality trends were more pronounced among Hispanic/Latinx individuals and those aged <65 years. No significant differences between urban and rural settings were observed (P=0.35), while the South accounted for the highest proportion of deaths (31.1%).ConclusionsBAV‐attributable mortality has increased over the past decade, with significant disparities by sex, age, and region. These results highlight the critical importance of developing targeted health care approaches and enhancing strategic management techniques to address specific population BAV‐related health concerns.Keywords: bicuspid aortic valve, death, trend, valvular heart diseaseSubject Categories: Mortality/Survival

Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is the most common congenital cardiac anomaly, affecting ≈1 in 50 to 1 in 200 individuals. Current estimates suggest that BAV occurs in 0.5% to 2% of the global population. 1 , 2 However, these data are probably underestimates due to limitations such as small sample sizes and selection bias in previous studies. 3 , 4 , 5 Additionally, most individuals with BAV remain asymptomatic and unrecognized, further complicating prevalence estimates. 6

Emerging evidence suggests distinct BAV phenotypes, influenced by cusp morphologies and the type of associated aortopathy. 2 These phenotypes have been linked to varying rates of disease progression and risks of complications. 2 Despite the potential for major cardiovascular complications such as aortic stenosis, aortic regurgitation, aortopathy, and acute aortic syndromes, medical management has remained limited to blood pressure control and general cardiovascular risk reduction. 2 , 7 Furthermore, guidelines recommend echocardiographic screening only in cases of aortic dilation, leaving many patients with undiagnosed aortic and aortic valvular disease. 8 , 9 Moreover, the current trends in BAV‐attributable death in the United States are lacking and warrant further investigation, particularly to evaluate the impact of contemporary management strategies on these patients and to inform preventive policies and public health initiatives. To provide contemporary trends in death related to this condition, we analyzed patterns in deaths associated with BAV across the United States from 2010 to 2020. This investigation specifically examined variations based on sex, racial and ethnic backgrounds, urban versus rural settings, and geographic regions.

Methods

Data Source and Definitions

Data were retrieved from the publicly available Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide‐Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research data set, which is regularly updated to provide information from death certificates of all US residents according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10). This database includes demographic and geographic data, such as age, sex, race, Hispanic/Latinx ethnicity, urbanization category, and census region. BAV‐attributable deaths, occurring between January 2010 to December 2020 in the United States, were ascertained when the ICD‐10 codes for BAV Q23.0 and Q23.1 were listed in the first position of the death certificate (defined as the disease or event that started the chain of events that led to death).

The study period spanned January 2010 to December 2020, aligning with standardized treatment guidelines and availability of mortality data. While ICD‐10 codes became widely used for clinical care after 2015, their application to death certificates began in 1999. 10 , 11 , 12 A subanalysis on the most common underlying causes of death, which included the most common complications associated with BAV and including aortic regurgitation, aortic stenosis, aortic dissection, endocarditis, and ischemic heart disease, 11 was also performed. The study did not require institutional review board approval as the analysis was based on deidentified and publicly available data.

Population Estimates

Two independent investigators (M.Z. and C.B.) conducted separate data extraction and validation processes. Population denominators for stratification variables were derived from the US Census Bureau data, 13 while the investigation was performed in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. 14 Deaths were classified as premature if occurring before age 65 years. In this study, ethnic classification was divided into 2 groups: those of Hispanic/Latinx origin, and those who were not. Racial categories were defined as follows: White, Black, American Indian or Alaskan Native, and individuals of Asian or Pacific Islander descent. Urban and rural classifications followed the 2013 National Center for Health Statistics scheme.

Statistical Analysis

Age‐adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100 000 population with the relative 95% CI were calculated by standardizing the related deaths using the annual national population totals from the US Census Bureau and the 2000 US standard population. 13 To report nationwide annual trends in BAV‐attributable death, we assessed the annual percentage change, the average annual percentage change (AAPC), and the relative 95% CIs. JoinPoint regression analysis was used to identify significant changes, or “joinpoints” across the study period. This statistical method was chosen since it offers several advantages compared with traditional linear trend analysis including the identification of acceleration or deceleration in the trend and the estimation of the magnitude and direction of trend changes. 15 A parallelism test (pairwise comparison) was used to compare different sets of trends with mean functions estimated by JoinPoint regression analysis. 16 Specifically, a significant P value on this interaction test indicated that the 2 trends, in terms of AAPC, were statistically significantly different from each other. Statistical analyses (M.Z.) were performed using JoinPoint regression (JoinPoint version 4.6.0.0; National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD). Statistical significance was prespecified at P<0.05 for findings in the entire population.

Results

Overall Trends

From 2010 to 2020, a total of 30 098 736 individuals (15 310 330 men and 14 788 406 women) died in the United States. BAV‐attributable death rates increased by 78.6% from 244 deaths in 2010 to 436 deaths in 2020, equating to 11.7 deaths per 100 000 population over the study period. The AAMR for BAV‐attributable death increased from 0.06 (95% CI, 0.05–0.07) per 100 000 in 2010 to 0.11 (95% CI, 0.10–0.13) per 100 000 in 2020 (AAPC, +4.6% [95% CI, 2.8–6.5]; P<0.001), without any inflection point (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Sex Differences

Throughout the entire study period, men exhibited higher BAV‐attributable mortality rates, while women demonstrated a greater increase in the AAMR (P for parallelism=0.02). In men, the AAMR increased from 0.11 (95% CI, 0.09–0.13) per 100 000 population in 2010 to 0.18 (95% CI, 0.16–0.20) per 100 000 population in 2020, with an AAPC of +5.3% (95% CI, 3.6–7.0; P<0.001). Similarly, in women, the AAMR increased from 0.03 (95% CI, 0.02–0.04) per 100 000 in 2010 to 0.05 (95% CI, 0.04–0.07) per 100 000 in 2020 (AAPC, +8.0% [95% CI, 3.3–12.9]; P<0.001; Table 1 and Figure 2A).

Racial and Ethnic Disparities

In White individuals, the AAMR for BAV‐attributable death increased from 0.09 (95% CI, 0.07–0.10) per 100 000 population in 2010 to 0.13 (95% CI, 0.12–0.15) per 100 000 population in 2020 (AAPC, +5.3% [95% CI, 3.0–7.7]; P=0.001). In Hispanic/Latinx individuals, the AAMR rose from 0.02 (95% CI, 0.01–0.04) per 100 000 population in 2010 to 0.08 (95% CI, 0.05–0.11) per 100 000 population in 2020 (AAPC, +7.5% [95% CI, 2.4–12.9]; P=0.008). Data discontinuity prevented the calculation of relative trends for Black Americans, Asian/Pacific Islanders, and Alaska Native/American Indians, although estimates for these demographic groups were included in the overall analysis and stratified by sex, urban density, and census regions (Table 1 and Figure 2B).

Age Trends

The BAV‐attributable mortality rate exhibited a bimodal distribution, with higher mortality rates observed in patients aged <1 year and those aged 55–64 years (Figure S1). From 2010 to 2020, a total of 2766 individuals aged from birth to <65 years (including 2135 men and 631 women) died due to BAV. This group demonstrated a greater increase in BAV‐attributable deaths compared with patients aged ≥65 years throughout the study period (P for parallelism=0.01). Among patients aged <65 years, BAV‐attributable death rose from 0.06 (95% CI, 0.05–0.07) per 100 000 in 2010 to 0.11 (95% CI, 0.10–0.12) per 100 000 in 2020 (AAPC, +6.7% [95% CI, 4.2–9.3]; P<0.001), showing no inflection points. In contrast, among subjects aged ≥65 years (771 subjects, including 276 men and 495 women), the AAMR increased from 0.12 (95% CI, 0.08–0.13) in 2010 to 0.16 (95% CI, 0.13–0.20) in 2020 (AAPC, +3.2% [95% CI, −0.4 to 7.0]; P=0.08; Table 1). Specifically, the AAMR increased linearly from 2010 to 2015 (annual percentage change, +7.6% [95% CI, 0.9–14.7]; P=0.03) and then plateaued from 2015 to 2020 (annual percentage change, −1.0% [95% CI, −7.1 to 5.5]; P=0.71; Table 1 and Figure 2C).

Geographic Patterns

Over the study period, BAV‐attributable death increased in both urban (AAPC, +5.4% [95% CI, 3.7–7.0];, P<0.001) and rural (AAPC, +5.9% [95% CI, 2.0–9.9]; P=0.007) areas without significant differences (P for parallelism=0.35) (Table 2 and Figure 2D). A greater increase in the AAMR was observed in the South (AAPC, +6.0% [95% CI, 3.1–9.0]; P=0.001), which also had a higher percentage of BAV‐attributable deaths (31.1%) compared with the rest of the country (Table S1 and Figure S2).

Underlying Cause of Death

An analysis of the underlying causes of death among individuals with BAV revealed that aortic regurgitation was the most listed cause (n=1246 [35.2%]), followed by aortic stenosis (n=525 [14.8%]), aortic dissection (n=254 [7.1%]), ischemic heart disease (n=208 [5.8%]), and endocarditis (n=93 [2.6%]). Subgroup analysis highlighted age‐related differences: aortic dissection was more frequently identified as the underlying cause of death in individuals aged <64 years compared with those aged ≥65 years (7.4% versus 2.9%, P<0.001). Conversely, ischemic heart disease was more commonly registered as the underlying cause of death in older individuals compared with their younger counterparts (13.3% versus 5.0%, P<0.001; Table 3).

Discussion

This study highlights a rise in BAV‐attributable death in the United States from 2010 to 2020, with significant sex, age, and regional disparities. While men demonstrated higher overall BAV‐attributed death, women have shown a greater increase over time. Counterintuitively, individuals aged <65 years were observed to have a greater increase in BAV‐attributable death than those who were at least 65 years. The South led the United States, accounting for almost one‐third of all BAV‐attributable deaths. Aortic dissection was the most frequent cause of death in those aged <64 years compared with ischemic heart disease in those aged ≥65 years.

While an observed mortality rate of 11.7 BAV‐attributable deaths per 100 000 may appear modest in isolation, it represents a significant burden when compared with the mortality rate of other structural heart diseases, such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (estimated from 0.2 to 5.6 deaths per 100 000 population), 17 or in aortic stenosis (≈3.0–8.0 per 100.000 population) 18 or even moderate/severe mitral valve disease (≈1.0–3.6 per 100 000 population per year). 19 This estimate reflects the substantial burden of BAV‐related death when considered within the broader epidemiological context, including both recognized and unrecognized cases of BAV. 5 Understanding such nuances of these mortality rates is crucial for evaluating the effectiveness of both prevention and treatment strategies. Advances in surgical techniques, such as improvements in aortic valve replacement procedures and better perioperative care, may have contributed to improved outcomes for some patients with BAV. 20 Increased public and clinician awareness, guideline recommendations for screening, and earlier diagnosis of BAV, supported by advancements in imaging and screening techniques, have likely allowed for more timely interventions, thereby improving survival rates for certain subsets of patients. 21

In the present analysis, we used ICD‐10 codes Q23.0 and Q23.1 to identify deaths related to BAV, consistent with established methodologies and prior validation studies. 22 While this approach allows for standardized case identification, inherent limitations of relying solely on these codes for epidemiological assessments must be considered. Indeed, BAV may present clinically through downstream complications such as aortic dissection or infective endocarditis, conditions that are often coded independently from the underlying valvular anomaly. 23 Moreover, BAV can also occur as part of broader monogenic syndromes, which may be assigned different ICD‐10 codes based on their primary clinical features. 24 All these factors may have contributed to the potential under‐ascertainment of BAV‐related death. Moreover, the classification of congenital cardiovascular diseases associated with BAV may be variably reported or omitted altogether due to local documentation practices, limitations in diagnostic confirmation at the time of death, or clinician coding preferences, resulting in a potential underestimation of the true burden of BAV‐related death. 25 Additionally, changes in diagnostic awareness, imaging practices, and coding behaviors over the study period could contribute to apparent increases in the BAV‐related mortality rate that reflect improved detection and attribution, rather than a genuine rise in disease burden. The observed trends should therefore be interpreted with caution, recognizing the possibility that shifts in clinical and administrative practices may partly account for temporal variations in mortality rates.

BAV disease contributes to a progressive valvulopathy–aortopathy characterized by a wide range of anatomic phenotypes and associated disorders, with an associated spectrum of prognoses. 23 , 26 Although previous studies have estimated the prevalence and mortality rate associated with BAV according to sex and age, these investigations have generally relied on highly selected populations and limited follow‐up periods. 27 , 28 In such scenarios, the absence of trend mortality data has hampered the ability to inform clinical decision‐making. For example, over the past decade, major advances including transcatheter aortic valve replacement and thoracic aortic endovascular repair have permeated clinical practice with an unclear impact on mortality estimates, especially among specific populations such as those with BAV.

Based on our analysis, we observed a higher overall BAV‐attributable mortality rate among men but a greater increase over time in women. The first observation may be explained by the commonly noted finding of male predominance for BAV. 28 The second observation may be more difficult to explain but may have roots in sex‐related differences in complications of BAV, specifically, aortic dissection. Moreover, aortic dissection, a severe complication of BAV, is more frequently missed in women, potentially leading to a higher related mortality rate. 29 In particular, type A dissections may contribute to an increased mortality rate in women, potentially due to delayed diagnosis or differences in disease presentation. 30 , 31 Additionally, pregnancy‐related complications of aortopathy pose a unique risk for women, as the physiological changes during pregnancy can exacerbate aortic disease. 32

The higher BAV‐attributable mortality rate observed in our investigation in US subjects aged <65 years can be attributed to several interrelated factors. First, over the past decade, there was a growing recognition of the long‐term complications linked to BAV, such as aortic stenosis and regurgitation as well as aortic dissection, which may manifest earlier in life than previously appreciated. 26 , 33 Additionally, lifestyle factors, including rising rates of obesity and arterial hypertension among younger populations, can exacerbate the progression of this valvular heart disease. 34 , 35 Furthermore, advancements in medical technology and imaging have led to better diagnosis and awareness of BAV‐related conditions, resulting in more individuals being identified at risk or ultimately diagnosed with the disorder. 36 We cannot also exclude that this heightened awareness may have contributed to an apparent increase in mortality rates in this age group because of an increasing coding of the disease.

The observation of a bimodal age distribution in BAV‐attributable death provides important insights into the disease's progression and highlights distinct clinical challenges. The first peak in infancy is likely driven by severe congenital manifestations of BAV, associated disorders like coarctation of the aorta, or perioperative death associated with surgical interventions, reflecting the immediate risks faced by these patients. 37 These peaks underscore the importance for early diagnosis and prompt management of BAV‐related complications in neonates and young children. In contrast, the second mortality peak, observed in later adulthood, is more likely attributable to progressive valvular dysfunction, endocarditis, and emergent aortic complications such as aortic dissection or aneurysm rupture. 38 This bimodal distribution suggests that surveillance practices should be tailored to the unique risks at different stages of life. For infants and young children, intensive monitoring during the early years, particularly in the perioperative period, may be crucial. Conversely, adult patients, especially those with established valvular disease or aortic dilation, would benefit from longitudinal surveillance to detect early signs of aortic complications. 39 Incorporating this understanding into clinical guidelines could help optimize monitoring strategies and potentially reduce the mortality burden associated with BAV by enabling timely intervention in both high‐risk age groups.

Our results showed a pronounced increase in BAV‐attributable death among Hispanic/Latinx individuals. The underlying reasons are probably multifactorial, including differences in health care access, socioeconomic status, insurance coverage, and health literacy, and may significantly influence outcomes among Hispanic/Latinx populations. 40 Furthermore, structural and cultural barriers, including language differences and immigration‐related challenges, have also been shown to affect cardiovascular disease management and outcomes in minority groups. 41 Additionally, potential genetic or familial predispositions specific to certain ethnic groups remain an area of active investigation in congenital heart disease, including BAV. 42

Although JoinPoint regression is a well‐established and powerful tool for detecting changes in mortality trends, its performance can be significantly affected by sample size, especially in subgroup analyses. In this regard, the subanalysis based on Hispanic/Latinx individuals involved relatively small absolute numbers of BAV‐related deaths, which may have limited the statistical power to reliably detect true changes in mortality trends. 43 Consequently, some real shifts may have gone undetected, while others could reflect random variation rather than true epidemiological patterns. Therefore, the observed increase in BAV‐attributable death among Hispanic/Latinx individuals should be interpreted as hypothesis generating. Further dedicated analyses, ideally using larger or pooled data sets, are needed to better characterize BAV‐related mortality trends in this population and to confirm whether the observed patterns reflect true disparities.

Previous US reports have described a higher congenital heart disease mortality rate in rural versus urban areas during the past 2 decades. 44 In contrast, we showed a similar increase in BAV‐attributable death in both rural and urban settings. Socioeconomic factors such as poverty, unemployment, and limited educational opportunities, often equally prevalent in rural and urban settings, may exert significant influence on health outcomes. 45 In such cases, focusing only on a rural–urban dichotomy may not accurately identify subpopulations at greater risk for BAV‐related death. 46 In the rare disease literature, a variety of barriers of care are present in both rural and urban settings such that care of these disorders is similarly challenging. 47 Analyses focused on other social determinants of health may provide additional insights to guide public health efforts.

Urban areas generally benefit from more advanced health care infrastructure, which often leads to earlier diagnoses, more frequent screening, and potentially more aggressive management of BAV. This can result in lower observed mortality rates compared with rural areas, where access to specialized care may be limited. By linking public health records from diverse geographic regions, it becomes possible to account for such disparities and provide a more accurate and equitable picture of BAV‐related death. Furthermore, linking these records can help mitigate biases in care‐seeking behavior, particularly in rural populations that may face challenges in accessing or receiving timely interventions. 44 This approach would not only improve the reliability of mortality trend estimates but also enhance the generalizability of findings and offer valuable insights for addressing health care access disparities in the management of BAV. Future studies of less common congenital disorders, including congenital lesions like BAV, should consider public health records linkage to overcome some of the limitations of other larger data sets related to site of care access and geography.

We observed that the higher percentages and absolute numbers of BAV‐attributable death were clustered in the South. Differences in these absolute mortality rates might reflect the already described poorer cardiovascular health in this census region as well as a higher prevalence of specific cardiovascular risk factors associated with BAV progression and complications, such as obesity, atherosclerosis, and arterial hypertension. 46 Finally, valve complications such as aortic regurgitation and stenosis as well as acute aortic syndromes remain major underlying causes of death in individuals with BAV, as previously reported by different analyses. 27 The prompt recognition and treatment of these complications, in particular aortic dissection and rupture, often optimally require timely access to large referral centers with multidisciplinary team approaches. 48 Geographic constraints and decreased availability of cardiovascular medicine specialists, cardiothoracic surgeons, and vascular surgeons likely present significant challenges to many regions in the South. 49

Limitations

The present findings should be interpreted within the context of the study design. As our analysis relied upon a comprehensive nationwide administrative data set, the accuracy of our results is contingent upon the quality and extent of the data collection. The lack of individual‐level clinical data limits insights into comorbidities and treatment strategies. While estimates for Black Americans, Asian/Pacific Islanders, and Alaska Native/American Indians were included in the overall analysis and stratified by sex, urban density, and census regions, we were unable to calculate relative trends for these demographic groups due to data discontinuity. This limitation restricts our ability to provide comprehensive insights into potential disparities and trends within these populations, highlighting the need for more robust and continuous data collection to address these gaps in future research. Since the data were derived from vital statistics and census information extrapolated from death certificates, we cannot rule out the possibility of misclassification or miscoding, which may have partially biased our results and the associated trends. Moreover, the use of ICD‐10 coding and data derived from an administrative data set to attribute deaths to BAV‐related complications may have not fully captured the complexity of clinical circumstances, especially in cases with multiple comorbidities, potentially leading to misclassification and over‐ or underestimation of BAV‐attributable death. However, to address this limitation, we also evaluated the occurrence of complications of BAV, which are generally coded with high accuracy. 50 Additionally, many patients with BAV remain asymptomatic throughout their lives, and a portion of these individuals may never receive a diagnosis. Furthermore, because the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide‐Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research data set focuses solely on mortality rates, it does not include information regarding potential competing causes of death, previous cardiovascular risk factors and events, severity of the aortic disease, comorbidities, or surgical treatments, further limiting our analysis. Nevertheless, the diagnostic criteria used in our analysis align with methodologies previously used in other studies evaluating BAV‐related mortality rates. In this regard, prior analyses conducted in the United States using the same ICD‐10 codes indicated a positive predictive value for BAV coding ranging from 76% to 90%. 12 Similarly, the specificity of death certificates for BAV, based on our search criteria, ranges from 92% to 99%. 12

The findings of this manuscript have potential policy implications that could address the observed inequities. To effectively mitigate these disparities, it would be beneficial to outline practical recommendations that can be implemented both at the clinical and community levels. Specifically, routine echocardiographic screening for at‐risk populations, particularly in groups that may be disproportionately affected by BAV, could enable timely interventions and improve long‐term outcomes. 28 Additionally, ethnoracially tailored outreach programs should be developed to better engage underserved communities, providing education on the importance of heart health and increasing access to diagnostic services. Finally, refining clinical practice guidelines to incorporate demographic risk patterns, such as the elevated risk for BAV in certain racial and ethnic groups, would allow clinicians to better identify and manage individuals and populations at higher risk for complications.

Conclusions

The BAV‐attributable mortality rate is increasing in the United States, with prevalent disparities related to sex, age, and census region. Further efforts based on a multifaceted approach focused on early detection, regular monitoring, management of comorbidities, and timely intervention, especially among such vulnerable populations, are needed to reduce the burden of BAV‐attributable death in the United States.
