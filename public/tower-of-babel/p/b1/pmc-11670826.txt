Myopic shift in pediatric cataract surgery associated with age and surgical complications
Authors: Celso Menezes Filho, Andre Messias, Paulo Henrique F Silva, Rosalia Antunes-Foschini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To investigate predictors for myopic shift after pediatric cataract surgery after at least 3 years follow-up.Study Design:Cross-sectional and retrospective study.Methods:This study included patients treated for congenital or infantile cataract operated up to 5 years of age between 2010 and 2017. Patients were recruited for ophthalmologic evaluation. Surgical and medical data were acquired in medical charts.Statistical Analysis:Univariate and multivariate regressions were performed to look for potential risk factors for myopic shift.Results:This study evaluated 81 eyes of 50 patients, with 62 (77%) being bilateral cases, 48 (59%) with intraocular lens implantation, and 37 (74%) patients being strabismic. Age at surgery was 7.7 (3.7–30.5) months and at evaluation was 93.5 (55.1–113.0) months. Total myopic shift was -4.32 ± 3.25 D, significantly greater in patients operated on up to 6 months of life (-5.73 ± 3.14 D). The distant best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.6 (0.3–1.0) log of minimum angle of resolution (logMAR). Regarding myopic shift, in univariate analysis, older age at surgery is a protective factor (+0.08 D for each month older, P = 0.001). The presence of strabismus (-2.52 D, P = 0.014), aphakia (-2.45 D, P = 0.006), distant BCVA (-0.15 D per 0.1 logMAR, P = 0.024), and surgical complications (-3.02 D, P = 0.001) are risk factors. In multivariate analysis, older age at surgery (+0.06 D, P = 0.012) and surgical complications (-2.52 D, P = 0.001) remain significant.Conclusion:In pediatric cataract surgery, myopic shift is greater when surgery is performed in a younger age and if associated with surgical complications.

Pediatric cataracts are among the most important causes of preventable childhood blindness worldwide[1]; they are one of the leading causes of deprivation amblyopia and are responsible for many years of impairment.[23] Treatment consists of surgical cataract removal and aphakia correction with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation, glasses, or contact lenses.[4] The postoperative follow-up is decisive for visual prognosis due to frequent complications,[5] the need to assess amblyogenic conditions such as strabismus[6] and evaluate the refractometric progression.

The myopic shift observed after surgery is related to changes in the axial diameter and keratometry of the operated eye,[789101112] but there is no consensus about the factors that trigger or are related to the magnitude of this progressive myopia after pediatric cataract surgery. Understanding these predictive factors might be important for surgical planning and postoperative clinical follow-up.

This study aimed to report refraction and visual acuity outcomes and investigate predictors for myopic shift in children who underwent pediatric cataract surgery.

Methods

This cross sectional study received approval (no. 3.564.785) from the ethics committee of Ribeirão Preto Medical School - University of São Paulo and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The children's parents or guardians signed a consent form.

Selection and recruitment of participants

Children who underwent surgery for congenital or infantile cataracts in our institution from 2010 to 2017, up to 5 years of age at the time of surgery, and with a minimum follow-up time of 3 years were eligible to participate in the study. Telephone calls were used first to make recruitment of children; if unsuccessful, written invites were mailed. Children whose parents or guardians accepted our invitation were scheduled for an examination. The patients were examined from November 2019 to April 2021.

Examination

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was measured using the standard Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study chart or LEA symbols (Good-Lite, Elgin, IL, USA) in verbal patients, positioned at 4 m, with log of minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) notation.

We used the prism-and-cover test to evaluate ocular alignment. Patients were considered strabismic if either constant or intermittent heterotropy of any magnitude was present at any distance. When the cover test was not possible or reliable, we performed the Krimsky or Hirschberg test. Patients with a deviation equal to or greater than 15 ∆ in these tests were considered strabismic. Patients who had undergone prior strabismus surgery were categorized as strabismic. As for nystagmus, any type or magnitude that could be observed on inspection was considered.

A single medical examiner performed refractometry through retinoscopy and annotated using spherical equivalent. Biomicroscopy, fundoscopy, and applanation tonometry were done when possible.

Medical records review

We reviewed the medical records for the following information: type of cataract, aphakia or IOL implantation, age at surgery, presence of ocular malformations (microphthalmia, microcornea, anterior segment coloboma), refraction data after surgery, and postoperative surgical complications (posterior capsular rupture, IOL decentration, anterior or posterior synechiae, visual axis opacification, and glaucoma). The calculation of the total refractometric variation of each eye was performed by the arithmetic subtraction of the value of the spherical equivalent of the current refractometry by the patient's first postoperative refractometry (considered to be between the 7th and 120th days after surgery) and described in negative or positive diopters according to the result. Patients were also stratified into age groups by age at surgery as follows: younger than 6 months, 6 months to younger than 24 months, and older than 24 months.

Statistical analysis

We used the R program (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for statistical analysis and GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to create the figures. We assessed normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented the data as either a mean with standard deviation or a median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on normality. Results were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

We used univariate linear regression with mixed effects models and random intercepts to verify each predictive factor's impact on the eye's myopic shift. We evaluated the following continuous independent variables: age at surgery, anisometropia (difference in the spherical equivalent value of both eyes) at the first refractometry after surgery and on the day of the examination, BCVA (obtained on the day of the ophthalmologic examination), and follow-up time between surgery and the day of the examination. We also evaluated the following independent categorical variables: cataract bilaterality, strabismus, primary IOL implantation, type of cataract (total or other types), surgical complications (present or absent), visual axis opacification (present or absent), vitrectomy in the first surgical procedure, microphthalmia, glaucoma, and nystagmus.

Predictive factors with a univariate value of P < 0.20 were used to develop a multivariate generalized estimating equations regression model. Residual analysis was then used to verify whether the adjustment of the final model was adequate.

Results

We examined 100 eyes of 50 children (23 males, 27 females, P > 0.05), of which 31 (77%) were bilateral and 19 (23%) were unilateral cataracts. Eighty-one eyes were surgically treated, and 19 contralateral healthy eyes in cases of unilateral cataracts were also included.

Table 1 summarizes the demographic data and the clinical classification of the type of cataract in both unilateral and bilateral cataract groups. The median (IQR) age at surgery was 7.66 (3.68–30.49) months. The median (IQR) age at first postoperative refraction was 9.20 (5.29–30.95) months and 93.47 (55.12–112.90) months on the day of the study examination. Of the total, 46% of eyes underwent surgery before 6 months of life, 40% were total cataracts, and 90% had anterior vitrectomy. The interval between the first surgery and the current evaluation was 72.99 ± 37.13 months. IOL implantation was performed in 61% of bilateral and 53% of unilateral cases, with monofocal three-piece IOLs. The median (IQR) power of the implanted IOL was +22.00 D (+19.00–+25.38). Secondary IOL implantation was performed in four eyes of three patients. The reported surgical complications were posterior capsule rupture (two eyes- 2%), IOL decentration (two eyes- 2%), anterior or posterior synechiae (two eyes- 2%), visual axis opacification (six eyes- 7%), and glaucoma (eight eyes- 10%). Microphthalmia was described in five operated eyes (6%). Of the five microphthalmic eyes, four (80%) had postoperative complications. Anterior segment colobomas were present in two eyes (2%) and microcornea in eight (10%). Four of the eyes with microcornea were also microphthalmic. Additional procedures were performed in 12 eyes: vitrectomy in four (5%), IOL repositioning in two (2%), pupillary membrane removal in two (2%), posterior capsulotomy in two (2%), and synechiolysis in two (2%). Of the eyes with surgical complications, 11 (48%) were aphakic.

The median (IQR) of the distance BCVA in the operated eyes was 0.60 (0.30–1.00) logMAR. In the best and worst eyes of bilateral cases, the median (IQR) of the distance BCVA was 0.40 (0.20–0.60) and 0.60 (0.30–1.00) logMAR, respectively, and in unilateral cases was 1.30 (0.47–1.82) logMAR.

The mean myopic shift of all operated eyes was -4.32 ± 3.25 D. There was a progressive reduction in total myopic shift according to age at surgery: -5.73 ± 3.14 D in the age group younger than 6 months, -4.00 ± 3.15 D in the group from 6 to 24 months, and -2.52 ± 2.57 D in those operated after 24 months [Fig. 1]. Mean myopic shift in aphakic patients was -5.57 ± 3.11 D and -3.44 ± 3.09 D in pseudophakic patients. Mean myopic shift in strabismic patients was -4.99 ± 3.33 D and in non-strabismic patients was -2.52 ± 2.24 D (P = 0.003; Student›s t-test) [Fig. 2]. The mean myopic shift of eyes with surgical complications was -5.87 ± 3.55 D and in eyes with malformations was -6.00 ± 4.45 D. The myopic shift of eyes with complications and/or malformations was greater than in eyes without them (-6.46 vs. -3.46 D, P = 0.001; Student›s t-test) [Fig. 3]. The healthy contralateral eyes of those with unilateral cataracts showed total and yearly mean myopic shift of -0.69 ± 1.31 D and -0.15 ± 0.30 D/year, respectively.

Table 3 shows the univariate and multivariate regression analyses. Each predictor was evaluated in at least 40 patients. There was a significant association between myopic shift and age at surgery (P = 0.001), presence of strabismus (P = 0.001), aphakia (P = 0.006), distance BCVA (P = 0.002), surgical complications (P = 0.001), and visual axis opacification (P = 0.039) in univariate regression analysis. The coefficient between each predictor and the total myopic shift shown in Table 3 is interpreted as follows: the presence of strabismus (as an example of a categorical predictor) increases the total myopic shift by -2.52 D; for age at surgery (as an example of continuous predictor), for each additional month of age on the date of surgery, there is a decrease in total myopic shift by 0.08 D. In multivariate analysis, significant predictors were age at surgery (P = 0.012) and postsurgical complications (P = 0.001). Residual analysis indicated good adequacy of the proposed model for the data.

Discussion

In this study, we looked for myopic shift predictors in 70 out of 81 eyes of 50 children who underwent pediatric cataract surgery up to 5 years of age from 2010 to 2017.

Our study's mean total myopic shift was -4.32 D over 78 months. Kim et al.[13] and Valera Cornejo and Flores Boza[14] reported myopic shift of -3.30 and -3.60 D in similar follow-up times. In our sample, the younger children (up to 6 months) showed higher mean total myopic shift (-5.73 D), in agreement with other authors’ findings.[710111516]

Regarding myopic shift ratio, we observed a - 0.62 D shift per year, which is similar to other reports. For instance, Ashworth et al.,[17] in a retrospective study of 42 eyes with unilateral and bilateral cataracts operated on at 18 weeks of life, reported a decrease to -0.87 D in spherical equivalent at the third year of follow-up, with a total myopic shift of -5.43 D in the first 12 months after surgery. Similarly, Weakley et al.[18] found a -0.97 D/year myopic shift after 18 months postoperatively, decreasing after this period, and Valeina et al.[11] described a mean myopic shift of -7.75 D in their subgroup operated before 6 months of life. Rationally, the myopic shift in children operated in earlier ages should be directly related to the aging-driven ocular axial growth expected in this group.[12] Lambert et al.,[19] in a study after 10 years of follow-up of the aphakic eyes in the Infant Aphakia Treatment Study, found a mean change in refractive error in aphakic eyes of −2.11 D/year up to age 1.5 years, −0.68 D/year from 1.5 to 5.0 years, and −0.35 D/year from age 5 to 10.5 years. In our study, the mean myopic shift/year was -1.19 D/year in aphakic eyes of unilateral cataracts and -0.67 D/year in aphakic eyes of bilateral cataracts. In a study showing long-term outcomes of cataract surgery with IOL implantation in children with bilateral cataracts, Sukhija et al.[20] found there was a refractive shift from a mean spherical equivalent of 1.64 D at 2 weeks after surgery to -1.42 D after at least 8 years of follow-up. Our study showed similar results in the bilateral cases that underwent IOL implantation, with a total myopic shift of -3.37 D in this group.

Regarding strabismus, its association with pediatric cataracts has already been described in 59%–77% of patients in the postoperative period, being more frequent in unilateral cataracts.[321222324] In these studies, strabismus was not directly associated with refractometric outcomes. In our sample, 74% were strabismic. According to Lee and Kim,[25] significant factors associated with strabismus in pediatric cataracts are age at surgery less than 1 year, preoperative VA worse than 0.7 logMAR; nystagmus in bilateral cases, and VA difference greater than 0.5 logMAR in unilateral cases. Birch et al.[3] also reported that visual deprivation over 6 weeks increases the risk of strabismus by 9.1 times in cases of congenital cataracts. The univariate analysis showed an increase in myopic shift of -2.52 D in the presence of strabismus (95% confidence interval [CI] -4.53 to -0.52, P = 0.014). The intrinsic relationship between strabismus and anisometropia might explain this association. Anisometropia contributes to sensory ocular misalignment.[26] Strabismus, in turn, interrupts the emmetropization process of the eye, generating anisometropia.[27] In this way, regardless of which finding appears first, there would be the formation of a vicious circle between anisometropia and strabismus. Thus, the pediatric patient who undergoes cataract surgery is exposed to worse outcomes due to different reasons: earlier surgery may lead to greater myopic shift; a high initial anisometropia, resulting from aphakia or IOL implantation with a greater initial ametropia; and by the presence of pre or postoperative strabismus, which may appear concomitantly with myopic shift and anisometropia. In the proposed multivariate model, however, the presence of strabismus is not associated with an increase in myopic shift (95% CI -3.63 – 0.56, P = 0.057). An increase in the sample size may elucidate this finding.

In the univariate analysis, we found a significant association between myopic shift and amblyopia. Kim et al.[13] and Valeina et al.[11] also observed significantly greater myopic shift in amblyopic eyes. In experimental animal studies, induced refractive errors by spherical lenses were more compensated in baby chickens than in monkeys. The authors argue that it may be due to the closest distance that baby chickens look at objects (very close to the ground) and worse VA.[28] Similarly, children with congenital cataracts have poor vision and tend to bring things closer to the eyes to improve visual functionality, and this habit might trigger greater myopization. However, in situations where amblyopia is present in humans, it is generally associated with hyperopia. This association can also be found in monkeys.[29]

The association of surgical complications with myopic shift needs to be better established. In this study, the mean total myopic shift in the group with surgical complications was -5.87 D, compared to -3.75 D in the group with no complications. In the univariate analysis, complications increased myopic shift by -3.02 D (95% CI -4.55 to -1.49, P = 0.001) and remained in the multivariate model at -2.06 D (95% CI -3.99 to -1.21, P = 0.001). One possible explanation may be the need for IOL implant in the capsular sulcus, leading to greater myopic shift,[73031] more frequent complications in microphthalmic eyes,[32] and worse distant BCVA in these eyes, a factor also evaluated in our study.

Visual axis opacification was associated with an increase in myopic shift of -2.78 D (95% CI -5.41 to -0.14, P = 0.039) in univariate analysis and contributed to the greater myopic shift in the group with surgical complications, which includes opacification, among others. This finding has no consensus in current literature: Tartarella et al.[6] described greater axial growth in pseudophakic eyes that developed posterior capsule opacity. Other authors did not find this association.[1214] Experiments with animals of different species demonstrated a continued elongation of the eyes when retinal images are obscured by diffusers or palpebral suture, and elongation ceases when the diffusers or eyelid suture are removed.[333435] Ocular growth seems to occur secondary to local feedback controls since elongation of only half of the eye may occur if negative lenses or diffusers obscure only that half of the retina.[36]

Regarding IOL implantation, aphakia was significantly associated with a myopic shift increase of -2.45 D (95% CI -4.21 to -0.69, P = 0.006) in univariate analysis. However, this is not a consensus. Weakley et al.[18] did not observe differences in the refractive growth rate between pseudophakic and aphakic patients. In a cohort with unilateral cataracts, Lambert et al.[37] observed that the patients submitted to IOL implantation had greater axial growth when compared to the aphakic ones. Magli et al.[38] also observed higher myopic shift in children who received primary IOL implantation compared to the ones with secondary implantation. However, Tartarella et al.,[6] in a cohort with bilateral cataracts, observed less growth in operated pseudophakic eyes when compared to contralateral nonoperated eyes. In our multivariate regression analysis, aphakia was not significantly associated with myopic shift (95% CI -2.06–2.04, P = 0.990), probably due to the difference in age at surgery between aphakic and pseudophakic patients. Data obtained from experiments on animals[33] and children[39] show that accommodation induction and refractive differences between eyes interfere with refractive changes over time and are related to the previous refractive state. In aphakic or pseudophakic children, the influence or existence of such mechanisms is unknown.

Unilateral cataracts are associated with greater final myopia[40] and greater myopic shift.[1421] Valera Cornejo and Flores Boza[14] observed myopic shift up to twice as high in patients with unilateral cataracts. This phenomenon can be attributed to greater axial growth in unilateral cases.[1437] In our study, laterality was not related to myopic shift.

In our analysis, patient follow-up time was not a significant predictive factor for myopic shift. The slowdown in the eye growth speed with age may explain this finding.[8941]

Pediatric cataract surgery and rehabilitation remains a challenge, and the predictors associated with myopic shift after surgery must be analyzed as a set, or by subsets of patients according to age at diagnostic, bilaterality, crystallin lens opacity, or other eye abnormalities. The multivariate regression model can be helpful in evaluating patients with pediatric cataracts, but there is a need to be confirmed in larger cohorts.

Our study has limitations related to the retrospective data collection, in particular the lack of biometric data during follow-up, and the irregular distribution of the age groups. The small sample size may induce underestimation of some predictors such as microphthalmia, or overestimate the frequency of strabismus, due to the greater interest of these parents to participate in the study.

The strengths of our study are that all the patients were evaluated by the same examiner, with a more homogenous examination, and that only two experienced surgeons performed the surgeries.

Conclusion

As in previous studies, we showed that children operated in earlier ages show greater myopic shift, but we reported that surgical complications might play an important role as a predictor for myopia development after surgery. Future prospective cohort studies are needed to understand betterthe causalrelationship between the variables involved in myopic shift.
