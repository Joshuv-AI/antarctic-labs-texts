Assessing leaky expression of Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the mouse genome using sensitive bioluminescent reporters
Authors: Toshiaki Nakashiba, Takashi Sugiyama, Satoshi Iwano, Mizuho Iwama, Atsushi Yoshiki, Atsushi Miyawaki, Kuniya Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLeaky expression of genes in Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the absence of Cre recombinase has the potential to undermine precision of conditional gene manipulation. Here, we reliably detected and quantified this phenomenon in mice using highly sensitive bioluminescence imaging (BLI). By generating a series of knockin mice carrying Cre-dependent DNA constructs incorporating AkaBLI system, we found that a transcriptional STOP cassette alone was insufficient, producing significant Cre-independent leaky expression. This leaky expression can be suppressed by a combination of transcriptional and translational controls, facilitating precise detection and quantification of Cre-mediated recombination. Furthermore, application of leak-suppressed AkaBLI reporter mice to Cre-driver mice led to unambiguous identification of Cre-mediated recombination at intended and unintended sites throughout the mouse body. Thus, our data provide a generalizable solution to the leaky expression associated with Cre-loxP system, thereby enabling generation of diverse model organisms that require tight gene expression control.

Introduction

Conditional mutagenesis using the Cre-loxP system is invaluable for the spatial and temporal control of gene inactivation, activation, and reporter expression in experimental animals.1,2,3 To inactivate genes in mice, conditional knockout can be achieved by crossing floxed mice, wherein two loxP sites of the same orientation as the flanking genes, and Cre driver mice, wherein the floxed genes are deleted from specific tissues or cell types.4,5 Likewise, conditional gene activation and reporter expression can be accomplished using another type of floxed mouse carrying a Cre-dependent DNA construct that can activate gene expression upon Cre-mediated recombination.6,7,8,9 Currently, various floxed and Cre driver mice have been generated, and their combination provides numerous mouse models for studying gene function and cellular behavior at the organismal level.

The most widely used Cre-dependent DNA constructs utilize transcriptional termination via the STOP cassette, located between a pair of loxP sites.6,7,8 This floxed STOP cassette is further placed between the promoter and the open reading frame (ORF) of the gene, preventing RNA polymerases from proceeding beyond the STOP cassette. However, excision by Cre recombinase allows RNA polymerases to pass through the ORF of the gene, leading to protein expression downstream of the DNA constructs. However, one of the long-overlooked problems with this type of Cre-dependent DNA construct is its “leaky” expression. Several studies have demonstrated significant, albeit weak, fluorescent reporter signals even in the absence of Cre recombinase.9,10,11 Furthermore, popular Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter mice (Ai9 and Ai14)12 also exhibit low levels of tdTomato expression prior to exposure to Cre recombinase (https://www.jax.org/strain/007909# and https://www.jax.org/strain/007908). The Cre-independent leaky expression of biologically harmful proteins, such as toxins and disease-causing mutant proteins, can lead to unpredictable phenotypic results, misinterpretation of experimental data, and, in worst cases, failure to generate mouse models.13,14 Owing to the negative nature of the experimental results obtained, we speculate that many research projects have been abandoned or left unpublished without producing sufficient knowledge or an effective solution to the leaky expression observed in Cre-dependent DNA constructs.

The lack of sensitive reporters is a major obstacle to the accurate quantification of leaky gene expression, hindering the development and evaluation of alternative strategies to achieve tight gene expression control. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) using luciferases is a highly sensitive assay for detecting and quantifying very weak signals, with the benefit of having extremely low background signals.15,16 However, fluorescent imaging (FLI) is not sufficiently sensitive for this purpose because weak leaky expression is often masked by tissue autofluorescence.11,17 We previously generated a Cre-dependent bioluminescent and fluorescent reporter mouse and found that BLI, not FLI, could detect and quantify Cre-independent leaky expression using in vivo imaging.18 Thus, BLI can be used as a highly sensitive assay to explore and evaluate alternative Cre-dependent DNA constructs that prevent leaky expression. AkaBLI, which uses Akaluc as a luciferase enzyme and AkaLumine-HCl as a luciferin substrate, is one of the most sensitive BLI systems, enabling the detection of single cells in the mouse body and extending the imaging of target tissues to the brain.19

Cre-dependent gene activation with tight regulation is crucial not only in mouse models but also in other biomedical research materials, such as viral vectors.20 Different from the one using the transcriptional STOP cassette, the transcriptional unit of the ORF can be placed in a reverse orientation to the promoter, which was further flanked by two pairs of orthogonal Cre recognition sites (loxP and lox2272) placed in opposite orientations.21,22 Upon Cre recombination, the ORF within this DIO (double-floxed inverted ORF; flip-excision, FLEX) cassette is flipped and locked into the correct orientation to the promoter, thereby allowing protein expression. Another strategy depends on translational control, in which the ORF is separated from the Kozak sequence and translation initiation codon ATG using a floxed STOP cassette.23,24 In this ATG-out configuration, the ATG codon becomes in-frame with the ORF only after Cre recombination. Although both strategies have been widely used for conditional gene expression and their leaky expression has been reported in viral vectors,25,26 whether and to what extent Cre-independent leaky expression occurs in the mouse genome has not been fully determined using sensitive assays, such as BLI. To ensure a fair comparison between various strategies using Cre-dependent DNA constructs, it is essential to use the same genomic locus, promoter, and post-transcriptional elements, as these factors generally influence the extent of gene expression.

Another challenge associated with the Cre-loxP system is the recombination of Cre in unintended tissues or cell types in the body. Many Cre driver mice have been generated using tissue- or cell type-specific promoters for the conditional gene knockout. Although the primary focus of most research is gene deletion at intended sites within the body, the specificity of Cre recombination at unintended sites throughout the body remains unclear. Indeed, gene deletion at these off-target sites can complicate the interpretation of experimental results.27,28 We previously attempted to visualize the spatial specificity of Cre recombination throughout the body by generating Cre-dependent Akaluc reporter mice using a transcriptional STOP cassette.18 In vivo, AkaBLI could determine the specificity of Cre recombination in several Cre driver mice. However, concerns have been raised that recombination events in a small number of cells may be overlooked because of Cre-independent leaky expression from Cre-dependent DNA constructs. The use of Cre-dependent DNA constructs that suppress leaky gene expression is highly desirable to ensure the precise detection and quantification of recombination events throughout the body.

In the present study, we investigated whether leaky expression was associated with the strategies employed in Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the mouse genome using the AkaBLI system.19 We found that the leaky expression was almost completely suppressed in the Cre-dependent DNA constructs with the DIO cassette and ATG-out configuration, whereas it remained when the transcriptional STOP cassette was used. Furthermore, we demonstrated that leak-suppressed Cre-dependent Akaluc reporter mice were highly efficient for the comprehensive evaluation of the spatial specificity of Cre recombination throughout the body using seven popular Cre-driver lines.

In this reporter mouse, Cre-independent leaky expression was detected as Akaluc bioluminescence signals by in vivo AkaBLI, whereas such leaky expression could not be detected in tissue sections using a fluorescent protein, Venus, or immunohistochemistry (IHC) to detect Venus (Figures 1B–1E, see Reporter only). This Cre-independent leak causes serious problems when labeling small numbers of cells with Cre recombinase. Akaluc expression was directed in two different populations of neurons by Cre driver mice: Vgat-Cre transgenic mice for inhibitory neurons,29 which represent approximately 20% of all neurons in the mouse brain,30 or DAT/iCre knockin mice for dopaminergic neurons, which are small in number, with only approximately 20,000–30,000 neurons in the mouse ventral midbrain (presumably making up <1% of all neurons in the mouse brain)31,32 (Figures 1B–1E). In vivo AkaBLI was conducted using an EM-CCD camera in mice that received an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of AkaLumine-HCl (15 mM, 5 μL/gbw: gram body weight).19,33 While Akaluc bioluminescence signals in Vgat-Cre mice were captured with exposure times of only a few seconds (Figure 1C), those in DAT/iCre knockin mice required prolonged exposure times of 30–60 s (Figure 1E). As exposure time increased, Cre-independent Akaluc bioluminescence signals became apparent in mice carrying only #1 (floxed STOP single polyA) (see Reporter only in Figures 1C and 1E). This Cre-independent leakage of Akaluc expression rendered it challenging to differentiate between Cre-dependent Akaluc signals in the context of in vivo AkaBLI in a small number of cells.

The leaky bioluminescence signal intensities detected in mice carrying only #1 (floxed STOP single polyA) were much greater than those of the non-specific liver signals caused by the AkaBLI system in naive C57BL/6 mice,34,35 which required a much longer exposure time of 10 min (Figure 2). Exploration of alternative strategies to suppress leaky gene expression in Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the mouse genome

We designed three Cre-dependent DNA constructs aimed at tightly regulating gene expression at the transcriptional and translational levels (Figure 3A). In the CAG-LNpAx3L-Venus/Akaluc construct (#2, floxed STOP triple polyAs), triple polyA signals were added to an STOP cassette to increase the efficiency of transcription termination. In the CAG-ATG-out-LNpAx3L-Achilles∗/Akaluc construct (#3, ATG-out), the Kozak sequence and translation initiation codon ATG were placed in front of the first loxP site, so that the ATG-less reporter ORF following the second loxP site would be in-frame only after Cre recombination (Figure 3B). Achilles is a fluorescent protein derived from Venus that has faster-maturing properties.36 In the CAG-DIO-Venus/Akaluc construct (#4, DIO), the reporter ORF was placed within the DIO cassette in a reverse orientation relative to the CAG promoter, which should produce only antisense transcripts. We generated three knockin mice (#2, #3, and #4) at the ROSA26 locus and assessed leaky bioluminescence signals using an EM-CCD camera after IP injection of AkaLumine-HCl (15 mM AkaLumine-HCl, 5 μL/gbw; Figure 3C). Although mice carrying only #2 (floxed STOP triple polyAs) exhibited similar or slightly lower bioluminescence signals than mice carrying only #1 (floxed STOP single polyA) on both the dorsal and ventral sides of the body, the bioluminescence signals of mice carrying only #3 (ATG-out) or #4 (DIO) were suppressed to a greater extent at levels similar to those detected in C57BL/6 mice (#0). Quantitative assessment (n = 6 mice per group; Figures 3D and 3E) showed that the signal intensities throughout the body were greatly and significantly reduced in mice carrying only one of the three new Cre-dependent DNA constructs tested (#2, floxed STOP triple polyAs; #3, ATG-out; and #4, DIO) compared to those carrying only #1 (floxed STOP single polyA). Compared with those of C57BL/6 mice (#0), the bioluminescence signal intensities of mice carrying only #3 (ATG-out) and #4 (DIO) were not significantly different; however, significant differences were observed in those of mice carrying only #1 (floxed STOP single polyA) or #2 (floxed STOP triple polyAs). Thus, the leaky expression of Akaluc was negligible in the Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the ATG-out configuration (#3) and DIO cassette (#4). These leak-suppressed Cre-dependent Akaluc reporter mice appear ideal for accurately quantifying signals in a small number of cells labeled with Cre recombinase.

Cre-dependent reporter expression from leak-suppressed Cre-dependent DNA constructs

Next, we examined Cre-dependent reporter protein expression in the mice. When crossed with CAG-Cre mice13 for germline modification to allow Akaluc and fluorescent reporter expression throughout the body, both reporter signals were detected (Figures S1 and S2). Upon AkaLumine-HCl IP administration (2 mM, 5 μL/gbw), Akaluc bioluminescence signals were captured with exposure times ranging from a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, indicating that all Cre-dependent DNA constructs knocked into the mouse genome were functional.

We then conducted the BLI on a small number of cells labeled with DAT/iCre knockin mice (Figure 4A). All reporter mice tested (#1, #2, #3, and #4) expressed Venus/Akaluc in DAT-positive cells in the brain (Figure S3). Akaluc bioluminescence signals (15 mM AkaLumine-HCl IP injection, 5 μL/gbw) were detected in the heads of all mice carrying both the DAT/iCre knockin and one of the Cre-dependent DNA constructs (1D, 2D, 3D, and 4D in Figure 4B). However, significant leaky expression compared to that in C57BL/6 mice made accurate quantification impractical when using the Cre-dependent DNA construct of #1 (floxed STOP single polyA) or #2 (floxed STOP triple polyAs). Based on the higher bioluminescence signal intensities in 4D mice compared to 3D mice, as well as the negligible background bioluminescence signals comparable to those in C57BL/6 mice (Figure 4C), we concluded that the DIO cassette (#4) performed the best among the Cre-dependent DNA constructs tested so far and that the majority of bioluminescence signals detected in 4D mice were indeed DAT/iCre-dependent. Next, we generated three Cre-dependent DNA constructs using the DIO cassette to express Akaluc, which was not fused to the fluorescent protein (Figure S4). We avoided the potential risk of reducing the enzymatic activity of Akaluc by fusing it with a fluorescent protein.

In the CAG-DIO-Akaluc (#5, DIO), CAG-ATG-out-DIO-Akaluc∗ (#6, DIO and ATG-out), and CAG-DIO-Venus/P2A/Akaluc (#7, DIO) constructs, the ORFs of Akaluc were placed in a reverse orientation within the DIO cassette. The difference between #5 (DIO) and #6 (DIO and ATG-out) is that #6 combines the DIO and ATG-out configurations using an ATG-less Akaluc ORF. Evaluation of Cre recombination sites throughout the mouse body using leak-suppressed Cre-dependent reporter mice

Next, we investigated whether the Akaluc reporter mice carrying leak-suppressed Cre-dependent DNA constructs could determine the sites of Cre recombination in the mouse body (Figure 5). In vivo AkaBLI (15 mM AkaLumine-HCl IP injection, 5 μL/gbw) was performed on the dorsal and ventral sides of the DAT/iCre knockin mice carrying one Cre-dependent DNA construct. Discussion

Cre-dependent gene activation is widely used in biomedical research to study the expression of fluorescent reporters and disease-causing mutant proteins in specific tissues and cell types. However, the leaky expression of Cre-dependent DNA constructs has often been overlooked and has not been a topic of research for a long time. The leaky expression of the widely used Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter mice (Ai9 and Ai14)12 is described on the Jackson Laboratory websites (https://www.jax.org/strain/007909# and https://www.jax.org/strain/007908). In addition, plasmids of the floxed STOP cassette consisting of triple polyAs caused leakage in vivo in the context of the mouse genome, are described on the Addgene website (plasmids #133570 and #133574, https://www.addgene.org/133570/ and https://www.addgene.org/133574/). However, information supporting these statements remains limited, which is attributable to the lack of sufficiently sensitive methods for identifying and quantifying this phenomenon. In mouse models, only a few studies have qualitatively reported the leaky expression of fluorescent proteins in a Cre-independent manner.9,10,11 In contrast, our recent findings demonstrate that a bioluminescent protein effectively detects weak leaky expression, whereas a fluorescent protein does not.18

AkaBLI is one of the most sensitive BLI systems, enabling the detection of single cells in the mouse body and extending the imaging of target tissues to the brain.19 This highly sensitive AkaBLI reliably quantifies the leaky expression of Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the mouse genome,18 making it an ideal assay for exploring and evaluating alternative Cre-dependent DNA constructs that prevent leaky gene expression. To thoroughly investigate this issue, a series of knockin mice carrying one of the Cre-dependent DNA constructs was generated at the same ROSA26 locus (Figures 3 and S4). These constructs utilized the same promoter and untranslated regions (post-transcriptional elements and polyA). Consequently, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt in which a nearly identical genomic environment has been used to compare Cre-dependent DNA constructs and evaluate their leaky expression.

The leaky expression observed when using floxed STOP cassettes, consisting of single or triple polyA sequences (#1 and #2 in Figure 3) is likely caused by RNA polymerase readthrough transcription termination signals and the transcription of the immature mRNA.46,47 Herein, transcription may further extend several additional kilobases before polyA tail addition, and translation initiation from the ATG codon may occur downstream of the second loxP site.48 The leaky expression could also occur through spontaneous recombination between the two loxP sites in the absence of Cre, or via cryptic RNA splicing mechanisms that skip the floxed STOP cassette.49 In contrast, the Cre-dependent DNA constructs in the DIO cassette or ATG-out configuration effectively suppressed leaky gene expression through dual regulation at the transcriptional and translational levels (Figure 3). Several studies have demonstrated that leaky expression can be detected in Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus (AAV) viral vectors using DIO cassettes.25,26 This was caused by recombination events between the inverted repeats of recombinase recognition sites (loxP and lox2272) during AAV plasmid production in bacteria. However, our Cre-dependent DNA constructs using the DIO cassette did not cause any noticeable leakage in expression, suggesting that the inverted repeats of loxP and lox2272, which are integrated in a single copy in the mouse genome, rarely recombine in a Cre-independent manner.

There are three considerations regarding the use of leak-suppressed Cre-dependent DNA constructs. First, the distance between the two loxP sites can be varied to any size in the ATG-out configuration. The efficiency of Cre recombination depends on the distance between the two loxP sites.50,51 The longer the length, the lower the recombination efficiency; conversely, the shorter the length, the higher the recombination efficiency. In contrast, the DIO cassette was limited because the distance between a pair of loxP/lox2272 sites could not be reduced to less than the length of the ORF. Thus, the ATG-out configuration would be better for experiments that require fine-tuning of the recombination efficiency. Second, in the ATG-out configuration, adding extra amino acids corresponding to the loxP DNA sequence at the N-terminus of the protein may result in decreased protein expression and functional activity. Indeed, when tested in dopaminergic neurons (Figures 4C and S4E), adding extra amino acids reduced the bioluminescence signal intensities in the head (see 4D vs. 3D in Figures 4C and 5D vs. 6D in Figure S4E). Third, the absence of leaky expression in the DIO cassette may be limited to only at the ROSA26 locus, because leaky expression may also emerge at other genomic loci where transcription naturally occurs in the reverse direction. This issue could be resolved by the combining the DIO cassette and the ATG-out configuration (#6 in Figure S4).

The leaky expression of the Cre-dependent DNA constructs can cause several serious problems in biomedical research, potentially misleading the interpretation of experimental data. Although the level of leaky expression is generally not very high, potent proteins such as toxins and disease-causing mutant proteins can interfere with the normal physiological activity of cells, even at the level of leaky expression. One stark contrast, presumably caused by leaky expression, is the generation of Cre-dependent diphtheria toxin (DTA) mice as cell ablation models. While many Cre-dependent DTA mouse models using the floxed STOP cassette exhibit a lethal phenotype even in the absence of Cre recombinase,13,14 other Cre-dependent DTA mouse models using the DIO cassette have been successfully established, allowing for efficient cell ablation after Cre recombination52 (Nakashiba et al., unpublished results). Similarly, we speculate that many failed attempts to generate mouse models expressing potent disease-causing mutant proteins may be explained, in part, by leaky expression in Cre-dependent DNA constructs using the floxed STOP cassette.

The leaky expression of the highly sensitive Akaluc reporter in the Cre-dependent DNA constructs caused false-positive signals in the BLI. When floxed STOP cassettes were used, the false-positive signals resulted in an incorrect determination of the Cre recombination sites (Figures 1, 3, and 5). However, the elimination of the leaky expression to the background levels using the DIO cassette allowed for the unambiguous determination of the sites of Cre recombination throughout the body, even when using only a small number of cells deep inside the body, such as dopaminergic neurons and insulin-secreting β-cells (Figures 5 and 6). Furthermore, the elimination of the leaky expression helped to uncover previously unreported sites of Cre recombination in several Cre-driver lines (DAT/iCre mice in Figure 5 and Emx1-Cre mice in Figure 6). This is particularly important for studies using conditional knockout mice because experimental data may be confounded by the knockout of genes at unintended recombination sites. As the Akaluc bioluminescence signal intensities were not attenuated in the form of the Venus fusion protein (Figure S4), CAG-DIO-Venus/Akaluc reporter mice should be the best among the Cre-dependent Akaluc reporter mice tested in this study. Since BLI generally has low spatial resolution, further characterization at cellular resolution could be performed using the Venus fluorescent protein. The combination of light sheet microscopy and tissue-clearing methods such as Scale and CUBIC53,54 may help to detect Venus-positive cells within the 3D structures of mouse tissues. Therefore, the leak-suppressed Cre-dependent reporter mice developed in this study would be useful for comprehensively determining the recombination sites of various Cre-driver lines throughout the mouse body.

Limitations of the study

Our goal was to identify effective strategies for reducing leaky expression in Cre-dependent DNA constructs integrated into the mouse genome. While we did not investigate the causes of leaky expression when using a transcriptional STOP cassette, we found that Cre-dependent DNA constructs employing the DIO cassette and/or the ATG-out configuration can effectively suppress leaky expression. These findings led to the development of a reporter mouse strain that identifies Cre recombination sites throughout the mouse body.

In the floxed STOP cassettes, our study is limited to comparing two states of polyA, #1 (floxed STOP single polyA) and #2 (floxed STOP triple polyAs). A variety of floxed STOP cassettes with different sequences (e.g., Neo, other selection markers, and reporters), orientations, and lengths (e.g., double, triple, quadruple polyAs, and more polyAs) have been used in Cre-dependent DNA constructs of ROSA26 mouse models. Further additions of polyA to our floxed STOP cassettes may reduce the leaky expression to the negligible levels. Future studies are needed to assess leaky expression in such Cre-dependent DNA constructs by generating knockin mice harboring highly sensitive bioluminescent reporters to detect and quantify leaky expression. Furthermore, our study is also limited to the ROSA26 locus using the strong and ubiquitous CAG promoter. At other genomic loci, the degree of leaky expression might be negligible, particularly when Cre-dependent DNA constructs are knocked into sites downstream of weaker or tissue-specific promoters. If leaky expression becomes problematic, one of our strategies may be applicable to Cre-dependent DNA constructs. Investigating the mechanisms of leaky expression at the genomic level for spontaneous recombination, and at the transcriptional level for RNA polymerase readthrough and/or cryptic splicing, together with the information about the genomic environment (e.g., the proximity of neighboring promoters and their transcriptional direction) would help determine which strategies to employ.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal models, husbandry and ethical statements

All experimental protocols and husbandry for mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (T2023-EP001 and T2024-EP001) and the Genetic Recombination Experiments Committee (T2022-02-17) of the RIKEN Tsukuba Branch, respectively. All mice were cared for and treated humanely in accordance with the Committee guidelines. All mice were provided commercial laboratory mouse chow and water ad libitum and housed under light conditions (lights on from 8:00 to 20:00) and specific pathogen-free conditions. In vivo AkaBLI, histological experiments, and epifluorescence imaging were performed on adult male and female mice, with ages specified in the respective figure legends.

Method details

Targeting vectors

For targeting vectors of the Cre-dependent Akaluc reporter at the ROSA26 locus, a plasmid described in a previous study18 was modified using the standard cloning technique as follows: 1) Synthetic DNA oligos for two copies of loxP, Kozak/ATG plus two copies of loxP, and DIO (consisting of two copies of loxP and lox 2272) were generated and separately cloned and inserted between the CAG promoter and WPRE. 2) The Neo pA ×3 DNA cassette, consisting of Pgk-neo-pA derived from FRT-PGK-gb2-neo-FRT (Gene Bridges GmbH, Germany) and two copies of SV40 pA, was inserted between two copies of loxP. 3) Venus/Akaluc, ATG-less Achilles/Akaluc, Akaluc, ATG-less Akaluc, and Venus/P2A/Akaluc, all of which were amplified by PCR, were cloned into one of the following sites: between two copies of loxP or within the DIO. 4) A gRNA target sequence (PITCh) was added to the plasmid backbone to cleave the targeting vector with CRISPR/Cas9, based on previous studies.55 5) A recognition site for Flp recombinase, FRT, was added immediately after the stop codon of all the Akaluc reporter DNA constructs mentioned above, in case more than two copies of the targeting vector were integrated at the ROSA26 locus. Flp-mediated recombination allows for removal of the extra copy of the vector and correct knockin of one copy of the Akaluc reporter DNA construct at the ROSA26 locus. The length of homology arms of our original targeting vector was 1.1 kb at the 5′ end and 2.8 kb at the 3′ end. Targeting vectors with shorter homology arms (approximately 500 bp at both the 5′ and 3′ ends) were also prepared and used for the microinjection, as described in the following section.

For the targeting vector of the DAT/iCre mouse strain, homology arms to the genomic region upstream and downstream of the stop codon of the DAT (also known as Slc6a3), each with a length of approximately 1.0 kb, were amplified by PCR, and Thosea asigna-derived 2A peptide (T2A)/nuclear localization signal (NLS)/iCre was cloned in-frame to the DAT protein coding sequence.

Generation of founder mice

Mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 with zygotes derived from C57BL/6NJcl mice. For Akaluc reporter mouse strains, targeting vectors mixed with ribonucleoprotein complex were injected into the pronuclei of zygotes produced by in vitro fertilization. The injection mixture was prepared using the SPRINT-CRISPR method.56 It consisted of Cas9 protein (100 ng/μL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), R26-T2 crRNA (50ng/μl), PITCh-gRNA3 crRNA (50 ng/μL), tracrRNA (100 ng/μL), targeting vector (10 ng/μL), and Cas9 mRNA (25 ng/μL) generated by the in vitro transcription. Both crRNA and tracrRNA were purchased from FASMAC, Atsugi, Japan and their sequences of the crRNAs were as follows: R26-T2-crRNA (5′-CGC CCA UCU UCU AGA AAG ACG UUU UAG AGC UAU GCU GUU UUG-3′) and PITCh-gRNA3-crRNA (5′-GCA UCG UAC GCG UAC GUG UUG UUU UAG AGC UAU GCU GUU UUG-3′).

For the DAT/iCre knockin mice, Cas9 mRNA and single guide RNA (sgRNA) were injected together with the targeting vector into the cytoplasm of two-cell stage embryos of C57BL/6NJcl mice using a modified 2C-HR-CRISPR method.57 The targeting vector was linearized using restriction enzymes, and then the 3′ end of the targeting vector was biotinylated with biotin-16-ddUTP (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using terminal transferase (NEB Japan, Tokyo, Japan). The resulting DNA fragment was purified from the agarose gel, and its biotinylation efficiency was assessed by a depletion experiment using streptavidin magnetic beads (NEB Japan) and found to be close to 100%. The monomeric streptavidin-fused Cas9 (Cas9-mSA) plasmid was generated by cloning synthetic oligos of monomeric streptavidin into the C-terminus of Cas9 of the T7-NLS hCas9-pA (RDB13130) plasmid, obtained from RIKEN BRC.58 In vitro transcription to generate Cas9 or Cas9-mSA mRNA was performed using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 ULTRA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Tokyo, Japan). A sgRNA was also generated by in vitro transcription using a PCR-based DNA template containing the T7 promoter, the genomic target sequence for the DAT (Slc6a3), 5′-CATTGGCTGTTGGTGTAAAG-3′, and the sequence of the sgRNA scaffold. The injection mixture comprised of Cas9-mSA mRNA (100 ng/μL), sgRNA (50 ng/μL), and targeting vector (10 ng/μL).

Evaluation of targeted knockin

After the implantation of embryos into the oviducts of foster mothers, initial screening for knockin founder mice of the Akaluc reporter in pups was performed by PCR using primer pairs that anneal to the genomic region external to the homology arms and the inserted DNA fragment corresponding to CAG or WPRE, to verify the 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively. For DAT/iCre founder mice, external primers annealing to the homology arms of the DAT locus as well as primers annealing to iCre in the forward and reverse directions were used. Primers that detected the ampicillin resistance gene were used to assess integration of the plasmid backbone of the targeting vectors into the genome. Targeted integration of the plasmid backbone into the ROSA26 locus was further assessed using primers annealing to genomic regions external to the homology arms and the plasmid backbone.18 All primer pairs used are listed in Table S2. Candidate founder mice were selected based on two criteria: 1) correct knockin at 5′ and 3′ ends; and 2) absence of integration of the plasmid backbone into the ROSA26 locus, except for the CAG-DIO-Akaluc mouse generation in which only one founder mouse with plasmid backbone integrated at the ROSA26 locus was obtained. In the DAT/iCre knockin founder mice, an approximately 230 bp DNA fragment derived from the sgRNA DNA template was coincidentally inserted just after the stop codon of the iCre CDS. This insertion into the 3′ untranslated region of DAT (Slc6a3) did not affect the specificity of iCre-mediated recombination, as confirmed by its co-localization with DAT in the brain. These founder mice were then bred with C57BL/6NJcl mice. Germ line transmission from at least one founder mouse in all Akaluc reporter knockins and DAT/iCre knockins was confirmed using the same primer sets as above, and subsequently bred with C57BL/6NJcl mice to establish discrete strains.

Animal breeding

The CAG-DIO-Akaluc strain that integrated the plasmid backbone into the ROSA26 locus were bred with C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-flpe)36Ito/ItoRbrc (RBRC01834).59 The progeny of mice double-positive for Flpe and Akaluc were genotyped using the primer sets described above and confirmed to lack a plasmid backbone in the genome. Homozygous mice of all Akaluc reporter strains used in this study were viable, and one-copy integration of the Cre-dependent reporter constructs was further assessed using primer sets listed in Table S2, annealing to the ROSA26 locus external to the homology arms at the 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively. Homozygous mice produced a single band (more than 10 kb) corresponding to those correctly targeted by one copy of the Cre-dependent reporter constructs at the ROSA26 locus, whereas heterozygous mice produced only a single band of a much shorter size (4.3 kb) corresponding to the WT allele, probably due to inefficient amplification of the longer knockin alleles by PCR (data not shown).

For the DAT/iCre and Vgat-Cre mouse crosses, heterozygous or homozygous females of Akaluc reporter mouse strains were bred with DAT/iCre or Vgat-Cre (C57BL/6J-Tg(Slc32a1-cre)65Kzy, RBRC10606) males. To express Akaluc reporters in the whole body, Cre-mediated recombination in the germline was achieved by crossing with CAG-Cre (B6.Cg-Tg(CAG-Cre)CZ-MO2Osb, RBRC01828) mice. Cre-mediated recombination was confirmed by PCR using primer sets for the detection of the Akaluc reporter, which was placed immediately downstream of the CAG promoter as a result of the excision of Neo pA ×3 or the conversion of DIO cassettes. The primer sets used are listed in Table S2. Genotyping protocols for the Vgat-Cre and CAG-Cre mouse strains are available from RIKEN BRC at https://mus.brc.riken.jp/en/.

As shown in Figure 6, the six Cre-driver lines were crossed with mice carrying the CAG-DIO-Venus/Akaluc DNA construct (#4). The Cre-driver lines used here are as follows: Vgat-Cre Tg (C57BL/6J-Tg(Slc32a1-cre)65Kzy, RBRC10606), Emx1-Cre KI (B6.129P2-Emx1<tm1.1(cre)Ito>, RBRC01345), Tubb3-Cre KI (C57BL/6N-Tubb3<tm1.1(Cre)Ksak>, RBRC11130), Pdx1-Cre KI (B6;Cg-Pdx1<tm1(cre)Yasu>, RBRC10170), Ins1-Cre KI (C57BL/6J-Ins1<em1(cre)Utr>, RBRC09525), and Lck-Cre Tg (B6.Cg-Tg(Lck-cre)1Jtak, RBRC04738). All breeding schemata consisted of female Akaluc reporter mice crossed with Cre-driver male mice. Genotyping protocols for these Cre-driver lines are available from RIKEN BRC at https://mus.brc.riken.jp/en/.

In vivo AkaBLI

Before the imaging session, all hair covering the region of interest was removed using a shaver, followed by the application of depilatory cream. Mice were anesthetized with a combination of anesthetics as follows: 0.3 mg/kg medetomidine, 4.0 mg/kg midazolam, and 5.0 mg/kg butorphanol. Bioluminescence images were acquired using an ImagEM 9100-13 (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan) and a lens (#903018, AstroScope, NY, USA) set up in a dark chamber. AkaLumine-HCl (Wako, Osaka, Japan) was dissolved in distilled water to a final concentration of 2 mM (Figure S2) or 15 mM (other Figures). At each imaging session, the AkaLumine-HCl solution was injected intraperitoneally at the dose indicated in the Results section in proportion to the grams of body weight (gbw) of the mice. For Figures 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6, and Figure S4B, image acquisition of the ventral side of the whole body started 5 min after substrate administration, followed by image acquisition of the dorsal side of the whole body 25 min after substrate administration. Both image acquisition sessions were separated by 20 min owing to the multiple exposure times ranging from 50 ms to 10 min. Exposure times are shown in the figures. For Figures 4 and S4D, image acquisition of the dorsal side of the head and upper abdomen began 25 min after substrate administration. Data from images acquired using the ImagEM 9100-13 were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) based on the calibration data provided by KoshiUni (Atto, Tokyo, Japan).

Histology

The dissected tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer overnight. The tissues were further treated with 30% sucrose overnight and then embedded in OCT compound (Sakura, Tokyo, Japan). Cryostat sections of the brain and spinal cord (50 μm thickness) were prepared for immunohistochemistry using rat anti-DAT antibody (AB369; Merck Millipore, MA, USA), rabbit anti-GFP antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and goat anti-Luc2 antibody (Promega, WI, USA). The tissue sections were first treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and then blocked with PBS containing 3% normal donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100. The sections were subsequently incubated with anti-DAT (1000×) and rabbit anti-GFP (1000×), or anti-DAT (1000×) and goat anti-Luc2 (500×) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with PBS, the sections were further incubated with Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-rat IgG (250×, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) and Alexa 488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (500×; Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4°C. For detection of anti-Luc2, ImmPRESS (Vectorlabs, CA, USA) was used for 2 h at 24°C. Signals were visualized using TSA-FITC (PerkinElmer, MA, USA). Cryostat sections of the thymus, liver, lung, kidney, spleen, and pancreas were also prepared at 16 μm thickness for immunohistochemistry using rabbit anti-GFP antibody (500×), followed by Alexa 488-conjugated or Alexa 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (500×). For anti-insulin/anti-GFP IHC, chicken anti-GFP antibody (500×; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and rabbit anti-insulin (500×, ab181547; Abcam) were used as primary antibodies, followed by Alexa 488-conjugated anti-chicken IgY (250×; Jackson ImmunoResearch) and Alexa 568-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (500×). All sections were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X710; Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Venus and Achilles epifluorescent imaging

Fluorescence images of the whole body were acquired using a Keyence GFP-lighting system (VB-L12; Keyence) equipped with a CCD camera (DFC450C; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 16.78). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Significant differences between two groups were determined using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sample sizes (numbers of mice used, N) are listed in the Figure legends for each experiment. Exact p values of all statistical analyses are available in Table S1.
