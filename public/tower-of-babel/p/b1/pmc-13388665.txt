Efficacy of seaweed-based carbon dioxide removal reduced by iron limitation and nutrient competition with phytoplankton
Authors: Manon Berger, Lester Kwiatkowski, Laurent Bopp, David T. Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) through seaweed cultivation has been proposed as a promising marine CDR approach due to its high afforestation potential and favorable carbon-to-nutrient ratios. However, recent studies suggest that the afforestation potential is constrained by iron limitation, and efficiency depends on relative stoichiometry with phytoplankton. Global CDR models overlook iron limitation and fail to capture how nutrient feedbacks with phytoplankton will reduce ocean carbon uptake. Here, an ocean biogeochemical model is used to assess how nutrient demand, affinity, and limitation influence the afforestation potential and CDR efficiency of seaweed cultivation. Iron limitation reduces afforestation potential by threefold after accounting for N and P limitation. Variations in nutrient affinity and demand cause large differences in CDR efficiency, from −43% to +78%. We show that if seaweed is less nutrient-efficient than phytoplankton, cultivation results in negative net CDR. Failing to account for these nutrient dynamics risks overestimating seaweed-based CDR efficacy.

Introduction

In response to the urgent challenge posed by climate change, strategies aimed at limiting global warming to 1.5 or 2∘C by 2100 require substantial deployment of carbon dioxide removal (CDR), with projected targets ranging from 24 to 860 GtCO2 throughout the 21st century1. One proposed CDR technique is seaweed cultivation, which builds on well-established coastal seaweed aquaculture for food production and envisions its upscaling to the nearshore open ocean for CDR. In this approach, the harvested biomass would be exported to the deep ocean or buried on land, although such large-scale disposal pathways are not yet well established or quantitatively assessed2,3. The potentially high carbon uptake capacity of seaweed makes it an attractive option for CDR4,5. However, the CDR potential and possible side effects of seaweed cultivation are poorly characterized6,7.

Notably, recent research has identified iron limitation as a possible bottleneck for sustainable seaweed cultivation8. However, iron dynamics have yet to be incorporated into seaweed-based CDR projections, which have focused on nitrate and phosphate macronutrient constraints. As a vital micronutrient, iron is required for essential metabolic functions of seaweed, including photosynthesis, growth, and nitrogen assimilation9. Moreover, because iron limits phytoplankton production in many ocean regions10, its reallocation to support seaweed cultivation could affect the biological carbon pump and potentially reduce the CDR potential of seaweed.

Nutrient (nitrate (N), phosphate (P), and iron (Fe)) stoichiometry (i.e., demands) and uptake affinities influence seaweed-phytoplankton competition dynamics, determining the CDR benefit of seaweed11. Seaweed CDR is evaluated against a counterfactual in which there is no cultivation and primary production is entirely dominated by phytoplankton. Seaweeds provide additionality relative to phytoplankton if they assimilate more carbon per unit nutrient. Conversely, if they are less nutrient-efficient, they would fail to provide additionality and could theoretically result in negative CDR11. If the assessed scope of nutrient demands and affinities were limited, it could bias assessments of seaweed cultivation potential. Yet, studies that examined the CDR potential of seaweed cultivation have generally tested a limited range of stoichiometry (e.g., C:N ratios of 16.3 and 20 (and ±10%), and C:P ratios of 49 and 111, as reported by Berger et al.12 and Wu et al.13, respectively) and one nutrient affinity, and those that have tested variable nutrient affinity have focused on seaweed production potential and overlooked geochemical and biological feedback mechanisms that influence CDR14. As such, there are currently poor constraints on the CDR potential of seaweed cultivation, its environmental co-benefits and consequences, and the potential optimum regions for deployment11,15.

This modeling study is the first to examine the effects of iron limitation8,11, nutrient demands, and affinities on ocean afforestation potential and CDR efficiency. Together, these two factors define the overall CDR potential of seaweed cultivation, i.e., the product of the ocean afforestation potential (the amount of seaweed that can be grown) and the CDR efficiency (the amount of atmospheric carbon removed per ton of seaweed biomass)15. We conduct an ensemble of simulations to explore the influence of nutrient limitation, affinity, and demand on seaweed CDR across global Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs, which extend 200 nautical miles from the coast), including feedbacks on phytoplankton, and the resulting effects on ocean afforestation capacity and CDR efficiency.

We used a modified version of the global ocean biogeochemical model NEMO-PISCES to simulate kelp cultivation (Laminariales), accounting for dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) and nutrient uptake as functions of light, temperature, and nutrient availability. A 2∘ resolution configuration was adopted; this limits the representation of fine-scale coastal processes, a trade-off that facilitated the centennial-scale, large-ensemble simulations necessary to sample observed variability in seaweed stoichiometry and uptake kinetics. We simulated 25 years of cultivation in EEZs followed by 50 years of cessation under a high-mitigation scenario (Representative Concentration Pathway 2.6; RCP2.6) for the period 2025–2100. The choice of RCP2.6 reflects the consensus that CDR approaches should target eliminating residual emissions alongside substantial reductions in CO2 emissions1. With the exception of afforestation potential simulations, seaweed production is scaled by enhancing afforestation in nutrient-rich regions to achieve a global production target of 0.5 PgC yr−1. This ensures CDR efficiency and environmental impact comparisons are performed at a consistent afforestation level. To assess the impact of nutrient demands and affinities on CDR potential, we compiled published data on stoichiometric ratios extending the work of Sheppard et al.11 with C:Fe ratios (Fig. 1). Results

Ocean afforestation potential is limited by iron

N and P limitation constrain ocean afforestation potential, confining high production areas primarily to high latitudes and upwelling regions. N and P limitation on seaweed growth (simulation CNP) reduces ocean afforestation potential compared to the idealized afforestation potential without nutrient constraints (simulation C), with only 15% of the idealized afforestation potential achieved after 25 years of cultivation (Fig. 2A, B). Mid-latitude regions are most affected, with production reduced by 80–100%. Approximately 36% of the cultivation area experiences near-total production loss, with reductions exceeding 95% relative to the idealized potential. The inclusion of iron limitation further diminishes afforestation potential, with only 5% of the idealized capacity realized (Fig. 2A, C). Concurrent N, P, and Fe limitation (simulation CNPFe), therefore, reduces afforestation potential by a factor of three compared to the simulation where only macronutrient limitation is considered (simulation CNP). This limitation further suppresses production in high latitudes and upwelling regions, confining viable cultivation primarily to coastal zones. Overall, 63% of the cultivation area faces near-total production loss, with reductions exceeding 95% relative to the scenario without nutrient limitation. Only a limited number of coastal regions maintain viable production. Annual seaweed production remains nearly constant over the 25 years of cultivation in all simulations.

Seaweed CDR is counterproductive in 20% of the ocean when iron is accounted for

Seaweed-based CDR generally enhances ocean carbon uptake, but its effectiveness is strongly influenced by nutrient limitation, which can sometimes cause a reduction in the ocean carbon sink. The inclusion of iron limitation further lowers the CDR flux to 0.73 GtCO2 yr−1 (0.2 GtC yr−1), with seaweed production remaining at 0.5 GtC yr−1 (Fig. 3, simulation CNPFe) due to enhanced biological feedback. Under these constraints, CDR efficiency drops to 24% after 25 years and recovers only to 60% after 50 years post-cessation.

Regionally, in the absence of nutrient limitation, the CDR flux is relatively uniform (Fig. 3D), aligning with areas of maximum afforestation potential (Fig. 2A). When macronutrient limitation is considered, the highest CDR fluxes occur in regions of maximum afforestation potential and nutrient-rich areas, particularly in the Southern Ocean and upwelling zones, with fluxes reaching up to 52 gCO2 m−2 yr−1. The inclusion of iron limitation, however, causes a substantial shift, with approximately 20% of the ocean exhibiting negative CDR fluxes, including many regions that had previously exhibited high CDR fluxes under N and P limitation. In these areas, instead of enhancing carbon uptake, seaweed cultivation results in a net reduction in ocean carbon uptake. For example, the Pacific eastern boundary upwelling zone shows a negative CDR flux of up to −50 gCO2 m−2 yr−1. Under combined N, P, and Fe limitation, the highest CDR fluxes are found in the Northwest Pacific and Northeast Atlantic, where local fluxes reach up to 29 gCO2 m−2 yr−1.

Iron amplifies the biological feedback limiting seaweed CDR efficiency

Under macronutrient and iron limitation, nutrient reallocation to seaweed production weakens the biological carbon pump (BCP) and reduces carbon sequestration. Consequently, the net CDR effect of seaweed is reduced, reflecting the BCP-driven reduction relative to the counterfactual scenario without cultivation.

Under macronutrient limitation, a decrease in carbon export flux of up to 0.31 PgC yr−1 (4% decline) is observed after 25 years of cultivation compared to the control (Fig. 4, CNP). The cumulative flux reaches −6.1 PgC after 25 years of cultivation and −9.2 PgC 50 years after cessation. This reflects a decline in BCP carbon sequestration, resulting in diminished CDR efficiency due to seaweed nutrient consumption (Fig. 3C).

The introduction of iron limitation further exacerbates this reduction of the carbon export flux to 0.45 PgC yr−1 (5.8% decline) reached during the second year of cultivation (Fig. 4, CNPFe). After cultivation ends, the carbon export flux recovers quickly, rising by 0.28 PgC yr−1 within 2 years. Despite the rebound, cumulative carbon export flux under N, P, and Fe limitation decreases by 14.4 PgC after 25 years of cultivation and recovers to only 10.6 PgC 50 years after cessation, with most recovery occurring within the first 15 years.

Regionally, reductions in carbon export flux under N and P limitation are most pronounced in the western equatorial Pacific, where fluxes decrease by 12.0 gC m−2 yr−1 (Fig.4D), aligning with the decline in NPPPHY (Fig. 5C, D). The inclusion of iron limitation leads to further reductions, with carbon export fluxes dropping by up to 60 gC m−2 yr−1, particularly in the Southern Ocean and along eastern boundary currents. Seaweed iron consumption amplifies phytoplankton production decline

In the control simulation (without seaweed cultivation) under the RCP2.6 scenario, phytoplankton primary production (NPPPHY) shows a climate change signal, with a global decline peaking in 2040, followed by a gradual recovery. In the idealized simulation C, where seaweed only consumes DIC (no nutrient limitation), phytoplankton production remains unaffected, showing no deviation from the control simulation. However, when N and P limitation is introduced, phytoplankton primary production decreases by up to 2 PgC yr−1, decreasing by 4.8% global NPPPHY compared to the control (Fig. 5, CNP). The introduction of iron limitation further exacerbates this reduction, decreasing phytoplankton primary production by up to 3.5 PgC yr−1 in the second year of cultivation, corresponding to an 8% decrease in global NPPPHY (Fig. 5, CNPFe). However, after cessation of cultivation, phytoplankton production recovers quickly, increasing to 1.9 PgC yr−1 2 years after cessation.

Regionally, N and P limitation triggers NPPPHY declines in the western equatorial Pacific, with phytoplankton production declining by 106 gC m−2 yr−1—equivalent to a 57% reduction (Fig. 5C, D). The addition of iron limitation intensifies this decline to 268.3 gC m−2 yr−1, or an 81% reduction, particularly in the Southern Ocean and eastern boundary regions. However, some regions in the subtropics exhibit increased phytoplankton production of up to 90 gC m−2 yr−1, corresponding to a 97% increase under seaweed N, P, and Fe consumption.

Favorable regions for seaweed CDR effectively non-existent

Favorable regions for seaweed-based CDR were identified as regions with high afforestation potential, high CDR efficiency, and minimal environmental impacts on phytoplankton. Under N and P limitation, a vast area of 24 million km2 or 20% of global EEZs, was determined to have high afforestation potential (Fig. 6A, green). Some regions of high afforestation potential coincided with areas of high CDR flux, including the Pacific and Atlantic upwelling zones and the Southern Ocean (Fig. 6A, magenta). Phytoplankton impacts were mostly localized in the western equatorial Pacific, likely due to upstream cultivation effects around Pacific islands. Influence of nutrient demand and affinity on CDR potential

The CDR potential of seaweed afforestation depends not only on the area and biomass that can be cultivated (afforestation potential) but also on how efficiently this biomass contributes to atmospheric carbon removal (CDR efficiency). Nutrient demand plays a key role in determining whether seaweed cultivation enhances ocean carbon uptake and provides additionality. Ultimately, the relative nutrient demands of seaweed and phytoplankton determine whether seaweed can assimilate more carbon than phytoplankton for a given nutrient pool. Among the nutrients simulated, P demand introduces the greatest uncertainty into both afforestation potential (Fig. 7A) and CDR flux and efficiency (Fig. 7B), followed by iron and then N. The lower P demand bound (C:P of 461) of seaweed can increase afforestation potential by up to 53%, a trend consistent over 25 years of cultivation. However, high P demand (C:P of 15) results in a net reduction in ocean carbon uptake, with a cumulative loss of 15 PgC over 25 years of cultivation (i.e., negative CDR). This negative CDR flux persists, even after 50 years of cessation, due to a long-lasting BCP feedback that reduces carbon export by 58 PgC after 25 years and does not recover. Iron demand introduces the second-largest source of uncertainty. The high Fe demand bound (C:Fe ratio of 172) reduces afforestation potential by 28%, while also leading to negative CDR, with a 9.4 PgC reduction in ocean carbon uptake over 25 years, associated with a 43.6 PgC decrease in carbon export (Fig. 7C). Conversely, low Fe demand (C:Fe ratio of 23,214) enhances both the CDR flux and efficiency, with an additional 9.8 PgC of ocean carbon uptake after 50 years of cessation, corresponding to 78% CDR efficiency, and reduced impact on carbon export (−6.7 PgC after 25 years). In comparison, variability in N demand has a much smaller effect on ocean afforestation potential, with projection anomalies of less than 2%. The impact of N demand on CDR flux is more pronounced. High N demand (C:N ratio of 4) results in a globally negative CDR flux, with losses of up to 3 PgC from reduced ocean carbon uptake, and a 12% reduction in global phytoplankton production. In contrast, low N demand (C:N ratio of 144) enhances CDR, with CDR efficiency reaching 70% after 50 years of cessation.

Nutrient affinity reflects the ability to uptake available nutrients. Hence, a high nutrient affinity allows seaweed to access nutrients more effectively, thereby reducing nutrient limitation. Variability in nutrient affinity contributes to uncertainty in afforestation potential. Variations in iron affinity have the strongest effect, with high Fe affinity (KFe of 0.29 nmolFe/L) increasing afforestation potential by 85%, while low Fe affinity (KFe of 5.15 nmolFe/L) decreases it by 43% (Fig. 7, right panels). Variations in N affinity are the second most influential, with a high affinity (KN of 1.1) increasing afforestation by 13%, and a low affinity reducing it by 40%. Variability in P affinity has a relatively minor impact (<8%). While nutrient affinity variations do not substantially alter total global seaweed production, they do affect its regional distribution, leading to slight changes in CDR efficiency.

Discussion

Iron diminishes seaweed CDR and exacerbates environmental impacts

Iron availability emerges as a key factor influencing seaweed CDR potential, affecting both ocean afforestation potential and CDR efficiency, confirming the need to include it in CDR discussions, as suggested by Paine et al.8. Iron is a key micronutrient required for photosynthesis and cellular metabolism in both phytoplankton and seaweed8. When seaweed iron requirements and uptake kinetics are accounted for in our simulations, both projected ocean afforestation potential and CDR efficiency decline (Figs. 2, 3).

Under macronutrient limitation, simulated CDR efficiency is 82% after 25 years of cultivation, aligning with previous global modeling studies on multi-centennial timescales13 but exceeding decadal estimates, which likely underestimate efficiency due to incomplete air-sea equilibration12. This also falls within the 7–50% reduction in CDR efficiency due to nutrient reallocation estimated from natural analogs16. Under iron-limited conditions, CDR efficiency is further diminished, with 20% of the ocean becoming counterproductive and exhibiting reduced ocean carbon uptake instead of enhancement (Fig. 3). Notably, regions that previously exhibited the highest CDR flux under only N and P limitation—such as the Southern Ocean and upwelling zones—experience reduced or even negative CDR flux when iron limitation is included (up to −50 gCO2 m−2 yr−1) (Fig. 3). This global reduction in CDR flux, along with the emergence of regions with negative CDR, is largely driven by stronger BCP feedback under iron limitation (Fig. 4). Since iron is already a limiting nutrient in many ocean regions, the additional iron demand from seaweed cultivation further depletes iron availability for phytoplankton, thereby constraining productivity. Iron limitation also alters the temporal dynamics of seaweed cultivation. Under iron limitation, CDR flux and phytoplankton primary production anomalies peak within just 2 years of cultivation, followed by a rebound effect after cultivation ceases (Figs. 4, 5). This rebound is attributed to the short seawater residence time of iron, with a similar response simulated in ocean fertilization experiments17. In surface waters, the residence time of dissolved iron typically ranges from only a few days to a few months18. Consequently, once the seaweed iron uptake ceases, background sources such as atmospheric dust deposition, riverine discharge, and sediment resuspension quickly restore ambient concentrations, leading to a rebound in the system’s iron availability.

Iron limitation not only reduces CDR potential but also intensifies the impact of seaweed cultivation on phytoplankton (Fig. 5). Phytoplankton responses are highly variable, with production declines in some regions and increases in others due to a shift in nutrient co-limitation. These changes are likely to cascade through marine ecosystems, disrupting trophic structures and affecting zooplankton, fish, and other organisms reliant on phytoplankton, the foundation of the marine food web19. Resulting shifts in community composition and productivity could lead to broader ecological and socioeconomic impacts, including changes in biodiversity and fishery yields. Furthermore, processes not represented in this modeling framework could further affect ecosystem dynamics and CDR potential. In particular, oxygen feedbacks20 and the potential introduction of nearshore microbial and benthic communities to offshore environments may induce fundamental shifts in oceanic ecosystems6.

Our simulations highlight the complexity of identifying optimal regions for ocean afforestation. While seaweed cultivation is often assumed to have high CDR potential due to the vast habitable areas within EEZs21, accounting for biogeochemical feedbacks, nutrient limitation, and phytoplankton impacts, is required to identify favorable deployment regions. Simulated high afforestation potential under macronutrient limitation (Fig. 6) aligns with production hotspots identified by Arzeno-Soltero et al.14. However, exceptions were noted in the North Pacific and Atlantic, where our model simulations showed lower production, likely due to surface nutrient biases in the NEMO-PISCES model (see Fig. S1 in Supplementary Materials). The Southern Ocean and upwelling zones emerged as favorable regions when only N and P limitation were considered, offering high production and CDR flux with minimal environmental impacts. These areas overlap with regions identified by He et al.22 as optimal for ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE), suggesting synergies between ocean-based CDR strategies in regions where air-sea CO2 transfer timescale exceeds surface residence time23. Iron limitation, however, drastically diminishes favorable regions and excludes key regions such as the Southern Ocean and upwelling systems. This underscores the challenge of optimizing production while maintaining high CDR efficiency and minimizing environmental impacts, with iron emerging as a key limitation in this paradigm. Although combining ocean iron fertilization with seaweed cultivation could help alleviate this limitation, it would entail another large-scale intervention, raising additional economic, logistical, and environmental concerns8. Indeed, after two decades of study, iron fertilization has been shown to cause widespread and potentially adverse ecological impacts17,24.

Sensitivity to nutrient demand and affinity

Nutrient affinity plays a critical role in modulating the afforestation potential by determining seaweed-phytoplankton nutrient competition dynamics. Previous studies found that nutrient affinity variability had a limited influence on afforestation potential compared to other biophysical constraints but did not explicitly account for competition with phytoplankton14. Incorporating this competition, we demonstrate that nutrient affinity, particularly iron affinity, substantially affects afforestation potential.

Our projections also highlight the role of nutrient demand in determining the efficacy of seaweed cultivation-based CDR (Fig. 7). For seaweed to uptake more carbon than phytoplankton using the same available nutrient pool, it must be more nutrient-efficient. Any carbon uptake advantage depends on how much lower seaweed nutrient demand is compared to phytoplankton11, as well as on the proportion of nutrients that remain unused by the BCP. Together, these factors determine overall CDR efficiency. Lower nutrient demands result in fewer nutrients being diverted from the BCP, and a smaller BCP feedback (Fig. 7B, C). Variability in P demand has the most substantial effect on CDR efficiency, causing uncertainties ranging from −70% to 61% in global efficiency, followed by iron and N. In comparison, Wu et al.13 found that a 20% increase or decrease in N demand leads to a 10% change in global CDR efficiency during continuous cultivation on a centennial scale. In contrast, our analysis using published stoichiometric ratios revealed a much larger range, with an increase of 800% and a reduction of 77% in N demand, resulting in a decrease of 45% and an increase of 10% in CDR efficiency, respectively.

This reveals a fundamental trade-off: maximizing CDR efficiency requires low nutrient demand and high nutrient affinity, yet low seaweed nutrient demand is typically found in nutrient-poor environments11, which inherently limits seaweed production potential. Optimizing seaweed cultivation for climate mitigation requires a careful balance between maximizing CDR efficiency and generating substantial seaweed biomass. Future efforts should carefully consider these trade-offs when developing effective and sustainable seaweed-based strategies.

Our findings underscore the need for Fe demand and affinity to be included in projections of seaweed-based CDR. Failing to account for such nutrient dynamics is likely to result in erroneous estimates of the potential of seaweed-based CDR as a mitigation strategy.

Methods

Model

Simulations were performed using version 3.6 of the Nucleus for European Modelling of the Ocean (NEMO), which integrates the Louvain-La-Neuve Sea Ice Model (LIM) version 325 and the Pelagic Interaction Scheme for Carbon and Ecosystem Studies (PISCES) biogeochemical model version 226. The PISCES model incorporates various forms of carbon, including dissolved inorganic carbon, dissolved organic carbon, particulate inorganic carbon (calcite), and particulate organic carbon, as well as living compartments such as nanophytoplankton, diatoms, microzooplankton, and mesozooplankton. It also includes total alkalinity and essential marine nutrients such as nitrate, ammonium, phosphate, silicate, and iron. The PISCES model represents three sources of dissolved iron: dust deposition, riverine input, hydrothermal vents, and sediment resuspension. Scavenging is computed following Parekh et al.27, with scavenging rates dependent on particle concentrations. The model includes a representation of colloidal losses of dissolved Fe via the aggregation of dissolved organic material, as described by Aumont et al.26. It assumes a constant ligand concentration and employs a quota approach for Fe stoichiometry in organic matter, with the regeneration efficiency of particulate Fe dependent on stoichiometry. PISCES includes two sizes of particulate Fe pools28. Air-sea CO2 fluxes follow the protocols of the Ocean Model Intercomparison Project29, with gas exchange determined by the air-sea partial pressure gradient and an instantaneous gas transfer velocity parameterized based on 10 m atmospheric wind speed30,31.

NEMO-PISCES simulations were performed using a 2∘ global ocean configuration (ORCA2). This resolution limits the representation of fine-scale coastal dynamics and local nutrient inputs; however, it enabled centennial-scale simulations that extensively explore the parameter space of seaweed nutrient demand and affinity.

Simulations

Seaweed production

Seaweed production in NEMO-PISCES is simulated as the uptake of DIC and nutrients in EEZs. EEZ definitions are based on the Maritime Boundaries Geodatabase, version 11 (Flanders Marine Institute, 2019, available at https://www.marineregions.org). Production is continuous and is considered immediately harvested and permanently sequestered with no associated carbon emissions. Consequently, any remineralization losses are unaccounted for, and the resulting estimates represents of CDR potential are likely high-biased. Seaweed production is temperature, light, and nutrient-limited, but explicit seaweed biomass is not represented (see Supplementary Materials for detailed production functions). These simulations, which incorporate mean, minimal, and maximal values of stoichiometric ratios (demand) and half-saturation constants (affinity) reported in the literature, provide insights into how variations in these biological parameters influence the production potential, the CDR efficiency and the impact on phytoplankton. While it may be unrealistic for global seaweed cultivation to consistently adopt the extreme C:N:P:Fe ratios and half-saturation constants, exploring such scenarios permits the assessment of the sensitivity of afforestation potential and CDR efficiency to these parameters at both global and regional scales.

Evaluation of simulated nutrient fields

PISCES exhibits no systematic bias in upper ocean nitrate, phosphate, and Fe concentrations, performing well in reproducing large-scale nutrient distributions26. Among the FeMIP models, PISCES demonstrates one of the highest correlation values for dissolved Fe concentrations (PISCES1 in Tagliabue et al.28), indicating reasonable agreement with observational datasets. However, discrepancies remain in certain regions. The largest biases for nitrate and phosphate are found in the North Pacific, with root mean square errors (RMSE) of up to 8.5 mmol m−3 for nitrate and 0.8 mmol m−3 for phosphate (Fig. S1 in Supplementary Materials). For dissolved iron, the most significant deviations occur in the Arctic and North Atlantic, with RMSE of up to 5 μmol m−3 (Fig. S1 in Supplementary Materials).

Scenarios

Seaweed cultivation was performed for 25 years (2025–2050), followed by a subsequent 50-year period without any cultivation (2050–2100). We ran offline biogeochemical model simulations with ocean physics derived from the ESM IPSL-CM5A-LR. The atmospheric CO2 concentration is prescribed with the Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) 2.6 scenario, a low-emission scenario. Under this scenario, atmospheric CO2 concentration peaks at 443 ppm in 2052 and decreases to 421 ppm by 2100.
