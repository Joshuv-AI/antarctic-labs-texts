INTRODUCTION

“Autoimmune epilepsy” (referred to more accurately herein as seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology) has gained significant attention in recent years, leading to the proposal of two conceptual definitions by the international league against epilepsy (ILAE) autoimmunity task force: acute symptomatic seizures secondary to autoimmune encephalitis and autoimmune‐associated epilepsy. 1 The latter was subsequently refined to autoimmune encephalitis‐associated epilepsy, to reflect the inextricable link between encephalitis and the development of epilepsy in all cases. 2 This distinction reflects whether seizures stem from a reversible provoking factor (i.e., immunotherapy‐responsive neuroinflammation) or an irreversible enduring predisposition (e.g., due to structural injury as sequelae of neuroinflammation), both of which have important implications for diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. 3 , 4 There is the potential for a continuum between these two entities, challenging clinicians to refine their diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. 2 , 5

Seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology (also known as epilepsy of immune etiology, as per ILAE classification 6 ) as a whole encompasses distinct syndromes identified through specific clinical features, antibody profiles, and diagnostic tools. While an autoimmune etiology may be suspected based on the clinical presentation (i.e., seizure semiologies, movement disorders, other neuropsychiatric manifestations), confirmation is recommended through the detection of neural antibody testing. This should be performed in line with best testing practices, including with incorporation of tissue‐based assay alongside monospecific tests such as line blot or cell‐based assay when appropriate. 7 , 8 However, neural antibody testing is not available at every institution, and the sensitivity and specificity may vary depending on testing methodologies and algorithms as well as whether serum or CSF is submitted. Prompt antibody‐specific diagnosis are imperative because they inform seizure management across acute and chronic disease stages and may enable timely immunotherapy initiation. Delayed treatment has been associated with persistent seizures, cognitive impairment and other neurologic complications including status epilepticus. 9 , 10 Numerous tools have been developed to help select patients with seizures of unknown etiology for neural antibody testing. Examples of predictive scores include the APE2 score, 11 which has been suggested for use primarily in patients with subacute onset of seizures along with other features indicative of encephalitis, as well as the ACES score, 12 which has been suggested for use primarily in patients with more chronic seizures in relative isolation. Despite substantial advancements in the approach to seizures or epilepsy of suspected autoimmune etiology, there remains considerable variability in their diagnosis and management worldwide. Disparities in knowledge and resources persist across regions, hindering optimal patient care. For example, a prior survey 13 in Western China revealed considerable limitations in knowledge about autoimmune encephalitis among neurologists, with notable barriers to treatment. Furthermore, a worldwide survey of neurologists assessing their approach to autoimmune encephalitis through two specific clinical scenarios demonstrated variability in practice patterns and access to diagnostic tests across different regions. 14

Therefore, given the aforementioned challenges, we aimed to assess the global understanding of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, explore variability in diagnostic and treatment practices across regions and healthcare settings, and identify barriers to recognition and management, including access to specialized testing. The findings will help guide efforts to standardize clinical approaches, develop targeted educational initiatives, and enhance patient care in this evolving field of neurology.

METHODS

Study design

A local steering committee (MNN, RW, AB) was formed to develop the survey questions, drawing on clinical expertise in diagnosing and treating seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, as well as a review of the current literature. Feedback was incorporated to refine survey questions, improving clarity and relevance while ensuring appropriate face and content validity. The survey was available in English language only. The finalized survey was then prepared for distribution to the national and international medical community.

Titled “Recognizing Autoimmune Seizures,” the survey was designed to collect information on practice variability, access to diagnostic testing, and familiarity with diagnosing and managing seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology. It was divided into three sections focusing on (1) demographics and professional background, (2) knowledge of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology including diagnostic criteria and clinical features, and (3) clinical practice patterns including access to testing and treatment approaches (Supplementary Appendix).

Study design and ethics approval

The survey was distributed to neurologists and healthcare professionals worldwide using purposive sampling. Invitations were shared through established networks, including the Canadian League Against Epilepsy (CLAE), the Young Epilepsy Section (YES) of the ILAE, and newsletters or mailing lists from organizations such as the Canadian Neurological Science Foundation (CNSF), Canadian Pediatric Epilepsy network (CPEN), American Epilepsy Society (AES), and various ILAE chapters. Snowball sampling was also employed, where initial respondents were encouraged to share the survey link with colleagues.

The survey was hosted on Qualtrics (www.qualtrics.com) and was open for responses from June 2023 to May 2024. Reminders were sent periodically to boost participation. The survey was voluntary, and no remuneration was provided. It was estimated to take 10–15 min to complete. Participants could respond anonymously or submit their contact information for future collaborations.

The study involved voluntary, anonymous survey responses from healthcare professionals only, with no patient data collected. This study was considered to be a Quality Improvement project and as a result, Research Ethics Board approval was not warranted.

Data analysis

Responses were collected via Qualtrics and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29. Descriptive analyses were performed, with percentage values reported for categorical variables. Chi‐squared tests were used for comparisons between two independent categorical variables. Partial responses were included in the analysis. For tables with expected cell counts less than five, Fisher's exact test was applied as an alternative. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For each question, the number of responses was recorded, and percentages were calculated based on the total number of responses to each specific question. Planned analyses compared response patterns across geographic regions and patient populations using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Aggregate data were summarized by patient population and region of practice. Due to small sample sizes and similarities in healthcare resources and testing access, Asia, South America, Central America, and other regions were grouped together into one category (n = 56, 36%), whereas Europe (n = 62, 40%) and North America (n = 36, 23%) were analyzed as separate regions. The data sets used and/or analyzed during this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

RESULTS

Respondent characteristics

A total of 180 surveys were initiated, of which 157 participants answered beyond the first question regarding their country of practice, forming the final sample size. Among respondents, 57% (n = 90) practiced in adult medicine, and 40% (n = 63) practiced in pediatric medicine. Geographically, respondents practiced primarily in Europe (40%; n = 62), North America (23%; n = 36), and Asia (17%; n = 27), followed by South America (8%; n = 13), Central America (7%; n = 11), and other regions (3%; n = 5) (Table 1). Most respondents had encountered fewer than 10 patients with autoimmune‐associated epilepsy (55%; n = 63) or acute symptomatic seizures secondary to autoimmune encephalitis (42%; n = 63). Only 4% (n = 5) and 6% (n = 9) had managed more than 50 cases in either category, respectively (Table 2). Respondents reported that positive neural antibody testing, at least once, resulted in management changes (82%; n = 102), initiation of targeted therapies (66%; n = 82), diagnostic closure (54%; n = 68), and reduced investigations (25%; n = 31) (Table 2).

Clinician familiarity with ILAE terminology and diagnostic tools

Among 154 respondents, 69% (n = 106) were familiar with updated ILAE definitions, and among these, 73% (n = 74) found the distinctions clinically impactful. Conversely, 43% (n = 61) reported poor recognition of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, primarily due to insufficient education (47%; n = 28) and novelty of the concept (32%; n = 19) (Table 2). Familiarity with ILAE terminology varied significantly by region (p = 0.024) and was highest in Europe (76%) and Asia, South/Central America/Other regions (73%), compared with North America (50%). In contrast, familiarity did not differ significantly by patient population (p = 0.067) (Figure 1A,B). Nearly half of respondents (49%; n = 73) were unfamiliar with any tools to select patients with seizures of unknown etiology for neural antibody testing (Table 2), and pediatric clinicians were significantly less familiar than adult clinicians (68% vs. 36% unfamiliar; p < 0.001) (Figure 1C).

Regional variations in access to neural antibody testing

Overall, 91% of respondents had access to neural antibody testing: 10% to individual antibody tests (n = 16), 45% (n = 70) to panel‐based testing, and 36% (n = 57) to both. As illustrated in Figure 2A, access to neural antibody testing differed substantially across regions (p < 0.001). Respondents in North America most often reported availability of both individual and panel‐based testing (46%), whereas Europe predominantly relied on panel‐based testing (65%). In contrast, clinicians in Asia, South/Central America, and other regions had markedly lower access to either modality: only 39% (n = 21) have access to both modalities, with panel‐based testing used slightly more often (26%; n = 14) than individual tests (22%; n = 12). Use of individual tests remained low even in Europe and North America (5% vs. 3%).

Barriers to access neural antibody testing

Thirty‐five percent (n = 52) of respondents reported difficulty accessing neural antibody testing, with rates significantly higher in Asia, South/Central America and other regions (66%) compared with North America (22%) and Europe (17%) (p < 0.001). Financial barriers were the main obstacle in these regions (87%; n = 45; p < 0.001) (Figure 2B). Public funding was the predominant funding source in Europe (79%; n = 42) and North America (64%; n = 18), but was far less common in Asia, South/Central America and other regions (27%; n = 10), where families (32%; n = 12) and private insurance (19%; n = 7) more commonly covered costs (p < 0.001) (Figure 2C). The diagnostic yield of neural antibody testing, however, was comparable across regions (p = 0.723), with respondents most commonly reporting a yield of <10% (Figure 2E).

Difficulty accessing testing influenced clinical practice. Clinicians in Asia, South/Central America, and other regions were significantly more likely to treat empirically with immunotherapy (72%; n = 38; p < 0.001), whereas North American respondents more frequently referred to subspecialists (37%; n = 11; p = 0.003) (Figure 2D).

Consideration of neural antibody testing

The majority of respondents reported they would consider neural antibody testing in patients presenting with seizures accompanied by broader neurological features indicative of encephalitis. Testing was most commonly considered in cases of seizures with additional neurological dysfunction (88%, n = 128), febrile infection‐related epilepsy syndrome (FIRES) (88%, n = 129), and autoimmune encephalitis‐associated epilepsy (77%, n = 112) among others. An autoimmune etiology was also frequently considered in those with drug‐resistant focal (temporal, 57%; n = 83) or extratemporal localization (47%; n = 69) but less commonly in generalized epilepsy (19%; n = 28) (Table S1).

Diagnostic patterns and antibody prevalence

On review of final diagnosis in patients evaluated for suspected seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, adult and pediatric providers reported comparable case ascertainment for acute symptomatic seizures secondary to autoimmune encephalitis (predominantly anti‐NMDAR), antibody‐negative autoimmune encephalitis, autoimmune encephalitis–associated epilepsy, and Rasmussen's encephalitis. Most respondents in both groups reported experience with fewer than 10 patients and only a minority indicated managing more than 10 cases (Table S2). Adult providers reported greater exposure to anti‐GAD65–associated epilepsy (p = 0.038), most commonly managing fewer than 10 cases, whereas pediatric providers more often reported no exposure. Conversely, pediatric providers more frequently diagnosed seizures related to anti‐MOG (p < 0.001) (Figure 3A), where a greater proportion reported managing more than 10 patients (Table S2).

Antibody identification patterns also differed by patient population and clinical exposure. A higher proportion of adult providers reported identifying anti‐GAD65 (73% vs. 52%, p = 0.014), anti‐LGI1 (83% vs. 36%, p < 0.001), anti‐CASPR2 (62% vs. 27%, p < 0.001), anti‐GABA(B) receptor (40% vs. 19%, p = 0.015), and high‐risk paraneoplastic onconeural antibodies (79% vs. 31%, p < 0.001). These differences were driven in part by higher patient volumes among adult clinicians, with a greater proportion reporting management of more than 10 patients for anti‐LGI1, anti‐CASPR2, anti‐GAD65, and paraneoplastic antibodies (Table S3). Conversely, a greater proportion of pediatric clinicians reported identifying anti‐MOG antibodies (84% vs. 58%, p = 0.001, Figure 3B; Table S3).

Therapeutic consideration and prognostic evaluation

Standard first‐line immunotherapy as laid out on the survey was highly utilized, with nearly universal adoption in both pediatric and adult populations. Second‐line treatment strategies showed notable variation between groups. Pediatric providers reported significantly higher use of second‐line intravenous immunotherapy (25% vs. 11%, p = 0.029). Adult clinicians were far more likely to use cyclophosphamide as a second‐line option (24% vs. 3%, p < 0.001). These values reflect the percentage of respondents choosing or willing to choose cyclophosphamide, and do not indicate how often cyclophosphamide is used in practice. Adult respondents were also more likely to select combined multimodal approaches as second‐line options (p = 0.043) (Figure 3C,D).

Prognosis impressions varied by antibody type. A higher proportion of respondents encountered drug‐resistant epilepsy as the prognosis in patients with high‐titer anti‐GAD65 antibodies (88%; n = 60) and high‐risk paraneoplastic antibodies against onconeural antigens (74%; n = 36). In contrast, acute symptomatic seizure course was more commonly identified as the usual prognosis in patients with anti‐NMDAR antibodies (64%; n = 66), anti‐MOG antibodies (75%; n = 45), and anti‐LGI1 antibodies (59%; n = 39) (Figure 3E, Table S4).

DISCUSSION

This global survey provides the first comprehensive assessment of how seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology are recognized, diagnosed, and managed across diverse healthcare systems, revealing substantial variability in familiarity, access to testing, and clinical practice. Key observations include a significant knowledge‐to‐practice gap despite high conceptual familiarity, marked regional disparities in diagnostic pathways, and uniformly similar diagnostic yields across regions despite differences in testing access. Financial barriers predominated in resource‐limited settings, whereas higher subspecialist referral patterns were observed in resource‐rich countries. In addition, familiarity with clinical decision tools was limited, particularly among pediatric clinicians. These findings have important implications for context‐specific education, resource allocation, and clinical practice worldwide.

Most of the survey's respondents reported familiarity with the updated ILAE definitions for seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, 1 however, this familiarity did not consistently translate into confident clinical recognition. Clinicians cited insufficient education of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology concepts as key challenges. This familiarity‐recognition gap aligns with prior observations that navigating various clinical syndromes of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology poses major challenges due to heterogeneous presentations and evolving terminology. 15 , 16 Nearly half of respondents had encountered fewer than 10 patients with seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, and only 4%–6% had managed more than 50 cases in either category, even among academic clinicians. While limited exposure may contribute to under‐recognition, the possibility that this low encounter rate reflects the rarity of an autoimmune etiology for seizures of unknown etiology in outpatient epilepsy clinics must be considered. 12 , 17

Regional differences in familiarity and experience were notable. North American respondents demonstrated the lowest familiarity with the ILAE terminology concepts, likely reflecting greater reliance on subspecialty referrals (37%, p = 0.003). This pattern is consistent with documented North American neurologists' higher referral rates to specialists. 18 Lower clinical exposure, such as managing fewer than five autoimmune encephalitis cases per year, has been associated with reduced use of comprehensive antibody testing and empiric immunotherapy. 14 This may promote healthcare silos, which have been described to limit knowledge dissemination and uptake of best practice guidelines 19 particularly in rare conditions such as seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology.

Beyond familiarity, the survey highlighted significant regional disparities in access to neural antibody testing. Respondents in North America and Europe reported consistent access, whereas clinicians in resource‐limited regions faced major financial constraints, limited public healthcare funding, and infrastructural barriers. These disparities reflect socioeconomic inequities in epilepsy care, where few centers in low‐ and middle‐income countries have reliable access to essential diagnostics. 20 Limited testing access led to empirical immunotherapy use, particularly in Asia and South/Central America (72%; p < 0.001), underlining adaptive practice but raising concerns about overtreatment, missed diagnostic precision, and the need for better prognostic counseling. Despite the observed differences in access and practice patterns, the yield of neural antibody testing was reported to be comparable across regions (p = 0.723) (Figure 2E). Approximately 50% reported positivity rates below 10%, closely mirroring the published 7.6% (95% CI: 4.6%–11.2%) pooled prevalence of neural autoantibodies observed in unselected epilepsy cohorts of unknown etiology. 21 These uniformly low yields reinforce the importance of high‐pretest‐probability screening to optimize antibody‐testing utility. Higher yields are observed in well‐defined clinical subgroups such as those with FBDS, which is virtually pathognomonic for anti‐LGI1. 22

Familiarity with tools to select patients with seizures of unknown etiology for neural antibody testing was limited overall, with nearly half of respondents (49%) reporting no familiarity with any listed tools. Pediatric clinicians were less familiar than adult clinicians (68% vs. 36% unfamiliar; p < 0.001), likely reflecting that these tools were primarily developed in adult populations, and some have shown limited specificity in children (22.2%). 23 Additionally, pediatric‐adapted tools such as P‐APE2 and PAPS remain newly validated and not widely disseminated. 23 , 24 Children can also present differently, more often with subacute development of seizures, movement disorders, and encephalopathy and subtle behavioral changes as opposed to the relatively more insidious presentations of neuropsychiatric symptoms with seizures that may be observed in some adult cases. 25 Antibody prevalences may also differ (e.g., anti‐MOG and anti‐NMDAR are more often seen in pediatrics, whereas anti‐GAD65 is more often seen in adults), 26 which was also confirmed by this survey. These differences highlight the need for age‐appropriate diagnostic approaches and broader dissemination of pediatric‐adapted scoring systems.

Neural antibody testing was most frequently pursued in those with acute presentations, such as FIRES, NORSE, and postencephalitic epilepsy, rather than in those with chronic drug‐resistant epilepsies where the yield has been reported to be very low. 12 , 17 Despite this, approximately 50% of respondents routinely consider neural antibody testing in those with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy which, in the absence of other features suspicious for neural antibody positivity, is likely to result in a low yield of testing among a relatively common presentation to outpatient epilepsy clinics. Furthermore, one‐fifth of respondents also routinely consider this testing in those with generalized epilepsy, a group that has been excluded from studies of neural antibody positivity due to the extremely low yield. Taken together, these findings indicate the need for clinical education on phenotypes that are suspicious for neural antibody positivity, in order to optimize test yield and utilization.

Prognostic impressions also varied by antibody type. Drug‐resistant epilepsy was identified as the typical outcome for anti‐GAD65 by 88% of respondents, consistent with refractory outcomes reported in the literature. 27 In contrast, acute symptomatic seizures were most reported as the usual prognosis for anti‐NMDAR (64%; n = 66) and anti‐MOG (75%; n = 45), aligning with literature showing generally favorable long‐term outcomes. 28 These distinctions have direct implications for immunotherapy intensity and future counseling. 29 Recognizing phenotype‐antibody correlations remains essential for accurate diagnosis and tailored therapy across diverse patient populations. 30 Yet, without access to testing, a barrier affecting 87% of clinicians in resource‐limited regions, these prognostic distinctions remain clinically unattainable in suspicious cases. Equitable access to antibody testing is therefore central to achieving true precision medicine in seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As this was a voluntary survey, selection bias may have favored respondents with either greater interest or limited experience in seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, making it difficult to determine whether participation bias inflated or diluted familiarity levels. The majority of respondents reported limited direct experience, raising uncertainty about whether clinicians with less exposure were more likely to participate or whether this distribution genuinely reflects the broader clinical landscape, where seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology remains rare in outpatient epilepsy clinics. Self‐reported estimates of case volumes and testing yields are also subject to recall bias. Geographic representation was uneven without equal representation globally. Nonetheless, inclusion of clinicians across diverse backgrounds provides a valuable snapshot of current global practice, though the modest sample size (n = 157) constrains regional comparisons. Our survey was only carried out in English, which systematically excluded non‐English speaking clinicians, particularly those practicing in community‐based and resource‐limited settings in Asia, Africa, Latin America, and Eastern Europe, regions where diagnostic disparities are likely most pronounced. The fact that 79% of our respondents were from academic centers reflects this selection bias: academic institutions typically have greater English proficiency, international connectivity, and participation in global professional networks (ILAE, AES, CLAE) through which we recruited.

Our global survey highlights distinct regional barriers. In resource‐rich regions, challenges center on education and care coordination, whereas resource‐limited regions face fundamental gaps in diagnostic infrastructure and affordability. Regional surveys echo these trends: Turkish neurologists reported limited laboratory support (83.7%) and guideline gaps (88.2%) despite high familiarity, 31 whereas in Western China, financial cost (80%–85%) and restricted antibody access (75%–80%) were the most common barriers. 32 These findings suggest that effective interventions require differentiated, context‐specific strategies rather than a universal, one‐size‐fits‐all approach. High‐resource settings need education and system integration, whereas low‐resource settings require investment in infrastructure, test availability, and affordability. 33 , 34 Important to note, this survey did not systematically review access to diagnostic infrastructure disparities in the assessment of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology that in clinical practice extend beyond neural antibody testing. These include neuroimaging, CSF analysis capability, and prolonged video‐EEG monitoring, barriers that collectively can impede early recognition and treatment in resource‐limited settings.

Future directions

Moving forward, core strategic priorities emerge from this survey. First, improved clinician recognition of features suspicious for neural antibody testing is mandatory to avoid indiscriminate testing in low‐probability scenarios. This can be established by specialized training tracks, both for adults and pediatrics, and implementing well‐established telemedicine consultation networks such as the ECHO (Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes) model to optimize case selection for testing and build regional hubs. These initiatives can be supported through specialty interest groups within the ILAE and AES. Second, advocacy for policy changes to expand public funding coverage for neural antibody testing in patients who have seizures with features demonstrably suspicious for neural antibody positivity, alongside reduced‐cost testing models and the development of regional testing hubs, is critical to achieving diagnostic equity, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries where out‐of‐pocket costs remain a prohibitive barrier. Third, development of age‐specific and regionally tailored practice guidelines is required for work up of seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology, encompassing both diagnostic pathways and management recommendations aligned with local resource availability. Pediatric‐focused antibody testing recommendations, for example, should reflect distinct pediatric antibody patterns, such as higher rates of anti‐MOG and anti‐NMDAR compared with adults, to support targeted resource allocation across diverse healthcare settings. Only through such multifaceted interventions can the recognized diagnostic gap in autoimmune‐associated epilepsy be meaningfully narrowed (Table 3).

CONCLUSION

This survey highlights that closing the diagnostic gap in seizures or epilepsy of autoimmune etiology requires region‐specific, multipronged strategies: expanding educational initiatives and reducing healthcare silos in resource‐rich settings, while simultaneously investing in diagnostic infrastructure and reducing financial barriers in resource‐limited regions, all unified by the broader dissemination of evidence‐based, phenotype‐specific clinical decision tools validated across diverse patient populations and practice settings. An optimal global model would integrate the adaptability of low‐resource systems with the diagnostic precision of high‐resource environments, supported by regional hub networks and point‐of‐care decision tools to bridge these disparities.
