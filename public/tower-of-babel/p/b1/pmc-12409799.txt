Real-world management of blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm at an academic center with a broad regional referral base
Authors: Nathan M. Krah, Rasmus Hoeg, Paul J. Shami, Shannon E. Elf, Lauren E. Lee, Naveen Pemmaraju, Ami B. Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BPDCN is an aggressive myeloid malignancy characterized by unique expression of CD123, CD4, CD56, CD303, CD304, TCL1 and TCF4. The development of tagraxofusp, a CD123-directed cytotoxin, has revolutionized BPDCN treatment, especially for patients unfit for chemotherapy. While most patients respond to frontline tagraxofusp, there are challenges associated with treatment. In this case series, we describe our institutional experience treating BPDCN both pre- and post-tagraxofusp approval. We summarize six cases, the majority of which occurred in elderly patients, and highlight unique challenges of treating BPDCN at a center that serves a large rural/frontier area with variable access to specialty care.

Blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN) is a rare hematologic malignancy with a median survival of 8–16 months [[1], [2], [3]]. The majority of BPDCN patients are male and Caucasian [3]. The average age at diagnosis is 65 years, but there is a bimodal distribution [[3], [4], [5]]. Cutaneous disease is the most common manifestation, though bone marrow, blood, lymph node, and central nervous system (CNS) involvement are also frequent [1,6].

BPDCN was first recognized as an independent entity in 2017 but shares hallmarks with other myeloid neoplasms [[7], [8], [9]]. The cell-of-origin is a myeloid-biased hematopoietic stem cell, and BPDCN can co-occur with other hematological malignancies, most notably chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) [[10], [11], [12]]. BPDCN cells express CD123, CD4 and CD56 but lack lineage-defining markers. Molecular abnormalities resemble those in other myeloid malignancies [1,13,14], but also encompass unique variants regulating NF-κB signaling and inflammation.

Historically, BPDCN was treated with multiagent chemotherapy modified from acute leukemia and lymphoma regimens [15]. Tagraxofusp, a CD123-directed recombinant cytotoxin consisting of human interleukin-3 fused to truncated diphtheria toxin, has revolutionized BPDCN treatment, especially for patients ineligible for chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Based on results from the pivotal STML-401–0114 (NCT02113982) trial, tagraxofusp became the first FDA-approved therapy for BPDCN [6]. Since 2018, tagraxofusp has become standard-of-care for BPDCN, utilized as a bridge to transplant in fit patients and as a durable disease control option in unfit patients. Although tagraxofusp has fewer side effects than chemotherapy, it can cause life-threatening capillary leak syndrome (CLS) [6].

We review six BPDCN patients treated at our institution in the Intermountain West, in which 25 % of the catchment population resides in rural/frontier areas.

Case 1 (pre-tagraxofusp approval)

A 57-year-old Hispanic male presented with diffuse rash and skin biopsy was diagnostic for BPDCN. Bone marrow biopsy (BMBx) was negative, but cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed involvement. He was treated with cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, methotrexate (Methotrexate), cytarabine (Cytarabine) and dexamethasone (Hyper-CVAD). He obtained complete remission after two cycles of Hyper-CVAD and nine doses of IT chemotherapy. The patient underwent an allogeneic HSCT from a matched unrelated donor after conditioning with cyclophosphamide, anti-thymocyte globulin, and total body irradiation with a 6 Gy cranial radiation boost. He remains in excellent health without evidence of BPDCN recurrence 8.5 years after HSCT.

Case 2 (pre-tagraxofusp approval)

A 17-year-old African American male presented with a 6 cm left thigh mass and inguinal adenopathy. Pathologic evaluation revealed BPDCN. BMBx and CSF analysis were negative. The patient received induction therapy using the Cancer and Leukemia Group B 10,403 regimen [16]. He completed remission induction and remission consolidation (through day 29) but developed methotrexate (Methotrexate) leukoencephalopathy. He later underwent carmustine, etoposide (Etoposide), cytarabine (Cytarabine) and melphalan (BEAM) conditioning and autologous HSCT. His post-transplant course was remarkable for residual neuropathy. He was in remission for >5 years post-transplant but later passed away of unrelated causes.

Case 3 (post-tagraxofusp approval)

An 87-year-old Caucasian male with vascular dementia presented to dermatology clinic with a 2-month history of widespread erythematous skin lesions (Fig. 1A and B) and acute-on-chronic confusion. Skin biopsy confirmed BPDCN. Workup demonstrated bone marrow, nodal, and CNS involvement. He was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for initiation of cycle 1 (C1) tagraxofusp. Given the patient’s baseline frailty, the duration of C1 was shortened to 3 days. He had evidence of tumor lysis, and his skin disease responded briskly to treatment. He received eight total doses of IT chemotherapy, clearing after the fifth treatment. He was admitted after C2 for altered mental status (AMS). Given his baseline dementia and concomitant CNS disease, determining whether his AMS was related to his underlying dementia or due to a treatment-related toxicity was difficult. No clear cause was identified. Tagraxofusp was discontinued and he succumbed to his disease within a month on hospice care.Fig. 1Characteristic skin findings and organomegaly associated with BPDCN. Case 4 (post-tagraxofusp approval)

An 86-year-old Caucasian male noted painless erythematous nodules on his face (Fig. 1C), chest (Fig. 1D) and oral mucosa and gingival biopsy revealed BPDCN. PET-CT scan was negative, but BMBx demonstrated BPDCN involvement with several molecular abnormalities (Table 1). CSF was negative. He was admitted to the ICU for C1 tagraxofusp. Despite prophylactic albumin infusions, he developed CLS requiring additional albumin, furosemide and methylprednisolone. He received all 5 doses of tagraxofusp with C1 and his cutaneous disease responded rapidly. C1 was complicated by line-associated deep vein thrombosis and subsequent hospitalization for fluid overload and pulmonary embolism. C2 of tagraxofusp was delayed allowing for recovery; but there was also difficulty coordinating care with a Nevada-based cancer center due to absent pump equipment, unfamiliarity with tagraxofusp administration and toxicity management. C2 was ultimately administered at our center and cycles were shortened to 3 days every 4 weeks given the travel distance. The patient reported back pressure and sensation of doom with C2 prompting slower infusion with symptom resolution and negative cardiac work-up. He completed 8 doses of prophylactic IT chemotherapy. Care was transferred to a closer academic center following C4. The patient experienced progressive disease with C7 and the number of tagraxofusp doses was increased from 3 to 5, resulting in disease control until C10. Treatment was switched to azacitidine 75 mg/m2 x 5 days and venetoclax 100 mg daily (given with voriconazole) for 14 days of a 28-day cycle. He had disease control through 6 cycles. Shortly thereafter, he experienced a mechanical fall and passed away.

Case 5 (post-tagraxofusp approval)

A 79-year-old Caucasian male presented to the dermatology clinic with an erythematous plaque on his right forearm. Skin biopsy revealed BPDCN. Bone marrow, PET-CT scan and CSF were negative. Mutation panel revealed multiple mutations (Table 1). He had persistent absolute and relative monocytosis at the time of his BPDCN diagnosis. C1 tagraxofusp was complicated by CLS following dose 3. He went on to receive all 5 doses. After 7 cycles BMBx revealed no evidence of BPDCN. He underwent autologous HSCT with BEAM conditioning. Post-transplant BMBx showed no evidence of BPDCN. He continues to have persistent absolute and relative monocytosis with persistence of multiple mutations, raising the possibility of an unappreciated CMML at diagnosis. Five years after transplant, he remains in remission.

Case 6 (post- tagraxofusp approval)

A 57-year-old healthy Caucasian woman presented with fatigue, weight loss, abdominal pain and cytopenias. Scans showed significant hepatosplenomegaly (Fig. 1E). BMBx revealed BPDCN with a small atypical CD117+ population and multiple mutations (Table 1). CSF analysis was positive. PET-CT demonstrated hypermetabolic splenomegaly. She started therapy with tagraxofusp, azacitidine and venetoclax on NCT03113643. BMBx at Day 21 showed no evidence of BPDCN with minimal residual disease negativity by flow cytometry. She received 12 doses of IT chemotherapy with clearance of the CNS. Following C2, BMBx revealed a hypocellular marrow and no evidence of BPDCN. She underwent HSCT from an 9/10 mismatched unrelated donor with myeloablative busulfan/fludarabine conditioning. She developed veno-occlusive disease (VOD) of the liver despite ursodiol and received defibrotide day +21 through day +35 with complete resolution of VOD. There was no evidence of disease on Day +90 BMBx with clearance of all molecular mutations. Like case 5, there was suspicion for concomitant CMML at diagnosis given her mutation profile, presence of an aberrant CD117+ myeloid blast population, and marked splenomegaly.

We describe our institutional experience treating BPDCN in the Intermountain West, which encompasses a wide catchment area comprised of rural/frontier regions with limited access to specialty care. This series contributes to the growing literature on BPDCN by highlighting real-world barriers to treatment across a broad geographic region, where coordination of tagraxofusp administration and management of its toxicities posed unique challenges. These experiences may be useful for providers coordinating care at resource-limited centers. Additionally, our report describes the successful use of consolidative autologous HSCT in two patients—an approach that is less commonly reported in the BPDCN literature. We also detail tagraxofusp dose modifications in elderly or frail patients and note two cases with suspected CMML overlap, contributing to ongoing discussions about clonal relationship between BPDCN and other myeloid neoplasms.

Since 2018, tagraxofusp has become a cornerstone treatment for BPDCN [6]. As highlighted in case 6, there are ongoing clinical trials testing tagraxofusp in combination with other agents. We describe obstacles associated with tagraxofusp administration due to geographic constraints and disease rarity, including unfamiliarity with CLS management, and inaccessibility of equipment. Cases 4 and 5 experienced CLS during C1 tagraxofusp, underscoring the need to closely monitor tagraxofusp therapy and consider administering prophylactic albumin in vulnerable populations. Additionally, we consider shortening the number of infusion days or prolonging cycles depending on individual patient factors.

Four patients completed HSCT at our institution. Two patients received autologous HSCT and were disease-free for >5 years (Cases 2 and 5). Of the two patients who received allogeneic HSCT, one was disease-free for 8.5 years (Case 1), while the other is newly in remission (Case 6). HSCT remains the only curative option for BPDCN but has high morbidity and mortality. Potential predisposing factors for VOD in Case 6 include hepatosplenomegaly, transfusion-related iron overload, tagraxofusp therapy and the conditioning regimen.

BPDCN often co-occurs with CMML [10,11], as suspected in Cases 5 and 6. As the risk demographics for BPDCN and CMML overlap, consideration for shared diagnoses is crucial. While both autologous and allogeneic transplants can be performed as consolidative therapy, allogeneic HSCT is preferred with a co-existing hematologic malignancy.

Based on our described institutional experience and the available literature on BPDCN, we strongly recommend allogeneic HSCT as the preferred consolidative strategy in eligible patients whenever possible. Autologous HSCT may be a reasonable alternative consolidative option in select patients, such as those with skin-only disease, and we describe two such cases with durable remissions using this strategy. For treatment induction, we aim to administer at least 2 cycles of tagraxofusp prior to HSCT, with careful assessment of treatment response with bone marrow biopsy, LP and PET-CT. Dosing modifications and supportive care strategies should be tailored based on age, frailty, and comorbidities.

Tagraxofusp has emerged as an important treatment option for BPDCN; however, barriers to access and safe administration remain. Educational outreach and staff training are essential to mitigate risks, such as CLS. In regions where tagraxofusp is unavailable or cannot be administered safely, venetoclax-based regimens are emerging alternatives [1,5,17,18]. Recent reports have shown promising results using venetoclax in combination with proteasome inhibitors and dexamethasone in unfit patients [19]. Our series reinforces the need for flexible treatment strategies in real-world settings and highlights opportunities for improving access and delivery of care in BPDCN.
