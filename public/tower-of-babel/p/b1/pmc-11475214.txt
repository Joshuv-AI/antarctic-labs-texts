Phylogenomics and plastome evolution of Lithospermeae (Boraginaceae)
Authors: Maryam Noroozi, Farrokh Ghahremaninejad, Mehrshid Riahi, James I Cohen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLithospermeae is the largest tribe within Boraginaceae. The tribe has been the focus of multiple phylogenetic studies over the last 15 years, with most focused on one genus or a few genera. In the present study, we newly sequenced 69 species of Lithospermeae and relatives to analyze the phylogenomic relationships among its members as well as the evolution of the plastid genome.ResultsThe phylogeny of Lithospermeae resolved from the plastid genome and nrDNA cistron is generally congruent with prior studies, but is better resolved and supported. Increasing character sampling across the plastid genome results in gradually more similar trees to that from the entire plastid genome. Overall, plastid genome structure was quite consistent across Lithospermeae. Codon Usage Bias (CUB) analyses demonstrate that across Lithospermeae plastid genomes were rich in AT and poor in GC. Mutation may play a greater role than selection across the plastid genome of Lithospermeae. The present study is the first to highlight the CUB characteristics of Lithospermeae species, which can help elucidate the mechanisms underlying patterns of molecular evolution and improve the expression levels of exogenous genes by codon optimization.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive phylogenomic analysis of Lithospermeae, significantly enhancing our understanding of the phylogenetic relationships and plastid genome evolution within this largest tribe of Boraginaceae. By utilizing an expanded genomic sampling approach, we have achieved increased resolution and support among the evolutionary relationships of the tribe, in line with but improving upon previous studies. The analyses of plastid genome structure revealed consistency across Lithospermeae, with a notable CUB. This study marks the first investigation into the CUB of Lithospermeae species and sets the stage for further research on the molecular evolution of plastid genomes across Boraginaceae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05665-6.Keywords: Codon usage bias, Natural selection, Plastid genome, Phylogeny, Translational efficiency

Introduction

Lithospermeae, one of the major groups of Boraginaceae, comprises ca. 470 species of annual and perennial, shrubs, and subshrubs [1, 2] Most genera of Lithospermeae are restricted to the Irano-Turanian and Mediterranean regions, where the tribe is most diverse; however, a few genera are endemic to southern Africa (Lobostemon Lehm., Echiostachys Levyns) [3] or East Asia (Ancistrocarya Maxim., Maharanga DC.), while Lithospermum L. is most diverse in the Americas [1, 4, 5]. Delimitation of the tribe has been, at least during the past 70 years, fairly straightforward [6], with the tribe characterized by species that bear nutlets with basal attachment, lacking elaiosomes, and a calcified pericarp [4]; however, both generic and species delimitation have been challenging, which is also the case for phylogenetic relationships within the tribe [7].

The systematics of Lithospermeae have been explored in a range of studies. Johnston (1954) and Seibert (1978) were pioneers in studying the tribe [6, 8]. Over the past few decades, several phylogenetic studies have been published on Lithospermeae. The first molecular study described the island radiation of Echium L. in Macronesia [9, 10], and Buys (2006) examined the phylogeny of the southern African relative, Lobostemon Lehm., using morphological data [11]. Thomas et al. (2008) focused on the phylogeny of polyphyletic Lithodora Griseb. s.l. These authors recognized that Lithodora s.s. is nested in a clade comprising various monotypic Mediterranean taxa, such as Paramolktia Greuter & Burdet and Mairetis I.M. Johnst., a result also recovered by Cecchi and Selvi [12]. Another group of species formerly in Lithodora is allied with Lithospermum and Buglossoides Moench. Thomas et al. (2008) segregated this group into the genus Glandora D.C. Thomas, Weigend & Hilger [7]. The phylogeny of Lithospermum, the second largest Lithospermeae genus, has been the focus of several publications [13, 14], which demonstrated that multiple genera based on autapomorphic features were resolved as nested within Lithospermum. These results have been consistent across multiple studies, including Cohen [15] who used hundreds of DNA regions from across the nuclear genome to examine the phylogenetics, floral evolution, species diversification, and biogeography of the group. Other studies have investigated the phylogenetics of smaller genera, such as Arnebia Forssk. [16] and Buglossoides Moench [17].

Although the generic limits and intergeneric relationships of many taxa were recovered across multiple prior studies, a well-resolved tribal-level phylogeny has not been resolved. Indeed, attaining high resolution of the phylogenetic relationships within Lithospermeae is still problematic. For example, based on morphology Johnston [6] recognized the relationship between the Japanese endemic Ancistrocarya and Lithospermum. Recently, Ancistrocarya was identified within a clade with Lithospermum, Buglossoides, Aegonychon Gray, and Glandora [2], but this result was based on only one nuclear region (ITS, an nrDNA region that can be challenging for phylogeny reconstruction) [18, 19], thus more evidence would help in discerning this relationship.

With few exceptions, molecular phylogenetic analyses of Lithospermeae have been carried out using either individual or a small number of plastid DNA sequences (e.g., matK, trnL-F), limited nrDNA sequences (e.g., ITS) or a combination of both [10, 12, 20]. Altogether, about twenty markers have been employed to examine Lithospermeae phylogenetic relationships, the most popular being ITS and trnL-trnF. However, the number of regions used in any single study is much lower, frequently two or three. For instance, for retrieving Ancistrocarya within clade with Lithospermum and relatives, only ITS was employed [2], and this is also the case for phylogenetic relationships of Lithodora that were investigated with ITS and the trnLUAA intron [7]. The number of markers used has often proven to be a limitation at generic levels as taxonomists failed to provide well-supported trees (e.g., [21, 22]), and in Lithospermeae, this has hampered our understanding of the evolutionary relationships of the group and resulting taxonomic revisions. The availability of a robust phylogeny is requisite for accurately studying patterns of evolution of traits at different taxonomic levels (e.g., tribe, genus), which was the case, for example, investigating the patterns of floral evolution in Lithospermum [15].

One approach to addressing the limited sequence data associated with a small number of DNA regions for phylogenetic analysis is genome skimming [23], using shallow sequencing depth to acquire abundant regions of the plant genomes (e.g., nuclear ribosome cistron and plastid genomes). This is particularly useful because the genomes of plastids, the photosynthetic organelles responsible for many plant cell functions including carbon fixation and biosynthesis of starch, fatty acids, amino acids, pigments, etc. [24–26], provide rich evolutionary and phylogenetic information [19, 27]. In angiosperms, the typical plastid genome has a conserved quadripartite circular structure composed of one large single copy (LSC), one small single copy (SSC) and two copies of an inverted repeat (IR) situated between the LSC and SSC [28]. Although plastome content and gene order have tended to be conserved among plants [26, 29], differences in size, structure, gene content patterns, and small- and large-scale rearrangements have been recognized [30, 31]. These shifts result in gene gains or losses attributed to the transfer of genes from SSC regions into the IRs or vice-versa, leading to plastome size variation among plant families [26]. Generation of a complete plastome sequence from representative species can provide information related to evolutionary history and permit the development of sequence-based tools, including organelle-specific markers, for ecological studies and DNA barcode-based diagnostics for species identification [32].

Furthermore, improved genome sequencing technology has increased the number of studies on plant genome codons [33, 34]. The different frequencies among synonymous and non-synonymous codon mutations is called the codon usage bias (CUB). Multiple factors can impact the pattern of codon usage, such as DNA replication [35], gene length [36], and mutation and selective pressure [37]. The use of codons is subject to selection pressure from these variables and is widely regarded as a major reason for the creation of new species [38]. Translational selection also impacted CUB patterns in some plants [33]. The identified CUB differences between species revealed different evolutionary mechanisms in shaping the CUB and increased the understanding concerning ways in which plastid genomes function in different taxa [33, 39]. Codon preference in the cpDNA has been determined for several vascular plants, such as Solanum tuberosum L. [39] and across Rosales [33], but codon usage pattern of cpDNA in Boraginaceae species has not been investigated.

To date, only a few species of Lithospermeae have sequenced plastomes [40, 41], and increasing the number of complete plastid genome sequences from members of Lithospermeae will allow for a greater understanding phylogenetic relationships and of genome evolution in the tribe. Currently, advancements in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have resulted in huge amounts of data for an evergrowing number of plant species [42]. In the present study, we used genome skimming for 50 species of Lithospermeae to address three objectives: [1] provide the first report on the plastid genome of many species of Lithospermeae, [2] undertake extensive investigation on the codon usage pattern to understand its evolutionary processes and the impact of mutation and selection in the tribe, and [3] reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships of the tribe.

Material and methods

Plant materials and DNA sequencing

Sixty nine species are newly sequenced, representing 50 species of the genera of Lithospermeae and 19 species from other tribes of Boraginaceae. Specimens were obtained from field collections, and herbarium specimens housed in the Missouri Botanical Garden (MO), Naturhistorisches Museum Wien (W), Conservatoire et Jardin botaniques de la Ville de Genève (G), Mie Prefectural Museum (MIE), South African National Biodiversity Institute (PRE), Australian National Herbarium (CANB), Hacettepe University. The formal identification of the plant materials obtained from the field was undertaken by James I. Cohen, who is a co-author of this paper. Voucher specimens have been deposited at herbaria of Cornell University [43] and Weber State University (WSCO). The formal identification of the plant materials obtained from the herbaria listed above was undertaken by the collectors and verified by Maryam Noroozi, the first author of this paper. DNA isolation and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted and purified from the leaves of a single individual per species using the Norgen Plant DNA Isolation Kit (Magnetic Bead System) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Norgen Biotek Corp., Thorold, ON, Canada). The libraries for each sample were prepared following the recommended protocol of the company and then sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform by SNPsaurus (SNPsaurus, Millrace, OR, USA).

Genome assembly, annotation

The quality of the original Illumina raw reads from the 50 Lithospermeae species was examined and compared using FastQC and MultiQC, with default settings [44]. Low‐quality sequences were subsequently removed (PHRED < 20) using Trim Galore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore), and high-quality reads were de novo assembled using GetOrganelle [45] with the following parameters used for plastid genome assembly – 15 rounds for assembly using kmer values of 21, 31, 45, 65, 85, 95, and 105 – and for the nrDNA cistron assembly – 7 rounds for assembly using kmer values of 35, 55, 85, and 115. GeSeq [46] was used to obtain annotations for each plastid genome. Circular plastid genome maps were drawn using Organellar Genome DRAW version 1.3.1 (OGDRAW) [47]. All newly obtained sequences were submitted to GenBank (PRJNA1098857) (Table 1).

Comparative analysis of the plastid genome

To investigate variation across the plastid genome, junction sites between the LSC/IRa/SSC/IRb regions were plotted in IRscope [48]. Simple sequence repeats (SSR) were identified using MISA [49] with default settings. Long repeat analysis was conducted using the REPuter [50] with parameters set to the minimum repeat size of 30 bp and hamming distance of three.

Codon usage analysis

To identify and understand codon usage patterns of Lithospermeae, the values of relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) were computed for every codon in each genome using PhyloSuite [51]. If the RSCU value is higher than 1.0, there is positive codon usage bias, while a value of less than 1.0 has a negative codon usage bias. If the RSCU value of a codon is 1.0, there is no codon usage bias (i.e., it means that this codon is chosen equally and randomly). RSCU values higher than 1.6 and lower than 0.6 indicate overrepresented and underrepresented codons, respectively [52].

Parity rule 2 (PR2) analysis was conducted to explore the composition of the four DNA bases at the third nucleotide position of each codon. PR2 is detected by the value of AT-bias [A/(A + T)] and GC-bias [G/(G + C)]. The dots at the center of the plot (A = U, G = C) indicate no biases between the two complementary strands of DNA in mutation and selection rates [53].

The effective number of codons (ENC) is used to measure codon bias. ENC values range from 20 to 61. An ENC of 20 indicates that there is only one codon for each amino acid, and a value of 61 means all codons are used equally. An ENC plot was created with the ENC values plotted against the GC3S values (third synonymous codon position GC content). The ENC plot may be used to explore the pattern for synonymous codon usage. If the points are on or around the standard curve, the codon usage of given genes is only constrained by mutation pressure.

Neutrality plots (GC12 vs GC3) were used to explore the degree of impact between natural selection and mutation pressures on the mode of codon usage [54]. GC12 is the average of GC1 and GC2. The GC content of the entire genome, first, second, and third codon positions (GC1, GC2, and GC3 respectively) were calculated with EMBOSS CUSP (https://www.bioinformatics.nl/cgi-bin/emboss/help/cusp) and then subjected to neutrality plot analysis. The heatmap, scatter plots, etc. were drawn with Prism v.8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA; www.graphpad.com).

Synonymous (Ks) and non-synonymous (Ka) substitution rates were analyzed to investigate the molecular evolutionary process of Lithospermeae. The protein substitution rate (Ka/Ks) values were calculated using KaKs_calculator 2.0 with the NG method [55]. Genes with Ka/Ks > 1, Ka/Ks = 1, and Ka/Ks < 1 indicate probable positive, neutral, and purifying selection, respectively. In addition, to investigate branch-specific estimates of Ka/Ks for each gene with Ka/Ks > 1, the free Ka/Ks (dN/dS) ratio branch model was implemented, using codeml, in PAML version 4.9j [56]. For analyses with codeml, the following parameters were run for each gene: the free-ratios model, no clock model, kappa and omega estimated, branch lengths ignored, and 10 rate categories.

To obtain the number of tRNA genes in each genome, tRNAscan-SE 2.0 [57] was run for 30 species of Lithospermeae. For each species, the tRNA copy number inferred using tRNAscan and the complete set of protein coding genes were provided as input, and CodonMuSe [58] was run in default mode such that the optimal model selection was conducted automatically.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic relationships of Lithospermeae were inferred using Maximum Parsimony (MP), Maximum Likelihood (ML), and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. Five data sets were generated for phylogenetic analyses: [1] the complete plastid DNA (cpDNA) of 63 species; [2] Nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) of 66 species; [3] the concatenated matrix, of the cpDNA and nrDNA regions, of 69 species; [4] a concatenated data set consisting of coding sequence (CDS) of the 77 protein-coding genes for 63 species; and [5] a concatenated data set consisting of 88 intergeneric regions for 63 species. One copy of the IR of all plastomes was manually removed in all analyses to avoid data duplication.

For each of the first three matrices, Maximum Parsimony (MP), Maximum Likelihood (ML), and Bayesian Inference (BI) phylogenetic analyses were conducted with TNT [59], RaxML-NG (v.8.2.12) [60, 61], and MrBayes v3.2.5 [62], respectively. For each of CDS and intergeneric regions dataset only Maximum Likelihood (ML) analyses were performed.

Maximum parsimony analyses were conducted using TNT [59] with same strategy as [63].

Maximum-likelihood (ML) analyses for each matrix were conducted using RAxML-NG at either CIPRES Science Gateway (Miller et al., 2010) or the Kettering University High Performance Computing Cluster (KUHPC). For RAxML-NG, two analyses were conducted: [1] on the unpartitioned data set and [2] a partitioned dataset according to the best partitioning scheme, inferred by PartitionFinder 2.1.1 using the following parameters: all models, link branch lengths, corrected Akaike Information Criteria (AICc) for model selection, and a greedy search scheme. 1,000 bootstrap replicates were conducted, and support values of Transfer Bootstrap Expectation (TBE) and Felsenstein Bootstrap Proportion (FBP) [64] were calculated.

Bayesian inference (BI) analyses were undertaken with MrBayes v3.2.5 [62] using following parameters: 12 chains run for 25,000,000 generations, with chains sampled every 1,000 generations, a heating parameter of 0.2, a GTR + I + G model with four gamma categories, and burnin of 20%. Trees were visualized using FigTree version 1.4.3 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

To explore potential patterns of incongruence between the plastid and nuclear genomes, tanglegrams were created with dendextend [65], and NetRAX [66] was employed to resolve a phylogenetic network of the tribe and relatives. The best tree resolved from the concatenated ML analyses was used as a starting tree, the two genomes were treated as separate partitions, and both the average and best displayed tree variants were reconstructed.

Subsampling of the plastid genome – The plastid genome was subsampled in various manners to investigate the impact of the inclusion of different amounts of plastid regions to tree stability. Using the 225 annotated regions of the genome (e.g., CDS, introns, tRNA, etc.), nine different subsampling schemes were used (10–90% of the possible DNA regions), with 1,000 randomly concatenated matrices for each percentage assembled. Each matrix was analyzed with RAxML [67] using the GTR + I + G model. The 1,000 trees from each percentage were compared to the best tree from ML analyses with the entire plastid genome (only one IR region) using SPR swaps in phangorn [68] (sprdist command), and then the results were graphed and statistically compared with Kruskal–Wallis test in ggpubr [69] (stat_compare_means command).

Results

Features of plastid genomes in lithospermeae

Features of plastid genomes of 42 species of Lithospermeae were studied. All studied species of Lithospermeae exhibited the typical quadripartite structure of angiosperms and ranged in length from 143,875 bp (Echium handiense Svent) to 151,776 bp (Aegonychon zollingeri (A.DC.) Holub). Length variations were also noted for the different regions: from 80,452 bp (Arnebia coerulea Schipcz) to 88,382 bp (Echium nervosum W.T.Aiton) for the LSC, from 17,058 bp (Onosma khorassanica Attar & Joharchi) to 17,383 bp (Moltkia coerulea Lehm) for the SSC, and from 19,202 bp (Lobostemon trichotomus DC) to 25,976 bp (Alkanna matthioli Tausch) for the pair of IRs (Table S1). The average GC content is ca. 37% for all studied species.

Results of IR boundaries showed that the genes found at the junctions were mainly rpl22, rps19, rps3, ndhF, trnH, and ycf1 (Fig. 1). The rps3 gene was replicated and fully embedded in the LSC, except in Echium and Lobostemon, and psbA was located completely in the LSC in almost all species, although exceptions include Maharanga in which some portion of this gene extends into one IR. The ndhF and ycf1 genes were located within the SSC, with only a small portion intruding into either IR. The rpl22 gene occupied the IRb/LSC junction with lengths of 1–155 bp extending into LSC. In addition, Glandora, Lithodora, Maharanga, and Cystostemon Balf.f. showed significant differences in boundary characteristics from the other genera (Fig. 1).

Comparison of chloroplast borders of large single copy (LSC), small single copy (SSC), and inverted repeat (IR) regions among the species from Lithospermeae

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were investigated in newly sequenced plastid genomes (Table S2). Four kinds of SSRs, i.e., mono-, di-, tri-, and tetranucleotide, were detected in plastid genomes of Lithospermeae (Table S2, Figs. 2, S1). The mono-nucleotide repeats accounted for 97% of the 1,642 microsatellites in species of Lithospemeae, followed by di-nucleotide repeats (2.34%). The number of trinucleotide repeats was higher than that of tetranucleotide repeats. The highest numbers of SSRs were found in Cerinthe (with 49) whereas Alkanna Tausch had the lowest number of SSR repeats [27].

A The unit of SSRs in the cp genome of Lithospermeae B. The region of SSRs in the cp genome of Lithospermeae species C. Repeat sequence analyses of plastid genomes in Lithospemeae

Four types of repeats comprising forward, reverse, complement, and palindromic were analyzed in the 41 species plastomes of Lithospermeae. Two thousand and four hundred ninety-four repeats were identified, and forward repeats were the most frequent, with 48%, followed by palindromic repeats with 47%. The genus with the most repeated sequences was Alkanna with 50 forward repeats and 59 palindromic repeats. The smallest number of repeated sequences was found among species of Lithodora with only 47 repeats (Table S3, Fig. 2).

Codon usage analysis

RSCU (relative synonymous codon usage) values of Lithospermeae plastid genomes were calculated for 61 synonymous codons and presented in Table S4, and Figure S1. Five codons, namely, UUA, UCU, GCU, AGA, and GGA were overrepresented (mean RSCU value > 1.6), and fifteen codons, including CUC, CUG, GUC, ACG, GCG, UAC, CAC, CAG, AAC, GAC, GAG, CGC, AGC, CGG, and GGC were underrepresented (mean RSCU value < 0.6) in all species. Of the 61 codons, 29 had the RSCU > 1, which were high-frequency codons. Among them, 16 codons ended in U, 12 codons ending in A, and one codon ending in G. The preferred codons (RSCU > 1, in bold, Table S4) in the all plastid genomes were strongly biased towards the codons ending in A or U.

The nucleotide composition at the third synonymous codon position (T3s, C3s, A3s, G3s) were calculated for genera of Lithospermeae. It is observed that the composition of purines at third synonymous codon positions (G3s and C3s) is very low compared to the composition of pyrimidines at third synonymous codon positions (A3s and T3s) (Fig. S1). It is clear that the T3s content is remarkably high, while C3s are the lowest when compared to other nucleotide contents in the third position. For instance, the pyrimidines’ composition (A3s + T3s) is 0.44 as compared to 0.17 of the purines (G3s + C3s).

The mean ENC (effective number of codons) value in Lithospermeae was 50.20, ranging from 49.32–52.71. The ENC value for 22 and 23 species was above and below 50, respectively. These results reveal that all species of Lithospermeae lacked strong codon bias in their plastid genome. According to the ENC-GC3S plots, all the species clustered together just below the expected ENC curve (Fig. 3). In the PR2 plot, the relationship between A/T and G/C composition on the third codon position was analyzed. The results showed that all points were nearly located at center and the bottom right of the graph (Fig. 3).

GC12 in this regression plot represents the average value of GC contents at the first and second positions in each codon, and GC3 is the GC content at the third position C. Neutrality plot analysis was employed to study the magnitude of mutation pressure and natural selection. A correlation between GC12 and GC3 values were observed (R2 = 0.472, p-value < 0.0001). The slope of the regression line was calculated to be 0.638, which suggests that mutation pressure was 63% and natural selection was 37%. Consequently, results indicate that the codon usage patterns were primarily shaped by mutation pressure (Fig. 3).

In total, 72 CDSs were used for Ka/Ks analyses. The results revealed that matK, ccsA, rps16, rps15, clpP1, accD, ycf1, rps7, psbE, and psbC had relatively high mean Ka values (Ka > 0.02), while 47 genes (e.g., ycf1, ycf4, rps7, psbC, and rpl22) had comparatively high average Ks values (Ks > 0.05). Overall, 67 genes had a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1 and only five genes (psbJ, psbC, rps7, ycf1, and clpP1) had a Ka/Ks ratio greater than 1 (Table S5, Fig. 2). The ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions on each branch of the optimal Bayesian tree was analyzed for these five genes. Ten (for clpP1) to two (for rps7) branches exhibited signals for positive selection with ω (Ka/Ks) values ranging from 1.03 to 999.

For 30 pecies, genome-wide values for GC bias, selection on transcript biosynthetic cost (Sc), and selection on transcript translational efficiency (St) were inferred using CodonMuSe. All species in this analysis had negative Sc values (mean Sc = − 0.1). Also, all species had negative values for St (mean St =—0.04) (Table S6). The nitrogen cost for each codon is identical among all examined species (Table S6), the tAI and nitrogen cost are not correlated.

Phylogenetic analyses

The whole plastome data set had an aligned length of 165,459 nucleotide sites, among which 35,085 (21%) were parsimony informative characters. The nrDNA data set had an aligned length of 20,228 nucleotide sites, among which 6,447 (25%) were parsimony informative characters. In total, the concatenated cpDNA and nrDNA matrix included 185,687 aligned nucleotides, with 41,532 (22%) parsimony informative sites.

The topologies obtained using MP, ML, and BI methods were nearly identical based on each data set. The 61 species representing 23 genera were divided into four clades. The first clade comprises Alkanna Tausch and this clade is sister to the rest of the Lithospermeae (1.00 posterior probability [PP], 100% bootstrap [BS]) in the concatenated dataset. The second clade includes Lithospermum and relatives (PP = 1.0, BS = 100%). In most analyses, Ancistrocarya is sister to Aegonychon (PP = 1.0, BS = 99.5–100%), and these two genera are sister to a clade composed of Lithospermum, Buglossoides, and Glandora (PP = 1.0, BS = 100%). However, in the ML analyss based on the nrDNA data set, Buglossoides is sister to all members of this clade. In addition, in this analysis Glandora is resolved as sister to Aegonychon (FBP = 50) although Glandora and Lithospermum resolved as sisters is well supported by the other analyses (PP = 1.0, BS = 100%). Based on all trees, except those from nrDNA analyses, the third group comprises Cerinthe, Neatostema I.M.Johnst., Lithodora, Moltkiopsis I.M.Johnst., and relatives, and all these genera are in a clade sister to Moltkia (PP = 0.97, BS = 58%). Multiple small differences in this group occur among different analyses. Two notable differences include the placement of Arnebia and Huynhia Greuter. Arnebia is sister to a clade of the second group (PP = 0.64–1, BS = 68–70%) and Huynhia is sister to Echium and relatives, in the cpDNA trees and ML analysis of the concatenated data set. However, in trees from the nrDNA (ML and MP) and the intergenic datasets, Arnebia and Huynhia are sister, and the clade of these two species is sister to Lithospermum and relatives. In BI analyses based on concatenated cpDNA + nrDNA and only the nrDNA datasets, Arnebia is sister to Huynhia and these two are sister to Moltkia (PP = 1) (Figs. 4, S3).

Maximum Likelihood phylogeny of concatenated cpDNA and nrDNA for Lithospermeae and relatives in Boraginaceae. Numbers above branches are Felsenstein's bootstrap proportions (FBP)

The fourth clade consists of Echium, Onosma L., and relatives and is split into two main lineages. In the first lineage Cystostemon + Maharanga form the sister clade of Onosma (PP = 1.0, BS = 99–100%). The second lineage includes Echium, Lobostemon, and relatives. Echium and Lobostemon are each monophyletic, and Pontechium Böhle & Hilger is sister to Echium in all analyses of cpDNA (PP = 1.0, BS = 98%); however, Pontechium is sister to Lobostemon or Lobostemon and Echium in trees based on nrDNA (PP = 1.0, BS = 100%) (Fig. 4).

Phylogenetic network results suggest reticulations occurring throughout the evolution of Lithospermeae, and seven reticulation events were resolved. Most of these were among more early diverging branches of the tribe (Fig. S4).

The subsampling results provide evidence that greater sampling of DNA regions results in trees that are more similar to the ML tree resolved with the entire plastid genome as well as are more similar to each other (Fig. 5). The subsampling results between neighboring percentages were statistically significant (least significant is between 40 and 50% and is p-value < 0.00001) As the matrices increase in the number of DNA regions included, the mean number of SPR swaps to produce identical trees declines gradually. While some trees from the 10% matrix can be fairly similar to the best ML tree, others are quite different and require at least 15 swaps to be identical to the best tree. For some trees, differences to the best ML tree occur at the tips of the tree and only involve relatively local rearrangements; however, for others, the topology is quite incongruent and more and farther swaps are needed to produce the identical best tree.

Discussion

Plastid genome features

The organization of plastid genomes of Lithospermeae is similar to that of other members of the Boraginales [34, 41, 70] and the plastid genomes of most other known Angiosperms, which includes the typical quadripartite structure with two copies of IR separating the LSC and SSC. The plastid genome sequences within genera were found to be relatively conserved, with less variation occurring between species than noted in prior studies [41]. For example, among the two species of Buglossoides sampled, we identified only minimal differences in the plastid genome length, presence/absence of genes, and GC content. However, plastid genomes from across Lithospermeae showed some differences when compared in terms of genome size and proportion of noncoding DNA and repetitive sequences (Table S1). Overall, synteny, the range of genome size, the gene and intron contents, and AT/GC compositions are fairly conserved across the plastid genomes of Lithospermeae. The IR boundary shifts across members of the tribe, demonstrating that contraction and expansion, have occurred, (Fig. 1), and this is a common feature of plastid genome evolution [71].

While chloroplast genomic sequences remain remarkably consistent within a species, variations, such as nucleotide substitutions, SSRs, indels, and other microstructural mutations present within these sequences, serve as valuable markers. These variations are instrumental in uncovering genetic diversity and directing molecular breeding efforts [72, 73]. SSRs are highly variable molecular markers and are frequently used in population genetics. They play an essential role in transcription regulation, chromosome construction, and gene expression [74]. Among the four chloroplast SSR types identified in Lithospermeae, mononucleotide SSRs were the most common. Similarly, mononucleotide SSRs were the abundant SSR type in other taxa, such as Poaceae [75, 76] and Fagaceae [77]. A total of 90% of the mononucleotide SSRs were constituted by A/T sequences, while only 10% is composed of a G/C motif. Interestingly, all of the dinucleotide SSRs also constituted A/T motifs. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that plastid SSRs have a strong A/T bias [75, 78, 79]. The bias may be closely related to the simpler changes to A/T rather than G/C [80]. The bias might be associated with the differing chemical and structural properties of A/T base pairs compared to G/C base pairs. A/T base pairs form two hydrogen bonds, while G/C base pairs form three [81]. The presence of an additional hydrogen bond in G/C pairs makes them more stable and less prone to mutations compared to A/T pairs. This stability makes G/C pairs less likely to undergo changes during replication or repair processes [82]. Additionally, G/C pairs might be underrepresented in certain regions of the plastid genome due to selective pressures favoring A/T-rich sequences, impacting the observed bias in SSRs [83]. Therefore, the higher susceptibility of A/T pairs to mutation and their relatively lower stability compared to G/C pairs likely contribute to the observed bias towards A/T motifs in plastid SSRs. This bias reflects the underlying dynamics of nucleotide alterations within the plastid genome and its implications for genetic diversity and evolutionary processes [84]. Most of SSRs were located in the LSC, rather than in the SSC and IR. SSRs were completely absent from the IR regions of two species (i.e., Lobostemon trichotomus and Maharanga emodi), It is worth noting that Lobostemon and Maharanga are situated within a larger clade, despite not being closely related species, so this absence could be the result of convergent evolution. The LSC and SSC regions tends to be more structurally variable compared to the more conserved IR regions in plastid genomes [85]. Due to its higher variability, the LSC might harbor a greater abundance of SSRs, and the higher level of conservation in the IR might limit the occurrence or persistence of SSRs within these regions. SSRs categorized in the present study could provide potential markers for intraspecific variation among species of the tribe.

Long repeat sequences (i.e., those with lengths greater than 30 bp) play important roles in creating rearrangements and increasing population genetic diversity [31, 86]. Of the 267 repeats identified in the present study, the most common were short repeats between 30 and 40 bp, and this is consistent with many rearranged plastomes [87]. Among these long repeats, forward and palindromic were most common, and complementary and reverse repeats were rare, which has been observed in other angiosperm groups as well, such as Aquifoliaceae [86]. Repeat sections frequently appeared in non-coding regions of the majority of angiosperm plants, and frequent variation on by slipped-strand mispairing and illegitimate recombination [88]. The preference for short repeat lengths and specific orientations within long repeats, coupled with their localization in non-coding regions, underscores their potential role in driving genetic variability and evolutionary processes in angiosperm plastid genomes. Further investigations into these mechanisms across a broader range of plant taxa would provide deeper insights into the functional significance and evolutionary implications of these repeat sequences.

Codon usage analysis

Each organism forms a specific codon usage pattern in the evolutionary process, and GC content is an important indicator in the evolution of genomic structures [89]. GC content usually reflects the strength of directional mutation [90]. In Lithospermeae, the GC content at the first, second, and third codon sites followed the pattern GC1 > GC2 > GC3, which is consistent with the recent finding of Li and Wei [91]. In the presented research, we discovered that the overall AT content in Lithospermeae genomes was higher than GC content, and third codons tended to end in A/T. This result provides additional support to the higher GC3s content known from most Eudicots [92, 93] and gymnosperms, such as Ginkgo biloba L. [94]. In contrast, Monocots, such as Oryza sativa L. [95], Hordeum vulgare L. [96], and Triticum aestivum L. [97], showed higher GC content and tended to end in G/C. In some families, such as Fabaceae, both the nuclear and chloroplast genomes exhibit similar patterns. For example, the results showed that species of Dalbergia L.f. have a preference for A/U-ending codons at the third position of protein-coding sequences in both the nuclear and chloroplast genomes [98]. However, our knowledge is limited for Lithospermeae, and further studies are needed to further explore this pattern.

The codon usage preference is closely related to gene expression and implicated in protein structure and function [99]. We detailed the overall synonymous codon usage of 44 species of Lithospermeae for the first time. The most abundant amino acid in Lithopermeae was Isoleucine. Interestingly, most codons ending in A/U have RSCU values greater than 1, while those ending in C/G are less than one, which demonstrated nucleotide composition played an important role in the pattern of Lithospermeae codon usage. This pattern of codon usage also was observed in other plants, including as Gossypium L. [100], Coleanthus Seidl [75], and Cuscuta L. [101].

The formation of codon usage bias is affected by many factors, such as gene length [36], tRNA abundance [102], and mRNA secondary structure [103], but the two strongest factors are mutation pressure [104] and natural selection [105]. In the present study, we used multiple methods to explore the codon bias in plastid genomes of Lithospermeae and to quantify the effect of mutation pressure and natural selection. First, an ENC-GC3s plot was generated to investigate the role of mutational pressure in shaping codon usage bias. The results of ENC-plot suggested that codon usage preference of the all studied species is influenced by mutation pressure and other factors, while natural selection was determined to play only a minor role. This pattern was observed in other taxa such as Solanaceae [106]. Second, a neutrality plot reveals differences in codon positions’ base composition and evolution mode of the third codon position [33]. Previous studies revealed that when codon use is affected by natural selection, there is no significant correlation between GC12 and GC3 (Kawabe and Miyashita, 2003). Most previous studies in plants revealed that natural selection was more important than mutation pressure (Liu et al., 2010; Liu and Xue, 2005; Wang et al., 2020). However, in the present study, the correlation between the two positions was significant, and the slope of the regression line is close to 0.6; therefore, it appears that the codon preference was affected by mutation pressure. In our PR2 data analysis, we observed a higher frequency of T and G being utilized at the third position of codons compared to the usage of A and C. This pattern was also noted in other members of Boraginales [91]. RNA editing, specifically C-to-U recoding, also could influence plastome evolution, but given recent results [107], this likely impacts species of Lithospermeae in similar manners, and, therefore, would not affect phylogenetic reconstruction. All the findings consistently indicate that mutation pressure plays a more substantial role than natural selection in shaping codon usage, albeit moderately. Some studies on Glycine max (L.) Merr. [108] and Helianthus annuus L. [109] have also indicated that codon usage bias is primarily influenced by mutation pressure. These findings imply that preferences for codon usage plants exhibit variation. The analysis above reveals that distinct genomes can be influenced by different pressures, resulting in codon usage preferences. The observed predominance of mutation pressure over natural selection influencing codon usage bias in this study could be attributed to various factors, such as population size [43]. Consequently, to ensure accurate results and a comprehensive understanding, further extensive studies are necessary to encompass a broader spectrum of species and account for the diverse range of population sizes within Lithospermeae and its genera.

The synonymous and non-synonymous nucleotide substitution patterns are essential in gene evolution studies [24]. The present study recognized that, overall, 67 genes had a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1 among the studied species, and of the 67 genes, 64 had a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 0.5, suggesting that most genes were undergoing strong purifying selection pressures. This is unsurprising in a highly conserved genome. The other five (i.e., psbJ, psbC, rps7, ycf1, and clpP1) of 72 protein-coding genes within Lithospermeae have a Ka/Ks ratio greater than 1 and are presumed to be under positive selection. Positive selection on clpP has been observed in several taxa, such as Lamprocapnos Endl. [110], Fabaceae [111], and Geranium L. [112]. Recent studies have indicated that positive selection in ycf1, in various angiosperm groups, may be a common phenomenon [113–115]. The genes rps7, psbC, and psbJ were also under positive selection in other groups, such as Amphilophium Kunth [26], Populus L. [116], and Zingiberoideae (Zingiberaceae) [117], respectively. The genes clpP and ycf1 recognized in this study are also the objects of positive selection in various flowering plants [118, 119]. The functions of clpP and ycf1 are related to plant metabolism and development [120], suggesting that these genes are the common targets in the evolutionary processes of plastid genomes [121]. In addition, the positive selection signatures found on these plastid genes within Lithospermeae provide evidence that these genes might be undergoing adaptative evolution in response to the environment [24, 26]. These results may be associated with remarkable ecological and morphological variation found among member of Lithospermeae. The genera of Lithopermeae show high diversification rates in the largest genus Onosma, and diverse floral morphologies occur in multiple groups, such as Lithospermum and Echium [2]. For example, branch-specific estimates of Ka/Ks for ycf1 and psbC genes showed positive selection within clades of Onosma and Lithospermum, diverse genera with ecological and/or morphological variation. Positive selection was noted frequently within Onosma, Lithospermum, and Lobostemon; however, the results for some genes, like clpP1, showed high Ka/Ks values within other taxa as well, such as Alkanna, Buglossoides, and Echium (Fig. S2). Future studies can further explore the significance of adaptive evolution in plastid genes across the tribe.

All species in this analysis had negative Sc values, indicating a genome-wide selective pressure to reduce the biosynthetic cost of transcript sequences through biased synonymous codon use. Also, all species had negative values for St, indicating a genome-wide selective pressure to decrease the translational efficiency of transcript sequences. This implies a selective pressure towards optimizing the economy of gene expression at both the biosynthetic and translational levels across these genomes. Early research by Sharp and Li (1987) established a correlation between evolutionary rate, codon usage bias, and mRNA abundance [122]. It is logical to assume that the trade-off between cost and efficiency could also influence gene expression levels or protein abundance. Highly expressed genes necessitate extensive mRNA translation. Lower biosynthetic costs enable the abundant production of mRNAs, while higher tAI values ensure rapid translation. Consequently, the optimization of cost and efficiency aligns well with highly expressed genes [123]. The positive correlation between tAI and nitrogen cost could mean that mutations increasing the translation efficiency of a codon are more likely to also increase its biosynthetic cost, and conversely, vice versa [123].

Phylogenetic analyses

The evolutionary relationships and classification of Lithospermeae have been the subject of multiple studies over the past two decades (e.g., [2, 10, 12, 15]), and most of these studies have used a limited number of cpDNA regions (see [15] and [41] as exceptions). For the first time, using complete plastid genomes and entire nuclear ribosomal cistrons of species across the tribe, we reconstructed a strongly supported phylogenetic tree of Lithospermeae. This phylogeny provides additional resolution for phylogenetic relationship of genera in the tribe. While the plastome-based phylogenetic tree of 50 Lithospermeae genomes is generally congruent with the latest phylogeny of Lithospermeae [2], there are some notable differences that are discussed below as are incongruent results among various analyses.

The trees inferred from concatenated plastid and nuclear DNA regions in the present study recovered fairly congruent results to those from previous studies [2, 124], but topologies based on nuclear and plastid genes are slightly different. In the present study, the most apparent difference between the plastid and nuclear genomes involves the clades sister to the rest of the tribe. In the cpDNA phylogeny, the clade of Echium and Onosma is in this position, but in the nrDNA phylogeny, Alkanna is resolved as sister. This latter position is congruent with prior analyses (e.g., [2, 124]) and the concatenated phylogeny in the present study. This incongruence is recovered in the phylogenetic network analyses, and the current hypothesis is that incongruence is due to incomplete lineage sorting during the early diversification of the tribe. However, this incongruence should be examined with greater sampling of nuclear loci as these should provide a better picture of phylogenetic relationships across the entire tribe. Alternatively, the incongruent results may be due to methodological issues because even though entire plastid genomes and nuclear ribosomal cistrons were used for phylogeny reconstruction in the present study, these still represent only two loci [19].

We found conflicting results between trees from different datasets concerning the position of Arnebia. Previous studies also have not provided clear resolution for the evolutionary relationship of Arnebia [2, 14, 16, 41, 124]. Our results revealed that Arnebia is sister to the clade of Lithospermum and relatives. This result is similar to that of Sun et al. (2022), which is unsurprising as these authors employed quite limited taxon sampling. Huynhia, a small genus of plants that used to be in Arnebia, is distinct in the combined and plastid ML phylogenies, but not the phylogeny from the nuclear DNA as well as combined and cpDNA (including only intergenic regions) analyses with other methods, such as MP and phylogenetic network analyses. Indeed, only two DNA regions were employed for the phylogeny that provided the evolutionary evidence to segregate H. purpurea (Erik & Sümbül) L. Cecchi & Selvi from Arnebia. Given that the present study is the first to explore the relationships of these genera with more than a few DNA regions, the evolutionary relationships of these groups should be examined with greater taxon sampling to resolve the position of Huynhia in relation to Arnebia.

The present study helps resolve relationships among a number of small genera in Lithospermeae that have been recognized as allied [7, 12]. With more limited sampling of DNA regions, it has been challenging to recognize the relationships among Neatostema, Moltkiopsis, Paramoltkia Greuter, and Lithodora, and prior studies have generally recovered polytomies among these taxa [2]. In the present study, Paramoltkia is sister to Lithodora, and Moltkiopsis is sister this clade. These three genera share similar corolla colors – purple, blue and red – and this could be a synapomorphy for the group, with yellow corollas being putatively ancestral in Neatostema [7]. Paramoltkia, Lithodora, and Moltkiopsis also have edaphic specialization [12, 125], which is not the case for Neatostema. This could provide another synapomorphy for this clade, and a well-resolved phylogeny of the group could suggest the order of colonization of types of ecosystems. Future studies that include Mairetis and Halacsya Dörfl., which both have yellow corollas, could provide additional insight into patterns of evolution of this clade with multiple small and specialized genera.

The position of Ancistrocarya, the rare Japanese endemic, within a clade with Lithospermum and relatives is highly supported in all trees. In phylogenetic trees from plastid and combined data sets, Ancistrocarya was sister to Aegonychon, but in nrDNA trees, Aegonychon and Glandora clustered together and were sister to Ancistrocarya. Our study recovered the same general position of Ancistrocarya as Chacón et al. [2], in a clade with Lithospermum and relatives, but not as sister to Lithospermum. This slight shift in position is likely due to the different gene-region sampling among analyses (i.e., one nuclear locus [ITS] vs plastid genome and entire nrDNA cistron). Ancistrocarya shares with Aegonychon and Glandora blue to pink corollas [6], and these corolla colors are absent in Lithospermum, with L. hancockianum Oliv. as the lone exception.

The infrageneric phylogeny of Lithospermum was most recently resolved by Cohen [15]. The position of North American and Eurasian species of Lithospermum in the present study is congruent with Cohen [15]. In the combined matrix, the African species, L. afromontanum Weim., was resolved as sister to all North American and Eurasian species. However, in both cp and nrDNA matrices, L. afromontanum was resolved as sister to the clade of Eurasian species. This conflicting relationship was also recovered in other studies [14, 126] using different taxa and DNA region sampling. Regardless, the data suggest that Lithospermum originated in the Old World, with one migration event from Eurasia to southern Africa and another to the Americas. The New World species are also resolved monophyletic, which differs from some prior studies in which some Old World species are the result of migration back from the New World (e.g., [2, 14]). All trees also recovered Ulugbekia Zakirov as a separate genus from Lithospermum. Prior studies [7, 14] showed a close relationship between Glandora and Lithospermum, and our study reconstructed similar results using a broader range of nucleotides.

The evolutionary relationships for two larger genera of the tribe, Echium and Onosma, differ from those recovered from recent studies. Based on only cpDNA data, Pontechium is sister to Echium, which was also recovered by Chacón et al. [2]; however, in nrDNA trees, Pontechium is sister to a clade of Lobostemon and Echium or just Lobostemon. Chacón et al. [2] recovered Echium as non-monophyletic, and while the present phylogenies resolved the genus as monophyletic, taxon sampling did not overlap between the two studies. In the present study, Onosma is sister to a clade composed of Cystostemon and Maharanga, Cohen [124] resolved Cystostemon and Maharanga as successive sisters to Onosma, and Chacon et al. [2] found Maharanga nested within Onosma, but an early diverging member of the group. Combining the strategies of the present study and Chacón et al. [2] would result in greater sampling of both taxa and DNA regions to help resolve the relationships of these two larger genera, particularly in relationship to their less-speciose relatives.

Our results suggest that it may be possible to reconstruct evolutionary relationships among genera of Lithospermeae, using a subset of the plastid genome, albeit with less resolution than with greater genomic sampling. The subsampling and SPR swap results provide evidence that it is possible to do so, but also that the trees from a subset of the entire plastid genome may be substantially different from the tree resolved with the whole genome. As the amount of DNA regions increases, there is a gradual change to more similar trees, and there is no particular number of DNA regions included that result in trees that are almost identical to the one from the entire plastome. While these results certainly do not suggest that few-gene phylogenies are necessarily unreliable, the results support the use of more DNA sequence data to recover relationships that differ from those recognized in few-gene studies, to resolve polytomies, and to identify reliable patterns of support. Indeed, the SPR swap data suggest that the few cpDNA regions used for phylogeny reconstruction may not have uncovered a clade of Echium, Onosma, and relatives as sister to the result of the tribe, obscuring patterns of incongruence between genomes. Furthermore, Cohen [15] employed hundreds of nuclear DNA regions to reconstruct a phylogeny of Lithospermum, and this phylogeny was quite different from those based on cpDNA regions. Even though similar phylogenies among genera are recovered between entire plastomes and a small number of cpDNA regions, the results in Lithospermum suggests that at lower taxonomic levels this situation may not be the case.
