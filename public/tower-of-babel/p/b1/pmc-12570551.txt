Genome-wide association and DNA methylation analyses of SSRI treatment response in major depressive disorder
Authors: Nan Lyu, Qian Zhao, Dannong Liu, Jingjing Zhou, Xuequan Zhu, Han Qi, Han Wang, Min Liu, Mengping Wei, Chen Zhang, Ling Zhang, Jian Yang, Gang Wang, Mario Juruena, Allan H Young
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are first-line treatments for major depressive disorder (MDD); however, patient responses vary significantly. This study explored genetic and epigenetic factors linked to SSRI response to better understand this heterogeneity.MethodsWe performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) in 852 MDD patients classified as SSRI responders or non-responders. A polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis was conducted to estimate the cumulative effect of common genetic variants. In a subset of 66 participants, genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was carried out using the Illumina Methylation EPIC array. Analyses included the identification of differentially methylated positions (DMPs) and regions (DMRs), as well as weighted gene co-methylation network analysis (WGCNA) and downstream pathway and protein–protein interaction (PPI) analyses.ResultsGWAS revealed several loci with suggestive associations, including intronic variants in SRCIN1 and NKAIN3, although none reached genome-wide significance. The PRS explained a small proportion of variance in SSRI response and did not yield statistically significant results. DNA methylation analysis identified nominally differentially methylated CpGs in genes involved in synaptic signaling and neurodevelopment (e.g., OLFM1, PTN, CACNB2, LHX6). In addition, WGCNA identified a co-methylation module that showed a trend-level association with SSRI response after adjustment for clinical covariates, and hub genes within this module were functionally linked to neuronal signaling in PPI network analysis.ConclusionsAlthough no single marker reached significance, the results suggest that synaptic plasticity and neurodevelopment may influence SSRI response. These genomic and epigenomic insights offer molecular clues that may inform future studies on the biological mechanisms underlying antidepressant response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07502-y.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and debilitating psychiatric condition, contributing significantly to the global burden of disease and disability-adjusted life years [1]. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most commonly prescribed class of antidepressants and are recommended as first-line pharmacological treatments for MDD by several international guidelines [2–4]. However, the STAR*D study revealed that more than half of patients show inadequate response to initial SSRI therapy, with only 36.8% achieving symptom remission after the first treatment step [5]. Many patients require multiple adjustments to identify an effective medication. The trial-and-error approach of antidepressant prescription imposes delays in symptom relief, risks of side effects, and significant socioeconomic burdens. Hence, identifying reliable biomarkers that predict SSRI response is a crucial step toward personalized psychiatry.

Genetic factors have been extensively studied in relation to the outcomes of antidepressant treatment. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have aimed to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to the efficacy of specific antidepressants. Although few loci have reached genome-wide significance, multiple suggestive associations have been reported. For example, Li et al. [6] reported three loci—rs4884091 in RNF219-AS1, rs4955665 in MDS1/EVI1, and rs150245813 at 10p11.1—linked to SSRI or SNRI response. Ren et al. [7] identified multiple variants involved in the synaptic plasticity associated with escitalopram response. Koch et al. [8], identified significant loci, including rs1106260 near OLFM1 for SSRI non-response, and rs60847828 near TMEM114 for SSRI/SNRI non-response. However, large-scale meta-analyses have yielded inconsistent results, and few findings have been consistently replicated [9, 10]. Moreover, the effect sizes of individual SNPs remain small. To address these limitations, polygenic risk scores (PRS) have emerged to estimate the cumulative effect of multiple SNPs across the genome. Emerging evidence suggests that higher PRS for depression may be associated with poorer antidepressant response, although many of these associations do not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons [10–12].

In addition to genetic factors, epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation have gained increasing attention as dynamic regulators of antidepressant treatment response. Unlike genomic variants, methylation patterns can reflect both environmental influences and pharmacological interventions, making them promising biomarkers for treatment prediction. Most early studies have employed candidate gene approaches, focusing on methylation changes in genes such as BDNF, FKBP5, and NR3C1 [13–16]. However, recent research has begun to explore genome-wide DNA methylation signatures through epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS).

Although large-scale methylation studies remain limited, a few pilot investigations have reported promising findings. One of the earliest studies, by Takeuchi et al. [17], analyzed Japanese patients treated with paroxetine and identified two CpGs in the PPFIA4 and HS3ST1 significantly associated with therapeutic response. Ju et al. [18] later identified differentially methylated CpGs in CHN2 and JAK2 were identified, associated with escitalopram response. Similarly, Martinez-Pinteño et al. [19] observed methylation differences at 21 CpGs in adolescents treated with fluoxetine, including loci such as RHOJ and OR2L13. Engelmann et al. [20] found nominal associations between treatment response and methylation in SORBS2 and CYP2C18 regions. Most recently, Schiele et al. [21] identified several suggestive CpGs potentially linked to treatment response, such as those annotated to RSPO2, SAG, IL16, and PRKCI, though none met genome-wide significance thresholds. These findings support the notion that DNA methylation profiles may serve as predictive indicators for SSRI treatment outcomes and could offer mechanistic insights into treatment resistance.

Nevertheless, previous GWAS of antidepressant response have often been limited by modest effect sizes, limited reproducibility, and difficulty in accounting for environmental interactions, whereas EWAS has generally been constrained by small samples and inconsistent findings. While our study shares some of these limitations, we aimed to provide complementary, hypothesis-generating insights by examining both genetic and epigenetic factors within the same clinical cohort. We conducted a GWAS and polygenic risk score analysis in 854 MDD patients, and DNA methylation profiling in a subsample of 66 individuals with clear treatment outcomes. These two approaches capture distinct yet complementary perspectives—GWAS highlights inherited susceptibility, whereas EWAS reflects environmentally responsive regulation—with the overarching goal of identifying biomarkers that could predict SSRI efficacy and inform personalized treatment strategies.

Methods

Participants

This was a retrospective observational study based on biobank resources and longitudinal data collected from multiple depression cohorts at Beijing Anding Hospital between 2017 and 2022. All patients were diagnosed with MDD using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) based on DSM-IV criteria [22, 23]. Psychiatric evaluations, including the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) [24, 25]and the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [26], were conducted at baseline, week 8 or week 12, alongside detailed medication records. A total of 852 patients were selected for the present study based on the following inclusion criteria: age between 18 and 65 years, a baseline HAMD-17 score ≥ 14, no use of antidepressants within 14 days prior to enrollment, initiation of antidepressant monotherapy after inclusion, and availability of treatment response evaluations. Exclusion criteria included missing key clinical or medication data or inability to determine SSRI treatment efficacy. Treatment response grouping was based on both baseline cohort data and follow-up information. Responders were defined as those with a ≥ 50% reduction in HAMD-17 score at week 8 or 12 and a confirmed history of positive response to SSRI treatment. Non-responders were defined as those with a < 50% reduction and a documented history of poor response to SSRIs.

In addition, DNA methylation profiling was performed in a subsample of 66 patients drawn from the larger cohort using an extreme-phenotype design: 33 SSRI non-responders (defined as non-response to at least two different SSRIs) and 33 SSRI robust responders. This strategy was intended to maximize phenotypic contrast within the limited methylation sample and should be considered exploratory in nature.

Samples and DNA extraction

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants at baseline. Samples were stored at − 80 °C until further use. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The extracted DNA was used for both genotyping and DNA methylation analyses.

Genotyping and quality control

Genotyping was performed using the Illumina Asian Screening Array (ASA), which includes approximately 743,745 SNP markers. SNPs were imputed using IMPUTE2 (version 2.3.2) [27] based on 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference panels. Quality control (QC) was conducted using PLINK v1.9. Samples with a genotyping missing rate greater than 10% were excluded. SNPs were removed if they had a missing rate >10%, minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01, or significantly deviated from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1 × 10⁻⁶). After imputation, SNPs with an INFO score < 0.8 were excluded, resulting in a total of 7,541,567 SNPs retained for downstream analysis. To control for population stratification, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in PLINK, and the top five principal components were included as covariates in subsequent association analyses.

A logistic regression analysis was then conducted to assess the association between individual SNPs and SSRI treatment response under an additive genetic model. The model was adjusted for sex, age, baseline HAMD score, episode type (first vs. recurrent) and the top five principal components (C1–C5). Association testing was carried out using PLINK (v1.9.0), and the results were visualized using a Manhattan plot. The threshold for genome-wide significance was set at P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, and for suggestive significance at P < 1 × 10⁻⁵. Variant annotation was performed using ANNOVAR [28], which maps SNPs to gene regions and known functional elements.

In addition, gene-based and gene-set enrichment analyses were performed using MAGMA [29] (https://cncr.nl/research/magma), which integrates SNP-level associations with gene function and biological pathways to identify potential mechanisms underlying SSRI treatment responses. MAGMA performs gene analysis using a multiple regression approach that explicitly incorporates linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNPs and allows detection of multi-marker effects [29].To further evaluate whether significant gene-based associations were driven by a single SNP or by multiple loci, we additionally performed LD clumping using PLINK v1.9 (parameters: p1 = 1 × 10−4, p2 = 0.01, r²=0.5, kb = 250). Pairwise LD (r²) was also estimated from our GWAS dataset. Pathway enrichment used MAGMA’s competitive gene-set analysis. Gene sets were obtained from MSigDB v7.4 (Entrez IDs; KEGG and Reactome pathways; and Gene Ontology BP/CC/MF). Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), with significance defined at q < 0.05.

Polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis

PRS were calculated using PRSice-2 [30] to assess the cumulative effect of multiple genetic variants on SSRI treatment response. The full dataset was divided into five folds for cross-validation. In each iteration, four folds were used as the training set to perform GWAS using PLINK 1.9, adjusting for sex, age, age at onset, episode type (first vs. recurrent), baseline HAMD score, and the top five genetic principal components (PCs). The optimal P-value threshold for PRS construction was determined in the training set using PRSice-2, which employs an adaptive grid of thresholds ranging from P = 5 × 10⁻⁵ to 1.0 (increments of 5 × 10⁻⁵ for P ≤ 0.1 and 1 × 10⁻⁴ for P >0.1), yielding a total of 6,001 thresholds. This selected threshold was then fixed and applied to the held-out test fold, where PRS were calculated using a clumping and thresholding approach (clumping window = 250 kb, r² = 0.1). Logistic regression was performed in each test fold to examine the association between PRS and treatment response, adjusting for the same covariates. Model performance was summarized by the variance explained (R²) at each threshold. The average R² across folds (± SD) was reported, together with Fisher’s combined P across folds.

DNA methylation analysis

DNA methylation profiling was conducted on peripheral blood DNA from 33 SSRI responders and 33 non-responders, using the Illumina MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip (935 K) which includes 937,691 probes. Raw data were processed using the ChAMP package [31] in R, including quality control at both the probe and sample levels. Probes were filtered based on detection P-value, bead count, sex chromosome location, and overlap with known SNPs. Normalization was performed using the BMIQ method [32], and methylation levels were expressed as beta values. Following quality control and normalization, a total of 866,239 probes were retained for analysis. Based on the sample size (n = 66) and assumed effect size (|Δβ| = 0.02), the estimated statistical power was approximately 4.6%, indicating that this analysis was underpowered to detect subtle methylation differences.

Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) were identified using the limma package (v3.5.81) [33], adjusting for age, sex, HAMD-17 baseline score, and cell type composition. While beta values were used for interpretation, transformation to M-values was also considered to improve model performance. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR; for prioritization, we applied a screening threshold of nominal P < 0.05 together with |Δβ| >0.02.

Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using the Bumphunter algorithm, with significance defined as adjusted P < 0.05 and requiring at least 7 CpG sites, following established pipelines [34, 35]. For each DMR, the Bumphunter Area statistic was computed as the signed sum of the smoothed CpG-wise effect estimates across the region; larger Area indicates greater magnitude and/or width of differential methylation, and the sign denotes direction (hyper- vs. hypomethylation in responders). For each region, a bootstrap-based nominal P value for Area was calculated, and the corresponding resampling-based family-wise error rate (FWER) as the genome-wide multiple-testing adjustment; FWER (Area) < 0.05 defined significance. CpG sites were annotated using the EPIC annotation (hg19) to map them to gene features.

To explore functional relevance, genes mapped from DMPs were analyzed by over-representation analysis (ORA) using clusterProfiler with org.Hs.eg.db, testing Gene Ontology categories (BP/CC/MF) and KEGG pathways; multiple testing was controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR. As a complementary approach, we also used Metascape (https://metascape.org), which implements an accumulative hypergeometric test with FDR correction and Kappa-based clustering of enriched terms; concordant enrichments across tools are reported.

Weighted gene Co-Methylation network analysis (WGCNA)

To investigate methylation modules associated with SSRI treatment response, we applied WGCNA to the top 10,000 most variable CpG probes from EPIC array data across 66 participants. After quality control and phenotype alignment, we constructed an unsigned co-methylation network using a soft-thresholding power of 7, selected to approximate scale-free topology (Supplementary Fig. 1, where power 7 provides a suitable balance between scale-free topology fit and mean connectivity). Modules were identified via hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting. Module–trait associations were assessed by correlating module eigengenes with clinical variables. We further applied multivariable linear regression analyses to evaluate associations of module eigengenes with SSRI response and reduction rate, adjusting for sex, age, age of onset, and baseline HAMD score. For each CpG, module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS; here defined as the correlation between CpG methylation levels and the trait of interest) were calculated, and hub CpGs were defined as those with MM >0.7 and GS >0.2 within trait-related modules. All analyses were performed using the WGCNA package (v1.72-1) in R. Linear regression models were built to assess interaction effects between sex and SSRI response on methylation levels of hub CpGs. Genes annotated to hub CpGs were further explored through protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using STRING (https://string-db.org/) and visualized in Cytoscape [36], where clustering was performed to identify functional groupings.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between SSRI responders and non-responders. For normally distributed continuous variables, independent samples t-tests were applied; for non-normally distributed variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. For GWAS and DNA methylation analyses, Bonferroni correction or false discovery rate (FDR) was applied to adjust for multiple testing, depending on the specific analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1) and PLINK (v1.9), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05, unless otherwise specified.

Power calculations

GWAS power was estimated using the online GAS Power Calculator (https://csg.sph.umich.edu/abecasis/cats/gas_power_calculator/). Based on 852 samples (583 responders and 269 non-responders), a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.2, an odds ratio (OR) of 1.5, an additive genetic model, and a genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10⁻⁸. EWAS power was estimated using the pwrEWAS R package [37]. Based on 33 samples per group (n = 66 total), an assumed methylation difference (Δβ) of 0.02, 935,000 CpG sites, and parameters for adult whole blood, and the FDR of 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 852 patients with depression were included in the study, comprising 583 in the SSRI-responsive group and 269 in the non-responsive group. Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. There was no significant difference in age (Z = −0.914, P = 0.361) or level of education (χ² = 0.010, P = 0.919) between groups. However, the proportion of females was significantly higher in the non-responsive group (79.2%) compared to the responsive group (65.4%; χ² = 16.68, P < 0.001). Regarding clinical features, age of onset (Z = −1.671, P = 0.095) and recurrence rates (χ² = 2.061, P = 0.151) showed no significant group differences. However, the baseline HAMD score was slightly higher in the non-responsive group compared to the responsive group (21.44 ± 4.34 vs. 20.72 ± 4.14, P = 0.022). There was no significant difference in YMRS scores at baseline (Z = 0.741, P = 0.458). The follow-up data at 8 and 12 weeks were also presented in the table, showing a significant reduction in symptoms in the responsive group, and persistent depressive symptoms in the non-responsive group (Table 1).

GWAS and Gene-Based analysis

GWAS analysis was performed to identify genetic variants associated with the response to SSRIs. The estimated statistical power of the GWAS was approximately 77.4%. The QQ plot showed no substantial genomic inflation (λ = 1.016), indicating adequate control of confounding factors (Supplementary Fig. 2). The Manhattan plot (Fig. 1) illustrates the distribution of association signals across all chromosomes, with the genome-wide significance threshold set at P < 5 × 10−8 (red line) and a suggestive threshold of P < 1 × 10−5) (blue line). Among the top suggestive SNPs identified in our analysis (listed in Table 2), multiple loci were found within or near genes associated with cell proliferation, migration, and neural function. The strongest association signal was observed at rs2429991 on chromosome 17 (P = 3.18 × 10−7), followed closely by rs2429992 and rs2471632, also located in the SRCIN1 intronic region (P = 4.72 × 10−7). Notably, several additional SNPs, including rs16929302, rs936619, rs936602, rs7840692, rs58261377, rs4738986, rs62509505, rs7812978, and rs12545543 were located within NKA1N3 and exhibited suggestive significance (P < 1.7 × 10−6). Additionally, rs10062049 and rs13180347 were located in an intergenic region between LOC100506526 and KIF2A.

Polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis

PRSs were calculated using PRSice-2 across a range of p-value thresholds, with model performance evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation. As summarized in Supplementary Table 2, the proportion of variance explained by PRS ranged from 1.9% to 4.6% (mean R² = 3.13%, SD = 1.09%). Among the five folds, the best-fitting model was observed in Fold 5 (R² = 0.0457, P = 0.0320) at a p-value threshold of 0.0003, using 63 SNPs (shown in Supplementary Table 3). Other folds demonstrated similar effect directions but did not reach nominal significance (P range = 0.0648–0.1762). All five folds showed negative regression coefficients (range: − 0.30 to − 0.49), indicating a consistent trend where higher MDD-related polygenic burden was associated with poorer SSRI treatment response. None of the associations survived multiple testing correction.

To address concerns about comparability across folds, we further re-analyzed the data by fixing the threshold at P = 0.0003 (the optimal threshold identified in training folds) and applying it consistently across all test folds (Supplementary Table 4). At this fixed threshold, the mean cross-validated Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R² was 0.0157 ± 0.0161, and Fisher’s combined P across folds was 0.0909. While one fold showed nominal significance, the overall cross-validated association did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the predictive ability of the PRS in our cohort is limited and not robust.

Genetic pathway enrichment

Our study further explored the biological mechanisms influencing SSRI response at the pathway level. Using MAGMA, we identified 132 pathways that were significantly enriched. The top 15 pathways, shown in Fig. 2, were associated with SSRI treatment outcomes and involved key biological processes such as renin–angiotensin–aldosterone signaling (RAAS), growth factor signaling, immune regulation, and cancer-related mechanisms. Notably, several RAAS-related pathways were enriched, including those involving ATP1A1 and KCNJ5 dysregulation. Pathways related to EGFR-mediated growth factor signaling were also prominent. Additionally, we observed enrichment in cancer-related pathways, such as those associated with poor ovarian cancer survival and liver cancer subtypes, as well as in immune-related pathways involving germinal center T helper cells.

Differential DNA methylation analysis

To further explore the potential epigenetic mechanisms underlying antidepressant response, we selected 33 patients with clinically typical phenotypes of SSRI-sensitive and SSRI-resistant cases, respectively, for DNA methylation analysis (Table 4). No significant differences were found in age (Z = 1.253, P = 0.210), sex distribution (χ² = 0.076, P = 0.782), education level (χ² = 0.091, P = 0.763), age of onset (Z = 0.629, P = 0.529), or recurrence (χ² < 0.001, P = 1.000). Baseline HAMD and YMRS scores were comparable (Z = 0.851, P = 0.395; Z = −0.943, P = 0.346, respectively). Notably, at weeks 8 and 12, the non-responsive group consistently showed higher HAMD scores than the responsive group, further validating their divergent treatment responses and supporting the selection for methylation analysis.

Co-methylation network analysis and hub gene prioritization

We applied WGCNA to the top 10,000 most variable CpG sites (highest standard deviation of beta values across 66 samples) to capture the most informative methylation variation. Hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree cutting identified six distinct co-methylation modules (Fig. 4A). Among them, the blue module (2,337 CpGs) showed the strongest correlation with sex (r = 0.84, P = 5 × 10⁻¹⁹) and a significant negative correlation with the HAMD-17 reduction rate (r = − 0.27, P = 0.03; Fig. 4B). Using thresholds of MM > 0.7 and GS > 0.2, we identified 181 hub CpGs annotated to 66 genes (Supplementary Table 5). To evaluate potential sex-specific effects, we tested sex × response interactions for each hub CpG, and no significant interactions were observed (all P > 0.05). In models adjusted for sex, age, age of onset, and baseline HAMD score, the blue module showed a trend-level positive association with binary treatment response (β = 0.03, P = 0.068), whereas its correlation with reduction rate was no longer significant. No other modules remained associated with treatment outcomes after adjustment (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Additionally, module membership (MM) in the blue module was significantly associated with gene significance (GS; CpG–trait correlation) for treatment response (r = 0.43, P = 8.4 × 10⁻106; Fig. 4C). Finally, although GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the 66 hub genes did not identify statistically significant pathways after multiple testing correction, protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis revealed three major clusters (Fig. 4D): (1) a metabolic/epigenetic regulation cluster containing genes such as SHMT1, KDM1A, and USP28; (2) an apoptosis- and cytoskeleton-related cluster including TUBB, CASP8, and BBC3; and (3) a neuronal signaling cluster, represented by genes such as CACNA1A, EPHA1, and PDE4B.

Discussion

This study explored genetic and epigenetic factors underlying SSRI treatment response in patients with MDD. Although no genome-wide significant SNPs or differentially methylated sites were identified after multiple testing correction, several suggestive genetic loci—including variants in SRCIN1 and NKAIN3—were associated with treatment outcomes. While no individual CpG sites or regions reached statistical significance in DNA methylation analyses, pathway enrichment of nominally associated loci indicated involvement of genes related to neurodevelopment, synaptic signaling, and hormonal regulation. Co-methylation network analysis revealed a module enriched for neuronal signaling pathways, which showed a trend-level association with treatment response after adjustment for demographic and clinical covariates. These results highlight the complexity of the genetic and epigenetic landscape underlying SSRI response in MDD.

Among the genes identified through both SNP-level and gene-based analyses, SRCIN1 and NKAIN3 emerged as particularly noteworthy candidates. SRCIN1 (SRC kinase signaling inhibitor 1), which showed the strongest association in our GWAS (rs2429991, P = 3.18 × 10⁻⁷), encodes the scaffold protein p140Cap, a negative regulator of Src family kinases [38]. It plays critical roles in regulating cell proliferation, migration, and synaptic plasticity. It has been widely studied in oncology as a tumor suppressor, where its downregulation—often through promoter hypermethylation—has been linked to increased proliferation, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) across several cancers [39–41]. Significantly, SRCIN1 is also enriched in glutamatergic synapses and has been shown to regulate dendritic spine morphology and actin cytoskeleton dynamics—processes critical for synaptic plasticity [42–44]. Given the established role of synaptic remodeling in SSRI efficacy, our finding that the variation changes in SRCIN1 are associated with treatment response suggests that altered SRCIN1 function may impair synaptic adaptability, thereby contributing to antidepressant resistance in MDD.

Based on our findings, NKAIN3 (Na⁺/K⁺ transporting ATPase interacting 3) emerged as the top candidate at the gene level associated with SSRI response. NKAIN3 encodes a transmembrane protein that interacts with the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and plays a crucial role in maintaining neuronal membrane potential and ion homeostasis. It is highly expressed in the brain, particularly in the hippocampus and fetal temporal lobe [45], regions essential for mood regulation. Several intronic SNPs in NKAIN3 showed suggestive associations in our GWAS (e.g., rs16929302, rs936619, rs936602). LD analysis further indicated that these top signals are in strong linkage disequilibrium (r² >0.7, with most pairs showing r² ≈ 0.99), suggesting that they reflect a single intronic locus rather than multiple independent associations. In addition, previous GWAS implicate NKAIN3 in neurodevelopmental and cognitive phenotypes: for dyslexia-related rapid automatized naming, an intronic variant rs16928927 showed a suggestive association (p = 2.25 × 10⁻⁸) [46]; in trauma-exposed individuals, rs34720850 was associated with right caudate volume (p = 8.3 × 10⁻⁷; FDR q = 0.077) [47]; and for eating-disorder phenotypes, a study of 2,564 female twins reported a suggestive signal at NKAIN3 (e.g., rs142014203; p = 8.83 × 10⁻⁷) [48]. Given the importance of membrane excitability, the involvement of NKAIN3 suggests that ionic regulation may be a novel mechanism underlying variability in antidepressant response.

The MAGMA-based gene set enrichment analysis revealed prominent pathways related to the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), growth factor signaling, and neurodevelopment. Notably, RAAS-related pathways involving ATP1A1, NR3C2, and HTR2C variants point to the potential role of neuroendocrine regulation in antidepressant response [49], possibly through mechanisms related to oxidative stress and serotonergic signaling [50]. The enrichment of EGFR–PLCG–ERK signaling further underscores the contribution of neuroplasticity and intracellular signaling dynamics [51, 52] in mediating SSRI efficacy.

In addition, we conducted a PRS analysis to explore the cumulative effect of common genetic variants on SSRI treatment response. No statistically significant association was observed between PRS and treatment outcome. While some prior studies have reported nominal associations between MDD-PRS and antidepressant response [11, 12, 53, 54], others have failed to detect significant effects [55, 56]. Our results are broadly consistent with these mixed findings, suggesting that the predictive value of PRS for SSRI response remains limited.

DNA methylation analysis provided a new perspective on understanding SSRI treatment outcomes. Although no CpGs remained significant, our top-ranked differentially methylated genes are enriched in biologically relevant pathways related to synaptic function and plasticity, core mechanisms of SSRI efficacy. For instance, OLFM1 (Noelin1) regulates AMPA receptor trafficking and dendritic spine stability [57, 58]. PTN (pleiotrophin), a neurotrophic factor, modulates long-term potentiation (LTP), a key process in mood regulation [59, 60]. Likewise, CACNB2 encodes a calcium channel subunit essential for neurotransmitter release and dendritic development [61]. LHX6 plays a vital role in the development of GABAergic interneurons, and its dysregulation may alter inhibitory tone in cortical circuits involved in mental disorders [62, 63]. SERINC2, which responds to neuronal activity by modulating lipid biosynthesis, also exhibits differential methylation [64, 65]. These findings suggest that methylation-based regulation of genes related to plasticity and neurodevelopment may affect individual responsiveness to SSRI treatment.

Consistent with our single-site methylation findings, co-methylation network analysis via WGCNA further highlighted the potential role of neuroplasticity-related processes in SSRI response. PPI network analysis showed that one such cluster included genes involved in neuronal signaling, synaptic function, including CACNA1A, EPHA1, and PDE4B, which are central to calcium signaling, axon guidance, and cyclic nucleotide metabolism. Previous WGCNA studies examining the response to fluoxetine treatment have also reported modules enriched for neural development [66], supporting the relevance of co-regulated gene networks in antidepressant efficacy. These functions are consistent with the pathways identified in our differential methylation–based enrichment analysis, further supporting a role for neuronal plasticity in SSRI treatment response.

Interestingly, this module showed a strong association with sex, despite the exclusion of sex chromosome probes. This suggests that autosomal DNA methylation patterns may be sex-biased, aligning with prior studies that report widespread sex-related epigenetic differences across the genome [67, 68]. However, follow-up interaction analyses did not reveal significant sex-by-response effects at the CpG level, indicating that while the global methylation structure may differ by sex, these differences may not substantially modulate SSRI responsiveness at the individual CpG level. Nevertheless, at the module level, a trend-level association with binary response remained after adjustment for clinical covariates, suggesting that this co-methylation pattern may capture treatment-related variance independent of sex effects. However, residual confounding—particularly by sex—cannot be excluded. Given the modest sample size of our cohort, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require replication in larger, sex-stratified cohorts to confirm their robustness.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although our GWAS included 852 participants, the sample size remains modest by the standards of psychiatric genetics and may lack sufficient power to detect variants with small effect sizes. For the PRS analysis, results were not robust across folds and did not remain significant under fixed-threshold cross-validation, indicating limited predictive ability in this cohort. Moreover, as the analysis relied solely on internal five-fold cross-validation within the same dataset, it does not resolve concerns of overfitting or address the issue of generalizability. These findings should therefore be regarded as preliminary and require replication in larger, independent external cohorts to establish validity. For the DNA methylation component, the relatively small sample size (n = 66) resulted in limited statistical power (estimated at 16%). To maximize phenotypic contrast, we applied an extreme-phenotype design, which increases the likelihood of detecting methylation differences but does not fully represent the broader cohort. As such, this analysis should be regarded as exploratory, and future studies with larger and independent samples will be required to validate these findings and improve statistical robustness.

Additionally, methylation was assessed in peripheral blood, which may not fully capture brain-specific epigenetic changes relevant to antidepressant response. While baseline methylation—measured prior to SSRI initiation in participants who were antidepressant-free for ≥ 14 days—allows an exploratory assessment of predictive value, our single-timepoint design precludes causal inference; directionality remains unresolved, and baseline methylation may be pre-existing, exposure-related, or confounded. To address this, future studies should implement longitudinal EWAS with pre- and multiple post-treatment timepoints and repeated measures. Finally, other potential confounders such as environmental exposures and lifestyle factors were not fully accounted for and may influence the observed epigenetic patterns.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated genetic and epigenetic factors associated with SSRI treatment response in MDD. Although no individual SNPs or DNA methylation sites reached genome-wide significance, suggestive associations were observed in genes related to synaptic plasticity and neurodevelopment. Enrichment analyses further supported the involvement of neuroplasticity-related pathways. In addition, WGCNA identified a co-methylation module enriched in neuronal signaling pathways, which showed a trend-level association with treatment response after adjustment for clinical covariates. These findings suggest that synaptic and neurodevelopmental processes may contribute to variability in antidepressant response and warrant further investigation.
