BMI partially mediates the association between the non–HDL-C/HDL-C ratio and depression in obese adults
Authors: Kunyu Qiu, Yilei Liu, Yan Zhang, Chengwei Hu, Jie Gu, Yanyan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression has become one of the major global public health burdens. In recent years, lipid metabolic abnormalities have garnered increasing attention as potential influencing factors. Among these, the ratio of non–high-density lipoprotein to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (NHHR) is regarded as an integrated indicator for assessing atherosclerosis risk. Obesity, as a hallmark of metabolic dysregulation, has a complex interplay with depression. This study aimed to investigate the association between NHHR and depression among obese adults and to evaluate the mediating role of body mass index (BMI) in this relationship.MethodsThis study utilized data from the 2013–2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), ultimately including 13,991 adult participants with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m². NHHR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and by quartiles, while depression was assessed using the PHQ-9 scale. The primary analyses were conducted without weighting to explore within-sample associations, with core analyses including nonlinear fitting and mediation analysis. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses incorporated NHANES sampling weights and complex survey design, applied multiple imputation for missing covariates, and performed stratified analyses to evaluate the generalizability of the results to the representative population.ResultsAfter multivariable adjustment, NHHR showed a significant nonlinear positive association with depression risk. Threshold effect analysis identified NHHR = 2.95 as the inflection point. To the left of this threshold, higher NHHR was significantly associated with increased depression risk (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.09), whereas the association changed beyond this point. Mediation analysis indicated that 15.81% of the total effect of NHHR on depression was mediated indirectly through BMI (β = 0.0013, 95% CI: 0.0008–0.0018). However, given the cross-sectional design, reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Sensitivity analyses further demonstrated that the association was more pronounced among individuals without diabetes, those with prediabetes, and ever-smoker, with evidence of interaction effects (P for interaction < 0.05). Notably, the core findings were consistent in direction and overall trend with the unweighted analyses, further supporting the robustness of our results, although the effect sizes were not identical.ConclusionsAmong obese adults, higher NHHR levels are significantly associated with an increased risk of depression, with BMI partially mediating this relationship and diabetes status and smoking history serving as moderators. These findings suggest that NHHR may provide valuable insight for early intervention in mental health disorders among obese populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01971-7.Keywords: Depression, NHHR, Obesity, Mediation effect, NHANES

Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of depression has steadily increased, becoming a significant global public health burden and a major contributor to morbidity and mortality. The World Health Organization projects that by 2030, depression will rise to become the leading cause of disease burden worldwide [1]. According to 2019 data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), approximately 18% of the population reported current depressive symptoms, while 29% reported a lifetime history of depression [2].

The ratio of non–high-density lipoprotein to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (NHHR) has gained widespread attention from researchers across Asia, Europe, and South America as a comprehensive indicator of lipid metabolism in recent years. Representing atherogenic lipoproteins, NHHR reflects the underlying risk of atherosclerosis and offers a more holistic assessment of lipid metabolic imbalance [3–5]. Compared with traditional single lipid markers, NHHR is considered an integrative, accessible, and stable tool for evaluating lipid-related cardiovascular risk [6].

A growing body of research has demonstrated a complex bidirectional relationship between obesity and depression, involving not only biological mechanisms such as metabolic abnormalities, chronic low-grade inflammation, and neuroendocrine dysregulation, but also behavioral pathways including psychosocial stress, cognitive distortions, and unhealthy lifestyles [7, 8]. Within this context, individuals with obesity tend to exhibit greater vulnerability to depression [9], particularly when NHHR is abnormal, which further increases the likelihood of depressive symptoms [10]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported an elevated risk of atherosclerosis among patients with depression and bipolar disorder, highlighting the important role of lipid metabolism in the onset and progression of mood disorders [11], thereby reinforcing its potential mechanistic link to depression risk.

Body mass index (BMI), as a key measure of obesity, may act as a partial mediator in the association between NHHR and depression [12, 13]. Notably, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² is defined as overweight or obesity [14]. This definition not only reflects the continuum from overweight to obesity but also captures the early accumulation of metabolic risk. Focusing on individuals with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² therefore emphasizes the impact of metabolic dysfunction on depression risk and enhances the clinical and public health relevance of the findings, providing valuable guidance for intervention strategies in high-risk populations.

While previous studies have primarily examined the relationship between NHHR and cardiovascular disease or sarcopenia [3–6], the present study hypothesizes that NHHR is associated with depressive symptoms in obese adults, with BMI potentially serving as a mediator. The study aims to test this hypothesis and provide further insight into the intersecting mechanisms linking metabolic dysfunction and mental health disorders.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. NHANES employs a complex multistage probability sampling design to obtain a nationally representative sample of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. Participants provide demographic, socioeconomic, and health information through household interviews, while mobile examination centers (MECs) conduct laboratory testing and physical assessments.

The NCHS Research Ethics Review Board approved all NHANES protocols, and all participants provided informed consent. Detailed information on NHANES study design and data can be found at www.cdc.gov/nchs/NHANES/.

This study included NHANES data from 2013 to 2023. Initially, 47,639 participants were screened. After excluding individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m² (n = 18,391), pregnant women (n = 143), participants under 20 years old (n = 7,597), those missing NHHR data (n = 4,060), missing PHQ-9 data (n = 1,570), and missing covariate data (n = 1,887), a final sample of 13,991 eligible participants was included (Fig. 1).

Exposure variable: NHHR

NHHR was calculated as the ratio of non–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C, defined as total cholesterol [TC] minus HDL-C) to HDL-C [15], expressed by the formula:

Based on previous studies [4, 16], participants were categorized into quartiles of NHHR (Q1–Q4) to facilitate between-group comparisons and to explore potential nonlinear trends. In addition, NHHR was also analyzed as a continuous variable.

Outcome variable: depression

Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a widely used screening instrument with high sensitivity and specificity for depressive disorders [17]. The PHQ-9 includes nine items covering symptoms such as diminished interest, depressed mood, sleep disturbances, fatigue, appetite changes, low self-worth, concentration difficulties, psychomotor agitation or retardation, and suicidal ideation, with four response options (not at all, several days, more than half the days, nearly every day). These items assess symptom frequency over the prior two weeks, scored 0 to 3, yielding a total score range of 0 to 27. A total PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 was used to define depression [17].

Covariates

Based on previous literature and clinical relevance [18–21], the following covariates were included: sex (male/female), age, education level (less than elementary school/grades 9–11 including no diploma/high school graduate, GED or equivalent/some college or associate degree/college graduate or above), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other), marital status (married/living with partner/widowed/divorced/separated/never married), poverty income ratio (PIR), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes status (yes/no/prediabetes), alcohol consumption (current, never, ever), and smoking status (current, never, ever).

Hypertension was defined by any of the following criteria: [1] average systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or average diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg; [2] current use of antihypertensive medication; or [3] self-reported history of hypertension [22]. The average SBP (ASBP) and average DBP (ADBP) were calculated following prior methods [23].

Diabetes was defined by any of the following: [1] fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; [2] HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; [3] random blood glucose or oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 2-hour glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; or [4] current use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin [24]. Prediabetes was defined by any of the following: [1] HbA1c between 5.6% and 6.5%; [2] FPG between 5.6 and < 7.0 mmol/L; [3] OGTT 2-hour glucose between 7.8 and < 11.1 mmol/L; or [4] clinical diagnosis of prediabetes.

Smoking status was categorized as never smoker (< 100 cigarettes in lifetime), ever-smoker (≥ 100 cigarettes lifetime but not currently smoking), and current smoker (≥ 100 cigarettes lifetime and currently smoking).

Alcohol consumption was categorized as never drinker (< 12 drinks lifetime), ever-drinker (≥ 12 drinks in any prior year but none in the past year, or < 12 drinks in the past year), and current drinker (≥ 12 drinks in the past year).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for categorical variables. Differences across NHHR quartiles were analyzed using t-tests and chi-square tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models were applied to examine both linear and nonlinear associations between NHHR and depression.

To investigate the association between NHHR and depression within obese individuals, three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed: an unadjusted model; Model 1 adjusted for sex, age, and race; and Model 2 further adjusted for marital status, education, PIR, hypertension, diabetes status, smoking, and alcohol consumption.

To assess the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, weighted analyses were performed to account for the complex multistage probability sampling design of NHANES, and multiple imputation was applied for missing covariates. Second, stratified analyses using multivariable logistic regression were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the NHHR–depression association across clinically and demographically relevant subgroups. Based on prior literature [25–27], demographic factors (age, sex) and cardiometabolic risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol use) may influence the relationship between metabolic indicators and mental health outcomes; therefore, these variables were selected as potential effect modifiers, and interaction terms were examined. For example, age (originally a continuous variable) was dichotomized as < 60 and ≥ 60 years to explore potential differences in the association between younger and older adults. This approach allows evaluation of whether the NHHR–depression relationship is consistent across clinically and demographically relevant subgroups.

A piecewise linear regression model was used to investigate the nonlinear relationship and threshold effect between NHHR and depression. The optimal inflection point was determined using a grid search algorithm, which iteratively evaluated a set of candidate values and selected the point that maximized the model likelihood as the threshold. The statistical significance of the threshold effect was confirmed via likelihood ratio tests, comparing the piecewise model with the linear model. To further assess the stability of the estimated inflection point, its 95% confidence interval was calculated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples.

Mediation analysis was conducted using the “mediation” package in R (version 4.3.2) to assess whether BMI mediates the association between NHHR and depression. A bootstrap procedure with 1,000 resamples was applied to estimate the sampling distribution of the mediation effects, which is a standard approach in mediation analysis that balances computational efficiency and estimate stability. 95% confidence intervals for the indirect, direct, and total effects were constructed using the percentile method, consistent with the recommended practice in the R “mediation” package, providing robust estimates of the effects and their uncertainty [28].

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) and EmpowerStats (version 4.2). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Association between NHHR and depression in obese individuals

This study found that in the unadjusted model, each 1-unit increase in NHHR was associated with a 7% higher risk of depression (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11). In Model 1, adjusted for demographic variables, each 1-unit increase in NHHR corresponded to a 12% higher risk of depression (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.16). In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), each 1-unit increase in NHHR was associated with a 5% higher risk of depression (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.09). The association between NHHR and depression was stronger in the unadjusted model and Model 1. After further adjustment for additional covariates in Model 2, the odds ratios decreased, reflecting the confounding effects of these variables. All results were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were performed. First, weighted analyses accounting for the complex NHANES sampling design, combined with multiple imputation for missing covariates, produced results consistent with the primary complete-case analyses, indicating that the NHHR–depression association was not substantially influenced by the sampling design or missing data (Supplementary Tables 3–5).

Second, to evaluate the consistency of the association across different population subgroups, stratified analyses and interaction tests were conducted based on age, sex, smoking status, alcohol use, diabetes, and hypertension. Statistically significant interactions were observed for diabetes status and smoking status, suggesting that these factors may modify the NHHR–depression association (P for interaction < 0.05). Specifically, the association was significant among individuals without diabetes (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.04–1.28) and those with prediabetes (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.13), but not among individuals with diabetes (P > 0.05). Similarly, the association was significant among ever-smokers (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.18), whereas no significant association was observed among current smokers or never-smokers (P > 0.05).

For other stratification variables, no statistically significant interactions were detected (P for interaction > 0.05), suggesting that the NHHR–depression relationship was generally consistent across these subgroups. Table 3; Fig. 2 (adjusted for all covariates in Model 2).

Threshold effect analysis of NHHR on depression

After adjusting for all relevant covariates, a threshold effect model was applied, and the piecewise regression model demonstrated a significantly better fit than the linear model (P = 0.021), identifying an inflection point at NHHR = 2.95. Specifically, the linear model suggested a modest positive association between NHHR and depressive symptoms (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.09), whereas the piecewise model identified a stronger association below the threshold (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.32) and no significant association above it (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.94–1.06). These findings indicate that the piecewise regression model more accurately captures the nonlinear relationship between NHHR and depression. Table 4; Fig. 3.

Discussion

Research suggests that the relationship between lipid metabolism and mental health may be nonlinear, with different lipid types potentially exerting distinct effects on various psychological conditions [29, 30]. In the present study, we observed a nonlinear association between NHHR and depression among obese adults. Our findings indicate a threshold effect of NHHR on depression risk in this population. Specifically, the risk of depression increased significantly with rising NHHR levels, but plateaued once NHHR reached 2.95. This relationship was particularly pronounced among individuals without diabetes, those with prediabetes, and ever-smoker. Additionally, BMI partially mediated the association between NHHR and depression in obese adults, accounting for approximately 15.81% of the total effect.

This study is situated within the context of the growing obesity epidemic and explores the potential for lipid profile management as a means of preventing depression. NHHR is an integrated lipid marker that reflects both atherogenic risk and lipid metabolism imbalance [31]. Non-HDL cholesterol encompasses several atherogenic lipoproteins, such as very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), while HDL cholesterol plays a protective role with its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and reverse cholesterol transport properties.

Several potential mechanisms may explain the observed nonlinear association between NHHR and depression in obese individuals: 1.Systemic and central nervous system inflammation: Elevated NHHR levels are associated with higher concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP [32, 33]. These inflammatory factors may influence central nervous system inflammation by altering blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability or modulating peripheral immune signaling, potentially affecting the structure and function of brain regions involved in mood regulation, such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex [34].2.BDNF and neural plasticity: High NHHR is linked to increased inflammation and oxidative stress, which are associated with reduced expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). BDNF plays a critical role in maintaining synaptic plasticity, regulating neurogenesis, and supporting anti-apoptotic processes, and its reduction may contribute to depressive-like behaviors [35, 36]. 3.Oxidative stress and neurotoxicity: Elevated NHHR may reflect a higher proportion of oxidation-sensitive lipoproteins, particularly LDL-C, which can be oxidized to ox-LDL and is associated with increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. This may impair neuronal function, and the synergistic effects of oxidative stress and inflammation can alter neuronal membrane structure and neurotransmitter transmission, creating an environment conducive to depressive phenotypes [37, 38].4.Tryptophan metabolism and serotonin availability: Lipid dysregulation and chronic inflammation may enhance the activity of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), accelerating the metabolism of tryptophan along the kynurenine pathway and reducing its availability for serotonin (5-HT) synthesis. Additionally, IDO pathway metabolites, such as quinolinic acid, may affect depressive-related brain regions through glutamate excitotoxicity and synaptic modulation [39].5.HPA axis function and cortisol levels: Some studies suggest that elevated NHHR may be associated with altered hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity and cortisol levels, which could influence hippocampal volume, neurogenesis, and mood regulation [40].6.Genetic and metabolic factors: Genetic polymorphisms and inflammatory markers may modulate the lipid–mental health relationship by affecting lipid and glucose metabolism, thereby indirectly influencing mood regulation and depression risk [41]. Evidence from metabolic intervention studies further supports this pathway [42, 43].

However, this relationship may not be strictly linear. While NHHR is generally positively associated with depression risk, a threshold effect appears to exist, such that when NHHR exceeds approximately 2.95, its contribution to increasing depression risk begins to plateau. Potential biological explanations include: 1.Negative feedback regulation of inflammation: Elevated NHHR may activate immune regulatory pathways that induce upregulation of anti-inflammatory factors, partially counteracting sustained pro-inflammatory effects and attenuating the increase in depression risk beyond the threshold [44].2.HPA axis desensitization: Chronic stress and high NHHR levels may trigger adaptive negative feedback in the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, reducing cortisol secretion responses and limiting further increases in depression risk [45].3.Saturation effects of neurotrophic factors and neurotransmitters: For example, BDNF or monoaminergic neurotransmitters may reach saturation under high NHHR conditions, such that additional lipid abnormalities have a limited impact on neural plasticity and mood regulation [46].These potential mechanisms suggest that the association between NHHR and depression risk may plateau at higher NHHR levels.

Sensitivity analysis further revealed significant interactions between NHHR and both diabetes status and smoking history. Among individuals without diabetes or in prediabetic stages, the association between NHHR and depression remained significant, but was not observed in diabetic patients. This may be due to the effects of routine treatments for diabetes (e.g. antihyperglycemics, lipid-lowering agents, anti-inflammatories), which potentially modulate lipid-related inflammatory and oxidative pathways and thus attenuate NHHR’s influence on depression risk [47].

Interestingly, a significant association was observed in ever-smokers but not in current or never smokers. Ever-smokers may retain a “physiological memory” of chronic inflammation, lipid disturbances, and oxidative stress due to long-term tobacco exposure, amplifying NHHR’s pathological relevance. Current smokers, despite ongoing exposure, may exhibit temporary metabolic adaptations, while never smokers may not reach the activation threshold for these pathological pathways. Notably, residual subclinical inflammation and metabolic abnormalities can persist even after smoking cessation [48, 49], highlighting the long-term impact of lifestyle exposures on the lipid-mental health axis.

Further mediation analysis showed that BMI accounted for approximately 15.8% of the effect of NHHR on depression. This finding suggests that lipid metabolism disturbances may contribute to depressive symptoms, at least in part, by influencing body weight. Substantial evidence from longitudinal studies and Mendelian randomization analyses has confirmed a causal link between higher BMI and increased depression risk, supporting the role of obesity in depression onset [8, 50, 51]. As an integrated lipid marker reflecting the balance between non–HDL-C and HDL-C, NHHR has been validated as a reliable indicator for dyslipidemia, obesity, and metabolic syndrome [52]. Moreover, recent cross-sectional studies have reported a positive association between NHHR and depressive symptoms [53]. Taken together, these findings provide epidemiological plausibility that elevated NHHR may increase depression risk by exacerbating obesity-related pathways, such as inflammation, insulin resistance, and psychosocial stress [32, 54]. However, it should be emphasized that the mediation results in this study are based on cross-sectional data and cannot establish causality. Future longitudinal and genetic studies are needed to further validate the causal sequence.

Our study has several strengths. First, it utilized the NHANES database, which provides a large, nationally representative sample. Second, by employing stepwise multivariable models, sensitivity analyses, and mediation assessments, we were able to more comprehensively explore the potential mechanisms linking lipid metabolism abnormalities and mental health in obese individuals. Compared with single lipid markers such as LDL-C or HDL-C, NHHR—an integrated indicator reflecting atherogenic lipoproteins and overall lipid metabolic imbalance—may more accurately assess metabolism-related depression risk in this population. Additionally, by applying a threshold effect model to examine nonlinear relationships, this study provides a foundation for developing targeted lipid management strategies for high-risk obese individuals.

However, this study has several limitations, and its findings should be interpreted with caution. First, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Second, depression was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to recall bias and measurement error, potentially attenuating the observed associations. Third, although demographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders were accounted for, residual unmeasured confounding—such as medication use or other mental health conditions—cannot be excluded. Finally, the study focused on obese adults and excluded pregnant individuals, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader population.

Conclusion

In obese adults, higher NHHR levels were significantly positively associated with the risk of depression, with the relationship exhibiting a nonlinear pattern within a certain range. This association was moderated by diabetes and smoking status. Additionally, BMI was found to partially mediate the relationship between NHHR and depression. Future studies should further validate these associations in longitudinal cohorts and investigate the underlying mechanisms through which lipid metabolism abnormalities influence depression, in order to inform targeted intervention strategies for high-risk populations.
