Identification of lactate metabolism-related genes as kidney fibrosis biomarkers through integrated transcriptomic and experimental analyses
Authors: Ye Kuang, Xiuhong Xiang, Chuanmei Peng, Ji Yong, Pei He, Shiyan Nian, Lei Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKidney fibrosis, the final pathological outcome of chronic kidney disease, lacks reliable biomarkers, and the role of lactylation in its pathogenesis remains poorly defined. We integrated multi-omics, machine learning, and experimental validation to identify lactylation-associated biomarkers using two public transcriptomic datasets. After batch correction and differential expression analysis, 13 overlapping lactylation-modified genes were obtained, and eight hub genes were identified via 113 model combinations. Downregulated hub genes regulate core renal metabolic pathways including TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, while upregulated genes mediate immune-inflammatory activation and cytokine signaling. Immune infiltration analysis showed significant immune cell enrichment in fibrotic tissues, with hub genes correlated with T cell subsets. Six hub genes were validated in UUO mouse models and human fibrotic kidney tissues. A five-gene diagnostic panel exhibited robust diagnostic performance with AUCs of 0.89–0.90. This study establishes a lactylation-related diagnostic signature, reveals metabolic-immune crosstalk, and supports early clinical diagnosis of kidney fibrosis.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a substantial global health concern. Globally, 697.5 million individuals suffer from CKD, representing 9.1% of the total population. This figure exceeds the count of patients with other non-communicable chronic conditions, including diabetes, osteoarthritis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and depression.1 As CKD advances to end-stage renal disease, kidney fibrosis represents the culminating pathological process precipitating irreversible renal failure. Compounding this concern, the early stages of CKD frequently exhibit subclinical progression, with patients remaining asymptomatic despite ongoing parenchymal damage.2 This insidious process persists until renal function is entirely compromised, significantly complicating disease prevention and therapy.

Renal fibrosis constitutes the common final pathway driving the progression of diverse CKD to terminal renal failure.3 Its development involves complex interactions among multiple pathophysiological processes, including dysregulated extracellular matrix metabolism, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, inflammatory microenvironment formation, oxidative stress, and epigenetic regulation.4 Although significant strides have been made in elucidating pivotal mechanisms encompassing the transforming growth factor β/small mothers against decapentaplegic (TGF-β/Smad) signaling cascade,5 dysregulated renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation,6 and monocyte/macrophage infiltration,7 the spatiotemporal dynamics linking cell fate decisions, metabolic reprogramming, and immune microenvironment remodeling during renal fibrogenesis remain incompletely elucidated. The progression of CKD instigates profound metabolic transformation in renal tubular epithelial cells and interstitial fibroblasts, manifesting as crippled oxidative phosphorylation and pathological glycolytic activation. This metabolic adaptation generates a lactate-rich intracellular milieu that enables protein lactylation modifications, ultimately governing fibrotic gene expression and signaling via integrated epigenetic and post-translational control mechanisms.8 Studies indicate that lactylation directly modulates profibrotic gene programs at the transcriptional level. Specifically, lactate generated by 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-biphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) drives histone H4K12 lactylation at nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway promoters, thereby triggering expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors.9,10 In diabetic nephropathy models, H3K14 lactylation promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition in renal tubular epithelial cells by upregulating the transcription factor kruppel-like factor 5 (KLF5) and suppressing the epithelial marker E-cadherin, with its modification level positively correlating with the degree of interstitial fibrosis.11 Beyond histones, lactylation extensively regulates non-histone protein functions. Lactylation of the mitochondrial fission 1 (FIS1) promotes its translocation to the outer mitochondrial membrane, inducing excessive mitochondrial fission and apoptosis, whereas lactylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) enhances its transcriptional activity and facilitates ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, thereby aggravating fibrotic progression.10,12 Collectively, these data position lactylation as a pivotal molecular conduit between metabolic reprogramming and phenotype switching. Specifically, it governs the renal immune microenvironment by inducing H4K12 lactylation to steer macrophages toward a profibrotic M2 phenotype and perpetuating glycolytic metabolism, thus cultivating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that accelerates fibrogenesis.13 Simultaneously, lactylation serves as a marker of tissue-specific active enhancers and promotes transcription of NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome-related genes, amplifying local inflammatory responses.14 Collectively, the central role of lactylation in integrating metabolic signals, inflammatory responses, and fibrotic progression is increasingly recognized, providing a novel theoretical framework for deeper mechanistic insights into renal fibrosis and mechanism-driven therapeutics.

Although the involvement of lactate in renal fibrosis has been partially elucidated, the transcriptional landscapes and pathophysiological relevance of lactate-associated genes in this fibrotic context remain insufficiently explored. In this study, fibrosis-related lactate genes were pinpointed via comprehensive mining of bulk transcriptomic datasets. Their functional annotations alongside their associations with immune infiltration in fibrotic kidneys were further investigated. Additionally, the predictive potential of key lactate-related markers for renal fibrosis was evaluated. Collectively, these findings contribute to an enhanced molecular understanding of renal fibrosis pathophysiology and may offer novel biomarkers for clinical diagnosis.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling in kidney fibrosis

The PCA of the GSE76882 and GSE22459 datasets demonstrated clear separation in the principal component space, indicative of substantial batch effects (Figure 1A). Following batch-effect removal, this separation was effectively mitigated (Figure 1B). Sample clustering analysis of the merged dataset before and after normalization further confirmed appropriate data integration (Figures 1C and 1D). After batch correction and normalization, a unified transcriptomic dataset comprising 14,797 genes across 339 samples (215 fibrotic kidney samples and 124 healthy controls) was established. Differential expression analysis between fibrotic and control tissues identified 1,052 DEGs, including 646 upregulated and 406 downregulated transcripts (Figures 1E and 1F). Gene ontology (GO) profiling of these DEGs highlighted substantial enrichments in biological processes intimately tied to fibrotic pathogenesis. Key enriched biological processes included “regulation of cell adhesion,” cellular components such as “extracellular side of the plasma membrane,” and molecular functions like “glycosaminoglycan binding” (Figures 2A–2C). Subsequent interrogation of KEGG pathways implicated DEGs in “phagosome” and “chemokine signaling” cascades (Figure 2D). Identification of lactylation-related hub genes

Machine learning-based screening identified lactylation-related genes associated with renal fibrosis. Across twelve algorithms, 113 models were generated for feature selection, with AUC determined in training, validation, and merged datasets (Figure 4A). The two models exhibiting the highest predictive performance (AUC) were selected, and their commonly identified feature genes were extracted. This process yielded eight hub genes: ALB, ALDOB, HAGH, HIST1H2BK, IKZF1, LGALS1, RALYL, and S100A11 (Figures 4B and 4C). Subsequently, upstream regulatory networks of these hub genes were explored using the RegNetwork database. Predicted upstream miRNAs and transcription factors revealed potential regulatory interactions. Functional enrichment of lactylation-associated hub genes in kidney fibrosis

Subsequent to hub gene identification, co-expression profiling was conducted between these eight genes and the entirety of the integrated transcriptomic background (Figure S2). Based on the resulting correlation profiles, GSEA was conducted to identify biological pathways associated with each gene (Figure 5). The four downregulated genes—ALB, ALDOB, HAGH, and RALYL—exhibited positive correlations with core metabolic processes in renal tissue, including “metabolism of amino acids and derivatives,” “biological oxidations,” and “the TCA cycle and respiratory electron transport.” Conversely, these genes displayed negative correlations with immune-inflammatory pathways, specifically “immunoregulatory interactions between a lymphoid and a non-lymphoid cell” and “interferon alpha/beta signaling.” In contrast, the four upregulated genes—HIST1H2BK, IKZF1, LGALS1, and S100A11—all demonstrated positive correlations with “immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid and non-lymphoid cells.” Among these, IKZF1, LGALS1, and S100A11 also correlated positively with “cytokine signaling in the immune system,” whereas HIST1H2BK was negatively associated with this pathway. Additionally, HIST1H2BK, LGALS1, and S100A11 were negatively correlated with “metabolism of amino acids and derivatives.” Collectively, these findings reveal distinct yet complementary associations: downregulated hub genes are linked to metabolic restoration, whereas upregulated hub genes correspond to heightened immune-inflammatory activity. Association of hub gene expression with immune microenvironment in kidney fibrosis

To dissect immune contributions to renal fibrosis, we mapped the relative abundance of 23 immune lineages in diseased versus healthy kidney tissues. Robust enrichment was observed for 22 populations (p < 0.05)—including activated CD4+/CD8+ T cells, γδ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), T follicular helper cells (Tfh), and T helper 1/2 (Th1/Th2) cells—while Th17 cells alone remained statistically indistinguishable between groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 6A). Correlation analysis among the differentially infiltrated immune cell types further revealed widespread and significant intercellular associations (p < 0.05) (Figure 6B). CIBERSORT analysis identified significant infiltration differences in 15 immune cell subpopulations between renal fibrosis (KF) and control tissues. KF tissues showed significantly increased infiltration of multiple pro-fibrotic immune subsets including B cells, macrophages, and T cell subpopulations, with reduced levels of plasma cells, eosinophils, and resting immune cells (all p < 0.05, Figure S3). Importantly, hub gene expression showed distinct immunomodulatory correlations: the downregulated genes (ALB, ALDOB, HAGH, and RALYL) exhibited significant negative associations with multiple activated or pro-inflammatory immune cells, including activated CD4+ T cells, Gamma delta T cells, T follicular helper cells, activated dendritic cells, immature B cells, type 1 T helper cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells. Conversely, the upregulated genes (HIST1H2BK, IKZF1, LGALS1, and S100A11) demonstrated significant positive correlations with these same immune cell subsets (Figure 6C). The downregulated hub genes and upregulated hub genes exhibit opposite regulatory correlations with the core pro-inflammatory/activated immune cell subsets that are significantly infiltrated in fibrotic renal tissues, highlighting their potential antagonistic roles in modulating the immune microenvironment during kidney fibrosis progression. Construction of biological subtypes and biological differences based on eight hub genes

An unsupervised clustering method was employed to categorize the samples for subsequent analysis. The clustering matrix demonstrated that a clear separation of samples was achieved at K = 2 using the algorithm based on the eight hub genes (Figure S5A). This distinction was further corroborated by PCA, which indicated a clear separation between cluster A and cluster B (Figure S5B). Differential expression of the eight hub genes across the two clusters was visualized by a heatmap, with each gene exhibiting statistically significant differences. Specifically, S100A11, HIST1H2BK, IKZF1, and LGALS1 were found to be significantly upregulated in cluster B, whereas RALYL, ALB, HAGH, and ALDOB were significantly downregulated. Consequently, the expression pattern of hub genes in cluster A was observed to be opposite to that in cluster B (Figures S5C and S5D). To further dissect the biological divergence between clusters, GSVA profiling was conducted. Validation of lactylation-related hub gene expression in experimental and clinical kidney fibrosis

To establish renal fibrosis, a unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model was generated in mice, with sham-operated animals serving as controls. Compared with sham-operated kidneys, UUO-treated kidneys exhibited an irregular morphology, a mottled grayish-yellow surface, firm consistency, and a nodular, coarse texture (Figure 8A). For the UUO model, HE staining showed significant tubular structural damage in the obstructed kidneys, including diffuse tubular dilatation, epithelial cell degeneration/necrosis/shedding, cast formation and interstitial widening; Masson’s staining revealed massive diffuse interstitial collagen deposition, which is the core defining feature of renal fibrosis. Consistent pathological changes were also observed in human KF specimens. (Figure 8B). Consistent with compromised renal function, UUO mice exhibited markedly elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) relative to sham controls (p < 0.01; Figure 8C). Notably, serum creatinine and BUN are systemic renal function markers, unable to directly reflect unilateral renal fibrosis severity in the UUO model due to contralateral compensation, and serve only as an auxiliary reference for overall renal function changes. As lactate is the sole substrate for protein lactylation, we first quantified in situ renal lactate levels, which were significantly increased in UUO fibrotic kidneys (p < 0.001, Figure S6A) and human fibrotic renal tissues (p < 0.001 Figure S6A). We further detected global lactylation via pan-lactyl-lysine IHC, and found that the positive rate of pan-lactyl-lysine staining was significantly increased in both UUO and human fibrotic kidneys (all p < 0.01, Figure S6B). RT-qPCR validation of renal tissues indicated partial concordance between hub gene expression and prior transcriptomic data: HAGH, ALDOB, and ALB were significantly downregulated, whereas LGALS1, S100A11, and IKZF1 showed marked upregulation in UUO mice (all p < 0.05). In contrast, RALYL expression did not differ significantly between groups, and HIST1H2BK exhibited an expression trend opposite to that observed in the transcriptome analysis (Figure 8D). Immunohistochemical staining corroborated these findings, showing reduced protein levels of HAGH, ALDOB, and ALB, and enhanced positivity for LGALS1, S100A11, and IKZF1 in UUO kidneys. No significant differences were detected for RALYL and HIST1H2BK (Figure 8E). Clinical validation using human fibrotic kidney specimens demonstrated that mRNA and protein levels of these genes paralleled observations from the UUO model. Compared with normal renal tissue, fibrotic samples showed significant downregulation of HAGH, ALDOB, and ALB and upregulation of LGALS1, S100A11, and IKZF1 (Figures 8F and 8G).

Collectively, based on the concordant results from both experimental and clinical validation, RALYL and HIST1H2BK were excluded due to inconsistent expression patterns. The remaining six genes—HAGH, ALDOB, ALB, LGALS1, S100A11, and IKZF1—were therefore identified as the final hub genes associated with lactylation in renal fibrosis.

Diagnostic performance of hub genes in kidney fibrosis

We performed feature importance analysis of 6 candidate lactylation-related genes for renal fibrosis prediction using three independent machine learning algorithms (LR, RF SVM). The results were highly consistent across all models: HAGH had the greatest predictive contribution in all three algorithms, while LGALS1 showed the lowest contribution (Figure 9A). The diagnostic potential of the six identified hub genes was evaluated for distinguishing patients with kidney fibrosis from healthy individuals using three distinct machine learning algorithms: LR, RF, and SVM. Model performance was comprehensively assessed through ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis. In single-gene analyses, HAGH consistently exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy across all three algorithms, with AUC values of 0.85 (LR), 0.82 (RF), and 0.84 (SVM), accompanied by low Brier scores (0.16–0.17) and the greatest net clinical benefit. In contrast, LGALS1 displayed the weakest predictive performance among the six candidates and we further performed a head-to-head comparison between the 5-gene model (without LGALS1) and the 6-gene model (with LGALS1 added). The results showed that adding LGALS1 did not improve any key diagnostic metrics, including AUC, brier score and accuracy, meaning it provided no incremental predictive value to the integrated panel. Upon exclusion of LGALS1, an integrated diagnostic model was constructed using the remaining five genes (ALB, HAGH, IKZF1, ALDOB, and S100A11). This combined model outperformed all single-gene models, achieving superior AUC values (0.89 for LR, 0.85 for RF, and 0.90 for SVM), the lowest Brier scores (0.14, 0.14, and 0.13, respectively), and the highest net clinical benefit across all three algorithms. Additional performance metrics further validated the enhanced predictive capability of the integrated model (Table 1). Discussion

Renal fibrosis, recognized as the common pathological endpoint in advanced CKD progression, is currently assessed in clinical practice primarily through invasive renal biopsy. However, this approach is associated with significant limitations for dynamic monitoring.15,16 Consequently, the identification of early biomarkers is considered critical for disease prevention and management. It has been established that following renal injury, metabolic reprogramming is induced in renal tubular epithelial cells, characterized by enhanced glycolysis and subsequent lactate accumulation.17,18 Lactate accumulation has been shown to promote histone lactylation, leading to the downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of α-smooth muscle actin, thereby driving the process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Concurrently, the NF-κB signaling pathway can be activated, further exacerbating inflammatory responses and fibrotic progression.9 Additionally, a vicious cycle is formed between lactate accumulation and oxidative stress, which synergistically accelerates fibrogenesis.19,20 Collectively, this evidence positions lactate as a pivotal regulator driving kidney fibrosis by integrating multiple mechanisms involving metabolism, epigenetics, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Transcriptomic data from the GEO database, combined with a set of 336 lactate-related genes, bioinformatic analyses, and animal experiments, were interrogated to reveal potential biomarkers for the early diagnosis of renal fibrosis. Five lactate-related hub genes—ALB, HAGH, IKZF1, ALDOB, and S100A11—were ultimately identified and were found to possess significant predictive value for renal fibrosis. Notably, all hub genes are experimentally validated lactylation-modified genes with a direct regulatory link to lactylation. Lactylation regulates these genes via direct post-translational modification and indirect epigenetic transcriptional regulation. Our findings confirm activation of the lactate-lactylation axis in fibrotic kidneys, which underpins the dysregulation of these hub genes in renal fibrogenesis.

In fibrotic renal tissues, ALB gene expression was observed to be significantly downregulated. The protein encoded by ALB, serum albumin, is essential for maintaining plasma colloid osmotic pressure and facilitating molecular transport.21,22 x Its reduced expression not only indicates injury and functional loss in renal tubular epithelial cells23 but is also associated with a shift toward a pro-fibrotic phenotype through the epithelial-mesenchymal transition process, during which synthetic functions are diminished.3 Albumin deficiency downregulates angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), resulting in elevated angiotensin II—a potent pro-fibrotic stimulus that drives TGF-β1 synthesis in tubular and interstitial cells, thereby perpetuating fibrogenic cascades.24 Moreover, sustained inflammatory responses during renal fibrosis have been demonstrated to suppress albumin synthesis, establishing a detrimental feedback loop.25 The HAGH gene encodes hydroxyacyl glutathione hydrolase, which is involved in the detoxification of methylglyoxal.26 Its downregulation may lead to methylglyoxal accumulation, thereby inducing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, which in turn can promote ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells and accelerate fibrotic progression.27 Similarly, decreased expression of HAGH was observed in fibrotic renal tissues in this study. Elevated expression of IKZF1 was detected in fibrotic kidneys, suggesting its potential involvement in amplifying local immune-inflammatory responses. The transcription factor IKAROS, encoded by IKZF1, has been reported to enhance the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and antigen-presenting molecules in dendritic cells, promote Th1/Th17 immune responses, and drive interstitial inflammation.28,29 Furthermore, IKZF1 can recruit histone deacetylase 3 to suppress succinate dehydrogenase complex iron sulfur subunit B expression, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction, succinate accumulation, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α stabilization, and amplified inflammation.30 ALDOB, a key enzyme in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, plays a vital role in proximal tubule metabolism.31 Its downregulation is indicative of impaired renal metabolic function, potentially leading to disordered fructose metabolism, abnormal lysosomal acidification, and renal tubular dysfunction,32 all of which may exacerbate cellular injury and fibrotic progression. This finding corroborates marked ALDOB downregulation in fibrotic kidneys, implying compromised metabolic capacity, aggravated cellular damage, and accelerated fibrosis. S100A11, a calcium-binding protein, participates in cytoskeletal regulation and signal transduction.33 Although direct evidence in renal fibrosis remains limited, studies on hepatic and cardiac fibrosis have suggested that S100A11 can promote fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition via modulation of the transforming growth factor β/Smad pathway.34,35 It is therefore plausible that S100A11 may contribute to renal fibrosis through a similar mechanism, though its precise role requires further validation. In summary, the hub genes identified in this study exhibit differential expression in renal fibrosis and are implicated in multiple pathological processes, including tubular injury, metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, and immune-inflammatory responses. These findings yield significant insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying renal fibrogenesis.

The identified hub genes were primarily enriched in two core functional modules during renal fibrogenesis: metabolic regulation and immune modulation. Downregulated genes—ALB, ALDOB, and HAGH—were mainly enriched in pathways involving amino acid metabolism, biological oxidation, the TCA cycle, and respiratory electron transport, indicating a role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis.36 These genes were additionally implicated in suppressing signaling cascades such as lymphoid-non-lymphoid cell interactions and interferon-α/β signaling, suggesting that their downregulation may compromise immunoregulatory functions, thereby promoting inflammation and fibrosis.37 As the primary plasma transporter of tryptophan, decreased albumin levels can lead to elevated free tryptophan, which enhances indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1/tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase-mediated tryptophan metabolism and influences the generation of immunoregulatory kynurenines.38 Albumin also exerts antioxidant effects via its cysteine-34 residue. Hypoalbuminemia has been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and metabolic acidosis, which may compromise mitochondrial respiratory efficiency.39,40,41 Moreover, albumin undergoes tubular internalization, proteolytic processing, and dendritic cell-mediated cross-presentation, priming CD8+ T cells to instigate antigen-specific tubulo-immune crosstalk.42 This interaction has been further corroborated in proteinuric nephropathy models, where the ALB1-24 fragment generated following proximal tubular uptake of albumin can be processed by infiltrating dendritic cells and presented via major histocompatibility complex class I molecules to activate CD8+ T cells.42 Albumin also modulates indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 expression by influencing tryptophan availability, and a feedback regulatory loop exists between indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 and interferon-α/β38,43 ALDOB downregulation may affect serine/glycine synthesis and one-carbon metabolism. Its catalytic product provides substrates for serine/glycine synthesis.44 Moreover, studies in hepatocellular carcinoma have demonstrated that ALDOB can epigenetically regulate transforming growth factor β expression, thereby influencing T cell function,45 suggesting a potential analogous role in the immunometabolic regulation of renal fibrosis. As a methylglyoxal detoxification enzyme, impaired HAGH function leads to methylglyoxal accumulation, fostering advanced glycation end product accumulation, disrupting enzymatic activity and one-carbon metabolic homeostasis, and inhibiting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.27 Methylglyoxal metabolites can also serve as ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, regulating dendritic cell function and Th17/regulatory T cell balance, and influencing γδ T cell activity.46,47 In contrast, the upregulated genes IKZF1 and S100A11 were positively correlated with immune modulation and cytokine signaling pathways. IKZF1 can regulate interferon-α production in plasmacytoid dendritic cells, playing a key role in type I interferon signaling.48 S100A11 may activate the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase and NF-κB pathways via the receptor for advanced glycation end products, inducing the release of IL-1β and TNF-α. This promotes dendritic cell migration and maturation, drives macrophage M1 polarization, and exacerbates renal interstitial inflammation.49,50 Thus, the five hub genes identified in this study constitute an interconnected metabolic-immune regulatory network in renal fibrosis. ALB, ALDOB, and HAGH appear to exert protective effects primarily by maintaining metabolic homeostasis and suppressing excessive immune responses, and their downregulation may collectively lead to metabolic imbalance and impaired immunoregulation. Conversely, the upregulation of IKZF1 and S100A11 likely promotes fibrotic progression by enhancing intercellular immune communication and pro-inflammatory signaling. This integrated perspective illuminates the pathophysiological mechanisms governing renal fibrogenesis and positions these genes as actionable therapeutic targets for pathway modulation.

Both downregulated (ALB, ALDOB, and HAGH) and upregulated (IKZF1 and S100A11) genes were significantly correlated with multiple T cell subsets in the immune microenvironment, indicating their cooperative role in regulating T-cell-mediated responses and remodeling the immune landscape in renal fibrosis. These genes were mainly associated with activated CD4+ T cells, γδ T cells, follicular helper T cells, Th1 cells, and regulatory T cells. Existing evidence underscores the importance of T cells in renal fibrosis. Regulatory T cells are involved in maintaining renal immune homeostasis; their depletion has been shown to aggravate renal injury, whereas adoptive transfer can attenuate inflammatory infiltration.51 Th1 cells, through the secretion of factors such as interferon-γ, can promote glomerular inflammation and injury progression.52 Clinical observations have also indicated a positive correlation between albumin levels and CD4+ T cell counts, suggesting that albumin may influence T cell function during fibrosis.53 Regarding metabolic-immune crosstalk, ALDOB downregulation may affect T cell function through multiple mechanisms. In diabetic nephropathy, interleukin-1β (IL-6), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) have been shown to suppress podocyte ALDOB expression. Its deficiency further activates the protein kinase B (Akt)/β-catenin pathway, promoting podocyte epithelial-mesenchymal transition and fibrosis.54 ALDOB deficiency may also disrupt the Th17/regulatory T cell balance, creating a local immunosuppressive microenvironment that exacerbates kidney damage.55 Furthermore, ALDOB participates in regulating T cell glycolytic metabolism, and the metabolic disturbances resulting from its deficiency may influence fibrosis via related signaling pathways.56 Among the upregulated genes, IKZF1 enhances antigen presentation and inflammatory responses, promoting the activation of Th1 and follicular helper T-like cells and establishing an inflammatory amplification loop.29 S100A11 engages the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGEs) to activate NF-κB and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades, eliciting IL-6 and TNF-α secretion and amplifying T-cell-mediated interstitial inflammation.57 Concurrently, S100A11 modulates Th17/Treg balance by reprogramming CD4+ T cell metabolism, thereby fostering fibrogenesis.58 Collectively, these hub genes orchestrate the fibrotic immune microenvironment through the regulation of T cell activation, differentiation, and effector function. Among them, ALB, ALDOB, and HAGH are primarily associated with T cell homeostasis and an inhibitory microenvironment, whereas IKZF1 and S100A11 tend to promote inflammatory T cell responses. These results imply that targeting specific T cell subsets and their related pathways may offer novel strategies for immune intervention in renal fibrosis.

Renal biopsy constitutes the definitive diagnostic criterion for kidney fibrosis. However, as an invasive procedure, it provides histopathological information only at a single time point and is not suitable for dynamic monitoring and prognosis assessment. Imaging modalities such as ultrasonography are susceptible to variations in patient physiological status and are associated with considerable inter-observer variability, limiting their accuracy for quantifying fibrosis severity. Consequently, the identification of early diagnostic indicators to enable timely initiation of anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic therapies remains an unmet critical clinical need. Therapeutic targeting of lactate-related pathways has shown preliminary efficacy in renal fibrosis. Investigations into lactate-related genes may yield novel biomarkers for this condition; however, whether such biomarkers can overcome the limitations of current diagnostic methods requires further clinical validation.

Based on the integrated analysis of GEO transcriptomic data, lactate-related genes, bioinformatic tools, and animal models, combined with clinical sample validation, five lactate metabolism-related hub genes (ALB, HAGH, IKZF1, ALDOB, and S100A11) were identified as closely associated with renal fibrosis pathogenesis. These genes were confirmed to participate in fibrosis via two core functional modules—metabolic regulation and immune modulation—forming a synergistic metabolic-immune network. Their expression patterns correlated significantly with renal interstitial T cell infiltration and showed predictive value for fibrosis. The constructed molecular classifier exhibited substantial discriminatory power. Taken together, these findings illuminate the mechanistic basis of renal fibrogenesis and establish actionable biomarkers for early, non-invasive clinical intervention.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, hub gene protein validation was limited to semi-quantitative IHC, lacking the absolute quantification of Western blot, which will be addressed in follow-up studies using fresh frozen renal tissues. Second, the 5-gene signature was only validated in two public transcriptomic datasets and a single-center clinical cohort; we have completed sample collection of a multi-center independent cohort with consistent enrollment criteria, and RT-qPCR validation of the signature’s generalizability is ongoing. Third, the signature was only validated in renal tissues, with no detection in non-invasive blood/urine samples, no systematic correlation with clinical fibrosis stage, eGFR or urinary biomarkers, and no prospective non-invasive validation; these translational studies will be the core focus of our follow-up multi-center clinical research. Finally, mechanistic interpretations of the hub genes in renal fibrosis are mostly extrapolated from other tissues or diseases, with no direct functional validation in renal fibrosis-specific models, and their causal role in fibrogenesis will be verified via follow-up in vitro and in vivo experiments. Despite these limitations, this study provides novel diagnostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for renal fibrosis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Data acquisition

Two gene expression datasets pertaining to kidney fibrosis, GSE76882 and GSE22459, were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database.64 The GSE76882 dataset, generated on the affymetrix HG U133 plus PM peg arrays (GPL13158), consisted of 99 kidney biopsy tissues with normal functioning and 175 kidney biopsy tissues with interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and functioned as the training set for model construction. The GSE22459 dataset, generated on the affymetrix human genome U133 plus 2.0 array (GPL570), comprised 25 kidney transplants with normal histology and 40 kidney transplants having fibrosis with inflammation, and was utilized as the independent external validation set.

Animal model of unilateral ureteral obstruction

Twenty male C57BL/6 J mice (Beijing Spike Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were randomized to UUO (n = 10) or Sham (n = 10) cohorts. Animals housed under specific pathogen-free conditions received ad libitum access to food and water. All protocols gained ethical approval from the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Yunnan Zequn Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Approval No. ZQSW21-2501-01).

Surgical interventions proceeded under isoflurane anesthesia (Catalog No. 792632, Merck & Co., Inc.). Following midline laparotomy, the left ureter was isolated. UUO mice underwent ureteral ligation near the renal pelvis, whereas Sham mice underwent isolation without ligation. The abdominal wall was sutured. Postoperatively, mice recovered and survived for 14 days.65

Human clinical specimens

Human kidney tissue specimens were collected from patients diagnosed with renal atrophy at Yan’an Hospital Affiliated to Kunming Medical University between January 2024 and January 2025. A total of 80 fibrotic kidney tissue samples and 80 non-fibrotic kidney tissue samples were included in this study. All specimens were evaluated by two independent senior renal pathologists in a double-blind manner, with histopathological examination as the gold standard. The KF group was defined as renal interstitial fibrosis area ≥25% (KDIGO 2024 clinical practice guideline for the evaluation and management of chronic kidney disease). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Yan’an Hospital Affiliated to Kunming Medical University (Approval No.: 2025-333-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to specimen collection. All procedures involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Method details

Data preprocessing

All samples from GSE76882 and GSE22459 underwent normalization before subsequent analysis. The R packages “limma” and “sva” were utilized to attenuate batch effects and reduce experimental variability across datasets.59 The two datasets were combined by preserving only common genes, so minimizing technical noise and amplifying biologically significant signals in later analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted with the “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” packages.60 The “preprocessCore” was employed for data normalization and the elimination of outlier-induced artifacts.61 A total of 336 genes changed by lactylation were aggregated from previously published studies and included in the analysis.66,67,68

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Pairwise differential expression analysis was performed using the “limma” R package to contrast renal fibrosis against control specimens. Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined by stringent thresholds: False discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value <0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >0.5.

Functional enrichment profiling

Gene Ontology (GO) annotation (spanning BP, CC, and MF categories) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping were implemented via “clusterProfiler”69 to characterize DEG functionality. The intersection between DEGs and lactylation-modified genes was isolated, with enrichment analyses subsequently applied to this subset to define lactylation-driven biological processes in fibrogenesis.

Identification of diagnostic biomarkers via machine learning

A comprehensive machine learning analysis was conducted on three transcriptomic datasets (GSE76882 as the training set; GSE22459 and a merged dataset as validation sets) to identify potential diagnostic biomarkers for renal fibrosis. Twelve classical machine learning algorithms were integrated, including Stepwise Generalized Linear Model (Stepglm, including both, backward, and forward directions), Elastic Net (Enet), Partial Least-Squares Regression with Generalized Linear Model (plsRglm), Ridge Regression, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Generalized Linear Model Boosting (glmBoost), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Naive Bayes Classifier (NBC).70,71 A two-stage modeling approach was employed, involving one algorithm for feature selection and another for classification modeling, resulting in 113 distinct machine-learning combinations. Candidate diagnostic hub genes were identified as the intersection of genes selected by the two top-performing algorithms. Model performance was evaluated based on Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC), with an area under curve (AUC) value exceeding 0.7 considered indicative of satisfactory diagnostic performance.72 Additional metrics (accuracy, recall, F1-score, specificity, Brier score) were used to assess overfitting risk. Strict anti-leakage controls were implemented: all preprocessing parameters were fitted exclusively on the training set and then applied to validation sets; differential expression analysis and feature screening were performed solely within the training set.

Functional annotation and immune contexture mapping of hub genes

For each hub gene, co-expression networks were constructed against the merged dataset transcriptome. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) profiling of Reactome pathways (https://curator.reactome.org/) was executed through “clusterProfiler” based on gene correlation rankings.73 Immune infiltration was deconvoluted using ssGSEA in the “Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)” R package,62 with independent validation performed via the MCP-counter algorithm in the “immunedeconv” R package. Spearman’s rank correlation interrogated relationships between hub gene expression and immune scores. “ggplot2” facilitated graphical presentation, with significance established at p < 0.05.

Molecular subtyping and functional profiling

Consensus clustering leveraging hub gene expression profiles was conducted on the merged dataset through “ConsensusClusterPlus”,63 delineating distinct molecular subtypes. Following curation of Hallmark, KEGG, and Reactome gene sets from MSigDB, ssGSVA enrichment scores were computed via “GSVA” to quantify pathway activity across samples. Inter-subtype pathway profiles were subsequently evaluated, with heatmaps rendered through “pheatmap” to illustrate differential enrichment landscapes.

Predict upstream miRNAs and transcription factors for hub genes

Putative upstream modulators—encompassing miRNAs and transcription factors (TFs)—targeting hub genes were retrieved from RegNetwork (https://regnetworkweb.org/).74 Regulatory architectures were subsequently charted using Cytoscape (v3.10.4, https://cytoscape.org/), depicting interactions between hub genes and their upstream controllers.

Biochemical assessment and tissue harvesting

On day 14, mice underwent terminal anesthesia, with blood harvested via cardiac puncture for biochemical analysis. Plasma creatinine (Sekisuimedical, Catalog No. 20162401431) and blood urea nitrogen (Roche Diagnostics, Catalog No. 20172400971) levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostic, Model: cobas 8000). Following blood collection, cardiac perfusion with normal saline was performed. Kidney tissues from both the obstructed (left) and contralateral (right) sides were harvested for subsequent histological and molecular analyses.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis

Kidney tissues from sham-operated and UUO model mice were collected and fixed for histopathological examination. Serial paraffin-embedded sections (4 μm thick) were prepared for staining. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Beyotime, Cat. No. C0105S) staining visualized general tissue architecture, whereas Masson’s trichrome (Beyotime, Cat. No. C0189S) characterized collagen deposition. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis utilized a commercial detection kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. P1353S) per the manufacturer’s guidelines. All antibodies were diluted 1:200 and incubated overnight at 4°C under conditions optimized per manufacturer guidelines or preliminary validation. Post-secondary antibody incubation, chromogenic visualization employed 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin. Microscopic imaging captured tissue architecture, with ImageJ assessing immunopositive areas or staining densities.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) of kidney tissue

Total RNA isolation from human and mouse kidney tissues utilized TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Cat. No. R0016). Concentration and purity (A260/A280) were assessed via NanoDrop micro-spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Model 840–317400). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis from 1 μg total RNA employed HiScript IV RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Cat. No. R423-01) per the manufacturer’s guidelines, with products stored at −20°C pending analysis. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) proceeded with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat. No. Q711-02) on an ABI 7500 Plus system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Thermal cycling entailed: initial denaturation (95°C, 30 s) followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95°C, 10 s) and annealing/extension (60°C, 30 s). All reactions ran in triplicate. Gene expression quantification relied on cycle threshold (Ct) values normalized against glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), with relative mRNA levels calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Please refer to Table S1 for the relevant primer sequences.

Lactic acid content detection

Lactate concentration in renal tissues was detected using a commercial lactate colorimetric assay kit (Beyotime, Cat. S0208S) following the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Briefly, 20 mg frozen renal tissue was homogenized in pre-cooled assay buffer on ice, then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected for absorbance detection at 570 nm. Lactate concentration was normalized to the total protein content of each sample, and the final result was expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Machine learning diagnostic modeling

All analyses used R 4.3.3 with caret, e1071, randomForest, dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2 packages. The dataset (6 candidate lactylation-related gene expressions, binary renal fibrosis [KF] outcome: KF = 1/Control = 0, no missing values) was split into 70% training and 30% independent test sets via outcome-stratified sampling. All gene data were centered and scaled by training set parameters. LR, SVM and RF were used for diagnostic modeling of each single candidate gene and the final 5-gene panel (excl LGALS1). Feature importance was assessed via RF built-in, absolute standardized LR coeffs, and permutation for SVM. All models were trained on training, predicted KF prob on independent test (threshold = 0.5), and evaluated via ROC, calibration and DCA.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Experimental replicates comprised at least three independent runs. Normality was confirmed prior to parametric comparisons. Two-tailed unpaired t-tests assessed two-group differences, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (with post-hoc corrections) evaluated multi-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
