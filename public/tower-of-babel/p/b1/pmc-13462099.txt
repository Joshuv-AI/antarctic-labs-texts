Adoption of circular agricultural practices among small-scale farmers
Authors: Likun Ma, Khadijeh Bazrafkan, Hassan Alipour, Pouria Ataei, Hamid Karimi, Hajar Zarei, Loke Kok Foong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of Circular Systems (CoS) is essential for enhancing the sustainability of agricultural systems and strengthening farm resilience. The linear model of ‘take-make-dispose’ has a significant impact on both agriculture and the environment. In particular, water scarcity and soil degradation are common challenges facing small-scale farmers located in regions like Fars, Iran. The socio-psychological factors influencing the adoption of circular agricultural practices (CAPs) by small-scale farmers in the Fars Province of Iran are studied in this paper using an applied version of a UTAUT2 model. A quantitative, cross-sectional survey of 321 small-scale farmers in the Fars Province was carried out using simple random sampling. Data collected from this survey were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) in SmartPLS 3.0 to evaluate 10 hypotheses. All 10 relationships were supported by the results. Less effort expectancy is the strongest predictor of the Behavioral Intention (β = 0.293) as well as Habit (β = 0.169). The strongest predictor of the behavior was behavioral intention (β = 0.535). In addition, facilitating conditions have a significant direct effect on both intention (β = 0.133) and use behavior (β = 0.225). This indicates that facilitating conditions represent not only a source of motivation but also a critical enabler. The research results support the validity of the UTAUT2 model in a new context—circular agriculture in a developing economy—thus extending the validity of the generalized UTAUT2 model and demonstrating the usefulness of socio-psychological models in the context of agricultural adoption research. Through this research, the findings present a clear policy avenue that also includes the importance of providing simpler ways for agricultural producers to practice methods to reduce the perceptions of complexity, use existing habits and social networks of farmers to their advantage, and finally provide tangible resources (financial, physical, human, informational) and support services to assist in overcoming the gap between intention and behavior so producers are able to continue implementing such practices on their farm over time.

Introduction

The global agricultural industry is facing a critical dilemma regarding ensuring food security while decreasing its environmental impact1–5. For generations, agriculture has largely followed a one-way (linear) model of production, use, and disposal, characterized by intensive input use, substantial waste generation, and the depletion and processing of natural resources. The linear model has produced tremendous short-term productivity increases, but it has come at an extremely high long-term price to the environment through loss of habitat (soil), lack of fresh water, decreased habitats/biodiversity, increased and carbon released into the atmosphere6–8. The concept of the circular economy has been developed in response to long-term failures within global agriculture. The application of circular economy principles in agriculture, or circular agricultural practices (CAPs), represents a new way to think about the interaction between food production and the earth, and has the potential to change the current agricultural paradigm entirely9,10. Importantly, the sustainability challenges addressed by circular agriculture are not confined to any single country or region. Water scarcity, soil degradation, and declining farm resilience increasingly characterize semi-arid and developing regions across the Middle East, North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of South Asia11. Therefore, understanding the behavioral drivers of CAP adoption in one resource-constrained setting may generate insights relevant to other developing economies facing similar agroecological and structural pressures.

Circular agriculture seeks to close nutrient loops through waste reduction and increasing biomass value. Circular agriculture does not adhere to the linear model and therefore encourages utilisation of previously mentioned practices. Practices such as recycling agricultural residues (e.g., through composting and biochar), integrated nutrient management, water harvesting and recycling, agroecological practices, and the integration of crop and livestock systems fit within circular agriculture12–15. The goal of Circular Agriculture is to establish regenerative agricultural systems such that by-products or outputs from one process are considered inputs for another process to lower the need for external finite resources; reduce levels of pollution; and promote healthy soils and resilient farms16,17. The benefits of adopting CAPs are many. The environmental benefits include higher organic carbon levels in soils and improved water quality, and the potential for greater economic returns because of lower input costs and more value-added from “waste”. Additionally, social benefits are created through enhanced food sovereignty and stability of the livelihoods of small-scale and other mixed systems farmers18–20.

Iran is facing an increased urgency to transition its agriculture towards circular practices. The agricultural sector, which is an important contributor to the national economy as well as a major employer, is currently facing acute and growing environmental limitations21–24. Chronic droughts, declining groundwater levels, increasing soil salinity, and reliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides threaten the long-term sustainability of Iran’s agricultural sector25–27. For example, Fars Province (located in the southwestern part of the country) is one of the regions in Iran where agribusiness is overwhelmingly dependent on a diminishing supply of subterranean water from its plains. Over-use of groundwater through traditional farming practices has caused land subsidence, soil degradation, and has created a very uncertain future for many of the province’s small-scale farmers28–30. This context makes Fars Province a critical and compelling case study for investigating the adoption of CAPs among its small-scale farmers, as the shift to more sustainable practices is not merely an option but a necessity for survival. While Fars Province represents a geographically specific case, it also reflects broader structural patterns common in many semi-arid developing regions: high dependence on groundwater, prevalence of smallholder farming systems, exposure to climate variability, and limited institutional capacity to enforce sustainability transitions. Thus, the province serves not merely as a local case, but as an analytically relevant context for examining how behavioral adoption theories perform under environmental stress and structural constraints.

Several policies to promote sustainable CAPs have been initiated by the Iranian government. These include incentives to implement water use efficiency, soil health management, and other sustainable agricultural practice extension programs31,32. However, the rate of adoption among many small-scale farmers has been slow and varied. The disconnect between the government policy’s intent and farmers’ actual adoption of CAPs presents a significant research problem. Farmers’ decision-making process for the adoption of CAPs are not well understood. In addition to the technical and economic barriers that may exist (such as limited access to credit, machinery, or markets for the sale of their circular products), it is increasingly recognized that socio-psychological factors such as farmers’ beliefs, social influences, and cognitive processes play a critical role in their decision-making for the adoption of CAPs. However, these factors have yet to be explored in any significant depth within the context of Iranian small-scale agricultural practitioners. It should be noted that this gap is not unique to Iran. Across developing economies, policy frameworks promoting sustainable agriculture frequently encounter behavioral resistance or uneven adoption33. Consequently, there is a need to test established behavioral models in non-Western, smallholder-dominated agricultural systems to determine which drivers are robust across contexts and which are shaped by local institutional and cultural conditions34,35.

The decisions made by small-scale farmers who use circular should not be seen as purely rational economic decisions based on a practice’s potential for profit or technical superiority, but rather, these decisions are made in the context of a farmer’s social and cultural norms, values, experiences, and risk perception36–38. A farmer may recognize the long-term benefits of composting but decide not to do so for a number of reasons such as perception of the process being too complicated, lack of peer support, or fear of short-term loss of yields39,40. A farmer may be motivated to use water-saving irrigation technologies because of their prospective lower water bills; however, the motivation may also be tied to a sense of environmental responsibility or desire to enhance social status within the community as a progressive farmer41. Therefore, effective policy, extension services, and communication campaigns must consider these factors and the human side of agricultural change in small-scale farmers42–45.

The purpose of this research is to address the existing gap by examining the social and psychological factors that influence the adoption of circular agricultural practices in Fars Province, Iran. The study is grounded in a robust theoretical framework from Information Systems and Technology Adoption, namely UTAUT2. However, UTAUT2 gave an array of models/explanatory constructs, that include performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, hedonic motivation, price value, and habit, that represent the same constructs/models that will be used to analyze and study the adoption of new agricultural practices. By applying UTAUT2 within the domain of circular agriculture in a resource-constrained developing economy, this study does more than examine a local case; it provides an empirical test of the model’s cross-context applicability. Specifically, the research evaluates whether core UTAUT2 constructs retain explanatory power in smallholder agricultural systems characterized by environmental risk, institutional constraints, and collective social structures. These constructs can be understood as capturing the social and psychological drivers of behavior and as guiding the decision-making process, including individuals’ perceptions of the benefits of specific agricultural practices, the ease of their implementation, and the influence of both social and internalized factors.

In adapting UTAUT2 for Circular Agriculture in the context of small-scale agriculture in Iran, this research makes a new and sophisticated contribution to theory. This study demonstrates the application of a well-established behavioral model to a new area and culture, therefore extending the generalizability of the model. Importantly, the study also seeks to differentiate between potentially universal behavioral mechanisms (e.g., perceived performance benefits and effort expectations) and context-sensitive drivers (e.g., institutional support structures or localized social norms). By doing so, the findings are positioned to inform both national policy in Iran and broader theoretical debates regarding the generalizability of technology adoption frameworks in sustainability transitions. The empirical findings from this research will provide detailed evidence-based information to help policymakers, agricultural extension officers and NGOs operating in Iran. By determining which sociopsychological factors have the greatest salience, interventions can be tailored to provide more effective support for the agricultural sector. For example, if Social Influence is found to be a dominant driver, then programs should target opinion leaders and encourage farmer-to-farmer knowledge exchange. Conversely, if effort expectancy is a significant hindrance, simplifying practices and providing hands-on training should be emphasized. Iran has an ecological and economic need to transition to Circular Agricultural Practices. Fars Province represents a critical microcosm for addressing this transition. At the same time, the analytical insights derived from this case may inform sustainability transitions in other semi-arid and smallholder-dominated regions where environmental degradation intersects with behavioral and institutional barriers to innovation adoption. The slow adoption of CAPs among small-scale farmers indicates that further investigation of the decision-making processes of this group is necessary. At the same time, the analytical insights derived from this case may inform sustainability transitions in other semi-arid and smallholder-dominated regions where environmental degradation intersects with behavioral and institutional barriers to innovation adoption. This paper argues that understanding and addressing the socio-psychological factors influencing farmers’ behavior is essential for increasing the widespread adoption of circular agricultural practices (CAPs). By applying and testing the UTAUT2 model, this research seeks to identify key drivers and barriers, thereby contributing to both theoretical advancements in adoption studies and the development of practical strategies to promote a more sustainable and resilient agricultural sector among small-scale farmers in Iran.

Theoretical background

The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology 2 (UTAUT2)

The UTAUT2 model, developed by Venkatesh and his colleagues46,47, extends the original UTAUT framework, which was derived from the integration of eight prominent theories explaining user acceptance of information technology, including TRA, TAM, the Motivational Model, and TPB48–50. By synthesizing constructs from these theories, UTAUT provides a unified framework for explaining both behavioral intention and actual technology use. It identifies four core determinants of behavioral intention—performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions—alongside moderating factors such as voluntariness of use, gender, experience, and age51,52.

To address consumer technology adoption issues, UTAUT2 provides a comprehensive view of how consumers perceive technology in a non-organizational, voluntary environment compared to the original UTAUT framework which did not fully account for all possible construct drivers in this area53. Venkatesh et al.46, introduced three additional constructs, Hedonic Motivation, Price Value, and Habit, as well as an adaptation of the moderating influences so they would fit within the realm of consumers. As a result, the model suggests seven constructs influencing an individual’s Behavioral Intention to utilize a given technology; (1) performance expectancy is how much an individual thinks that utilizing a certain technology will enable them to achieve improvements in either their job performance or overall quality of life; (2) effort expectancy is the level of effort required to utilize the technology54; (3) social influence: the extent to which an individual perceives that important others believe they should use the technology; (4) facilitating conditions: the individual’s belief that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support use of the technology55,56; (5) hedonic motivation: the fun or pleasure derived from using the technology; (6) price value: the cognitive trade-off between the perceived benefits of the technology and the monetary cost of using it; and (7) habit: the extent to which an individual tends to perform behaviors automatically because of learning. In UTAUT2, behavioral intention, habit, and facilitating conditions are theorized to directly influence use behavior48,49,57,58.

Through numerous empirical studies in multiple fields, the UTAUT2 model is demonstrated to be a strong and reliable model that has been validated59–62. Although the model originated from technology, specifically mobile technologies (e.g., mobile internet and mobile banking), the constructs within UTAUT2 are generalizable and have been shown to hold in many different areas, such as the adoption of m-health apps in healthcare, the adoption of e-learning platforms in education, and the use of green products63–65. However, despite its broad application, limited research has explicitly examined the performance of UTAUT2 in resource-limited, smallholder-dominated agricultural systems, particularly in developing and semi-arid contexts where environmental risk, institutional fragility, and collective social norms shape decision-making processes. The model’s broad applicability is becoming increasingly evident in the agricultural domain, where the UTAUT/UTAUT2 framework has been used to examine farmers’ adoption of precision agriculture technology, mobile-based agricultural advisory services, and sustainable irrigation practices66–71. Nevertheless, most prior applications have focused on discrete technologies rather than systemic sustainability transitions such as circular agricultural practices, which require behavioral, cultural, and structural adaptation simultaneously. This distinction is theoretically important because adopting CAPs involves not only technological acceptance but also shifts in production philosophy, resource management norms, and inter-farm cooperation. UTAUT/UTAUT2 is a comprehensive model that captures experiential (hedonic) and intrinsic factors (hedonic motivation), utilitarian calculations (performance expectancy and effort expectancy), social influences (social influence), and contextual enablers (facilitating conditions) along with the automaticity and habitual nature of behavior (habit)67,71–73. By applying UTAUT2 in the context of circular agriculture in Iran, this study provides an empirical test of whether the model’s core behavioral mechanisms remain robust under environmental stress and structural constraint. In doing so, the study contributes to ongoing debates regarding the cross-context generalizability of technology adoption theories beyond Western consumer markets. UTAUT2 captures the combined effects of economic considerations, social dynamics, and individual motivations, including habitual behavior, thereby reflecting the complex decision-making processes of farmers considering the adoption of circular agricultural practices (CAPs). Accordingly, it provides both a strong theoretical foundation and empirical support for understanding the sociological and psychological factors influencing adoption.

Hypotheses development

H1

Performance expectancy will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt CAPs.

The concept of performance expectancy reflects the perceived usefulness and the perceived relative advantage of a new practice66,67,71 and serves as a foundation for many models of technology adoption. Regarding CAPs, farmers are more likely to develop a favorable intention to adopt when they perceive that the practice will yield direct economic benefits for their farming operations. This perceived benefit serves as a significant determinant of their decision-making and adoption behavior. Economic benefits that farmers can expect from adopting CAPs include increased crop yield, improved soil fertility and structure, reduced pest and disease incidence, and enhanced long-term land sustainability66,68,70. There is strong theoretical support for this expectation; both the Theory of Reasoned Action and the Theory of Innovation Diffusion state that decision-makers will be motivated to behave in a manner that they believe will result in a desired outcome74. Many studies conducted within the field of agricultural extension provide empirical evidence that supports the link between farmers’ perception of improved yield performance being a major factor influencing their decision to adopt. For example, conservation agriculture research conducted in Africa found that the perception of improved yield performance was a significant factor that drove adoption by farmers75–77. Integrated pest management (IPM) and organic farming research also show that the advantages of both of these agricultural systems are positively associated with farmers’ willingness to adopt them78,79. Farmers in Fars Province, Iran, are under tremendous economic and environmental stress and perceive that CAPs would allow them to achieve greater resource efficiency and farm resilience, which will serve as a key motivating factor for their intent to implement such practices in the future (Fig. 1).

H2

Less effort expectancy will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt circular agricultural practices.

The ease perceived by people when using new technologies, or in this case, new agriculture methods is termed “effort expectancy”48. The use of CAPs may differ from traditional agriculture methods, and will include new methods, such as composting, using worms to improve soil, and building structures to support the collection of rainwater. These practices have the potential to be seen as difficult, require much labor, or be difficult to gain knowledge about18–20. Perceived ease to use is the major determiner of intentions for using new technologies, as shown by the Technology Acceptance Model74,80,81. Based on this rationale, individuals generally prefer options that demand lower cognitive and physical effort, assuming all other factors remain constant82–85. A wide variety of agricultural studies have demonstrated that this is supported with strong empirical evidence. For example, Abebe et al.86 and Wordofa et al.87 studied the adoption of soil conservation practices in Ethiopia. They both found that complexity is a key barrier to this. Similarly, two studies conducted on dairy farmers in Germany88,89 showed that when farmers perceive that it will be easy to implement the practice of grazing, they will make a greater commitment to adopting those practices than if they were to believe that implementing the practice would be more difficult. In the case of farmers in Fars Province, who may have restricted availability to use their time and/or labor, and also have limited access to technical support, they will be likely to reject any practice that is deemed to require more training or is more difficult to implement than what they are currently using. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the less effort required to implement CAPs (i.e., the less effort expectancy), the greater the intention by farmers to adopt those practices (Fig. 1).

H3

Social influence will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt CAPs.

Social Influence reflects how an individual’s social network—including a person’s family, friends, neighbors, and influential community members—impacts their intentions to act90,91. In many rural/agricultural communities, where communities are likely to have strong social ties and collective norms, social influence can be very strong92–97. The theoretical basis of this construct derives from the Theory of Reasoned Action and Theory of Planned Behavior, Social Cognitive Theory, which assert that an individual’s behavior is influenced by how they believe others feel they should behave98–100. Empirical evidence101,102 exists for the strength of Social Influence in the agricultural sector. Additionally, recent studies conducted within Iranian Agriculture98,103,104 demonstrate that Social Influence from neighboring farmers and extension agents have a substantial impact on the adoption of sustainable agricultural practices. Research conducted by Zhong et al.105, Mahdavi106, and Atta-Aidoo et al.107, looking at water conservation methods used in India and Ghana, indicated that farmers are more likely to adopt new irrigation technologies when they observe similar successfully implemented innovations among their peers. Small-scale farmers in Fars Province, Iran, often rely on informal means of knowledge exchange. They place high value on social standing and typically depend on their immediate associates for support when making decisions regarding the adoption of new irrigation practices. Support and encouragement from their reference groups will result in the creation of expectations through normative pressures and socially acceptable behaviors that lead to increased intention to adopt (Fig. 1).

H4

Facilitating conditions will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt CAPs.

In UTAUT2, Facilitating Conditions are believed to have a direct influence on Use Behavior, but they also affect Behavioral Intention indirectly via perceptions about Facilitating Conditions51,52. This finding is consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior, which posits that Perceived Behavioral Control will have an impact on both Behavioral Intention and Use Behavior48,49. As a result, when farmers perceive that there are adequate resources, information, and support for them (high Facilitating Conditions), they can reasonably expect to have sufficient resources to adopt and therefore form a strong intention to adopt108. Evidence supporting this relationship exists. For example, Xie et al.68 found that perception of government and other types of support correlates positively with the adoption of ecological agriculture among farmers in Chian. Many recent studies of sustainable practices109,110 confirm that when farmers have confidence that the necessary training programs, subsidies, or equipment are available for use, they feel ready to experiment with the methods they would like to try. In Fars Province, farmers’ awareness of and trust in support systems for CAPs—such as accessible extension services, reliable input supply chains, and potential financial incentives—are expected to increase their intention to adopt CAPs by reducing perceived risks and enhancing confidence in successful implementation (Fig. 1).

H5

Hedonic motivation will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt CAPs.

Hedonic motivation in UTAUT2 reflects the pleasure or satisfaction a user derives from engaging with a particular technology. In agriculture, this can manifest as the intrinsic value associated with farming practices and the use of techniques that align with the farmer’s personal values, which can influence their adoption decisions. This inherent value could manifest itself in the empirical study of Community Agri-Food Partnerships or CAPs, which would include the enjoyment of testing out or discovering new techniques and methods, as well as finding pleasure in being in sync with the natural world, having improved biodiversity within the farmer’s soil, and a sense of pride in being environmentally responsible. The underlying theory of hedonic motivation comes from the motivational model, which separates extrinsic motivators from intrinsic ones. While hedonic motivation is often ignored in economic models of farmer decision-making, there is considerable empirical evidence to support its role as a motivator. Research on organic farmers as well as studies conducted in various countries on sustainable practices within farming communities70,111, have demonstrated that value-driven beliefs about caring for the earth and being connected to the natural world are powerful motivators and sources of fulfilment for many organic farmers. According to research by Wijayanto et al.112 regarding the adoption of agroforestry in Indonesia and its effect on well-being has been done; the study discovered that there is an important role of non-monetary satisfaction. In Fars Province of Iran many farmers have a cultural and historical connection to their lands. The intrinsic joy and moral satisfaction that comes with engaging in a more ecologically sustainable agricultural practice, are seen to be positive motivators for farmers’ intention to adopt C/A/P in addition to other utilitarian reasons (Fig. 1).

H6

Price value will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt CAPs.

The concept of Price Value reflects farmers’ evaluation of the benefits of a practice relative to the costs involved, considering the extent to which perceived negative aspects are minimized. Many small-scale farmers face a tough decision when weighing the cost versus the benefit of making a change to their farming practices, as profit margins are low113. CAPs will be particularly important in this situation because they often require new purchases (such as composting bins and drip irrigation systems) with the intention to increase savings on chemical fertilizer costs, pesticide costs and water costs, yet require an upfront investment (i.e., money) to buy those new systems. The Price Value construct theoretically has roots in the Rational Choice Theory (RCT) which states that humans are naturally motivated to seek the most utility (benefit) for the least cost, and just like many other constructs within the realm of agriculture, the economic perspective is well represented through the agricultural literature regarding the adoption of sustainable agricultural practices. For example, cost-effectiveness has been shown to be a greater indicator/subset of positive adoption behavior than other factors, like environmental concern and other attitudinal factors114,115. The research conducted by Haji and his colleagues80 on the use of pressurized irrigation systems in Iran indicates that even after acknowledging the many benefits these systems provide, the financial burden of purchasing a heavy amount of equipment remains a key barrier to the successful adoption of irrigation systems for smallholders. For farmers in Fars Province, the perception that CAPs offer good “value for money”—where the economic benefits, whether from input cost reduction or premium product markets, are perceived to outweigh the initial and operational costs—is hypothesized to be a critical driver of a positive behavioral intention to adopt these circular practices (Fig. 1).

H7

Habit will have a positive influence on farmers’ behavioral intention to adopt CAPs.

Habit is a factor in how people carry out their actions automatically through learning; their past actions serve as motivators for their actions in the future48,49. Agricultural routines (i.e., the methods that farmers employ) are often based on the expertise that has been developed over numerous years of experience and often over multiple generations116,117. Theoretical foundation for how habit influences intention is based on cognitive automaticity (the cognitive association between a given environment and a particular action/cognitive reaction). Each time a person repeats an action, their mental association between their cognitive responses and their habits will grow stronger. Consequently, an automatic response that has been repeated in a similar context becomes easier to access and therefore more probable118,119. A farmer’s habit may lead them to follow a certain course of action directly, or their habit may lead to an intention that leads them to select a default option or follow a path of least resistance119. There is empirical evidence to support the idea that past behaviors have a strong effect on future behaviors; however, in agricultural contexts, there are numerous research studies that have documented farmers’ habitual patterns of behavior affecting the intentions of farmers. For example, studies concerning sustainable farming practices have indicated that the past behavioral patterns of farmers have a significant influence on the behavior of farmers120–122. Research conducted in Ethiopia123 found that a farmer’s long-established habit of utilizing conventional tillage has created an intention-behavior gap for them to adopt conservation agriculture. In the context of Fars Province, if a farmer has already begun to incorporate elements of circularity (e.g., recycling some on-farm waste) out of routine, this established habit is hypothesized to positively predispose them, forming a stronger intention to formally adopt and expand their use of CAPs, as it aligns with their existing automated behaviors (Fig. 1).

H8

Behavioral intention will have a positive influence on farmers’ use behavior of CAPs.

Most socio-psychological models view behavioral intention as the core proxy for an individual’s motivation to perform a given behavior, and therefore, as a tangible expression of an individual’s conscious commitment48,49,57,58. The relationship between intention and behavior is one of the pillars of the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), and the UTAUT framework. According to theory, individuals with behavioral intentions will likely perform the intended behaviors if they have the autonomy to do so. A strong behavioral intention must be the first indicator of behavioral performance92,93. In agriculture, many empirical studies demonstrate this. Specifically, Avemegah et al.124, studying the adoption of soil conservation practices in South Dakota, reported that farmers’ adoption behaviors were most directly predicted by their behavioral intentions. Likewise, a meta-analysis of Klöckner125 conducted in the environmental domain established a strong correlation between intention and pro-environmental behavior. For small-scale farmers in Fars Province, a firm and well-formed intention to engage in CAPs (driven by the various socio-psychological factors previously outlined) is hypothesized to be a primary driver that leads to the observable implementation and sustained use of these practices on their farms, assuming no major external barriers intervene (Fig. 1).

H9

Facilitating conditions will have a positive influence on farmers’ use behavior of CAPs.

Facilitating Conditions are the factors that affect an individual’s ability to perform a certain behavior based on the infrastructure (both organizationally and technically) provided by the organization51,52,126. According to UTAUT2, Use Behavior is influenced directly by Facilitating Conditions and that an individual can only perform a behavior if Facilitating Conditions are in place46. For CAP adoption, this means that CAP adoption requires access to all of the essential resources such as (i) financial assistance or loans, (ii) required supplies (e.g., compost bin, mulch materials), (iii) training and technical assistance from extension services, and (iv) access to markets for CAP products, including organic produce. The concept of facilitating conditions is an extension of the Theory of Planned behavior’s construct of perceived behavioral control99. The absence of Facilitating Conditions has been cited as a major barrier to the global adoption of sustainable agricultural practices. Kabir and Rainis127 found that access to extension services was critical for farmers in Bangladesh when they were considering adopting integrated farm management practices. Savari et al.128 found that the lack of government support, including financial assistance and access to credit, are major barriers to Iranian farmers’ adoption of soil conservation methods. For farmers in Fars Province, the presence of robust facilitating conditions (such as government-backed training programs, micro-finance schemes for green technologies, and established supply chains for organic inputs) is hypothesized to be a critical enabler that translates their intention to adopt CAPs into actual, sustained use behavior (Fig. 1).

H10

Habit will have a positive influence on farmers’ use behavior of CAPs.

According to Venkatesh et al.46, the UTAUT2 model proposes that Habit is a direct predictor of Use Behavior, which suggests that there are cases where individuals engage in behaviors without engaging in conscious thought processes. Verplanken and Aarts118 and Wood and Neal119 identify the role of Habits as occurring automatically and without needing to form an intention or plan. In the case of farmers’ established agricultural methods, many behaviors that farmers engage in are directly related to their Habits, meaning that they are performed almost automatically because they follow the same habitual process for executing those behaviors120–122. There are also theoretical bases for a direct path from Habit to Behavior, which can be explained by the development of cognitive dual-system theories, which distinguish between internationalist-based reasoning behaviors and cue-based automatic behaviors129. Studies conducted in an agricultural context have shown that there is considerable empirical support for the direct impact of Habit on Behavior, with the study by Huang et al.120 on the use of pesticides found that Habi t was a strong predictor of farmers’ Behaviors because they used the same Habit of applying pesticides, regardless of whether this was a calculated or unconscious response to the specific conditions faced by the farmers whenever they applied pesticides. Overall, as habits develop and become more established for specific CAPs, they can become automated (Fars Province, e.g. practice of returning crop residues to the soil). Therefore, even if a farmer’s conscious intention wavers, the force of habit can sustain the use behavior, making it a crucial direct determinant of the continued adoption of circular practices (Fig. 1).

While the hypotheses are tested within the specific context of Fars Province, the theoretical logic underpinning H1–H10 derives from well-established cross-disciplinary behavioral theories (e.g., TRA, TPB, Rational Choice Theory, and cognitive automaticity theory). Therefore, the structural relationships proposed in this model are expected to reflect broader behavioral mechanisms that may operate in other smallholder-based and sustainability-transition contexts. At the same time, the relative strength of specific paths (e.g., social influence or facilitating conditions) may vary depending on institutional arrangements, extension systems, market maturity, and cultural norms. By empirically examining these relationships in a semi-arid developing economy, this study enables a distinction between potentially universal adoption drivers (such as performance expectancy or effort expectancy) and context-sensitive determinants shaped by local governance, infrastructure, and social capital structures. Thus, the present research does not assume automatic generalizability; rather, it positions Fars Province as a theoretically informative case through which the boundary conditions of UTAUT2 can be assessed in sustainability-oriented agricultural transitions.

Research methodology

Research design

The socio-psychological factors that affect how small-scale farmers in Fars Province, Iran use CAPs were investigated with a quantitative cross-sectional survey research design. A quantitative approach is the best choice because it gives the researchers numerical measures of variables so that they can use statistical techniques to test the hypotheses they have made based on a theoretical model (Saunders et al., 2019). The study aimed to quantify how constructs from the Technology Acceptance Model 2 (UTAUT2), including performance expectancy, less effort expectancy, and social influence, affect farmers’ behavioral intention and use behavior. Since data were collected at one point in time, a cross-sectional design provided a snapshot of the relationships between the variables. A longitudinal study would be better for establishing causality than a cross-sectional study; however, a cross-sectional design is effective and efficient in identifying patterns and strengths of correlational relationships between predictors and outcomes (Creswell & Creswell, 2018). The data collection instrument was a structured questionnaire, which ensured standardization in the responses and allowed for robust statistical analysis to validate the adapted UTAUT2 model in the context of Iranian circular agriculture.

Study area

In Fars Province, one of the largest agricultural regions in southwestern Iran, the local farming culture has been instrumental in the development of the country’s food production system. At the same time, the region faces some of the most severe environmental challenges globally, highlighting the need for more effective adoption of CAPs. This is primarily due to Fars Province’s role as a major producer of strategic crops such as wheat, barley, corn, sugar beets, and citrus. However, the agricultural sector has become extremely vulnerable due to the over-exploitation of groundwater, resulting in significant declines in water tables in plains such as Marvdasht and Darab. In addition to the drop in water tables, soil salinity and degradation (Keshavarz & Karami, 2016) are becoming acute challenges for farmers in the Fars region, where a large proportion of farmers rely on small-scale agricultural production methods. Therefore, as a result of this combination of environmental pressures and the prevalence of small-scale farmers, Fars Province has been identified as an ideal and critical case to study the transition from the traditional linear agricultural production model to the potentially more sustainable circular agricultural production model. In this study, the focus was on the practices of farmers across all agroecological systems in the Fars region, from mountains to semi-arid plains, in order to better understand the drivers for adoption of CAPs in this environment and provide valuable insights for local and national policymakers.

Population, sampling, and data collection

This study’s target populrandomation included every small-scale farmer who practices circular agricultural practices (CAPs) throughout Fars Province. In partnership with the Fars Province Agricultural Jihad Organization (which maintains a full database of registered farmers), an official and updated registry of small-scale farmers was obtained, including information on farmers’ names, national identification codes, village location, farm size, type of main crop, and contact details (telephone number and address). This registry constituted the sampling frame for the present study. Before sample selection, the database was screened to ensure that only active small-scale farmers (defined as farmers cultivating less than 10 hectares during the most recent agricultural year) were included in the sampling frame. Farmers who were retired, inactive, or had incomplete contact information were excluded. The final sampling frame consisted of 186,325 eligible farmers distributed across different counties of Fars Province. Given the large geographical spread of farmers across diverse agroecological zones in Fars Province, a stratified random sampling approach was adopted to enhance representativeness and reduce potential sampling bias. Specifically, farmers were first stratified based on county-level administrative divisions (and proportionally distributed according to the number of small-scale farmers in each county). This stratification ensured adequate representation from mountainous, semi-arid plain, and irrigated agricultural systems within the province. Within each stratum (county), simple random sampling was applied. A random number generator in SPSS was used to assign random numbers to all farmers listed in the sampling frame, and respondents were selected proportionally from each stratum according to its population weight. In this way, each farmer had a known and non-zero probability of selection. The size of the population and its geographical dispersion justified the use of a probability sampling technique to ensure representativeness and reduce potential sampling bias. In determining the minimum sample size required for this study, the statistical power analysis technique as suggested for partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was used. Statistical power was estimated using the G*Power software (Faul et al., 2009); for an effect size of 0.15, a power of 0.95, and a significance level of 0.05 for the proposed model (containing up to 10 predictors), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 386. To account for possible non-response, 450 farmers were initially contacted. Farmers were first contacted via telephone using the contact information available in the sampling frame to explain the purpose of the study and request their participation. In cases where telephone contact was unsuccessful, trained enumerators coordinated with local agricultural extension agents and farmer cooperatives to arrange in-person visits. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. A combination of methods was used to collect data using questionnaires. Trained enumerators conducted face-to-face interviews both in the field and through local farmer cooperatives, while a digital questionnaire was available for farmers who had internet access. Of the 386 questionnaires that were returned, 321 were completed correctly, resulting in a valid response rate of 83.2%, which is an excellent response rate for survey research. The 65 questionnaires that were incomplete or invalid were excluded from the final analysis.

Research instrument

The researcher employed a three-part structured survey as the chief data collection tool. Part A requested basic demographic and farm characteristics (e.g., gender, age, education level, farming experience, size of farm and main sources of agricultural information). Part B was used to measure the variables from the updated UTAUT2 model through five-point Likert Scales (1 = Strongly Disagree – 5 = Strongly Agree). The specific variables from Part B and their sources can be found in Table 1. Performance expectancy was defined as the degree to which farmers believe that CAPs would enhance productivity and sustainability on their farms. Part C measured the dependent variable (use behavior), defined as the frequency with which participants reported using various CAPs. Part C was also measured using five-point Likert Scales ranging from (1 = Never) to (5 = Always), including practices such as composting, integrated pest management, and precision water management. Before the questionnaire was distributed to participants, a pilot test was conducted with 30 farmers who were not included in the final sample. All 30 pilot participants completed the questionnaire, and the data were analyzed using SPSS to assess internal consistency reliability. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficients for each construct were above the minimum acceptable level of 0.70, indicating satisfactory reliability.

In addition to reliability testing, content validity was evaluated by an expert panel consisting of 10 specialists selected based on their academic background and professional experience. The panel included three professors in agricultural extension, two experts in environmental psychology, two specialists in environmental sociology, two researchers in sustainable agriculture, and one expert in survey methodology. All experts held doctoral degrees and had a minimum of five years of research or field experience related to sustainable agriculture, behavioral research, or instrument development. The experts were asked to review the questionnaire using a structured evaluation form assessing clarity, relevance, representativeness, and comprehensiveness of each item. Based on their feedback, several revisions were implemented. Specifically, ambiguous wording in five items was clarified, technical terminology was simplified to improve comprehension among small-scale farmers, two overlapping items were removed to reduce redundancy, and minor adjustments were made to the sequencing and formatting of questions to improve logical flow and response accuracy. Following incorporation of these revisions, the final version of the questionnaire was administered for full-scale data collection. After data collection, construct reliability and validity were rigorously assessed using SmartPLS 3.0. The composite reliability (CR) scores were all above 0.7, indicating internal consistency reliability. The average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct exceeded 0.5, establishing convergent validity. Discriminant validity was assessed for all constructs using the Fornell–Larcker criterion, whereby the square root of the AVE for each construct was greater than its correlations with other constructs.

Data analysis

Data analysis consisted of two primary steps, using SmartPLS 3.0 software that utilizes the Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM). For the research models in this study, the application of the UTAUT2 to a novel application of theory development necessitated that PLS-SEM is a methodology to consider. In addition, PLS-SEM handles non-normally distributed data as well as complicated models that incorporate various constructs and indicators. The first step of analysis was the evaluation of the measurement model (outer model). The evaluation provides the measurement of indicator loadings, composite reliability to determine internal consistency reliability, and convergent and discriminant validity discussed previously. This step supports the construction of an accurate measurement of the constructs being evaluated, as well as being easily distinguished from one another. The second stage of analysis tested the structural model (inner model). This testing evaluated the predicted relationships among constructs by determining the strength and direction (β) of those relationships. Using a bootstrapping method with 500 subsamples, the individual path coefficients and their significance (t-statistics and p values) were assessed. The model’s predictive power was evaluated by examining the coefficient of determination (R²) for the key endogenous constructs, specifically Behavioral Intention and Use Behavior.

Results

Measurement model results

The measurement model has been assessed and demonstrates strong psychometric properties for most constructs; thus, provides an excellent basis on which to conduct structural model testing (Table 2). Results indicate good reliability of indicators, internal consistency and high convergent validity. With the exception of UB5 (0.343) and PV3 (0.598), all outer loadings are higher than the minimum recommended level of 0.708. Although UB5 exhibits a relatively low outer loading, it was retained in the final model for both empirical and theoretical reasons. Despite this low loading, the Use Behavior construct demonstrates acceptable Composite Reliability (CR > 0.70) and an AVE above the recommended threshold of 0.50, indicating satisfactory convergent validity at the construct level. According to Hair et al. (2019), the removal of an indicator is not mandatory when overall reliability and validity criteria are met. Furthermore, UB5 captures a contextually relevant dimension of actual use behavior in circular agriculture, and removing it solely to improve statistical fit could narrow the conceptual domain of the construct and weaken its content validity. In addition, the results of the VIF values for all items are well under the conservative cutoff of 3.3, indicating that the potential for multicollinearity issues among the measures is minimal. Additionally, each of the constructs has shown to have excellent internal consistency reliability through the Composite Reliability (CR) scores being above 0.70 and ranging from 0.799 (Price Value) to 0.903 (Performance Expectancy). Therefore, the items for each construct consistently measure the same underlying concept (Table 2).

The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values provide an adequate measure of convergent validity for a majority of the constructs analyzed. The AVE values for the Behavioral Intention (0.650), Habit (0.753), Hedonic Motivation (0.641), Performance Expectancy (0.699) constructs are all above the minimum threshold of 0.50, suggesting that they are able to account for a large proportion of variance in their respective measurement indicators. However, some constructs have AVE values very close to or slightly higher than the minimum. Less effort expectancy (AVE = 0.574), price value (AVE = 0.575) and social influence (AVE = 0.586) are at the cut-off but are lower in value. According to Hair et al. (2019), an AVE value below 0.50 is acceptable if the composite reliability (CR) of the construct exceeds 0.70, which is the case for all these constructs, providing justification to retain them, as the CR confirms their internal consistency. The most concerning construct is Use Behavior, which has an AVE value of 0.528. While this is higher than the minimum threshold of 0.50, this value is confounded by very low loadings from UB5. If UB5 were removed from the model, it is likely that the AVE for Use Behavior would significantly increase, thus providing additional evidence for the validity of this construct (Table 2).

As shown in Table 3, the Fornell-Larcker discriminant validity assessment of the measurement model indicates that it meets the required psychometric standards. The main requirement for the Fornell-Larcker criterion is that the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for each construct (presented on the diagonal) must be greater than its correlations with all other constructs in the model. The results indicate that all nine constructs satisfy this requirement. Specifically, the square root of the AVE for Behavioral Intention (0.806) exceeds its highest correlation with Use Behavior (0.774). Similarly, the square root of the AVE for Social Influence (0.765) is greater than its strongest correlation with Behavioral Intention (0.743). A comparable pattern is observed for Performance Expectancy, where the square root of the AVE (0.836) is higher than its correlation with Use Behavior (0.740). Although several inter-construct correlations are moderately high—such as the correlation between Less Effort Expectancy and Facilitating Conditions (0.742)—these values remain below commonly accepted multicollinearity concern thresholds (e.g., 0.80–0.90), and indicator-level VIF values are well below the conservative cutoff of 3.3, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a threat to the stability of the estimates. Conceptually, while Less Effort Expectancy and Facilitating Conditions are theoretically related within the UTAUT2 framework—both reflecting supportive aspects of adoption—they capture distinct dimensions, with the former referring to perceived ease of use and the latter reflecting the availability of external resources and support infrastructure. The observed correlations therefore indicate logical theoretical relatedness rather than construct redundancy. This pattern holds throughout the matrix, providing strong evidence that each construct captures a unique yet theoretically coherent phenomenon within the adapted UTAUT2 framework. The established discriminant validity confirms the integrity of the measurement instrument and justifies proceeding with confidence to structural model evaluation and hypothesis testing.

Structural model results using SMART-PLS

Based on the analysis of the structural model, empirical evidence strongly supports the use of the adapted UTAUT2 framework to explain the adoption of CAPs by small-scale farmers in Fars Province, Iran. All ten hypothesized relationships were statistically significant (p < 0.05), thereby confirming the importance of socio-psychological factors within this context (Figs. 2, 3; Table 4). The structural model shows that the variables predicted a significant amount of the variance in behavioral intention to use (R² = 0.755) and actual use behavior of CAPs (R² = 0.635). A farmer’s behavioral intention to adopt CAPs was most strongly influenced by less effort expectancy (β = 0.293, p < 0.001), which is particularly notable in the context of circular systems because they may be seen as complex because of the integration of species, the long-term management cycles, and intensive knowledge requirements associated with these activities. The effect of less effort expectancy was so strong that it indicates that simplified extension messages, demonstrations of low-labor techniques (such as natural mulching and simple composting systems), and a clearly defined step-wise process for implementing CAPs, would be the most important means to increase a farmer’s intention to adopt these systems. Additionally, habit (β = 0.169, p < 0.001) and performance expectancy (β = 0.175, p = 0.001) were the second- and third-strongest significant predictors, respectively, of behavioral intention to adopt CAPs. Habit formation is paramount to making use of current sustainable systems. Agricultural extension services have the opportunity to nudge farmers by framing the new CAPs, as extensions of their existing sustainable practices/benefits. In this study, performance expectancy has a moderate beta value, indicating that farmers do not believe there will be a dramatic increase (immediate) to crop yields from application of CAPs. Also, the significant effect of performance expectancy on intention indicates that farmers do believe they will see improvements to the health and productivity of their crops over time (and are therefore willing to adopt CAPs to realize such improvements) and support the statement on the ecological services of CAPs outweigh the short terms economic benefit. The results indicated that social influence (β = 0.144, p = 0.035), facilitating conditions (β = 0.133, p = 0.021), economic value or price value (0.094, p = 0.004), and hedonic motivation (β = 0.091, p = 0.043), although smaller than the influence of performance expectancy represent significant and positive influences on farmer intention to adopt CAPs (Figs. 2, 3; Table 4). The finding of social influence reinforces the fact that farming in this area is a very social and community-oriented activity and therefore, the goals of farmers to adopt CAPs will not only depend on their success with adopting the CAPs, but will also depend upon the level of acceptance by the farmer’s peers and by local or regional agricultural leaders. Although the effect size of price value is small in this study, the finding suggests that price value remains an essential economic consideration, even for agricultural practices that provide environmental benefits.

Confirming the model’s ability to explain farmers’ use behavior is essential. Behavioral intention has the strongest direct effect on use behavior (β = 0.535, p < 0.001), supporting the premise of intention-based models. This finding indicates that when farmers intend to adopt CAPs, they are more likely to follow through with actual implementation. Discussions and policy implications

Less effort expectancy emerged as the most significant predictor of the behavioral intention to adopt CAPs. This is an enticing finding for small-scale circular agriculture in Iran and indicates that the perceptions of ease and difficulty in learning about and implementing the combined integrated practices are the key influencers for farmers’ decisions to adopt the practice. Although this study is situated in Fars Province, the centrality of effort expectancy aligns with core assumptions of UTAUT2 and suggests that perceived complexity may represent a broadly generalizable barrier to circular agriculture transitions in other developing regions facing structural and resource constraints. This finding is supported by a diverse body of literature on the adoption of sustainable agricultural practices on a global scale, which constantly describes complexity and perceived difficulty as a major limitation to the adoption of sustainable agricultural practices. In particular, existing research on the adoption of conservation agriculture in African countries has confirmed that practices viewed as labor intensive or technically complicated will experience resistance to adoption regardless of the longer-term benefits of adoption87,134. In addition, research by Mbow et al.135 in Africa found that the perceived management complexity associated with integrating trees with crops and livestock acts as a barrier for farmers to adopt CAPs, particularly in areas where weak extension support exists. These cross-regional similarities indicate that while water scarcity in Fars provides the immediate problem context, the mechanism linking perceived effort to adoption intention is likely transferable to other semi-arid and resource-constrained agricultural systems. Therefore, it is important for policymakers and extension services to take action based on this finding. In the process of designing and developing CAPs, it is imperative that simplification and modularization are a prominent focus. Rather than presenting CAPs as one complete, complex transformation of a farming system to the farmer, extension programs should break it down into smaller components that are manageable and can be added on incrementally and with little effort. For example, instead of promoting alley-cropping utilizing a complete circular system, CAP programs should promote the use of one nitrogen-fixing tree species (such as Acacia) for soil improvement through alley-cropping. Likewise, CAP programs should promote the use of simple composting methods for composting (such as pit composting) rather than the use of technically demanding vermicomposting systems. In addition, training and technical support for farmers should shift away from traditional theoretical lectures and focus more on practical hands-on training and demonstrations conducted on model farms, so that farmers can see how easy it is to implement specific techniques. Finally, CAP programs can reduce the cognitive burden on farmers and make the management practices less daunting by providing them with simple pictorial guides and checklists in their local language. By systematically reducing the perceived effort of implementing low-effort farming practices, these programs can create a pathway for unlocking the high level of intention that is predicted to be generated by this key driver.

Habit exerts a positive influence on both behavioral intention and use behavior through two distinct pathways. This suggests that habitual farming practices can play a dual role: while they may constrain change, they can also facilitate the adoption of new practices. In the context of CAPs, this indicates that existing habits may support behavioral transitions when appropriately aligned with sustainable practices. The greater the number of farmers with pro-environmental (PE) habits are likely to have a greater chance of intention to adopt CAPs, as well as an increased likelihood of engaging in automatic behaviors associated with those CAPs. This is consistent with behavioral science literature showing that habit can serve as a heuristic operating in complex settings120–122. In agricultural research Teshome et al.123 indicated that previous behaviors among farmers are significant predictors of future practices, as they are typically influenced by the habit of performing the same routine. This highlights the significant influence that established routines have on the inertia of future behavior. Importantly, the dual role of habit identified in this study contributes to the broader behavioral literature136,137 by demonstrating that in circular agriculture contexts, habitual practices can be strategically leveraged rather than treated solely as barriers to innovation. This insight may extend beyond Iran to other traditional farming systems where routinized practices shape long-term land management decisions. As a result, in the development of behavioral interventions for farmers, practitioners should also focus on how to build on these already present positive routines. To accomplish this, Extension Agents should begin their assessments by determining which sustainable habits currently exist on the farm (for example recycling specific crop residue), and what other existing habits (traditional soil-water conservation) can be built upon to promote additional environmentally sustainable behaviors. New CAP components may be viewed as logical extensions or improvements of the existing habits of farmers (e.g. Farmers who have the habit of regularly collecting livestock manure). For example, instead of referring to the practice of composting manure with crop waste as the introduction of something “new,” use phrases like “taking the next step”, thus, creating the opportunity for farmers to think about their existing systems, and the opportunity for them to view and incorporate composting into their production systems as part of their normal process. Therefore, it reduces the psychological novelty of making this change and demonstrates the continuity of their behaviors through the practice of systematic composting within the context of the existing system. By providing this framework, policy can further support farmers by recognizing and rewarding them for making incremental improvements to their current systems, instead of just recognizing those farms that convert completely to a certified CAPs. By honoring farmers’ existing knowledge, this creates a lower perceived risk and cognitive burden associated with adopting a completely new system.

The findings from this study indicate that while the positive association between intent to adopt circular agriculture and the perceived value of using it is significant, the impact of perceived value is smaller than the impact of the effort associated with using it. Thus, this finding highlights a major difficulty for promoting CAPs. Although CAPs provide multiple ecosystem services—such as improved soil quality, enhanced climate resilience, increased biodiversity, and reduced long-term risks—these benefits are often not reflected in short-term crop yields, which remain the primary metric for evaluating farmers’ performance. As such, the findings support the theory that although farmers view CAPs as beneficial, they may be cautious or skeptical concerning how much immediate agronomic benefit they will obtain from CAPs, especially considering many times they have experienced competitive scenarios (for example, competition for water and light) or no visible short-term benefits. The same rationale is present in a study in a semi-arid country where long-term durable CAPs benefits are frequently overlooked in favor of the pressing needs associated with producing food on an annual basis138. This pattern suggests a potentially generalizable tension in developing economies between short-term productivity pressures and long-term ecological benefits, reinforcing the need to adapt UTAUT2 constructs to sustainability transitions where performance gains may be delayed rather than immediate. Therefore, the ways in which we communicate the benefits of CAPs must be adjusted to help create an understanding of how he/she will “perform” once he/she adopts CAPs. In addition to communicating the benefits of CAPs in broad terms (i.e., “you will produce more food”), communicate the intermediate benefits that will occur before these long-term ones should be communicated. To document the biophysical (such as soil organic matter and water infiltration) and economic (savings achieved by farmers through using less chemical inputs and having additional sources of revenue) benefits obtained from establishing long-term on-farm demonstration sites that used participatory research; this will provide farmers the ability to conduct their own research, by measuring and monitoring the results of their practices on their own farms, so that they can establish verifiable and credible proof of the benefits they experienced. Finally, establishing relationships between premium markets for products produced using CAPs and environmentally friendly practices will help to provide economic performance incentives for farmers while providing benefits to the environment.

Social influence and facilitating conditions have very important roles in the decision-making process of a farmer’s decision to adopt a newly available technology. In addition to having an expectation of support available to them (facilitating conditions), farmers feel an added confidence to make a more serious intent to adopt a technology when they observe others, including their peers, respected community leaders and extension agents, using that technology (social influence). This pattern is extremely strong within rural communities similar to those found in Fars Province. While the strength of social influence may vary across cultural contexts, its statistical significance in this study supports prior cross-country validations of UTAUT2. This finding indicates that socially embedded diffusion processes remain central in collective rural societies. Future comparative studies could explicitly test whether the magnitude of this effect differs between highly individualistic and highly collectivist agricultural systems. The importance of such determining factors is also highlighted by Rogers’139 Diffusion of Innovations theory and by empirical studies, such as Teshome et al.123 in Ethiopia, which show that social networks and access to extension personnel are among the most significant influences on farmers’ adoption of sustainable practices. Therefore, the combined influence of these factors suggests a 2-pronged approach to increasing the adoption of new technology. First, in order to utilize the social influence aspect of adoption, agricultural programs should focus on identifying and empowering “champion farmers” or local opinion leaders who have already adopted conservation agriculture practices. Champion farmers serve as much more than just a technical example to other farmers. Champion farmers also serve as trusted social referents. Secondly, establishing methods for farmers to share information/farm knowledge with each other (e.g., field days, cross-visits and social media groups) can increase the influence of the social influence aspect of adopting conservation agriculture practices more effectively than simply providing top-down extension services. To improve farmers’ confidence in the system, it is critical that the communication regarding facilitating conditions is accompanied by evidence that the necessary support will be there when they need it. Timely access to quality farming inputs, creating financing options for farming investments (such as microcredit or input vouchers), and ensuring extension agents are always available for on-site assistance are some of the elements that need to be addressed. Additionally, planning for the future needs to incorporate the identification of community leaders through social networks as a way to develop the confidence of the community in the system, not just one individual at a time.

Although they are not the primary source of motivation, hedonic motivation and price value enhance our understanding of how intentions are formed. Hedonic motivation refers to deriving pleasure from working in harmony with nature, observing its processes, and engaging directly with the environment. Similar motivations have been observed among organic farmers in Europe, who cite personal satisfaction and connection to their land as key reasons for adoption140. Price value helps explain how farmers assess whether to adopt sustainable or regenerative farming techniques. This involves a continuous process of balancing the financial, environmental, and social value of any farming technique. The results of these studies provide insights for improving both the experiential and economic attractiveness of CAPs. By incorporating these extended UTAUT2 constructs, this study tests the model’s applicability in sustainability-oriented agricultural innovation, extending its external validity beyond digital technology adoption to circular bioeconomy transitions in developing contexts. Participatory experimentation and citizen science in extension work offer farmers opportunities to document changes on their fields (e.g., using simple soil testing kits or counting pollinators). Storytelling and visual documentation can also enhance intrinsic satisfaction. To enhance the perceived financial value, efforts must target both sides of the cost–benefit equation. On the cost side, smart subsidies or cost-sharing programs can reduce initial investment barriers. On the benefit side, developing value chains and establishing market linkages for circular products (timber, fruit, medicinal plants, carbon credits) can increase returns. Additionally, simple farm-gate economic analyses that account for cost savings (e.g., reduced fertilizer, pesticide, and irrigation inputs) and risk diversification help producers assess the long-term financial value proposition of CAPs.

The structural model of use behavior provides the most valuable information for how to create actual change on the ground. The strong, direct link between behavioral intention and use behavior shows that the core assumption of planned behavior models is correct and that the development of an intention is the first critical step. However, the large effect of facilitating conditions and habit on behavior demonstrates that simply creating an intention is insufficient. This pattern of evidence is consistent with research in implementation science, in which the intention-behavior gap is typically closed by environmental cues and automaticity141. Within agriculture, for example, a study examining water conservation technologies in India indicated that even when there was a strong intention to adopt such technologies, actual adoption only occurred when facilitating conditions such as subsidies and technical assistance were present142. This finding reinforces the broader theoretical insight that intention–behavior gaps are structurally mediated, and suggests that in resource-constrained developing economies, institutional support mechanisms may play an even more decisive role than in high-income contexts. This finding has significant policy implications. Policy investments should not focus solely on changing people’s minds through persuasion; instead, greater emphasis should be placed on enabling individuals to implement change. For example, a successful CAP extension program should allocate funding to both communication/education and service delivery/infrastructure components that facilitate implementation. This includes establishing local institutional systems to support circular agricultural activities, providing affordable and context-specific irrigation systems to aid CAP development, and creating clear guidelines to navigate legislative or regulatory restrictions related to planting or harvesting activities. The persistent influence of habit suggests that once a behavior is introduced, it is important to help farmers make it habitual. This can be achieved by developing reminder systems, providing tools suitable for seasonal planning, or creating peer accountability structures, all of which support the maintenance of the desired behavior over time.

The results demonstrate that an integrated, multi-faceted policy approach, which addresses farmers’ decision-making from the initial consideration of CAPs through to sustained implementation, is essential for promoting higher adoption rates. A policy framework that addresses a single facet of support (i.e., technical training or, financial incentives, or, etc.) will not be successful. The findings advocate that a cohesive strategy is the most effective way to reduce perceived complexity, leverage the social networks of new adopters, provide empirical ecological and economic evidence to support CAP, and ensure that new adopters have access to reliable sources for ongoing technical and financial support. The proposed integrated CAPs adoption policy framework is as follows: (1) develop a Behavioral Design Unit within the existing Agricultural Extension Service (AES) that will be given the responsibility to provide information about potential barriers to practice, and to develop “easy-start” packages to simplify practice protocols; (2) develop a Social Leverage Program that will systematically identify and train farmer champions to educate their peers and build community learning platforms; (3) develop a Green Inputs and Services Guarantee that will ensure immediate access to needed physical resources (e.g., seedlings, tools), and to ongoing access to technical advice; (4) develop a Value-Chain Development Initiative to connect farmers with markets for multiple products, and to develop a pathway for payment for ecosystem services; and, (5) develop a Participatory Monitoring & Evidence System that will engage farmers to use their observations to document the benefits of the adoption of conservation agriculture. This framework builds policy mechanisms around the specific socio-psychological motivators and barriers to adoption identified in this research. Although derived from empirical data in Fars Province, the proposed framework is intentionally structured around theoretically grounded UTAUT2 constructs, allowing it to be adapted and tested in other developing regions pursuing circular agriculture under water scarcity or climate stress. Future research should employ cross-regional comparative designs to distinguish context-specific drivers (e.g., severity of water scarcity, institutional capacity) from universally relevant behavioral mechanisms.

Conclusions, limitations, and pathways for future research

The current research demonstrates the application of a modified UTAUT2 model to understand the social-psychological factors affecting small-scale farmers’ adoption of CAPs in Iran. Moving beyond a purely techno-economic perspective, this study considers the cognitive and socially contextualized aspects of farmers’ decision-making, shedding light on the adoption puzzle for smallholder farmers. Empirical testing of all 10 hypotheses confirms the complex nature of these decisions. Findings indicate that farmers’ intention to adopt CAPs is strongly influenced by both perceived effort (effort expectancy) and habitual practices (habit), highlighting the dual role of ingrained behaviors in facilitating adoption. Importantly, habit exerts a direct influence on use behavior, demonstrating that intentions alone are insufficient for consistent implementation. Similarly, facilitating conditions directly affect adoption behavior by providing tangible support and enabling resources, further bridging the intention–action gap. This triad of enabling factors—low effort, habit, and facilitating conditions—provides an empirical foundation for designing effective intervention strategies. The study also confirms that farmers are not merely production-maximizers; they are complex decision-makers whose choices are shaped by prior social experiences, perceptions of feasibility and benefit, and the supporting infrastructure. Therefore, policies promoting CAP adoption must integrate an understanding of these behavioral components alongside agronomic technology recommendations to ensure successful and sustained implementation.

Nevertheless, this study has some constraints, which represent fruitful areas for future academic research. The first limitation of the survey’s cross-sectional design is that it does not support definitive causal statements or tracking the changes in beliefs and behaviors over time. For example, as farmers transition from being adopters of a new practice to more skilled users, the association between Habit and Use Behavior may change. Additional longitudinal studies are strongly encouraged to identify these evolving pathways of influence, as well as identify ways in which variables like Performance Expectancy are impacted as farmers receive longer-term benefits, or when the influence of Social Influence declines as a practice becomes accepted. Second, the specificity of the study, focusing on small-scale circular agriculture in Iran, leads to the adaptation and validation of the UTAUT2 model, but this study results should be tested further for their generalizable application to different agricultural contexts, such as large-scale monoculture, or different forms of circular practices, such as industrial symbiosis associated with agri-processing. Studies that contrast different agroecological regions of Iran, such as arid and semi-arid climates and regions, as well as studies across different nations facing the same agricultural challenges, will be valuable. As such, further research will shed light on which determinants are universally applicable, and which ones are determined by the context in which each of the drivers have been assessed thus providing an avenue for the development of policy toolkits that are based on theory and appropriately adapted to meet individual region contexts. Additionally, while the quantitative model clearly defines what factors are relevant to the adoption of specific technologies, it provides little to no insight in regards to the “why and how” behind these relationships. For example, we have established that Social Influence is an important driver of adoption; however, we do not know whether this influence is coming from Parents, Neighbors, Religious Leaders, or Extension Agents. In addition, while less effort expectancy captures the perception of complexity, it does not indicate which specific technical, labor or knowledge elements create the most apprehension. As a result, a mixed-methods approach will need to be employed moving forward. Exploring listeners’ stories, cultural nuances, and the unique barriers that lie between respondents and the quantitative score (ex: Land Tenure Issues that hinder the CAP) through in-depth qualitative research (focus groups and Ethnographic Interviews) will help to identify specific elements by which communications and support mechanisms can be optimized. Lastly, the study was limited to farm level drivers of adoption. Although these factors certainly play an important role in the transition to Circular Argo-Bioeconomy, there are also a number of macro-level/systemic variables which affect this transition. At the micro-level, there are current barriers such as national subsidy programs that may unintentionally create disincentives for circular practices; the extent of the value chain associated with secondary circular products; the ability/infrastructure available to collect and process biomass; and finally, there are regulatory issues. The integration of the micro-level behavioral model created in this study into a meso- and macro-level policy analysis will provide an exciting and needed avenue for future research. In particular, it will be important to understand how different policy instruments (e.g., input subsidies and incentives for ecosystem services) create synergies with the socio-psychological forces driving the adoption/diffusion of circular agriculture to enhance or inhibit large-scale systemic change. Further, the results from this analysis would result in a multi-level, multi-scale comprehensive template from which to drive the sustainable transformation of the agricultural industry in an equitable manner.

Ultimately, this research contributes to the understanding of the adoption of CAPs by providing a validated behavioral framework for the explanation of circular agriculture adoption. The results of this study provide an established foundation for a pathway forward for integrated policy development, which should consider the following three areas: (1) Simplicity of practice, allowing for easy implementation, (2) Social networking as a means of providing access to resources, and (3) Assurance of real-world implementation through tangible support for the circular agricultural community. By acknowledging the limitations of the study (i.e., a snapshot of the data and geographic region, a limited sample size and depth, and the scope of the research was confined to the farm-level), the authors have not only completed a specific research project but also created a foundation for future interdisciplinary research. Moving forward along the lines of this research will be critical to the co-design of effective and human-centered strategies that lead to supporting widespread adoption of circular models, thus improving the resilience of farmers, communities and the delicate agro-ecosystem they depend on.
