Antibiofilm activity against common pathogenic bacteria in Thailand and liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry chemical profiling of Ha-Rak formula and its components: an in vitro study
Authors: Sirinthra Fondee, Suveerawan Limsuvan, Witchuda Kamolvit, Pravit Akarasereenont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial biofilms reduce antibiotic penetration, create tolerant bacterial subpopulations, and contribute to difficult-to-treat infections and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). The Ha-Rak Formula (HRF), a traditional Thai polyherbal remedy used for febrile illnesses, has not been systematically evaluated for antibiofilm activity.ObjectiveThis study assessed the in vitro antibiofilm effects of HRF and its five constituent herbs against clinically relevant bacterial pathogens in Thailand and characterized their chemical profiles using liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS).MethodsWater and 80% ethanol extracts were prepared from authenticated roots and commercially sourced root-stem materials. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against six pathogenic bacteria were determined by broth microdilution at final concentrations of 64 − 16,384 µg/mL. Biofilm biomass was quantified by crystal violet staining at final extract concentrations of 400 and 1000 µg/mL, and untargeted LC-QTOF-MS with multivariate analysis was used for chemical profiling.ResultsAll extracts had MIC values > 16,384 µg/mL against planktonic cells, except the ethanol extract of root-derived HRF, which inhibited Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883 only at the highest tested concentration (MIC = 16,384 µg/mL). In contrast, root-derived extracts reduced biofilm biomass across multiple species at sub-MIC concentrations. Ethanol extracts displayed distinct chemical profiles enriched in putative carbohydrates and conjugates.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that HRF has stronger activity against biofilm formation than against planktonic bacterial growth under the conditions tested. Root-only and root-stem formulations both exhibited antibiofilm activity under the conditions tested, while water and ethanol extracts showed distinct chemical profiles. Further standardization, safety assessment, and mechanistic studies are required before clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05478-0.Keywords: Ha-Rak formula, Minimum inhibitory concentration, Biofilm formation, Antibiofilm activity, Chemical profiling

Introduction

Bacterial infections remain a major global public health challenge. In 2019, 33 bacterial species caused 7.7 million deaths worldwide, ranking second after ischemic heart disease [1]. Antimicrobial-resistant infections result in approximately 700,000 fatalities each year and may escalate to 10 million by 2050 [2]. In Thailand, infections caused by antimicrobial-resistant pathogens contribute to more than 100,000 cases, over 30,000 deaths, and economic losses of approximately 40 billion baht each year. Some of the most resistant pathogens include Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [3].

Biofilms are structured microbial communities embedded in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance matrix. Biofilm formation is a multistep process involving reversible attachment, irreversible adhesion, microcolony formation, production of extracellular matrix components, maturation, and dispersion. The matrix limits antimicrobial penetration, protects bacteria from host immune responses, and supports slow-growing or metabolically tolerant subpopulations. Consequently, biofilm-associated infections are often more persistent than planktonic infections and require alternative antibiofilm strategies, including approaches derived from medicinal plants and natural products [4].

The traditional Thai herbal formulation Ha-Rak Formula (HRF) has long been used to treat fever and is also known as Benjalokawichien or Ya-Gaew-Ha-Duang [5]. HRF is composed of the roots of five medicinal plants: Harrisonia perforata (Blanco) Merr., Tiliacora triandra (Colebr.) Diels, Capparis micracantha DC., Clerodendrum indicum (L.) Kuntze, and Ficus racemosa L. It is recognized in the Khamphi Takka Sila as a primary remedy for fever [6]. The pharmacological effects of HRF and its individual components have been documented, including antipyretic [7], anti-inflammatory [8], and antibacterial activities [9–11].

Among its constituents, Harrisonia perforata (Blanco) Merr., Clerodendrum indicum (L.) Kuntze, Tiliacora triandra (Colebr.) Diels, Capparis micracantha DC., and Ficus racemosa L. have all been reported to exhibit antibacterial activity against one or more bacterial species (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and β-hemolytic Streptococcus group A) [9, 12–15].

Classical HRF specifically uses the roots of the five constituent species. Available root-specific information remains uneven across the five herbs; however, root-derived materials of several HRF constituents have been associated with antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or related biological activities [9, 12–15]. In contemporary practice, commercial HRF materials may contain roots mixed with stems. Direct comparison of authenticated root-only materials and commercial root-stem materials is therefore important for evaluating whether plant-part substitution or admixture alters chemical composition and biological activity.

Medicinal plants are key sources of sustainable antimicrobial candidates, but plant part selection can influence biological activity because secondary metabolites are not uniformly distributed among roots, stems, leaves, and other organs. Substituting or mixing plant parts can therefore change the chemical fingerprint and activity of an herbal preparation. Advanced phytochemical profiling using LC-QTOF-MS is useful for comparing such materials, identifying putative chemical markers, and relating chemical differences to antibiofilm effects [16, 17].

Although HRF is traditionally used to treat fever associated with infection, scientific evidence for its antibiofilm activity remains limited. Therefore, this study evaluated the MIC values and antibiofilm activities of HRF root-only and root-stem extracts against six clinically relevant bacterial pathogens in Thailand and characterized their chemical profiles using LC-QTOF-MS.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Roots of the five plants constituting the Ha-Rak formula (HRF) were collected from Chachoengsao Province, Thailand: Ficus racemosa L. (MD; latitude 13.4420020, longitude 101.4982600), Clerodendrum indicum (L.) Kuntze (TY; latitude 13.383822, longitude 101.697140), Tiliacora triandra (Colebr.) Diels (YN; latitude 13.4418830, longitude 101.4980190), Harrisonia perforata (Blanco) Merr. (KT; latitude 13.384684, longitude 101.697288), and Capparis micracantha DC. (CC; latitude 13.4383470, longitude 101.5002170). These authentic fresh samples were collected for taxonomic authentication and voucher specimen preparation and were subsequently washed, oven-dried, and processed for extraction. The plant materials were collected between December 2020 and April 2022 from community land in Chachoengsao Province, Thailand, with prior permission from local community representatives and traditional healers. No formal government collection permit was required because sampling was conducted on non-protected community land and did not involve endangered or protected species. The collection complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and with the principles of the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD).

Plant materials were taxonomically identified by Ms. Juthamas Neetiwatanapong, a botanist at the Botanical Center, Suan Luang Rama IX, Bangkok, Thailand. All five voucher specimens (FD. Sirinthra-01 to FD. Sirinthra-05) were deposited at the Suan Luang Rama IX Herbarium, Bangkok, Thailand, under Herbarium accession No. 9424. The voucher numbers and accession information for each species are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Tables S1-S2.

Commercial HRF (roots mixed with stems) in the form of dried samples was obtained from a single batch supplied by the Center of Applied Thai Traditional Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. Commercial dried root-stem materials of the five constituent herbs used for comparison were obtained from the same source. Commercial HRF and commercial root-stem materials were authenticated by Ms. Juthamas Neetiwatanapong through macroscopic and organoleptic examination and by comparison with the corresponding authenticated voucher specimens (FD. Sirinthra-01 to FD. Sirinthra-05) prepared from authentic fresh samples collected in Chachoengsao Province, Thailand, together with standard botanical descriptions of the respective species prior to extraction.

Accordingly, plant materials used for extraction in this study consisted of two groups: (i) authentic fresh samples collected from Chachoengsao Province, Thailand, and (ii) commercial dried samples obtained from the Center of Applied Thai Traditional Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand.

The voucher information listed in Supplementary Table S2 refers to the authenticated voucher specimens (FD. Sirinthra-01 to FD. Sirinthra-05) used as reference materials for taxonomic authentication of the corresponding commercial plant materials.

Extract preparation

Freshly collected authenticated roots were washed, cut into small pieces, and oven-dried at 60 °C for 15 h before grinding. In contrast, commercial root-stem crude materials were obtained as dried materials and therefore did not require the drying step prior to processing. All dried plant materials were then ground into powder and stored under dry conditions.

Powders (100 g) were extracted with distilled water or 80% ethanol (500 mL; 5:1 v/w). Aqueous extraction was performed by boiling for 30–45 min, while ethanolic extraction was conducted by maceration at room temperature for 3 days. After filtration, the plant residue was re-extracted twice under the same conditions using fresh solvent (500 mL each cycle). The filtrates obtained from the initial extraction and the two re-extraction cycles were pooled prior to freeze-drying. Extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 1 and No. 4 filter papers, concentrated under reduced pressure (ethanol extracts), freeze-dried, and stored at − 20 °C. Extraction yields are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1–S2. The aqueous extraction procedure is traditionally used in Thai Traditional Medicine (TTM) for medicinal preparation.

Bacterial strains

Six bacterial strains, including two Gram-positive bacteria (Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213) and four Gram-negative bacteria (Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883), were used in this study. Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) was used to revive strains and verify purity before MIC testing, whereas Luria-Bertani (LB) agar/broth was used for routine propagation and biofilm assays unless otherwise specified. Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) was used for broth microdilution MIC testing [18].

Inoculum preparation

Each bacterial strain was subcultured overnight at 37 °C in LB or MHB medium according to the subsequent assay. Cells were collected in 5 mL of sterile saline or salt-free LB broth, and optical density was calibrated at 550 nm using a spectrophotometer [19, 20]. The bacterial suspensions were adjusted and diluted to 5 × 105 CFU/mL for MIC testing and 1 × 106 CFU/mL for biofilm formation assays [18].

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

MIC values were determined in sterile 96-well microplates following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines with minor modifications ([18, 21–22]). Extract stock solutions were prepared and twofold serially diluted in cation-adjusted MHB to obtain final test concentrations of 64 − 16,384 µg/mL after inoculation with bacteria (5 × 105 CFU/mL). The final solvent concentration did not exceed 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the solvent control showed no inhibitory effect (Supplementary Table S5). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h.

Levofloxacin was used as the reference antibiotic for Gram-negative bacteria, while vancomycin served as the reference antibiotic for Gram-positive bacteria. Assay validity was verified using E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. aureus ATCC 29213 as quality control strains, with MIC values of the reference antibiotics falling within CLSI-recommended ranges as specified in CLSI M07-A12 [22] and M100 [21] (Supplementary Tables S3-S4).

Biofilm formation assay

The effects of HRF and its constituent extracts on biofilm formation were evaluated using the crystal violet assay [18]. Extract concentrations of 400 and 1000 µg/mL were selected based on our previously published protocol [18] and because they were substantially lower than the observed planktonic MIC values (> 16,384 µg/mL for most extracts), thereby representing sub-MIC concentrations and allowing assessment of antibiofilm activity with minimal contribution from direct growth inhibition. Bacterial suspensions (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were incubated with water or 80% ethanol extracts at final concentrations of 400 and 1000 µg/mL for 48 h at 37 °C to allow stable biofilm formation [18–20]. Freeze-dried extracts were dissolved in 0.1% DMSO to prepare working solutions at 2 mg/mL. For biofilm assays, bacterial suspensions adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL were mixed with extract working solutions to a final volume of 200 µL per well. Depending on the extract concentration tested, 160 µL or 100 µL of bacterial suspension was combined with 40 µL or 100 µL of extract solution to obtain final extract concentrations of 400 or 1000 µg/mL, respectively. Matched solvent control wells received the same volumes of bacterial suspension and 0.1% DMSO without extract. Biofilms were stained with crystal violet, solubilized, and quantified at 550 nm [19, 20]. Appropriate positive, negative, medium, solvent, and untreated biofilm controls were included.

For microbiological assays, a biological replicate was defined as an independent experiment performed on a separate day using a freshly prepared bacterial culture and freshly diluted extract. A technical replicate was defined as replicate wells within the same microplate run. Each condition was evaluated in three independent biological experiments, and each biological experiment contained three technical replicate wells (n = 3 biological replicates, each with triplicate technical measurements). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

LC-QTOF-MS analysis

Chemicals and solvents

LC-MS grade acetonitrile, methanol, formic acid and analytical grade ethanol were purchased from Scharlau (Spain). Purified water was prepared using a Milli-Q water system (Millipore, France).

Preparation of Ha-Rak formula and its five herbal component extracts for LC-QTOF-MS

Water and ethanol extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, while the pellets were discarded. The dried powdered extracts of Ha-Rak Formula (HRF) and its five constituent herbs (1 mg each) were mixed with 1 mL of 100% methanol and vortexed for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution. The mixtures were then centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were diluted with 100% methanol to obtain final analytical concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL. Each sample (total volume 1 mL) was subjected to LC-QTOF-MS analysis, with a 5 µL aliquot injected into the system [23].

Chromatographic conditions

A 5 µL volume of each extract was injected and analyzed using a reverse-phase column (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3, 1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) coupled with a QTOF mass spectrometer (Synapt G2-Si, Waters). Untargeted analyses were performed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. Instrument calibration was conducted prior to mode switching using 2.5 mM sodium formate. During acquisition, mass data were corrected using a lock spray of leucine enkephalin (200 pg/µL; [M + H] + at m/z 556.2771 for positive mode and [M-H]- at m/z 554.2615 for negative mode). Quality control samples were introduced at the beginning, after every 10 samples, and at the end of the sequence to ensure precision and consistency.

The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, applied with a linear gradient as follows: 2–98% B over 0–9 min, held at 98% B for 1 min, 98 − 2% B over 10–12 min, and held at 2% B for 3 min. Strong wash solvent was 90% methanol in water, while weak wash and needle wash solvents were Milli-Q water. Seal wash solvent was 50% methanol in water. The column was maintained at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min.

LC-MS/MS was conducted with a capillary voltage of 3 kV, cone voltage 40 V, source temperature 150 °C, desolvation temperature 500 °C, and gas flow 1000 L/h. Data were processed using Progenesis QI, normalized to total ion current (TIC), log2-transformed, and Pareto-scaled prior to multivariate analysis (EZinfo, ClusVis). Compounds were putatively identified at Metabolomics Standards Initiative level 2 (MSI level 2) based on MS/MS spectra and retention behavior using ChemSpider, Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), PubChem, and in-house libraries (± 5 ppm). Principal component analysis (PCA) score plots (%R2X) were generated, and results are reported as relative abundances.

Metabolomics pathway analysis

The metabolic pathways of 15 putatively identified phytochemicals found in water and ethanol extracts of HRF, derived from roots alone and from roots mixed with stems, were examined using publicly accessible databases, including KEGG, HMDB, PubChem, and other pertinent sources. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 to determine the top 25 KEGG metabolic pathways linked to these compounds. For pathway assignment, detected metabolites were matched to database identifiers, and KEGG compound identifiers were used for pathway mapping. For example, Stachyose (KEGG C01613) and Isomaltose (KEGG C00252) mapped to galactose metabolism and starch/sucrose metabolism, respectively. Metabolic pathway maps were developed using data from the KEGG database [24] along with pertinent literature.

Statistical analysis

Data on antibacterial assays and chemical constituents of HRF and its five herbal components are presented as mean ± SD, percentages, PCA plots, graphs, and tables. Chemical profiling analyses were performed using MassLynx™, Progenesis QI, UNIFI, EZinfo, ClusVis, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

All in vitro MIC and biofilm formation assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Differences among extracts and concentrations were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. When assumptions for parametric testing were not met, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 18 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of HRF and its five herbal component extracts

The MICs of HRF and its constituent extracts were determined by broth microdilution against six clinically relevant bacteria. Quality control using Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 showed MICs of reference antibiotics within CLSI-recommended ranges [21, 22], confirming assay validity (Supplementary Tables S3-S4). No inhibition was observed for most extracts against the tested species up to 16,384 µg/mL. The only extract with a measurable MIC was the ethanol extract of root-derived HRF, which inhibited Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883 only at the highest tested concentration (MIC = 16,384 µg/mL) (Tables 1 and 2). Solvent controls showed no effect, and antibiotics performed as expected (Supplementary Table S5).

Using commonly applied natural-product screening criteria, crude plant extracts with MIC values above approximately 1000 µg/mL are generally considered weak or inactive for direct antibacterial activity [25, 26]. Therefore, the MIC findings indicate that HRF and its constituent extracts had no meaningful direct inhibitory effect on planktonic bacterial growth under the conditions tested. Their potential effects on biofilm formation were subsequently investigated at sub-MIC concentrations.

Antibiofilm activity of HRF and its five herbal component extracts

Both water and ethanol extracts of HRF and its constituent herbs significantly reduced biofilm biomass of all six tested pathogens at the tested concentrations (p < 0.05). Root-only extracts generally exhibited stronger antibiofilm activity than root-stem preparations (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was the most susceptible to root-only extracts, particularly HRF water extract and all extracts of TY and YN, whereas MD and HRF ethanol extracts were most effective against Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883. Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 was highly responsive to all HRF extracts (Supplementary Tables S6-S7), while Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606 showed the greatest sensitivity to HRF extracts prepared from roots mixed with stems.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of HRF and its five herbal component extracts

In positive ESI mode, KT-R, YN-R, and MD-R showed chemical profiles comparable to HRF-R, whereas in negative mode, ethanol extracts of HRF-R, CC-R, and TY-R were most similar (Figs. 7 and 8). TY-R matched HRF-R water extract in negative mode and, together with KT-R, showed similarity in positive mode. Ethanol extracts of TY-R, MD-R, and YN-R shared profiles with HRF-R in both modes. Fifteen phytochemicals were putatively identified in HRF and its five herbal extracts, including β-Gentiobiose, Stachyose, Prunose III, Panose, N-trans-Caffeoyltyramine, Methyl succinate, 2,7-Dihydroxy-3,5-dimethoxy-9,10-dihydrophenanthrene, Isotetrandrine, (E)-3-Tridecene-5,7,9,11-tetrayne-1,2-diol, Blestrianol D, Furomollugin, Fuzitine, Isomaltose, Mannotriose, and 1-Hydroxy-2,3,5-trimethoxyxanthone. Overall, KT-derived extracts exhibited the highest compositional similarity to HRF formulations (Fig. 9).

Phytochemical compounds detected in HRF and its five herbal components with published antibacterial, anti-adhesion, or biofilm-related activity

Eight of the fifteen putatively identified phytochemicals with published antibacterial, anti-adhesion, or biofilm-related activity were detected in HRF-R and/or HRF-M extracts (Supplementary Table S8). HRF-R generally exhibited higher abundances of carbohydrate-related compounds than HRF-M (Supplementary Figs. S2-S9; Tables S9-S16). At 100 µg/mL, the highest levels were observed in ethanol extracts, particularly TY-R for Stachyose (1,404,661 AU), Panose (441,568 AU), Isomaltose (2,249,996 AU), and Mannotriose (1,739,762 AU), while KT-M showed the highest N-trans-Caffeoyltyramine (2,160,580 AU) and Furomollugin (721,081 AU). These quantitative differences highlight enrichment of selected bioactive or bioactivity-associated compounds in specific components and plant-part preparations.

Metabolic pathway of HRF and its five herbal component extracts

HRF and its constituent extracts (roots or root-stem mixtures) contained seven annotated phytochemicals that could be matched to major metabolite databases: Stachyose, Panose, Isomaltose, β-Gentiobiose, Methyl succinate, Isotetrandrine, and N-trans-Caffeoyltyramine. Carbohydrates and their conjugates were the predominant class and showed the highest enrichment score (p < 0.0001). HRF-R exhibited higher levels of most carbohydrates, whereas HRF-M was enriched in N-trans-Caffeoyltyramine. Several of these compounds have been reported to possess antibacterial, anti-adhesion, or biofilm-related activities (Fig. 10, Supplementary Table S8), and the database identifiers used for pathway mapping are summarized in Supplementary Table S17. Pathway mapping was performed by matching the annotated metabolites to their corresponding HMDB, PubChem, and KEGG identifiers. The resulting KEGG compound IDs were then analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 and the KEGG pathway database. Discussion

Bacterial infections remain a global health challenge driven by antibiotic resistance. In Thailand, major pathogens include Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [4, 27]. This study provides the first systematic evaluation of the antibiofilm activity of HRF and its five constituent herbs against six clinically relevant bacterial pathogens in Thailand. The principal finding is that HRF and its component extracts did not meaningfully inhibit planktonic bacterial growth, but they reduced biofilm biomass at sub-MIC concentrations. This distinction is important because direct antibacterial activity and antibiofilm activity represent different biological effects and should not be interpreted interchangeably. Ethanol and water extraction effectively recover diverse constituents [28, 29].

The MIC results showed no planktonic growth inhibition for nearly all extracts at concentrations up to 16,384 µg/mL. The only measurable MIC was observed for the root-derived HRF ethanol extract against Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883 at 16,384 µg/mL. Based on commonly cited natural-product screening criteria, crude extracts with MIC values above approximately 1000 µg/mL are generally considered weak or inactive for direct antibacterial activity, although no universal clinical breakpoint exists for complex herbal extracts [25, 26]. Therefore, HRF should not be described as a direct antibacterial agent against planktonic cells under the conditions tested.

In contrast, HRF and its constituent extracts significantly reduced biofilm biomass in all six bacterial species at 400 and 1000 µg/mL. Because these concentrations were below the planktonic MIC values, the antibiofilm effects were unlikely to be explained simply by growth inhibition. The antibiofilm effects increased with extract concentration, although non-linear dose–response patterns were observed, likely reflecting extract complexity and solubility limitations, consistent with previous reports [30–32]. The findings indicate that biofilm formation was reduced under the experimental conditions tested. However, the crystal violet assay measures total biofilm biomass and does not distinguish between effects on bacterial adhesion, extracellular matrix production, biofilm architecture, or cell viability. Furthermore, quorum-sensing activity and biofilm-related gene expression were not evaluated. Therefore, the biological and molecular mechanisms responsible for the observed reduction in biofilm biomass remain unclear, and no conclusions regarding virulence modulation or specific molecular pathways can be drawn from the present data.

The comparison between root-only and root-stem materials is directly relevant to HRF standardization. Classical HRF uses roots of the five constituent plants, whereas commercial materials may contain roots mixed with stems. In this study, root-only extracts generally showed stronger antibiofilm activity than root-stem preparations, suggesting that plant part composition may influence biological activity. At the same time, root-stem HRF retained antibiofilm activity and several bioactivity-associated compounds, indicating that commercial root-stem preparations may still contribute to biofilm modulation. These findings support the need for clear plant-part specification in HRF quality control.

The LC-QTOF-MS findings also support plant-part-dependent chemical variation. The ethanol extracts of HRF-R and HRF-M contained all 15 putatively identified compounds listed in Supplementary Table S18, whereas water extracts showed fewer detectable compounds and different relative abundances. Eight of the fifteen putatively identified HRF compounds have published antibacterial, anti-adhesion, or biofilm-related activities (Supplementary Table S8). This detection pattern, particularly the detection of all 15 putatively identified compounds (Supplementary Table S18) in the ethanol extracts of HRF-R and HRF-M and their differential occurrence among extracts (Supplementary Tables S9–S16), supports plant part- and solvent-dependent chemical variation, consistent with previous reports [23, 33].

Increasing analytical sample concentrations from 25 to 100 µg/mL increased signal intensity and the number of detectable features in LC-QTOF-MS profiling, reflecting concentration-dependent analytical detection rather than direct evidence of enhanced biological activity [34]. These include carbohydrates and conjugates such as Stachyose, Isomaltose, Panose, and Mannotriose; a fatty acid ester, Methyl succinate; a hydroxycinnamic acid derivative, N-trans-Caffeoyltyramine; an alkaloid, Isotetrandrine; and Furomollugin [35–37].

Solvent selection influenced both phytochemical profiles and antibiofilm activity. Ethanol generally yielded greater chemical diversity than water and, in several cases, stronger antibiofilm effects. Previous studies have indicated that several of the detected compounds possess antibacterial or biofilm-related properties, and the chemical profiles of herbal extracts may vary depending on extraction conditions [38]. However, the enhanced in vitro activity of ethanol extracts does not automatically imply clinical superiority, because traditional HRF use is primarily aqueous and because safety, bioavailability, metabolism, and tissue exposure were not evaluated in this study.

No pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic measurements were generated in this study; therefore, clinical exposure, tissue concentrations, and therapeutic relevance cannot be inferred from the present in vitro data.

This study has several limitations. First, only reference bacterial strains were evaluated, and clinical isolates should be included in future studies. Second, the crystal violet assay measures total attached biomass and does not distinguish live cells, dead cells, or extracellular matrix. Third, cytotoxicity and selectivity were not assessed, so the therapeutic window remains unknown. Fourth, compound identities were putatively assigned at MSI level 2 and should be confirmed with authentic standards. Finally, the specific molecular mechanism underlying antibiofilm activity was not determined. Despite these limitations, the data provide a reproducible in vitro foundation for future HRF standardization and mechanistic studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HRF and its five constituent herbal extracts can reduce biofilm biomass in clinically relevant bacterial pathogens at concentrations that do not meaningfully inhibit planktonic growth. HRF should therefore be interpreted as a candidate antibiofilm or biofilm-modulating preparation rather than a direct antibacterial agent under the conditions tested. Root-only preparations generally showed stronger antibiofilm activity than root-stem preparations, but commercial root-stem materials retained detectable antibiofilm effects and key phytochemical features. These findings support further work on HRF standardization, plant-part authentication, safety, and mechanistic evaluation before clinical application.
