Factors Affecting Treatment Persistence in Japanese Patients with Psoriasis Prescribed Biologics: A Real-World Study Using an Insurance Claim Database
Authors: Celine Miyazaki, Junya Masuda, Phiona I-Ching Tsai, Hidehisa Saeki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPoor persistence to biologics can result in suboptimal health outcomes and increased economic burden for chronic conditions, including psoriasis (PsO). In Japan, studies evaluating factors responsible for biologic treatment persistence in patients with PsO are limited. We assessed biologic treatment persistence (median treatment duration and overall treatment survival) and associated factors in patients with PsO in a real-world setting.MethodsThis retrospective analysis of insurance claims records from the Japan Medical Data Center (JMDC) database included patients with PsO [International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code: L40.x] ≥ 18 years of age who had received biologic treatment. Treatment persistence was analyzed using data from 2016 to 2020 by biologic class and by individual biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, ustekinumab, guselkumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, and brodalumab) in bio-naïve (who initiate first biologic at index) and bio-experienced patients. Kaplan–Meier survival (treatment persistence), and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression (predictive factors) analyses were used.ResultsOverall, 1528 patients with PsO were included (mean age 47.4 years). Infliximab had the longest median treatment duration (33.6 months), while brodalumab had the shortest (9.7 months) among biologics evaluated. Of the biologics evaluated, 1-year treatment survival was highest with guselkumab (83%), and lowest with brodalumab (45%). Bio-experienced patients showed slightly longer median treatment duration than bio-naïve patients (22.8 versus 18.1 months). Factors predictive of treatment persistence were sex [male; hazard ratio (HR) 0.84, p = 0.016] and specific PsO diagnostic codes, such as L40.0 (PsO vulgaris; HR 0.69; p = 0.006), L40.1 (generalized pustular PsO; HR 0.75; p = 0.034), and L40.9 (PsO unspecified; HR 0.72; p = 0.001). Meanwhile, age and Charlson Comorbidity Index score were significantly associated with adalimumab and infliximab treatment persistence, respectively.ConclusionAmong biologics evaluated, infliximab had the longest median treatment duration, and guselkumab had the highest 1-year treatment survival. Sex and specific PsO diagnostic codes influenced overall treatment persistence. These findings could inform long-term treatment plans for PsO in real-world clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-024-01274-1.Keywords: Biologics, Guselkumab, Japan, JMDC, Psoriasis, Treatment persistence, Treatment survival

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Treatment persistence with biologic therapy is a key consideration for determining long-term treatment strategies for patients with psoriasis (PsO). With increasing numbers of biologics available for PsO treatment, understanding overall treatment persistence and factors affecting treatment persistence are essential for informing a long-term treatment plan where patients could achieve sustainable skin clearance.Many studies have evaluated treatment persistence of biologics in Japanese patients with PsO; however, data regarding patient characteristic-related factors impacting treatment persistence are limited. What was learned from the study? Of all biologics evaluated, infliximab had the highest median treatment duration of > 30 months. Guselkumab had the highest 1-year treatment survival rate, with > 80% of patients continuing treatment for at least 1-year.Males and patients with specific PsO diagnostic codes (L40.0, PsO vulgaris; L40.1, generalized pustular PsO; and L40.9, PsO unspecified) were more likely to continue biologic treatment. When evaluated by individual biologics, variables such as age, Charlson Comorbidity Index score, and specific PsO diagnostic codes showed significant associations with treatment discontinuation for certain biologics.Our study sheds light on treatment persistence with biologics in Japanese patients with PsO in a real-world setting. Understanding factors affecting treatment persistence may help to determine suitable treatment plans, which could help patients to continue the treatment for a longer duration and achieve sustainable treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Psoriasis (PsO) affects 2–3% of the global population and 0.34–0.44% of the population in Japan [1–3]. The emergence of biologic therapies targeting specific cytokines involved in the pathogenesis of PsO has drastically changed the treatment landscape of PsO. Several biologics, including infliximab, adalimumab, ustekinumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, brodalumab, guselkumab, risankizumab, certolizumab pegol, tildrakizumab, and bimekizumab are currently available for the treatment of PsO in Japan, and are typically used when non-biologic treatments fail to control disease or are not tolerated [4].

Treatment persistence, defined as the time from treatment initiation to discontinuation [5], has gained considerable interest in the management of chronic conditions such as PsO, and could inform long-term treatment strategies [3]. Treatment (drug) survival, defined as a patient remaining on a biologic treatment over different time points [6], has been directly associated with efficacy responses, tolerability, and patient motivation, among other factors, and discontinuation of therapy may result in suboptimal health outcomes and add to the overall economic burden of PsO [7–14]. Several studies evaluated treatment persistence in patients with PsO in real-world settings in the USA, and found that age, sex, body mass index, income status, comorbidities, concomitant metabolic conditions, drug efficacy and safety, and insurance status were associated with treatment survival [7, 15, 16]. Although similar studies evaluated treatment persistence in Japan [17, 18], data regarding factors associated with biologic treatment persistence are limited. Moreover, these prior studies may not have appropriately captured the spectrum of current biologic treatments and whether treatment persistence could be influenced by patient characteristics such as specific PsO diagnostic codes or prior exposure to biologics in a real-world clinical setting in Japan. In this study, we aimed (1) to evaluate overall biologic treatment persistence on the basis of overall median treatment duration and treatment survival by individual biologics and (2) to determine predictive factors that could impact overall biologic treatment persistence in patients with PsO, using patient data from the Japan Medical Data Center (JMDC) claims database.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This was a retrospective study with secondary use of insurance claims records from the JMDC database. This is a patient-level, longitudinal database that consists of anonymized receipt data (inpatient, outpatient, and dispensing) and medical information, including patient demographics, drug names and dosages, annual company health check-up data, and diagnosis based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes from multiple health insurance associations since 2005. The JMDC database covers approximately 17 million employees and their families, representing ⁓14% of the total Japanese population as per the 2023 estimate [19]. In this study, all patients with PsO enrolled in the database between 2009 and 2020 were evaluated for eligibility for inclusion. The study period for biologic treatment was from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2020, and patients’ biologic treatment was followed longitudinally. Biologic treatments reimbursed for PsO along with the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes and prescription days’ supply were captured from the database. A treatment grace period was defined as the allowable time between discontinuation and resumption of a biologic and was determined by referencing other academic literature and the treatment label of the administration posology. The index date was established as the date of the first use of a biologic for PsO treatment within the study period. Patient comorbidities and PsO-related medications/therapies were recorded during the baseline period (12 months prior to the index date). Treatment duration was evaluated during the follow-up period (duration from the index date until the end of data capture/date of death or loss to follow-up, whichever came first). The details of the study design are presented in the supplementary material (Fig. S1). All required approvals and permissions for data extraction from the JMDC database and for performing pre-specified analyses were obtained. The study used de-identified patient data and was conducted in compliance with all laws and regulations, national guidelines, and other standards regarding the handling of personal information. Informed consent and ethics committee approval were not applicable based on the Ethical Guidelines for Epidemiological Research issued by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Welfare and Labor. The details of the study have been published elsewhere [20].

Study Population

Male and female patients ≥ 18 years of age with a diagnosis of PsO (defined as the presence of at least one of PsO diagnosis record as per the ICD-10 code [L40.x; supplementary material Table S1] after 1 January 2009 were identified at the initial selection. The specific PsO diagnostic codes included were L40.0 (PsO vulgaris), L40.1 (generalized pustular PsO), L40.4 (guttate PsO), L40.8 (other PsO), and L40.9 (PsO unspecified; supplementary material Table S1). Patients who received ≥ 1 biologic treatment during the study period (2016–2020) were reviewed for specified criteria for potential evaluation. Patients with PsO who had or later developed psoriatic arthritis (PsO-PsA) were included in the overall PsO cohort (all patients referred to as PsO thereafter). As per the inclusion criteria, patients were required to have a record of at least one healthcare visit with a PsO diagnosis and/or have at least one biologic medication for PsO dispensed within 12 months prior to the index date. Exclusion criteria included first PsO diagnosis after initiation of the first biologic treatment, age < 18 years at the time of first biologic dispensation, and a diagnosis of only PsA (ICD code, L40.5) or acrodermatitis (L40.2) or palmoplantar pustulosis (L40.3) due to confounding in treatment for arthritis or limited approved use for certain biologic treatments. Additionally, patients who had a treatment episode of < 1 year (duration between the first and last date of treatment dispensation) on the basis of records from the time of biologic initiation were also excluded. Variables and Assessment

This analysis evaluated overall biologic treatment persistence, and factors associated with treatment persistence in patients with PsO between 2016 and 2020 in Japan. Overall treatment persistence was defined as the duration of time from the date of biologic initiation to the date of discontinuation [5], and overall treatment survival was defined as a patient remaining on a biologic treatment over different time points [6]. To derive the median treatment duration, consecutive dispensations of the same treatment were merged into one treatment episode to construct the total duration of treatment, and the median duration was calculated among all patients in the same treatment group. A treatment episode was continued if the next claim for the same treatment fell within the allowed grace period. Treatment discontinuation was defined as the end of treatment follow-up, which was denoted by no prescription refill or dispensation after 90 or 180 days of treatment gap (grace period). Treatment episodes during the first year of reimbursement for a newly initiated biologic treatment (biologic reimbursement period) but with inadequate follow-up time (< 1 year from the start of reimbursement) were excluded from the persistence analysis, to ensure adequate treatment follow-up for evaluation (Supplementary material Table S2). Patients who initiated a biologic treatment at the end of the study follow-up period or at the end of the data capture period were censored from the analysis.

Overall treatment persistence was analyzed and stratified by biologic class [Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin(IL)-17, IL-12/23, and IL-23 inhibitors] and by individual biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, ustekinumab, guselkumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, and brodalumab). Certolizumab pegol and risankizumab were not included in the analysis, as the data for these biologics were not available for evaluation in 2020 and had insufficient duration of follow-up to analyze treatment persistence. Eligible patients were further stratified into two subgroups on the basis of treatment history on the index date: (i) patients with no prior exposure to biologic treatment and initiate biologics only at index (bio-naïve) and (ii) patients with a history of prior biologic treatment (bio-experienced). Bio-naïve patients who later initiated biologic treatment during the follow-up period were considered “bio-experienced” from that point forward and were categorized on the basis of the first-ever biologic received.

Statistical Analysis

Overall treatment survival at different time points was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Treatment episodes extending beyond the end of follow-up or initiated after the end of follow-up were marked as censored, and the censor date was set as the end date of the treatment episode. Patients were also censored at the date of death. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to examine the effect of covariates [including age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, biologic treatment experience, and specific PsO diagnostic codes] on time to discontinuation of treatment (treatment persistence) and were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value of p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Regression coefficients and HRs were reported for the overall analysis population and by biologics. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine treatment persistence in patients stratified by biologics and treatment experience considering a 90-day grace period. Descriptive statistics were used for all analyses, and data are presented as mean (standard deviation), median and range [25th percentile (Q1) and 75th percentile (Q3)], frequency, and percentages. All analyses were performed using the statistical analysis software (SAS) Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.2.0.

Results

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 98,116 patients diagnosed with PsO were retrieved from the JMDC database between 2009 and 2020. Of these, 1883 patients received ≥ 1 biologic treatment for PsO between 2016 and 2020. After screening as per inclusion and exclusion criteria, data from 1528 patients were included in this analysis (Fig. 1).

Detailed baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of patients with PsO stratified by biologic class (classified on the basis of first-ever biologic record) are presented in Table 1. In the overall population (N = 1528), the mean age was 47.4 years, with a male predominance (72.7%), and 39.0% of patients were PsO-PsA. Among the biologic classes evaluated, the TNF-α inhibitor group had slightly fewer PsO than PsO-PsA patients (48.1% versus 51.9%), while the IL-17 (61.4% versus 38.6%), IL-12/23 (80.1% versus 19.9%), and IL-23 (78.8% versus 21.2%) inhibitor groups had higher proportions of PsO than PsO-PsA patients. The mean time between the first diagnosis of PsO and initiation of the first biologic treatment was 1.5 years, and the median duration of follow-up from initiation of the first biologic treatment was 2.2 years.

Overall Treatment Persistence

Treatment persistence stratified by individual biologics and treatment experience is provided in Table 2 and Fig. 2. Among the biologics evaluated, infliximab showed the longest median duration of treatment (33.6 months, 95% CI 26.9–42.4), while brodalumab showed the shortest median duration of treatment (9.7 months, 95% CI 6.9–13.8). Median treatment duration data were not available for guselkumab, as many patients were still on treatment by the end of the follow-up period [median (min–max) follow-up: 11.2 (0.4–19.9) months]. Median treatment duration was slightly longer for bio-experienced than bio-naïve patients [22.8 months (95% CI 19.1–27.3) versus 18.1 months (95% CI 15.9–20.5)]. Among individual biologics evaluated, the highest overall 6-month treatment survival rate was observed with infliximab (89%), followed by guselkumab (88%) and ustekinumab (86%). Treatment with guselkumab showed the highest 1-year overall treatment survival rate (83%), followed by infliximab (77%) and ustekinumab (73%).

Cox Regression Analysis for Time to Treatment Discontinuation

Among all variables included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis for the overall population, sex and specific PsO diagnostic codes showed significant associations with the risk of treatment discontinuation (Table 3). Males were associated with a 16% lower risk of treatment discontinuation compared with females (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.73–0.97; p = 0.016). Patients with specified PsO diagnostic codes of L40.0 (PsO vulgaris; HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.53–0.90), L40.1 (generalized pustular PsO; HR 0.75; 95% CI 0.57–0.98), and L40.9 (PsO unspecified; HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.59–0.87) were significantly associated with a lower risk of biologic discontinuation [31% (p = 0.006); 25% (p = 0.034); and 28% (p = 0.001)], respectively, compared with the patients who did not have these respective specific PsO diagnostic codes.

The findings from the multivariate Cox regression analysis stratified by individual biologics were inconsistent with those for the overall population and across different biologics (Table 4). Sex showed no significant association with treatment persistence for any individual biologic. However, age was found to be significantly associated with an increased risk of discontinuing adalimumab (each year of increase in age was associated with a 1.2% increase in the risk of discontinuation). In addition, the CCI score was significantly associated with an increased risk of discontinuing infliximab (20% increased risk with each 1-point increase in CCI score). Regarding specific PsO diagnostic codes, patients with a diagnostic code of L40.0 (PsO vulgaris) were more likely to continue treatment with secukinumab and adalimumab, patients with a diagnostic code of L40.1 (generalized pustular PsO) were more likely to continue treatment with secukinumab, and patients with a diagnostic code of L40.9 (PsO unspecified) were more likely to continue treatment with adalimumab. In contrast, patients with a diagnostic code of L40.8 (other PsO) showed an increased risk of discontinuing ixekizumab (HR 4.14; 95% CI 1.72–9.98; p = 0.002; Table 4).

Discussion

This retrospective observational study evaluated the overall biologic treatment persistence in PsO with respect to the median treatment duration and overall treatment survival, and factors associated with biologic treatment persistence in real-world clinical practice in Japan. Although all prescribed biologics were assessed from the time of initiation of the first biologic treatment through the end of the follow-up period, risankizumab and certolizumab pegol were not included in the final analysis, as both were more recently approved for the treatment of PsO and had insufficient data at the time of study initiation. Patient characteristics for our PsO cohort did not differ from those previously reported, with a median age of 48.4 years and a greater proportion of male than female patients [20].

The overall persistence analysis demonstrated a longer median duration of treatment with infliximab (TNF-α inhibitor), followed by ustekinumab (IL-12/23 inhibitor) among all biologics. This observation may, in part, be related to infliximab representing the only biologic for PsO administered by intravenous (IV) infusion and that ustekinumab is the only IL-12/23 inhibitor indicated for the treatment of PsO. As per the Japanese guidelines, IV administration of infliximab requires patients to visit a healthcare facility, in particular for monitoring potential infusion reactions [4, 21]. Furthermore, the risk of developing autoantibodies against infliximab and subsequent immune responses to infliximab treatment may increase with interruptions in treatment with infliximab [22]. Consequently, patients who are responding well to infliximab may more likely to continue the treatment by their healthcare provider to minimize these potential risks, which could contribute to the high persistence rates observed for infliximab in our study [21].

With regard to ustekinumab, no other IL-12/23 inhibitor is approved for treating PsO, which may account, in part, for the long median duration of treatment observed for ustekinumab in our study. Ustekinumab is also dosed less frequently than other biologics evaluated in our analysis, which could also provide an incentive to maintain treatment over time. Of note, a meta-analysis including 46 studies reported higher treatment persistence with ustekinumab among all biologics for treating patients with PsO consistent with our results [10]. Furthermore, the overall findings of our main study are supported by a sensitivity analysis including a 90-day grace period for all biologic treatments and are in line with several studies that evaluated biologic treatment persistence in patients with PsO and those with PsO-PsA [3, 11, 17, 23]. As noted, the median duration of treatment data was not available for guselkumab, provided an insufficient number of patients had discontinued guselkumab by the end of the follow-up period to permit determination of this outcome.

In our analysis, patients treated with guselkumab showed a low rate of treatment discontinuation, with only 14% discontinuing treatment episodes, while the rates of discontinuing treatment were ⁓50% for all other biologics evaluated. In agreement with our findings, lower discontinuation rates have been reported with guselkumab treatment of PsO in retrospective claims database studies [24, 25]. A US IBM MarketScan database study including ~21,000 patients with PsO reported that patients continuing guselkumab had a median duration of treatment of 26.2 months before discontinuation, while corresponding values for patients receiving ixekizumab, secukinumab, and adalimumab were 12.5, 12.4, and 9.9 months, respectively [24]. A similar real-world observational study including 3251 patients with PsO also reported higher treatment persistence with guselkumab [25]. Longer time to treatment discontinuation could potentially reflect the excellent overall efficacy and safety profile of guselkumab in patients with PsO.

As per Japanese treatment guidelines [21], infliximab, ustekinumab, guselkumab, and risankizumab are not approved for administration by self-injection, requiring a hospital appointment for each dose provided. This may lead to patients receiving more monitoring support and provide greater incentives for continuing effective treatment. Similarly, our previous study showed optimal adherence for biologics that are not approved for self-administration in Japan [20]. Together, these findings suggest that routine treatment administration with regular monitoring could enhance treatment persistence beyond treatment response alone.

Among all biologics evaluated, guselkumab demonstrated the highest 1-year overall treatment survival rate, although survival rates at 6 months were similar for guselkumab and infliximab. In our study, observation periods for IL-23 inhibitors were shorter than those for other biologics, including TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-12/23 inhibitors, as data availability for the class of IL-23 inhibitors was at a maximum of 2 years. Notably, our findings are in line with results from other previously published studies [26–31]. An analysis of data from the prospective British Association of Dermatologists Biologics and Immunomodulators Register (BADBIR) study, which included 16,122 treatment episodes with adalimumab, ustekinumab, secukinumab, guselkumab, and ixekizumab, demonstrated the highest treatment survival with guselkumab [27]. A retrospective, observational, single-center study from China including 386 patients with PsO reported the highest 1-year cumulative treatment survival rate with guselkumab (72.2%), followed by ixekizumab (67.1%), secukinumab (63.0%), and adalimumab (37.1%) [26]. Several other studies have evaluated biologic treatment survival over a shorter duration period [32–34]; however, longer-term duration studies are warranted for better understanding of specific factors affecting biologic treatment persistence.

Treatment survival is an important concept, as it helps quantify the long-term effectiveness of biologic therapies and can be used as a reference for treatment decision-making [34]. From the multivariate Cox regression analysis, results showed that sex and specific PsO diagnostic codes showed significant associations with risk of treatment discontinuation. Our study suggests that males were more likely to continue biologic treatment compared with females and that the male sex is favorably associated with treatment survival, as documented in earlier studies [35, 36]. This finding is also in line with a retrospective claims database study including 2,707 US Medicare patients continuing biologic treatment for PsO [15], which reported that males had a 27% lower risk of treatment discontinuation than females (odds ratio 0.73, 95% CI 0.62–0.86; p < 0.001) [15]. Furthermore, it was reported that male patients may have more severe disease in general and require biologic therapy more frequently than females [37]; thus, male patients may be more likely to continue biologic treatment. Additionally, results from the prospective BioCAPTURE registry from the Netherlands, including 396 biologic treatment episodes (adalimumab, 137; etanercept, 90; ustekinumab, 137; secukinumab, 24; and infliximab, 8) for patients with PsO showed that females had lower treatment satisfaction due to a higher incidence of adverse events related to biologic treatment compared with male patients [38]. In turn, these factors collectively may contribute to higher rates of treatment discontinuation in females than males. In contrast, the prospective BIOBADADERM cohort study, which included 2881 Spanish patients with PsO, found no difference in treatment persistence for prescribed biologics between males and females [39].

In our study, patients with specific PsO diagnostic codes, such as L40.0 (PsO vulgaris), L40.1 (generalized pustular PsO), and L40.9 (PsO unspecified) were more likely to continue treatment. This may imply that therapeutic unmet needs remain for patients with L40.4 (guttate PsO) and L40.8 (other PsO) PsO diagnostic codes, even though biologic treatment may improve these PsO conditions [40]. Of note, age, CCI score, and prior exposure to biologics did not show any statistically significant association with treatment persistence in the overall study cohort. As there are no robust data regarding reasons for biologic discontinuation in these patients due to the nature of insurance claims data, the specific PsO diagnostic codes may imply that disease characteristics associated with severity, withdrawal flares, and risk of recurrence might motivate patients to stay on suitable biologic treatment [41, 42].

When considering individual biologic treatments, an association between sex and risk of treatment discontinuation was not observed in our study. However, in the adalimumab group, the risk of treatment discontinuation was significantly associated with increasing age, while in the infliximab group, the risk of treatment discontinuation was significantly associated with higher CCI score. A retrospective US Medicare study including 1084 patients treated with adalimumab reported that a higher proportion of patients > 65 years of age discontinued treatment compared with patients < 65 years of age (46.6% versus 40.6%, respectively) [15]. A population-based cohort study including 1750 Danish patients with PsO/PsA from the DANBIO registry reported that patients with a higher CCI score (≥ 2) who were treated with a TNF-α inhibitor had a shorter duration of treatment persistence (1.3 years) compared with patients with a CCI score of 0 (2.6 years, log-rank p < 0.001) [43]. Apart from TNF-α inhibitors, patients with L40.8 diagnostic code (other PsO; erythrodermic PsO) showed a significantly increased risk of treatment discontinuation for ixekizumab in our study. This finding may reflect similar outcomes from a report on treatment survival in patients with erythrodermic PsO who failed secukinumab indicating that efficacy was not sustainable [44]. The study suggested that prior use of biologics, particularly with primary failure of a biologic in the same class, could be a predictive factor for treatment discontinuation [44]. In our previous report on the same PsO cohort used in the current study, a majority of patients switched treatment within the same class [20]; therefore, it could be that secondary failure with an IL-17 inhibitor might play a role in treatment discontinuation.

Moreover, several studies have shown that smoking, diet, genetic expressions, baseline comorbidomes, and duration of inadequate conventional therapy to advanced therapy affect treatment persistence and pharmacological response/resistance in chronic illnesses including PsO [45–50]. Smoking causes certain changes in the gene expression profiles that regulated T-cells to the colon and skin, leading to detrimental outcomes in patients with chronic diseases [45]. Diet and circadian rhythm influence the treatment response in patients with PsO, especially those undergoing phototherapy [50]. Similarly, genetic expressions and molecular mechanisms underlying pathogenesis and progression of chronic diseases including PsO impact immunity and indirectly guide the drug response and treatment persistence, necessitating the genetic profiling for development of treatment approaches for PsO [46–48]. Furthermore, several comorbidomes (autoimmune and inflammatory systemic diseases, inflammatory bowel diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cirrhosis, endocrine diseases, mental disorders, and cardiovascular diseases) present at the onset of PsO may impact biologic resistance and treatment persistence, necessitating comprehensive patient management plans [49]. Additionally, long-term use of inadequate conventional therapies without appropriate escalation to the advanced therapy in suitable patients could increase the PsO burden and associated comorbidities [50].

In our analysis, bio-experienced patients demonstrated longer treatment persistence compared with bio-naïve patients, yet the difference was not statistically significant. It should be noted that bio-naïve patients who initiated the biologic treatment during follow-up were considered bio-experienced from that point. Additionally, bio-experienced patients would prefer not to switch treatment frequently and continue the prescribed biologics for longer periods. These events might lead to longer median treatment duration in bio-experienced patients. Nonetheless, the difference in the treatment duration between bio-experienced and bio-naïve patients was only for a few months. Additionally, patients might stay on certain biologics (e.g., IL-23 inhibitor) due to sustained and durable efficacy [51]. A retrospective study using claims data from the IBM MarketScan Commercial and Medicare Supplemental Databases found similar trends in treatment persistence between bio-naïve and bio-experienced patients [24].

There are several limitations of this study that should be taken into consideration. First, this is a secondary health insurance registry study that includes claims data collected mainly for administrative purposes. Therefore, relevant variables [such as life-style factors (smoking and diet), genetics, safety, symptom severity, and health-related quality of life] influencing treatment persistence are not available. Second, compared with other biologics, patients treated with guselkumab had a shorter duration of follow-up (maximum duration, 5 years versus 2 years, respectively) due to its more recent approval for the treatment of PsO. Although availability of longer-term data for guselkumab may be more limited, a trend for better persistence was observed for guselkumab overall. Nonetheless, longer periods of accrued data may help consolidate the power of estimates and provide a clearer picture of real-world treatment survival. Third, the analysis was subject to several potential sources of bias, such as older patients/retirees (> 65 years) being discontinued from the employee insurance system in Japan leading to the underrepresentation of the elderly population. Fourth, reasons for treatment discontinuation were not available, as this study was based on a healthcare insurance claims data. However, the overall estimate of biologic treatment discontinuation was captured for Japanese patients with moderate to severe PsO. Last, the number of facilities that are approved for prescribing biologic treatment in Japan is relatively limited, which could also impact the results of this study. Additionally, the use of the claims data to study treatment persistence and survival does not account for patients’ preference and consideration regarding treatment (e.g., IV or subcutaneous administration) received. These limitations are generally inherent in real-world studies using claims data and should be taken into consideration while interpreting the results from this study.

Conclusion

In this study, we assessed treatment persistence in terms of median treatment duration and overall treatment survival in a real-world setting for patients with PsO receiving biologic treatment in Japan. Patients with PsO receiving infliximab and ustekinumab demonstrated longer overall median treatment duration compared with other biologics evaluated. However, the highest 1-year treatment survival rate was observed for guselkumab, followed by infliximab and ustekinumab. Sex and specific PsO diagnostic codes were significantly associated with the risk of treatment discontinuation, which adds pertinent information on factors associated with overall treatment persistence. This may serve as useful information for clinicians when selecting appropriate long-term treatment during routine clinical practice in Japan. Additional studies of longer duration analyzing factors affecting treatment persistence and treatment survival rates are required to better understand treatment outcomes, improve adherence to chronic treatment, achieve better patient satisfaction, and manage PsO treatment effectively.
