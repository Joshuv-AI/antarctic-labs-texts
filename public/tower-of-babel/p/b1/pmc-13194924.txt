Divide and recombine approaches for fitting logistic regression to large-scale health surveillance data: application to diabetes risk prediction in BRFSS
Authors: Md. Mahadi Hassan Nayem, Soma Chowdhury Biswas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global rise in diabetes mellitus poses a major public health concern, highlighting the urgent need for effective risk prediction models to support early identification and prevention strategies. Yet, applying conventional statistical techniques to large-scale health surveillance data remains challenging due to memory limitations and high computational demands. One promising alternative, the divide and recombine (D&R) approach partitions the data into smaller subsets, fits logistic models independently within each, and combines the estimates to approximate full-sample maximum likelihood results. While the D&R methodology and its theoretical properties are well established in the statistical literature, such validation on national-scale health surveillance data remains limited. This study provides a comprehensive empirical validation of the D&R strategy for fitting logistic regression models efficiently on massive datasets, demonstrating its computational scalability, statistical robustness, and reproducibility in a real-world public health setting. We utilize the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) data from 2014 to 2024, encompassing over 2.4 million observations and 16 demographic, behavioral, clinical, and socioeconomic predictors. Monte Carlo simulations using 5 million synthetic observations and 1,000 replications confirm that the D&R method matches the statistical efficiency of centralized estimation (relative efficiency > 99.8%) while reducing computation time by more than 52% and memory usage by 77–89%. Application to BRFSS data further demonstrates the framework’s practical scalability, successfully recovering well-established diabetes risk factors including age, body mass index, general health status, alcohol use, cardiovascular disease history, and smoking, consistent with the epidemiological literature. These findings confirm that the D&R framework enables large-scale chronic disease modeling on standard computing infrastructure without reliance on high-performance systems, with direct implications for population health monitoring and preventive care resource allocation.

Introduction

The global epidemiological landscape has undergone a profound transformation over recent decades, characterized by a marked shift from communicable to non-communicable diseases (NCDs) as the predominant contributors to mortality and morbidity. While infectious diseases such as coronavirus disease (COVID-19), dengue fever, hepatitis, and HIV/AIDS continue to demand substantial public health attention due to their rapid transmission potential1–3, NCDs now constitute the leading causes of death and disability-adjusted life-years globally, accounting for 71% of all deaths worldwide4,5.

Among NCDs, diabetes mellitus has emerged as a particularly pressing public health challenge. In the United States, diabetes ranks as the seventh leading cause of death, affecting more than 37.3 million individuals (11.3% of the population) as of 20196. The disease burden is compounded by substantial underdiagnosis, with approximately 20% of individuals with diabetes and 80% with prediabetes remaining unaware of their condition7. Beyond mortality, diabetes imposes severe morbidity through complications including retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, cardiovascular disease, and lower-extremity amputation8, with affected individuals experiencing approximately 60% higher risk of premature death compared to those without diabetes6.

The economic burden is equally substantial. Annual medical expenditures for diagnosed diabetes patients average $16,752, with $9,601 directly attributable to diabetes-related care9,10. At the macroeconomic level, diabetes-related costs in the United States exceeded $327 billion in 2017, including $237 billion in direct medical costs and $90 billion in reduced productivity9. Furthermore, diabetes substantially increases vulnerability to emerging infectious diseases; among hospitalized COVID-19 patients, 39.7% had diabetes as an underlying comorbidity, rising to 46.5% in the 50–64 age group11,12.

Global projections portend continued escalation of disease burden. The International Diabetes Federation estimated that 537 million adults worldwide were living with diabetes in 2021, with this figure projected to reach 783 million by 2045—a 46% increase13,14. Regional disparities are pronounced, with the Middle East and North Africa expected to experience an 86% increase from 73 million to 135.7 million cases by 204515. These epidemiological trends underscore the critical importance of early risk identification and targeted preventive interventions. However, comprehensive population-wide screening faces substantial operational and financial constraints, with estimated costs exceeding $15 billion annually in the United States alone16,17.

Statistical modeling and machine learning methodologies offer scalable alternatives for risk stratification by leveraging large-scale health surveillance datasets. Logistic regression remains a foundational tool for binary disease outcome prediction due to its interpretability, established clinical validity, and ability to quantify risk factor associations18,19. Recent systematic reviews have documented extensive application of logistic regression in diabetes prediction, with studies reporting area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.70 to 0.85 for models incorporating demographic, anthropometric, and clinical predictors20,21. However, application to large-scale health surveys presents methodological challenges related to computational constraints, class imbalance, missing data, and feature correlation22–24.

The computational challenges associated with fitting generalized linear models (GLMs) to datasets exceeding memory capacity have motivated development of divide and recombine (D&R) approaches25. The D&R framework partitions large datasets into manageable subsets, fits models independently on each partition (potentially in parallel), and recombines subset-specific parameter estimates to approximate full-data maximum likelihood estimates. Theoretical investigations have established that D&R estimators achieve asymptotic efficiency equivalent to centralized estimation under appropriate partitioning strategies26,27. Recent implementations, including the R package drglm, have operationalized D&R estimation for logistic regression with robust standard error estimation and out-of-memory data handling28.

This study focuses on a large-scale empirical validation of the established divide-and-recombine logistic regression methodology on national health surveillance data, focusing on scalability, reproducibility, and practical feasibility. While the D&R methodology and its theoretical foundations are well established in the statistical literature, comprehensive empirical validation on real-world health surveillance data at the scale of millions of observations remains limited. This study addresses this gap by providing rigorous computational and statistical validation of D&R-based logistic regression in the context of large-scale chronic disease modeling. Our work makes three primary contributions:

First, we conduct extensive Monte Carlo simulations (1000 replications with 5 million observations each) to quantify the finite-sample statistical properties of D&R estimators across multiple partition sizes, providing practical guidance on partition selection that complements existing asymptotic theory.

Second, we demonstrate the feasibility and reproducibility of D&R methods on a large-scale, real-world health surveillance dataset (BRFSS 2014–2024, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n > 2.4$$\end{document} million), comparing computational efficiency and statistical equivalence with conventional full-data maximum likelihood estimation.

Third, we investigate the complex multivariate relationships among demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical predictors of diabetes risk in a contemporary, nationally representative sample, providing updated epidemiological insights relevant to public health practice and policy.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. “Divide and recombine logistic regression: methodological framework” reviews the D&R logistic regression framework, including score equations, Hessian structure, and recombination strategy. “Computational implementation” describes the sequential partitioning methodology used to construct manageable data subsets. “Monte Carlo simulation study” presents comprehensive Monte Carlo simulation results evaluating statistical accuracy and computational performance. “Application to BRFSS data” applies the D&R method to the BRFSS dataset, detailing variable selection, preprocessing, model fitting, and comparative analyses. Finally, “Discussion and conclusions” discusses key findings, practical implications, limitations, and future directions.

D&R estimation framework

The D&R estimation framework builds upon the compression scheme proposed by29, which enables high-quality aggregation of logistic regression estimates in multidimensional data space. The compression methodology retains model parameters and auxiliary measures from each data segment, facilitating model reconstruction through aggregation formulas. The compression scheme satisfies two critical criteria: first, the compressed data support lossless or asymptotically lossless aggregation of regression measures; second, the space complexity of compressed data remains minimal and independent of the number of tuples within each segment29.

The D&R framework encompasses two fundamental components: data division strategies and result recombination methods. According to30, dataset division can be accomplished through conditioning-variable division or replicate division. Conditioning-variable division employs stratification based on one or more variables, preserving specific data structures within subsets (e.g., stratifying by year, geographic region, or outcome class). Replicate division utilizes random sampling without replacement, enabling simpler and faster computations but potentially incurring modest losses in precision relative to stratified approaches. The partitioning scheme must ensure that divisions are manageable in memory while allowing for further subdivision if necessary.

The recombination procedure encompasses statistical, analytic, and visual recombination approaches31. Statistical recombination refers to the aggregation of statistical outputs obtained from each subset, as exemplified by the parameter estimation framework we now describe. Analytic recombination involves continued analysis of subset-level outputs, treating them as reduced datasets that enable subsequent analytical procedures. When outputs achieve substantial data reduction, analyzing these compressed datasets becomes an essential component of the D&R approach. Visual recombination explores detailed data at their most granular level, utilizing subsets containing fine-grained information as highlighted by32.

Assumptions

The D&R framework relies on several key assumptions: independent partitions, adequate subset size, homogeneous subsets, and standard maximum likelihood regularity conditions. Specifically, observations are assumed to be independent both within and across subsets, which is satisfied by random or sequential partitioning of independent data. Each subset must contain sufficient observations for reliable estimation, typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_k \ge 10p$$\end{document} where p is the number of parameters. The data-generating process should be consistent across subsets, as substantial heterogeneity may lead to biased aggregation. Standard maximum likelihood regularity conditions must hold, including identifiability, continuity of derivatives, and existence of moments.

Potential limitations include complete separation, numerical instability, and temporal dependence. If any subset exhibits complete or quasi-complete separation, subset-specific MLEs may not exist or may be unreliable, requiring penalized estimation methods (e.g., Firth correction). For very small subsets or highly correlated predictors, Hessian matrices may be near-singular, leading to numerical instability in aggregation. For time-series data, sequential partitioning may violate independence assumptions, necessitating alternative partitioning schemes.

Computational implementation

Subset k contains observations from row \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \sum _{j=1}^{k-1}n_j\right) +1$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{j=1}^{k}n_j$$\end{document}; and Subset K contains observations from row \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \sum _{j=1}^{K-1}n_j\right) +1$$\end{document} to n.

This approach, also termed serial partitioning33, offers several practical advantages: memory efficiency, parallelization, simplicity, and flexibility. Data can be processed in chunks without loading the entire dataset, subsets can be processed independently on different computing nodes, no complex stratification logic is required, and partition sizes can be chosen based on available memory.

Choice of number of partitions

The choice of K involves a trade-off between computational efficiency and statistical precision. Larger K results in smaller subsets that require less memory and can be processed faster, but may increase approximation error if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_k$$\end{document} becomes too small relative to p. Smaller K yields larger subsets that provide more stable estimates but require more memory and longer processing time per subset. We recommend the following guidelines for choosing K: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_k \ge \max \{10p, 1000\}$$\end{document} for all k. For datasets where n is very large relative to available memory M, we suggest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = \lceil \frac{n \cdot s}{M \cdot c} \rceil$$\end{document}, where s is the average size per observation (in bytes), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c \in (0,1)$$\end{document} is a safety factor (typically 0.5–0.7) to account for computational overhead.

Robustness to partitioning schemes and population heterogeneity

An important question is whether the D&R estimator is sensitive to the specific partitioning scheme. Under the assumptions stated in “Theoretical properties and assumptions”, the D&R estimator converges to the same limiting distribution regardless of how the data are partitioned, provided that each subset contains a representative sample of the population, subset sizes grow proportionally with the total sample size, and the number of subsets does not grow too rapidly. The sequential partitioning strategy adopted in this study satisfies these conditions when applied to data that have been randomly permuted or are otherwise exchangeable. For datasets with inherent ordering (e.g., temporal trends), we recommend random shuffling prior to sequential partitioning to ensure homogeneity across subsets.

In Stratified random partitioning, observations are sampled proportionally from pre-defined strata (e.g., survey year, geographic region) to populate each subset, represents a valid alternative that explicitly enforces distributional balance, it introduces additional implementation complexity without guaranteed gains in statistical performance when the dataset is sufficiently large and randomly permuted. In our BRFSS application, we assessed the adequacy of simple random permutation by conducting empirical homogeneity checks across three dimensions. First, temporal homogeneity was verified by confirming that survey year distributions across D&R subsets deviated by less than 2% from overall sample proportions. Second, geographic homogeneity was assessed by comparing state-level diabetes prevalence across randomly formed subsets, with maximum deviations below 3%. Third, demographic homogeneity was confirmed by comparing distributions of age, sex, and BMI categories across subsets, with deviations consistently below 2% from overall proportions.

These empirical checks confirm that simple random permutation produces subsets sufficiently homogeneous for reliable D&R aggregation in this setting, and that stratified partitioning would not materially alter subset composition or estimator stability. The aggregation formula in Eq. (14) assumes that subset-specific MLEs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\beta }_k$$\end{document} estimate the same underlying parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0$$\end{document}; the homogeneity checks above support this assumption for our data. Nevertheless, for datasets exhibiting stronger structural heterogeneity, such as those with pronounced geographic clustering or highly imbalanced outcome distributions across temporal strata, stratified partitioning should be considered. We recommend that practitioners routinely compare subset-specific outcome prevalences as a diagnostic: large discrepancies across subsets may indicate that stratified partitioning or stratum-specific modeling is warranted.

Monte Carlo simulation study

To evaluate the finite-sample accuracy and computational performance of the D&R approach for logistic regression, we conducted a comprehensive Monte Carlo simulation study. This analysis quantifies how closely the D&R-based logistic model reproduces the true underlying data-generating process through repeated random sampling and model fitting. While the asymptotic properties of D&R estimators are well established theoretically26,27, empirical validation at realistic sample sizes provides practical guidance for applied researchers.

Practitioners should therefore ensure adequate subset sizes even when computational constraints motivate aggressive partitioning.

Summary of simulation findings

These comprehensive simulation results confirm the robustness and consistency of the D&R-based logistic model across multiple dimensions. The method produces unbiased estimates that are statistically indistinguishable from full-data CMLE, with mean absolute bias less than 0.0003 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K \le 100$$\end{document}. Relative efficiency exceeds 99.8% for all partition sizes tested in this range, with negligible efficiency loss even for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=100$$\end{document}. Standard errors are accurately estimated, and 95% confidence intervals achieve nominal coverage (94.7–95.1%). Sampling distributions are approximately normal, consistent with asymptotic theory. Computation time is reduced by 52–55% (speedup factor 2.1–2.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}) while memory usage is reduced by 78–89%. Performance remains stable across different partition sizes within the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K \in [25, 100]$$\end{document}, providing flexibility in implementation based on available computational resources. The near-constant computation time across partition sizes indicates good scalability to even larger datasets. However, performance deteriorates when subset sizes fall below 10p, confirming the importance of our recommended minimum subset size guidelines.

These findings provide strong empirical validation for the theoretical framework presented in “Divide and recombine logistic regression: methodological framework” and justify the application of D&R methods to large-scale epidemiological datasets. The simulation study demonstrates that D&R achieves an optimal balance: the statistical rigor of full-data maximum likelihood estimation combined with the computational efficiency necessary for big data applications.

Application to BRFSS data

Data overview and variable selection

This study leverages the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) dataset spanning 2014 through 2024, representing one of the most extensive continuously conducted health surveillance systems in the United States. The BRFSS employs standardized telephone-based survey methodology (landline and cellular) to systematically collect data on health-related risk behaviors, chronic health conditions, and preventive service utilization among the non-institutionalized adult population aged 18 years and older34.

The BRFSS utilizes a complex multistage sampling design with stratification by state and involves probability sampling within households. While the original survey includes sampling weights to account for selection probability and non-response, the present analysis focuses on methodological validation of the D&R approach using unweighted estimation. The longitudinal nature of this dataset enables temporal analysis of health trends and examination of evolving patterns in diabetes prevalence and associated risk factors across more than a decade of surveillance data.

Following extensive systematic review of epidemiological and machine learning literature pertaining to diabetes prediction35–39, we identified 16 independent variables demonstrating robust empirical associations with diabetes outcomes. The selection criteria prioritized variables with established clinical or epidemiological relevance to diabetes pathophysiology, consistent availability across all survey years (2014–2024), sufficient data quality and completeness (missing rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 20\%$$\end{document} in each year), and absence of severe multicollinearity (pairwise correlations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.7$$\end{document}).

Outcome definition and clinical considerations

The dependent variable, diabetes status (DIABETE4), was operationalized as a binary outcome through careful consideration of clinical heterogeneity. Respondents were classified based on their response to: “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have diabetes?” The original response structure included multiple categories: Yes; Yes, but female told only during pregnancy; No; No, prediabetes or borderline diabetes; Don’t know/Not sure; Refused; and missing values.

Consistent with established epidemiological practices distinguishing chronic from transient glycemic disorders, we excluded gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) from our diabetes classification schema. This exclusion is predicated on the pathophysiologically distinct nature of GDM, which represents a temporary state of glucose intolerance occurring during pregnancy and typically resolving postpartum40. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, characterized by autoimmune-mediated beta-cell destruction41,42, and Type 2 diabetes, characterized by progressive insulin resistance and beta-cell dysfunction43,44, GDM exhibits unique pathogenesis linked to pregnancy-associated hormonal changes. Consequently, respondents indicating diabetes diagnosis exclusively during pregnancy were systematically excluded from the analytical cohort.

Our binary categorization consolidates Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes under a unified diabetes variable, reflecting the clinical reality that approximately 90–95% of cases are Type 2, while 5–10% are Type 145,46. Because category-specific counts were sparse and computational cost high, we focused on binary diabetes outcomes. Extension to multinomial D&R for distinguishing Type 1, Type 2, and gestational diabetes is a planned future direction. Respondents reporting prediabetes were classified as non-diabetic, as prediabetes represents an intermediate glycemic state rather than overt diabetes.

Data preprocessing protocol

This section details the preprocessing steps applied to ensure data quality and analytical validity, including temporal randomization to achieve homogeneous D&R subsets, our approach to handling missing data, our age restriction rationale, and characteristics of the final analytical sample.

Temporal randomization

To ensure homogeneity across D&R subsets and avoid temporal clustering, we implemented random permutation followed by sequential partitioning: merging BRFSS datasets from 2014–2024, randomly shuffling all observations using a uniform random permutation, and applying sequential partitioning to create K subsets as described in “Computational implementation”. Given the large sample size within each subset, random permutation yields near-proportional representation of survey years in expectation. Empirical verification confirmed that the absolute deviation of yearly proportions across all subsets was less than 2% from their overall sample proportions, indicating that explicit stratified partitioning would not materially alter subset composition. This procedure prevents year-specific clustering that would arise from sequential partitioning without permutation, thereby reducing potential bias from temporal trends or cohort effects. Although a stratified random partitioning strategy based on survey year could further enforce exact proportional balance, the negligible empirical deviation observed under random permutation indicates that additional stratification is unlikely to materially affect estimator stability in this setting.

Missing data handling

To maintain analytical rigour and minimise bias associated with uncertain or incomplete responses, we implemented complete-case analysis by excluding observations with missing values for any study variable, responses indicating “Don’t know/Not sure”, explicit refusal to respond, and gestational diabetes cases. The missingness mechanism analysis revealed an overall missing rate of 18.3% of raw observations, with missing data more common among older respondents (24.1% for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 75 years vs. 15.7% for < 65 years). Income had the highest item-level missing rate (12.4%), while diabetes status had minimal missingness (0.8%).

While complete-case analysis reduces sample size and may introduce selection bias if missingness is related to both predictors and outcome, it ensures that all analytical inferences are based on complete and internally consistent information. To assess the potential impact of this approach, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation via the mice package47. Due to the substantial computational and memory demands associated with imputing the full dataset (n = 2,498,880), multiple imputation was performed on a randomly selected subset of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 500{,}000$$\end{document} observations (approximately 20% of the analytical sample), preserving the joint distribution of predictors and outcome.

Across 20 imputed datasets, coefficient estimates differed by less than 5% on average (mean absolute difference = 0.012 log-odds units), with the direction and statistical significance of all predictors unchanged. These results suggest that the complete-case analysis did not materially alter substantive conclusions. Nevertheless, because imputation was conducted on a subset rather than the full sample, this sensitivity analysis should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence regarding the robustness of findings.

Age restriction rationale

Preliminary descriptive analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in diabetes prevalence across age strata: 2.1% for ages 18–39, 14.8% for ages 40–64, and 26.3% for ages \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge 65$$\end{document}. This observation aligns with extensive epidemiological evidence demonstrating exponentially increasing diabetes risk after age 40–45 years48–50. To enhance statistical efficiency, clinical relevance, and address class imbalance, we restricted our analytical sample to individuals aged 40 years and older. This age threshold optimization serves dual purposes: focusing analytical resources on the population at elevated risk and mitigating class imbalance issues by increasing prevalence from 2% to 17% overall. The age restriction improved model convergence properties and reduced the risk of complete separation in sparse cells of the contingency table formed by predictor combinations.

Final sample characteristics

Following these preprocessing steps, the curated dataset comprised a sample size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 2,498,880$$\end{document} observations over the time period 2014–2024 (11 years), with outcome prevalence of 17.2% diabetes (429,608 cases), 16 predictors (42 levels after dummy coding), and age range 40–99 years (mean = 58.4, SD = 12.7).

Analytical methods

This subsection describes the analytical approach used to validate the D&R methodology on the BRFSS diabetes dataset. We detail the model fitting procedures including partition configurations, the comparative analysis strategy involving standard and optimized GLM implementations, and model convergence diagnostics including numerical stability checks and goodness-of-fit assessments.

Model fitting and comparative strategy

Following the preprocessing procedures described above, the final analytical dataset was partitioned using the sequential division strategy outlined in “Computational implementation”. We implemented three partition configurations: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = 25$$\end{document} with subset size approximately 100,000 observations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = 50$$\end{document} with subset size approximately 50,000 observations, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = 100$$\end{document} with subset size approximately 25,000 observations. For each configuration, logistic regression models were fitted independently to each subset, and the subset-specific estimates were subsequently aggregated according to the D&R framework presented in “Divide and recombine logistic regression: methodological framework”.

To validate the accuracy and reliability of the D&R approach on real data, we compared results against two established methods: standard GLM using the glm() function in R, representing conventional MLE, and speed-optimized GLM using the speedglm package51, which uses efficient sparse matrix operations. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.0 on the same hardware configuration described in “Monte Carlo simulation study”.

Model convergence and diagnostics

Prior to comparing parameter estimates, we verified model convergence for all methods. All models converged successfully with maximum gradient of the log-likelihood less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-8}$$\end{document} for all subsets and condition number of the Hessian matrices less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{4}$$\end{document}, indicating numerical stability. There was no evidence of complete or quasi-complete separation. Basic model diagnostics were performed: variance inflation factors (VIF) were all less than 5, suggesting no severe multicollinearity. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2 = 1583.3$$\end{document} (df = 8, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.001$$\end{document}). Given the very large sample size, this statistically significant result likely reflects minor deviations from perfect calibration rather than substantial model misspecification, as the Hosmer–Lemeshow test is known to be highly sensitive in large datasets. Model discrimination was good, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.8206 (95% CI: [0.8200, 0.8213], DeLong method;52). Taken together, these diagnostics indicate numerically stable estimation and good discriminative performance, with no evidence of major specification errors.

Interpretation of risk factors

The logistic regression model identified several significant predictors of diabetes risk, consistent with established epidemiological evidence. We interpret the coefficients as log-odds ratios; exponentiating these values yields odds ratios (ORs) that quantify the multiplicative change in diabetes odds associated with each predictor, holding all other variables constant. All interpretations explicitly state the reference category for clarity.

Demographic and anthropometric factors

Age demonstrated the strongest and most consistent association with diabetes prevalence. Using the 40–44 age group as the reference category, we observed monotonically increasing coefficients across successive age categories. The 60–64 age group exhibited a coefficient of 1.986 (OR = 7.29, 95% CI: [1.94, 2.03]), indicating over seven-fold higher odds of diabetes compared to the 40–44 age group. The relationship peaked in the 75–79 age group with a coefficient of 2.835 (OR = 17.03, 95% CI: [2.79, 2.88]), before declining slightly in the oldest age categories. This pattern is consistent with the well-established age-related increase in diabetes risk, attributable to progressive beta-cell dysfunction, increased insulin resistance, and accumulation of other metabolic risk factors48,49. The slight decline after age 75 likely reflects survival bias, whereby individuals with diabetes and severe complications have higher mortality and are thus underrepresented in the oldest age categories.

BMI categories demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship with diabetes risk. Using normal weight (BMI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<25$$\end{document}) as the reference category, overweight individuals (BMI 25–29.9) had a coefficient of 0.520 (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: [1.60, 1.76]), indicating 68% higher odds of diabetes compared to normal-weight individuals. This risk increased substantially among obese individuals: Class I obesity (BMI 30–34.9) showed a coefficient of 1.063 (OR = 2.89, 95% CI: [2.76, 3.03]), indicating nearly three-fold higher odds; and Class II/III obesity (BMI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge 35$$\end{document}) exhibited a coefficient of 1.650 (OR = 5.21, 95% CI: [4.97, 5.46]), indicating more than five-fold higher odds compared to normal-weight individuals. These findings underscore the critical role of adiposity in diabetes pathogenesis and are consistent with current clinical guidelines emphasising weight management as a primary prevention strategy53.

General health status

Self-rated general health showed strong associations with diabetes prevalence. Using excellent health as the reference category, odds of diabetes increased progressively with declining health status: very good (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: [1.89, 1.97]), good (OR = 3.65, 95% CI: [3.59, 3.72]), fair (OR = 5.60, 95% CI: [5.48, 5.71]), and poor (OR = 6.07, 95% CI: [5.93, 6.22]), each compared to individuals reporting excellent health. This association likely reflects bidirectional relationships: diabetes adversely affects perceived health through symptoms and complications, while poorer self-rated health may indicate co-occurring cardiometabolic conditions that share common pathways with diabetes54.

Behavioral risk factors

Alcohol consumption showed a statistically significant association with diabetes prevalence, though its interpretation requires caution. Relative to current drinkers, non-drinkers exhibited a coefficient of 0.466 (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: [1.58, 1.61]), indicating 59% higher odds of diabetes. This association may reflect reverse causation rather than a protective causal effect of alcohol consumption: individuals diagnosed with diabetes frequently reduce or discontinue alcohol use following medical advice, and those in poorer general health may also be less likely to consume alcohol55. Because the BRFSS does not distinguish between lifetime abstainers and former drinkers who ceased consumption due to health concerns, causal interpretation is not possible.

Smoking status demonstrated a significant association with diabetes prevalence. Relative to current smokers, non-smokers exhibited a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.182$$\end{document} (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: [0.82, 0.84]), indicating 17% lower odds of diabetes compared to current smokers. This finding is consistent with epidemiological evidence linking active smoking to insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism56, with proposed mechanisms including nicotine-induced sympathetic activation, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress.

Physical inactivity was associated with higher odds of diabetes prevalence. Relative to individuals who exercised in the past month, those reporting no physical exercise had a coefficient of 0.187 (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: [1.19, 1.22]), indicating 21% higher odds of diabetes. This association is consistent with the established protective effects of physical activity on insulin sensitivity, glucose metabolism, and weight management.

Socioeconomic and demographic factors

Income demonstrated a clear dose–response inverse gradient with diabetes risk. Full results for all intermediate categories are presented in Table 9. These gradients likely reflect socioeconomic differences in access to preventive healthcare, nutritious food environments, and opportunities for physical activity.

Educational attainment showed a similar inverse association with diabetes risk. Relative to individuals with less than high school education, high school graduates had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.113$$\end{document} (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: [0.88, 0.91]), indicating 11% lower odds of diabetes; those with some college education showed a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.058$$\end{document} (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: [0.93, 0.96]), indicating 6% lower odds; and college graduates exhibited a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.158$$\end{document} (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: [0.84, 0.87]), indicating 15% lower odds. Sex demonstrated a significant independent association with diabetes prevalence. Relative to males, females had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.297$$\end{document} (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: [0.74, 0.75]), indicating 26% lower odds of diabetes after adjustment for all covariates. This sex differential is consistent with prior epidemiological evidence and may reflect differences in hormonal influences on insulin sensitivity, body fat distribution, and healthcare utilisation patterns.

Marital status showed modest associations with diabetes prevalence. Relative to married individuals, never-married individuals had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.101$$\end{document} (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: [0.89, 0.91]), indicating 10% lower odds of diabetes. Widowed, separated, and divorced individuals exhibited coefficients of 0.025 (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: [1.01, 1.04]), 0.084 (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: [1.06, 1.12]), and 0.040 (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: [1.03, 1.06]), respectively, each indicating marginally higher odds compared to married individuals. Individuals in unmarried partnerships had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.091$$\end{document} (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: [0.89, 0.94]), indicating 9% lower odds. These associations may reflect differences in social support, shared health behaviors, and socioeconomic resources across marital status groups.

Clinical comorbidities

Several clinical comorbidities exhibited inverse associations with diabetes prevalence that warrant cautious interpretation. Cardiovascular disease showed a counterintuitive inverse association, with those carrying a CVD history exhibiting a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.398$$\end{document} (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: [0.66, 0.68]), indicating 33% lower observed odds of diabetes relative to individuals without CVD history.

Chronic kidney disease similarly showed an inverse association with diabetes prevalence. Relative to individuals without CKD, those with CKD had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.736$$\end{document} (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: [0.47, 0.49]), indicating 52% lower observed odds of diabetes. This finding is counterintuitive given the well-established bidirectional relationship between diabetes and renal dysfunction.

Asthma was associated with modestly lower observed odds of diabetes prevalence. Relative to individuals without asthma, those with asthma had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.121$$\end{document} (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: [0.88, 0.90]), indicating 11% lower observed odds of diabetes. Depression showed a marginal inverse association with diabetes prevalence.

Relative to individuals without depression, those with depression had a coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.072$$\end{document} (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: [0.92, 0.94]), indicating 7% lower observed odds of diabetes.

Arthritis showed no meaningful association with diabetes prevalence after covariate adjustment. Relative to individuals without arthritis, those with arthritis had a coefficient of 0.000 (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: [0.99, 1.01]), indicating comparable odds of diabetes across both groups. This null finding may reflect adequate adjustment for shared risk factors, particularly age and BMI.

The inverse associations observed across CVD, CKD, asthma, and depression are unlikely to represent protective causal effects, as substantial evidence links diabetes with increased risk of all four conditions. These paradoxical findings may be attributable to a combination of mechanisms: survival bias, whereby individuals with both diabetes and severe comorbidities face elevated mortality and are underrepresented in cross-sectional surveys; collider bias arising from conditioning on variables that lie on the causal pathway between diabetes and these conditions; differential diagnostic ascertainment; confounding by medication use; and the inability of cross-sectional data to establish temporal precedence between conditions57.

Discussion and conclusions

Summary of key findings

The primary contribution of this study is empirical validation of divide-and-recombine methods on national-scale health surveillance data. Through extensive Monte Carlo simulation studies involving 5 million observations and 1,000 replications, we established that D&R estimators achieve statistical properties equivalent to centralized maximum likelihood estimation (relative efficiency > 99.8%) while reducing computational time by 52–55% and memory usage by 78–89%. Application to the BRFSS dataset comprising over 2.4 million observations revealed substantively important associations between demographic, behavioral, clinical, and socioeconomic factors and diabetes risk, with findings largely consistent with established epidemiological evidence.

Methodological contributions and practical implications

While the D&R methodology and its theoretical foundations are well established in the statistical literature26,27,29, our study makes important contributions to empirical validation and practical implementation. We apply and empirically evaluate the established D&R framework at scale, demonstrating that simple sequential partitioning (after randomization) yields D&R estimators with properties equivalent to more complex stratified partitioning schemes, simplifying implementation for practitioners. Our finite-sample simulation study quantifies performance across realistic data dimensions and partition sizes, providing practical guidance that complements existing asymptotic theory. We established a multi-dimensional evaluation framework encompassing bias, variance, coverage probabilities, computational efficiency, and memory usage, enabling holistic assessment of D&R performance.

Our investigation of minimum subset size requirements (see “Monte Carlo simulation study”, paragraph on small-sample simulations) provides empirical validation of the recommended guideline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_k \ge \max \{10p, 1000\}$$\end{document}, demonstrating that performance deteriorates when subset sizes fall below approximately 10p observations per parameter. This finding has important practical implications for practitioners balancing computational constraints against statistical reliability.

Application to BRFSS data demonstrated that theoretical and simulation results translate to real-world epidemiological datasets with complex structure, missing data, and outcome imbalance. Our sensitivity analyses identified \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K \in [25, 100]$$\end{document} as an optimal range for most applications, balancing statistical precision and computational gains.

The D&R framework offers practical advantages for data processing pipelines, particularly when datasets arrive incrementally, when memory constraints preclude loading entire datasets, or when parallel computing resources are available. The equivalence between D&R and full-data MLE estimates, demonstrated both through simulation and real-data application, provides strong justification for adopting D&R approaches in routine health surveillance analytics. For public health agencies and research institutions working with large-scale survey data, the D&R approach enables analysis on standard computing infrastructure that would otherwise require high-performance computing resources.

Substantive public health implications

The substantive findings regarding diabetes risk factors carry important implications for public health practice and policy. The strong associations observed for modifiable risk factors provide actionable targets for prevention. Obesity prevention represents the highest-priority intervention target, with the clear dose-response relationship (OR ranging from 1.68 for overweight to 5.21 for severe obesity) indicating that population-level strategies addressing obesogenic environments merit increased investment. Physical activity promotion, with 21% higher odds for those not exercising, suggests that even modest increases in population physical activity levels could substantially reduce diabetes burden, with community-level interventions complementing individual counseling. Smoking cessation adds to tobacco’s well-established cardiovascular and cancer risks, with current smokers showing approximately 20% higher odds of diabetes, making comprehensive tobacco control policies essential for diabetes prevention.

The pronounced age gradient and socioeconomic disparities identify priority populations for targeted screening and prevention programs. Resource-constrained public health systems may achieve greatest impact by focusing on adults aged \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 50 years with obesity, lower-income and lower-education populations, individuals with poor/fair self-rated health, and those with sedentary lifestyles. Risk stratification algorithms incorporating these factors could enable efficient allocation of intensive lifestyle interventions to those most likely to benefit.

The persistent socioeconomic gradients after adjustment for BMI and health behaviors emphasize the importance of addressing social determinants. Income gradients ranging from OR = 0.93 for $15,000–$25,000 to OR = 0.72 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}$75,000 (compared to <$15,000) demonstrate that economic factors influence diabetes risk through pathways beyond measured behavioral mediators. Policies targeting education, income security, food access, and neighborhood environments represent critical upstream interventions that could reduce diabetes disparities at the population level.

Limitations and methodological considerations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Our analysis employed complete-case deletion for missing data, which reduced the effective sample size by 18.3%. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation indicated minimal bias, with coefficient differences below 5%; however, more advanced approaches such as inverse probability weighting that account for complex survey design may further improve precision and generalizability.

BRFSS relies on self-reported information and is therefore subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and potential misclassification. Diabetes status reflects diagnosed rather than true prevalence and may exclude undiagnosed cases, which are estimated to comprise approximately 20% of all diabetes cases.

The binary outcome combines Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes despite their distinct etiologies and risk factor profiles. Although Type 2 diabetes accounts for 90–95% of cases, separate modeling using multinomial logistic regression would require more detailed clinical information than is available in BRFSS.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The inverse associations observed for cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, asthma, and depression (“Application to BRFSS data”) likely reflect survival bias, reverse causation, and complex temporal dynamics that cannot be disentangled without longitudinal data. These findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive associations among prevalent cases rather than evidence of causal relationships.

Sampling weights designed to account for BRFSS’s complex survey structure were not incorporated. While this decision was appropriate for computational validation, future applications should integrate survey weights to produce population-representative estimates.

Although partition sizes ranging from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=10$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=150$$\end{document} were examined in sensitivity analyses, extremely small subset sizes that may induce complete separation or numerical instability were not systematically explored beyond the supplementary investigation described in “Monte Carlo simulation study”.

Future research directions

Several extensions could further advance this work. Expanding the D&R framework to more complex modeling contexts, including multinomial logistic regression for polytomous outcomes, mixed-effects models for clustered or longitudinal data, and Cox proportional hazards models for time-to-event analyses, represents a natural progression. Adaptive partitioning strategies that optimize subset formation based on data characteristics, including stratification by key covariates, may further enhance computational efficiency and precision.

Evaluation of D&R performance under extreme class imbalance, particularly for outcomes with prevalence below 1%, would extend applicability to rare disease settings. Comparative assessment against other scalable approaches, including online learning algorithms, sketching-based methods, and distributed optimization techniques such as the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers58, would provide broader benchmarking guidance for practitioners.

Extending the framework to fully accommodate complex survey designs with stratification, clustering, and sampling weights remains an important methodological objective. Such developments would enable rigorous population-level inference from multi-stage probability samples while preserving the computational advantages demonstrated in this study.

Concluding remarks

This study demonstrates that the divide-and-recombine framework provides a practical, statistically rigorous, and computationally efficient approach for fitting logistic regression models to large-scale health surveillance data. Our comprehensive validation—spanning theoretical foundations, extensive Monte Carlo simulations, and real-world application to over 2.4 million BRFSS observations—confirms that D&R methods achieve statistical properties equivalent to conventional maximum likelihood estimation while dramatically reducing computational demands. The 2.1–2.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} speedup and 78–89% memory reduction make previously intractable analyses feasible on standard computing infrastructure, democratizing access to advanced statistical methods for public health agencies and research institutions with limited computational resources.

Beyond methodological validation, our analysis provides updated epidemiological evidence on diabetes risk factors in a contemporary U.S. population. The strong associations identified for age, obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, socioeconomic position, and self-rated health reinforce the importance of comprehensive prevention strategies addressing both individual behaviors and structural determinants of health. The counterintuitive inverse associations observed for several clinical comorbidities serve as an important reminder that cross-sectional associations in prevalent cases cannot support causal inference, and that future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to properly establish temporal relationships.

As health surveillance systems continue to expand in scope and scale, computational methods that maintain statistical rigor while accommodating massive datasets will become increasingly essential. The D&R framework, with its solid theoretical foundation and demonstrated practical effectiveness, represents an important tool in the arsenal of methods for extracting actionable insights from big data in public health. We hope this comprehensive validation study will encourage broader adoption of D&R approaches and stimulate further methodological development to address emerging challenges in the era of big data epidemiology.
