Associations between teacher-student relationships and EFL achievement: a chain mediating model of EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement
Authors: Yajun Wu, Yiyan Li, Xia Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuided by Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, particularly the process-person-context-time (PPCT) model, this study examined whether teacher-student relationships (TSR), conceptualized as a classroom-context factor, were associated with English-as-a-foreign-language (EFL) achievement through EFL-learning grit as a person-level motivational resource and behavioral engagement as a proximal learning process. Evidence for this integrated mechanism remains limited in Chinese secondary EFL settings.MethodsParticipants were 718 Grade 7–8 EFL learners in Chinese secondary schools. Using domain-specific measures and standardized municipal English test scores, we tested a serial mediation model with confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) while controlling for age, gender, and socio-economic status (SES).ResultsTSR was positively associated with EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement. EFL-learning grit was positively associated with behavioral engagement, and behavioral engagement was positively associated with EFL achievement. Indirect effects supported mediation via behavioral engagement and through the serial pathway (TSR→ grit→ behavioral engagement→ EFL achievement), whereas the grit-only indirect pathway was not significant.ConclusionThis study contributes to EFL and educational psychology research by integrating contextual, personal, and process factors within a PPCT-informed model, by using domain-specific constructs and an objective achievement indicator, and by showing that TSR is associated with achievement primarily through engagement-related processes rather than through a direct pathway. The findings extend evidence from Chinese secondary EFL classrooms and suggest that relationship-centered teaching practices that strengthen perseverance and sustained participation may support achievement, although causal inferences remain limited.

Introduction

Teacher-student relationships, along with parent-child relationships and peer relationships, constitute the three most important interpersonal relationships for students [1]. Secondary school students in puberty are in a stage of rapid physical, psychological, and cognitive changes filled with contradictions. Secondary school teachers serve as the ideal support to fill the gap caused by reduced family dependence and insufficient self-independence in students. Within the Chinese educational framework, secondary schools commonly implement a fixed-class structure and a homeroom teacher system, where homeroom and subject teachers accompany students throughout their entire secondary school stage [2–4]. This setup enhances the frequency of teacher-student interactions and the influence of such relationships in students’ daily academic and personal lives. Hence, the significant impact of TSR on students’ academic achievement and well-being has been extensively discussed, encompassing dimensions such as learning motivation [5], academic engagement [6], self-efficacy [7], grit [8, 9], academic performance [10], and subjective well-being [11].

Meta-analytic and second-order reviews consistently suggest that positive TSR are associated with better academic outcomes, but the magnitude of this association varies substantially across studies and is often attenuated when more proximal learning process (e.g., engagement) are considered [12–14]. In EFL classrooms, where progress depends on sustained effort over time and active participation in classroom tasks, clarifying the mechanisms linking TSR to achievement is particularly important for theory building and for designing targeted interventions. One important construct in this regard is grit, which generally refers to perseverance and sustained commitment toward long-term goals [15]. In the context of EFL learning, grit may be especially relevant because learners often face delayed progress, repeated setbacks, limited authentic language exposure, and considerable examination pressure [16–18]. Existing studies suggest that grit is positively associated with language learning outcomes, yet its role is not always direct and may depend on other motivational and behavioral processes [19–22].

Another key construct is engagement, which is commonly viewed as a multidimensional construct including behavioral, emotional, cognitive, and agentic dimensions [23–25]. Among these, behavioral engagement refers to learners’ effort, attention, persistence, and active participation in learning activities [26, 27]. This dimension is particularly important in EFL classrooms because language development depends heavily on whether students actually participate in tasks, attend to instruction, persist in practice, and remain involved in classroom interaction. Prior research has shown that supportive TSR are associated with stronger engagement, and that engagement is positively related to academic achievement [6, 28–30]. Moreover, grit may help explain why some learners remain behaviorally engaged even when language learning is difficult. However, previous work in EFL education has often examined TSR, grit, or engagement in isolation, or has relied on non-domain-specific measures, learning the combined roles of these constructs insufficiently understood [28, 31–33].

To address these gaps, based on ecological systems theory, this study examines whether TSR with English teachers relates to EFL achievement indirectly through EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement in EFL learning. Using a large sample of secondary-school EFL learners and standardized municipal English test scores, the present study (1) integrates contextual (TSR), personal (grit), and process (behavioral engagement) factors in a single model, (2) distinguishes multiple direct and indirect pathways, including a serial mediation pathway, and (3) offers practice- and policy-relevant implications for supporting engagement and achievement in secondary EFL classrooms.

Literature review

The Chinese secondary-school EFL context

Chinese secondary-school EFL learning unfolds within a distinctive instructional ecology that may heighten the importance of relational, motivational and behavioral factors for academic achievement. English is a compulsory subject in China and is closely tied to routine school examinations as well as broader high-stakes assessment regimes [34]. Consequently, instruction in many secondary schools continues to prioritize vocabulary development, grammatical accuracy, reading comprehension and written practice [35, 36], even as curriculum reforms increasingly promote communicative approaches [37]. At the same time, opportunities for sustained and authentic English use beyond the classroom remain limited for many learners [38], rendering the classroom the principal site of meaningful language input, practice, feedback and encouragement. In addition, Chinese secondary schools typically operate with stable class groupings and frequent teacher–student contact through fixed-class and homeroom arrangements [2, 39]. In this context, teacher–student relationships may be especially consequential because they shape not only students’ affective experience of learning English, but also their perseverance in a demanding subject and their day-to-day participation in classroom activities. Taken together, these contextual features underscore the importance of examining how classroom relationships may be associated with EFL achievement through learner grit and behavioral engagement.

Theoretical foundation

Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, particularly the PPCT model, provides the organizing framework for the present study [40, 41]. The PPCT model proposes that developmental outcomes emerge through recurrent proximal processes, which are shaped jointly by person characteristics, contextual conditions, and time [42]. Applied to the present study, TSR with English teachers are conceptualized as a microsystem factor because they reflect learners’ day-to-day relational experiences in the immediate classroom environment. EFL-learning grit is treated as a person-level motivational resource that may help learners sustain effort and perseverance when language learning becomes difficult. Behavioral engagement is conceptualized as the proximal process because it captures students’ recurring effort, attention, participation, and persistence in English-learning activities. EFL achievement is the developmental outcome resulting from these ongoing interactions among context, person, and process [43].

Although the time component is not directly modeled in the present cross-sectional study, the PPCT framework still offers a strong rationale for examining whether supportive classroom relationships are associated with achievement indirectly through learner motivation and engagement. Specifically, the framework suggests that contextual support may nurture person-level resources, and these resources may be translated into achievement mainly through students’ repeated classroom behaviors [19, 20]. Accordingly, the present study proposes that TSR may strengthen EFL-learning grit [44], grit may facilitate behavioral engagement, and behavioral engagement may be the most immediate pathway to higher EFL achievement [45].

Teacher-student relationships

Teacher-student relationships refer to the quality of interpersonal interactions between teachers and students [46], encompassing students’ perceptions of support, respect, and positive affiliation during classroom interactions [47]. From an ecological perspective, TSR constitute a critical microsystem feature that can influence students’ motivation, learning behaviors, and achievement-related outcomes [48]. Both teachers and students are motivated to foster positive and high-quality TSR [49], as these relationships are among the most significant interpersonal dynamics within the school context [50], impacting both parties. For example, in a study involving Chinese junior high school teachers, TSR were found to have a direct positive effect on innovative teaching practices, as well as an indirect positive effect through the mediating role of teacher self-efficacy [51]. From the students’ perspective, TSR also positively influence key indicators of academic performance and well-being, with studies showing positive correlations between TSR and students’ learning engagement, grit, well-being, and academic achievement [6, 8, 52].

Although numerous studies have reported positive associations between TSR and academic achievement, the direct association is often reduced when more proximal mechanisms are taken into account [53–55]. This suggests that the influence of TSR may operate less through a simple direct pathway and more through learner-level psychological and behavioral processes. In EFL classroom, where progress depends on sustained effort and active classroom participation [16–18], it is especially important to clarify which personal resource and which proximal learning process may transmit the benefits of supportive TSR. However, prior EFL research has rarely examined these mechanisms in an integrated model, particularly among Chinese secondary-school learners using objective English achievement data. The present study therefore next considers EFL-learning grit as a plausible person-level mechanism through which TSR may be linked to EFL achievement [56]. Viewed through the PPCT framework, if TSR represents the contextual condition, the next question is which person-level motivational resource may help translate such contextual support into sustained classroom participation. One strong candidate is EFL-learning grit.

EFL-learning grit

Grit was originally conceptualized as perseverance and passion for long-term goals [15]. However, second language acquisition research has increasingly argued that grit should not be treaded only as a domain-general disposition when examining language learning [57]. Because second/foreign language development is typically gradual, effortful, and setback-prone, L2 learning calls for sustained investment in a particular domain rather than a fully general trait [58, 59]. Accordingly, the present study focuses on EFL-learning grit, defined as learners’ perseverance and sustained commitment directed specifically toward learning English [15]. It is particularly relevant in EFL settings because learners often face delayed progress, repeated setbacks, limited authentic language exposure, and high examination pressure [16–18]. Prior studies suggest that grit is positively associated with language learning outcomes [19, 20, 60]; however, meta-analytic and review evidence also indicates that the association between grit and achievement is often modest and context-dependent [21, 22]. This suggests that grit may contribute to achievement not necessarily through a strong direct pathway, but often through more immediate learning processes.

In addition, previous studies have frequently overlooked the domain-specific nature of grit by relying on general measures [61], which may not adequately capture perseverance in English learning. Existing research has also tended to examine either the antecedents of grit or its outcomes in isolation [19, 20, 62], with relatively few studies positioning grit simultaneously as a product of classroom support and as a predictor of subsequent learning behavior and achievement. therefore, the present study conceptualizes EFL-learning grit as a person-level motivational resource and examines whether it mediates the association between TSR and both behavioral engagement and EFL achievement. Yet a person-level motivational resource alone does not fully explain how achievement is produced in everyday classroom life. From a PPCT perspective, person characteristics are expected to influence outcomes largely through learners’ ongoing proximal processes. In the present study, that proximal process is behavioral engagement.

Behavioral engagement

Behavioral engagement is commonly defined in general educational research as effort, attention, persistence, and participation in learning activities [26]. In SLA, however, engagement has been conceptualized in more domain-sensitive ways [24]. Recent work on language engagement emphasizes that engagement in L2 learning is a distinct and conceptually heterogeneous area of research, and task-based L2 studies have shown that behavioral engagement may be reflected in time on task, turns taken, words produced, and active participation in communicative tasks, while related cognitive involvement may include negotiation of meaning/form and self-repair [63–65]. In the present study, behavioral engagement is conceptualized at the classroom level as students’ effort, attention, and participation in English class, while also acknowledging that engagement in L2 learning can take richer interactional forms through communicative language use [24]. As a cornerstone of meaningful learning [24], high behavioral engagement is associated with increased classroom involvement, positive learning attitudes, and enhanced motivation. It is one of four dimensions of academic engagement—alongside cognitive, emotional, and agentic engagement [25]— and is considered the most fundamental, with other dimensions indirectly influencing outcomes through it [66, 67]. As a tangible indicator of motivation, behavioral engagement is empirically linked to academic achievement and has been the extensively studied in relation to environmental factors (e.g., TSR), individual factors (e.g., grit, emotions), and behavioral factors (e.g., learning strategies) [29, 30, 68–70].

Consistent with social cognitive and ecological perspectives, behavioral engagement is shaped jointly by environmental factors (i.e., TSR) and individual factors (i.e., grit) [71, 72]. However, prior research has often examined the determinants of behavioral engagement from a unidimensional perspective [73, 74], with few studies integrating multiple dimensions. This study addresses this gap by investigating the combined effects of environmental factors (i.e., TSR) and individual factors (i.e., EFL-learning grit) on behavioral engagement. Additionally, this study considers the domain-specific nature of behavioral engagement, providing EFL educators with valuable insights into intervention strategies for enhancing behavioral engagement.

Potential chain mediating effects of grit and behavioral engagement

Taken together, the reviewed literature suggests a coherent PPCT-informed pathway. Supportive TSR may provide the contextual conditions that nurture learners’ EFL-learning grit; stronger grit may help learners remain more behaviorally engaged in English learning; and greater behavioral engagement may in turn support higher EFL achievement [9, 31, 75, 76]. In other words, the effect of classroom context on achievement may operate through a sequence in which contextual support strengthens person-level motivational resources, and these resources are translated into achievement through proximal learning processes [43].

Despite this theoretical rationale, few studies have tested these relationships simultaneously in one integrated model, especially among Chinese secondary-school EFL learners, using domain-specific measures and standardized English test scores. Accordingly, the present study examines both the individual mediating roles of EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement and their sequential mediating role in the association between TSR and EFL achievement.

The current study

Anchored in EST, this study seeks to examine the direct and indirect effects of TSR on EFL achievement, with EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement as mediating factors. Within the EST framework, the proximal process—a key mechanism of development—could be reinforced when motivational resources are bolstered by a supportive environment, thereby fostering positive developmental outcomes. In this context, TSR is conceptualized as a critical motivational context that cultivates EFL learners’ grit through mechanisms such as recognition of their voices, emotional care, encouragement, and autonomy support. Enhanced EFL-learning grit equips learners with the motivational resources necessary to transform external classroom environmental advantages into a proximal developmental process. This proximal process is expected to ultimately enhance EFL learners’ academic achievement. Up to now, empirical studies investigating the motivational mechanisms linking environmental factors to developmental outcomes remain limited, particularly in the domain of EFL education. By incorporating TSR, EFL-learning grit, behavioral engagement, and EFL achievement into a cohesive EST-based model (see Fig. 1), this study contributes novel theoretical insights and practical implications for improving EFL achievement. Methods

Participants and procedures

This study involved 718 EFL learners from 17 intact classes across three secondary schools in Foshan City, Guangdong Province, China. The sample comprised 423 Grade 7 students (58.90%) and 295 Grade 8 students (41.10%), aged 12 to 15 years (M = 12.83, SD = 0.73), including 342 girls (47.60%) and 376 boys (52.40%). To enhance sample heterogeneity, the three participating schools were purposively selected from different official accreditation tiers in the local secondary-school system. One school from each tier agreed to participate and was anonymized as School A, School B, and School C. Within each school, approximately half of the Grade 7 and Grade 8 classes were recruited through convenience sampling, resulting in 5 classes from School A (n = 230, 32.03%), 6 classes from School B (n = 267, 37.19%), and 6 classes from School C (n = 221, 30.78%). Although this sampling strategy does not support claims of national representativeness, it was designed to capture variation across school tiers within the Foshan context and thus provides a reasonably diverse sample of local secondary-school EFL learners. Finally, the questionnaire survey was administered in pencil-and-paper format during regular English class sessions, with each session lasting approximately 20 min.

Ethical considerations were rigorously addressed prior to data collection. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangzhou University (Reference No.: [2023]133). In addition, written informed consent was obtained from the participants, and verbal informed consent was secured from their parents or legal guardians. The survey was administered in Chinese to ensure comprehension among participants. Following Brislin’s translation-backtranslation procedure [77], the original English items were translated into Chinese and independently back-translated into English by bilingual members of the research team. Discrepancies between the source and back-translated versions were discussed and resolved through consensus to improve semantic and conceptual equivalence before the questionnaire was administered.

Measurement tools

All questionnaire-based measures in the present study were treated as unidimensional constructs. In addition to prior evidence from earlier studies, their factorial validity in the present sample was examined through CFA before testing the structural model. Also, because the present study focuses on EFL learning, both grit and behavioral engagement measures were operationalized as domain-referenced adaptations of source instruments originally developed in general educational settings. This approach was intended to preserve the core meaning of the constructs while specifying the target learning domain.

Teacher-student relationships scale

Participants’ perception of their relationships with their English teachers were assessed using the four-item TSR scale [78]. This instrument was treated as a unidimensional measure of positive TSR. Responses were evaluated using a six-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). An example item from this scale is: “I get al.ong well with my English teacher.” Scores were calculated as the mean of the responses, with higher values reflecting a higher quality of relationships between English teachers and EFL learners. Previous studies have supported the scale’s construct validity and internal consistency [29, 79]. In the present study, this scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86), and its factorial validity was further examined in the CFA reported in "Measurement model" section.

EFL-learning grit scale

To evaluate participants’ levels of grit in learning English, four domain-specific items adapted from the grit scale were employed [80]. In this study, this instrument was modeled as a unidimensional measure of EFL-learning grit. Following a domain-referenced measurement approach, the item wording was modified to refer specifically to EFL learning (e.g., “I am good at dealing with the setbacks encountered in English learning”). This adaptation was considered theoretically appropriate because prior research suggests that grit is at least partly domain specific, and language-specific operationalizations of grit have shown satisfactory psychometric properties and stronger associations with L2 motivation and achievement than domain-general grit [59, 60]. Higher scores on this scale indicate greater levels of grit in learning English. Prior research has substantiated both the construct validity and internal consistency of this scale [8, 81], providing a solid foundation for its application in the present study. In the present study, this scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.87), and its dimensional structure was further examined through CFA (see "Measurement model" section).

Behavioral engagement scale

Participants’ behavioral engagement in learning English was measured by the four items adapted from the student engagement scale [25]. This scale was treated as a unidimensional measure of behavioral engagement in the EFL context. The items were contextualized to English classroom activities (e.g., “I listen carefully in English class”). Higher scores indicated greater behavioral engagement. The reliability and validity of this scale have been validated in existing research [82, 83]. This scale demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.91), and its factorial validity was further examined in the CFA reported in "Measurement model" section.

EFL achievement

Participants’ English scores from the most recent English test were used to evaluate their EFL achievement. The English examination paper was uniformly developed by the Municipal Bureau of Education in accordance with the teaching syllabus and textbooks, ensuring its face validity [82, 84]. The test was designed to assess multiple competencies of EFL learners through the following components: listening and speaking tasks to measure listening and speaking skills; vocabulary and grammar items to evaluate vocabulary mastery and grammatical proficiency; reading comprehension tasks to assess reading ability; sentence translation tasks to measure translation competence; and English writing assignments to evaluate writing proficiency.

Covariates

Variance in teacher-student relationships, grit, academic engagement, and academic achievement is observed across dimensions such as gender, age, and SES. First, in a study among Dutch secondary school students, male students experienced more teacher-student conflicts and relatively fewer favorable TSR compared with female counterparts [84, 85]. Moreover, the association between TSR and academic engagement also exhibited gender differences [86]. Therefore, gender was controlled as a covariate when examining the relationships among TSR, grit, behavioral engagement, and EFL achievement. Second, age-stratified group comparisons demonstrated that individuals aged under 21 years old exhibit significantly grittier than those aged over 21 years old [87]. Furthermore, age moderates the association between TSR and academic achievement [88]. These suggest that age should also be controlled. Third, SES could also significantly influence interpersonal relationships (e.g., TSR) and academic achievement as well as the linkage between TSR and academic achievement [89–91], suggesting controlling SES as covariate. In sum, gender, age, and SES were controlled as covariates to account for their potential effects on the study variables as well as the relationships among the study variables.

Data analyses

Item-level missingness was minimal (0.2%∼1.1%) but was addressed prior to analysis. Little’s MCAR test [92] rejected the assumption that data were missing completely at random, χ2(132) = 182.980, p < .001. Accordingly, we retained all cases and handled missing data using full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) with robust standard errors in Mplus 8.3 [93]. FIML provides efficient and typically unbiased parameter estimates when missingness is ignorable [94].

Data analyses proceeded in four steps: (1) assessment of common method bias to minimize concerns about systematic self-report error; (2) descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations to summarize the data and examine bivariate associations; (3) confirmatory factor analysis to evaluate the measurement model and estimate latent correlations; and (4) structural equation modeling to test the hypothesized relations among TSR, grit, behavioral engagement, and EFL achievement (Fig. 1). Model fit was evaluated using established cutoffs: CFA and TLI ≥ 0.90, and RMSEA and SRMR ≤ 0.08 [95–97].

Results

Common method bias

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to evaluate the potential issue of common method bias [98]. A single-factor CFA was performed, incorporating all items from the study variables. The results indicated a poor model fit, with χ2(65) = 1053.932, p < .001, CFI = 0.852, TLI = 0.823, RMSEA = 0.146, SRMR = 0.066. These findings suggest that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern in the dataset.

Preliminary analyses

Table 1 reports descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations for all study variables. Univariate normality was evaluated using Finney and DiStefano’s criteria (|skewness| < 2, |kurtosis| < 7) [99]. All variables met these thresholds, supporting the use of maximum likelihood estimation. Internal consistency was satisfactory: Cronbach’s alpha exceeded 0.70 for the focal constructs (TSR, EFL grit, and behavioral engagement) [100], and reliability across all scales was high (α = .86∼.91). Correlations among the study variables were significant (p < .01). In addition, gender, age, and SES were significantly associated with at least one study variable and were therefore included as covariates in subsequent analyses.

Measurement model

A three-factor CFA was conducted to test the measurement model, including teacher-student relationship (four indicators), EFL grit (four indicators) and behavioral engagement (four indicators). The model demonstrated good model fit to the data: χ2(51) = 213.114, p < .001, CFI = 0.973, TLI = 0.965, RMSEA = 0.067, 90% C. I. [0.057, 0.076], SRMR = 0.028. All standardized factor loadings were substantial (TSR: .713∼.828; grit: .766∼.833; behavioral engagement: .770.∼876), exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50 [101]. These results support the intended unidimensional structure of each scale and provide evidence for the construct validity of the measurement model in the present sample. Because the correlations among TSR, grit, and behavioral engagement were moderately high, we conducted additional checks of construct distinctiveness and multicollinearity. The hypothesized three-factor model fit the data better than an alternative model combining grit and behavioral engagement into a single factor (two-factor model: χ²(53) = 416.724, CFI = 0.939, TLI = 0.925, RMSEA = 0.098, SRMR = 0.037), supporting the discriminant validity of the constructs. In addition, collinearity diagnostics indicated that VIF values ranged from 1.588 to 2.638, suggesting that multicollinearity was not severe.

Structural model

Given the satisfactory fit of the measurement model, SEM analysis was conducted to test the proposed model in Fig. 1. Results show that the model fitted the data well with χ2(96) = 320.262, p < .001, CFI = 0.964, TLI = 0.956, RMSEA = 0.057, 90% C. I. [0.049, 0.064], SRMR = 0.053. As shown in Fig. 2, TSR exhibited a positive association with EFL grit (β = 0.701, SE = 0.032, p < .001) and behavioral engagement (β = 0.288, SE = 0.033, p < .001). EFL-learning grit was positively associated with behavioral engagement (β = 0.738, SE = 0.029, p < .001) but was not directly related to EFL achievement (β = − 0.047, SE = 0.059, p = .424). Behavioral engagement, in turn, positively predicted EFL achievement (β = 0.246, SE = 0.055, p < .001). The model explained 46.5% of the variance in EFL-learning grit, 81.8% of the variance in behavioral engagement, and 19.4% of the variance in EFL achievement. The direct path from TSR to EFL achievement was not significant (β = 0.018, SE = 0.050, p = .723). Taken together with the significant indirect effects via behavioral engagement and via the serial pathway through grit and behavioral engagement, this pattern indicates indirect-only mediation. More specifically, TSR was associated with EFL achievement through pathways involving behavioral engagement, whereas the grit-only indirect pathway was not supported.

Discussion

Grounded in an ecological perspective on classroom processes [48], the present study examined how environmental (e.g., TSR), individual (e.g., grit), and behavioral (e.g., behavioral engagement) factors jointly relate to EFL achievement among EFL learners shaped by Confucian heritage culture. From a PPCT perspective, these findings are best interpreted not as discrete associations among variables, but as a dynamic ecology of interdependent influences. Within this ecology, TSR constitutes a salient microsystem feature of students’ everyday English-learning environment; EFL-learning grit reflects a person-level motivational resource; behavioral engagement operates as a proximal process through which learners translate effort into routine classroom action; and EFL achievement represents the developmental outcome. Viewed in this light, the classroom context appears to be linked to achievement less through a direct pathway than through its association with students’ personal and behavioral functioning. Supportive relationships with English teachers may therefore matter not merely in themselves, but because they cultivate perseverance-related resources that are subsequently expressed as sustained participation, attention and effort in learning. In line with the four research questions, we tested whether TSR, grit, and behavioral engagement were positively associated with EFL achievement (RQ1), and whether TSR was linked to EFL achievement indirectly through grit (RQ2), through behavioral engagement (RQ3), and through the serial pathway of grit and behavioral engagement (RQ4). Overall, the findings support a process-oriented account in which relational experiences in the classroom are associated with EFL achievement largely thorough students’ motivational-behavioral functioning, highlighting the importance of considering achievement as the produce of interconnected contextual, psychological, and behavioral mechanisms rather than a single determinant.

RQ1: associations among TSR, grit, behavioral engagement, and EFL achievement

Consistent with RQ1, EFL-learning grit, and behavioral engagement were each positively associated with EFL achievement. This pattern underscores that EFL learning outcomes are shaped by factors operating at multiple levels—relationships in the classroom, learner’s perseverance-related dispositions, and their enacted participation in learning activities. While many prior studies have investigated correlates of achievement from a relatively singular vantage point (e.g., focusing primarily on contextual supports or on individual attributes) [10, 30, 60, 102], the present findings highlight the value of an integrated approach in which context (e.g., TSR), personal resources (e.g., grit), and behavior (e.g., behavioral engagement) are considered together. This aligns with Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory [48], which emphasizes that development and learning emerge from dynamic transactions between individuals and their immediate environments. In practical terms, this suggests that interventions to enhance EFL achievement may be most impactful when they simultaneously address relational conditions, learner dispositions that sustain long-term effort, and students’ day-to-day learning behaviors.

RQ2: the non-significant mediating role of EFL-learning grit

Regarding RQ2, the indirect pathway from TSR to EFL achievement through grit along was not significant. Although TSR was positively associated with grit [9, 56], grit did not independently transmit the effect of TSR to EFL achievement in the model. This finding diverges from some prior research in which grit has been identified as a mediator between supportive settings and academic outcomes [31]. From the dimension of grit theory, this pattern suggests that grit may not function as a “stand-alone” conduit from relational support to academic achievement; rather, grit’s influence may be more consequential when it is expressed through proximal learning behaviors (e.g., sustained participation, persistence in academic tasks). This result could also be explained from the perspective of CHC settings. In CHC, the longstanding norms of “respecting teachers and valuing the way of Dao (the way of learning/knowledge)” may encourage learners to accept teacher guidance, develop emotional reliance on teachers, and show behavioral compliance with classroom expectations [103, 104]. Such norms can strengthen the immediate link between TSR and achievement-relevant behaviors and outcomes, potentially leaving less unique variance for grit to explain as an intervening mechanism. Moreover, compared with Western cultural emphases on individual self-actualization, CHC contexts often prioritize collective and duty-based goals over individual will [105]. Within this context, perseverance may be experienced less as a distinctive personal disposition and more as a normative expectation, which could attenuate grit’s unique mediating role between TSR and academic achievement.

RQ3: behavioral engagement as mediator between TSR and EFL achievement

In support of RQ3, behavioral engagement significantly mediated the association between TSR and EFL achievement. This finding is consistent with prior evidence showing that TSR supports academic achievement partly by fostering students’ participation, effort, and persistence in learning activities [75, 106, 107]. Conceptually, behavioral engagement represents a proximal “behavioral translation” mechanism: supportive relationships are not only emotionally beneficial but also shape how consistently students attend, participate, complete tasks, and persist when learning becomes challenging. A likely explanation is that high-quality TSR typically involves timely affective and academic support, constructive feedback, adaptive instruction, and personalized encouragement—features that are closely linked to higher levels of behavioral engagement [108, 109]. In CHC, diligence and effort-making are particularly emphasized as learner virtues [110], engagement-related behaviors may be especially diagnostic of learning quality and, consequently, strongly predictive of academic achievement [111, 112]. Thus, these findings highlight behavioral engagement as a central pathway through which TSR is associated with EFL achievement in CHC context.

RQ4: serial mediation via EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement

Finally, consistent with RQ4, TSR was indirectly associated with EFL achievement through the serial pathway of EFL-learning grit and behavioral engagement. This result provides an important refinement to the mixed pattern observed for RQ2 and RQ3: grit did not mediate TSR-EFL achievement on its own, but it did contribute to a meaningful process chain in which TSR was linked to stronger grit, which in turn was linked to higher behavioral engagement, and ultimately to better EFL achievement. This serial pathway aligns well with EST [48]. From the standpoint of EST, TSR can be understood as a proximal classroom condition that shapes students’ internal resources and everyday behaviors that directly support learning. From the perspective of grit theory, perseverance and sustained effort toward long-term goals are expected to facilitate academic achievement primarily by helping learners remain engaged, persist through setbacks, and maintain effortful practice over time. The non-significant grit-only pathway, together with the significant serial pathway through grit and behavioral engagement, suggests that person characteristics do not automatically translate into developmental outcomes. Rather, their influence appears to become developmentally consequential when they are enacted in proximal processes. In the present study, grit seemed to function less as a stand-alone bridge between TSR and EFL achievement and more as a resource that increased students’ likelihood of engaging behaviorally in EFL learning. This interpretation helps explain why the association between TSR and achievement became visible most clearly through engagement-related pathways.

Although not central to the primary hypotheses, the significant associations of gender and socioeconomic status (SES) with English as a foreign language (EFL) achievement merit attention. Female students outperformed male students, and students from higher-SES backgrounds attained higher levels of achievement than their lower-SES peers. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as gender and SES were included as covariates rather than as focal explanatory variables. Even so, they indicate that EFL achievement is shaped not only by relational and motivational processes within the classroom, but also by broader demographic and resource-related conditions. From a practical perspective, the results underscore the importance of targeted support for students from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds, while also drawing attention to possible gender-related differences in learning practices and classroom participation.

PPCT also foregrounds the temporal dimension, which is essential to interpreting the present findings. The processes implied by the model are unlikely to emerge from isolated classroom events. Rather, supportive interactions with English teachers may gradually shape students’ grit and behavioral engagement through repeated exchanges within lessons and across everyday learning episodes (micro-time), as well as over longer instructional intervals such as weeks or semesters (meso-time). Across broader developmental timescales, the cumulative effects of these recurring processes may become manifest in differences in EFL achievement. This interpretation may be especially pertinent in the Chinese secondary-school context, where fixed-class structures and sustained contact with teachers can intensify the continuity of teacher–student interaction [113]. At the same time, reciprocal dynamics are also theoretically plausible: students who are more engaged or more successful may evoke warmer and more supportive responses from teachers [114], thereby further strengthening TSR over time. Because the present study relied on cross-sectional data, however, these temporal and reciprocal dynamics should be regarded as theoretically grounded interpretations rather than empirically tested conclusions.

Implications

This study offers both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, by testing the TSR–grit–behavioral engagement–achievement pathway in Chinese secondary EFL classrooms, the study extends ecological systems theory in a context characterized by sustained teacher-student contact and strong cultural emphasis on respect for teachers and effortful learning. The findings suggest that, in this setting, teacher-student relationships are associated with achievement mainly through engagement-related processes rather than through a direct pathway. This contributes a more context-sensitive understanding of how contextual support, personal resources, and proximal learning behaviors jointly relate to EFL achievement.

In practice, the findings suggest that Chinese secondary EFL classrooms may benefit from relationship-centered instruction embedded in everyday teaching routines. Given the fixed-class and homeroom-based structure common in Chinese schools [2, 39], English teachers are well positioned to build stable TSR through regular check-ins, respectful communication, timely academic feedback, and consistent follow-up on students’ participation and homework [115, 116]. In schools operating with a homeroom teacher system, English teachers may also coordinate with homeroom teachers to reinforce participation routines and support students after learning setbacks.

The findings also indicate that simply encouraging students to be more perseverant may be insufficient. Because behavioral engagement emerged as the most proximal pathway to achievement, teachers should help students translate perseverance into concrete learning behaviors, such as listening attentively in class, contributing to pair or group tasks, completing assignments consistently, revising after test errors, and maintaining regular English practice after setbacks. In exam-oriented EFL settings [117], structured participation routines, brief post-test reflection tasks, and teacher-monitored review practices may be especially useful for converting motivational resources into sustained engagement [118].

Finally, these findings may also have relevance for other Confucian heritage EFL contexts in which teacher authority, relational harmony, and effort-related norms are similarly emphasized [119, 120]. However, such transferability should be interpreted cautiously, because differences in curriculum, assessment systems, and school organization may influence how teacher-student relationships, grit, and engagement operate across contexts.

Limitations and directions for future research

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that point to directions for future research. First, the cross-sectional approach limits causal inference among the study variables. Longitudinal designs and experimental or intervention studies are recommended to examine temporal ordering and causal mechanisms more rigorously. Such designs could provide stronger evidence for the proposed theoretical pathways and clarify whether improvements in TSR precede increases in grit and engagement, or whether reciprocal relations also occur. Second, all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce common-source bias [121], despite attempts to reduce common method variance. Future research is suggested to diversify data sources by combining questionnaires with classroom observations, learning analytics, and in-depth interviews, and by collecting complementary reports from teachers, parents, and peers. Third, although gender, age, and SES were controlled, other potentially influential variables, including prior academic achievement and achievement goals, may also affect the focal constructs and their interconnections. Incorporating these variables would support a more comprehensive account of EFL achievement. In addition, future studies may benefit from examining multiple dimensions of engagement (behavioral, emotional, and cognitive) and considering the nested nature of educational data to better capture contextual influences. Moreover, future research could extend the present classroom-level measure of behavioral engagement by examining more fine-grained forms of L2 engagement, such as oral participation, language production, and negotiation of meaning during communicative tasks.

Conclusion

The present study proposed and empirically supported a process model linking TSR, grit, behavioral engagement, and EFL achievement among Chinese learners influenced by Confucian heritage culture. The results indicated positive associations among TSR, grit, behavioral engagement, and EFL achievement, underscoring that EFL success reflects the interplay of environmental, individual, and behavioral factors. Importantly, behavioral engagement emerged as a key mediating mechanism connecting TSR to EFL achievement, and grit and behavioral engagement together formed a significant serial indirect pathway from TSR to achievement. These findings suggest a layered mechanism in which contextual supports shape learner psychological resources and, most proximally, learners’ learning behaviors, which ultimately contribute to achievement. Accordingly, school administrators, EFL educators, and learners should work collaboratively to cultivate positive TSR and to strengthen students’ perseverance-related resources and active behavioral participation in EFL learning, thereby facilitating improved EFL achievement.
