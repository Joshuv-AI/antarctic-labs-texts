Differences in rumen microbiome protein metabolism are associated with individual variation in rumen bloating on high-concentrate diets
Authors: Jinju Mao, Chunzheng Zhang, Lizhi Wang, Zhisheng Wang, Bai Xue, Quanhui Peng, Rui Hu, Jianxin Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study primarily investigated the differences in rumen microbial composition and functional profiles among goats with varying degrees of rumen bloat under a high-concentrate diets (HCDs) feeding condition. Forty Nubian goats fed a HCDs were selected as experimental animals and subjected to rumen bloating (RB) scoring. From these, six goats with high bloat scores (HB group) and six goats without obvious bloat (NB group) were selected. Rumen content samples were collected via oral stomach tubes. Rumen fluid foaming characteristics and pH values were measured, and metatranscriptome sequencing was conducted to analyze microbial functions.ResultsThe results demonstrated that compared to the NB group, the HB group exhibited significantly higher foaming capacity, foam stability, and viscosity of ruminal fluid, along with a significantly lower pH value (P < 0.05). Additionally, α-diversity indices (Chao1, ACE, and observed_species) were significantly elevated in the HB group (P < 0.05). Annotation of rumen microbial species showed that the relative abundance of Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Dialister, and Dialister sp. CAG:486 was significantly higher in the HB group, whereas Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Spirochaetes, Prevotella, Bacteroides, Treponema, Lactobacillus, Leptotrichia hofstadii and Prevotella oralis were significantly reduced (P < 0.05). Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed significant enrichment in several biological processes. In the Gene Ontology (GO) database, DEGs were predominantly associated with cellular protein modification, protein maturation, and ribosome biogenesis. According to KEGG pathway analysis, DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to endocytosis, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum.ConclusionIn summary, under uniform feeding conditions, individual variations in goat rumen microbiota functions are associated with differences in microbial community structure and gene expression patterns. Alterations in the protein metabolism function of the rumen microbiome are associated with the presence of foaming proteins, which in turn are linked to individual differences in rumen foam production capacity. Ultimately, these associations may result in differing susceptibility and severity of RB among animals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s42523-026-00605-1.Keywords: Rumen bloating, Rumen microbiome, High-concentrate diets, Protein, Rumen foam

Introduction

In ruminant farming, increasing the proportion of concentrate in the diet to enhance growth and fattening performance is a widely adopted strategy [1, 2]. During the finishing phase of meat production, dietary concentrate levels often exceed 80%. However, prolonged feeding with high-concentrate diets (HCDs) can lead to ruminal dysfunction, with rumen bloating (RB) being one of the most prevalent metabolic disorders associated with this feeding regimen [3–5]. Early studies attributed RB primarily to the high content of rapidly fermentable carbohydrates (such as starch) in HCDs [6].These substrates undergo rapid fermentation in the rumen, generating gas at a rate that exceeds the animal’s capacity for expulsion, thereby leading to gas accumulation and bloat formation. More recent evidence, however, increasingly indicates that HCDs consumption promotes the formation of persistent foam within the rumen [7, 8]. This foam acts as a physical barrier, entrapping gas bubbles and impeding their coalescence and subsequent release. When such foam occupies the ruminal lumen and esophageal opening, it prevents bubble aggregation, resulting in a sharp increase in intraruminal pressure that obstructs eructation and ultimately causes rumen foamy bloating [4, 9].

Notably, even when animals are fed the same diets, only a subset develops RB, suggesting significant inter-individual variability in susceptibility to RB [10, 11]. The underlying mechanisms for this variation remain unclear. Previous research has demonstrated that, despite similarities in breed, age, sex, diet, and management conditions, ruminants exhibit substantial individual differences in rumen microbial composition [12]. Given that microbial composition dictates functional potential, we hypothesize that variations in rumen microbial function may contribute to differential susceptibility to RB. Currently, metatranscriptomic sequencing represents the most reliable method for analyzing rumen microbial functions. While metagenomics can effectively reveal the genetic potential and functional gene repertoires of microbial communities, it does not directly measure gene expression and therefore cannot distinguish between the functional potential encoded in the genome and the actual transcriptional activity occurring at a given moment. In contrast, metatranscriptomics captures actively transcribed genes based on the presence of mRNA, offering a critical advantage for studying acute metabolic disorders such as RB, in which immediate functional expression is likely the driving force. This technique thereby distinguishes between active and dormant microbial populations and provides insights into which populations are transcriptionally active. It has been extensively applied in studies of rumen microbiology [13, 14]. Building upon these findings, the present study employed metatranscriptomic analysis to investigate variations in the composition and functional profiles of rumen microbiota among goats with differing susceptibilities to RB under HCDs feeding. The objective was to elucidate the microbial mechanisms contributing to RB development and to provide scientific references for optimizing HCDs use in livestock production while mitigating the risk of bloat.

Materials and methods

The animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Policy and Welfare Committee of the Agricultural Research Organization of Sichuan Province (Approval No. 20190193), China and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Ethical Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University.

Animals and sampling

This experiment was performed in the Animal Nutrition Research Institute of Sichuan Agricultural University, China. Forty Nubian goats of similar age, similar body weight (30.1 ± 0.8 kg on average), consistent genetic background, and identical feeding management were used as experimental animals. They were housed in separate pens with free access to water. Prior to the beginning of the trial, animals were fed oat straw at ad libitum intake with no concentrate supplementation. At the start of the present study, goats were switched to a HCDs over 14 days, with successive increments of concentrate up to 80%. During this transition, feed intake was monitored and recorded to ensure full adaptation to the diet; if any animal failed to adapt completely, the pre-feeding period would have been extended accordingly. Goats were fed with this HCDs at 2% of BW twice daily at 8:00 and 17:00 and lasted for 30 days. During the final 8 days of the experiment, bloat scores (BS) were recorded daily for each animal. Three independent scorers evaluated the degree of rumen bloat using the 0–3 severity scale described by Min et al. [15], with scoring performed 2–3 h after the morning feeding. The scoring criteria were as follows: a score of 0 (BS0) indicated no obvious signs of bloat; a score of 1 (BS1) indicated mild left-sided bloat; a score of 2 (BS2) indicated marked left-sided bloat with the rumen distending dorsally and upward; and a score of 3 (BS3) indicated severe bloat, with distension extending above the animal’s back, visible from the right side, and the animal exhibiting an asymmetrical gait. The daily bloat score for each animal was calculated as the average of the three scorers’ ratings. The overall bloat score (BS) for each goat was then determined as the mean of the daily scores over the 8-day period. Based on these scores, most animals had BS values of 1 or 2. Six goats showed signs of severe bloat (BS3), and six goats showed no obvious bloat (BS0). Rumen fluid was subsequently collected after the morning feeding via oral stomach tube from the six goats with high BS (HB; BS = 3) and the six goats with no bloat (NB; BS = 0) for further analyses. Approximately 50 ml of these rumen contents were collected directly into sterile tubes and immediately stored at -80 °C for subsequent microbial subtranscriptomic analysis. In addition, the remaining rumen contents were filtered through four layers of gauze to obtain rumen fluid for further analysis. Immediately after collection, rumen fluid pH was measured using a portable pH meter (pH-100, Tianqi Information Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). A portion of rumen fluid was used to determine rumen fluid protein content, employing the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method. First, 1 ml of rumen fluid was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was extracted for protein content analysis. Specific procedures were conducted according to the instructions of the Protein Quantification (TP) Assay Kit (A045-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), with final readings taken at OD595nm using a multifunctional microplate reader (SpectraMax190). The samples were then transported to the laboratory in a warm water bath maintained at 30–35 °C for analyses of viscosity and foaming properties, including foaming capacity and foam stability.

Rumen fluid foam production performance

The foaming capacity and foam stability of rumen fluid were evaluated with reference to the modified Ross-Miles and Rudin methods [16, 17]. Prior to analysis, rumen fluid samples were incubated in a water bath at 39 °C for 30 min to ensure consistent conditions. The procedure was as follows: 30 mL of rumen fluid was placed into a 100 mL sealed separatory funnel, and CO₂ gas was introduced slowly via a catheter at a pressure of 0.1 MPascal (MPa) for 60 s. Under standardized ventilation conditions, the formation of foam was observed. The volume of foam generated was recorded at 30 s, 1 min, and 5 min after gas flow cessation. The average of these three measurements was taken as the foaming capacity, expressed in milliliters (mL). Foam stability was determined by measuring the time required for the entire foam column to collapse completely, expressed in minutes. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure data reliability. In addition, the viscosity of the rumen fluid samples was measured using a vibration viscometer (SV-10, A&D Company Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and expressed in millipascal-seconds (mPa·s).

Metatranscriptomic analysis of the rumen microbiome

RNA extraction and metatranscriptomic sequencing

The total RNA in the rumen content was extracted following the previous described procedure [18]. Subsequently, rigorous quality control of total RNA was performed, which included: (1) analysis of RNA integrity and detection of DNA contamination using agarose gel electrophoresis; (2) assessment of RNA purity with a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer; (3) precise quantification of RNA concentration using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer; and (4) accurate evaluation of RNA integrity via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.

Metatranscriptomic library preparation and sequencing were conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) following their standard pipeline for prokaryotic samples. In brief, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted from total RNA to enrich for messenger RNA (mRNA), because prokaryotic mRNA lacks poly(A) tails. The enriched mRNA was then fragmented into 250–300 bp segments. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, and second-strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Subsequent library construction involved end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, fragment size selection, and PCR amplification. Following construction, the library was preliminarily quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and diluted to 1.5 ng/µl. Insert size was analyzed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Upon meeting size requirements, the effective library concentration was accurately determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), ensuring it exceeded 2 nM to guarantee sequencing quality.

Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in 2 × 150 bp paired‑end mode. A summary of sequencing data quality is provided in Table S1. The average raw sequencing depth was 10–12 Gb per sample, and after filtering, clean bases ranged from 9.9 to 12 Gb per sample (Table S1). Raw sequence data have been deposited in the NCBI SRA database under BioProject accession number PRJNA1301529.

Pre-processing of sequencing results

The raw data were preprocessed using fastp (version 0.23.2) (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) to ensure the accuracy and reliability of subsequent analyses. This step involved removing adapter sequences, trimming low-quality bases (Phred score < 20), and filtering out reads shorter than 50 bp. Potential host (goat)-, human-, and dietary plant-derived reads were removed using Bowtie2 (version 2.4.4) (http://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/index.shtml) [19] with the goat reference genome (ARS1) as the host genome, resulting in clean data. Clean reads were assembled into transcripts using Trinity v2.15.1 with default parameters for paired - end reads (minimum k - mer coverage = 2, minimum contig length = 200 bp) [20]. Assembly statistics are summarised in Table S2. Briefly, a total of 1,852,174 transcripts were generated, with an N50 of 897 bp, mean length of 647 bp, and total nucleotides of 1,198,399,255 bp. After redundancy reduction using Corset (see below), 1,626,240 unigenes were obtained with an N50 of 833 bp, mean length of 616 bp, and total nucleotides of 1,001,795,626 bp.

Based on Trinity splicing, transcripts were clustered using Corset (version 1.06). Corset is a bioinformatics tool designed for clustering de novo assembled transcripts and summarizing read counts to clusters for differential expression analysis (https://github.com/Oshlack/Corset). Then, the H-Cluster algorithm separates differentially expressed transcripts from these clusters, creating new ones. This process aggregates redundant transcripts and enhances the detection rate of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [21].

Taxonomic annotation

Unigenes were aligned against the Micro_NR database using DIAMOND (blastp mode) with an e‑value threshold of 1e− 5, retaining up to 50 hits per query. The Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA) algorithm implemented in MEGAN was used to assign species - level taxonomy. Abundance and gene count tables were generated at each taxonomic level (kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species). Metastats was used to identify species differing between the HB and NB groups. P - values from hypothesis testing were adjusted using the Benjamini - Hochberg method.

Functional annotation

Unigenes were compared with functional databases (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO)) using DIAMOND software. For each sequence, the comparison result with the highest score was selected for subsequent analysis. The relative abundance of different functional tiers was counted from these results. From the functional annotation results and gene abundance tables, a table of the number of genes annotated to each functional category per sample was obtained. From the abundance table on each classification level, the number of annotated genes was counted, relative abundance profiles were displayed, and abundance clustering heatmap, PCA, and NMDS dimensionality reduction analyses were performed.

Differential gene enrichment analysis

For samples with biological replicates, differential expression analysis between conditions/groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.20.0). DESeq2 employs statistical procedures to determine differential expression in numerical gene expression data using models based on negative binomial distributions. The resulting p-values were adjusted to control the false discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were designated as differentially expressed.

GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed on the differential gene sets using GOseq and KOBAS software. These analyses are based on the hypergeometric distribution principle, where the test set consists of genes that are differentially expressed and annotated to the GO or KEGG databases, and the background set consists of all genes that are annotated to these databases.

Statistical analysis

All experiment data were collated and preliminary processed using Microsoft office 2019. The statistical analysis of the current study was mainly performed using SPSS 19.0 (SPSS Inc.,Chicago, IL, USA). The data were shown as Mean (n = 6) and SEM. The differences of rumen fluid bloat parameters were analyzed using two sample unpaired T-tests and the differences in microbial relative abundance and functional gene relative abundance between groups were analyzed using Mann–whitney u test. P < 0.01 indicates highly significant differences, 0.01 ≤ P < 0.05 indicates significant differences, and P ≥ 0.05 indicates insignificant differences.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that RB caused by feeding HCDs to ruminants is of the foamy type [22]. This type of bloat is caused by the formation of a large number of stable foam in the rumen. The rapidly fermentable carbohydrates in HCDs are degraded by rumen microorganisms, producing a large amount of gas [23, 24], and the release of gas is obstructed by the foam, ultimately leading to the accumulation of gas in the rumen and causing bloating. The results of this experiment showed that, compared to the NB group, the foam production characteristics of rumen fluid in the HB group was significantly enhanced (Table 3), further confirming that feeding HCDs leads to foam-type RB. Interestingly, it was found that the viscosity of rumen fluid in the HB group was higher (Table 3). This is not coincidental: Studies have clearly demonstrated that higher rumen fluid viscosity correlates with an increased risk of bloat [25]. This phenomenon may be attributed to higher concentrations of soluble non-starch polysaccharides (such as β-glucans) in HCDs, which dissolve and act like ‘glue’ to thicken rumen contents, increasing rumen content viscosity [26]. This elevated viscosity impedes gas release, thereby promoting foam accumulation and predisposing animals to bloat. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that beyond polysaccharides, protein content exhibits significant positive correlations with both foam production and foam stability, while also showing significant associations with liquid viscosity [9, 26].

It is worth noting that this study found that among 40 goats fed the same HCDs under identical feeding conditions, 6 did not experience bloating, and the degree of RB varied significantly among the 34 goats that did experience bloating. Most of them only experienced mild bloating, while only 6 experienced severe bloating (Table 2). This phenomenon is consistent with the findings of Pitta et al.: even when feed and management conditions are identical, there are significant individual differences in RB susceptibility among ruminants [10]. Currently, there is no clear mechanism to explain this individual variation. However, existing research suggests that the rumen microbial composition of different individual goats varies significantly, even when breed, age, sex and diet are consistent [12]. This suggests that rumen microbial structure may be a key factor in the differences in bloating susceptibility. To verify this, the study used metatranscriptome sequencing technology to compare the rumen microbiome of the NB group and the HB group. The results showed significant differences in α-diversity between the two groups, and the relative abundance of certain dominant bacterial groups, such as Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria, also exhibited significant differences (Tables 4 and 5). The microbial communities in the gastrointestinal tract of animals are shaped by both stochastic and deterministic processes [27, 28]. Microbial stochastic processes refer to the random community assembly of microorganisms during events such as birth, death, and speciation. This may lead to variations in microbial communities among different individuals even under identical conditions [27]. Additionally, the abundance of certain microorganisms in the rumen microbiome is influenced by host genetic factors, which also contributes to individual differences in rumen microbiome composition [29, 30]. Therefore, factors such as the uncertainty of rumen microbial stochastic processes and host genetics may account for the observed differences in rumen microbiota between the HB and NB groups. Furthermore, during animal growth and development, competition for resources and space among ruminal fungi and bacteria acts to amplify these inter-individual microbial variations [31]. For example, the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota phyla of fungi, as well as certain bacteria such as the Bacteroidetes phylum, exhibit comparable capabilities for carbohydrate degradation [32, 33]. When basal fungal abundance is high, their potentially greater substrate utilization efficiency may restrict resource availability for competing bacterial populations, thereby inhibiting bacterial growth. Spatially, fungal hyphae can form complex network structures on feed particle surfaces, occupying physical space and hindering bacterial attachment and colonisation of feed particles, thereby affecting their growth and reproduction [34]. The results of this study show that rumen pH in the HB group was significantly lower than in the NB group (Table 3). Some Ascomycota and Basidiomycota fungi may exhibit greater tolerance to lower pH values or better survival and reproduction in acidic environments [35]. Conversely, bacteria such as Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Spirochaetes are more sensitive to pH changes [36–38]. When pH decreases, their growth and metabolism are inhibited, and their relative abundance may further decrease.

Rumen microbes are the core ‘factory’ of the digestive system in ruminant animals [39, 40]. The results of gene functional annotation of rumen microbes in this experiment showed that the primary function of rumen microbes is the digestion and metabolism of nutrients. Among these, carbohydrate metabolism is the most active function of rumen microbes, as it accounts for the highest proportion of metabolic pathways. Differences in rumen microbial community structure drive corresponding functional variations [18]. When the rumen microbial functions of goats in the NB and HB groups were compared, it was found that over 250,000 genes showed differences in expression between the two groups. These DEGs were primarily enriched in pathways related to protein synthesis and degradation. The ribosome, the central cellular organelle responsible for protein synthesis, directly governs protein output through its synthetic capacity [41]. In the HB group, genes related to ribosome biogenesis were found to be upregulated, suggesting that the microorganisms in this group were better equipped for efficient protein production. At the same time, the HB group showed significantly enhanced functionality in post-translational protein modifications (such as phosphorylation and glycosylation), indicating greater changes in protein structure. In beer, the lipid transfer protein 1 (LTP1) acquires an amphiphilic structure through glycosylation modification [42, 43]. This structural change reduces its surface tension [44], thereby significantly enhancing beer foam stability. Drawing on this analogy, we hypothesize that similar protein modifications in rumen fluid might contribute to foam formation, but this remains to be directly tested in the rumen environment. Further analysis also revealed that the HB group exhibited gene upregulation in processes such as protein maturation, endoplasmic reticulum processing, and UMP. This indicates that theses microorganisms not only ‘produce more’ but also ‘modify more’. These findings are strikingly consistent with prior studies on beer, which established that proteins are key factors in promoting the formation of beer foam and maintaining its stability [45, 46]. Natural proteins such as LTP1 and the Z protein found in malt undergo processes including heating, enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial fermentation to form foam proteins that improve the foaming power and foam stability of liquids [47]. Furthermore, studies have also identified a significant association between foam formation in urine and the protein content of urine [48, 49]. Albumin, functioning as a surfactant, reduces urine surface tension when present at increasing concentrations. This reduction in surface tension facilitates foam formation in urine [50]. Helpfully, these studies from other systems provide a valuable reference for the increased foam stability and foaming capacity of rumen fluid in the HB group observed in the present study.

To explore potential microbial drivers of protein metabolism, we analyzed the relationship between major KEGG pathways enriched in DEGs (related to protein synthesis and degradation) and the relative abundance of the top 30 rumen microbial species. The results showed that the endocytosis pathway was significantly negatively correlated only with Prevotella sp. lc2012. This result suggests that this strain may indirectly affect the efficiency of intracellular protein transport in the rumen by regulating the nutrient uptake process mediated by microbial cell membrane receptors [51]. Previous studies have also observed that Prevotella spp. can form extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) biofilms on cell membranes. These biofilms physically impede the formation of clathrin-coated vesicles, thereby reducing the efficiency of clathrin-mediated endocytosis [52]. This is consistent with the findings of the present study. PPER is a critical process in intracellular protein synthesis, folding and modification [53, 54]. It is closely related to the proper folding and functional maintenance of proteins. This experiment revealed positive correlations between the pathway and the relative abundance of multiple rumen microbial species (e.g., Leptotrichia hofstadii, Bacteroides xylanisolvens, and Prevotella oralis) (Fig. 6). These bacterial genera generally have the capacity to produce butyrate, a ligand that activates the unfolded protein response in cells by promoting the expression of GRP78/BiP and alleviating endoplasmic reticulum stress [55]. Furthermore, extracellular polysaccharides from the Bacteroides genus may enhance the host’s capacity for protein folding via the calreticulin-calnexin cycle [56]. Taken together, we hypothesize that specific rumen microorganisms may regulate endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis through their metabolic products, thereby improving protein synthesis efficiency - a hypothesis that requires direct experimental validation. The UMP represents the predominant selective protein degradation mechanism in eukaryotic cells [57]. In this study, the UMP pathway exhibited a more complex bidirectional regulatory relationship with rumen microbiota. This pathway demonstrated positive correlations with microbes including Dialister sp. CAG:486, Firmicutes bacterium CAG:103, and Rozella allomycis, but negative correlations with Faecalibacterium sp. CAG:74 and Fibrobacter succinogenes. These results indicate a close association between the protein degradation system and microbial metabolic strategies. Microbes showing positive correlations may promote targeted degradation of specific proteins by secreting ubiquitin ligase analogs or regulating host ubiquitinating enzyme expression. Conversely, most negatively correlated microbes possess butyrogenic capacity and likely reduce excessive microbial protein degradation by inhibiting E3 ubiquitin ligase activity via short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate [55]. Furthermore, our data revealed significant antagonistic regulatory patterns exhibited by multiple microbes across both the PPAR and UMP pathways. However, these two processes are not completely opposed to each other, but rather represent two key, complementary, dynamically synergistic components of the regulation of intracellular protein homeostasis. The PPER pathway oversees folding and glycosylation of newly synthesized peptide chains, ensuring correct spatial structure - acting as the first line of protein ‘quality control’. The UMP pathway identifies and degrades misfolded, aged, or dysfunctional proteins, including those that have not been properly folded in the ER and have been transported to the cytoplasm - serving as a remedial mechanism to prevent abnormal protein accumulation. Based on our correlational data, we propose that certain rumen microorganisms may precisely regulate protein synthesis and degradation pathways through metabolic products or virulence factors, thereby influencing protein concentration in rumen fluid. Importantly, our study does indeed demonstrate that protein concentration in rumen fluid was significantly higher in the HB group than in the NB group (Table 3). We therefore hypothesize that this differential protein concentration among individual goats is a key factor driving variations in foaming capacity, ultimately contributing to inter - individual differences in rumen bloat severity. Nevertheless, direct causal evidence is lacking, and further experiments (e.g., protein supplementation or neutralization in vivo) are needed to confirm this mechanism.

This study yielded a number of interesting findings, but it also has limitations. In this experiment, only the protein concentration in rumen fluid was measured; the origin, structural characteristics, and mechanisms of generation of these proteins remain unclear. Moreover, the relevant hypotheses have not yet been validated. Future research should focus on elucidating the functions of key microbial species and determining whether they are directly involved in gas production. Additionally, causal relationships between these factors need to be verified. Addressing these questions will be a critical step toward understanding the mechanisms underlying individual differences in rumen bloat severity in goats.

Conclusion

In summary, differences existed in the structure and function of rumen microbes between goats with different degrees of RB when fed the same HCDs. The rumen microbiota DEGs are primarily enriched in pathways related to protein synthesis and degradation, including cellular protein modification process, protein maturation, ribosome biogenesis, endocytosis, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum. This indicates that, even under uniform feeding conditions, the structural and compositional differences in the rumen microbiota among individuals can influence protein synthesis and degradation functions, thereby affecting rumen foam stability and ultimately leading to variations in the susceptibility and severity of RB in the host (Fig. 7).
