Randomised Clinical Trial on the Effect of Intermittent Vibrational Force Application During Orthodontic Treatment With Aligners on Root Resorption
Authors: Leonardo Teti, Alicia Pérez‐Idarraga, Marta Salcedo‐Miranda, Alberto Cacho, Fara Yeste‐Ojeda, Conchita Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe aim of this RCT was to analyse the relationship between intermittent vibratory forces and external apical root resorption (EARR) in patients treated with clear aligners, building on prior research on vibrational effects on biomarkers.Materials and MethodsA parallel, three‐arm randomised clinical trial included adults to be treated with clear aligners, randomly assigned by a computerised randomisation list to: Group A (vibration from treatment onset), Group B (vibration after 6 weeks), or Group C (no vibration). While patients and orthodontists were aware of group assignments, evaluators remained blinded. Digital orthopantomographs (OPGs) taken at treatment initiation (T0) and completion (T4) were analysed using Linge & Linge, Fritz & Krieger, and Malmgren methods. Crevicular fluid levels of receptor activator of nuclear factor‐kappa B ligand (RANKL) and osteoprotegerin (OPG) were measured as bone remodelling markers at different time points (T0, T1, T2, T3, T4).ResultsFifteen patients were analysed in Groups A and C, and 14 in Group B. No significant differences in EARR were found between groups, except for a significant increase in Group B (T2–T0) using Fritz & Krieger correlated with OPG (p = 0.010). Mean EARR was 4.13% (Group A), 4.08% (Group B), and 2.52% (Group C) using Linge & Linge; and 3.26%, 2.82%, and 2.12%, respectively, using Fritz & Krieger. No adverse effects were observed.ConclusionThe effect of intermittent vibratory forces on EARR remains inconclusive, highlighting the need for further research to optimise vibration protocols and understand its impact.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05316636

Background

Orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) is induced by mechanical stimulation, triggering bone remodelling in the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, a process initiated by inflammation [1]. This inflammatory response involves electrochemical, vascular, and cellular phenomena caused by the forces applied during orthodontic movement. Inflammatory mediators and growth factors play a key role in cellular activation, influencing osteoclast and osteoblast activity during bone remodelling and regulating the rate of tooth movement.

With the growing number of adult patients seeking orthodontic treatment, the demand for aesthetic options, particularly clear aligners, has significantly increased. Over time, clear aligners have improved in material, gingival margin design, attachments, and auxiliary techniques, enhancing their effectiveness and expanding their indications. Furthermore, patients seek to shorten treatment durations, driven by the desire for reduced risks of gingivitis, cavities, and decalcifications, as well as less impact on facial aesthetics and quality of life. A faster treatment process could also potentially decrease the incidence of root resorption, a common side effect of orthodontic movement linked to inflammatory mediators [2, 3].

External apical root resorption (EARR) is the most common type of root resorption in orthodontic patients, characterised by irreversible loss of the apical root [4]. Orthodontically induced external apical root resorption (OIEARR) is a frequent complication in patients using orthodontic appliances, resulting from an inflammatory process that breaks down cementum, dentin, and periapical tissue [5]. The clinical manifestation and prevalence of OIEARR vary, with rates reaching up to 90%, as assessed through panoramic, CBCT, or periapical radiographs [4].

Various techniques have been explored to accelerate tooth movement, including chemical and physical techniques. Physical methods include the application of electromagnetic fields, iontophoresis devices, vibratory stimuli, alveolar decortications, force intensity variations, low‐intensity lasers, and alveolar osteogenic distraction [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Two Cochrane systematic reviews assessed surgical and non‐surgical methods for accelerating tooth movement, concluding that more research is needed and limited evidence exists on the effectiveness of vibratory devices [12, 13]. Among these, intermittent vibratory forces are particularly favoured by patients due to ease of use.

Different vibration devices have been developed to promote OTM. Studies such as those by Nishimura et al. demonstrated significant results in accelerating tooth movement via vibration, while others, including Woodhouse et al. and DiBiase et al. [8, 14, 15], found no significant acceleration in OTM. Some studies, like that of Mayama et al. [16], reported faster tooth movement without adverse effects, including root resorption, while Yildiz et al. [17] found no differences in root resorption between patients using vibration devices during canine retraction.

Given the growing demand for shorter treatment times, it is crucial to determine whether intermittent vibratory forces can effectively accelerate tooth movement in clear aligner therapy, while also assessing their impact on root resorption as a potential side effect, and understanding their underlying mechanism of action across different aligner change rates.

The purpose of this randomised clinical trial was to analyse the relationship between intermittent vibratory forces and root resorption found in patients treated with clear aligners, as well as to explore its correlation with inflammatory biomarkers of orthodontic tooth movement. The null hypothesis was that there is no relationship between the use of intermittent vibratory forces, EARR, and the levels of inflammatory biomarkers in the gingival crevicular fluid during OTM.

Material and Methods

Trial Design and Changes After Trial Commencement

This three‐arm parallel randomised clinical trial (1:1:1 allocation) was approved by the San Carlos hospital's ethics committee (ref: C.P.‐C.I: 15/313, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05316636) and adhered to the original protocol, following CONSORT reporting guidelines [18].

Participants, Eligibility Criteria and Setting

Patients meeting the eligibility criteria were invited to participate in this RCT and provided informed consent. All were treated with clear aligners (Invisalign System) at the Orthodontic Postgraduate Program at the Faculty of Dentistry, Complutense University of Madrid, Spain, or in a private clinic, under the care of the same orthodontist.

The study included adults between 21 and 50 years old undergoing non‐extraction aligner treatment for malocclusion with at least 14 pairs of aligners. Eligible patients had moderate crowding (< 5 mm), a moderate curve of Spee, and increased overbite, requiring incisor levelling through intrusion. Exclusion criteria included smoking, poor oral hygiene (plaque index > 3), periodontal disease (probing depth ≥ 4 mm), or any systemic or chronic conditions that could affect bone metabolism or inflammation, such as bisphosphonate use.

All patients included in the study were in the initial active phase of orthodontic treatment using clear aligners, before any refinement stage.

Interventions

All treatments were performed using Invisalign aligners. All participants were required to wear the aligners for at least 22 h per day. The orthodontist reviewed treatment simulations for all patients using ClinCheck (Align Technology), with an adjustment rate of 0.25 mm per aligner. This force system was consistent with the aligner protocol for bodily tooth movement and intrusion. Intermittent vibratory force was applied with the AcceleDent Aura device (OrthoAccel Technologies, Bellaire, TX) for 20 min per day at a frequency of 30 Hz and a force of 0.3 N.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the presence of root resorption in the upper and lower incisors, assessed in millimetres, as a percentage, or using a visual scale. Root resorption was evaluated through digital panoramic radiographs taken at the beginning (T0) and end (T4) of treatment. All radiographs were calibrated and analysed at the tooth level to assess changes in the crown‐to‐root ratio, using the Dolphin Orthodontic Imaging software (Chatsworth, CA, USA). Three methods were used to assess EARR (Figure 2): The Linge and Linge method (modified by Brezniak) measures root resorption by using the tooth's crown length as a correction factor to account for variations in radiographic exposure, assuming the crown length remains constant throughout treatment [19]. Secondary outcomes included variations in the concentrations of receptor activator of nuclear factor‐kappa B ligand (RANKL) and osteoprotegerin (OPG) in gingival crevicular fluid at different time points (T0, T1 [4 weeks], T2 [6 weeks], T3 [12 weeks], and T4 [18 weeks]). Samples were taken at all time points for groups B and C, while group A samples were collected at T1, T2, and T4. For each patient, crevicular fluid was sampled from the moving incisor at the pressure point identified in the ClinCheck simulation, where the tooth moved in a vestibular direction. Detailed methodology is provided in Pérez Idarraga et al. [22].

Additional secondary outcomes included: Silness and Löe plaque index, Modified gingival index, and Bleeding on probing scores (measured in the tooth under study and two adjacent teeth at each time point).

Clinical assessment of aligner fit was recorded at each visit as a dichotomous outcome for both upper and lower incisors. A gap between the aligner and the incisal edge was noted using magnification glasses, and standardised photographic records were analysed to confirm fit.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size calculation for this RCT was based on a broader study evaluating biochemical mediators related to tooth movement, which served as the primary variable. Following Kawasaki et al. [23], a mean between‐group difference of 0.41 mm in tooth movement over 168 h was considered clinically relevant. During this period, mean RANKL values were 70.075 pg/μL (SD: 27.6) with force application and 40.625 pg/μL (SD: 25.5) without it. Using these data, an effect size of 1.108 was established, and a sample size of 14 patients per group was determined to achieve 80% power at a 5% significance level. To account for a potential 10% loss to follow‐up, the sample size was increased to 15 patients per group.

Randomisation and Blinding

Patients were randomly assigned to study groups using a computerised randomisation list generated by GraphPad Software Inc. (USA). Allocation concealment was ensured through pre‐prepared opaque sealed envelopes containing treatment allocation cards, which were opened sequentially by a single researcher. While patients and orthodontists were not blinded to group assignment, evaluators remained blinded, as the groups were indistinguishable.

Statistical Methods

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess the intraexaminer reliability of the radiographical measurements. Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of the distributions of all quantitative variables. After confirming normal distribution, mean values and standard deviations for EARR were computed and compared using one‐way ANOVA. Frequency distributions for qualitative outcomes (gender and aligner fit) were reported and compared using Chi‐square tests. Biomarker data were analysed both within and between groups using repeated measures ANOVA, with time and group as factors, and adjustments were made using the Bonferroni correction. Correlations were calculated using Pearson's correlation coefficients. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY), with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

The reproducibility of the EARR assessments was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) across the three measurement methods. ICC values ranged from 0.89 to 0.96, indicating high reliability for all methods.

Participants

A total of 45 patients were initially enrolled and randomly assigned to one of the three treatment groups. One patient was lost to follow‐up, resulting in 44 patients completing the study. An intention‐to‐treat (ITT) analysis was conducted to include all initially randomised patients, ensuring that data from the patient lost to follow‐up were analysed according to their original group assignment (Figure 3).

Outcomes

EARR Comparisons

EARR was assessed using three different methods: Linge & Linge, Fritz & Krieger, and Malmgren. Comparisons among the three groups revealed no statistically significant differences in the amount of EARR, regardless of the method used (p‐values: Linge & Linge = 0.238, Fritz & Krieger = 0.231, Malmgren = 0.44) (Table 2). When analysing mean EARR percentages, Group A exhibited a mean resorption of 4.13% using the Linge & Linge method, 3.26% using the Fritz & Krieger method, and a mean Malmgren score of 1.8. Group B showed similar trends, with 4.08% (Linge & Linge), 2.82% (Fritz & Krieger), and a Malmgren score of 1.43. Group C, the non‐vibration control group, demonstrated the lowest EARR values (2.52%, 2.12%, and 1.27%, respectively). While EARR values were slightly higher in the vibration groups, the differences were not statistically significant, indicating no measurable effect of vibration on root resorption.

Variations in RANKL and OPG Concentrations in Gingival Crevicular Fluid

Biomarkers concentrations were assessed at different time points (T0, T1, T2, T3, and T4). Mixed‐model ANOVA revealed no significant differences in RANKL (Group A: p = 0.31, Group B: p = 0.8, Group C: p = 0.49) or OPG (Group A: p = 0.24, Group B: p = 0.58, Group C: p = 0.59) over time or in relation to the application of vibration or the frequency of aligner changes. However, a nonsignificant improvement in biomarker concentrations was observed when aligners were changed every 7 days with vibration applied. Additionally, no statistically significant differences were found between or within groups for the plaque index, gingival index, or bleeding on probing scores. These results are further detailed in Pérez Idarraga et al. [22].

Correlations of EARR With Biomarker Concentrations

Correlations between EARR and the inflammatory biomarkers RANKL and OPG at different time points were assessed (Table 3). In Group B, a significant negative correlation was found between EARR measured at T2 to T0 using the Fritz & Krieger method and OPG concentrations (R = −0.684; p = 0.01), with a trend toward significance when using the Linge & Linge method (R = −0.503; p = 0.08). No significant correlations were observed in Groups A and C. For the remaining comparisons, no significant associations were found between biomarker levels at different time points and the extent of root resorption across the groups.

Aligner Fit

Clinical assessment of aligner fit showed minimal cases of detectable misfit across all visits, with no significant differences among groups (p = 0.798). Neither the application of vibratory forces nor the aligner change protocol (7‐ or 14‐day intervals) had an impact on the clinical fit of the aligners throughout treatment.

Discussion

This study analysed EARR in patients treated with clear aligners, with and without vibratory forces, using three established radiographic assessment methods: Linge & Linge, Fritz & Krieger, and Malmgren. An ITT analysis was conducted to preserve the integrity of randomisation and minimise potential bias from patient dropout.

Methods for Assessing EARR

EARR was evaluated using digital panoramic radiographs, a widely used imaging modality in orthodontics due to its accessibility and lower radiation exposure. However, its accuracy is limited compared to cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) [24], which eliminates geometric distortions and anatomical superimpositions. While panoramic radiographs remain a standard diagnostic tool, their precision depends on image quality, magnification control, and standardisation techniques. Methods relying on proportional measurements, such as Linge & Linge and Fritz & Krieger, attempt to correct for radiographic distortions but may still be affected by image inconsistencies [5, 20]. The Malmgren scale, although useful for grading resorption severity, introduces a subjective component that can create variability among evaluators [5, 20, 24].

CBCT has been shown to be more reliable for assessing root resorption, as it compensates for positional changes during treatment and offers volumetric analysis [25]. However, its routine use is limited by higher radiation exposure and cost, making it ethically unsuitable for routine orthodontic monitoring, particularly in paediatric and adolescent patients [5, 26]. Current guidelines recommend its use only when clinical or two‐dimensional radiographic findings are inconclusive.

While panoramic radiographs offer a practical and ethically acceptable option for monitoring root resorption in clinical settings, they lack the detail and localised accuracy of periapical radiographs. Although periapical imaging was not included in this study due to field‐of‐view constraints and standardisation challenges, its inclusion in future research could improve the precision of EARR assessment and provide a valuable comparative perspective.

Recent computational and digital advancements have improved measurement reproducibility, even when using panoramic images. Software, such as Dolphin Imaging and ARIAS, has been validated for orthodontic research, enabling more accurate EARR assessments [27, 28]. Future studies should explore hybrid protocols integrating digitisation techniques, correction algorithms, and (AI)‐assisted image analysis to enhance diagnostic precision. The incorporation of biomarkers [29] and volumetric imaging [30] may further advance early detection and prevention strategies for EARR in orthodontics.

Effect of Vibration on EARR

Intermittent vibratory forces have been proposed as a non‐invasive technique to accelerate tooth movement and potentially reduce adverse effects such as EARR and periodontal complications [3, 16, 17]. However, the increase in osteoclastic activity during treatment may pose a potential risk, especially in patients with a genetic predisposition or specific anatomical characteristics that favour the development of EARR. Studies have shown conflicting results—some reporting no significant increase in EARR with vibration [17], while others suggest potential adverse effects when combined with high orthodontic forces [30]. DiBiase et al. [31] investigated the effect of AcceleDent on EARR using periapical radiographs and found no significant impact on maxillary central incisors. Their results are consistent with ours, though direct comparisons are limited due to differences in appliance type (fixed appliances) and imaging method. In contrast, El‐Bialy et al. [32] found that low‐intensity pulsed ultrasound (1.5 MHz) significantly reduced root resorption, but their methodology differed from ours in terms of force application and target teeth (canines only).

Studies using higher vibrational forces (50 Hz, 150 g), such as those by Yilmaz et al. [30], also reported no effect on EARR, consistent with our findings. Mayama et al. [16] and Abd Elmotaleb et al. [33] further support this, showing that supplemental vibration did not increase root resorption in patients treated with fixed appliances. However, these studies primarily examined patients treated with fixed appliances undergoing canine retraction, making direct comparisons with our aligner‐based study difficult.

While all previous research on vibration and EARR involved fixed appliances, our study is among the first to investigate its effects in clear aligner therapy. Our results indicate that vibration did not significantly impact EARR, regardless of the aligner change protocol (7 or 14‐day intervals). The absence of significant differences may be attributed to sample size limitations (n = 45), though this was based on prior power calculations.

Correlation Between EARR and Biomarkers

Since OIEARR is mediated by the RANKL/OPG axis, where RANKL promotes bone resorption and OPG acts as its inhibitor [34, 35], it has been hypothesised that vibration could modulate these biomarkers [8]. However, similar to Yilmaz et al. [30], our study found no significant differences in RANKL and OPG levels between patients with and without vibration. A key finding in this study was the significant negative correlation observed in Group B (aligner changes every 14 days initially, followed by vibration and 7‐day changes) between EARR at T2 to T0 (Fritz & Krieger, p = 0.010) and OPG levels, with a trend toward significance using Linge & Linge (p = 0.080). This suggests a possible interaction between aligner change frequency and vibration timing in influencing root resorption, warranting further investigation. The negative correlation between OPG and EARR may indicate that while OPG plays a protective role in bone resorption, its expression alone may not be sufficient to counteract orthodontic forces in all case [35]. However, this is an isolated finding among many correlations tested across groups, time points, and measurement methods. Therefore, it should be interpreted with caution, as it may represent a type I error rather than a true biological association. Inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL‐1β and TNF‐α, have also been associated with osteoclast activation and increased EARR [36, 37], with some studies reporting a correlation between elevated IL‐1β levels and greater EARR in orthodontic patients [38]. However, our findings did not show a uniform correlation between these biomarkers and EARR, which could be influenced by individual biological responses, genetic variability, applied force magnitude [37], and vibration frequency, amplitude, and duration [34]. These results reinforce that EARR is a multifactorial process, where biomarker regulation alone does not always translate into clinically observable differences. While the RANKL/OPG axis and inflammatory cytokines contribute to root resorption, their predictive value remains uncertain. Additionally, the absence of notable biomarker changes further suggests that vibration alone may not be sufficient to influence the biological mechanisms of root resorption and repair [2, 22]. Future studies should explore alternative vibrational parameters, patient‐specific factors, and combination therapies to better understand their impact on EARR and develop personalised prevention strategies.

Overall, vibration did not produce a clinically significant reduction in EARR. The lack of a clinically relevant reduction in EARR aligns with previous studies [5, 20, 31], supporting the idea that vibration, as currently applied, does not significantly affect resorption processes. This study provides new insights into the effects of intermittent vibratory forces on EARR in clear aligner therapy. While vibration did not significantly impact root resorption, a correlation between OPG levels and EARR in one group suggests complex biological interactions. These findings highlight the need for standardised vibration protocols and further research to optimise clinical applications. Understanding the biological and mechanical factors influencing root resorption could improve patient safety and treatment outcomes in orthodontics.

Limitations and Generalizability

Limitations of this study include a relatively small sample size, which may have contributed to the lack of statistically significant differences. Additionally, the sample size calculation was based on biomarker data (RANKL levels), not on the clinical outcome of EARR, which was the primary outcome of this study. As a result, the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller, yet clinically relevant differences in root resorption. Therefore, this limitation should be considered when interpreting the results, as a type II error cannot be ruled out.

Additionally, root length measurements were based on two‐dimensional radiographs, as ethical concerns prevented the use of cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) to minimise radiation exposure.

Although the overall study design combined two variables—vibration application and aligner change frequency—the structure of this three‐arm RCT allowed for their independent evaluation within specific time periods. Vibration was intentionally paired with a 7‐day aligner change protocol, as this combination is clinically recommended to enhance tooth movement and reduce overall treatment time. For example, the effect of vibration alone could be assessed by comparing group A and group C during weeks 0–6, while the effect of aligner change frequency without vibration could be evaluated by comparing groups B and C during the same period. However, we recognise that beyond these intervals, the combination of variables may introduce confounding and limit the interpretation of long‐term effects attributed to each factor independently. Future studies using a fully factorial design would be valuable to clarify the interaction and individual contributions of these variables.

Future studies should focus on standardising protocols, including vibration application, treatment duration, and vibration frequency, to enhance comparability and reproducibility.
