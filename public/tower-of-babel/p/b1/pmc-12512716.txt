Application of the flipped classroom combined with BOPPPS model in standardized residency training for gastrointestinal surgery‌
Authors: Bin Luo, Fangyao Zhou, Jiabin Zheng, Weixian Hu, Zejian Lv, Zifeng Yang, Xingyu Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies demonstrate that both the flipped classroom and the bridge-in, objective, pre-assessment, participatory learning, post-assessment, summary model (the BOPPPS model) work well in medical education. However, limited research has systematically integrated these two approaches into standardized residency training program. This study explores the effectiveness of this combined teaching model in the standardized residency training for gastrointestinal surgery.Methods68 trainees in the standardized residency training program at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, were split into two groups: a control group receiving traditional rotation-based training and an experimental group undergoing the flipped classroom approach combined with the BOPPPS model. Introduction

With the continuous advancement in medical education, standardized residency training, as a critical component of medical education, directly impacts the professional competence and practical skills of clinicians [1, 2]. Gastrointestinal surgery, a major subspecialty within the surgical field, is characterized by diverse disease profiles, complex operative procedures, high demands for clinical reasoning, and strong specialization [3]. Residents are required to acquire solid theoretical knowledge, proficient technical skills, and rapid clinical decision-making abilities within a limited timeframe.

However, traditional medical education, primarily relies on lecture-based teaching and experiential exposure without a standardized pedagogical structure. In this model, trainees often passively absorb information in didactic lectures and rely on inconsistent, opportunistic exposure to clinical cases during ward work or surgical observation. There is limited emphasis on structured clinical reasoning, active engagement, or formative feedback. Based on our department’s internal teaching reflections and informal feedback from previous trainee cohorts, several recurring challenges were identified: fragmented knowledge integration, low confidence in handling emergencies, and inconsistent development of procedural competencies. These issues reveal a critical gap between knowledge delivery and skill acquisition, highlighting the need for an educational model that is both structured and learner-centered to better support competency development in gastrointestinal surgery.

In this context, the ‌Flipped Classroom‌ and ‌BOPPPS instructional model‌ have emerged as focal points in medical education [4–8]. The Flipped Classroom reverses the traditional teaching process by delivering knowledge acquisition before class and reserving in-class time for interactive discussions, case analyses, and practical exercises. This approach enhances learner engagement and autonomy, making it suitable for theoretical and standardized knowledge domains.

The BOPPPS model, a student-centered instructional framework, consists of six structured phases: ‌bridge-in‌, ‌objective, ‌pre-assessment‌, ‌participatory learning‌, ‌post-assessment‌, and ‌summary‌. It aims to improve teaching efficacy through systematic design, particularly for skill-based clinical training requiring immediate feedback, thereby reducing instructional randomness.

This study explores the ‌combined application of the Flipped Classroom and BOPPPS model‌ in standardized residency training for gastrointestinal surgery.

Materials and methods

General information

A total of ‌68 trainees‌ undergoing standardized residency training in the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery at ‌Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital‌ were enrolled between ‌August 1, 2024, and March 1, 2025‌. Trainees admitted from ‌August 1 to November 1, 2024‌, were defined as control group and received ‌traditional rotation-based training‌. Trainees admitted from ‌December 1, 2024, to March 1, 2025‌, were defined as experimental group and underwent ‌Flipped Classroom combined with the BOPPPS model‌.

Traditional rotation-based training

Trainees in the control group underwent conventional rotation-based training, which reflected the standard teaching approach prior to the educational reform. Upon entry into the department, residents attended a half-day orientation training, which introducing departmental protocols, common gastrointestinal diseases, and key responsibilities during the rotation. Following orientation, trainees were assigned to clinical teams under the supervision of attending surgeons.

Throughout the rotation, teaching occurred primarily through daily ward rounds, bedside case discussions, attending operations, and didactic lectures. Weekly academic sessions covered topics such as gastrointestinal tumors, acute abdomen, and perioperative management. However, participation in these activities was largely passive, with minimal structured interaction or feedback. Learning opportunities were heavily dependent on the individual initiative of trainees and the discretion of supervisors.

At the end of the rotation, trainees were required to complete a theoretical examination, and a practical skills assessment supervised by clinical instructors. Additionally, instructors completed a 10-item evaluation questionnaire to comprehensively assess each trainee’s performance throughout the rotation, based on the institutional core competency framework (Supplementary questionnaire 1).

Preparation for the flipped classroom combined with BOPPPS model

To ensure consistent and effective implementation of the flipped classroom combined with the BOPPPS model, a structured instructor sensitization and training process was conducted prior to the teaching reform. A departmental workshop was held. During this session, the educational rationale, structure, and instructional strategies of the flipped classroom and BOPPPS model were introduced in detail. Instructors received training on designing teaching sessions following the BOPPPS framework, including formulating clear objectives, using formative assessments, and conducting interactive, learner-centered activities.

To support instructional consistency, standardized teaching materials—such as pre-class micro-lectures, surgical videos, reading assignments, and quiz templates—were prepared and distributed by the teaching secretary. Instructor were also involved in the curriculum design process, which promoted ownership and alignment between teaching goals and clinical competencies.

Flipped classroom combined with BOPPPS model

The experimental group adopted the flipped classroom combined with the BOPPPS model. Prior to entering the department, trainees would independently study the learning materials prepared by the teaching secretary to grasp relevant theories and become familiar with basic skills, such as wound dressing for contaminated wounds, sterile surgical draping, and laparoscopic operations. Trainees additionally reviewed procedure-specific surgical video compilations to internalize critical steps in gastrointestinal surgery procedures. Then trainees received a pre-rotation quiz to assess the self-directed learning outcome. These assessment outcomes were systematically disseminated to assigned clinical instructors. At the end of their rotation, the trainees would undergo skill assessments and theoretical exams, and the trainees would also complete a 10-question survey on the new teaching model (Supplementary questionnaire 2). The instructors would also conduct multi-dimensional evaluations of the trainees’ performance (Fig. 1).

Illustrative case: Gastrointestinal perforation for emergency management training

Prior to entering the department, trainees completed the Bridge-in, Objective, and Pre-assessment phases as part of the pre-class preparation. They were introduced to a brief clinical scene involving a patient with suspected perforation to stimulate interest and contextualize learning (Bridge-in). The Learning objectives emphasized emergency management, such as rapid assessment, identification of imaging features, determining surgical indications, and planning immediate management (Objective). Trainees then reviewed curated self-directed learning materials, including micro-lectures on emergency management protocols, procedural videos on laparotomy and laparoscopic repair, and clinical guidelines. After self-study, a pre-class quiz assessed their understanding of acute gastrointestinal emergencies and identified knowledge gaps. Importantly, the quiz results were shared with instructors, who used the data to optimize in-class teaching by focusing on areas where trainees demonstrated weaker performance (Pre-assessment).

The in-class phase involved cycles of structured Participatory Learning and Post-assessment. Trainees worked in small groups to analyze real CT scans of gastrointestinal perforation cases, discuss differential diagnoses, and formulate emergency surgical plans and postoperative treatment plans. Instructors provided immediate formative feedback, helping trainees refine their emergency decision-making and prioritize interventions based on clinical findings. The interactive case discussions allowed instructors to address specific challenges, such as how to quickly assess the severity of perforation and the need for urgent surgical intervention. Immediate feedback reinforced key clinical judgment skills and encouraged trainees to critically reflect on their decisions in real-time.

Finally, the Summary phase was assigned to the post-class period, where trainees reflected on their learning experience, reviewed feedback, and summarized key takeaways in a structured reflection form. Additionally, theoretical examination and practical skills assessment were conducted at the end of rotation. These assessments provided a summative evaluation of both knowledge acquisition and practical competency, reinforcing accountability and closing the instructional loop.

Theoretical examination and practical skills assessment

Theoretical assessment consisted of 50 questions aimed at evaluating trainees’ foundational knowledge in gastrointestinal surgery as well as their clinical reasoning abilities. The practical assessment covered three key skill domains: aseptic technique, basic laparoscopic operation setup and execution, and emergency decision-making and initial management of acute abdominal cases. All assessment components were developed in alignment with the core competencies and learning objectives.

Results

Demographic information of trainees

This study comprised 68 trainees divided into two cohorts: a control group (n = 35) and an experimental group (n = 33). The control group comprised 13 trainees from general surgical residency base (37.1%), 20 trainees from other surgical base (57.1%), and 2 from non-surgical base (5.7%), with 74.3% holding physician’s practice license. In comparison, the experimental group included 12 trainees from general surgical residency base (36.4%), 8 from other surgical base (24.2%), and 13 from non-surgical base (39.4%), with only 42.4% licensed to practice. This demographic breakdown—notably the experimental group’s higher proportion of non-surgical trainees (39.4% vs. 5.7%) and lower licensure rate (42.4% vs. 74.3%)—objectively highlights the greater training challenges faced by the experimental group (Table 1).

Comparison of trainees’ final assessment scores

The outcomes of both the theoretical and practical evaluations conducted at the end of the rotation are graded into five distinct levels: scores below 60 are classified as D, scores ranging from 60 to 69 as C, scores between 70 and 79 as B, scores from 80 to 89 as A, and scores of 90 or higher as A+.

In the control group, 5 trainees achieved an A + grade, 25 achieved an A grade, and 5 achieved a B grade in the theoretical assessment. In comparison, 16 individuals of the experimental group achieved an A + grade, 15 with an A grade, and 2 with a B grade. Regarding the practical assessment, the control group had 17 participants achieving an A + grade and 18 securing an A grade, whereas the experimental group saw 24 individuals attaining an A + grade and 9 earning an A grade. Overall, both the theoretical and practical assessment scores of the experimental group were superior to those of the control group (Table 2).

Clinical preceptor’ comprehensive evaluation of resident trainees

The instructors assessed trainees’ performance using a 10-item evaluation questionnaire, with each item scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Fail, 2 = Pass, 3 = Moderate, 4 = Good, 5 = Excellent). Compared to the control group, the experimental group demonstrated superior performance in several key areas: synthesizing clinical cases during morning handovers (p = 0.040), managing emergency and critical care situations while on-call (p = 0.038), and adhering to intraoperative aseptic principles during operations (p = 0.011) (Table 3).

Questionnaire on trainees’ perception of the new teaching model

Additionally, the trainees in the experimental group completed a 10-question survey on the new teaching model after their rotation. Responses were scored from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly Agree”). A remarkable 97.0% of the trainees believed that the new teaching model was scientific (90.9% Strongly Agree, 6.1% Agree, and 3.0% Neutral). Furthermore, all trainees agreed that the new teaching model was worthy of promotion (93.9% Strongly Agree, 6.1% Agree). The trainees felt that the new teaching mode could stimulate their interest in learning, enhance self-study abilities, refine clinical reasoning capacities and clinical competence. Moreover, they believed that the new teaching mode could improve their performance during surgery, facilitate better communication with their instructors, and enhance their overall rotation experience (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study discovered that integrating the flipped classroom approach with the BOPPPS model not only capitalizes on the strengths of the flipped classroom in delivering knowledge, thereby notably enhancing trainees’ proficiency in both theoretical understanding and practical application, but also guarantees the attainment of educational goals and the assessment of learning achievements through the systematic framework of the BOPPPS model. It exerts a significant impact on trainees’ clinical reasoning and collaborative skills.

The flipped classroom approach is currently extensively utilized in undergraduate teaching reform and has achieved remarkable teaching outcomes [9–11]. By reorganizing the teaching process in a “pre-class self-directed learning plus in-class interactive engagement” format, the flipped classroom seamlessly integrates with the residency training model. Firstly, prior to specializing, trainees already possess a solid foundation of comprehensive medical knowledge and fundamental skills, which makes it less daunting for them to engage in self-directed learning of specialized and advanced clinical content. Secondly, clinical practice involves in-depth interactions among trainees, instructors, and patients, thereby addressing the problem of “superficial classroom interactions” commonly observed in undergraduate classroom. Through repeated clinical practice, with instructors offering guidance, correcting errors, and summarizing throughout the process, teaching quality can be significantly enhanced. The flipped classroom has showcased its advantages in teaching across various domains. For instance, a case-based flipped classroom learning notably enhanced the practical skills and teaching satisfaction of ophthalmology interns [12]. Additionally, the integration of micro-lectures with the flipped classroom approach bolstered trainees’ diagnostic capabilities for complex cases in intensive care training [13]. However, the effectiveness of the flipped classroom is highly contingent upon instructional design. In the absence of systematic guidance, knowledge gaps may arise due to deficiencies in the design of self-study courses. Alternatively, inadequate pre-class preparation by trainees, coupled with ineffective classroom interactions and a lack of feedback mechanisms, can undermine teaching effectiveness. The BOPPPS model, which centers on “objective orientation” and “closed-loop feedback”, ensures the scientific rigor of instructional design and fosters engagement among both teachers and students through its six key components. By aligning with the training content outlined in the residency program syllabus, the pre-class self-study courses can be developed to encompass essential knowledge and skill points, with an emphasis on enhancing trainees’ clinical reasoning and practical capabilities, thus ensuring the scientific and comprehensive nature of the self-study materials. “Closed-loop feedback” encompasses entry quiz before department rotation, daily work feedback, and final assessment before rotation completion. Entry quiz can pinpoint trainees’ knowledge and skill deficiencies, enabling instructors to provide targeted remedial guidance once trainees commence their residency. Unlike undergraduate courses where each class lasts for 45 min, the teaching duration in residency training is significantly longer that spans several months, and instructors’ observations, interactions, and guidance during trainees’ clinical practice can all serve as daily work feedback, allowing instructors to adjust teaching progress and strategies in real time. Final assessment includes skill assessments, theoretical examinations, and surveys, which systematically evaluate teaching effectiveness and facilitate continuous improvement of teaching methods based on assessment outcomes and survey feedback.

Gastrointestinal surgical diseases cover a wide range of situations, including emergencies, tumors, and minimally invasive procedures, which requiring residents to swiftly integrate and adaptively apply multidisciplinary knowledge throughout their standardized training. Current research indicates that while teaching approaches centered around problem-based learning (PBL) or standardized patients can improve clinical skills [14, 15], some trainees still complain about the fragmentation of knowledge and a lack of systematic understanding. Our study demonstrated that the combination of the flipped classroom and BOPPPS, through structured instructional design, can bridge the gap between theory and clinical practice. This combined approach not only fosters trainees’ self-directed learning and critical thinking but also aligns with the high-level competency training goals of gastrointestinal surgery residency programs.

While a formal survey of instructor members’ subjective experiences was not conducted, instructors were actively engaged in the implementation of the new teaching model. Informal conversations during debriefing sessions indicated a generally positive attitude toward the structured and learner-centered format. Furthermore, the objective performance outcomes—particularly the higher scores in both theoretical and practical assessments, as well as better evaluations on clinical competencies—indirectly reflect the model’s acceptance and effectiveness from the faculty perspective.

This teaching methodology was introduced as a pilot study. Its implementation in the current cohort allowed us to evaluate feasibility, identify operational challenges, and observe initial outcomes in both theoretical knowledge and practical competencies. While preliminary results are encouraging, we acknowledge that further validation across multiple resident cohorts and over extended training cycles will be essential to confirm its generalizability and long-term impact. This phased approach ensures that the methodology can be refined based on iterative feedback before wider adoption within the residency training program.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is not a randomized controlled trial. Given the inherent equity in educational settings, it is challenging to randomly allocate the same batch of trainees into two distinct teaching models. Secondly, there is variability in the baseline characteristics of the trainees involved in this study. Specifically, the experimental group comprises a higher proportion of individuals from non-surgical training base and those who have yet to obtain their physician’s practice license. Despite these differences, the trainees in the experimental group achieved superior outcomes, thereby underscoring the benefits of the novel teaching model. Thirdly, the sample size of this study is relatively modest. In the teacher-completed questionnaires, certain trends favoring the experimental group were observed, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. Future research should aim to expand the sample size.

Conclusions

This study indicates that the combined application of the flipped classroom and the BOPPPS model can significantly improve the rotation experience of residents, enhance their performance, and boost their final grades. According to the survey questionnaire, the trainees in department of Gastrointestinal Surgery believe that this integrated teaching model is scientific, useful, and worthy of promotion.
