Unraveling the relationship between the vaginal microbiome and return to estrus in post-weaning sows
Authors: Min Liu, Mengqing Zhou, Jia Zhang, Congying Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReturn to estrus in post-weaning sows has a significant impact on reproduction performance. Changes in sex hormone levels influence the composition of the vaginal microbiota in sows. However, the relationship between return to estrus and the vaginal microbiota in post-weaning sows remains poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we characterized the vaginal microbiota of 74 Landrace × Yorkshire post-weaning sows including 40 normal-return and 34 non-return sows by sequencing the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene. The results revealed significant differences in both the composition and predicted functional capacities of the vaginal microbiota between normal-return and non-return sows. Non-return sows were characterized by reduced abundances of Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Lactobacillus, and Christensenellaceae R-7 group, and increased abundances of Facklamia and Weissella. Furthermore, the predicted functional pathways of Clostridium acetobutylicum acidogenic fermentation and pyruvate fermentation to butanoate were significantly enriched in normal-return sows. Significant differences were also observed in the co-occurrence networks of the vaginal microbiota between normal and non-return sows, revealing distinct microbial interaction patterns between normal return and non-return sows. A random forest analysis identified several vaginal bacterial taxa that showed high discriminatory capacity between normal and non-return sows (AUC = 0.907), including Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group and Christensenellaceae R-7 group that were strongly associated with return to estrus in post-weaning sows.ConclusionsWe observed significant differences in the composition and predicted functional capacities of the vaginal microbiota between normal-return and non-return sows, and identified several bacterial taxa associated with return to estrus of weaned sows. These findings enhance our understanding of the relationship between the vaginal microbiota and return to estrus in post-weaning sows and provide a foundation for future mechanistic and interventional studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05467-y.

Background

Return to estrus in post-weaning sows significantly influences reproduction performance and the economic efficiency of the pig industry, as it affects the number of weaned piglets per sow per year (PSY). Generally, most sows exhibit standing estrus within seven days after weaning [1]. However, in some cases, sows fail to return to estrus for more than 14 days. After weaning, the removal of the suckling stimulus relieves the lactational suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, leading to follicular development and a progressive increase in estrogen synthesis and release from the ovaries, which ultimately triggers the return to estrus [1–3]. The surge in estrogen levels not only induces estrous behavior, but also influences the vaginal epithelium by promoting the proliferation and maturation of epithelial cells, leading to increased thickness of the vaginal endomembrane and glycogen production [4, 5]. These changes in the vaginal environment support a healthy balance of the vaginal microbiota and contribute to reproduction health in sows.

Recently, the vaginal microbiota has been reported to be related to female health and reproduction [6–8]. In swine, accumulating evidences have indicated that the vaginal microbiota composition varies with reproductive and physiological status and plays a critical role in reproduction performance. Studies in Göttingen minipigs revealed that prepubertal animals harbor higher abundances of Lactobacillaceae compared to sexually mature non-pregnant individuals [9]. Furthermore, pregnant sows exhibited a lower diversity of the vaginal microbial compared to gilts [10]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the vaginal microbiota composition is associated with reproduction performance [11], fertility [12] and longevity [13] in sows. Moreover, alterations in the vaginal microbiota have been linked to reproduction disorders, including pelvic organ prolapse in late-gestation sows [14] and endometritis in post-weaning sows [15]. These findings collectively indicated that the vaginal microbiota in sows is responsive to reproduction hormones and physiological states, and plays a pivotal role in maintaining reproduction health. In our previous study, we demonstrated that the gut microbiota might influence return to estrus in post-weaning sows by regulating sex steroid hormone metabolism [16]. Given that hormonal fluctuations after weaning also shape the vaginal microbiota composition, and that the vaginal microbiota directly contacts with the reproduction tract, we extended this investigation to the vaginal microbiota in the same cohort. Previously, we also reported that the vaginal microbiota of sows may be related to return to estrus after weaning using V4 region 16 S rRNA gene sequencing in an independent cohort [17]. However, that study lacked validation and was limited by an OTU-based analysis with lower taxonomic resolution.

In this study, we investigated the association of the vaginal microbiota with return to estrus in 74 post-weaning sows having comprehensive reproduction performance records including weaning-to-estrus interval, parity, and litter size by using high-throughput sequencing of the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene. We identified vaginal bacterial taxa that were associated with return to estrus in post-weaning sows. Furthermore, we performed co-occurrence network analysis to reveal distinct microbial interaction patterns of the vaginal microbiota between the normal-return and non-return sow groups. Additionally, we developed a random forest classification model and identified multiple vaginal bacterial taxa that were associated with return to estrus and showed discriminatory capacity between normal and non-return sows. These findings enhance our understanding of the relationships between the vaginal microbiota and return to estrus in post-weaning sows, and the identified bacterial taxa would provide bases to further investigate the causality of the vaginal microbiota with sow estrus.

Materials and methods

Experimental sows and sample collection

The study workflow is shown in Fig. S1. A total of 74 Landrace × Yorkshire weaned sows were employed in this study. The parities of experimental sows ranged from parity 1 to parity 8, with the majority (60.8%) being parity 4–6 sows (parity 1: n = 4; parity 2: n = 8; parity 3: n = 3; parity 4: n = 13; parity 5: n = 15; parity 6: n = 17; parity 7: n = 8; parity 8: n = 6). All sows had normal reproduction history in their previous cycles, with a mean litter size at previous farrowing of 12.1 ± 4.3 piglets (total born). All experimental sows were housed and managed under similar conditions at a commercial pig farm located in Wuyi, Fujian Province, China. Sows were housed in individual farrowing crates during lactation and moved to group-housed gestation pens immediately after weaning for estrus detection. All sows were provided with commercial formulated diets containing 67% corn, 26% soybean meal, 1% soybean oil, and 6% lactating sow premix. Clean drinking water was supplied ad libitum via nipple drinkers. Moreover, all sows were healthy with no clinical symptoms of diseases based on daily observations and had not received any antibiotics or probiotics treatment for a minimum of two months prior to sample collection.

We collected a total of 74 vaginal samples on the day of weaning (28 days postpartum) using 15-cm disposable sterile swabs (Weier Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) to obtain samples from the posterior region of the vagina. Prior to sampling, the external vulva was cleaned with disinfectant. After collection, the swab head was cut with sterilized scissors and placed in a sterile 2-mL tube and put it in liquid nitrogen. Scissors were sterilized with 75% alcohol to avoid cross-contamination between samples. All the vaginal swab samples were promptly immersed in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction. Sow estrus behaviors were recorded daily since the weaning day, as described in detail in our previous study [17]. In brief, estrus was monitored twice daily (8:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m.) by observing vulvar swelling, redness, and standing reflex in response to back pressure. Sows that did not display estrus behaviors for more than 14 days from the weaning day were categorized as non-return sows. Of the 74 experimental sows, 40 belonged to the normal-return group, in which sows exhibited estrus behaviors within 4–6 days after weaning, while 34 sows were considered non-return sows, all of which failed to return to estrus for more than 14 days after weaning. The two groups showed no significant differences in parity or litter size at previous farrowing (Table 1).

Vagina microbial DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing

Microbial DNA from the vaginal swab samples was extracted using the FastDNA™ SPIN Kit for Soil (116560200, MP Biomedicals, Burlingame, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s procedures. The quality of the microbial DNA samples was assessed using 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis (JY600C, JUNYI, Beijing, China) and the DNA concentration and purity were quantified with a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). All DNA samples were stored at -80 °C until further use. ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (ABI, Los Angeles, CA, USA) was employed to amplify the V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene with the primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The conditions for PCR amplification were an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturing at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. The purified PCR products were constructed the libraries and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform with a paired-end strategy (SY-410-1003, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatic analysis of sequencing data

After removal of the barcodes, all sequence reads of tested samples were processed using the QIIME2 Core 2021.11 pipeline [18]. The demultiplexed paired-reads were merged via the command ‘qiime vsearch join-pairs’, and raw reads were filtered based on quality scores via the command ‘qiime quality-filter q-score’ with default parameters. The high-quality sequence reads were subsequently denoised using the Deblur algorithm [19] with parameters --p-trim-length 390, ultimately resulting in the acquisition of an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) feature table for subsequent analyses. ASVs corresponding to mitochondrial gene sequences were filtered out. Based on rarefaction curves (Fig. S2A), the samples with less than 4,000 reads were excluded. A pre-trained Naive Bayes taxonomy classifier for the V3-V4 region (silva-138-99-seqs-338-806-classifier.qza) was used for taxonomic assignment of ASVs via the command ‘qiime feature-classifier classify-sklearn’ with default confidence threshold. The abundance of each bacterial taxa was calculated by summing all reads of ASVs within that taxonomic unit. To generate a phylogenetic tree, ASV sequences were aligned with MAFFT [20] and FastTree [21] algorithms using the ‘qiime phylogeny align-to-tree-mafft-fasttree’ command. The tree was subsequently visualized using iTOL (v.6.8) [22].

Functional capacity prediction of the vaginal microbiomeFunction

Prediction of functional capacities of the vaginal microbiota with amplicon sequences was carried out using PICRUSt2 [23] (v.2.5.2). ASVs obtained from the Deblur algorithm were used for functional prediction by inputting sequences and abundance files in the BIOM format through picrust2_pipeline.py. Functional annotations were added through add_descriptions.py based on the MetaCyc Metabolic Pathway Database [24]. LEfSe [25] (v.1.1.2) was used to identify the differential MetaCyc pathways of the vaginal microbiota between the normal and non-return sow groups at the significance thresholds: P < 0.05 and LDA score > 2.0. LEfSe format conversion was performed using lefse_format_input.py, and significantly discriminative pathways were obtained by lefse_run.py.

Co-occurrence network of the vaginal microbiota

The correlations among 676 ASVs present in at least 20% of all samples derived from 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data were assessed based on the Sparse Correlations for Compositional data (SparCC) algorithm [26] implemented in FastSpar (v.1.0.0) [27]. A total of 1,000 bootstrap samples were generated via the fastspar_bootstrap command. The correlation matrices of the resampled data matrices were calculated with 50 iterations. P-values were then calculated from the 1,000 bootstrap correlations using 1,000 permutations with the fastspar_pvalues command. The co-occurrence networks of the vaginal microbiota for both groups were constructed and visualized using the ggraph package (v.2.2.1) in R. Potential ‘driver’ taxa responsible for the microbial network shifts between the normal-return and non-return groups were identified based on the Neighbor Shift (NESH) scoring described in the Netshift framework [28].

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of the α‑ and β‑diversity of the vaginal microbiota between normal-return and non‑return sows

Alpha-diversity indices including Observed species, Chao1, and Shannon were calculated and visualized using the MicrobiotaProcess [29] in R package (version 1.7.8). The mp_cal_alpha() and mp_plot_alpha() functions were utilized for the calculation and visualization, respectively. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the alpha-diversity of the vaginal microbial compositions between normal-return and non-return sows. The sequencing depth of vaginal samples was rarefied to 17,287 tags to avoid the effect of uneven sequencing depth. The q2-diversity plugin in QIIME2 was used to analyze the beta-diversity based on the Bray-Curtis distance. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the microbial dissimilarity to assess the differences in the microbiota compositions between normal-return and non-return sows. The mp_diff_analysis() function from the MicrobiotaProcess R package (version 1.7.8) was used to identify the ASVs and taxa exhibiting significantly different abundances between normal-return and non-return sows at the significance thresholds: FDR < 0.05 and LDA score > 2.0.

Correlation analysis between the vaginal microbiota and functional capacities

To assess the relationships between the changes in the vaginal microbiota and the shifts in predicted functional capacities, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between differential vaginal microbial genera, ASVs, and MetaCyc pathways. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The correlations were visualized with the ComplexHeatmap package in the R software (v.4.1.2). FDR < 0.05 was treated as the significance threshold.

Random forest analysis

To discriminate between normal-return and non-return sows, a random forest classification model was constructed based on the vaginal bacterial genera with k-fold cross-validation, where k represented the number of samples (leave-one-out cross-validation, LOOCV). Accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were derived from the confusion matrix of LOOCV predictions. The classification performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated using the pROC package [30] in R software (v.4.1.2).

Association analysis between vaginal genera and return to estrus

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the potential association between vaginal genera and return to estrus in post-weaning sows using R software (v.4.1.2). To ensure the stability of the statistical model and avoid complete separation issues, the analysis was restricted to 19 bacterial genera that were not only identified as differential bacterial taxa in the LEfSe analysis (LDA score > 2.0, FDR < 0.05), but also showed high feature importance in the random forest model. Both crude (non-adjustment) and adjusted models (adjusted for parity and litter size) were established and used for analysis.

Results

Vagina microbial composition of sows after weaning

16 S rRNA gene sequencing analysis of 74 vaginal swab samples elucidated the compositions of the vaginal microbiota in sows after weaning. A total of 6,018 ASVs were obtained using the deblur denoising pipeline. Rarefaction curves based on the alpha-diversity index indicated that the amount of sequence reads for each vaginal sample was sufficient for further analysis. After the taxonomic annotation of ASVs, we obtained a profile of taxonomic compositions of the vaginal microbiota. The vaginal microbiota in weaned sows displayed considerable diversity, spanning 32 bacterial phyla (Fig. 1). Among these, Bacillota (59.95%), Pseudomonadota (15.25%), Bacteroidota (13.58%), Actinomycetota (6.37%), and Campylobacterota (2.02%) were the predominant phyla in vaginal samples (Fig. S2B). Anaerococcus (7.1%), Streptococcus (6.49%), Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (6.48%), Escherichia-Shigella (4.86%), and Veillonella (4.49%) were the most abundant genera (Fig. S2C).

Changes in the vaginal microbiota composition between normal-return and non-return weaned sows

We observed significant differences in the α-diversity of the vaginal microbiota between normal-return and non-return sows. The Chao1, observed taxa, and Shannon indexes in normal-return sows were significantly higher than those in non-return sows (Fig. 2A). Moreover, PCoA based on the Bray-Curtis distance showed significant differences in the β-diversity of the vaginal microbial community between the two sow groups (Fig. 2B and C). These results indicated the significant changes in the vaginal microbiota composition between the two sow groups. We then compared the relative abundances of 20 bacterial taxa with the highest abundances at the genus level between the two sow groups. Among these dominant genera, Streptococcus (8.92% vs. 4.42%, non-return vs. normal), Bacteroides (5.5% vs. 2.35%), Porphyromonas (4.52% vs. 2.06%), and Peptostreptococcus (2.74% vs. 1.44%) showed higher abundances in non-return sows, whereas Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (5.19% vs. 7.56%), Turicibacter (2.77% vs. 4.97%), Terrisporobacter (2.38% vs. 4.95%), and Lactobacillus (1.15% vs. 3.54%) had higher abundances in normal-return sows. Notably, Lactobacillus was markedly reduced in the vagina of non-return sows (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D, Table S1).

Identification of vagina microbial taxa associated with return to estrus

We further identified bacterial taxa showing differential abundances between normal-return and non-return sows. At the genus level, a total of 28 genera showed significantly differential abundances. Among them, two genera were significantly enriched in non-return sows, including Facklamiaji and Weissella, while the other 26 genera were significantly enriched in normal-return sows (LDA > 3, FDR < 0.05), including Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Terrisporobacter, Turicibacter, Lactobacillus, Christensenellaceae R-7 group, Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group and Muribaculaceae (Fig. 3A and Table S1). At the ASV level, 17 ASVs were observed to have differential abundance between the two sow groups. Among them, 13 ASVs showed significantly higher abundance in normal sows, including ASV0015:g_Terrisporobacter, ASV0008:g_Clostridium sensu stricto 1, ASV0007: g_Turicibacter, ASV0014:s_Lactobacillus johnsonii, ASV0010:g_Romboutsia, ASV0105:g_dgA-11 gut group belonging to Rikenellaceae, ASV0302:g_Lachnospiraceae XPB1014 group, ASV0030:s_Limosilactobacillus reuteri and ASV0068:o_Oscillospirales, whereas ASV0392:g_Streptococcus, ASV0342:s_Facklamia tabacinasalis, ASV0054:g_Aerococcus, and ASV0336:g_Staphylococcus were significantly enriched in non-return sows (LDA > 3, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3B and Table S2).

Shifts in the co-occurrence network of the vaginal microbiota between normal sows and non-return sows

The sow vaginal microbiota is a complex ecosystem characterized by intricate interactions. Co-occurrence networks were constructed for the normal-return and non-return groups separately to compare the microbial interaction patterns between the two sow groups. We found that the co-occurrence network of the vaginal microbiota in the normal-return group was quite different from that in the non-return group (Fig. 4A and B). More nodes were detected in the network of the vaginal microbiota of the non-return group (Fig. S3A and Table S3). Moreover, the network density and clustering coefficient in the non-return group were higher than those in the normal group, indicating a greater number of independently associated microbial sub-units in the non-return network. Furthermore, there was a lower average path length in the network of non-return sows, suggesting greater network compactness (Fig. S3A and Table S3). These topological differences suggested distinct co-occurrence patterns of the microbial community between normal and non-return sows. Given that Lactobacillus was significantly more abundant in the vagina of normal-return sows and has been widely recognized as a beneficial commensal genus in the vaginal microbiota, we further examined the co-occurrence patterns of ASVs annotated to Lactobacillus within each network. In the normal-return sow group, ASVs annotated to Lactobacillus exhibited intra-genus co-occurrence interactions (Fig. S3B), suggesting close ecological associations among members in this genus. In contrast, no intra-genus interactions were detected among Lactobacillus ASVs in non-return sows. These Lactobacillus ASVs were exclusively connected with ASVs from other taxa (Fig. S3C). Subsequently, the Netshift method was used to identify the ‘driver’ taxa in each of the co-occurrence networks of the vaginal microbiota of normal and non-return sows. We identified 29 driver microbes contributing to the network shifts from normal to non-return states. The top driver taxa were ASV0351: s_Corynebacterium stationis, ASV0353: s_Weissella jogaejeotgali, ASV0054: g_Aerococcus, ASV0008: g_Clostridium sensu stricto 1, ASV0399: s_Clostridium perfringens, ASV0044: g_Terrisporobacter, ASV0111: g_p-251-o5, ASV0192: s_Limosilactobacillus reuteri, etc. (Fig. 4C). These taxa might play important roles in the distinct co-occurrence patterns of the microbial community between normal and non-return sows.

Alterations in predicted functional capacities of the vaginal microbiome between normal-return and non-return sows

We predicted the metabolic pathways of the vaginal microbiota based on the 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data using PICRUSt2. The predicted functional pathways of the vaginal microbiota were significantly altered between the normal-return sows and non-return sows (P < 0.0001), suggesting the changes in the predicted functional capacities of the vaginal microbiota (Fig. S4A and B). LEfSe analysis identified a total of 53 pathways showing significantly differential abundances between the two groups (LDA > 2, P < 0.05). Specifically, 24 pathways were significantly enriched in normal-return sows, such as aerobic respiration I (cytochrome c), L-isoleucine biosynthesis IV, anhydromuropeptides recycling, superpathway of arginine and polyamine biosynthesis, TCA cycle VIII (helicobacter), super-pathway of Clostridium acetobutylicum acidogenic fermentation, and pyruvate fermentation to butanoate etc., while the other 29 pathways were significantly enriched in non-return sows, including peptidoglycan biosynthesis II (staphylococci), lactose and galactose degradation I, sucrose degradation III (sucrose invertase), super-pathway of L-alanine biosynthesis, queuosine biosynthesis, L-lysine biosynthesis II, polyisoprenoid biosynthesis (E. coli), and peptidoglycan biosynthesis V (β-lactam resistance) (Fig. 5).

Subsequently, we investigated the associations between the differential ASVs, genera, and predicted functional pathways (Fig. S4C and S5). Almost all bacterial taxa that were significantly enriched in normal-return sows showed a positive correlation with the pathways that were also significantly enriched in this group, but exhibited a negative correlation with the pathways that were significantly enriched in non-return sows. Notably, we observed that all bacterial genera, such as Lactobacillus, Turicibacter, Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Terrisporobacter, and Christensenellaceae R-7 group, which were significantly enriched in normal-return sows, displayed a significantly positive correlation with methanogenesis from acetate. ASV0935: g_Streptococcus, Bacteroidales (ASV0687: g_p-2534-18B5 gut group, Prevotellaceae UCG-001, and Bacteroidales RF16 group), and Clostridia (including Christensenellaceae R-7 group, Clostridia vadinBB60 group, ASV0302: g_Lachnospiraceae XPB1014 group, ASV0008:g_Clostridium sensu stricto 1, and ASV0068:o_Oscillospirales) that were significantly enriched in normal sows showed significantly positive correlation with pyruvate fermentation to butanoate, as well as the super-pathway of Clostridium acetobutylicum acidogenic fermentation, which was also enriched in normal-return sows. Additionally, significantly positive correlations were observed between the relative abundances of Christensenellaceae R-7 group, ASV0687: g_p-2534-18B5 gut group and ASV0068:o__Oscillospirales, and the abundance of the predicted Adenosylcobalamin biosynthesis pathway. Conversely, ASV0336: g_Staphylococcus, which was significantly enriched in non-return sows, showed a significantly negative correlation with the aforementioned pathways and a positive correlation with formaldehyde assimilation II (RuMP Cycle), formaldehyde oxidation I, and peptidoglycan biosynthesis II (staphylococci) (Fig. S4C, S5 and Table S4, S5).

Vaginal bacterial taxa showing the potential to be biomarkers for distinguishing normal-return and non-return sows

We aimed to investigate whether the vaginal bacterial taxa could serve as biomarkers for distinguishing normal-return and non-return sows. A random forest classification model was constructed based on the vaginal bacterial genera identified from estrus return-associated ASV analysis. Under leave-one-out cross-validation, the model demonstrated good discriminatory capacity, with an AUC of 0.907 (Fig. 6A), along with an accuracy of 86.5%, sensitivity of 79.4%, and specificity of 92.5% (Fig. 6B). Among the top-ranked genera, Sphaerochaeta, Bacteroidales RF16 group, and Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group demonstrated the greatest feature importance based on mean decrease in accuracy (MDA) (Fig. 6C). To further confirm the associations of these bacterial taxa with return to estrus, we performed a logistic regression analysis on 19 bacterial taxa that were not only identified as differential bacterial taxa in the LEfSe analysis, but also showed high feature importance in the random forest model. Robust associations were observed between these taxa and return to estrus in post-weaning sows in both crude and adjusted models (Table 2). Notably, consistent with the results obtained in LEfSe analysis, the relative abundances of [Eubacterium] coprostanoligenes group, Christensenellaceae R-7 group, Muribaculaceae, and Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group were all positively associated with normal estrus return in weaned sows. These results suggest that these vaginal bacterial taxa could serve as potential biomarkers for future intervention investigation.

Discussion

The return to estrus in post-weaning sows is a crucial determinant of reproduction performance and is influenced by various factors. Longer weaning-to-estrus intervals have a detrimental effect on PSY, leading to substantial economic losses in the pig industry. In our previous study using the same cohort, we demonstrated that gut microbiota composition was associated with estrus return through the regulation of sex steroid hormone metabolism [16]. In this study, we analyzed the composition of the vaginal microbiota in sows from the same cohort and identified significant differences in the composition and predicted metabolic functions between normal-return and non-return sows. The results from this study provided an important complement for evaluating the effects of both the gut and reproduction tract microbiota on sow reproduction, such as sow return to estrus.

Unlike the vaginal microbiota in healthy women, where Lactobacillus is the dominant genus [31, 32], Anaerococcus was the predominant genus in the vaginal microbiota of sows in our study, which was consistent with observations in Ellegaard Göttingen Minipigs [9] as well as in commercial Landrace × Yorkshire sows [17]. Meanwhile, it has been reported to be a core bacterial component shared across multiple parities [13] and is closely linked to reproduction performance [11]. These findings highlight the distinct differences in the vaginal microbial ecology between humans and pigs. Significant differences in the composition of the vaginal microbiota were observed between normal-return and non-return sows. Perhaps the levels of sex steroid hormones in sows after weaning shape the composition of the vaginal microbiota. Consistent with our previous findings in an independent cohort [17], normal-return sows were characterized by increased α-diversity of the vaginal microbiota. It has been reported that reduced levels of estrogens could cause vulvovaginal atrophy and decrease vaginal secretions containing nutrients to support bacterial growth [33–35], which could contribute to the decreased α-diversity of the vaginal microbiota in non-return sows. We identified the vaginal taxa related to sow return to estrus after weaning. Aerococcus, Facklamia, Corynebacterium, Staphylococcus [36, 37], Streptococcus [38], and Weissella [39], which have been reported to be pathogens, were enriched in non-return sows. Additionally, Bifidobacterium showed significantly higher abundance in non-return sows, which might be affected by the hormone levels of the sows, as a previous study found that elevated progesterone levels during late gestation promote an increase in the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium [40]. In contrast, beneficial commensal bacteria were more abundant in normal-return sows, including lactic acid-producing bacteria, such as Lactobacillus [41], Turicibacter [42], and the short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)-producing bacteria, such as Treponema [43], Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Lachnospiraceae XPB1014 group [44, 45], Christensenellaceae R-7 group [46], Oscillospirales [47], Romboutsia [42], Muribaculaceae [48], and Rikenellaceae [49]. Lactobacillus protects the vaginal environment by producing lactic acid and inhibits the reproduction of pathogenic bacteria [50]. In addition, estrogen facilitates the accumulation of glycogen in vaginal epithelial cells, which can be utilized preferentially by Lactobacillus spp. as a primary carbon source [51], suggesting a potential mechanism linking hormonal status, the vaginal microbiota composition, and return to estrus in sows. Importantly, the current study validated key findings from our previous study [17], where several key bacterial taxa showed consistent associations with return to estrus across different sow cohorts and commercial farms. Specifically, Corynebacterium and Weissella were enriched in non-return sows in both studies, while members of the order Clostridiales including Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Lachnospiraceae XPB1014 group, and Christensenellaceae R-7 group, as well as Oscillospirales, showed consistently higher abundances in normal-return sows. This reproducibility provides strong evidence for these bacterial taxa as robust biomarkers associated with return to estrus.

Consistently, the predicted superpathway of Clostridium acetobutylicum acidogenic fermentation, pyruvate fermentation to butanoate, methanogenesis from acetate, and 3-phenylpropanoate degradation were found to be significantly enriched in normal sows. These predicted functional pathways showed significant positive correlations with the relative abundances of putative lactic acid- and SCFA-producing bacteria, suggesting that these bacteria might possess the metabolic potential to maintain the acidic vaginal environment of sows through the production of lactic acid and SCFAs, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of potentially pathogenic bacteria. An additional possible explanation is that lactic acid serves as a precursor for the synthesis of SCFAs by specific microbial taxa [52]. Lactic acid-producing bacteria might create a more favorable environment for the survival of SCFAs-producing bacteria. It is important to note that our functional predictions were derived from PICRUSt2, which infers metabolic potential from 16 S rRNA gene sequences rather than measuring actual metabolic activity. These compositional and functional differences between the two sow groups were further reflected in the co-occurrence network analysis. Specifically, the presence of intra-genus co-occurrence interactions among Lactobacillus ASVs exclusively in the normal-return group suggested that members of this genus formed a coherent and cooperative ecological unit, reflecting distinct microbial interaction patterns affecting the return to estrus in post-weaning sows. Given that Lactobacillus is known to play a beneficial role in maintaining vaginal homeostasis and has been successfully used as probiotics [53, 54], these taxa represent promising candidates for future probiotic development in the pig industry.

However, it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, the association results and discriminatory bacterial taxa from the random forest test would need to be confirmed in independent, large sow cohorts. Specifically, despite achieving a discriminatory capacity with an AUC of 0.907, the random forest test was built on a relatively small sample size, which raised the risk of overfitting. Further validation would be needed to confirm the discriminatory capacity of the identified bacterial taxa as reliable biomarkers. Additionally, although we controlled the effects of major confounders through batch management (similar lactation length, health status, diet, and housing), we did not measure body condition scores or individual feed intake during lactation, which might influence post-weaning estrus return. Second, although ASV-based analysis provided higher resolution than traditional OTU clustering, species-level taxonomic assignment based on the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene has inherent limitations and should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the functional characterization of the vaginal microbiome based on predicted pathways from 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data remains limited. Third, metabolomic analysis of vaginal secretion samples along with hormonal measurements could uncover possible mechanisms underlying post-weaning estrus return. Finally, as an observational study, our findings established the associations between the vaginal microbiota and return to estrus, but did not determine the causality. The potential roles of lactic acid-producing bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, and SCFAs-producing bacteria as biomarkers associated with normal-return to estrus should be confirmed in future studies.

Conclusions

The results from this study demonstrated that the vaginal microbiota of post-weaning sows differed significantly between normal-return and non-return individuals. Several bacterial taxa, including Lactobacillus and members of the Christensenellaceae R-7 group, were consistently associated with return to estrus. These findings enhance our understanding of the relationship between the vaginal microbial composition and reproduction status in sows, and provide a foundation for further mechanistic studies in the future.
