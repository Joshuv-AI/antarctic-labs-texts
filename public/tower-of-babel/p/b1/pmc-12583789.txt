Beyond Body Mass Index: Toward a Focus on Body Composition and Function
Authors: Kyung Hee Park
Published: 2025

In epidemiological and clinical research on obesity and chronic disease, body mass index (BMI)—a simple ratio of weight to height—has long served as the standard measure of adiposity, and large-scale population studies have consistently shown its strong association with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and premature mortality.1-3 Although BMI remains the most widely used criterion in clinical and public health settings, it has clear limitations in adequately reflecting the distribution and quality of adipose and lean tissues, which are key determinants of metabolic health. BMI is an indirect and conceptually limited metric that does not adequately reflect the heterogeneity of excess adiposity, the fundamental pathophysiological feature of obesity.4 While it provides a convenient index for population surveillance, reliance on BMI alone may lead to clinical misclassification—labeling metabolically healthy obesity or overlooking those with normal BMI but excessive visceral adiposity or diminished muscle mass. Such discordance between anthropometric measures and true metabolic risk is particularly evident in aging and Asian populations, where the relationship between BMI and body composition is highly variable.4,5

In Asian populations, cardiometabolic complications frequently arise at relatively lower BMI thresholds.5 Accordingly, defining obesity solely by BMI may be insufficient to accurately assess obesity-related health risks in these populations. This recognition has prompted growing attention toward alternative and complementary metrics that capture both the quantitative and qualitative aspects of body composition. Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has emphasized the importance of body composition profiling, including quantification of fat mass, lean mass, and skeletal muscle mass, in elucidating obesity-related pathophysiology. Skeletal muscle constitutes the body’s largest metabolic organ and plays a central role in maintaining glucose homeostasis, lipid oxidation, and systemic anti-inflammatory balance. Beyond its role in locomotion, skeletal muscle acts as a key regulator of whole-body energy metabolism through endocrine and paracrine signaling, influencing hepatic glucose production, adipose-tissue lipolysis, and vascular function.6

Loss of skeletal muscle mass or deterioration of muscle quality impairs key regulatory processes in glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby promoting insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, and cardiovascular dysfunction.7-9 Sarcopenic obesity—the coexistence of excess adiposity and skeletal-muscle depletion—represents a critical phenotype linking impaired muscle metabolism, anabolic resistance, and chronic low-grade inflammation to systemic metabolic dysfunction.10 Large-scale epidemiological studies have further confirmed that unfavorable alterations in body composition are closely associated with cardiometabolic diseases, including atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), chronic kidney disease, and T2DM.11,12 Collectively, these findings emphasize that the adverse health impacts of obesity arise not merely from excess body weight, but from the metabolic and functional consequences of altered body composition.7-9,11-13 Preservation of skeletal muscle integrity should therefore be regarded as a cornerstone of metabolic health, extending beyond its traditional link to frailty and aging.13

In this issue of the Journal of Obesity & Metabolic Syndrome, two complementary studies reinforce this evolving perspective. Xu et al.14 demonstrate that lower relative skeletal muscle mass independently predicts both the incidence and persistence of MASLD in a community-based cohort, supporting the concept of a muscle–liver axis whereby skeletal muscle exerts protective effects against hepatic fat accumulation and metabolic dysfunction. In parallel, the 2024 obesity fact sheet by Cho et al.15 documents a persistent rise in the national obesity prevalence and, notably, a 16.8% prevalence of low muscle mass among Korean adults. Together, these findings underscore that the Korean obesity epidemic reflects not merely a quantitative excess of body weight, but a qualitative imbalance in body composition that compromises metabolic resilience.

While both investigations utilized bioelectrical impedance analysis rather than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or other imaging-based modalities, their emphasis on skeletal muscle as a determinant of metabolic health provides valuable, pragmatic evidence for clinical and epidemiological applications. Future studies employing standardized, high-precision methods are warranted to further explore the interplay among adiposity, muscle mass, and metabolic outcomes. Moreover, longitudinal data integrating lifestyle, genetic, and metabolic determinants could advance precision phenotyping of obesity and inform targeted interventions.

This conceptual transition from a weight-based to a composition- and function-based framework aligns with the evolving concept of ‘clinical obesity,’ in which excess adiposity attains clinical relevance only when it leads to measurable metabolic, structural, or functional impairment.13 Obesity is now recognized as a chronic, multisystem disease characterized by organ-specific metabolic dysfunction and increased morbidity risk. This reconceptualization underscores the importance of integrated prevention and management strategies that prioritize metabolic recovery and functional restoration rather than simple weight reduction.

As the concept of clinical obesity continues to evolve, incorporating standardized body-composition assessment into both clinical practice and population-level surveillance may help transform obesity management from a weight-centric to a health-centric paradigm. Such an approach would enable individualized, mechanistically-informed strategies for obesity prevention, treatment, and risk stratification—ushering in a new era of precision obesity medicine.
