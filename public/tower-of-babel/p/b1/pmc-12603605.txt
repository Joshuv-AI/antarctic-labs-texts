Core Purposes of Mental Health Peer Work: Empowerment, Stigma Reduction, Relational Safety and Walking Alongside People
Authors: Nicholas Haines
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMental health peer work harnesses personal experience of distress and recovery to support others. While growing evidence highlights its benefits, little is known about how individual narratives map onto core peer work functions in everyday practice.AimTo present an autoethnographic narrative that embodies four fundamental purposes of mental health peer work—empowerment, stigma reduction, relational safety and walking alongside—in a manner that is instructive to mental health nurses who have peer workers as colleagues.ApproachDrawing on my lived experience—from a year of my childhood in France to severe illness as a university student, through diverse roles culminating in peer work—I use illustrative client vignettes to show how shared experience fosters connection and recovery.Findings(1) Empowerment: Gentle, consistent support enabled a woman with complex trauma to participate in art shows. (2) Stigma reduction: Normalising lithium use helped a young man face bipolar disorder treatment with confidence. (3) Relational safety: Incorporating client‐led rituals (e.g., tarot readings) built the trust needed for deeper conversations. (4) Walking alongside: Presence and honest advocacy offered meaning to a man grappling with poverty and despair.ConclusionLived experience is a unique resource that enriches mental health teams. Sharing peer principles with mental health clinicians can facilitate the process of integrating peer workers into mental health teams.Keywords: empowerment, lived experience narrative, mental health peer work, peer support, relational safety, stigma reduction

No child says, ‘When I grow up I'll have a mental illness and become a mental health peer worker!’ Like all paths to peer work mine involved some pain, some growth and lots of serendipity.

When I was 11, my parents, younger brother, younger sister and I lived in France for a year. My Dad had won a Food and Agricultural Organization fellowship to lead a forestry research project based in France. We lived in a cosy cottage on a hill overlooking the Seine. On the other side of the river was the Fontainebleau forest. Our home was next to a wheat field and another forest. We experienced four truly distinct seasons—an autumn ablaze with hues of red, orange and yellow, a winter enlivened by the thrill of seeing snow and skiing in the Alps, a spring that brought green shoots and daffodils and relief from the bitter cold, and a summer warm enough for swimming but rarely hot. I learned a lot of French. I saw the Mona Lisa and was underwhelmed (it is a tiny thing and you can only see it from a distance). Monet's cottage in Giverny, on the other hand, made a deep impression. I got used to 2‐hour lunch breaks.

I have loving parents. I excelled at school. I won a scholarship to attend Trinity College at the University of Melbourne. I started an Arts/Law programme there and the first year went well.

In my second year of university I had a viral illness. At first it felt like a common cold but the symptoms lingered for weeks. Unlike a normal cold I also felt a jarring tiredness. At last the cold‐like symptoms stopped but I still felt drained all the time, despite having adequate sleep. My motivation and mood were dismal and stayed that way week after week. My mind, once sharp and eager to work, felt sluggish and lost in dense fog. I did not know what was happening. I did have some friends and professional supports in Melbourne but I was somewhat isolated from my support system in Brisbane. For the first time in my life I struggled to read and write. I could not complete assignments. This appalled me. I looked forward to nothing, was cheered by nothing, not exercise or people or music or nature… to me the world was a void. For the first time in my life I thought of ending my life. My parents, understandably, were deeply worried, so I left Melbourne and returned to Brisbane.

Severe depression became a recurring experience for me. I have been able to do quite a lot in my life but illness has been a heavy burden. I had to modify my expectations. I sometimes need acute electroconvulsive therapy. I have often felt frustrated by the gap between the life I have had and the life I might have led if I had used my abilities to the full, unfettered by illness.

I have done many things I enjoyed but relapses made it hard to develop a career. I was a Teacher's Aide at a school for migrant children. I worked for a media management company. I got a Master's degree in Community Development and worked on a foreign aid project in Cambodia. I did mental health support work in Brisbane. And then I became a mental health peer worker.

People are riveting. Our lives are brief, a cosmic nanosecond, but also vast. The scope of what people feel, conceive and create demands awe. It is a peer worker's privilege to see that all the time. I talk with people who wrestle with big feelings and want my help. In those moments people relax their guard. They discard pretence. Image management is low on their list of concerns. I talk with a music teacher who dislikes his body, tempted to use the weighing scales he sees when we pass the physical exam room, feeling loved by his girlfriend one day and abandoned the next, content one hour but distraught the next. I talk with a cleaner who lost her job because of back pain, now in a battle with poverty, a senseless poverty forced on her by government policy, sleeping on a friend's couch while her pension claim is processed, selling cherished jewellery just to keep herself afloat. I talk with an architect enmeshed in a rancid divorce, flexing every sinew to be steadfast for her children, showing up to work each day with a weary smile, drinking three bottles of Moscato a night and trying to cut back to two. No matter how many times I do this I still wonder ‘How will I connect with this person, surely the differences between us are too great, the demographic differences, the differences in our life experiences, the differences of temperament, what do I have to offer?’. Then I relearn what I learned hundreds of times before: shared experiences of pain are a great leveller. Suffering is intrinsic to being human. Being attentive goes a long way. Being curious goes a long way. So does the simple act of not judging someone. If you are gentle, compassionate, supportive and responsive, then the differences between you and them matter much less than the fact that you are there, fully committed to serving them, placing your knowledge at their disposal for them to use as they see fit.

Peer work in general involves having a personal lived experience of challenging circumstances and using that experience as a resource that helps other people to navigate similar hardship. There are peer workers for mental health distress, for addiction recovery and for disability, to name just a few. There is a distinction between a consumer peer worker and a carer peer worker. A consumer peer worker has experience of receiving support, whereas a carer peer worker has experience of caring for someone who receives support. My essay will focus on my experience as a mental health consumer peer worker—a term that I use interchangeably with mental health peer worker. Mental health carer peer workers exist in our health care system too, but they are outside the scope of my essay.

In my peer work roles, lived experience has rarely meant having the same diagnoses and demographic attributes as the client. The main lived experience required is mental distress and recovery. This is what averts the asymmetry that characterises clinician–patient relationships. My lived experiences of illness episodes and recovery pathways have affected my knowledge, attitudes and behaviours in ways that promote equality and empathy.

Empowerment was the focus of my work with a woman who had complex PTSD related to sexual abuse, an alcohol use disorder and social anxiety. We spoke a couple of times a week, mostly by phone, and I helped her finalise the paperwork for an alcohol detox program she was about to undertake. I prepared for each conversation by anticipating topics that might come up and questions I could ask if the conversation stalled but I did not impose an agenda. We discussed her art work, her efforts to overcome social anxiety and attend art shows, and anything else she wanted to raise. In a feedback email to me she singled out my ‘endearing gentleness’ and the consistency of our conversations as factors that encouraged her to attend a recent art show. She also remarked that her experience of sexual abuse normally prompts her to request a female worker when she engages a service but she did not do that with our service because her first meeting with me made her feel ‘safe straight away’. I do not think I was doing anything special but my lived experience, while very different from hers, conditioned me to be tentative and gentle. I know how vulnerable a person can be when they approach a service and do not know what to expect. I know how unhelpful it can be when someone jumps into problem‐solving mode. I know how annoying it can be to face the well‐intentioned but grating positivity of a person blessed with good mental health their entire life.

Stigma reduction was at the forefront of my work with a young man who had recently experienced his first manic episode. Our conversations spanned a range of topics including his involvement in a community orchestra and his coaching of a soccer team. When he first started taking lithium, he commented on how frequently he needed to get blood tests done. My personal familiarity with this drug—I use it for its relapse prevention properties for depression—enabled me to assure him that the therapeutic monitoring, while frequent and bewildering in the beginning, reduces markedly to once every six to 12 months once your doctor is satisfied that you are taking the optimal dose. I told him that lithium is one of the most extensively researched interventions in psychiatry; its effect size is large, and it is considered one of psychiatry's biggest success stories. My aim was to normalise lithium and bipolar disorder—to portray them as compatible with a good life. He seemed to be coping well already, but I felt that some preemptive stigma reduction might help him to be upfront with his doctor and family if his condition deteriorated.

Relational safety was illustrated in a whimsical way when I encountered a resident of a rehabilitation facility. I knocked on her door, introduced myself and asked if she would be interested in a conversation. She told me she was learning how to interpret tarot cards and asked if I would like a demonstration. So we sat at a table in the recreation area, she spread out the cards, she asked me to state a question I wanted answered, she asked me to select cards, and then she told me what they presaged. In the weeks that followed, we would typically include this ritual in our conversations, but she also opened up about challenges in her life. I think she used the tarot readings skilfully for several purposes—to create a buffer between her and a conversation partner she was still getting to know, to gauge my willingness to be authentic and vulnerable from the types of questions I posed for the tarot cards, and to display her growing mastery of a mystical art. I doubt that she would have felt safe enough to tackle the issues we ended up addressing if we had not built that base of relational safety first.

Walking alongside someone—simply being present without judgement—was all I could do for a man who had been unemployed for a decade and had severe depression. He was living in poverty because of the grotesque inadequacy of income support payments. He was interested in learning graphic design and had done a short course in it as a precondition for his income support. He was unsure about how to proceed so I made some phone calls and learned that graphic design firms require a Bachelor's degree for an unpaid internship and a Master's for paid positions. The ‘Work For The Dole’ course he had done was worthless. I had the unenviable task of sharing that bad news. I felt so powerless. His suicidal thoughts had abated and we were close to the 4‐week mark when we had to discharge people. I made sure he knew in advance when our last conversation would be. During that final call he wept. He said the only thing he had been looking forward to recently was my phone calls. I wished we were not a short term service. I wished I controlled national fiscal policy. I wished I could implement a Job Guarantee program. I wished I could massively expand public housing. But all I could do was muster some words of encouragement and hope that they would not ring hollow. After we said our goodbyes I put down the phone, wiped the tear spatter from the desk, and prepared for my next call.

I want to work in the mental health field for decades. Peer work remade my relationship with my own story, which I began to see as a resource, not a stain, not a part of me I am ashamed of and want to disown. That insight, coupled with a deeper appreciation of what clinical psychologists can do, prompted me to return to university to study psychology. On most days I can see a world that teems with colour, like that splendid autumn forest in France.
