Clinical outcomes of a novel technique for the treatment of oroantral fistulas: a 3-year retrospective study
Authors: Ali Khalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes of the fistula mucosal debridement technique in the treatment of oroantral fistulas (OAFs) measuring ≤3 mm in diameter.Materials and methods:This retrospective study analyzed 20 patients with OAFs (≤3 mm) treated using mucosal debridement at Manara University (2019–2022). Data were extracted from electronic records, including demographics, fistula size, and healing outcomes. Debridement was performed weekly (maximum 3 sessions) using a 1.5-mm carbide bur. Healing was assessed clinically, and data were analyzed with SPSS version 22 (descriptive statistics, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis).Results:Complete closure was achieved in 15 out of 20 cases (75%): 5 out of 20 (25%) after the second session and 10 out of 20 (50%) after the third. No significant associations were found between healing and age/gender (P > 0.05, log–rank test). Failures (5/20, 25%) required alternative surgical closure.Conclusion:The mucosal debridement technique resulted in the complete closure of the fistula opening in 75% of cases involving OAFs measuring ≤3 mm in diameter after the third debridement. This method is straightforward, non-invasive, and does not require surgical intervention or additional materials for the management of narrow-diameter OAFs.

Introduction

The oroantral fistula (OAF) is an epithelialized pathological communication between the oral cavity and the maxillary sinus, most commonly occurring as a complication following the extraction of maxillary molars or premolars whose roots approximate the sinus floor[1,2]. This condition allows the passage of microorganisms and fluids between the sinus and oral cavity, potentially leading to chronic sinusitis if left untreated[3].

The incidence of OAFs ranges from 0.31% to 4.7% following the extraction of upper first and second molars[2]. While small communications (<2 mm) may close spontaneously, epithelialization of the tract typically occurs within 48–72 hours, resulting in a persistent fistula[4]. Larger defects (>3 mm) rarely heal without intervention due to the constant motion of orofacial muscles and negative sinus pressure[5].

Traditional management approaches include various surgical techniques, such as buccal advancement flaps (Rehrmann technique), palatal rotational flaps, and buccal fat pad grafts[6–8]. Although effective, these methods often require extensive surgical intervention, may reduce vestibular depth, and can cause significant postoperative discomfort[9]. More recent approaches have explored biomaterials like platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) and fibrin glue, which offer less invasive alternatives with comparable success rates[10,11]. The objective of this retrospective study was to assess the effectiveness of the fistula mucosal debridement technique in treating small OAFs with a diameter of ≤3 mm. This study was conducted in accordance with the revised STROCSS 2025 guidelines for retrospective cohort studies[14].

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Manara University under reference number 46 on 30 July 2023 and was conducted at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Manara University Hospital, a tertiary referral center. The study period (September 2019–September 2022) was selected to ensure adequate case accrual while maintaining contemporary treatment protocols.

This retrospective study evaluated patients treated at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Manara University, between September 2019 and September 2022. A total of 20 cases of OAFs with diameters of ≤3 mm were identified from electronic health records and included in the analysis. Data were systematically extracted, including patient demographics, fistula characteristics, treatment details, and follow-up outcomes. The mucosal debridement technique was performed weekly using a round carbide bur (1.5 mm diameter) under local anesthesia until closure was achieved or a maximum of three sessions. Healing was assessed clinically at each visit using a periodontal probe.

Patient records were screened to identify eligible cases based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Demographic and clinical data, including age, gender, fistula size, number of debridement sessions, and healing status, were compiled into a standardized database. To ensure accuracy, two independent reviewers cross-verified the data, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. The study adhered to a data management plan approved by the institutional ethics committee, which included regular backups, encrypted storage, and restricted access to maintain confidentiality. Missing or ambiguous records were excluded from the analysis. Statistical analysis focused on healing rates, with censoring applied for lost-to-follow-up cases. This research is registered at Research Registry under the identifying number researchregistry11862.

Data were collected from patient records at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Manara University between September 2019 and September 2022. Patients were selected based on the following criteria:

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients who had small OAFs with a diameter of ≤3 mm, and (2) Fistulas treated using the fistula mucosal debridement technique.

Exclusion criteria: (1) History of acute sinusitis, and (2) Fistulas that have been previously repaired.

Surgical procedure

The technique involves mechanical debridement of the fistula edges using a round carbide bur (diameter: 1.5 mm) attached to a low-speed handpiece (20 000 rpm). Debridement is repeated weekly until closure is achieved. Cases without closure after three sessions (3 weeks) are considered failures.

The oral cavity was disinfected with antiseptic mouthwash, followed by the disinfection of the fistula site with concentrated povidone–iodine. Local anesthesia was administered using gauze soaked in a 10% lidocaine solution. Complete debridement of the fistula margins was performed using a surgical drill until bleeding occurred along the entire edge of the fistula (Fig. 1). Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 (SPSS Inc., IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were used, and time-to-event analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Censoring was applied in cases where healing was not observed during the follow-up period or if the patient withdrew from the study.

Results

The results demonstrated successful closure of OAFs in the majority of cases using the proposed technique. Patients experienced minimal discomfort and rapid recovery.

Patient characteristics

A total of 20 patients (13 males, 7 females), with a mean age of 44.7 years (range: 22–70 years), met the inclusion criteria.

Clinical analysis

Overall, 75% of fistulas healed successfully. Specifically, 50% of cases healed after the second debridement, and 25% after the third. Discussion

The management of OAFs presents a significant challenge in oral and maxillofacial surgery, balancing the imperative for predictable closure against the morbidity and complexity of surgical intervention. This retrospective study evaluated a novel, minimally invasive mucosal debridement technique for the treatment of small OAFs (≤3 mm), achieving a 75% success rate (15/20 cases) within a 3-week treatment window. Our findings indicate that 50% of cases achieved complete closure after the second debridement session (end of week 2), and a further 25% after the third session (end of week 3). The remaining 25% (5/20) of cases, which failed to respond, were subsequently managed successfully with traditional surgical closure, indicating that this technique serves as an effective first-line conservative approach rather than a universal solution. The lack of a significant association between healing outcomes and patient age or gender (P > 0.05) suggests that the success of the technique is primarily dependent on the mechanical process itself and perhaps intrinsic patient healing factors not captured in this study.

The fundamental principle underpinning this technique is the disruption of the epithelialized tract and the stimulation of wound healing by secondary intention, a concept borrowed from dermatology, where dermabrasion is used to promote scar tissue remodeling and regeneration[12,13]. In the context of an OAF, the fistula tract is lined with epithelium, creating a permanent pathway between the oral cavity and the maxillary sinus. By mechanically debriding the margins of the fistula with a carbide bur until punctate bleeding is observed, we effectively convert a chronic, epithelialized wound into an acute, bleeding wound bed. This fresh wound is rich in platelets, cytokines, and growth factors, initiating a robust inflammatory and proliferative healing cascade. The subsequent formation of a stable fibrin clot and granulation tissue provides a scaffold for mucosal cell migration and proliferation, ultimately leading to closure from the margins inward. The weekly repetition of this process is designed to address any early re-epithelialization of the tract that may occur before complete closure is achieved, thereby persistently guiding the healing toward the desired outcome. Ultrastructural studies support the efficacy of mechanical abrasion in promoting regeneration compared to other resurfacing methods[15].

This approach must be contextualized within the existing spectrum of OAF management strategies. Traditional surgical techniques, such as the buccal advancement flap (Rehrmann technique), palatal rotational flaps, and buccal fat pad grafts, remain the gold standard for larger or persistent fistulas and boast high success rates, often cited between 87% and 93%[6,7,9]. The Rehrmann technique, in particular, is widely utilized for its reliability. However, these methods are not without significant drawbacks. They require extensive surgical dissection, leading to postoperative pain, swelling, and trismus. A critical long-term disadvantage of buccal advancement flaps is the reduction in vestibular depth, which can permanently compromise prosthetic rehabilitation[8]. Palatal flaps, while offering a robust, keratinized tissue source, introduce donor site morbidity, potential damage to the greater palatine artery, and prolonged patient discomfort[16,17]. Similarly, the use of the buccal fat pad, though highly vascularized and effective, involves entering a new anatomical space with its own set of potential complications[18].

In recent years, the field has witnessed a paradigm shift toward biologically augmented, minimally invasive approaches aimed at mitigating the shortcomings of traditional surgery. The advent of autologous platelet concentrates, such as PRF, has been a cornerstone of this shift. PRF offers a compelling alternative, acting as a natural fibrin scaffold enriched with a sustained release of growth factors (e.g., PDGF, VEGF, and TGF-β) and leukocytes that orchestrate healing and possess inherent antimicrobial properties[19,20]. Studies have demonstrated impressive success rates of 90–100% for PRF in closing OAFs, particularly when used in layered techniques[20,21]. The “double-barrier technique” described by Jung et al[19], which combines PRF with a buccal flap, exemplifies this modern approach. Furthermore, the combination of endoscopic middle meatal antrostomy with PRF closure represents a sophisticated simultaneous management of both the OAF and any underlying sinusitis[20,21].

Other biomaterials have also shown promise. Fibrin glue, an autologous or commercial two-component sealant, provides immediate hemostasis and adhesion, reducing the need for extensive suturing[22,23]. Similarly, hyaluronic acid has emerged as a regenerative adjunct due to its potent anti-inflammatory, osteogenic, and angiogenic properties[24,25]. Although its direct application in OAF closure is less documented, its proven efficacy in promoting wound healing and socket preservation following tooth extraction suggests a potential role in managing OAFs associated with extraction sites[24,26]. More advanced bioengineered approaches, such as the use of autologous cryoplatelet gel combined with bone graft substitutes, as described by Scala et al[27], aim not only to close the communication but also to reconstruct the sinus floor.

Our proposed mucosal debridement technique occupies a unique niche within this evolving treatment landscape. Its primary advantage is its profound simplicity and minimal invasiveness. It requires no surgical flaps, no donor sites, no specialized biomaterials, and no advanced training. This translates to several key benefits: (1) elimination of surgical morbidity; (2) preservation of anatomy; (3) cost-effectiveness; and (4) patient acceptance. However, the technique is not without its limitations. The 25% failure rate indicates that it is not universally applicable. Failures may be attributable to undiagnosed chronic sinusitis, larger underlying bony defects, or high tension in the fistula area. When comparing our 75% success rate to the >90% rates often reported for surgical and PRF-based techniques, it is crucial to recognize the different patient cohorts. Our study exclusively focused on very small fistulas (≤3 mm). In this specific context, a 75% success rate with a non-invasive method is clinically highly significant.

This study has several strengths, including a clearly defined protocol and the use of time-to-event analysis. Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged, including the retrospective design, small sample size, lack of long-term follow-up, and absence of postoperative radiographic imaging. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and control groups would be invaluable to solidify the position of this technique in the treatment algorithm.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the mucosal debridement technique is effective in achieving closure of small OAFs (≤3 mm). The method is simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective. Further research with extended follow-up and radiographic evaluation is warranted to confirm long-term efficacy and safety.
