Multidisciplinary Management of Tracheal Perforation: A Case of Successful Reconstruction and Stenting
Authors: Sofia Valdes‐Camacho, Regina M. Murillo‐Torres, Tatiana A. Rojas‐Zapata, Alejandra Yu Lee‐Mateus, Paola Gutierrez‐Gallegos, Bastien A. Valencia‐Sanchez, Elise Anderson, Kelly S. Robertson, Sebastian Fernandez‐Bussy, Jeffrey R. Janus, David Abia‐Trujillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAirway perforation is a potentially lethal complication from tracheal or mediastinal malignancies that require complex management. Advanced thyroid cancer is known to have high morbidity, and patients with tracheal metastatic lesions are vulnerable to injury. Therapeutic management typically comprises a combination of surgical and nonoperative treatment modalities, whose adverse effects can lead to significant airway injury. We present the case of a 67‐year‐old man with papillary thyroid carcinoma who developed a tracheocutaneous fistula following proton beam therapy and chemotherapy for metastatic disease. Airway repair and recanalization were successfully achieved through a combined surgical and bronchoscopic approach.

Introduction

Airway perforation is most commonly associated with trauma; however, in rare instances, invasive thyroid cancer can result in life‐threatening organ or tracheal perforation [1]. Management of thyroid malignancies often involves a combination of surgical and nonoperative approaches, including radiation and systemic therapies [2]. While these treatments can be highly effective, they also carry inherent risks, regardless of the tumour's degree of invasion or differentiation [3]. Prolonged inflammation and treatment‐related tissue damage may weaken the structural integrity of the airway, leading to friability and perforation [4]. Managing such cases is particularly challenging and demands a coordinated, multidisciplinary effort to ensure timely and effective intervention.

This report highlights the collaborative management of a 67‐year‐old patient who required tracheal reconstruction and central airway stenting following airway dehiscence from thyroid carcinoma.

Case Report

A 67‐year‐old man with a past medical history of papillary thyroid carcinoma status post total thyroidectomy presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath, neck pain and worsening odynophagia. His oncologic history was complicated by extensive invasion of the recurrent laryngeal nerve, transesophageal groove, and tracheal wall, and included treatment with adjuvant I‐131 radioactive iodine (RAI), proton beam therapy, and palliative Lenvatinib. A neck computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a right anterolateral tracheal dehiscence with air extravasation into adjacent soft tissue (Figure 1A) and bronchoscopic evaluation showed a 2 cm left lateral wall tracheal defect (Figure 1B). Thyroid bed exploration by Otorhinolaryngology (ENT), identified a 4 cm × 1–2 cm tracheocutaneous fistula (TCF) spanning 35% of the tracheal circumference, with chondronecrosis, exposed tracheal rings, and purulence. After debridement, a myofascial flap was harvested from the right pectoralis major, tunnelled under the skin between the trachea and carotid sheath and then sutured to the tracheal defect and surrounding musculature. The patient was transferred intubated to the Intensive Care Unit postoperatively.

(A) Axial neck computed tomography (CT) showing interval dehiscence of the right posterolateral trachea (green arrow) with air extravasation into the adjacent soft tissues. (B) Flexible bronchoscopy revealing a right lateral tracheal defect just beyond the vocal cord (yellow line).

Four days after the initial surgery, the patient was brought to the operating room for high‐risk extubation and bronchoscopic evaluation, where dehiscence closure was confirmed. However, a partial myofascial graft protrusion causing proximal central airway obstruction of approximately 70% lumen occlusion was also noticed. A 14 × 40 mm fully covered AERO (Merit Medical Systems, UT, USA) stent was placed.

Six days later, the expected graft involution was complicated by distal stent migration that required bronchoscopic management. The AERO stent was removed with rigid bronchoscopy, followed by endoluminal myofascial flap plasty using argon plasma coagulation (APC) to mitigate airway obstruction. Subsequently, a 16 × 40 mm uncovered self‐expandable metallic stent (Boston Scientific, MA, USA) was placed to maintain airway patency and promote granulation tissue (Figure 2). A month after the surgery, bronchoscopic follow‐up confirmed proper stent positioning with mild granulation tissue. The stent was removed and the granulation tissue was treated with APC and mechanical debulking.

Flexible bronchoscopy demonstrating airway collapse (A) before (green arrow) and (B) after stent placement.

Over the following 2 months, two additional APC sessions were required for proximal tracheal narrowing of 30%–40%, resulting in symptomatic improvement, and successful airway recanalization. Six months later, electrocautery, hybrid knife cuts, and balloon dilation were also performed to maintain airway patency. One year later, CT imaging and recent bronchoscopy visualization demonstrated a cylindrical airway with no evidence of dehiscence (Figure 3). No other complications related to the original defect were reported.

Imaging a year later shows no evidence of the original defect. (A) Flexible bronchoscopy reveals mild narrowing at the previous dehiscence site with a fully epithelialized muscle flap (green arrows). (B) Axial neck CT demonstrates a cylindrical subglottic airway with post‐treatment mucosal thickening but no signs of residual disease, subcutaneous emphysema, or fistulous tracts (green arrow).

Discussion

In accordance with National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, our patient's thyroid malignancy was managed with total thyroidectomy, followed by RAI therapy as postoperative management. For advanced or RAI‐refractory cases, oral multikinase inhibitors like Lenvatinib, are first‐line options for their survival benefits, though they carry common adverse effects including hypertension, proteinuria, and severe, life‐threatening cutaneous and digestive symptoms [2, 3, 5]. Severity or rate of progression vary, often leading to dose reduction or discontinuation, which can contribute to tumour regrowth [3].

Lenvatinib's strong anti‐angiogenic effects, particularly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibition, may result in complications such as fistula formation [6]. A Japanese study reported 18 fistula cases, both cutaneous and oesophageal, among 111 patients treated with Lenvatinib, while another study found 14 fistula cases in 95 patients undergoing treatment [6, 7]. Our patient had been receiving this medication for 8 months when the tracheal defect was identified, consistent with reported timeframes of 3–7.5 months for dehiscence to occur [3, 7]. Proton beam therapy further increases the risk by disrupting key wound‐healing factors, such as transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ), VEGF, tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), and proinflammatory cytokines [8].

Symptoms associated with tracheocutaneous fistulas vary by defect size and comprise skin irritation, infection, weakened cough and voice, and recurrent aspiration pneumonia [9]. In this case, the patient experienced months of odynophagia, dysphagia, and persistent cough, with multiple failed sinus infection treatments before the defect was identified. This nonspecific presentation delayed diagnosis and ENT intervention was critical. Surgical repair typically involves excising the fistulous tract and closing the defect. The approach depends on the complexity of the case: through direct suturing with surrounding tissue reinforcement or through flap reconstruction for larger defects. While free flaps for head and neck reconstruction are increasingly common, pedicled regional flaps remain valuable, particularly for immunocompromised or irradiated patients, as in our case [10, 11, 12]. The pectoralis major myocutaneous flap is a well‐established option due to its stability and robust vascular supply, which ensures reliable irrigation, and promotes bacterial removal and collagen deposition [11]. Larger defects may require costal cartilage grafting with a free forearm flap, as described by Hamahata et al. [13].

A key postoperative concern is flap‐collapse and protrusion into the airway. This known risk was addressed with endoluminal airway restoration by the Interventional Pulmonology service. Given the dynamic volume changes of myofascial flaps, maintaining airway patency required adaptive strategies, encompassing a combination of bronchoscopic and stenting techniques. To ensure airway stability and provide further sealing effect, an AERO stent was initially placed. However, the stent migrated, likely due to its radial force, and was therefore removed. An Ultraflex uncovered nitinol stent, with lower radial force, was subsequently placed to reduce the risk of recurrent migration while maintaining airway patency, preserving mucociliary function, and promoting granulation tissue formation and airway re‐epithelization, a beneficial effect in cases of airway dehiscence [14]. The uncovered stent was successfully removed 10 days later without complications. Additionally, APC induces protein denaturation and water evaporation through heat, leading to tissue shrinkage and coagulation [15]. Our patient underwent three separate APC sessions, mechanical debulking, thermal ablation and balloon dilation, all of which provided immediate symptomatic relief.

Although Lenvatinib‐associated fistula formation and organ perforation have been previously reported in thyroid cancer, the literature largely focuses on incidence, risk factors, and prognosis [6]. Prior reports have described tracheal perforation and TCF formation during Lenvatinib therapy; however, detailed management strategies for airway salvage remain limited. Similarly, pectoralis major flap reconstruction has been detailed for large tracheal defects, as well as airway stenting use in airway dehiscence to maintain patency and facilitate tissue healing [9, 10, 14]. The novelty of the present case lies in the combined multidisciplinary approach: surgical repair of a Lenvatinib‐associated TCF using a pectoralis myofascial flap, followed by sequential bronchoscopic stenting and endoluminal therapies to manage dynamic flap remodelling, prevent recurrent airway obstruction, and achieve durable airway stability.

Prognosis post‐fistula development is typically poor, with a reported average survival of 3 months in similar cases [6]. Despite complications, our patient's quality of life improved significantly following the intervention and survived over a year after diagnosis. Complex cases of airway dehiscence require coordinated and transparent communication across a multidisciplinary team, enabling early recognition of complications and timely, effective intervention to improve patient outcomes.
