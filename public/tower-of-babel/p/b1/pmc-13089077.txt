Conjugation of sunflower meal protein hydrolysates with chlorogenic acid: Structural changes and functional properties
Authors: Sarina Ma, Xili Dege, Zhenyuan Li, Karim Gafurov, Dilshoda Safarava, Qin Guo, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAppropriate modification of sunflower meal protein with polyphenols can greatly increase its functional properties and improve the utilization rate of by-product as a lower price protein source. The present work analyzed effects of covalent conjugation of chlorogenic acid (CGA) to sunflower meal protein hydrolysate (SMPH) on its structural and functional properties. Covalent modification at increasing CGA concentrations (0.2–1.0 mg/mL) caused color changes, reduced free amino, thiol, and tyrosine groups, as well as significantly increased particle size and polyphenol bound equivalents of SMPH. Enhanced random coil content indicated protein unfolding and structural disorder. Fluorescence intensity and surface hydrophobicity decreased, indicating that CGA and SMPH had interacted. Antioxidant activity and emulsifying capacity improved dose-dependently, with 1.0 mg/mL CGA yielding the highest ABTS·+ and DPPH· radical scavenging, and an emulsifying activity index. This study has practical application for the high-value utilization of sunflower meal protein in functional foods.Keywords: Sunflower protein hydrolysate, Chlorogenic acid, Antioxidant capacity, Structural changes, Emulsifying activity

Introduction

Sunflower meal, a by-product derived from sunflower through mechanical pressing or pre-pressing followed by solvent extraction, has become increasingly significant, with an annual production in China exceeding 400,000 tons, ranking second among oilseed cake meal outputs (Li et al., 2025). Sunflower meal is characterized by its high protein content (40%–50%) and represents a valuable plant-based protein resource (Alexandrino et al., 2017; de Oliveira Filho and Egea, 2021; Subaşı, Vahapoğlu, Capanoglu and Mohammadifar, 2022). Compared with other common oil extraction by-products; such as cotton meal or soybean bran (Zhang et al., 2024; González-Pérez and Vereijken, 2008); sunflower meal protein do not contain toxic components and have a lower content of antinutritional components; and notable biological potency (up to 65%); indicating substantial nutritional value (Alexandrino et al., 2017; Karefyllakis et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Nevertheless; its practical utilization remains limited; resulting in relatively low economic value. Recent studies have indicated that enzymatic hydrolysis effectively modifies the structural configuration of sunflower meal proteins; substantially reducing their molecular weights and exposing hydrophobic amino acid residues. These structural transformations significantly enhance the biological activity; functional properties; and associated health effects of the resulting polypeptides (Yuan et al., 2021; Salami et al., 2019). Sunflower meal protein hydrolysates (SMPH) have demonstrated bioactivity with beneficial health implications (Ma et al., 2023). However; the inherent susceptibility of specific amino acids; such as cysteine; tryptophan; and methionine; to oxidative degradation; along with the instability and degradation of residues such as asparagine and glutamine; significantly constrains their broader applications (Johns & Hertzler, 2021). Consequently; advanced molecular modification techniques targeting protein hydrolysates or peptides are necessary to mitigate these limitations and enhance their practical applicability (Cui et al., 2023).

CGA, an essential bioactive polyphenolic compound, exhibits potent antioxidative, antimutagenic, antibacterial, and antiviral properties (Lu et al., 2020; Náthia-Neves & Alonso, 2021). Widely utilized across the food, pharmaceutical, and healthcare sectors, CGA is naturally present in sunflower meal at concentrations ranging from 0.5% to 2.4% (Martin-Pedrosa et al., 2000). Despite its potential, the efficient exploitation of sunflower meal resources is significantly hindered by the rapid enzymatic oxidation of CGA to o-quinone derivatives, catalyzed by polyphenol oxidase. These quinone derivatives are considerably unstable and rapidly polymerize with protein amino acid residues, causing undesirable dark green coloration of proteins, poor sensory properties, and limited commercial viability (Budryn et al., 2015; Keppler et al., 2020). Covalent interactions of CGA with sunflower protein isolates positively influence their solubility. Nonetheless, excessive concentrations (molar ratio ≥ 1:1) adversely affect protein color, impeding its food application. Therefore, optimizing the molar ratio of CGA to sunflower proteins or their enzymatic hydrolysates is essential for enhancing their functional characteristics and practical utility (Karefyllakis et al., 2018). Notably; modifying rice protein hydrolysates under alkaline conditions through covalently conjugating CGA (25%) significantly improved emulsification and antioxidant capacities (X. Pan et al., 2019). Similarly; wheat gluten hydrolysates modified by covalent conjugation with CGA demonstrated enhanced thermal stability; increased antioxidant activity; and reduced allergenic potential compared to untreated hydrolysates (He et al., 2023). Likewise; soybean hydrolysates conjugated with various polyphenols; including proanthocyanidins; epigallocatechin gallate; gallic acid; and caffeic acid; exhibit notably improved emulsifying; antioxidant; biological; and functional properties (Huang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2026). Despite these findings, the precise interaction mechanisms and specific binding sites between polyphenols and oilseed meal proteins, particularly the covalent binding interactions and binding sites involving CGA and protein hydrolysates, remain inadequately elucidated. Moreover, there has been little research on combination of SMPH modification by polyphenols, Jia et al. (2022) studied covalent and non-covalent modification of sunflower protein with CGA. Thus, it is of high research value to investigate the combination of SMPH and CGA.

In this study, we developed covalent conjugates of CGA with SMPH and systematically investigated the structural modifications, including changes in surface hydrophobicity, secondary structure, and particle size distribution after conjugation at varying CGA concentrations. Additionally, we characterized the functional attributes of these conjugates, focusing on their thermal stability, antioxidant activity, and emulsifying ability. The outcomes of this study provide critical insights that facilitate the development of novel peptide-CGA conjugates and advance their use in food industry as functional food components.

Materials and methods

Materials

Defatted sunflower meal powder (designated as “low-heated”; with a 0.87 g/g protein level when it was washed) was acquired in Inner Mongolia Academy of Agricultural & Animal Husbandry Sciences, Hohhot, China. CGA (≥98% purity) was obtained in Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The alcalase (2 × 105 U/g) was obtained from Novezymes Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The remaining chemicals were obtained in Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

SMPH preparation

Sunflower meal protein powder (5 g) was dispersed within distilled water (75 mL), followed by 1 h of continuous stirring of resulting suspension under room temperature (RT). Subsequently, this resultant suspension was heated to 50 °C, and 0.5 mol/L NaOH solution was added to adjust pH level to 8.0. Enzymatic hydrolysis was initiated by adding alcalase at the 1:25 (w/w) enzyme-to-protein mass ratio. Throughout hydrolysis, mixture pH was kept at 8.0 for 4 h. Later, this resultant mixture was subjected to 10 min of boiling for reaction termination, followed by cooling to RT. Subsequently, this cooled hydrolysate underwent centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 15 min) and freeze drying, with supernatant pH being adjusted to 7.0 before freeze drying. Under these optimized conditions, the degree of hydrolysis reached 27%, and the peptide yield was 78%.

Preparation of covalent conjugates between SMPH and CGA

An alkaline method for covalent conjugate formation was adopted according to the established protocol of You et al. (2014). SMPH (1 g) was mixed with varying amounts of CGA (5, 10, 20, 50, or 100 mg) into phosphate buffer (100 mL, pH 9.0). This resulting solution later underwent 24 h of continuous stirring under RT. We utilized an untreated SMPH solution as the control sample. After this reaction, all conjugates were dialyzed using the dialysis membrane for a 72-h duration at the 1000 Da molecular weight threshold under 4 °C for removing the free CGA. The dialysis water was replaced four times, and dialysis membranes were unchanged. The same procedures were conducted in the control group. Each covalent conjugate preparation was performed in triplicate.

Free amino, thiol groups, and tyrosine measurement

For identifying functional groups related to covalent reaction, we measured free amino and thiol group quantities within conjugates using a slightly modified approach utilized by Wu et al. (2018). To quantify amino groups, we dissolved 20 mg ortho-phthaldialdehyde into 0.5 mL methanol, and added the resulting solution into the sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution (1.25 mL, 20% w/v). This mixture was then combined with of sodium borate buffer (12.5 mL,1 mol/L, pH 9.85) containing 50 μL of β-mercaptoethanol (50 μL). A 2 mL aliquot of the prepared reagent was mixed with.

200 μL of the conjugate solution (5 mg/mL). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 min at 35 °C in a water bath, after which the absorbance was measured at 340 nm. The content of free amino groups was analyzed using the calibration curve of L-leucine as a standard.

For the thiol group determination method, the 15 mg conjugate aliquots were dispersed in Tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane hydrochloride buffer (5 mL, 50 mmol/L, pH 8) that contained 8 mol/L urea for preparing the conjugate solution. A separate solution consisting of 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid, 4 mg) as well as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, 1 mg) within Tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane hydrochloride buffer (1 mg, 50 mmol/L, pH 8). Subsequently, 1 mL of this reagent solution was reacted with 1 mL of the conjugate solution (1 mg, 5 mg/mL) for a 1-h duration. The tyrosine content within the conjugate samples was measured according to Petraru et al. (2021) employing the automated amino acid analyzer (Aracus 300, MembraPure GmBH, Berlin, Germany).

Color determination

The colorimetric evaluation of the samples was performed using a CS-600 color difference meter (CHN Spec, China). Initially, the instrument was calibrated automatically using black and white reference plate. After calibration, the measurement mode was activated, with the standard sample L*, a*, and b* values being recorded. Color measurements were conducted for each sample, and average values were calculated from triplicate readings. The instrument was switched off after completing the tests.

Analysis of covalent conjugate structures

Particle size

Distilled water was added to dilute samples to 1 mg/mL for analysis, with refractive indices being set at 1.46 and 1.33, respectively, for protein and distilled water. Particle size analysis was conducted using dynamic light scattering (DLS).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The TEM sample preparation followed the protocol described by Zhou et al. (2020) after minor adjustments. Briefly, the diluted sample solution aliquot (0.01 mg/mL) was deposited on the support film-covered copper grid as droplets. After 20 min of incubation at RT, grids were subjected to air drying. Negative staining was performed using a 2% phosphotungstic acid solution applied dropwise for 1 min. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; H-7800, Hitachi, Japan) was employed for visualizing the morphological features of the SMPH-CGA complexes and SMPH samples (TEM; H-7800, Hitachi, Japan).

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity was quantified using the fluorescent probe 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonate (ANS), following descriptions by Xu et al. (2019) after slight adaptations. Specifically, 25 μL of an 8 mM ANS solution (8 mM) was introduced into the sample solution (5 mL), thoroughly mixed, and equilibrated for a 3 min period. Fluorescence intensities were measured using emission and excitation emission wavelengths of 470 and 390 nm separately. Later, we analyzed surface hydrophobicity index from the slope of the relative fluorescence intensity as a result of sample content plot using linear regression analysis.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-2500, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized for fluorescence spectroscopy, as described by Pan et al. (2019), after minor adaptations. Conjugates were diluted to 1 mg/mL using distilled water. Emission and excitation slit widths were set to 3 nm and 5 nm, spectively. The conjugate fluorescence emission spectra were measured within 310–400 nm, following excitation at 280 nm.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR)

FTIR spectra were acquired with the Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Tensor 27, Bruker Optics Inc., Karlsruhe, Germany). To prepare samples 1.5 mg of freeze-dried hydrolysates and conjugates with 198.5 mg potassium bromide (KBr) and subsequently compressed into pellets at a pressure of 25 MPa. Spectral scans were performed from 4000 cm −1 to 400 cm −1, averaging 64 scans. Potassium bromide was used as the standard reference.

Determination of polyphenol bound equivalents

The measurement of polyphenol bound equivalents was conducted according to You et al. (2014), with slight modifications. Sample solution (0.5 mL, 1 mg/mL) was added to Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (2.5 mL, 10 N). The mixture was shaken and then leave it in the dark at room temperature for 2 min. Next, Na2CO3 solution (2 mL,7.5%, w/v) was added to the mixture and react for another 2 h in the dark. CGA standard solutions were used to plot a standard curve of absorbance versus concentration. The absorbance was measured by UV spectrophotometer at 765 nm, the Na2CO3 solution was treated in the same way and used as a blank.

Covalent conjugate functional properties

Thermal stability analysis

The DSC-60 thermal analysis system (TA Instruments, America) was employed to analyze thermal stability following depictions by Pan et al. (2022) after slight adaptations. Briefly, approximately 1.8 mg of each sample was accurately weighed into the aluminum pan was hermetically sealed with a perforated aluminum lid. Subsequently, these conjugate samples underwent heating from 30 °C at the 10 °C/min heating rate to 140 °C at the 30 mL/min continuous nitrogen flow, with the empty aluminum pan being the reference. The Universal Analysis software was utilized for calculating peak denaturation temperature from each thermogram.

DPPH radical scavenging activity measurement

Antioxidant capacity, represented by DPPH radical scavenging activity, was evaluated according to depictions by Pan et al. (2022), after minor adaptations. Briefly, we thoroughly added the sample solution (25 μL, 2 mg/mL) in freshly prepared DPPH solution (975 μL, 1.75 × 10−4 mol/L in ethanol). The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min at ambient temperature, after which the absorbance at 515 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer to quantify residual DPPH radicals. Distilled water was used as a blank control, and additional controls comprising samples mixed with anhydrous ethanol were included. The Trolox calibration curve was employed for calculating the scavenging capacity, which was represented by μmol Trolox equivalents (TE)/mg of sample.

ABTS radical scavenging activity determination

The slightly adapted approach by Pan et al. (2022) was adopted for assessing ABTS radical scavenging activity. To prepare ABTS stock solution, ABTS (10 mg) was added into potassium persulfate solution (2.6 mL, 2.45 mol/L), and later incubated for 12 h away from light facilitate the formation of radicals. A total of 50 μL of the conjugate samples (2 mg/mL) were combined with ABTS solution (950 μL), prior to 6 min of incubation at RT. Absorbance values were read at 734 nm, with distilled water being blank control. The Trolox standard calibration curve was utilized to quantify ABTS radical scavenging activity. The results were represented by μmol Trolox equivalent (TE)/mg of sample.

Emulsifying activity measurement

To evaluate the emulsifying properties, the freeze-dried hydrolysate or conjugate sample (30 mg) was dissolved into distilled water (15 mL) to yield the 2 mg/mL solution. Then, we combined this aqueous sample solution was then combined with soybean oil at a volumetric ratio of 3:1 and homogenized for a 2 min duration 12,000 rpm, emulsion aliquots (100 μL) were collected at the container bottom at 0 and 10 min and later added into SDS solution (10 mL, 0.1% w/w). Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Origin 8.5 software was used for graphing and differences between groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Role of covalent modification with CGA in SMPH physicochemical properties

Color determination

Data encompass L*, a*, and b* values, where the L* stands for brightness and values of 0 and 100 indicate black and white, respectively. The a* and b* values denote chromaticity; the positive a* is denoted in red, with increasing absolute values indicating a deeper red hue, and the negative a* is denoted in green, with increasing absolute values indicating a deeper green hue. Similarly, a positive b* value indicates yellow, with increasing absolute values indicating a deeper yellow hue, whereas a negative b* value indicates blue, with increasing absolute values indicating a deeper blue hue.

Initially, at low CGA content, the covalent compounds were lighter in color, with L* values exceeding 74, and were pale yellow or white. However, a slight green hue appeared at concentration of 0.2 mg/mL CGA, with an a* value of −0.70 ± 0.05, intensifying to −1.32 ± 0.22 at a CGA concentration of 1.0 mg/mL (Table 1). Increasing CGA concentrations significantly altered the L*, a*, and b* levels. The L* values decreased depending on increasing CGA concentration, suggesting the severe greening of the protein hydrolysate due to deeper covalent binding of CGA to the sunflower seed protein hydrolysate. This was consistent with previous reports that covalently modified sunflower seed proteins exhibit a similar green color at a molar ratio of CGAto protein of 1:1 and above (Jia et al., 2022; Karefyllakis et al., 2018). Hydrolysates of covalently modified proteins with CGA concentrations within 0.05–0.1 mg/mL showed a pale yellow color with good organoleptic properties.

Changes in the free amino, thiol groups, tyrosine contents and polyphenol bound equivalent of SMPH conjugates

Covalent modification via radical grafting involves polyphenol binding to free amino or thiol groups on amino acid side chains, reducing the availability of these functional groups. From Table 2, the free amino acid level of the protein hydrolysate decreased significantly with increasing CGA content. At 1.0 mg/mL CGA, covalent interactions reduced free amino acid level from 207.92 ± 6.58 nmol/mg to 24.39 ± 6.94 nmol/mg, indicating that CGA binds to sunflower meal hydrolysate through covalent interactions. This reduction was consistent with previous studies in which a similar reduction of free amino groups occurs in lactoferrin-polyphenol covalent interaction (Liu et al., 2016). Additionally; Table 2 indicated that covalent conjugation resulted in a lower free thiol group content than that in the control hydrolysates. The lowest thiol group content (6.85 ± 0.03 nmol/mg) was observed in conjugates with the highest CGA concentration. The tyrosine group content decreased from 14.29 ± 0.06 μg/mg (control) to 10.91 ± 0.04 μg/mg at a CGA concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. This might be attributed to the fact that CGA cross-linked with thiol groups in the hydrolysate of the protein meal; and that the interaction with the thiol groups was enhanced as the concentration of CGA increased; significantly reducing hydrolysate level. Previous studies corroborated these findings; Ali (2019) reported substantial decreases in free amino; thiol; and tryptophan content upon covalent interactions between whey protein isolates and Rosmarinus polyphenol. Similarly; the rice protein hydrolysates with CGA covalent interaction also resulted in reduced free amino acid; thiol; and tyrosine levels (Pan et al., 2022).

Polyphenol-bound equivalents serve as indicators for evaluating the impact of different preparation methods on protein hydrolysate-polyphenol interactions. Table 2 demonstrated that the polyphenol binding apparently elevated (p < 0.05) depending on increasing CGA concentrations in the SMPH-CGA conjugates. The maximum polyphenol-bound equivalent (74.67 ± 0.78 μg/mg hydrolysate) was achieved at a CGA concentration of 1.0 mg/mL, confirming enhanced covalent binding at higher CGA concentrations. Similar results were reported by Zhang et al. (2024), who observed increased polyphenol-bound equivalents with elevated proanthocyanidin concentrations in soybean meal hydrolysate-proanthocyanidin conjugates.

Function of covalent modification of CGA in SMPH structure

Particle size analysis

Fig. 1A showed particle size of the SMPH and SMPH-CGA complexes. Their particle size increased significantly with increasing CGA concentration, reaching a maximum at 1.0 mg/mL (p < 0.05). This might be due to that CGA addition induced conformational changes in the SMPH, resulting in an open internal structure. This structural modification likely enhanced the exposure of the binding sites on SMPH, facilitating interaction with CGA. At lower CGA concentrations, limited SMPH-CGA interactions occurred. However, increasing the CGA concentration promoted extensive crosslinking between the SMPH and CGA molecules, leading to the formation of larger aggregates. Such trend showed positive relation to the polyphenol-bound equivalents.

Particle size (A) and TEM images (B) of SMPH-CGA conjugates with different CGA concentrations (0.05–1.0 mg/mL).

The morphological characteristics of the SMPH and SMPH-CGA complexes were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1B). SMPH alone was uniformly dispersed as smaller particles without notable aggregation. In contrast, at higher CGA concentrations, SMPH and CGA may interact through enhanced hydrophobicity and hydrogen bonding, allowing intermolecular aggregation of the.

protein hydrolysate to form a more stable structure, which leads to an increase in its.

particle size. It had been similarly demonstrated in previous studies that catechin.

facilitated intermolecular bridging and promoted polymerization within soy protein isolate molecules, resulting in pronounced aggregation and forming the distinct network structure (Dai et al., 2022; Jakobek, 2015). This aggregation enhanced structural stability. The TEM results were in high agreement with the data on particle size distribution, polyphenol binding equivalents, where the maximum particle size (Fig. 1A) and polyphenol binding equivalents (Table 2) were recorded at a CGA concentration of 1.0 mg/mL.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy conducted for evaluating structural alterations of the covalent conjugates. From Fig. 2A, the control SMPH showed prominent absorption bands at 3294 cm−1 (associated with N—H stretching and hydrogen bonding), 1651 cm−1 (amide I, corresponding to C—O stretching coupled with hydrogen bonding involving COO-), and 1541 cm−1 (amide II, arising from C—N stretching combined with N—H bending vibrations). Following the incorporation of CGA, the peak of SMPH initially observed at 3294 cm−1 shifted to 3292 cm−1 because hydrogen bonds involving aliphatic and aromatic O—H groups were formed. The addition of a large amount of CGA combined with protein hydrolysate introduces numerous hydroxyl groups, and this on the one hand induced the exposure of side chain groups in the protein structure, and on the other hand conferred more hydroxyl groups to the complex, which induced a blue shift here. Similar blue shifts had been observed in soybean meal hydrolysate–proanthocyanidin complexes, associated with increased O—H groups introduced by proanthocyanidin (Zhang et al., 2024). Additionally; a characteristic peak observed at 2964 cm−1 on the SMPH spectra shifted to 2962 cm−1 on the SMPH-CGA complex spectra; representing the C—H vibrations of the CH3 and CH2 groups within SMPH (Wen et al., 2023); further supporting the hydrophobic interactions between SMPH and CGA. Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Notably, intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy has been frequently employed for elucidating the interactions between protein hydrolysates and phenolic compounds. As shown in Fig. 2C, increasing concentrations of CGA led to a gradual reduction of SMPH fluorescence intensity, indicating that aromatic amino acids, specifically tryptophan and tyrosine residues, participated in covalent interactions with CGA (Pan et al., 2022). In addition, there was a significant redshift in the emission peak within 337 nm to 342 nm. This redshift usually indicated that the tyrosine residues became more hydrophilic by exposure to the solvent microenvironment, which means the protein structure unfolding (Pan, Gao and Ao, 2005). Such finding consistent with the results of the FTIR spectroscopy analysis (Section 3.2.2), and also conformed to alteration of tyrosine content, as shown in Table 2, where free tyrosine decreased with increasing interaction of CGA with SMPH, resulting in a redshift in the fluorescence spectra of the conjugate. The interaction of CGA with SMPH resulted in a fluorescence burst and redshift of the complex.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity is a critical property that reflects conformational and interaction changes in proteins and their hydrolysates, such as peptides. As shown in Table 3, the surface hydrophobicity consistently declined as the CGA concentration increased. This phenomenon indicates conformational changes and partial unfolding of the SMPH molecules upon their interaction with CGA. Similar outcomes have been reported in previous studies, where polyphenol conjugation significantly reduced the surface hydrophobicity of hemp protein isolates (Liu et al., 2023). Similarly; surface hydrophobicity decreased in proanthocyanidin conjugation modified soybean protein isolate hydrolysates (Zhang et al., 2024). This reduction was mainly due to the large amount of covalent binding between protein molecules and CGA; leading to the re-embedding or shielding of some hydrophobic groups; which in turn reduced the binding affinity of the fluorescent probes; and thus the overall surface hydrophobicity. Dai et al. (2023) also found that as the catechin concentration increased, the surface hydrophobicity of the compound formed with soybean protein isolate decreased, the addition of polyphenols may lead to exposure of hydrophobic groups and introduction of new hydrophilic groups (hydroxyl and carboxyl groups). Furthermore, it might also be due to the aggregation of proteins caused by polyphenols, which leads to a decrease in hydrophobicity.

Function of covalent modification of CGA in SMPH thermal stability

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) directly measures thermal transitions in hydrolysates during temperature variations, making it an effective evaluation tool. The DSC thermograms of the SMPH and SMPH-CGA conjugates can be observed from Fig. 3A. The oneset and peak denaturation temperature (Td) was quite similar for the native SMPH and all the conjugated hydrolysate samples (55.31–57.42 °C and 60.50–62.31 °C, respectively). The results showed that the hydrolysate conjugation by CGA had little impact on the oneset and peak Td of the hydrolysate. The denaturation enthalpy (ΔH) of SMPH was measured at 2.24 ± 1.02 J/g hydrolysate. Conjugation with lower CGA contents (0.05 mg/mL and 0.1 mg/mL) led to the slight elevation in ΔH values, indicating modest structural stabilization induced by covalent bonding. However, ΔH decreased upon further increases in CGA contents (from 0.2 mg/mL to 1.0 mg/mL), although a slight increase in the onset Td was concurrently observed. These findings were in line with the pronounced secondary structural modifications documented in the FTIR analysis (Fig. 2A). Similar enhancements in thermal stability were reported for sunflower protein covalently modified with CGA, where Td increased from 92.6 °C to 94.6 °C (Jia et al., 2022). The results indicated that the unfold and extension of the secondary structures were induced by covalent conjugation, however, the stability and emulsifying properties of SMPH-CGA conjugates were improved.

Effects of covalent modification of CGA on the in vitro antioxidant activity of SMPH

Antioxidant capacity was assessed using the ABTS·+and DPPH· radical scavenging assays. From Fig. 3B, all SMPH-CGA conjugates exhibited significantly enhanced antioxidant activity compared to native SMPH (p < 0.05). This improvement is associated with the increased hydroxyl (OH) groups introduced through CGA grafting onto SMPH, which mirrors the antioxidant enhancement observed previously in soybean meal hydrolysates combined with proanthocyanidins (Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore; antioxidant activity was positively correlated with CGA concentration. Specifically; conjugates prepared with 1.0 mg/mL CGA displayed the highest scavenging activities against ABTS·+and DPPH· radicals (54.84 ± 3.87 and 104.57 ± 1.06 μmol Trolox equivalent (TE)/g sample; respectively). This observation could be explained by the elevated α-helix and random coil levels observed in the SMPH-CGA conjugates (Fig. 2B); facilitating flexible and unfolded structures that expose antioxidant amino acids more effectively to interact with free radicals (Zhang et al., 2024).

Function of covalent modification of CGA in SMPH emulsifying activity

As shown in Fig. 3C, emulsifying ability index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) of SMPH increased (P < 0.05) after covalent conjugation. Similarly, enhancements in EAI and ESI had been reported with a combination of soybean meal hydrolysate and proanthocyanidin (Zhang et al., 2024). Specifically; the SMPH-CGA conjugates demonstrated the highest EAI (41.65 ± 0.29 m2/g) at 1.0 mg/mL CGA; primarily attributed to an increased in random coil structure and pronounced protein unfolding. The covalent complexes formed by CGA with proteins have relatively more disordered structures; allowing them to exhibit better adsorption potential at oil-water interfaces. Furthermore; CGA potentially reduced surface hydrophobicity (Table 3) of protein-coated droplets through interacting with nonpolar groups exposed; thereby decreasing inter-droplet hydrophobic attraction (Pi et al., 2023). This interaction likely contributed to the observed enhancement in the ESI of the SMPH-CGA conjugates. The conjugates exhibited the highest ESI value (92 ± 2.31%) at a CGA concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. This also increases the protein's affinity for the oil/water interface; thereby enhancing the protein's emulsifying activity. This maybe because the covalent modification of CGA in appropriate amounts with the enzymatic hydrolysates causes the unfolding of protein structure; leading to the exposure of many aromatic residues. These findings align with previous research on soybean meal hydrolysates; where ESI initially increased and subsequently declined with increasing proanthocyanidin concentrations (Zhang et al., 2024).

Conclusion

The present work analyzed the covalent complex structure and antioxidant property between SMPH and CGA across a range of doses (0–1.0 mg/mL). The covalent reaction between CGA and the nucleophilic amino acid side chains in SMPH elevated random coils and unfolded protein structures contents. Consequently, there were numerous hydrophobic groups exposed under the hydrophilic condition, resulting in a significant reduction in surface hydrophobicity. As CGA concentrations increased, polyphenol-binding equivalent number, particle size, and potential in complexes all remarkably elevated. The covalently modified samples exhibited color changes at CGA concentrations of 0.2 mg/mL and above. The conjugate, formed with 74.67 ± 0.78 μg/mg hydrolysate of polyphenol binding, demonstrated enhanced antioxidant capacity and superior emulsifying properties in comparison with other CGA concentrations. Such findings provide valuable insights into the covalent interactions of protein hydrolysates with polyphenols, offering the theoretical foundation for the potential applying of SMPH-CGA complexes as functional food additives and high-quality low price protein source in the food industry.
