Built-in tandem catalysis in hierarchical pores for efficient vinyl chloride production
Authors: Bolin Wang, Xingyun Li, Yuxue Yue, Shuheng Tian, Ruoting Liu, Haifeng Zhang, Ding Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTandem catalysis over disparate active sites is frequently designed to promote the catalytic efficiency for industrial reactions. Herein, a built-in tandem system in a single hierarchical porous catalyst is constructed for vinyl chloride production from ethylene dichloride and acetylene cascade. Using a pore-selective filling strategy, Ru, CuRu, and AuRu components are sequentially confined in the macro-, meso- and micropores of carbon material. Mechanism study shows that ethylene-dichloride dehydrochlorination occurs on macropore Ru sites, generating vinyl chloride. H and Cl radicals react with acetylene on mesopore CuRu sites, and the residual acetylene is hydrochlorinated over micropore AuRu sites. The stronger acetylene adsorption of CuRu and AuRu in a VCM-rich atmosphere enables acetylene conversion near the thermodynamic limit and stable operation for 1200 h on stream. This built-in tandem catalysis in hierarchical pores provides a general paradigm for cascade reactions in a single porous catalyst.Subject terms: Catalysis, Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalyst synthesis

Introduction

Poly-vinyl chloride (PVC) has become the third-largest synthetic polymer with a worldwide production of over 30 million tons per year1. The vinyl chloride monomer (VCM) can be industrially produced by the ethylene oxychlorination reaction2, which, however, relies heavily on oil-derived feedstocks. For coal-rich countries, acetylene hydrochlorination is the preferred route for VCM synthesis3–8, but its large-scale application is still hampered by the use of toxic mercury chloride (HgCl2/C) catalysts9–11. Recently, ethylene-dichloride (EDC) and acetylene cascade (EAC) to produce VCM12–15 (CH2ClCH2Cl + C2H2 = 2CH2CHCl, ΔH0 = –29 kJ mol−1) has attracted increasing attention. EAC combines the oil- and coal-derived feedstocks, which can alleviate energy constraints and avoid the use of HgCl2/C catalysts. Meanwhile, the EAC process produces stoichiometric VCM, thus saving the tedious product purification. Classical EAC is composed of two distinct steps involving the endothermic decomposition of EDC (step 1: CH2ClCH2Cl = CH2CHCl + HCl, ΔH0 = 69 kJ mol−1) and the following exothermic acetylene hydrochlorination (step 2: C2H2 + HCl = CH2CHCl, ΔH0 = – 98 kJ mol−1). The opposite thermal demands make it challenging to coordinate the two steps (steps 1–2) on a single active site. Moreover, the product VCM shares a similar π-conjugated structure with acetylene15,16, leading to competitive adsorption on the same active site. This makes it difficult to achieve a complete conversion of acetylene even at an ultra-low space velocity. Recent density functional theory (DFT) works have also demonstrated that the adsorption of VCM and related small molecules is highly sensitive to surface composition and electronic structure17–19. Therefore, it will be rational to construct a tandem catalytic system by guiding the sequential EDC decomposition and acetylene hydrochlorination reactions over different active sites.

Process engineering using two-stage cascaded reactors with distinct catalysts has been practiced in VCM manufacture20,21. The catalyst loaded in the second reactor is responsible for the continuous conversion of the unreacted acetylene from the first reactor. Researchers have also explored an alternative stacked catalyst-bed tandem system with a C3N4/AC catalyst layered on top of an Au/C3N4/AC catalyst20, which could effectively separate the EDC dehydrochlorination and acetylene hydrochlorination processes. Despite the above endeavors, the multiscale tandem configuration still cannot deliver satisfactory VCM productivity, which is hindered by the inefficient spatiotemporal transfer of the reaction intermediates (e.g., short-lived Cl and H radicals derived from step 1). Meanwhile, precisely matching the desired catalyst functions with the varying reactant composition remains challenging.

Herein, the hierarchical porous carbon was used as a catalytic reactor, wherein the reactants promptly flow within the interconnected channels, cascading from macropores to mesopores and further to micropores. A novel built-in tandem catalysis within hierarchical pores was orchestrated, in which specific active components located in macropores, mesopores, and micropores, synergistically catalyze the EAC reaction. By screening a variety of components, Ru was selected to occupy the macropores, being responsible for catalyzing the EDC decomposition. CuRu and AuRu, which possess higher acetylene adsorption capability in a VCM-enriched atmosphere, were confined in the mesopores and micropores, respectively, where they drive the acetylene hydrochlorination reaction. A strategic, selective pore-filling strategy was successfully implemented to enable the precise injection of Ru, CuRu, and AuRu into macropores, mesopores, and micropores, selectively (Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst). Consequently, the catalyst demonstrated excellent catalytic activity for EAC, with the acetylene conversion achieving the thermodynamic limit. The catalyst showed robust stability during the 1200 h reaction time. With systematic characterizations and DFT calculations, the built-in tandem catalysis mechanism in hierarchical pores was elucidated. We believe the advanced tandem catalysis system developed in this work could offer a universal platform for catalyzing EAC and other significant cascade reactions.

Results and discussion

Catalyst selection for EDC and acetylene cascade

EAC was performed in a fixed-bed micro-reactor (Supplementary Fig. 1), which integrates endothermic EDC dehydrochlorination and the following exothermic acetylene hydrochlorination (Supplementary Discussion 2.1: Supplementary Figs. 2, 3 and Supplementary Table 1), with acetylene activation having been proved to be the rate-determining step14 (Supplementary Discussion 2.2: Supplementary Figs. 4–9). As previously reported1,2,13,15, N, P doping in carbon has been shown to be activated in promoting dehydrochlorination (Supplementary Table 2) and acetylene hydrochlorination21 (Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, herein, N, P-doped porous carbon (NPC) was engineered by carbonization of ionic-liquids, which enabled simultaneous heteroatom doping and hierarchical pore formation through nanoscale ion-cluster templating22. By independently measuring the EAC performance over the upper (bed-I), middle (bed-II), and bottom (bed-III) catalyst beds of NPC (Fig. 1-a1), it is interesting to observe that ~80% acetylene is converted in the upper bed-I. However, further acetylene conversion is inhibited in the lower region (bed-II, -III, Fig. 1b). Given the exothermic nature of EAC (Supplementary Table 4), temperature variations may serve as powerful indicators of reactant conversion. By in-situ tracking the temperature at different positions of the catalyst bed during the reaction, it was observed that there is a significant temperature rise (>20 K) in bed-I (Supplementary Fig. 10), while negligible temperature variation is detected in bed-II, -III. The mass transfer limitations are completely excluded in EAC (Supplementary Discussion 2.3). This therefore proved the poor reactivity of the downstream catalysts, which is likely caused by the VCM accumulation23,24, hindering the adsorption of the reactants (acetylene and/or EDC). In this regard, transient response transformation experiments were performed to validate the influence of VCM on reactant adsorption. It is confirmed that EDC adsorption is hardly affected by VCM, but acetylene adsorption is significantly hindered in a VCM-enriched atmosphere (Supplementary Figs. 11–13). This is attributed to the competitive adsorption of VCM and acetylene1,4,10,16, due to their similar σ and π bonding. To this end, two cascade reactors are commonly applied in industry21 (Fig. 1-a2) with the second reactor responsible for the continuous conversion of the residual acetylene from the first reactor. Alternatively, another promising tactic is to build a tandem catalysis system, placing catalysts with stronger acetylene adsorption capabilities in the lower catalyst bed to guarantee a complete acetylene conversion under a high-VCM-concentration atmosphere.

To the best of our knowledge, Au25–29, Ru30–33 and Cu34–37 are the most explored components for activating acetylene, which are anticipated to be qualified to catalyze the EAC process. Thus, the acetylene conversion capability was first screened using various components, including Au, Ru, Cu, AuRu, CuRu, and AuCu, in VCM-enriched atmosphere (~20 vol% C2H2, 20 vol% EDC, and 60 vol% VCM in Fig. 1-a2). AuRu/NPC exhibits the highest activity (Fig. 1c), however, it deactivates the fastest under accelerated deactivation reaction conditions (10,000 h−1, Supplementary Fig. 14), likely due to coke deposition on bimetallic Au-based catalysts23. In comparison, Ru/NPC and CuRu/NPC show slightly inferior catalytic activity, while their durability is higher than that of AuRu/NPC. Consequently, AuRu/NPC, Ru/NPC, and CuRu/NPC are selected as the optimal catalyst components (detailed in Supplementary Fig. 14). To balance activity and durability, and optimize the cascaded process, a ternary-catalyst-bed (Fig. 1-a3) was constructed. A better trade-off between activity and durability is achieved from the optimized stacking mode of Ru/NPC(upper)-CuRu/NPC(middle)-AuRu/NPC(bottom) than from other combinations (Supplementary Fig. 14). The high performance likely arises from the progressively increased acetylene adsorption capacity from the upper to the bottom catalyst bed. This is confirmed by the highest C2H2 desorption temperature and desorption amount for AuRu/NPC in the C2H2-Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) graph in Fig. 1d, thereby ensuring the tandem conversion of acetylene in VCM-enriched atmosphere.

The above results sufficiently underline the efficacy of the tandem catalysis by constructing a ternary-catalyst bed. In a practical heterogeneous catalytic process, the feed flows from macropores → mesopores → micropores in the hierarchical pores of the catalysts38, which mimics the flow routes from top to bottom in the catalyst bed with differentiated reactant concentrations. This inspires us to establish the tandem catalysis system by confining selected metallic components of Ru, CuRu, and AuRu in macropores, mesopores, and micropores, separately, to judiciously match the catalyst adsorption capability with the gradient reactant concentrations, which has not been explored before (Fig. 1-a4).

Construction of built-in tandem catalysis in hierarchical pores

To confine specific metallic components in targeted pores, a pore-selective filling strategy was developed. Capillary forces are well known to drive solvent infiltration into porous materials during impregnation39,40, following the sequence: micropores → mesopores → macropores. Therefore, we can first selectively impregnate metallic components into micropores using an amount of metal solution equivalent to the micropore volume, followed by calcination to decompose the precursor, resulting in the target micropore-confined metal catalyst (M1micro). To load catalysts into mesopores, micropores are protected by pre-filling with a blocking agent (containing no metals). Then, another immiscible solvent carrying the second metal species is used to fill the mesopores via an equivalent-mesopore-volume impregnation method, followed by calcination to obtain M2meso-M1micro. By analogy, a similar procedure was repeated to build the final ternary M3macro-M2meso-M1micro tandem porous catalyst. Specifically, the previously screened Ru, CuRu, and AuRu were selected as catalysts to occupy the positions in the macropores, mesopores, and micropores, respectively, for the preparation of Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC, with NPC serving as support. Detailed pore filling characterizations and discussions were shown in Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Figs. 15–18 and Supplementary Tables 5, 6. These results indicate that the micropores are nearly saturated at ~20 vol% solvent filling, the mesopores are effectively blocked at ~50 vol%, and further filling progressively seals the macropores.

In brief, the ionic liquid [BMIM+-Tf2N-] (IL3, Supplementary Fig. 19) was applied as the sacrificial blocking agent for pore blocking, and another immiscible ionic liquid [BMIM+-Cl−] (IL4) was used as the solvent for dissolving metal precursors. As illustrated in the schematic diagram (Fig. 2a), the following steps are involved: (I) Micropore Filling. RuCl3 and HAuCl4 were dissolved in IL4 (volume equivalent to micropores) and dropwise loaded into the NPC support. The microporous filling was confirmed by the disappearance of micropores in the pore volume distribution (steps 1-2, Fig. 2c). Subsequent calcination at 587 K decomposed IL4 and metal precursors, anchoring the metal components in the micropores and releasing the microporous space (step 3, Fig. 2c); (II) Mesopore Filling. The micropores were pre-protected using IL3, which is immiscible with IL4. Then, RuCl3 and CuCl2 dissolved in IL4 were introduced into the mesopores, followed by calcination at 587 K to decompose IL4 and the metal precursors. IL3 remained intact in the micropores due to its higher decomposition temperature (Fig. 2b); (III) Macropore Filling. IL3 blocked the mesopores, and RuCl3 dissolved in IL4 was loaded into the macropores. The final calcination at 776 K decomposed IL4, IL3, and the metal precursors, yielding the Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst. The stepwise pore filling and release processes were validated by variations in pore volume distribution (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 6). As references, individual pore-confined catalysts (e.g., Rumacro/NPC) and dual-pore-confined catalysts (e.g., Rumacro-CuRumeso/NPC) were fabricated with similar procedures.

To further visualize the spatial distribution of metal species within the hierarchical pores, focused ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) was performed on Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC (Fig. 2d). A cross-section with a length of 19.75 μm and a milling depth of 6.11 μm was opened on a representative catalyst surface (Fig. 2-d1), where macropores, mesopores, and micropores are clearly observed, respectively. Elemental line-scan profiles across these regions (Fig. 2-d2) show that Ru gives a dominant signal in the macropore region, while Cu and Au are nearly undetectable, indicating that Ru species mainly occupy macropores. In the mesopore region, both Cu and Ru are detected with comparable intensity, proving that CuRu sites are confined in mesopores. In contrast, the micropore-rich region exhibits strong Au and Ru signals with negligible Cu, evidencing the preferential location of AuRu sites in micropores. The FIB-SEM results may offer direct evidence for the catalyst location, sufficiently proving the efficacy of the capillarity-driven and pore-selective filling strategy.

The atomic configurations of the metal species are explored by X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES, Supplementary Fig. 20) shows that the adsorption edge energies of Au, Ru, and Cu in the Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst are higher than those of the corresponding metallic foils, indicating their high valence features. The corresponding Fourier transforms (FT) are displayed in Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 21–23. Au species exhibited a single metal-neighboring shell peak (Fig. 2-e1) corresponding to Au-N41, with a bond distance of 1.99 Å (Supplementary Table 7). The absence of Au-Au intensity around 2.2 Å at the corresponding wavelet transform (WT, Figs. 2-f1) confirms the atomic states of Au. For Ru species, the scattering paths of Ru-N (2.00 Å)42 and Ru-Cu/Ru (2.56 Å)43,44 are detected (Fig. 2-e2). However, overlapping peak positions make it difficult to distinguish Ru-Ru and Ru-Cu coordination in the Ru-based WT spectra (Fig. 2-f2). The Ru-Cu coordination was further accurately identified from mesoporous Cu-based R-space, where Cu-Ru and Cu-N bonds were observed (Fig. 2-e3). Two intensity maxima at approximately 1.9 Å and 2.6 Å in the WT spectra confirmed the presence of Cu-N21 and Cu-Ru43 bonds (Fig. 2-f3). Noting that the coordination number of Ru-Cu/Ru in the Ru-based fitting result is 5.9 ± 0.2 (Supplementary Table 7), and 1.2 ± 0.3 for the Cu-Ru coordination in Cu-based fitting, this implies the existence of surplus Ru-Ru coordination in macropores and micropores. Due to the disordered organization in hierarchical pores, it is challenging to directly distinguish the metallic dispersion for Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC by HAADF-STEM characterization (Supplementary Fig. 24). To address this, the reference single pore-confined catalysts (Rumacro/NPC, CuRumeso/NPC, and AuRumicro/NPC) were studied. HAADF-STEM and atomic structure simulations revealed that Ru exists as atomic clusters in macropores (Ru ACs, Fig. 2-g, h1). Cu/Ru presents as diatomic single-atoms in mesopores (Cu/Ru SAs, Fig. 2-g, h2). And Au single-atoms and Ru atomic clusters are confined in micropores (Au SAs-Ru ACs, Fig. 2-g, h3). These metallic sites are thermodynamically stable (Supplementary Fig. 25) and align well with the XAS analysis. The pore-specific configurations can be rationalized by considering the metal–metal cohesive energies45. Ru exhibits a strong intrinsic tendency to aggregate (Ec: –7.11 eV, Supplementary Table 8), which dominates over coordination stabilization even in micropores, leading to nanocluster formation in both macro- and micropores. In contrast, Au shows weak self-cohesion (–3.52 eV) but strong affinity toward Au-N coordination41, thus preferentially stabilizing as isolated atoms. In mesopores, the high Cu/Ru heteronuclear cohesive energy (–7.49 eV), along with adequate spatial allowance, favors the formation of thermodynamically stable diatomic sites over separate nucleation. These trends align with the observed configurations (Fig. 2g, h) and confirm that the final structures arise from thermodynamic control rather than random distribution. Once these pore-specific sites are thermodynamically established, they remain spatially immobilized during calcination. The metal precursors decompose and anchor in solid form, and the available thermal energy (0.05–0.07 eV at 587–776 K) is far below the migration barriers (0.2–0.9 eV, Supplementary Fig. 25) required for inter-pore relocation. Overall, the above configurations in distinct pores are determined by cross-validating four orthogonal techniques of FIB-SEM, XAS coordination environments, reference single-pore configurations, and energetics, which collectively exclude alternative scenarios and support a structurally differentiated pore-specific arrangement.

Catalytic performance evaluation

Bader charge calculations, and DOS analysis revealed that the designed metallic components exhibit a strong capacity to capture acetylene in VCM-rich atmosphere (detailed in Supplementary Figs. 26–36 and Supplementary Tables 9, 10), providing a theoretical basis for acetylene conversion under high VCM concentrations. As shown in Fig. 3a, the Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst displays the highest reaction rate, approximately 1.2 times that of Rumacro-CuRumeso/NPC, and ~1.4 times that of Rumacro/NPC, highlighting the efficacy of the built-in tandem porous catalysis. Figure 3b shows the adsorption energy difference, ΔEads = Eads(C2H2)−Eads(VCM), over Ru, CuRu and AuRu sites are –0.73, –0.91, and –1.52 eV, respectively, indicating that the acetylene adsorption capacity in VCM-enriched atmosphere follows AuRu > CuRu Ru, which is consistent with the experimental results (Fig. 1d). This strongly supports the strategy of optimally orchestrating AuRu in micropores, CuRu in mesopores, and Ru in macropores to achieve tandem catalysis, consistent with the confirmed stacking order in the ternary-bed evaluation system (Figs. 1-a4).

To evaluate the intrinsic activity, the influence of the catalyst-bed length on reactivity was investigated. Merely elongating the bed of the Rumacro/NPC catalyst did not increase the acetylene conversion beyond 92% (Fig. 3c), indicating its limited ability to sustain acetylene capture in a VCM-enriched environment. By introducing mesopore-confined CuRu (Rumacro-CuRumeso/NPC), a promoted acetylene conversion of ~98.2% is achieved. The ternary catalyst (Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC) achieved the highest acetylene conversion of 99.3%, approaching the thermodynamic limit of 99.8% (Supplementary Discussion 2.1). Notably, the catalyst bed length required to reach the highest acetylene conversion is significantly shortened for Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC (indicated as red shading, Fig. 3c), confirming the effectiveness of the built-in tandem catalysis in hierarchical pores for enhancing the reactant adsorption and activation. Interestingly, despite extending the catalyst bed length, the conversion over Rumacro/NPC and Rumacro-CuRumeso/NPC systems remained significantly below the thermodynamic limit. This suggests that, beyond intrinsic activity differences, these catalysts may suffer from internal porous diffusion effects, which restrict the effective utilization of downstream active sites in VCM-rich environments. In such cases, stronger C2H2 adsorption alone (Fig. 3b) is insufficient to sustain conversion unless mass transport toward active centers is efficiently maintained. The hierarchical Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC architecture addresses this limitation by spatially segmenting active sites. This gradient-resolved configuration minimizes internal diffusion resistance and ensures reactant progression aligns with site-specific chemical demands, thereby achieving near-equilibrium conversion.

Besides the catalytic activity, stability is another key criterion for practical application. The Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst exhibits superior durability over 500 h on stream at a higher space velocity of 320 h-1 (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 11). In contrast, the most active AuRu component of the AuRumacro-AuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC shows lower durability, likely due to the reported carbon deposition23, as evidenced by its sharply decreased specific surface area (Supplementary Fig. 37). For the AuRumacro-AuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst, more energy is required to release the generated VCM compared with the other two catalytic systems (embedded in Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 38, 39). This indicates that VCM tends to cover the AuRu-based catalyst surface, resulting in pore blockage. Moreover, the Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst shows higher activity than the ternary-bed system composed of [Rumacro/NPC + CuRumeso/NPC + AuRumicro/NPC], highlighting the facilitated tandem contribution within hierarchical pores (Fig. 3d). This is consistent with the above discussion on the length of the catalyst bed (Fig. 3c). The enhanced stability and activity of the built-in Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst are not solely due to compositional synergy, but also arise from a structural advantage in coordinating mass transport and sequential reactivity. Unlike conventional tandem beds with macroscopic interfaces, the hierarchical architecture embeds spatially ordered metal sites within a single porous matrix, minimizing diffusion distances and facilitating timely intermediate turnover. This configuration mitigates VCM accumulation and active site blockage, thus reducing carbon deposition and deactivation risks. These findings provide a structural-functional integration beyond simple material optimization.

Tandem catalysis mechanism

The gaseous flow sequence in porous materials typically follows the order of macropores, mesopores, and micropores. This sequence is fundamentally governed by pore size-dependent flow mechanisms, with larger pores offering lower resistance and dominant viscous flow, while smaller pores exhibit Knudsen diffusion46,47. For tandem catalysis within hierarchical pores, macropore-confined Ru sites are served as the starting sites for the dehydrochlorination of EDC (EDC → VCM + HCl) due to its highest EDC adsorption capacity than other porous sites (Supplementary Fig. 40), which is evidenced by the generated HCl signal (Supplementary Fig. 41). Meanwhile, the formation of Cl* radicals was directly verified by electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements (Fig. 4b inset), which shows characteristic radical signals as a function of time (Supplementary Fig. 42), consistent with reported Cl* features48,49. These results indicate partial HCl dissociation (HCl → Cl* + H*). Based on experimental results, the desorption of VCM and HCl (black lines, steps a–c in Fig. 4a, b) and the generation of H* and Cl* were modeled (steps c-d for energies in Fig. 4a and diagrams in Fig. 4b), following the thermodynamic trend with a lowest activation energy of 0.27 eV (inset black histogram in Fig. 4a). The above reaction process is referred as the “EDC decomposition” process in subsequent discussion.

To study the probability of other pathways occurring at the Ru site, three additional thermodynamically unfavorable pathways were calculated: the one-step reaction of acetylene and EDC (C2H2 + EDC → 2VCM, yellow lines in Fig. 4a), acetylene hydrochlorination (C2H2 + HCl → VCM, blue lines in Fig. 4a), and the radical reaction pathway (C2H2 + Cl* + H* → VCM, red lines in Fig. 4a). Detailed reaction energies are listed in the under region of Fig. 4a (thermodynamically favorable), while their diagrams are shown in Supplementary Figs. 43–45. Among these routes, the minimum activation energy is the acetylene hydrochlorination pathway (0.51 eV, inset blue histogram in Fig. 4a), which is significantly higher than that of the proposed EDC decomposition process (inset black route, 0.27 eV). Therefore, both theoretical calculations and experimental results confirm that the EDC decomposition is the only feasible pathway over the Ru site. The reaction intermediates generated at the Ru site can migrate to other porous sites, potentially triggering synergistic effects. Compared with EDC, acetylene exhibits stronger adsorption on mesoporous CuRu and microporous AuRu (Supplementary Fig. 40). Obviously, EDC decomposition is hindered if the sites are preferentially adsorbed by acetylene. In addition, the conversion of EDC over the macroporous Ru site is 99.54%, and that of acetylene is 92% (Supplementary Fig. 46), which means that EDC was essentially completely converted at the Ru site, while the subsequent mesoporous and microporous sites are responsible for the residual acetylene conversion.

The extreme lack of EDC makes it impossible to trigger the one-step reaction of acetylene and EDC on CuRu and AuRu sites, supported by their extremely high activation energies (1.64, 2.51 eV, yellow lines/histograms in Fig. 4a). For the mesoporous CuRu site, the radical pathways exhibit the lowest energy barrier of 0.41 eV (red lines in Fig. 4a), which is lower than that of acetylene hydrochlorination pathway (0.45 eV) and one-step reaction of acetylene and EDC (1.64 eV). In contrast, acetylene hydrochlorination is favored over the AuRu site (blue lines in Fig. 4a), with an activation energy of 0.49 eV, lower than that of 1.20 eV for the radical pathways and 2.51 eV for the one-step reaction. These findings demonstrate that the lowest energy pathways catalyzed on mesoporous CuRu and microporous AuRu sites are radical reactions and acetylene hydrochlorination, respectively.

Importantly, the sequential activation along the macropore → mesopore → micropore is not arbitrary but thermodynamically enforced. Once EDC is fully reacted at macroporous Ru sites (99.54% conversion, Supplementary Fig. 46), its downstream concentration is nearly zero, making EDC-involving pathways on CuRu and AuRu sites both thermodynamically disallowed and kinetically prohibitive. DFT calculations reveal prohibitively high barriers for EAC on CuRu (1.64 eV) and AuRu (2.51 eV) sites, and no appreciable EDC adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 40), thereby excluding reversed or randomized reaction sequences, such as micropore → mesopore → macropore. Further kinetic analysis reveals that multiple reaction pathways coexist owing to the different reaction orders of the reactants (Supplementary Fig. 47). The superior performance of the three-pore Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC system over the two-pore configurations Rumacro-CuRumeso/NPC and Rumacro-AuRumicro/NPC (Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49) powerfully validates the feasibility of a tandem catalytic route through hierarchical pores. The guiding descriptor, ΔEads = Eads(C2H2)-Eads(VCM), effectively identifies the active site confinement within pores, ensuring the tandem conversion of acetylene in mesopores and micropores. These findings validate the directionality and pore-function assignment of the built-in tandem system. Therefore, optimal reaction pathways have been identified (Fig. 4b). Specifically, EDC first undergoes hydrogen chloride removal at the Ru site (steps a–c), followed by the generation of VCM and HCl (steps c–d). Part of the HCl is dissociated to H and Cl radicals (step d). These species migrate and engage in addition reactions with acetylene at the CuRu (steps e, f) and AuRu sites (steps i, j), ensuring minimal energy barriers for VCM production (steps g, h, k). At CuRu sites, acetylene reacts with radicals, while classical acetylene hydrochlorination occurs at AuRu sites.

In conventional tandem systems50, where catalytic sites are spatially separated across distinct catalyst beds and/or reactors, the transfer of reaction intermediates (e.g., short-lived intermediates such as H* and Cl*) is often hindered by long diffusion distances and interfacial mismatches. These constraints increase the likelihood of radical recombination or side reactions, leading to poor catalytic continuity, downstream deactivation, and local carbon accumulation. In contrast, the hierarchical porous architecture developed here integrates functionally matched active sites within a single catalyst, enabling rapid, nanometer-scale progression of reactive species. This structural proximity ensures efficient intermediate turnover while minimizing their undesired accumulation, thus effectively reducing surface blockage and coke formation. The resulting integration of pore-scale transport and site-specific reactivity accounts for the superior activity and long-term stability of the Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst demonstrated in Fig. 3d. In addition to mechanistic advantages, this design also offers expanding feasibility. The pore-selective filling strategy, relying on capillary infiltration and solvent immiscibility, is also experimentally validated on non-carbon porous matrices (e.g., hierarchical porous Al2O3, Supplementary Figs. 50–52). However, when the support is changed to Al2O3, an unsatisfactory catalytic performance is presented (Supplementary Fig. 53), mainly due to its irreplaceable function of carbon material as support for activating acetylene51. Furthermore, the lack of nitrogen-based metal coordination in Al2O3 fundamentally determines the different catalytic behavior. Thus, the non-carbon material confined tandem catalysts may be more suitable in other reactions, which will be explored in future work. As an attempt for the practical applications, an industrial side-line test using an ultra-low-loading Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC (total metal loading 0.1 wt%) sustained ~99.3% acetylene conversion and >99.5% VCM selectivity over ~1200 h (Supplementary Fig. 54). Ultra-low coke (2.36 wt%, Supplementary Fig. 55) was generated, demonstrating that the built-in tandem architecture is durable and scalable under realistic operating conditions. Together with the techno-economic evaluation in Supplementary Table 12, which shows a >16% reduction in catalyst-related cost per ton of VCM compared with the HgCl2/AC benchmark. These results underscore the practical feasibility of the hierarchical tandem system.

In summary, we have established a built-in tandem catalysis platform based on a hierarchical Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC architecture, in which Ru, CuRu, and AuRu are selectively confined in macropores, mesopores, and micropores via a capillarity-driven sequential pore-filling strategy. Guided by a differential adsorption-energy descriptor, ΔEads = Eads(C2H2) − Eads(VCM), this pore-resolved arrangement overcomes competitive adsorption in VCM-rich environments and drives acetylene conversion to near its thermodynamic limit, while maintaining >99.3% conversion over 1200 h on stream at metal loading down to 0.1 wt%. Mechanistic analysis reveals a catalytic pathway sequentially connected within pores, involving three steps: EDC dehydrochlorination, free radical reactions, and acetylene hydrochlorination. By integrating hierarchical mass transport, site-specific chemistry, and economical noble-metal usage within a single porous reactor, this strategy provides a general blueprint for translating multistep vinyl-chloride synthesis into single-catalyst tandem reactors.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

All metal-based catalysts were synthesized by a sequential pore-filling incipient wetness impregnation followed by programmed decomposition. Taking Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC as an example: Au and Ru precursors (HAuCl4, RuCl3) were dissolved in ionic liquid [BMIM+-Cl−] and introduced into the NPC micropores (1.0 wt% Au, 0.5 wt% Ru, loading matched to the derived micropore volume, see Supplementary Fig. 15), which was then decomposed at 587 K. The micropores were temporarily blocked with ionic liquid [BMIM+-Tf₂N−]. Subsequently, Cu and Ru precursors (CuCl2, RuCl3) were introduced into the mesopores (0.3 wt% Cu, 0.5 wt% Ru, matched to the mesopore volume) and decomposed at 587 K, followed by mesopore blocking with [BMIM+-Tf₂N−]. Finally, RuCl3 in [BMIM+-Cl−] was introduced into the macropores (0.5 wt% Ru, matched to the macropore volume) and calcined at 776 K, fully decomposing both ionic liquids and yielding the Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst. Further details on the catalyst synthesis (including the preparation of single- and dual-pore-confined references and the ultra-low-loading Rumacro-CuRumeso-AuRumicro/NPC catalyst, total metal loading 0.1 wt.%) and the preparation of the supports are provided in the Supplementary Experimental 1.1 Catalyst preparation.

Catalyst characterization

The pore structure of the materials was analyzed using nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K with a Micromeritics Tristar II 3020 apparatus and mercury porosimetry with a Quantachrome PM-3310 apparatus. Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) were performed with a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC instrument. Focused ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) was performed on a FEI Helios NanoLab 650 microscope. The atomic-resolution structure was characterized by a JEOL JEM-ARM 200 F aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy was operated on a Tecnai G2 F30 S-Twin. TPD was measured on an Omnistar GSD320 mass spectrometer. ESR spectroscopy was performed using a JES FA200 X-band ESR spectrometer. X-ray absorption fine structure was performed at the BL14W1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility.

Computational details

DFT calculations were performed utilizing the Vienna Ab initio simulation package (VASP-5.4) code. The Perdew-Becke-Ernzerhof functional was employed with inner electrons represented as projector augmented waves. The valence monoelectronic states expanded as plane waves with a cutoff kinetic energy of 450 eV. Dispersion contributions were introduced through the D3 approach. Relaxation was allowed in the top two layers and followed by 20 Å of vacuum. The k-points sampling was a Γ-centered 3 × 3 × 1 grid. A dipole correction was employed along the z direction. The climbing nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method was performed to confirm the transition state structures. In all cases, the optimization thresholds were 10−5 and 10−4 eV for electronic and ionic relaxations, respectively. The computational details are shown in the Supplementary Experimental 1.2 Computational details.

Catalytic evaluation

EAC was performed in a fixed-bed micro-reactor using EDC and acetylene (C2H2) as the reactants (Supplementary Fig. 1). Before the reaction, the reactor was swept by N2 at 573 K (unless stated otherwise) for 1 h to remove air and moisture. Then the gas was switched to the composition of EDC and C2H2 (molar ratio of EDC/C2H2 is 1:1). The outlet gas was washed by NaOH solution (2M, Sinopharm) to remove the unreacted HCl, and then analyzed by a gas chromatography (Fuli GC9790) with a hydrogen flame ionization detector. All evaluation procedures are detailed in the Supplementary Experimental 1.3 Catalyst evaluation.
