Trauma triggered catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia manifesting as cardiac arrest in a child: a case report
Authors: Ying Song, Yujia Wang, Jing Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is an inherited, stress-provoked arrhythmia that can precipitate life-threatening ventricular fibrillation, syncope, or sudden cardiac death. Although most CPVT cases arise from pathogenic variants in the cardiac ryanodine receptor gene (RYR2), data on trauma-triggered episodes in children remain scarce.Case presentationA previously healthy 18-month-old Chinese boy suffered cardiopulmonary arrest after falling from a chair. Introduction

Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a severe inherited arrhythmogenic syndrome that predominantly affects children and young adolescents. It is characterized by bidirectional or polymorphic ventricular arrhythmias, which commonly induce syncope during physical exertion or emotional stress, and may lead to sudden cardiac death. The ryanodine receptor 2 (RYR2) gene is one of the major disease-causing genes of CPVT. The RYR2 protein serves as a critical intracellular calcium channel, which primarily regulates calcium release and myocardial contraction. Established therapeutic strategies include β-blockade, flecainide, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement, and left cardiac sympathetic denervation. When syncope results in fall or minor trauma, CPVT may be incorrectly ascribed to accidental injury, delaying cardiac evaluation [1]. We describe an 18-month-old boy, at an unusually young age for CPVT presentation, who experienced cardiac arrest after falling from a chair; comprehensive genetic analysis identified a pathogenic RYR2 variant, confirming the diagnosis.

Case report

An 18-month-old Chinese boy fell from a chair while eating dinner at home, struck the back of his head on the floor, and was found unconscious by his mother on February 8, 2025. No choking episode or seizure was witnessed prior to the event. His mother found that the child had no spontaneous breathing and immediately administered cardio-pulmonary resuscitation (CPR) with rescue breaths at home. The medical emergency team arrived 10 min after initiation of CPR and identified that the child was in a state of asystole. At 11 months of age, he experienced an unexplained syncopal episode while playing. After being patted on the back for 3 min, he gradually regained consciousness. He did not seek medical attention at that time. Family history revealed that the child’s grandfather had experienced sudden cardiac death of unknown cause during his youth.

The child was rushed to the local hospital with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3 and no pupillary light reaction. Arterial blood gas assay showed a pH of 6.743, PCO2 30.9 mmHg, PO2 237 mmHg, lactates 22.0 mmol/L, and base excess − 29.9 mmol/L and K + 7.44 mmol/L. Emergency measures included endotracheal intubation, saline fluid resuscitation, intravenous adrenaline (0.01 mg/kg) and sodium bicarbonate. CPR was commenced for 30 min with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), but hemodynamics remained unstable. Therefore, the child was transferred to our hospital’s intensive care unit for advanced care. The resting 12 - lead electrocardiogram (ECG) disclosed no abnormalities, such as those indicative of long - QT syndrome or Brugada syndrome. Bedside focused assessment with sonography for trauma and transthoracic echocardiography examination were negative for any intraabdominal free fluid or pericardial effusion and suggested that the heart structure was normal. Cranial CT showed cerebral edema, along with chest CT findings of extensive bilateral opacities and consolidation predominantly in the posterior segments. Post-cardiac arrest care included neuroprotection with analgesia and sedation (fentanyl and midazolam continuous infusion), manage cardiovascular dysfunction, pulmonary protective ventilation, and targeted temperature management. Due to severe hypotension, the patient was treated with low-dose norepinephrine combined with epinephrine infusion. The blood pressure was improved, but non-sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia occurred. 24-hour Holter ambulatory monitoring revealed polymorphic ventricular premature beats and ventricular tachycardia (Fig. 1). He was put on VA-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) because of persistent tissue hypoperfusion (lactate, 11 mmol/L; oliguria) and refractory cardiogenic shock. Catecholamines were stopped once ECMO was started. During ECMO support, the patient’s blood pressure stabilized, with improved tissue perfusion and cardiac function, and no episodes of ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation were detected on ECG. ECMO was successfully discontinued after 3 days. However, during hospitalization, the patient experienced two episodes of ventricular fibrillation without an obvious trigger, leading to cardiac and respiratory arrest. Both episodes were successfully terminated with electrical defibrillation, restoring sinus rhythm. Given the potential link to hereditary arrhythmias, propranolol was initially administered at a dose of 2 mg per 8 h, and then increased to a dose of 5 mg per 8 h, which effectively prevented further episodes of ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation. The patient was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation on hospital day 17.

Neurological examination revealed Glasgow Coma Scale score was 7 (E2V1M4) and increasing muscle tension. The EEG showed a continuous diffuse θ wave type, and brain MRI showed the change of hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy. Holter ambulatory monitoring revealed sinus bradycardia with sinus arrhythmia, 7 atrial premature beats, intermittent ST-segment changes, and no ventricular arrhythmia. Therefore, the patient was transferred to the rehabilitation hospital for further treatment.

Whole-exome sequencing (Fig. 2) identified a heterozygous missense variant in the RYR2 gene: c.14471G > C (p.G4824A). Genetic screening of both parents and the elder brother confirmed that none of them harbored this variant, thereby confirming a de novo origin. The variant is not recorded in the gnomAD database and has not been reported in ClinVar, HGMD, or published literature, indicating it is a novel mutation. Based on the criteria of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines, this variant was classified as likely pathogenic.

After discharge, the propranolol dose was gradually increased to 2 mg/kg/day, currently at a total daily dose of 25 mg. During nearly one year of follow-up, the patient maintained good medication adherence and remained free of syncope and cardiac arrest.

Discussion

CPVT is an inherited arrhythmia characterized by catecholamine-mediated, sympathetic stimulation-induced polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. It typically manifests during childhood or adolescence, with a mean age of onset between 7 and 12 years, although its precise incidence remains undetermined. Patients with CPVT frequently develop bidirectional or polymorphic ventricular tachycardia triggered by adrenergic stimulation (e.g., exercise or emotional stress), which may lead to palpitations, syncope, convulsions, or even sudden cardiac death.

Diagnosis is challenging due to the absence of abnormalities on resting ECG and normal cardiac structure. According to the expert consensus recommendations [2], CPVT is diagnosed in individuals aged < 40 years with a structurally normal heart, normal ECG, and unexplained exercise or catecholamine-induced bidirectional VT or polymorphic ventricular premature beats or VT. Exercise stress testing (EST) or catecholamine provocation testing (CPT) is essential for documenting adrenergic ventricular ectopy/arrhythmia in CPVT. However, previous studies [3, 4] have demonstrated that EST and CPT have limitations of low sensitivity and poor reproducibility, with a certain rate of false negatives. To date, genes identified to be associated with CPVT include RYR2, CASQ2, TRDN, TECRL, CALM1, CALM2, CALM3 [5]. Among them, autosomal dominant mutations in the RYR2 gene represent the most common type of CPVT, accounting for approximately 60%-70% of cases [6]. For survivors of cardiac arrest, focused genetic testing for arrhythmia syndromes may contribute to a secure diagnosis. In this previously healthy child with a normal resting ECG, cardiac arrest occurred immediately after minor trauma. The underlying mechanism remains unclear but may involve adrenergic stimulation from emotional stress. A pathogenic RYR2 variant confirmed the diagnosis of CPVT, although EST could not be performed because of post-anoxic encephalopathy.

According to published literatures [7, 8], high risk factors for adverse cardiac events in patients with CPVT include male sex, young age at diagnosis, proband status, a history of prior cardiac arrest, and multiple genetic variants. In this case, the patient presented with several of these high-risk features, particularly symptom onset at 18 months of age, which is markedly earlier than the typical age of first presentation for CPVT. Although relevant clinical data remain limited due to the rarity of CPVT, the risk of fatal arrhythmia recurrence in such cases is substantial. Consequently, researchers are currently dedicated to developing risk stratification models to guide clinical management of patients after a diagnosis of CPVT [9].

The primary therapeutic objectives during episodes of CPVT are to control VAs, manage cardiogenic shock, and mitigate systemic ischemia-reperfusion injury, particularly neurological damage. The patients with ROSC after cardiac arrest are critically ill, and the treatment emphasizes the management of hemodynamics. CPVT patients with cardiac arrest have myocardial injury caused by cardiac arrest syndrome and CPVT-related VAs, which can lead to severe hemodynamic instability and cardiogenic shock deterioration. ECMO support can be considered in the acute phase. While reports on ECMO in pediatric CPVT are scarce [10, 11], this modality has been successfully used in cardiac arrest caused by CPVT, particularly when return of spontaneous circulation is achieved but severe ventricular dysfunction persists. Catecholaminergic agents (e.g. epinephrine, norepinephrine) are generally contraindicated during cardiac arrest or malignant VAs in the context of CPVT. Two published cases [10] documented recurrent ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation temporally linked to repeated epinephrine administration, further supporting the avoidance of adrenergic stimulation in this genotype-specific arrhythmia syndrome.

International consensus statement [2] advises that all patients with an established diagnosis of CPVT limit/avoid strenuous exercise, competitive sports, and emotionally stressful environments. Long-term management includes long-term Beta-blockers, flecainide, left cervical sympathetic denervation, and ICD. β-Blockers, preferably nadolol or propranolol, constitute first-line pharmacotherapy. When VAs or arrhythmic events remain incompletely controlled by β-blockade, current guidelines endorse flecainide as the first-line adjunctive agent. For patients with a history of sudden cardiac arrest or life-threatening arrhythmic events, ICD implantation is recommended even in the setting of combination antiarrhythmic therapy [12]. Although ICD were formerly considered a mainstay of CPVT management, recent evidence indicates that ICD implantation does not improve survival and is associated with a high risk of complications, including electrical storms and inappropriate shocks [13, 14]. Therefore, the decision to implant an ICD in children should be carefully weighed against the potential for severe device-related complications. In this case, the patient has been maintained on oral propranolol at 2 mg/kg/day with good adherence, and no malignant ventricular arrhythmic events have occurred during follow-up. Flecainide was not added because it is not commercially available in China. Regarding ICD implantation, although both epicardial and transvenous approaches were technically feasible, the parents declined due to concerns about lead dysfunction, inappropriate shocks, and procedural risks, given the patient’s extremely young age.

Conclusion

We experienced a healthy child resuscitated from sudden cardiac arrest during a relatively minor trauma who was diagnosed as CPVT and had a novel mutation c.14471G > C in the cardiac RYR2 gene. While CPVT is an unusual cause of sudden cardiac arrest in children and adolescents, clinicians should maintain a high suspicion for this disorder in cases of unexplained pediatric cardiac arrest or syncope. Timely diagnosis, facilitated by genetic testing, and preparedness for advanced circulatory support, including the rapid initiation of ECMO in refractory cases, are pivotal to improving outcomes.
