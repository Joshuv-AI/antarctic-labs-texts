Erianin is a therapeutic candidate for addressing neuroinflammation triggered by intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Shi-Wei Li, Hong-Cai Wang, Mao-Song Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroinflammation is a common consequence of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), leading to neurological impairments. Research indicates that the gut microbiome can influence neuroinflammatory responses. Erianin, is a potential therapeutic agent in the treatment of inflammation. Yet, the specific impact of erianin on ICH-induced inflammation and its interaction with the gut microbiome remain areas of ongoing investigation.MethodsICH mouse model was established and treated with erianin. Neurobehavioral tests, brain water content, immunofluorescence, western blotting, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining were performed to measure the neurological defects and neuroinflammation and neuron apoptosis. Immunofluorescent staining and western blotting assay were performed to assess the activation states of microglia and inflammation. The quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and FITC-dextran assays were utilized to measure the intestinal barrier integrity. The composition of the gut microbiota was analyzed by sequencing the 16 S rRNA extracted from fecal samples.ResultsAdministration of Erianin notably decreased inflammation in the brain and improved neurological function in ICH mice by inhibiting the proinflammatory activation of microglia. Additionally, Erianin bolstered intestinal barrier integrity, evidenced by decreased levels of lipopolysaccharide-binding protein. Furthermore, treatment with Erianin led to observable shifts in the gut microbiota. Notably, the activation of the ERK signaling pathway was found to counteract the neuroprotective effects of Erianin following ICH.ConclusionsErianin is a therapeutic candidate for addressing neuroinflammation triggered by ICH, with its mechanisms of action likely involving the modulation of ERK signaling and the gut microbiome.Keywords: Intracerebral hemorrhage, Mouse model, Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction, 16S rRNA, ERK signaling

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), also known as spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, is a type of stroke caused by the rupture of blood vessels within the brain, leading to the accumulation of blood within the brain tissue [1]. ICH accounts for approximately 10–20% of all strokes and is characterized by a sudden onset of symptoms such as severe headache, nausea, vomiting, motor and sensory deficits, and altered consciousness [2]. The causes of ICH are various, including hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, vascular malformations, and the use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications [2]. The ICH could lead to potential complications including brain edema, increased intracranial pressure, and neurological deterioration [3, 4]. Treatment strategies for ICH involve managing the underlying cause, controlling blood pressure, and addressing the consequences of the hemorrhage, such as surgical intervention in severe cases [5, 6]. Nevertheless, in the clinical setting, the surgical elimination of hematoma aimed at addressing the initial cerebral insult subsequent to ICH has not demonstrated advantages for patient outcomes and infrequently influences the recuperation of neurological function [6]. The remnants of red blood cells and associated blood constituents elicit further cerebral damage post-ICH, resulting in inflammation, oxidative stress, neuronal cell death, mitochondrial impairment, and the disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [7].

Neuroinflammation is the first event upon hematoma formation, serving as a critical defense mechanism against subsequent cerebral damage post-ICH [8]. This inflammatory response involves the activation of microglia and the attraction of peripheral leukocytes to the area surrounding the hematoma [9]. Subsequently, the stimulated microglia and peripheral leukocytes secrete a variety of proinflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)−1β, chemokines, reactive oxygen species, and additional noxious substances, contributing to cerebral edema, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and neuronal apoptosis [10, 11]. The neuronal apoptosis triggered by ICH prompts an influx of neutrophils and leukocytes into the brain, intensifying the inflammatory insult [12]. Consequently, anti-inflammatory interventions may offer a promising therapeutic approach in the aftermath of ICH.

Erianin is a natural product isolated mainly from the orchid family Dendrobium, including Dendrobium bulbosum. It belongs to the bibenzyl compounds with the chemical name of 2-methoxy-5-[2-(3,4,5-trimethoxy-phenyl)-ethyl]-phenol and has been reported to exert antitumor effects against a variety of cancers. Chen et al. showed that erianin could inhibit the growth and migration of lung cancer cells through calcium/calmodulin-dependent iron death, which revealing erianin as a potential compound for lung cancer therapy [13]. In addition, other studies have shown that erianin had a potential in the treatment of inflammation, diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy and other diseases, which is expected to be a promising therapeutic agent for a variety of diseases [14, 15]. However, few studies were reported to unveil its effects on ICH.

In this study, we investigated the effects of erianin on ICH progression, including the neurological outcomes, neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis. We further explored the potential regulatory mechanisms underlying the protective effects of erianin on ICH.

Animals

Male C57 BL/6J mice that aged 8–10 weeks old and weighted 20–25 g were bought from Huafukang (Beijing, China) and housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (at 25 °C, 12 h light/12 h darkness cycle) with free access to food and water. All experiments were conducted following the protocols of Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 1996). The protocols were approved by the Committee on the Use and Care of Animals of Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital university.

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) mouse model

Following an overnight fast, mice were anesthetized using 0.3% pentobarbital sodium at a dosage of 75 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection. A 1 µl Hamilton syringe was used to inject 0.075 U of collagenase type VII (Sigma, St. Louis, USA) dissolved in 0.5 µl of 0.9% sterile saline into the right globus pallidus, with reference to the bregma at 2.0 mm lateral, 0.5 mm posterior, and 4.0 mm deep to the skull surface, over a period of 2 min. The injection needle was slowly removed in two stages, with a 5-minute interval between them. Control mice received an equivalent volume of 0.9% sterile saline at the identical site. A temperature-controlled heating pad was utilized to maintain the mice’s body temperature at 37 °C throughout and after the procedure. The mice were then randomly assigned to one of five groups: a sham group, an ICH group, and an ICH group treated with erianin at 25 mg/kg. Erianin or distilled water was administered orally to the mice daily for three days. The mice were euthanized on the third day to investigate short-term effects and underlying mechanisms and on the seventh day to assess the long-term effects following treatment withdrawal.

Short-term neurobehavioral assessments

Assessments were conducted by two masked researchers at 1 day post-intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). To minimize stress and fatigue, the three behavioral assays (modified Garcia test, forelimb placement test, and corner turn test) were performed sequentially in a single session per mouse, with brief rest intervals (approximately 5 min) between tests. The entire assessment session for each mouse was completed within approximately 30–45 min.

Modified Garcia Test (Duration: ~15–20 min per mouse): This test was performed first. Mice were placed in an open field and observed for spontaneous activity over a 5-minute acclimatization period. Subsequently, seven specific activities were assessed and scored (0–3 points each): spontaneous movement, axial sensation, vibrissae response, limb symmetry, directional turning, forelimb stepping, and climbing ability. The total score was calculated on a 21-point scale.

Forelimb Placement Test (Duration: ~5 min per mouse): Following the Garcia test and a rest interval, this test was conducted. Each mouse was gently held by the torso so that its forelimbs hung freely without support. The experimenter lightly stimulated the mouse’s whiskers on one side against the edge of a tabletop. The ability of the contralateral forelimb to place onto the table surface in response to whisker stimulation was observed and recorded. This procedure was repeated 10 times for each forelimb (left and right whisker stimulation), alternating sides in a randomized order. The number of successful placements for each forelimb was tallied.

Corner Turn Test (Duration: ~3 min per mouse): After another brief rest interval, the corner turn test was administered. Each mouse was placed facing into the apex of a 30° angled corner. As the mouse exited the corner, its directional choice (turning left or right) was recorded. This was repeated for a total of 10 consecutive attempts per mouse. The preference for turning towards the side contralateral to the lesion (expressed as a percentage of left turns out of total turns) was calculated.

Long-term neurobehavioral assessments

The Morris water maze test was administered from days 21 to 25 post-ICH to assess the mice’s capacity for spatial learning and memory. In this test, mice were introduced at different starting points around the maze and were expected to find a platform that was clearly visible above the water within 60 s. Following this, they were allowed to remain on the platform for a brief period of 5 s, during which their swimming route, the latency to reach the platform, and the distance swum were documented for each mouse. On the concluding day of the test, a probe trial was executed; the platform was taken away, and the time the mice spent in the area that previously contained the platform was measured. A digital tracking system (the Noldus Ethovision from Tacoma, WA, USA) was utilized to accurately capture and record the experimental data.

Brain water content

Brain edema was evaluated via the wet-dry weight method. Briefly, at 24 h post-ICH, mice were euthanized and brains were harvested. The ipsilateral hemisphere was separated and weighed immediately to obtain the wet weight. The tissue was then dried at 105 °C for 24 h and reweighed to obtain the dry weight. The brain water content was calculated as: [(wet weight - dry weight)/wet weight] × 100%.

Hematoma volume

Hematoma volumes were quantified at 24 h post-ICH using Drabkin’s method. The ipsilateral hemisphere was homogenized in PBS and centrifuged. The supernatant was reacted with Drabkin’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, D5941) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm, and hematoma volume (µl) was calculated against a standard curve of hemoglobin.

Immunofluorescence

Mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused with 20 mL of chilled 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) as previously described. The brains were extracted, post-fixed in PFA for a night, and then immersed in a 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C for 72 h for cryoprotection. Subsequently, 5 μm thick coronal brain sections were prepared for further examination. For histological analysis, 5 μm sections were prepared, rinsed with PBS, and incubated with a blocking solution containing 10% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were then stained with primary antibodies against Iba-1 (1:300 dilution, Abcam) and MPO (1:300 dilution, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. After thorough washing, the sections were treated with species-specific secondary antibodies labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 (Life Technologies, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h and counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Abcam, ab104135) to visualize cell nuclei. Fluorescence staining was observed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Mannheim, Germany). For each mouse, three sections were randomly selected, and five random fields per section were analyzed at ×400 magnification to assess fluorescently labeled cells.

TUNEL staining for detection of neuronal apoptosis

Prepared sections were blocked with 5% BSA containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h. The sections were then incubated with a neuron-specific nuclear protein (NeuN) antibody (1:100) at 37 °C for 3 h, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody (1:100) and TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. After counterstaining with DAPI and mounting, three sections per mouse were randomly selected, and five random fields per section were analyzed at ×400 magnification to evaluate fluorescently stained cells. The TUNEL assay was performed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, Fluorescein (Roche, Cat. No. 11684795910, sufficient for ≤ 50 tests).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Following anesthesia, the mice underwent intracardiac perfusion with cold 0.1 M PBS, after which the brains were extracted and carefully dissected. RNA was isolated from the hematoma area in the basal ganglia using TRIzol reagent from Invitrogen, part of Thermo Fisher Scientific, located in Waltham, MA, USA. This RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix, a product of Takara BioInc in Shiga, Japan. qRT-PCR was performed to assess gene expression levels using TB Green Premix Ex Taq, also supplied by Takara. β-actin served as an endogenous reference gene for data normalization. The relative gene expression levels were determined by normalizing to the control group and applying the 2^-ΔΔCt calculation method. The primer sequences used were as follows:

ZO-1: Forward: 5′-CGGCTACCACACAGAAGCTG-3′, Reverse: 5′TGGCTGTCCTTCATTCCGTC-3’;

ZO-1: Forward: 5′-CGGCTACCACACAGAAGCTG-3′, Reverse: 5′TGGCTGTCCTTCATTCCGTC-3’;

Occludin: Forward: 5′-TGCTCAGTCCGAAAGGTGAC-3’, Reverse: 5′-CGGATAAAAGGAAGCGATGG-3’;

Occludin: Forward: 5′-TGCTCAGTCCGAAAGGTGAC-3’, Reverse: 5′-CGGATAAAAGGAAGCGATGG-3’;

Claudin-4: Forward: 5′-AGGCTGGGACTGTATGCTGA-3’, Reverse: 5′-GGGTCCTTGGTGGTGAAGAT-3’;

Claudin-4: Forward: 5′-AGGCTGGGACTGTATGCTGA-3’, Reverse: 5′-GGGTCCTTGGTGGTGAAGAT-3’;

β-actin: Forward: 5′-GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG-3’, Reverse: 5′-CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT-3’.

β-actin: Forward: 5′-GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG-3’, Reverse: 5′-CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT-3’.

Western blot assay

Brain protein samples were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat# R0010). An equal quantity of protein from each sample was loaded onto an SDS-polyacrylamide gel for electrophoretic separation, followed by transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was then blocked and subsequently incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies included: anti-phospho-ERK (Thr202/Tyr204, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #4370), anti-phospho-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #4668), anti-phospho-GSK3β (Ser9, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #5558), and anti-β-actin (1:2000, Proteintech, #66009-1-Ig). Secondary anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibodies were applied to the membrane for a 2-hour incubation at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were then detected using an ECL Plus chemiluminescence reagent kit (GE Health, USA).

Plasma lipopolysaccharide (LPS) assay

The movement of plasma LPS from the intestinal lumen was quantified as an indicator of intestinal permeability. The concentration of LPS was determined using a lipopolysaccharide enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (AiFang Biological, based in Hunan, China, Cat# AF51932) as per manufacturer’s protocol.

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-Dextran assay

Mice were subjected to a 12-hour fast prior to the oral administration of FITC-dextran (molecular weight: 4000, Sigma, Cat# FD4). Following a 4-hour deprivation of water, the mice were anesthetized using sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 75 mg/kg. Blood samples were then collected and centrifuged at 3000 g for 4 °C in a low-light environment to minimize photobleaching. The absorbance of the resulting supernatants was quantified using a microplate reader (Thermo, USA).

Gut microbiota analysis

Cecal content was collected at day 7 post-ICH for high-throughput 16 S rDNA sequencing. DNA was isolated from the cecal content using the CTAB/SDS extraction method. Subsequently, the extracted DNA served as a template for gene amplification with specific barcoded primers via PCR, employing Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC Buffer (New England Biolabs, Hitchin, England). The amplified DNA fragments were then sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The resulting sequences were processed for quality control using QIIME software (version 1.9.1).

Statistical analyses

Results were presented as the mean ± SD. The Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized to assess differences between two groups. For comparison between more than two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Erianin alleviated the neurological deficits after ICH

We performed Garcia test, corner turn test, and forelimb placement test to measure the neurological deficits of ICH mouse. As shown in Fig. 1A, erianin treatment reduced the Garcia test score of ICH mice, suggesting the elevated neurological functions. Erianin administration also significantly reduced the proportion of left turn after ICH (Fig. 1B) and recovered the motor function (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, erianin treatment notably reduced brain water content (Fig. 1D) and hematoma volume (Fig. 1E) compared with that of mice after ICH.

Erianin alleviated the neurological deficits after ICH. (A) Garcia test, (B) corner turn test, and (C) forelimb placement test were performed to measure the neurological deficits. (D) Brain water content. (E) Hematoma volume. **p < 0.01 vs. Cont, ##p < 0.01 vs. Model

Erianin treatment improved long-term neurological functions after ICH

We conducted the Morris Water Maze test to assess the long-term neurological outcomes in mice following ICH. The ICH model group exhibited a notable increase in escape latency and swim distance required to locate the platform, as compared to the healthy control group (Fig. 2A-C). Conversely, the group treated with erianin demonstrated a significant reduction in escape latency and a decrease in the distance swum (Fig. 2A-C). Additionally, to evaluate neuronal apoptosis in the area surrounding the hematoma, TUNEL staining was performed at 24 h post-ICH. The findings indicated a substantial rise in TUNEL-positive cells in the perihematomal region of the ICH group when compared with the control group at the 24 h post-ICH. However, treatment with erianin reduced the number of TUNEL-positive neurons (Fig. 2D).

(B-C) Escape latency and swim distance of Morris water maze on days 21 to 25 after ICH. (D) TUNEL assay to measure the apoptosis of neurons. **p < 0.01 vs. Cont, ##p < 0.01 vs. Model

Erianin treatment inhibited neuroinflammation after ICH

Immunofluorescence staining was utilized to assess microglia activation and neutrophil infiltration by measuring the levels of Iba-1 and MPO in the perihematomal region 24 h post-ICH. The count of Iba-1 and MPO-positive cells in the perihematomal area was markedly higher in the ICH model group compared to the control group (Fig. 3A). Erianin treatment, however, led to a significant reduction in the number of Iba-1 and MPO-positive cells in the perihematomal area compared to the ICH model group at the same time point (Fig. 3A).

Erianin treatment inhibited neuroinflammation after ICH. (A) Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the levels of Iba-1 and MPO in the perihematomal region 24 h post-ICH. **p < 0.01 vs. Cont, ##p < 0.01 vs. Model

Erianin protects intestinal barrier integrity and reduces gut-derived endotoxemia after ICH

To assess the integrity of the intestinal barrier, we examined the transcriptional levels of genes encoding for tight junction proteins in the intestine. Following ICH, there was a noticeable reduction in the mRNA levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-4; however, erianin intervention led to a marked increase in the expression of these proteins (Fig. 4A). Concurrently, the intestinal barrier’s permeability was assessed using FITC-labeled dextran (FD4), with elevated serum FD4 levels after ICH being reduced by erianin treatment (Fig. 4B). Additionally, erianin significantly lowered the serum levels of LPS and proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, which were elevated due to ICH (Fig. 4C-D). Collectively, these findings suggest that erianin improves intestinal barrier function and reduces endotoxemia originating from the gut following ICH.

(B) Serum FD4 concentration measured by FITC-labeled dextran. (C) Serum LPS and (D) inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) concentrations. **p < 0.01 vs. Cont, ##p < 0.01 vs. Model

Erianin affects intestinal flora after ICH

The analysis of 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data from cecal content collected at day 7 post-ICH revealed that over 4,000 annotated species were identified, and similar patterns of sequencing data sufficiency and species richness were observed in the healthy control group, ICH model group, and model + erianin treatment group (Fig. 5A-B). Venn diagrams showed the number of shared and unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) in each group, with 189 shared ASVs and a total of 4,626 ASVs among the control, model, and model + Erianin groups (Fig. 5C). At the phylum level, the three groups were primarily dominated by Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidota, with the model group having a higher proportion of Proteobacteria than the other two groups, and the erianin treatment group showed a lower proportion of Bacteroidota than the other two groups (Fig. 5D-E). At the genus level, Stenotrophomonas was significantly enriched in the model group, Acinetobacter and Sphingobium were significantly higher in the healthy control and treatment groups than in the model group, and Enterococcus was significantly enriched in the treatment group (Fig. 5F).

The analysis of microbial community richness and diversity indicated differences in alpha diversity, with the healthy control group exhibiting higher alpha diversity and the model group showing the lowest (Fig. 6A). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed significant beta diversity differences among the three groups (Fig. 6B). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was employed to determine the effect size of each distinct microbial community. Microorganisms significantly different in the model group were identified with an LDA score of 2 and a corrected false discovery rate (FDR) with p < 0.05, including Xanthomonadales (Fig. 6C). Functional prediction analysis using PICRUSt2 for KEGG Orthology revealed that the model group was significantly enriched in ABC transport system ATP-binding protein, putative transposase, iron complex outer membrane receptor protein, etc., the healthy control group was significantly enriched in long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase, ABC-2 type transport system permease protein, branched-chain amino acid transport system permease protein, etc., and after erianin treatment, it was significantly enriched in LacI family transcriptional regulator, phosphoglycolate phosphatase, acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase, etc. (Fig. 6D). T-test was used to examine the differences in functions and pathways between different groups, with the model group and treatment group showing significant differences in KEGG Orthology functions, mainly focusing on putative ABC transport system permease protein, acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase, putative oxidoreductase, etc. (Fig. 6E-F). MetaCyc Pathway analysis mainly enriched on mycolate biosynthesis, fatty acid elongation, stearate biosynthesis II, superpathway of fatty acid biosynthesis initiation, etc. (Fig. 6G-H).

Erianin alleviates ICH via regulating the ERK signaling

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of erianin, we assessed the activation status of the ERK signaling pathway in perihematomal tissues at 24 h post-ICH. Western blot analysis showed that phosphorylation of ERK (p-ERK), JNK (p-JNK), and GSK3β (p-GSK3β) was markedly increased in the Model group compared with the Control group (Fig. 7A), whereas erianin treatment (Model + Era group) significantly suppressed this phosphorylation (p < 0.01 vs. Model group). To determine whether ERK activation influences the protective actions of erianin, we co-administered an ERK activator. The neurological improvements induced by erianin, as evidenced by higher Garcia test scores and reduced corner turn asymmetry, were significantly attenuated by ERK activation (Fig. 7B–C). Similarly, the erianin-mediated upregulation of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-4 mRNA expression was suppressed (Fig. 7D), and the reductions in serum FD4 and LPS levels were abolished (Fig. 7E–F). Collectively, these findings indicate that erianin confers protection against ICH-induced neurological and intestinal barrier damage primarily through inhibition of the ERK signaling pathway.

(B) Garcia test and (C) corner turn test were performed to measure the neurological deficits. (D) RNA levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-4 were measured by qRT-PCR. Discussion

In the current study, we first investigated the neuroprotective effects of erianin and explored its underlying mechanism in a mouse model of ICH. We demonstrated that erianin treatment improved both short- and long-term neurological functions, reduced brain edema, inhibited microglia activation and neutrophil infiltration, and suppressed neuronal apoptotic cell death in perihematomal areas after ICH.

It is well-established that immune and neuroinflammatory reactions significantly contribute to the secondary brain damage following intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and these responses are crucial determinants of patient outcomes [9]. Microglia and macrophages, the primary immune cells within the brain, become activated in response to ICH to mediate inflammation. These activated cells can exhibit both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory characteristics [16]. Pro-inflammatory microglial cells secrete cytokines that can intensify neuroinflammation and exacerbate ICH-induced brain injury.

The gut microbiota’s role in neuroinflammation related to various central nervous system (CNS) conditions has been recognized [17]. For example, in rats with chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, negative impacts on the microbiota, such as decreased short-chain fatty acid levels and cognitive deficits, have been observed, and these can be mitigated by fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) [18]. In traumatic brain injury (TBI) mice, the microbiota has been shown to decrease neuroinflammation and enhance neurogenesis [19]. Similarly, in spinal cord injury, where the gut microbiome’s balance is severely disrupted, FMT has been found to improve both motor function and intestinal health, as well as reduce neuroinflammation [20, 21]. ICH also leads to gut dysbiosis, which can exacerbate neuroinflammation [22]. Our data provide evidence that erianin may exert neuroprotective effects through a dual mechanism: direct modulation of central immune responses and indirect regulation via the gut–brain axis. Post-ICH, increased intestinal permeability has been reported, potentially activating microglia through proinflammatory cytokines from the gut [23, 24]. In our study, erianin improved the expression of tight junction proteins (Occludin, ZO-1, Claudin-4), reduced serum FD4 and LPS levels, and shifted the gut microbiota composition toward a profile associated with reduced systemic inflammation. Such changes may attenuate peripheral immune activation, thereby reducing the influx of inflammatory mediators and cells into the brain, ultimately limiting neuronal apoptosis. This is consistent with previous findings that restoring gut barrier integrity can suppress microglial activation in CNS injury models [18, 19].

Furthermore, the administration of erianin post-ICH was correlated with decreased phosphorylation of JNK, GSK3β, and ERK, as well as decreased levels of Iba-1, MPO, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Given that ERK signaling is a central pathway in microglial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, our ERK activator experiment suggests that erianin’s beneficial effects on both neurological outcomes and gut barrier integrity are, at least in part, mediated through ERK inhibition. The finding that ERK activation abrogated erianin’s effects on intestinal tight junction expression and endotoxemia further supports a bidirectional gut–brain mechanism in which central ERK signaling modulates peripheral barrier function, and conversely, gut-derived inflammatory signals may feed back to exacerbate ERK-driven neuroinflammation.

In consistent with our findings, previous studies have indicated the effects of erianin on ERK signaling pathway and cell growth. Zhu et al. reported that erianin could suppress the growth of bladder cancer cells through mitochondrial apoptosis and JNK pathway [25]. Liu et al. demonstrated that erianin could prevent the growth of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells through the apoptotic pathway [26]. Our study expands these observations to a neuroinflammatory context, showing that erianin’s regulation of ERK signaling may link peripheral (gut) and central immune responses.

In conclusion, our data indicate that the treatment with erianin could reduce brain inflammation and cell death following ICH, likely through integrated modulation of the ERK signaling cascade in the brain and restoration of gut barrier integrity, leading to reduced peripheral endotoxemia and dampened microglial activation. This combined central–peripheral action provides a plausible mechanistic basis for erianin’s neuroprotective effects after ICH.
