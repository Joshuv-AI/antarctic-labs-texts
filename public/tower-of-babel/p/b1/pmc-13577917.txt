Englumafusp alfa plus glofitamab in B cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma: a phase 1 trial
Authors: Martin Hutchings, Michael Dickinson, Giuseppe Gritti, Carmelo Carlo-Stella, William Townsend, Francesc Bosch, Nancy L. Bartlett, Guillaume Cartron, Herve Ghesquieres, Roch Houot, Harriet Walter, Fritz Offner, Natalie Dimier, Candice Jamois, Sylvia Herter, Abiraj Keelara, Sabine Wilson, Jeremy Gallien, Heather Hinton, Isabel Prieto Gonzalez-Albo, Koorosh Korfi, Georgios Kazantzidis, Marie-Hélène Wasmer, Katharina Lechner, Franck Morschhauser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is an unmet need for effective, off-the-shelf therapies for relapsed or refractory aggressive B cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL). Part 2 of the current study was an open-label, nonrandomized, phase 1 study of escalating doses of the CD19–4-1BBL co-stimulatory molecule, englumafusp alfa, in combination with glofitamab in patients with relapsed or refractory B-NHL. Obinutuzumab pretreatment was administered 7 days before the first glofitamab dose. Glofitamab step-up dosing in cycle 1 was followed by 11 cycles of glofitamab plus englumafusp alfa. Englumafusp alfa was administered at escalating doses, with the initial dose on cycle 2 day 8 (C2D8) or cycle 1 day 10 (C1D10). Primary objectives were to establish the maximum tolerated dose, and safety and tolerability. A total of 134 patients were enrolled, including 109 with aggressive B-NHL and 25 with indolent B-NHL. The maximum tolerated dose of englumafusp alfa was not reached; one dose-limiting toxicity occurred (grade 5 Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia). Adverse events were reported in 98.5% of all patients, with grade 3/4 adverse events in 59.0%. Grade 5 adverse events occurred in ten patients. In the subgroup of C2D8 patients with aggressive B-NHL (n = 83), overall response and complete metabolic response rates were 68.7% and 56.6%, respectively; among those without previous exposure to chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy (n = 41), the corresponding rates were 73.2% and 65.9%. Pharmacodynamic changes following englumafusp alfa administration supported its co-stimulatory mode of action. These data demonstrate that the addition of englumafusp alfa to glofitamab is associated with encouraging efficacy and robust pharmacodynamic modulation, as well as a safety profile consistent with glofitamab monotherapy, in patients with relapsed or refractory B-NHL. CTIS identifier: 2022-502616-37-00; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04077723.

Main

Despite advances in therapy, approximately one-third of patients with diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) relapse after, or are refractory to, first-line therapy1–3. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies and bispecific antibodies have been shown recently to improve outcomes in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) DLBCL, revolutionizing treatment algorithms4–12. However, although CAR T cell therapies offer curative potential in 30–40% of patients with R/R DLBCL, real-word applicability is limited by severe toxicities, logistical challenges and access issues13–17.

Bispecific antibodies have emerged as an off-the-shelf alternative to CAR T cell therapies, providing broader access and reduced logistical complexity6,7,12. Nonetheless, compared to CAR T cell therapies, there remains a potential for improvement in the proportion of patients achieving a response to monotherapy, and the long-term durability of these responses. For example, glofitamab, a CD20×CD3 bispecific antibody, has demonstrated efficacy in patients with R/R DLBCL and at least two previous therapies, with complete responses achieved in 39% of patients, of which 78% were ongoing at 1 year7.

Building on insights from second-generation CAR T cell therapies, which incorporated co-stimulatory domains such as 4-1BB to enhance T cell persistence and antitumor activity18–21, the addition of co-stimulatory strategies to bispecific antibody regimens could enhance therapeutic outcomes. Englumafusp alfa—a CD19–4-1BBL co-stimulatory antibody-like fusion protein—is designed to address this need by augmenting T cell activation and preventing T cell exhaustion22. The molecule binds to CD19 on B cells and to 4-1BB on T cells, delivering a potent co-stimulatory signal when paired with a T cell-engaging antibody such as glofitamab22, which has the potential to increase response rates and duration. In preclinical studies, englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab showed promising antitumor activity in vivo, improving both the depth and durability of response, beyond those observed with single-agent glofitamab22. This approach offers the potential benefits of co-stimulation in an off-the-shelf format, and may represent a large step forward in the field of immunotherapy.

Here we report the results of a phase 1 study (BP41072; CTIS identifier: 2022-502616-37-00; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04077723) of escalating doses of englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab in patients with R/R B cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL), with a focus on patients with aggressive B-NHL who initiated englumafusp alfa on cycle (C) 2 day (D) 8 (C2D8), given the unmet medical need in this population and observed benefit–risk profile with the latter dosing schedule.

Results

BP41072 is an ongoing, open-label, multicenter, phase 1/2 dose-escalation and dose-expansion study. Part 2 of the study was an open-label, nonrandomized, phase 1 study of escalating doses of englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab. Patients with indolent or aggressive B-NHL who had relapsed after or failed to respond to at least one previous line of therapy received obinutuzumab (1,000 mg) pretreatment 7 days before the first glofitamab dose to mitigate the risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS). Glofitamab was administered over 12 cycles, starting with step-up doses in C1 (standard regimen: 2.5 mg on C1D1 and 10 mg on C1D8; extended regimen: 0.2 mg on C1D1, 1.5 mg on C1D3 and 10 mg on C1D8; 14-day cycle) and continued at target dose (30 mg) on D1 of C2–12 (21-day cycles). In the dose-escalation cohorts, englumafusp alfa was started on C2D8 and continued on D1 of each subsequent cycle. In subsequent cohorts, englumafusp alfa was started during glofitamab step-up dosing on C1D10 to explore the benefit–risk of delivering the co-stimulatory signal at an earlier point. Primary objectives were to establish the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and safety and tolerability of englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab. Secondary objectives included evaluation of anti tumor activity, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics and mechanism of action. Part 1 of the study was an open-label, nonrandomized, phase 1 study of escalating doses of englumafusp alfa in combination with obinutuzumab in patients with R/R B-NHL, but these data were not included in the current analysis and will be reported elsewhere. Part 3 of the study is the dose-expansion stage that is currently ongoing to further evaluate the antitumor activity of englumafusp alfa plus glofitamab dosing regimens showing promise in part 2 in patients with R/R B-NHL.

Patients

Between 21 September 2020 (first patient in) and 12 July 2023 (last patient in), 134 patients were enrolled into part 2 of the study at 15 sites in Australia, Belgium, Denmark, France, Italy, Spain, the UK and the USA (Supplementary Table 1). All patients had received at least one dose of study medication as of 20 January 2025 (Fig. 1). Median age was 63 years (range, 20–86) and 82 (61.2%) patients were male (Table 1). A total of 109 patients had aggressive B-NHL (DLBCL, n = 78; transformed follicular lymphoma, n = 23; transformed-other, n = 4; grade 3b follicular lymphoma, n = 2; primary mediastinal B cell lymphoma (PMBCL), n = 2) and 25 had indolent B-NHL (grade 1–3a follicular lymphoma, n = 24; marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), n = 1). The median number of previous lines of therapy was three (range, one to eight) and 116 (86.6%) patients had received at least two previous lines. In total, 58 (43.3%) patients had received previous CAR T cell therapy. The median time between previous CAR T cell administration and enrollment was 5.7 months (range, 1.9–13.8). Four (3.0%) patients had received previous glofitamab. A total of 71 (53.0%) patients had primary refractory disease and 52 (38.8%) were refractory to previous CAR T cell therapy. In the dose-escalation cohorts evaluating the C2D8 schedule, 0.36 mg to 75 mg doses of englumafusp alfa were investigated. In the cohorts evaluating the C1D10 schedule, 10 mg, 30 mg and 75 mg doses were evaluated. Across all cohorts, the median number of englumafusp alfa cycles received was 9.0 (range, 1–11), whereas the median number of glofitamab cycles received was 9.5 (range, 1–12).

A total of 56 (41.8%) patients completed the full 12 cycles of treatment and 78 (58.2%) patients completed <12 cycles of treatment (Fig. 1). The most common reason for completing <12 cycles of treatment was progressive disease (49 out of 78 patients, 36.6%).

Safety

Among all safety-evaluable patients (N = 134), any-grade adverse events (AEs) occurred in 132 (98.5%) (Table 2). Grade 3/4 AEs and serious AEs occurred in 79 (59.0%) and 84 (62.7%) patients, respectively. Nine (6.7%) patients experienced a total of ten AEs leading to withdrawal of study treatment (infections, n = 6 events (COVID-19 or COVID-19 pneumonia, n = 3; giardiasis, n = 1; herpes zoster, n = 1; pneumonia, n = 1); congestive cardiac failure, myocardial ischemia, gastrointestinal hemorrhage and decreased platelet count, all n = 1). Fifty-nine (44.0%) patients experienced an AE leading to dose modification/interruption. Grade 5 AEs occurred in ten (7.5%) patients (COVID-19 pneumonia, n = 3; COVID-19, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP), pleurisy, postacute COVID-19 syndrome, pneumonia, organizing pneumonia and sepsis, all n = 1 each), with one event occurring after obinutuzumab pretreatment but before the initiation of glofitamab and englumafusp alfa, seven events occurring during combination treatment and two events occurring during the post-treatment follow-up period. Four events were assessed as related to treatment by the investigators (PJP (related to glofitamab only), pleurisy (related to englumafusp alfa and glofitamab), pneumonia (related to englumafusp alfa, glofitamab and obinutuzumab) and organizing pneumonia (related to englumafusp alfa and glofitamab)). The MTD of englumafusp alfa was not reached. The most common AEs were CRS (n = 74; 55.2%), anemia (n = 45; 33.6%) and COVID-19 (n = 44; 32.8%) (Table 2). CRS was predominantly low grade (grade 1, 48.5%; grade 2, 14.2%; grade 3, 0.7%) (Extended Data Table 2). A total of 26 (19.4%) patients received tocilizumab for CRS management and 22 (16.4%) received corticosteroids; 14 (10.4%) patients received both. Three patients were admitted to the intensive care unit. Infections were reported in 88 (65.7%) patients, with COVID-19-related events in 44 (32.8%). Neutropenia was the most common grade 3/4 AE, reported in 33 (24.6%) patients, whereas febrile neutropenia occurred in 2 (1.5%) patients (both grade 3). Thrombocytopenia was reported in 41 (30.6%) patients. Hepatic-associated events occurred in 28 (20.9%) patients, most of which were liver enzyme laboratory abnormalities (alanine aminotransferase increase, n = 12 (9.0%), aspartate aminotransferase increase, n = 11 (8.2%)). Hepatobiliary disorders occurred in seven patients and were grade 1/2 (n = 3; 2.2%) or grade 3 (n = 4; 3.0%). A grade 1 (nonserious) immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) event was reported in one patient during glofitamab step-up dosing (Table 2).

Among 83 safety-evaluable patients with aggressive B-NHL in the C2D8 cohorts, AEs occurred in 81 (97.6%) (Table 2). Grade 3/4 AEs were reported in 48 (57.8%) patients and serious AEs in 55 (66.3%) patients. AEs leading to withdrawal of study treatment occurred in 5 (6.0%) patients (assessed as related to study treatment by the investigators in 2 (2.4%) patients) and AEs leading to dose modification or interruption occurred in 34 (41.0%) (related to study treatment in 16 (9.3%)). Grade 5 AEs were reported in 6 (7.2%) patients.

Among 66 safety-evaluable patients with aggressive B-NHL in the C2D8 cohorts who initiated glofitamab with standard step-up dosing, CRS occurred in 33 (50.0%) patients (grade 1, 42.4%; grade 2, 15.2%; grade 3, 1.5%) (Extended Data Table 2). CRS occurred mostly during glofitamab step-up dosing in C1 (Extended Data Fig. 1a); first CRS events occurred in 28 patients after the C1D1 dose (2.5 mg; median time of onset after dose: 14.4 h (range, 5–30)), when peak cytokine release was also observed (Extended Data Fig. 1b), in ten patients after the C1D8 dose (10 mg; median time of onset from dose: 20.6 h (range: 15.2–39.0)) and in eight patients after the C2D1 dose (30 mg; median time of onset from dose: 30.4 h (range: 30.4–30.4); onset data from only one patient was available). CRS occurred in 5 (8.3%) patients after the first englumafusp alfa dose on C2D8 (median time of onset from dose: 32.7 h (range, 32.7–32.7); onset data from only one patient was available) and was all grade 1. CRS was uncommon from C3D1 onwards (2 (3.9%) patients) and was all grade 1.

Efficacy

Antitumor activity was evaluated comprehensively in the subgroup of patients with aggressive B-NHL, given the unmet medical need in this population. Among all response-evaluable patients (n = 109), the overall response rate (ORR) was 67.9% (95% confidence internal (CI), 58.3–76.5) and the complete metabolic response (CMR) rate was 50.5% (95% CI, 40.6–60.3; Table 3). Activity was more pronounced in the C2D8 dosing schedule cohort (n = 83) than the C1D10 cohort (n = 24). Pharmacokinetics

At intermediate-to-high dose levels, steady-state clearance was 0.219 l day−1 (s.d. 0.116), comparable to a typical immunoglobulin G1 antibody exhibiting linear elimination or saturated target-mediated drug disposition23.

Discussion

In the current study, we evaluated a T cell-engaging bispecific antibody in combination with a targeted, co-stimulatory agent. The combination of englumafusp alfa plus glofitamab was well-tolerated at all dose levels explored, and was consistent with the known safety profile of glofitamab monotherapy7, with no new or synergistic safety signals.

The MTD of englumafusp alfa was not reached; one DLT was reported. CRS was the most common AE, reported in 55.2% of the overall population. Consistent with glofitamab monotherapy, events occurred primarily in C1 during glofitamab step-up dosing,7 before englumafusp alfa administration, and were predominantly grade 1/2, suggesting that the addition of englumafusp alfa to glofitamab does not worsen the frequency or severity of CRS. Unlike first generation anti-4-1BB antibodies, such as urelumab24, severe hepatotoxicity was not observed and hepatic AEs (20.9%) were due mostly to liver enzyme abnormalities. Infections occurred in 65.7% of patients compared with 38% in the glofitamab monotherapy trial7. COVID-19 could account for some of the difference observed in the overall infection incidence, with COVID-19 events reported in 32.8% of patients in the current study compared with 9% in the glofitamab monotherapy study7. Despite the higher overall rate of infections in the current study, these events did not result in a higher incidence of treatment withdrawal. Notably, the overall rate of treatment withdrawal due to AEs in the current study was consistent with glofitamab monotherapy (6.7% versus 9%, respectively), as was the rate of grade 5 events (7.5% versus 5.2%, respectively)7. However, cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution owing to differences in study design, accrual dates, patient populations and timing of trials with regard to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Combination therapy was highly active in patients with aggressive B-NHL. The CMR rate was higher in the C2D8 cohort (56.6%) than in the C1D10 cohort (33.3%). The C2D8 dosing schedule was selected subsequently for further development, as it optimizes the therapeutic window of englumafusp alfa and reduces visit density during the intensive first cycle of therapy.

Combination therapy also showed promising efficacy in clinically relevant subgroups of patients with aggressive B-NHL. Notably, in the C2D8 cohort, substantial CMR rates were observed in patients with two or more previous therapies (52.9%) and in those without previous CAR T cell therapy (65.9%). Efficacy was particularly encouraging in the small sample of aggressive B-NHL patients with one previous line of therapy, with a CMR rate of 76.9%.

Outcomes in patients with aggressive B-NHL who had received two or more previous lines of therapy (3L+ cohort) were generally comparable with those in the intent-to-treat population of the TRANSCEND NHL 001 study that evaluated the 4-1BB CAR T cell therapy lisocabtagene maraleucel in 3L+ patients with aggressive B-NHL, with a CMR rate of 52.9% (versus 44% in TRANSCEND NHL 001), median PFS of 7.4 months (versus 4.8 months) and median OS of 20.1 months (versus 14 months)4. Outcomes also compare favorably with glofitamab monotherapy in patients with R/R DLBCL and at least two previous therapies (ORR, 52%; complete response rate, 39%)7. Unlike glofitamab monotherapy, however, frequent conversions from an initial PMR to a later CMR suggest a potentially prolonged durability of the antilymphoma effect.

Englumafusp alfa plus glofitamab was highly active in the subgroup of patients with indolent B-NHL (ORR, 91.7%; CMR rate, 79.2%; 12-month PFS rate, 82.6%), most of whom had received at least two previous lines of therapy. Outcomes were comparable with those achieved with the 4-1BB CAR T cell therapies lisocabtagene maraleucel (ORR, 97%; complete response rate, 94%; 12-month PFS rate, 81%) and tisagenlecleucel (ORR, 86.2%; complete response rate, 69.1%; 12-month PFS rate, 67%) in patients with R/R follicular lymphoma and two or more previous lines25,26.

The pharmacodynamic profile of englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab supports the intended mechanism of CD19-targeted 4-1BB co-stimulation, namely to enhance effector T cell responses without exacerbating systemic inflammation. The dose-dependent expansion of CD8+ Tem cells, alongside reduced expansion of PD1+CD8+ Tem cells and Temra cells, in contrast to glofitamab monotherapy data27, suggests that 4-1BB co-stimulation may shift T cell responses towards a more functionally sustained state. This aligns with the established role of 4-1BB in promoting T cell fitness, limiting exhaustion and sustaining effector function under persistent antigen stimulation28,29. The association of these pharmacodynamic changes with CMR, deeper ctDNA responses and improved PFS further supports a mechanistic link between T cell reprogramming and clinical benefit, and suggests that targeted 4-1BB co-stimulation can recapitulate key functional features of second-generation CAR T cell therapy. Exploratory baseline analyses in previous CAR T cell-exposed patients point to a host immune context that may be less permissive to effective re-engagement by glofitamab plus CD19-targeted 4-1BB co-stimulation. In particular, enrichment of activated and CD4 Tem cells, together with reduced central memory CD4 T cells, is consistent with a more chronically stimulated and differentiated compartment that may have reduced proliferative reserve and helper capacity30. This may help explain why activity was attenuated in the previous CAR T cell-exposed population, suggesting that baseline immune fitness, rather than target abundance alone, may have limited effective downstream T cell re-engagement.

Beyond CAR T cell therapy, emerging treatments in second-line non-Hodgkin lymphoma (2L NHL), such as bispecific antibodies in combination with various other drugs, are gaining traction. However, these often rely on standard chemotherapy backbones (for example, gemcitabine plus oxaliplatin) or antibody–drug conjugates (for example, polatuzumab vedotin) that maintain a cytostatic payload and may be associated with systemic toxicities. In this study, we have demonstrated the clinical safety, efficacy, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of a bispecific co-stimulatory agent in combination with a T cell-engaging bispecific antibody.

Several limitations should be considered. Part 2 of the study was designed to evaluate escalating doses of englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab and was not therefore powered for definitive efficacy comparisons or formal assessment of biomarker-defined subgroups. Sample sizes within individual dose cohorts were limited, and exploratory biomarker analyses were restricted to subsets of patients with available longitudinal peripheral blood and ctDNA samples, or pretreatment tumor tissue. Pharmacodynamic and clinical associations, including those related to previous CAR T cell exposure, should also be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger cohorts.

Our findings support the principle of T cell co-stimulation to elicit increased T cell persistence, analogous to the mode of action of second-generation CAR T cell therapies. Leveraging this principle, the combination of englumafusp alfa and glofitamab offers a potential off-the-shelf alternative to CAR T cell therapies. Because this pure immunotherapy approach entirely avoids chemotherapy-related toxicities and evades chemotherapy resistance, it may be particularly relevant for elderly patients or those with several comorbidities who may not meet the strict eligibility criteria for CAR T cell therapy or intensive cytotoxic regimens. Consequently, although further large-scale investigation is warranted to define the clinical benefit and optimal patient population, these data support further investigation of glofitamab combined with CD19-targeted 4-1BB co-stimulation in patients with R/R indolent and aggressive B-NHL.

Methods

BP41072 is an ongoing, open-label, multicenter, phase 1/2 dose-escalation and dose-expansion study. Part 1 of the study was an open-label, nonrandomized, phase 1 study of escalating doses of englumafusp alfa in combination with the anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody obinutuzumab in patients with R/R B-NHL and will be reported elsewhere. Part 2 of the study was an open-label, nonrandomized, phase 1 study of escalating doses of englumafusp alfa in combination with the CD20×CD3 bispecific antibody glofitamab in patients with R/R B-NHL and is reported herein. Part 3 is the dose-expansion stage of the study that is currently ongoing to further evaluate the anti tumor activity of englumafusp alfa plus glofitamab dosing regimens showing promise in part 2 in patients with R/R B-NHL. All stages of the study were conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization guideline for Good Clinical Practice and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board and/or ethics committee at each participating center (Supplementary Table 1). All patients provided written informed consent and were not compensated.

Patients

Patients eligible for part 2 of the study were aged ≥18 years, with aggressive B-NHL (DLBCL, PMBCL, Richter’s transformation, transformed follicular lymphoma or grade 3b follicular lymphoma) or indolent B-NHL (grade 1–3a follicular lymphoma, MZL or mantle cell lymphoma) and had relapsed after or failed to respond to at least one previous line of therapy. Patients had one or more measurable lesion by computed tomography scan and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in the study protocol.

Study treatment

Obinutuzumab (1,000 mg) was administered 7 days before the first glofitamab dose to mitigate the risk of CRS7. Glofitamab (intravenous infusion) was administered over 12 cycles, starting with step-up dosing in C1 (14 days) and continuing with the target dose in C2–C12 (21 days each). The standard step-up regimen was 2.5 mg on C1D1 and 10 mg on C1D8, followed by the 30 mg target dose on D1 from C2 onwards. An alternative extended step-up regimen was 0.2 mg on C1D1, 1.5 mg on C1D3 and 10 mg on C1D8. Premedication was administered before each glofitamab dose per the protocol.

In the dose-escalation cohorts, englumafusp alfa (intravenous infusion) was administered at escalating dose levels based on a modified continual reassessment method with escalation with overdose control. In those cohorts, englumafusp alfa was started on C2D8 and continued on D1 of each subsequent cycle. In subsequent cohorts, englumafusp alfa was started during glofitamab step-up dosing on C1D10.

Objectives and endpoints

Primary objectives were to establish the MTD and safety and tolerability of englumafusp alfa in combination with glofitamab following obinutuzumab pretreatment. Primary endpoints were the nature and frequency of dose-limiting toxicities and the incidence, nature and severity of AEs. Secondary objectives included evaluation of antitumor activity, with ORR and CMR as key endpoints, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics and mode of action.

Safety and efficacy assessments

AEs were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0. CRS was graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy consensus criteria31.

Response was assessed according to the Lugano classification32, using fluorodeoxyglucose-positron tomography and computed tomography scans at screening and on C3D1, C6D1 and C9D1, at the end of treatment and every 6 months during follow-up until disease progression.

Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetic endpoints included the Cmax and Cmin of englumafusp alfa in serum and area under the curve.

A population pharmacokinetic analysis was conducted using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling with NONMEM software version 7.5.0 (Icon Development Solutions)33. A first-order conditional estimation with interaction method was used for model development. A preliminary two-compartment model incorporating both linear and Michaelis–Menten (saturable) elimination pathways was selected to describe englumafusp alfa disposition, serving as a mechanistic approximation of target-mediated drug disposition (Extended Data Fig. 5). Bodyweight was included as a covariate on clearance and volumes of distribution.

Pharmacodynamics and tissue biomarkers

Blood samples were collected from all patients at predefined time points during treatment per the protocol. T cells, B cells and natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, activated T cells, B cells and NK cells, proliferating T cells, naive and memory T cells were evaluated centrally (Q2 Solutions) using validated flow cytometry assays as described below. Plasma samples were collected for cytokine (CXCL-10, interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-8, IL-10, IL-2, soluble CD25, interferon-γ, tumor necrosis factor) analysis using validated multiplex immunoassays on a ProteinSimple Ella platform. ctDNA dynamics were evaluated by the Avenio ctDNA assay34 (Roche) at protocol-specified time points in patients with aggressive B-NHL.

Patients entering the study also provided a pretreatment screening biopsy sample, fresh or a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded archival specimen (<100 days from study D1 and uninterrupted by a previous therapy), for retrospective immunohistochemical assessments. Biopsy specimens were sent to the central pathology laboratory (Discovery Life Sciences) and fresh tissues were fixed and embedded on receipt. All biopsy samples were quality checked by a pathologist on hematoxylin and eosin staining to confirm sufficient tumor content. Consecutive sections were provided for immunohistochemical analysis using CD19 (LE-CD19 mouse monoclonal, stained on Ventana Benchmark Ultra), CD20 (L26 mouse monoclonal, stained on Ventana Benchmark Ultra Scanner: iScan HT 20X) and CD8/Ki67 (CD8 SP239 rabbit monoclonal, Ki67 30-9 rabbit monoclonal, stained on Ventana Discovery Ultra) assays. Digital quantifications were performed on the whole tumor region, including the diffuse or the pseudofollicles and associated tumor stroma. For CD19, modified H-scores were calculated as 2 × (% moderate positive stain) + 3 × (% strong positive stain). As a note, weakly positive stained cells were not captured due to the narrow dynamic range making detection of these cells technically challenging. CD8/Ki67 nucleated cells were enumerated and the Ki67+CD8− cell densities (per mm2) were provided across the entire tumor region. A board-certified pathologist reviewed the results.

Flow cytometry

Sample preparation

For the A167 T cell, B cell and NK cell (TBNK) assay, whole blood was collected in CytoChex tubes, transferred to a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) tube and stained with the antibody cocktail. Samples were lysed with BD FACSLyse and acquired.

For the A207 naive and memory T cell (CD279) assay, whole blood was collected in CytoChex tubes and transferred to polystyrene tubes (for full staining or fluorescence minus one control). Following incubation with the respective antibody cocktails, red blood cells were lysed with BD FACSLyse and samples were washed and acquired on a BD FACS Canto II cytometer.

The A234 T cell proliferation and activation assay uses whole blood collected in sodium heparin tubes. The sample preparation involves adding 100 µl of the specimen to tubes containing surface antibody cocktails (CD3, CD4, CD8 and HLA-DR). These samples are then processed on an LWA for red blood cell lysis and cell fixation and permeabilization using BD FACSLyse and Perm Buffer II. Subsequently, intracellular staining with Ki67 or an isotype control (IgG1) is performed, followed by a final wash program on the LWA before immediate acquisition on a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer.

Cell population abundance

Cell populations from the TBNK assay are reported as percentage of lymphocytes, with the exception of CD14+, which are reported as percentage of leukocytes. For all other assays, target cell populations are reported as percentage of the immediate parent population or lymphocytes as indicated in the population designation.

Gating strategy

The A167 Q-TBNK assay gating strategy begins by excluding debris in the forward scatter (FSC)-A versus side scatter (SSC)-A plot. A specific gate is created to identify and exclude CD14+ monocytes to ensure they do not contaminate the subsequent lymphocyte analysis. From the remaining population, leukocytes are selected based on CD45 expression, and lymphocytes are further isolated as CD45− bright events with low SSC. This lymphocyte population is then used to identify CD3+ T cells (subsequently split into CD4+ and CD8+ subsets), whereas the CD3− population is analyzed to enumerate CD19+ B cells and CD16+ and CD56+ NK cells.

The gating strategy for the naive and memory T cells assay begins by identifying singlet populations using FSC (FSC-H versus FSC-W) and SSC (SSC-H versus SSC-W) properties to eliminate doublets and debris. From the singlet population, lymphocytes are selected based on scatter properties, followed by the identification of CD3+ T cells. This CD3+ population is then divided into CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets. Within these CD4+ and CD8+ populations, quadrant gates using CD45RA and CD197 (also known as CCR7) expression are applied to define naive T cells (CD45RA+CD197+), central memory T cells (CD45RA−CD197+), Tem cells (CD45RA−CD197−) and Temra cells (CD45RA+CD197−). Finally, CD279 (PD1) expression is identified on these subsets using gates established by fluorescence minus one controls.

For the T cell proliferation and activation (HLA-DR/Ki67) assay, the gating strategy begins by excluding doublets and debris through the identification of FSC and SSC singlets, followed by the selection of lymphocytes based on FSC-A versus SSC-A properties. From the lymphocyte population, CD3+ T cells are gated and subdivided further into CD4+ (helper T) and CD8+ (cytotoxic T) subsets. These subsets are then analyzed for activation and proliferation markers, where HLA-DR+ and Ki67+ populations are gated individually (using isotype controls for Ki67 validation). These gates are then combined in the hierarchy to define the dual-positive CD4+Ki67+HLA-DR+ and CD8+Ki67+HLA-DR+ populations.

The antibody reagents used in the TBNK assay, naive and memory T cell (CD279) assay and T cell proliferation and activation (HLA-DR/Ki67) assay are presented in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

Statistical analysis

The exact sample size for part 2 of the study could not be predetermined, because it was dependent on the number of cohorts required to reach the MTD and/or recommended phase 2 dose. A modified continual reassessment method with escalation with overdose control design was used to guide the dose escalation based on the occurrence of DLTs. The MTD was defined as the dose with the highest probability that the DLT rate is within the target of 20–30%, with a low probability (<25%) of overdosing (DLT rate >30%). Based on simulations for cohorts of three patients, the part 2 dose-escalation was estimated to require up to 66 patients. Including backfill cohorts, the maximum number of patients could increase up to 180.

The Medidata RAVE electronic data capture system was used to capture, manage, clean and report the trial data. Descriptive statistics were used to describe and summarize the data. Tables were generated using SAS version 9.4. Figures were generated using R version 4.4.1. Sex was based on self-reported demographics obtained at screening.

Safety was assessed in all patients who received at least one dose of any study treatment (safety-evaluable population). Efficacy was assessed in all patients who received at least one dose of any study treatment and had their first planned response assessment before the data cut-off (response-evaluable population). The Clopper–Pearson method was used to determine the 95% CI for CMR and ORR. Duration of response, PFS and OS were analyzed by Kaplan–Meier estimates; the Brookmeyer–Crowley method was used to construct the 95% CI for the duration of response, median PFS and median OS.

The nonlinear association between dose and frequency of peripheral T cell changes relative to baseline (C2D8 predose) were analyzed with the following model, pharmacodynamic change from baseline (CFB) ≈ baseline value + log dose + log dose2. Pharmacodynamic markers reported with nominal P value < 0.05 by the model were selected for the calculation of composite PD score. Normality through log transformation was assumed for flow cytometry cell fractions and cytokine levels.

Protocol deviations

Investigators were asked to document and promptly report to the Sponsor and institutional review board and/or ethics committee any protocol deviations that might have had an impact on patient safety and/or data integrity. Protocol deviations were reviewed by the Sponsor to assess whether any represented a serious breach of Good Clinical Practice guidelines and required reporting to health authorities. Prospective requests to deviate from the protocol were not allowed. Corrective measures are identified in the summary of protocol amendments, which has been included in the Supplementary Information.
