SFTGNN: An Efficient Spherical Fourier Transform-Enhanced Graph Neural Network for High-Accuracy Crystal Property Prediction
Authors: Zhen Jiang, Hua Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Crystalline materials, characterized by periodic atomic arrangements, play a fundamental role in materials science due to the strong correlation between their structure and physical properties. While graph neural networks (GNNs) have shown promise in predicting crystal properties, many existing models either neglect crucial angular features such as bond angles or suffer from high computational costs, as seen in methods like ALIGNN with O(k 2) complexity for local angle calculations. In this work, we introduce SFTGNN, a novel architecture that integrates spherical Fourier transforms (SFT) with GNNs to efficiently incorporate three-dimensional geometric information into crystal property prediction. By projecting atomic local environments into the spherical harmonic domain, SFTGNN captures angular dependencies without the need to explicitly enumerate all bond angles, while reducing the computational complexity of bond angle encoding from O(k 2) to O(k) and obviating the requirement for additional graph construction. We have validated the effectiveness of this method and evaluated the predictive performance of the model on the Materials Project and Jarvis data sets. SFTGNN achieves state-of-the-art results in multiple crystal property prediction tasks, while training 5.3 times faster than ALIGNN. These results highlight the potential of combining spatial geometric representations with graph-based learning for efficient and accurate modeling of crystal structures.

Introduction

The prediction of the properties of crystal materials has always been a core issue in materials science and solid-state physics. With the rapid development of computer simulation techniques and machine learning methods, calculations based on first-principles, such as density functional theory (DFT), and data-driven machine learning methods have gradually become important tools for the research of crystal materials. These methods can provide powerful theoretical support for material design and optimization, and they have demonstrated significant application potential especially in the discovery of new functional materials and the performance optimization of existing materials.

In traditional machine learning methods, it is usually necessary to manually design crystal descriptors to represent the characteristics of crystals. However, this approach lacks universality because different property predictions often require different descriptor designs. In contrast, deep learning methods can avoid feature engineering. They can directly extract high-order abstract features from the input data through the model and automatically learn effective crystal descriptors. Crystal structures are composed of unit cells that are periodically arranged in three-dimensional space. Therefore, in deep learning, it is crucial to accurately represent and utilize this structural information. For the atoms in each unit cell, their chemical environments must be precisely expressed, and the mapping relationship between this environment and the properties of the crystals must be learned through a deep learning model. Currently, the widely adopted approach is to represent crystals as graph data and use graph neural networks (GNNs) to process them in order to complete the prediction of crystal properties.

In traditional modeling of crystal structure graphs, the nodes of the graph are represented as atoms in the unit cell, and the edges between nodes are represented as chemical bonds between atoms. In this approach, the chemical bonds between atoms are merely based on distance information, while ignoring the geometric information on the angles between chemical bonds within the crystal. For a 3D structure like a crystal, this part of the information is crucial. Moreover, for many important material properties (especially electronic properties such as the bandgap), they are highly sensitive to structural properties such as bond angles and local geometric distortions. Therefore, in order to represent the crystal structure more accurately, it is necessary to explicitly include the angular information.

Prior work has addressed this need by explicitly enumerating all bond angles around each atom and incorporating these angles during message passing; ALIGNN is a representative example. Because it can represent the crystal structure more accurately and has achieved excellent prediction performance of crystal properties.

However, this approach introduces practical drawbacks: it requires constructing additional graph data and incurs substantial computational overhead during both training and inference. For instance, in crystal graph modeling, consider a central atom with k neighboring atoms. Each neighboring atom forms a distinct chemical bond with the central atom, resulting in a total of k chemical bonds; enumerating all distinct pairwise bond angles among these k bonds produces C k 2 unique angles. Specifically, when encoding angular information via explicit enumeration of these pairwise bond angles, the computational complexity scales as O(k 2), a quadratic dependency that often leads to substantial degradation in the model’s computational efficiency, especially for systems with dense local atomic environments.

We have observed that the angular information on chemical bonds within the local chemical environment of the central atom is a global property: the direction of any single bond, when altered, affects the angles between all other chemical bonds and that bond. Accordingly, we model the local atomic environment holistically rather than explicitly enumerating bond-angle pairs. To this end, we propose SFTGNN, which maps the local chemical environment of atoms in crystals from the spatial domain to the spherical harmonic domain using spherical Fourier transform (SFT), effectively encoding the complex three-dimensional geometries of crystal structures. Combined with graph neural networks (GNN), our model achieves the mapping from the spherical harmonic domain to the property domain for accurate pattern recognition. We conducted comprehensive experiments on the Material Project and Jarvis data sets to evaluate our method. The experimental results demonstrate that our method not only accurately models the crystal structure but also achieves state-of-the-art performance in multiple crystal property prediction tasks. Moreover, when handling angular information, the computational complexity of our method is only O(k) and no additional graph data is required, significantly improving computational efficiency.

Background and Related Work

Crystal Representation

The structure of a crystal can be represented as a unit cell that repeats periodically and infinitely in 3D Euclidean space. The unit cell contains the smallest repeatable structure of the crystal. Specifically, let n be the number of atoms in the unit cell, and the crystal can be represented as M = ( A , L ). Here, A={(xi,pi)}i=1n where x i represents the atomic type of the ith atom in the unit cell, usually denoted by the atomic number, and p i represents its position in the unit cell. L=[l1,l2,l3]ϵR3×3 is the lattice matrix, which describes how the unit cell repeats in 3D space, as shown in Figure a.

Although crystal is three-dimensional in nature, a two-dimensional schematic is used for clarity. (b) Local chemical environment and multiedge graph construction: The dashed circle represents the spatial extent of the central atom’s local chemical environment. All atoms within this region, excluding the central atom itself, are connected to it via edges. (c) The resulting multiedge graph generated by modeling all atoms within a unit cell: in the local environment of the yellow atom, two corresponding atoms from adjacent unit cells are also connected to it. In the complete crystal structure, each atom in the unit cell repeats itself periodically in 3D space. Specifically, given any integer vector k∈Z3 and the unit cell structure A , the positions and types of all atoms in the crystal can be obtained as A k = {x i , p i + k L }. Therefore, the complete crystal structure Ã can be represented as 1 Ã=∪k∈Z3Ak

To adapt to the input of graph neural networks, crystal structures are usually represented as graph data. In this representation, atoms within the unit cell are represented as nodes in the graph, and chemical bonds between atoms are represented as edges in the graph. , For practical considerations, when constructing the edges of the crystal graph, only the local chemical environment within a certain range centered around an atom is considered. A commonly used approach is to select all atoms within a certain cutoff distance from the central atom as neighbors and establish edges. The cutoff radius is typically set to 4 Å or 8 Å. − Another commonly used method is the fixed number of nearest neighbors (k-nearest neighbors method): selecting a fixed number (such as the first k) of the atoms closest to the central atom as neighbors. ,−

Since the range of the local chemical environment set for the central atom usually includes unit cells other than the current one, and more importantly, to reflect the characteristics of the infinite periodic repetition of crystals, as shown in Figure b,c, during the process of edge construction, the number of nodes in the graph is not expanded. Instead, the indices of the edges established with atoms in adjacent unit cells are transferred to the atoms at the corresponding positions in the current unit cell, thereby constructing a multiedge graph with all atoms in a single unit cell as nodes.

GNNs for Crystalline Materials

Graph neural networks (GNNs) for materials modeling are commonly developed for two related but distinct objectives: (i) property predictiondirectly predicting scalar or state-dependent properties from crystal graphs; and (ii) interatomic potential learning, which approximates the potential energy surface to enable molecular dynamics (MD) by predicting total energies, atomic forces, and, when relevant, stresses and magnetic moments. Our work addresses the first objective.

Property-Prediction GNNs

SchNet introduced continuous-filter convolutions over interatomic distances that naturally handle periodic boundary conditions (PBCs), providing translation and permutation invariance together with an invariant readout for scalar targets (e.g., formation energy, band gap, elastic moduli). MEGNet augments the crystal graph with node, edge, and global state features, allowing explicit conditioning on thermodynamic variables so that related targets across states can be learned jointly. ALIGNN emphasized the role of angular information by constructing an atomistic line graph in which bonds are nodes and pairs of bonds sharing an atom are connected; message passing alternates between the crystal graph and its line graph, injecting angular cues into atom- and bond-level embeddings (see Figure ). This mechanism entails enumerating bond angles around each atom (i.e., neighbor pairs), so angular relations are modeled explicitly rather than inferred solely from distances. Matformer further clarifies symmetry requirements for crystalline property prediction by jointly enforcing Euclidean E(3) invariance at the unit-cell level and periodic invariance across equivalent periodic choices (e.g., boundary shifts or integer supercells); it respects cross-boundary neighbors and incorporates lattice information so that both local geometry and global periodic patterns are captured. Complementarily, PotNet integrates explicit interatomic potential terms and efficient approximations of infinite range periodic summations, mitigating finite cutoff limitations and improving accuracy on standard property benchmarks.

Illustration of the line graph construction process in the ALIGNN model. In the illustration on the right, the orange lines serve as the vertices in the line graph, while the green lines represent the edges between these vertices, with their angles used as edge features.

Guided by these insights, we adopt the message passing neural network paradigm for supervised property prediction, emphasizing E(3) invariance encoders, invariant readouts, explicit treatment of periodicity, and physics informed descriptors. We do not train on reference forces nor validate via MD; evaluation is conducted on static-structure, scalar property-prediction tasks.

Interatomic-Potential Models (Scope and Distinction)

While sharing similar graph encoders, interatomic-potential models optimize different targets and therefore impose stricter symmetry: internal features and outputs are O(3) equivariant, ensuring that vectors and tensors (e.g., forces and stresses) transform correctly under rotations and reflections. MACE exemplifies higher order O(3) equivariant message passing for efficient representation of many-body interactions. CHGNet is a large-scale pretrained interatomic potential that jointly predicts total energies, atomic forces, stresses, and magnetic moments, enabling molecular dynamics simulations that explicitly account for electronic charge states and magnetic ordering. PET-MAD couples point–edge Transformers with broad pretraining and attains competitive accuracy on energies and forces in downstream simulations. Despite architectural affinities with property prediction models, interatomic potential methods differ in supervised targets, data sets, and evaluation protocols: common assessments include errors on atomic forces and the stability and physical fidelity of MD trajectories over long time scales, whereas property prediction studies typically report errors on scalar properties computed from static structures.

Spherical Fourier Transform

Spherical Harmonics Functions and Spherical Fourier Transform

The essence of the Fourier transform lies in decomposing complex signals into linear combinations of orthogonal basis functions. For one-dimensional signals, sine, cosine functions (or complex exponential functions) serve as the basis functions. However, when describing systems with spherical symmetry in three-dimensional space, spherical harmonic functions are indispensable.

Spherical harmonic functions Y l m (θ, φ) are orthogonal and complete basis functions defined on the surface of a 3D sphere. They are solutions to Laplace’s equation in spherical coordinates and can characterize the angular dependence of functions on the sphere. Here l denotes the degree (angular-momentum index), m the order, θ the polar (colatitude) angle, and φ the azimuthal angle. For detailed definitions see Supporting Information, Section S1.

The orthogonality and completeness of spherical harmonic functions enable any function f(θ, φ) defined on the sphere to be expanded as a weighted sum of spherical harmonic functions. This expansion process constitutes the spherical Fourier transform (SFT), which includes two key operations:

Forward transform (from spatial domain to spherical harmonic domain): calculates the weight coefficients α l m , which reflect the contribution of each spherical harmonic functions to the target function. The formula is 2 αlm=∫0π∫02πf(θ,φ)Ylm(θ,φ)sin⁡θdθdφ

Inverse transform (from spherical harmonic domain to spatial domain): reconstructs the target function through a weighted sum. The formula is 3 f(θ,φ)=∑l=0∞∑m=−llαlmYlm(θ,φ)

The above two equations are the inverse transform and the forward transform of the spherical Fourier transform, respectively. This indicates that for the target function f(θ, φ), the information it represents can be compressed into a set of spherical harmonic coefficients α = [α0 0, α1 –1, α0 0, α0 1...]. This compact coefficient representation not only retains critical angular information but also avoids the computational redundancy of explicit spatial-domain descriptions. For practical reasons, an infinite number of degrees of spherical harmonic functions are not used. However, the more spherical harmonic functions are employed, the stronger the representational ability, and the linear combination of spherical harmonic functions will be closer to the target function. In this way, the target function is transformed from the spatial domain to the spherical harmonics domain through the spherical Fourier transform. Similar to the frequency domain of the conventional Fourier transform, we can transform certain specific signals to the spherical harmonics domain for analysis, which lays the foundation for our subsequent integration with GNN.

Application of Spherical Harmonics in Material Modeling

Prior work has already explored spherical harmonics for angular encoding in graph neural networks. In particular, MACE employs spherical harmonics within an orthogonal group (O(3)) equivariant tensor framework so that internal features and outputs transform correctly under rotations. This design is suited to interatomic potentials that predict energies, atomic forces, and often stresses or magnetic moments, where vector and tensor quantities must rotate consistently. Spherical harmonics in MACE are coupled via irreducible-representation algebra (for example, Clebsch–Gordan products) to propagate equivariant multibody interactions with few message passing layers.

By contrast, SFTGNN targets scalar crystal property prediction under periodic boundary conditions and therefore adopts a Euclidean (E(3)) invariant formulation with an invariant readout. We combine spherical harmonics with a spherical Fourier transform to obtain a compact, invariant encoding of each atom’s entire local angular environment. This avoids explicit enumeration of pairwise angles or construction of a line graph and yields linear scaling with the neighbor count. Periodicity is treated explicitly at the graph and feature levels so that equivalent periodic representations of the same crystal produce consistent predictions.

In summary, both models use spherical harmonics but for different purposes: MACE uses them to maintain O(3) equivariance for vector and tensor targets in interatomic potential learning, whereas SFTGNN uses them to build an efficient invariant angular representation for scalar properties on periodic crystals. To enable a direct comparison, we implemented an adapted MACE baseline with an invariant readout and periodic crystals and evaluated it on our scalar property benchmarks; the adaptation details and results are provided in the Supporting Information, Section S3.

The Relationship between Spherical Harmonic Functions and Electron Orbitals

In quantum mechanics, the electron wave function, especially the wave function ψ describing electrons in an atom, is usually decomposed into a radial part R(r) and an angular part Y(θ, φ). For systems with spherically symmetric potential energy, the angular part of the wave function can be represented by spherical harmonic functions. 4 ψ(r,θ,φ)=R(r)Y(θ,φ) For the radial part, it represents the distance distribution of electrons from the atomic nucleus; for the angular part, it represents the angular distribution of electrons on the spherical surface. In quantum mechanics, the spherical harmonic function Y l m is actually a mathematical tool for describing the angular momentum and angular distribution of electrons. Each electron orbital (such as the s orbital, p orbital, etc.) can be represented by different forms of spherical harmonic functions.

Depending on the value of the angular momentum quantum number l, the shapes of electron orbitals and the forms of spherical harmonic functions differ. Common types of electron orbitals include s orbitals, p orbitals, d orbitals, and f orbitals. Each orbital corresponds to distinct spherical harmonic functions, as illustrated in Figure . The use of spherical harmonics here means that not only does each degree of spherical harmonics represent a frequency component of the spatial distribution, but it can also represent the electronic states of different orbitals, providing the possibility for the model to simulate the complex electronic interactions within the crystal.

Illustration of the shapes of electron orbitals with different angular quantum numbers l. The superscript of each orbital label indicates its magnetic quantum number m. For example, the orbital p–1 corresponds to an electron orbital with l = 1 and m = −1.

Inside a crystal, valence electrons (mainly from s, p, and d orbitals) usually participate in interatomic interactions. Electrons in these orbitals act together through overlapping, hybridization, and the formation of energy bands, determining the crystal’s structure, electrical conductivity, magnetism, and other important physical properties. Electrons in f orbitals usually participate in interactions with ligands. Especially in metal complexes with a high coordination number, f orbitals may participate in the formation of complex coordination bonds, thereby also affecting the properties of the crystal. When the angular quantum number l = 4, it belongs to the g orbital. However, in the electron configurations of known ground-state atoms, no element has electrons filled in the g orbital. Among the known elements in nature, electrons are filled up to the f orbital at most (l = 3).

MPNN

MPNN (message passing neural networks) is an architecture of graph neural networks (GNNs) designed for processing graph-structured data. MPNNs serve as a general framework, and many other graph neural networks, such as graph convolutional networks (GCNs) and graph attention networks (GATs), can be regarded as special cases of MPNNs. They propagate information within graphs through a message passing mechanism, thereby enabling representation learning for nodes or entire graphs.

The core idea of MPNN is to pass messages between the nodes of a graph through an iterative process and update the node states. Specifically, MPNN includes the following key steps:(1)Message computation: calculate the messages received by each node from its neighboring nodes.

Methods

Modeling of Atomic Chemical Environments

Suppose there are k neighboring atoms within the fixed-range neighborhood of a certain atom Z i in the crystal. Each atom has an atomic number Z j and its respective spherical coordinates (r j , θ j , φ j ), where r j represents the distance from the central atom, while θ j and φ j are the polar and azimuthal angles, respectively. Then its local chemical environment can be defined as 9 f(θ,φ)=∑jZjδ(θ−θj,φ−φj),⁣θ∈(0,π),φ∈(0,2π) where δ­(θ, φ) denotes the Dirac function, its value is zero at all points except zero, and its integral value over the entire space is 1. In quantum mechanics, the Dirac function is usually used to represent a point source, such as the position of a particle. According to the sifting property of the Dirac function ⟨δω|f⟩ = f(ω), combined with the translation form δω(x) = δ­(x – ω), it can be used to sift out the values in a specific direction in the spherical function. The expression Z j δ­(θ – θ j , φ – φ j ) can represent the presence of a point source with a value of Z j at the position centered around the central atom i and in the direction of (θ j , φ j ). For the entire target function f(θ, φ), it describes the spatial distribution of all neighboring atoms in the local chemical environment of the central atom i. If the intensities of these point sources are simply set as the atomic numbers of the corresponding atoms, this approach will fail to retain the information on the central atom and will not be able to distinguish the local chemical environments of different central atoms. Moreover, for a given pair of atoms, different distances between them also correspond to different chemical bonds or interatomic interactions. For example, in ice crystals, there are both hydrogen–oxygen covalent bonds and hydrogen bonds between hydrogen and oxygen atoms, with different bond lengths and chemical properties. Therefore, the target function should contain more information and is defined as 10 f(θ,φ)=∑jBijδ(θ−θj,φ−φj),⁣θ∈(0,π),φ∈(0,2π) 11 Bij=(Zi,Zj,rij) where B ij is the encoding of the chemical bond formed between the central atom and the neighboring atom. Model

Feature Encoding

For each node in the crystal graph, it represents the information on the atom at the corresponding position in the unit cell. For the feature encoding of this part, we follow the atomic encoding representation in CGCNN. Specifically, we encode the chemical properties such as the group number, period number, electronegativity, covalent radius, number of covalent electrons, first ionization energy, block, and atomic volume of various elements into a 92-dimensional feature vector. These feature vectors contain abundant chemical information, which is helpful for the model to predict and calculate the properties of crystals.

To explore the impact of node feature (atomic feature) optimization on model performancea factor highlighted as crucial for material property prediction in recent studieswe have also conducted comparative experiments using alternative atomic feature representations. Detailed information about these alternative features and the corresponding experimental results are provided in Supporting Information, Section S2.

For each edge in the crystal graph pointing to the central atom i, it contains the structural information in its local chemical environment. The features of the radial part and the angular part are encoded as r ij * and s ij respectively. r ij * is obtained by using the Gaussian radial basis function to expand the spherical coordinate r ij of the neighboring atom into a high dimensional feature vector, while s ij is a feature vector preprocessed by spherical harmonics, and its calculation formula is as follows 16 sij=[Y00(θij,φij)wa,Y1−1(θij,φij)wa,Y10(θij,φij)wa,...]

These two parts of information serve as the attributes of the edges connected to the central atom i, which are used for the calculations in each interaction layer.

Message Definition

In deep learning, the distances between atoms and atom pairs are usually represented as high dimensional feature vectors. When calculating the spherical harmonic coefficients of each neighboring atom of the central atom using Formula , each component in s ij needs to be directly multiplied by the encoding of the chemical bond B ij t , which undoubtedly leads to a huge amount of computation. To address this issue, we adopt a gating mechanism similar to that in the gated recurrent unit (GRU). A small neural network is used to learn B ij t and dynamically calculate a coefficient vector. Each component in α is multiplied by a corresponding coefficient. This not only significantly reduces the computational load but also endows the model with a more expressive ability. 17 Bijt=hit∥hjt∥rij* 18 mija,t=MLP(sij)⊙δ(MLP(Bijt)) In the above formula, ∥ represents the concatenation operation, ⊙ represents the Hadamard product, δ represents the Sigmoid activation function, and MLP represents a multilayer perceptron, which consists of two linear layers and a SiLU activation function. All MLPs that appear here and subsequently have this structure.

Considering the output range of the Sigmoid function, the information in the high dimensional vector B ij t is compressed and transformed into the interval [0, 1]. To enable the spherically harmonically preprocessed s ij to adapt to this change, we also perform a nonlinear transformation on it, mapping it to a high dimensional feature space. This provides more representational power for the data and can help capture complex relationships and patterns.

The above is the modeling of the angular part of the local chemical environment of atoms in the crystal. The radial part is processed in a similar way and can be expressed as 19 mijr,t=MLP(Bijt)⊙δ(MLP(Bijt))

This processing method has been applied in models such as CGCNN and PotNet, which are based solely on distance modeling, demonstrating its feasibility. Although the message definitions of the angular and radial parts are similar in mathematical form, they have different meanings. The δ­(MLP(·)) in the radial part, as a learnable small network, is used to distinguish the interactions between neighbors, while the δ­(MLP(·)) in the angular part is used to complete the convolution calculation of the spherical harmonic coefficients. In the actual implementation of the model, the messages of the angular part and the radial part share the MLP and δ­(MLP(·)) in a concatenated manner to reduce the number of parameters and decrease the model complexity. The complete message can be defined by the following equation 20 mijt=MLP(Bijt∥sij)⊙δ(MLP(Bijt))

This message contains radial and angular information about neighboring atoms. As a compact and comprehensive feature representation, it can be used for the subsequent learning of the model.

For feature update, we employ a residual structure. This enables each Interaction Block to learn a residual mapping instead of a complete mapping. For deep networks like MPNN, it reduces the learning difficulty of the entire training task, alleviates the gradient vanishing problem, and improves the prediction performance of the entire network. Additionally, a SiLU activation function is added after the residual operation to endow the model with more nonlinear characteristics.

Finally, the calculation process in the readout stage can be described as follows 23 ŷ=MLP(Avg(hi:i∈G)) where h i represents the state vector of all atoms within the crystal unit cell. This vector already contains structural information on their respective local chemical environments that have been transformed into the high dimensional feature space. The readout module uses the average pooling operation to integrate these features and obtain the structural information on the entire crystal. Subsequently, an MLP is employed to complete the mapping from structural information to property information.

Model Efficiency Analysis

Suppose that in the local environment of an atom in a crystal, there are k neighboring atoms. For these k neighboring atoms, the geometric information (radial and angular information) of each atom is encoded into k edges connected to the central atom. During the calculation process, each edge is calculated separately, and then the messages transmitted by these k edges are summed up. The computational complexity of the angular part is O(k). For the entire crystal, assuming that there is an average of n atoms in each unit cell, the total computational complexity of the model is O(nk).

To demonstrate the efficiency of our model, we compared SFTGNN with advanced models such as ALIGNN, Matformer, PotNet and Crystalformer in terms of training time per epoch, total training time, total testing time, and the number of model parameters. These time-related tests were all conducted on the task of predicting crystal formation energy in the Jarvis data set. As can be intuitively seen from Table , our model has complexity and computational efficiency at the same level as methods like Matformer and PotNet, which only consider distance information within crystal. Compared with ALIGNN, there is a significant improvement. Specifically, the training time per epoch on the training set is only 15.9% of that of ALIGNN, the total training time is only 28.2% of ALIGNN’s, and the testing time on the test set is only 30.7% of ALIGNN’s. This significant improvement in efficiency is attributed to the low computational complexity of our model in handling angular information.

The results reveal that SFTGNN-A achieves competitive accuracy in geometry-sensitive properties (e.g., bandgap), validating the efficacy of spherical Fourier transform in capturing angular dependencies. Conversely, SFTGNN-R demonstrates robustness in energy-related tasks, highlighting the importance of radial distance encoding. Critically, SFTGNN’s superior performance across all tasks (MAE improvements up to 13.0%) confirms the methodological superiority of dual-component integration, as the combined message definition in Formula harmonizes angular geometry and radial distances to emulate real-world interatomic interactions. This ablation study not only validates the necessity of structural feature fusion but also provides a theoretical basis for designing efficient GNN architectures in crystal modeling.

To dissect the pure angular information contribution and validate the structural geometry property relationship, we further tested SFTGNN-P (pure angular model), which eliminates all radial distance cues by removing B ij ’s distance component d ij in the message definition. This design isolates the spherical Fourier transform’s capability to represent crystal geometry independently of interatomic distances, a critical methodological innovation to distinguish angular-dominated vs distance-dominated property dependencies.

Experimental results reveal a striking dichotomy: for energy related properties (formation energy, total energy), SFTGNN-P shows a 48.4% MAE degradation (Table ), confirming that bond distances are indispensable for modeling interatomic potential energy landscapes. In contrast, geometry-sensitive properties (bandgap, Ehull) exhibit only 9.2–13.0% accuracy decline, demonstrating that spherical harmonic encoding of angular features (e.g., bond angles, local geometric distortions) suffices for capturing crystal structure–property relationships. This divergence aligns with quantum mechanical insights: energy properties depend on electron density overlap (sensitive to distance), while bandgap arises from orbital angular momentum interactions (dominated by angular geometry).

Notably, SFTGNN-P’s performance decay is mitigated in properties like Ehull, where angular-dependent structural stability dominates. This validates the spherical Fourier transform’s physical interpretabilityby mapping local environments to spherical harmonic domains, the model emulates electron orbital angular distributions (e.g., p/d-orbital hybridizations), which directly influence crystal geometry-related properties. The results establish a theoretical boundary: while angular information is crucial for geometric representation, radial distance encoding remains indispensable for energy-driven property prediction, providing a principled basis for hybrid GNN architectures in materials science.

To explore the impact of the maximum degree of spherical harmonics (l max) in SFTGNN-P on crystal property prediction, we systematically tested the model’s performance on the Jarvis data set with l max varying from 0 to 4, isolating only pure angular information in the message passing process. This experimental design aims to address a crucial scientific question: how can harmonic degrees balance geometric representation accuracy, computational efficiency, and physical interpretability in crystal property prediction? The results, visualized as bar charts in Figure , reveal a distinct pattern. l max = 3 emerges as the optimal point for capturing angular dependencies. For instance, in bandgap­(OPT) prediction, the mean absolute error (MAE) drops from 0.303 eV at l max = 0 to 0.193 eV at l max = 3, reflecting a significant accuracy improvement. Increasing l max to 4 yields a MAE reduction to 0.192 eV. As Basri and Jacobs (2003) demonstrated, the contribution of spherical harmonics to the accurate representation of the target function rapidly diminishes as the degree increases. Here, the additional dimensions introduced by higher-order harmonics contribute progressively less to modeling the local atomic environment. Simultaneously, the dimensionality of s ij surges by 56.2% (from 16 to 25 dimensions) when l max goes from 3 to 4. As pointed out in previous research, blindly increasing the degree of spherical harmonics not only leads to a sharp rise in computational costs but also compromises the advantage of parameter sparsity, without bringing substantial practical benefits. This “dimensionality explosion” also exacerbates overfitting. Notably, the test error for bandgap­(MBJ) shows a discernible increase at l max = 4, indicating that the expanded feature space compromises model generalization. From a physical perspective, as discussed in Section , spherical harmonics with l = 4 (g-orbitals) are unoccupied in ground-state elements (natural filling stops at f-orbitals, l = 3), so modeling these nonphysical features disrupts the simulation of electron orbital interactions. This study establishes that l max = 3 optimally balances geometric fidelity and computational parsimony. Higher degrees offer negligible practical benefits, aligning with the “sparsity principle” in physical modeling. The findings underscore that angular encoding in crystal GNNs should prioritize physical relevance over mathematical completeness, providing a theoretical basis for efficient geometric model design.

Limitations

The Contradiction between Spherical Harmonics and the Dirac Function

In this study, for the convenience of mathematical calculations and to combine with the message passing process of the message passing neural network (MPNN), we adopted the Dirac function to represent the spatial distribution of neighboring atoms around a certain central atom in the crystal. For the Fourier transform, this belongs to local high-frequency information. Regarding the Fourier transform, this distribution is local high-frequency information. Similar to the Fourier basis functions, spherical harmonics have global support on the sphere, which makes them face the same difficulties. For example, when attempting to approximate a spherical function with characteristics of a small spatial scale using a spherical harmonic sequence, the Gibbs phenomenon often occurs. In this situation, significant pseudoglobal oscillations are introduced into the approximation. To reduce the influence of these pseudo-oscillations, higher degree spherical harmonics are required. However, the frequency of spherical harmonics increases with the increase of the order and degree. Therefore, when representing higher-frequency changes, more spherical harmonic basis functions are needed. This not only leads to a sharp increase in computational cost, but more seriously, it loses the advantage of parameter sparsity, increases the risk of model overfitting, and reduces its generalization ability. Although SFTGNN-A already contains relatively comprehensive information about the local chemical environment of crystals, due to this limitation, its prediction performance has not reached the expected level. Therefore, we have to combine it with traditional distance-based modeling methods to improve the prediction accuracy of crystal properties. Future research will explore more advanced methods, such as using spherical wavelet functions instead of spherical harmonics, so as to more effectively fit local high-frequency information, alleviate the contradictions in the current method, and improve the prediction performance.

Physical Interpretability

Although the spherical harmonics used in our method correspond to the electronic states of different orbitals in quantum mechanics, which endows the model with profound physical significance in simulating the complex electronic interactions within the crystal. However, whether this process actually exists, and if so, how its specific mechanism operates, remains a question that urgently requires in-depth study. Future work will be dedicated to further exploring this issue, thereby providing the model with more explicit physical interpretability.

Modeling of Infinite-Range Potential Energy

Crystals are substances composed of unit cells that are periodically and infinitely repeated in three-dimensional space. The success of PotNet and Crystalformer has demonstrated that the calculation of the interatomic potential energy within an infinite range in crystals is crucial for accurately representing crystal structures and precisely predicting crystal properties. However, the model we proposed is still limited to a finite range in the modeling of the chemical environment within the crystal. This affects the model’s ability to capture the interactions between distant atoms, thus influencing the prediction accuracy. To overcome this limitation, in the future, we plan to adopt a graph neural network (GNN) based on a heterogeneous graph and combine it with the method proposed in this paper to further enhance the prediction performance.

Conclusions

The high complexity of calculating the bond angle information in the crystal structure seriously reduces the computational efficiency of the prediction performance of deep learning models. To alleviate this problem, we propose a method that combines spherical Fourier transform with GNN to process the bond angle information of the crystal structure more efficiently, reducing the time complexity of the relevant calculations from quadratic to linear. This not only significantly improves the computational efficiency of the model but also achieves state-of-the-art prediction performance. The results show that using spherical Fourier transform to map the structural information of crystals to the spherical harmonic domain and using GNN for pattern recognition can not only accurately represent the crystal structure but also ensure high computational efficiency.
