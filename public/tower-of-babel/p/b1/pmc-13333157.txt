Association of Valvular Disease Progression Using Echocardiographic Findings in a Large U.S. Cohort
Authors: Abes A Bautista Neughebauer, Amin Yehya, Raymond L Benza, Deepak Talreja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe prevalence of valvular disease in the US is approximately 2.5%. Given that over 5 million Americans are affected, it is crucial to find those at higher risk of progression. The goal of this paper is to uncover which patients are more likely to have valvular disease progression using demographic factors, EKG and echocardiogram (ECHO) findings.MethodsThis paper utilized the EchoNext Database, which pairs 100,000 electrocardiograms and echocardiograms based on specific structural heart disease labels. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to find risk factors for progression for aortic stenosis (AS), aortic regurgitation (AR), mitral regurgitation (MR), tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and pulmonary insufficiency (PI). Progression was defined as worsened valvular disease on follow up ECHOs.ResultsThere were 100,000 encounters of EKGs/ECHOs included in the study, and 36,286 individual patients. Independent risk factors of AS progression were age and QRS duration. Age, QRS duration, AS, AR, MR and pericardial effusion were independent risk factors for AR progression. MR progression was independently associated with PR, QRS and QTc durations, MR and left ventricular ejection fraction. QRS duration, MR, TR and right ventricular function were independent factors for TR progression. Lastly, for PI progression, male sex and AS were found to be independent risk factors.ConclusionsThe combination of demographic, electrocardiographic and echocardiographic findings can help better assess the progression of valvular disease.Keywords: ECG, echocardiography, progression, risk factors, valvular heart disease

Introduction

Valvular heart disease (VHD) is an increasingly prevalent pathology, affecting 11.7% of the individuals older than 75 in the United States, with over half remaining undiagnosed [1, 2, 3]. Although many remain asymptomatic for years, cardiovascular morbidity and structural remodeling still occur [1, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Despite advances in treatment modalities, patients may be identified for intervention too late based off current guidelines. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the gold standard for both diagnosis and follow up of VHD, having replaced more invasive diagnostic methods [1, 3]. Several established predictors of progression are derived from subsequent TTEs.

Among the valvular pathologies, most research combining echocardiographic and ECG features has focused on AS. LBBB, QRS prolongation, Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH) and strain patterns have been identified as predictors for progression. For other left‐sided or right‐sided VHD, evidence remains limited [1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. LVH/strain patterns have been linked to progression of AR, while p wave changes, QRS widening and atrial fibrillation are seen in advanced MR. For right sided VHD, atrial fibrillation/flutter, right axis deviation, and T wave changes are associated with worse disease severity [8, 9, 11]. Current guidelines for VHD stratify disease stages (A–D) based on anatomy, symptoms, ventricular/atrial remodeling, and hemodynamic consequences, but do not incorporate ECG‐based predictors of progression [12, 13]. Furthermore, timing of intervention is largely driven by symptoms. The aim of this paper is to highlight that serious cardiac remodeling occurs despite being asymptomatic, and that the recommended intervals for follow up may be too long for certain patients. Integrating ECG, echocardiographic findings and clinical data offers a more precise approach to risk stratification and improves the timing for interventions.

Methods

Patient Identification and Data Sources

We utilized the EchoNext Database [14, 15, 16], which pairs 100,000 electrocardiograms and ECHOs based on specific structural heart disease (SHD) labels. Patients between 18 and 90 years old at Columbia University Irving Medical Center who had a 12‐lead ECG and TTE in the same encounter were identified between 2008–2022. Overall, 36,286 individual patients were included. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was waived due to the use of publicly available and already deidentified patient data. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. GE MUSE ECG management system was used to extract data from each ECG at a frequency of 250 Hz. This was then paired with variables to help describe the dataset, including age, PR interval, QRS interval, atrial rate, age, and sex.

Syngo Dynamics (Siemens) and Xcelera (Philips) systems were used to access echocardiographic data. TTE with prosthetic valves, missing LVEF or wall thickness measurements were excluded. Continuous variables included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), interventricular septum thickness (IVS), posterior wall thickness, pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) and TR maximum velocity (TR Max Velocity). Categorical variables included AR, MR, TR, PI (none/trace, mild, moderate, severe), AS (none/trace, mild, moderate, severe), RV systolic function (normal, mildly reduced, moderately reduced, severely reduced), and pericardial effusion (none/trace, small, moderate, large).

Each ECG was ascribed with a binary label to indicate presence of SHD from the echocardiographic findings from the same encounter. To be labeled as positive, the ECG must have been performed within 1 year before the diagnostic TTE.

For each patient with VHD, progression was determined by evaluating successive ECHOs. For patients who had worse VHD or new‐onset development of VHD between successive exams, VHD progression was noted. More specifically, the baseline ECHOs were compared with serial follow‐up ECHOs obtained during the study period. The development of VHD in absence of baseline VHD on prior echocardiography was considered VHD progression (i.e. none to mild, none to moderate or none to severe). Additionally, worsening VHD severity on successive ECHOs was also classified as VHD progression (i.e. mild to moderate, moderate to severe or mild to severe). Patients that only had a single encounter and no follow‐up exams were excluded from further analyses, as it would not be possible to document progression. Patients with multiple encounters were included in the analysis, as restricting the analysis to a single follow‐up encounter per patient would introduce selection bias by disproportionately excluding patients with longer follow‐up or higher healthcare utilization. As the primary objective was the assessment of progression events across serial studies, this design was deemed appropriate to avoid underestimation of disease progression and introduction of selection bias.

Statistical Analysis

Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for VHD progression. All available variables were included in the univariate analysis, using t‐test, chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Continuous variables (e.g. age, ventricular rate (VR), PR interval) were divided into two categories based on the median value for each variable in the entire cohort. This was favored to the use of clinically abnormal cutoffs in order to facilitate interpretation across multiple regression models and hypothesis‐generating. This further permits the identification of subclinical changes associated with VHD progression

Variables that were significantly associated with valvular disease progression on univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. To account for within‐patient correlation arising from repeated echocardiographic examinations, multivariable analyses were performed using generalized estimating equation (GEE) logistic regression models with a binomial distribution. An exchangeable working correlation structure was specified, with patient identifier used as the clustering variable. Given the large number of candidate predictors and substantial correlation between related electrocardiographic and echocardiographic measures, ordinal variables were dichotomized into binary variables for the multivariate GEE models. This aimed to reduce model complexity and multicollinearity among related variables. Similarly, continuous electrocardiographic and echocardiographic parameters were dichotomized at their cohort median values to facilitate model interpretation and maintain adequate events‐per‐variable ratios. As typical for all multivariate models, cases with missing data for the variables included in the multivariate model were removed from the analysis. The threshold of significance was set at 0.05 and two‐tailed statistical tests were used for all comparisons. Model overfitting was assessed using conventional events‐per‐variable (EPV) recommendations for logistic regression [17]. Bonferroni's correction was used for adjusting for multiple comparisons by adjusting the threshold of significance for the number of variables included in the final multivariate model for each type of VHD. The analysis was done on SPSS v.22 (IBM Corporation, New York, USA). Aortic Stenosis (AS)

A total of 2,847 (7.8%) patients had AS at baseline and a total of 428 progressions of AS were observed. In the multivariable model, age >63 years was the strongest independent risk factor of AS progression (OR 2.67, 95% CI 1.95–3.66; p < 0.001). Prolonged QRS duration (>90 ms) was also independently associated with progression (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.09–1.78; p = 0.009, Table 2 , Table S1 , Figure 1A ).

Aortic Regurgitation (AR)

A total of 4,674 (12.9%) patients had baseline AR and a total of 809 progressions of AR were observed. In the multivariable GEE model, age >63 years (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.09–1.63; p = 0.005), QRS duration >90 ms (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.13–1.60; p = 0.001), baseline AS (OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.28–2.21; p < 0.001), and baseline MR (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.16–1.71; p < 0.001) were independently associated with AR progression. Conversely, baseline AR (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.10–0.40; p < 0.001) and pericardial effusion (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64–0.91; p = 0.003) were associated with lower odds of progression (Table S2 , Figure 1B ).

Mitral Regurgitation (MR)

A total of 11,578 (31.9%) patients had baseline MR. During follow‐up, a total of 2,840 progressions of MR were observed. In the multivariable GEE model, PR interval >154 ms (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.05–1.26; p = 0.004), QRS duration >90 ms (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.12–1.36; p < 0.001) and QTc >449 ms (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.08–1.31; p < 0.001) were independently associated with MR progression. In contrast, baseline MR (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.18–0.26; p < 0.001), pericardial effusion (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79–0.97; p = 0.014), and LVEF >57.5% (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.85; p < 0.001) were associated with lower odds of MR progression (Table S3 , Figure 2A ).

TR

A total of 12,673 (34.9%) patients had TR at baseline and a total of 3,462 progressions of TR were observed. In the multivariable GEE model, QRS duration >90 ms (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.23; p = 0.009), baseline MR (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.17–1.46; p < 0.001) and reduced RV function (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.20–1.55; p < 0.001) were independently associated with TR progression. In contrast, baseline TR was associated with lower odds of progression (OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.11–0.16; p < 0.001, Table S4 , Figure 2B ).

Pulmonary Insufficiency (PI)

A total of 3,549 (9.8%) patients had PI at baseline. During follow‐up, a total of 1,504 progressions of PI were observed. In the multivariable GEE model, male sex (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.06–1.38; p = 0.005) was independently associated with PI progression, whereas baseline AS was associated with lower odds of progression (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.50–0.93; p = 0.017). Baseline PI could not be included in the final multivariable GEE model because of quasi‐complete separation resulting in model non‐convergence (Table S5 , Figure 2C ).

Discussion

Utilizing a large database of ECG and echocardiographic findings, we determined risk factors for VHD progression. To our knowledge, this represents the largest such effort aiming at risk stratification for VHD.

Many papers describe how electrocardiographic markers reflect ventricular remodeling, such as QRS prolongation and LVH/strain. Current literature has expounded on these as important correlates of disease severity in AS. This paper found older age and QRS duration to be independent risk factors for AS progression. In asymptomatic native AS, modest QRS prolongation (≥100 ms) is associated with a five‐fold increased risk of sudden cardiac death, while ECG LVH with strain on CMR (Cardiac Magnetic Resonance) indicates fibrosis and predicts adverse events [18, 19, 20]. This paper further highlights those findings and aims to more closely link ECG changes to AS progression itself. Degenerative AS is primarily driven by valve calcification and fibrosis, especially in aging adults. In addition to more frequent comorbidities (e.g. hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease), age accelerates mechanical stress and calcific burden, augmenting inflammatory and osteogenic signaling, overtime worsening baseline AS. In this paper, wider QRS, which typically reflects underlying conduction abnormalities, was identified as a risk factor for AS progression and other studies have demonstrated a 10‐ms increase in QRS duration confers an 18% increase in cardiovascular risk, consistent with progressive myocardial fibrosis and slowed conduction observed in AS [21].

AR progression has several risk factors, including older age, QRS duration, baseline AS, AR, MR and presence of pericardial effusion. AR and pericardial effusion were found to be protective. Understanding the distinction between AS and AR is essential to interpret the following findings. AS is characterized by pressure overload, and subsequent concentric LV hypertrophy, whereas AR is associated with eccentric hypertrophy from chronic LV overload. This type of remodeling changes the myocardial structural and conduction properties, and therefore the depolarization phase of the action potential [22]. AR progression is affected by valve/root pathology, including decreased elastin, increased collagen cross‐linking, age‐related leaflet prolapse, and increased aortic root dilation, all of which are distinct from the calcific degeneration seen in AS [23]. Although ventricular ion‐channel remodeling is well described in LVH and heart failure, specific alterations in K+, Ca2 +, or Na+ channel expression are not well characterized in AR, nor in vivo QT prolongation directly caused by such changes. However, indirect evidence supports that the structural remodeling of chronic volume overload may alter repolarization properties and QTc prolongation may reflect advanced myocardial remodeling in AR [24, 25, 26, 27]. Closer follow‐up protocols should be implemented in these higher‐risk patient groups. The relationship between AR progression and pericardial effusion is less understood but may be associated with underlying heart failure and common risk factors [24, 25, 26, 27].

PR, QRS and QTc durations, MR and LVEF were found to be statistically significant risk factors for MR progression. Literature regarding MR has often centered on atrial remodeling, atrial fibrillation and P‐wave changes [28, 29, 30]. QRS and QTc prolongation may reflect the underlying LV remodeling, like in AR. This is only meant to be interpreted as an observational association. Higher baseline MR was protective as both the mitral annulus and LV are often maximally dilated, reducing the odds of further anatomic worsening.

TR progression was linked to QRS duration, MR, TR and RV function, with baseline TR being protective of subsequent progression. Like MR, studies on TR often describe the connection to Afib/Aflutter in the context of eccentric hypertrophy [31, 32, 33]. QRS duration carries prognostic value as an independent risk factor, representing mechanical‐electrical dyssynchrony. In TR, RV volume overload leads to tricuspid annular dilation and distortion of the His‐Purkinje system, leading to intraventricular conduction delay. Another risk factor for TR progression is baseline TR severity. Severe TR progresses slower, as the tricuspid annulus and RV are maximally dilated. It is therefore not unexpected that declining RV function is a risk factor of TR progression. PI progression was associated with male sex and AS, while baseline PI was not included in the model due to quasi‐complete separation and almost perfect protection against PI progression. Higher baseline PI severity can similarly be described via maximal dilation of the pulmonary annulus and RV, protecting against further worsening of the disease.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces the potential for residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment, particularly as patients undergoing serial echocardiography often differ from those who do not require follow‐up imaging. Second, the timing and frequency of echocardiographic and electrocardiographic assessments were not standardized and reflected routine clinical practice rather than a predefined imaging protocol. As a result, patients underwent variable numbers of follow‐up examinations at inconsistent intervals, and progression rates may have been over‐ or under‐estimated depending on surveillance intensity and healthcare utilization. Patients with lower healthcare access or less frequent follow‐up may therefore have had fewer opportunities for progression events to be detected, raising the likelihood of misclassification bias. Furthermore, because the dataset only provided examination years rather than exact dates, formal time‐to‐event analyses (e.g. Cox regression) could not be reliably performed. Third, although established ECG markers were evaluated, ECG interpretation may vary across readers, and subtle repolarization abnormalities may be influenced by comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, CKD) rather than valvular pathology alone. Fourth, echocardiographic quantification of valvular disease severity remains operator‐dependent and subject to inter‐observer variability, which may impact the accuracy of progression measurements and increase the likelihood of misclassification bias. Fifth, our cohort may not fully represent early‐stage, asymptomatic disease, given that patients who receive serial imaging are often older or have more comorbidities, limiting generalizability. Sixth, we lacked systematic cardiac MRI or biomarker data, restricting the ability to directly correlate ECG abnormalities with myocardial fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, or subclinical myocardial disease. Additionally, the analysis was limited to demographic, electrocardiographic, and echocardiographic variables available within the dataset, and several important clinical confounders associated with VHD progression were not captured, including hypertension, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, VHD etiology, bicuspid aortic valve, prior valve interventions, medication use, and the clinical indication for repeat imaging. Therefore, residual confounding is possible, and the identified associations should be interpreted within the context of the observational design. Model overfitting was not a concern as the large number of progression events minimized this concern using commonly accepted criteria [17]. Finally, social determinants of health, such as access to routine ECHO follow up and financial restraints reflect limitation in evaluating progression of valvular disease in minority groups.

In conclusion, our paper highlights the utility and feasibility of applying electrocardiographic markers to evaluate the progression of VHD. This illustrates that electrical remodeling often precedes or parallels structural deterioration and may serve as an early warning signal for accelerated valve dysfunction. Given the effective cost of ECG and its wide accessibility, integrating ECG abnormalities with echocardiographic findings provides a holistic method for risk stratification. Study limitations include the retrospective nature, lack of standardization of follow‐up intervals, dichotomization of continuous variables and lack of important clinical confounders, suggesting these results should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating rather than validative predictive tools. Risk stratification with the integration of electrical markers and classical echocardiographic parameters may provide an opportunity for earlier identification of high‐risk patients, more tailored surveillance intervals, and potentially earlier intervention.
