The janus-faced role of uric acid in COPD: evidence from East Asians population
Authors: Shengyuan Zheng, Zehao Yu, Xiongfeng Tang, Yanguo Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uric acid as the final purine metabolite exhibits both antioxidative and proinflammatory properties. The association between uric acid and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains ambiguous. This is particularly true within East Asian populations which lack comprehensive evidence. This study synthesizes data from 20 observational studies via a meta-analysis to compare uric acid levels in COPD patients and healthy controls. Furthermore, a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach was utilized, employing genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from East Asian populations to investigate the genetic causal relationship between uric acid and COPD. The meta-analysis demonstrated that uric acid concentrations in patients with COPD were significantly elevated compared to those in healthy controls (SMD = 1.57, 95% (CI): 1.11–2.02). This disparity was more pronounced in patients undergoing acute exacerbations and those with more severe airflow limitations. Conversely, MR analysis indicated that elevated uric acid levels were associated with a reduced risk of developing COPD (OR = 0.680, 95% CI: 0.543–0.852). The MR findings were corroborated through sensitivity analyses. Genetically predicted higher serum uric acid was causally associated with a lower risk of developing COPD in East Asians, whereas phenotypically elevated uric acid in patients with established COPD was associated with greater disease severity and exacerbation, suggesting distinct roles of uric acid in COPD susceptibility versus disease progression.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent condition characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation, primarily resulting from structural abnormalities in the alveoli or airway lesions1. This disease imposes a significant global health burden, with incidence and mortality rates of 2744 per 100,000 and 42.4 per 100,000, respectively2. Although a complete cure for COPD remains elusive, it is regarded as a preventable condition3. Consequently, identifying modifiable risk factors and developing effective predictive indicators are essential for the prevention and management of COPD.

Uric acid, the final product of purine metabolism, serves as an indicator of oxidative stress and inflammatory status in the body4,5. Current research indicates a complex relationship between uric acid and COPD. On one hand, uric acid possesses potent antioxidant properties and plays a crucial role in the respiratory epithelium, aiding in the defense against external oxidative damage6,7. On the other hand, patients with COPD frequently experience hypoxemia. This condition induces abnormalities in adenosine metabolism and the subsequent release of purine intermediates, resulting in elevated uric acid production2. Although some studies propose that uric acid levels may significantly influence the development and progression of COPD such as the prediction of lung function decline8,9, other studies contend that no causal relationship exists between the two10. The heterogeneity of existing studies contributes to the ambiguity surrounding the precise relationship between uric acid and COPD. This uncertainty is particularly pronounced in East Asian populations, where genetic evidence is insufficient.

We focused on East Asian populations for several reasons. First, serum uric acid metabolism exhibits population-specific genetic architecture. Key urate transport genes, including SLC2A9 and ABCG2, show differences in allele frequency and effect size across ancestries, with ABCG2 variants playing a particularly prominent role in East Asian populations11,12. These genetic differences may influence both uric acid regulation and its downstream biological effects. Second, the epidemiological profile of COPD differs substantially in Asia compared with Western populations. In addition to cigarette smoking, exposure to biomass fuel combustion and indoor air pollution remains an important contributor to COPD risk in many Asian regions. Such environmental factors may interact with oxidative stress pathways in which uric acid participates13,14. Finally, the current Mendelian randomization studies exploring the association between uric acid and respiratory system diseases have certain limitations in terms of population representativeness. Especially in the East Asian population, there are still relatively few related studies. We need multi-regional and multi-ethnic studies to clarify whether the relationship between uric acid and COPD is consistent among the general population.

To address the limitations of previous research, this study integrates uric acid data from both COPD patients and healthy controls. This study conducted a meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the phenotypic association between these groups. Furthermore, we utilized Mendelian Randomization (MR) to investigate the genetic causal relationship by leveraging genetic variants that are fixed at conception and thus less susceptible to environmental influences. The objective is to elucidate the association between uric acid levels and COPD in East Asian populations, thereby bridging the gap in regional genetic research and providing novel scientific evidence for early risk prediction and pathogenesis studies of COPD.

Methods

Meta-analysis

Search strategy and protocol

We conducted a comprehensive search in Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and CNKI to identify all relevant studies from database inception to July 31, 2025. Search strategies were tailored to each database, with full strategies detailed in Table S1. This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Material 3) and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251040917)15.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this review were specifically defined as follows: (1) Study design: Cohort studies, case-control studies, or cross-sectional studies. (2) Study population: The case group comprised patients with COPD diagnosed via pulmonary function tests (FEV1/FVC < 0.70) or hospital medical records, while the control group consisted of healthy individuals matched by age, geographic region, healthcare level, and other relevant factors. (3) Outcome measures: Studies that assessed circulating serum or plasma uric acid levels using validated methods.

The exclusion criteria for this study were delineated as follows: (1) conference abstracts, conference reports, and other documents lacking full text and accessible data; (2) cases that remain undiagnosed or are not documented within hospital medical records; (3) studies involving animals and in vitro experiments; (4) review articles and meta-analyses; and (5) literature devoid of valid data results.

Full-text review was completed by two reviewers following screening. Discrepancies at each stage of screening between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted: authors’ names, year of publication, country of study, sample size, male-to-female ratio, clinical status of COPD (acute exacerbation or stable phase), number of COPD groups, number of control groups, mean age, uric acid levels in the COPD group and the control group, source of the samples, and GOLD stages. Uric acid levels were expressed in μmol/L (with the conversion factor of 1 mg/dL equating to 59.5 μmol/L).

Quality assessment of included studies

The quality of the studies was evaluated by two independent reviewers utilizing the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), as recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration. The NOS comprises eight items within the case-control section, categorized into three domains: selection, comparability, and exposure, which are allocated scores of 4, 2, and 3 points respectively. Studies achieving a total score of 6 or higher were deemed to have a low risk of bias. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data processing and analysis

The primary analysis focused on comparing uric acid levels between populations with COPD and those without. Raw data from the literature were utilized to compute the effect size and the 95% confidence interval for the association between COPD and uric acid levels. In instances where studies included multiple clinical statuses of COPD (e.g., acute exacerbation COPD, stable COPD, and healthy controls), data were extracted and analyzed separately. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was employed as the combined statistic. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I-squared index; a random-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was present (I-squared > 50%), whereas a fixed-effects model was used when I-squared ≤ 50%. Funnel plots were employed to identify publication bias.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to further investigate heterogeneity, categorizing studies by region (East Asia vs. non-East Asia), sample size (≤100, 101–499, and ≥500), and clinical status of COPD (stable vs. acute exacerbation). To evaluate the robustness of these findings, sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method. Secondary analyses involved comparing uric acid levels between patients with acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) and those with stable COPD, as well as among varying degrees of airflow limitation (GOLD stages 1 and 2 vs. GOLD stages 3 and 4). Meta-analysis was executed utilizing Review Manager 5.4 and STATA 17.0.

Mendelian randomization study

Study design

A two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed using summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to explore the potential causal relationship between genetically predicted uric acid levels and COPD. In a reverse MR analysis, we examined the potential causal impact of genetically predicted COPD on uric acid levels. Figure 1 illustrates the MR analysis framework. This study adheres to the STROBE-MR guidelines, as detailed in Supplementary Material 416. The design overview of MR analysis in this study.

Data sources

Summary-level GWAS data for uric acid (expressed as mg/dL) variants were obtained from the IEU Open GWAS Project database (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90018757). These data include 129,405 individuals from the Japan Biobank (BBJ), with an average uric acid level of 5.5 mg/dL17. GWAS data for COPD in East Asian populations (GWAS ID: bbj-a-103) were also obtained from the IEU GWAS database, including 3315 cases and 201,592 controls18.

Selection of instrumental variables

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) meeting genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs). To minimize linkage disequilibrium (LD) bias, an LD threshold of r² < 0.001 and a distance of 10,000 kb were applied. In addition, the total variance explained by the genetic instruments (R²) and the corresponding F-statistics were calculated to evaluate instrument strength and the likelihood of weak instrument bias. An F-statistic greater than 10 was considered indicative of sufficient instrument strength, suggesting that weak instrument bias was unlikely19.

In the forward MR analysis, 59 uric acid-associated SNPs were initially selected, of which 56 were available in the COPD GWAS dataset. Horizontal pleiotropy was further assessed using the MR-PRESSO framework. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied for the MR-PRESSO tests. Before outlier correction, the MR-PRESSO Global Test indicated evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (P < 0.001). Three outlier SNPs (rs11525583, rs2231142, and rs4752101) were subsequently removed. In addition, 6 palindromic SNPs (rs16856823, rs34315986, rs3775948, rs477138, rs6026578, and rs9468498) were excluded during harmonization, leaving 47 SNPs for the final forward MR analysis. For reverse MR, 7 COPD-associated SNPs were selected, all available in the uric acid GWAS data. After excluding 3 outliers (rs1468131, rs1585258, rs7299183) and 1 palindromic SNP (rs503464), 3 SNPs remained. All IVs are detailed in Supplementary Material 1 (Table S2).

Statistical methods

We utilized a range of genetic association analysis techniques, each predicated on varying assumptions of pleiotropy, to assess the genetic relationship between COPD and uric acid levels. These methods included the inverse variance weighted (IVW) approach, the weighted median (WM) method, and the MR-Egger method. The IVW method is widely recognized as the benchmark in Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, as it employs the Wald ratio technique to estimate the causal effect of each instrumental variable (IV) single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and subsequently conducts a weighted summary analysis18. In the absence of horizontal pleiotropy, the IVW approach yields an unbiased estimate of effect. The MR-Egger method evaluates the average pleiotropy of the IVs through its intercept, although its results are typically less precise20. The WM method remains robust, providing causal effect estimates even when up to 50% of the IVs are invalid21. To assess heterogeneity, Cochran’s Q statistic was computed using both the IVW and MR-Egger regressions, with a P-value greater than 0.05 indicating no significant heterogeneity22. In cases where heterogeneity among IVs was detected, a random-effects IVW model was employed; otherwise, a fixed-effects IVW model was utilized. In MR-Egger regression, the intercept term was used to test for horizontal pleiotropy bias in the included SNPs: an intercept close to zero indicates no horizontal pleiotropy among the IVs23. Leave-one-out analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of removing individual SNPs on the causal estimate24. MR analysis was implemented using the TwoSampleMR package in R (version 4.5.0). Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Literature search results

A comprehensive search yielded 6819 articles from the Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and CNKI databases. Initially, 1058 duplicate articles were removed. Subsequently, based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 5593 articles were excluded following a review of their titles and abstracts. A further 148 studies were excluded after a full-text review. Ultimately, 20 studies met the criteria for inclusion in the analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Mendelian randomization study results

Forward Mendelian randomization analysis

Ultimately, 47 SNPs associated with uric acid levels were included as instrumental variables. The total 47 instrumental SNPs explained 4.48% of the variance in serum uric acid, and all instruments had F-statistics > 10 (mean F = 153.74; minimum F = 30.00), indicating that weak instrument bias was unlikely. The MR results using different methods to assess the causal relationship between genetically predicted blood uric acid levels and COPD risk are shown in Fig. 5A. Cochran’s Q test revealed significant heterogeneity among the instrumental variables (Table S6), so a random-effects IVW analysis was performed. This analysis demonstrated that each standard deviation increase in genetically predicted uric acid levels was negatively associated with COPD risk (OR = 0.680, 95% CI: 0.543 - 0.852). The estimates of the effects of these SNPs on blood uric acid and COPD are presented in a scatter plot (Fig. 5B). The MR-PRESSO Global Test before outlier correction was significant (P < 0.001), indicating evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Accordingly, three outlier SNPs (rs11525583, rs2231142, and rs4752101) were removed before the final MR analysis. After outlier correction, the MR-Egger intercept (Intercept = −0.005, P = 0.633) did not suggest directional pleiotropy (Table S6). Reverse Mendelian randomization analysis

A reverse Mendelian randomization analysis was performed to investigate the potential causal effect of COPD on serum uric acid levels (Figure S8A). The MR-PRESSO Global Test before outlier correction was significant (P < 0.001), suggesting the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. Three outlier SNPs (rs1468131, rs1585258, and rs7299183) were therefore removed using the MR-PRESSO outlier test, and one palindromic SNP (rs503464) was excluded during the harmonization process. After these filtering steps, three SNPs remained as instrumental variables for the reverse MR analysis.

Because of the limited number of available instruments, MR-Egger regression was considered underpowered for reliably assessing directional pleiotropy and is therefore presented for completeness only. Cochran’s Q test indicated no significant heterogeneity among the remaining instrumental variables (Table S6). The reverse MR analysis suggested a significant causal effect of COPD liability on uric acid levels (β = −0.037, 95% CI: −0.041 to −0.033). The MR-Egger intercept did not indicate evidence of directional pleiotropy (Intercept = 0.002, P = 0.857) (Table S6). Scatter plots and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Figures S8B and S8C.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between uric acid levels and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) through integrated observational and genetic analyses. A meta-analysis of 20 studies showed that COPD patients had significantly higher uric acid levels, particularly during acute exacerbations or severe disease, supporting its role as a biomarker of severity. However, Mendelian randomization analysis revealed a negative causal effect of genetically predicted uric acid levels on COPD risk. This protective causal relationship thereby challenges the conventional linear view, indicating uric acid may serve not only as a biomarker but also as a protective factor in COPD development.

Our meta-analysis is consistent with previous reports. Two earlier meta-analyses comprising 7 and 8 studies, respectively, also reported elevated uric acid levels in COPD patients compared to non-COPD groups25,26. A Korean cross-sectional study found a higher prevalence of elevated uric acid in never-smokers with COPD than in those without27. Two Japanese studies demonstrated a positive correlation between uric acid levels and airflow limitation severity28,29. Furthermore, a Danish study involving 114,979 participants found that increased uric acid levels were associated with poorer lung function and a higher prevalence of COPD30. These consistent results support the utility of uric acid as a biomarker of disease severity in stable COPD. This association may be explained by COPD-related pathophysiological mechanisms. Chronic hypoxemia in COPD can induce tissue hypoxia, promoting ATP degradation into purine intermediates and increasing uric acid production31. Moreover, systemic inflammation during acute exacerbations may disrupt purine metabolism and impair renal uric acid excretion, further elevating uric acid levels2,32. Notably, significant uric acid elevation is primarily observed in COPD patients with hypoxemia but not in non-COPD individuals33. Consequently, uric acid levels in clinical settings are more likely a consequence and biomarker of COPD severity and systemic inflammation rather than a causative factor.

The pooled effect size for uric acid (SMD = 1.57) appears large for a single biomarker. Several factors may inflate standardized differences. First, studies enrolling acute exacerbations or hospitalized patients may capture pronounced systemic oxidative stress and hypoxemia-related metabolic shifts, leading to higher uric acid than stable outpatient COPD. Second, substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² consistently high) suggests differences in clinical status, case-mix, comorbidities, medication use (e.g., diuretics), renal function, and laboratory assays. Third, unit reporting and transformation (e.g., mg/dL vs μmol/L) and varying adjustment levels can contribute to dispersion, and SMD can be sensitive to within-study variance. Accordingly, we used random-effects models, conducted prespecified subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses, and interpret the meta-analytic findings as indicating directionally consistent associations but with limited precision in the magnitude due to heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses suggested that differences in study region and clinical status may partially contribute to the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies.

To address concerns of heterogeneity and potential confounding in the meta-analysis, we employed Mendelian randomization (MR). This method uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to strengthen causal inference, as they are fixed at conception and less susceptible to confounding or reverse causality. MR thus provides a more robust assessment of the relationship between exposure and outcome16.

The genetic evidence derived from MR analysis indicates a direction contrary to that of the observational findings. Specifically, MR analysis suggests that elevated uric acid levels may exert a potentially causal protective effect against the development of COPD in East Asian populations (Odds Ratio = 0.68). This result contrasts with previous research. For instance, a Chinese hospital-based cohort study reported that the risk of COPD increased significantly with higher serum urate levels34. Their MR analysis, which utilized genetic data from European populations, also supported this conclusion. Conversely, a Danish MR study found no causal link between uric acid and COPD or lung function30. But this study included only two uric acid-related SNPs (SLC2A9 rs7442295 and ABCG2 rs2231142), and rs2231142 (ABCG2) in our study did not show significance (P = 0.163), suggesting that the coverage of tool variables and genetic explanatory power might have been weak. A previous cohort study comprising 205,484 participants demonstrated that current smokers with lower blood uric acid levels exhibited an elevated risk of developing COPD35. These findings have led to a reevaluation of the antioxidant role of uric acid. Oxidative stress is posited to be a crucial factor in the pathogenesis of COPD and the deterioration of lung function10. As a major antioxidant in the airway epithelial lining fluid, uric acid provides first-line defense against oxidants from cigarette smoke and environmental pollutants7. Elevated uric acid levels have been shown to reduce oxidative stress, improve emphysema phenotypes, and attenuate lung function impairment36. These findings suggest that elevated uric acid enhances pulmonary antioxidant defense, thereby reducing oxidative tissue damage and potentially lowering the risk of COPD.

The discrepancy between our findings and those of Rao et al. may have several explanations. First, population-specific genetic architecture may play an important role. Serum urate regulation differs across ancestries, particularly for key transport-related loci such as ABCG2 and SLC2A9, whose allele frequencies and effect sizes vary substantially between East Asian and European populations. For instance, the association of ABCG2 rs2231142 with hyperuricemia (HUA) and gout risk is ethnicity‑dependent, with the frequency of the T risk allele being nearly threefold higher in East Asian than in European populations12,37,38. Second, the instrumental-variable composition differed across studies, which may influence instrument strength and may also result in partially different aspects of uric acid biology being captured by the MR analysis39,40. Third, environmental and epidemiological contexts may also differ between populations, including smoking patterns, biomass exposure, and other oxidative stress-related factors relevant to COPD pathogenesis13,14. Therefore, the conflicting MR findings do not necessarily indicate that one result is incorrect, but rather suggest that the relationship between uric acid and COPD may be context-dependent and potentially modified by ancestry-specific biology.

The reverse MR result should be interpreted with particular caution. After outlier removal and harmonization, only three instrumental SNPs remained for the reverse analysis, which substantially limited statistical power and reduced the robustness of the causal estimate. In particular, sensitivity analyses that rely on multiple instruments, especially MR-Egger regression, were underpowered and therefore of limited value for assessing directional pleiotropy. Accordingly, although the reverse analysis suggested that genetically predicted COPD was associated with lower uric acid levels, this finding should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive. The observed signal may reflect complex shared pathways between COPD susceptibility and uric acid metabolism, but this remains speculative. Larger East Asian datasets with stronger reverse-direction instruments are needed to validate this result.

Our findings reveal a complex and context-dependent relationship between uric acid and COPD. At first glance, the observational and MR findings appear paradoxical: observational data link higher uric acid levels to greater COPD severity, whereas Mendelian randomization suggests that genetically elevated uric acid may be protective. However, these findings may reflect different biological contexts rather than a true contradiction. Genetically elevated uric acid is more likely to represent a lifelong baseline metabolic background before disease onset, at which uric acid may help attenuate oxidative damage and reduce COPD risk, consistent with the MR results. In contrast, elevated uric acid measured after disease onset, particularly in the setting of hypoxia, systemic inflammation, acute exacerbation, and advancing disease severity, is more likely to reflect a secondary metabolic response and thus serve as a biomarker of disease progression. This interpretation is consistent with both our meta-analysis and previous observational studies. Thus, while genetically influenced elevation in uric acid may indicate a potentially protective baseline state, acquired elevation in clinical COPD appears more reflective of pathological burden. This distinction supports the proposed bidirectional model (Fig. 6) and suggests that a comprehensive interpretation of uric acid in COPD should take into account both disease stage and genetic background. Although this framework raises the possibility that uric acid may shift from a predominantly antioxidant factor to a more pathologically reflective marker beyond a context-dependent range, our data do not allow identification of a specific clinical threshold. The strength of this study lies in its innovative combination of meta-analysis and MR analysis. The integration of observational and genetic methods provides robust causal evidence, transcending traditional linear perspectives on the relationship between uric acid and COPD and advancing the understanding of the disease. However, this study has several limitations. First, the impact of hyperuricemia on respiratory function was not systematically evaluated. Uric acid demonstrates both antioxidant and pro-oxidant properties; hyperuricemia may promote oxidative stress and associated risks41. The dose-response relationship and threshold for this shift require further study. Second, the bidirectional model we proposed does not fully account for the results of the reverse MR analysis, which is further complicated by a lack of existing literature on this association. Notwithstanding, reverse causality generally has limited influence on MR results—a key strength of the method16. Third, the meta-analysis included only observational studies, which are susceptible to confounding and bias, motivating the supplementary MR analysis. In addition, survival bias may also be present, as patients with more severe COPD and markedly elevated uric acid levels may have a higher mortality risk and therefore be underrepresented in cross-sectional or case-control studies. Fourth, serum uric acid may not accurately reflect airway epithelial lining fluid levels, which could be more directly relevant to COPD pathogenesis. Finally, the MR analysis relied on genetic instruments from public databases. Although pleiotropy was addressed, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, as both meta-analysis and genetic data primarily involved East Asian populations, generalization to other ethnicities should be made with caution.

Conclusion

This study elucidates a complex and context-dependent bidirectional association between uric acid and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Genetically or basally elevated uric acid may confer a protective effect against the onset of COPD, whereas clinically observed increases in uric acid levels in patients with established COPD may serve as indicators of disease progression and severity. These findings caution against interpreting elevated serum uric acid in COPD solely as a harmful factor, as it may also reflect a compensatory antioxidant response and the underlying disease stage. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating uric acid should consider disease stage and clinical context.
