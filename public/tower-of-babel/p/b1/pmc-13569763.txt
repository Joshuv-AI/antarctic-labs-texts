Estimating the Underreporting of COVID-19 Burden in Selected Countries in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America in the Post-pandemic Era
Authors: Carlos Fernando Mendoza, Julia Spinardi, Iustina Chirila, Claire Spencer, Moe Hein Kyaw, Ben Yarnoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe true number of coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) cases may be highly underestimated because of reduced efforts to track cases through testing in the postpandemic era. Earlier estimates of this underreporting have varied across regions, and countries in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America have been notably impacted. This study investigates the true burden of COVID-19 cases in 2023 and 2024 across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of national public health surveillance data from 2022 to 2024 on COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America. Using a method developed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to estimate the burden of COVID-19 and influenza, we estimated the number of cases for 2023 and 2024 by calculating the ratio of cases to hospitalization and cases to deaths, using 2022 as the baseline. We compared the estimated and reported number of cases each year to assess the level of underreporting.ResultsThe mean ratio of estimated cases to reported cases showed a high albeit variable level of underreporting in 2023 and 2024 across regions. In 2023, the levels of underreported cases were 1295%, 319%, 560%, and 3703% in Asia, the Middle East, Latin America, and Africa, respectively. The number of estimated underreported cases generally increased in 2024 to 2326%, 1415% and 16,664% in Asia, Latin America, and Africa, respectively, but in the Middle East decreased to 87% based on hospitalization data. The trend for death data was similar.ConclusionsThe results highlight the high level of underreporting of COVID-19 cases, which has increased over time in many regions. Therefore, the true global burden of COVID-19 could be substantially higher than indicated by publicly available data. Continued COVID-19 surveillance is crucial for stabilizing public health measures such as prevention and target vaccination programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s41030-026-00371-0.Keywords: COVID-19, Disease burden, SARS-CoV-2, Underreported infections, Underreporting estimation

Introduction

As of January 2025, COVID-19, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has resulted in over 777 million cases and 7 million deaths globally (across 240 countries reporting data) [1]. However, evidence suggests that the true health burden of COVID-19 may be substantially underestimated by surveillance data: an estimated 2.2 billion additional cases may not have been reported between February 2020 and December 2022 in 27 countries [2]. This underestimation may have increased after 2022 as testing for COVID-19 became less frequent. Therefore, the reduction in burden, attributed to the number of cases observed in 2023 and 2024 may only represent an increase in underreporting. Understanding the extent to which the burden of COVID-19 is underestimated is critical to making timely and effective public health policy decisions.

The World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 a global emergency on 30 January 2020, prompting public health authorities worldwide to allocate substantial resources toward identification, control, and treatment of COVID-19 infections [3]. During this period, the reporting of SARS-CoV-2 infections was more robust, and there were efforts to track cases through testing at the community level, hospital admissions, and death reports. However, underreporting remained widespread, especially during later periods. With the end of the global emergency on 11 May 2023, surveillance activities were either terminated or narrowed in scope, further exacerbating challenges of underreporting [4].

Even before the end of the global emergency, in 2022, many jurisdictions scaled down public health measures, including testing and case reporting requirements [2]. Consequently, the absence of active testing and streamlined reporting led to increased underreporting, impacting the accuracy of public health surveillance data for assessing the true burden of COVID-19 [2].

There have been substantial differences in estimated underreporting across regions, particularly in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America. Underreporting was generally lowest in European and North American countries, but underreporting rates were much higher in countries in the Middle East, Asia, Africa, and South America. For example, in Colombia, the underreporting factor (underreported infections per reported infection) was 111.7, compared with 12.5 and 13.4 for the UK and USA, respectively, during the Omicron variant period from 4 February 2020, to 11 December 2022 [2]. The true current burden may be particularly underestimated in settings with limited testing availability or strained public health infrastructure [2, 5].

The underreporting of COVID-19 cases creates a significant gap in economic burden estimates because COVID-19 cases impose direct and indirect costs and may lead to long-term complications associated with long COVID. Estimating the true number of COVID-19 cases in the community can also support healthcare systems in preparing for potential burdens and strains on hospital settings. Therefore, it is necessary to develop methods for estimating the true burden of COVID-19 cases to support public health decision-making. With the expiration of the public health emergency and authorization to collect public health data, hospital admission and COVID-19 mortality data have become the primary indicators of COVID-19 burden [4]. Previous studies have estimated the underreporting of COVID-19 during the pandemic period; however, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is a notable evidence gap regarding the extent to which COVID-19 burden may have been underestimated in countries across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America in 2023 and 2024 [2].

In this study, we calculated the underreporting ratio of COVID-19 cases in select countries in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America in 2023 and 2024. This information can be used to inform future decision-making for resource planning and vaccination strategies as well as preparing for new surges in variants.

Methods

Approach Overview

This study used a retrospective analysis of public health surveillance reports in 2022, 2023, and 2024 of COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths across countries in Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, and Asia to estimate the number of cases in 2023 and 2024. The objective was to estimate the true number of COVID-19 cases by comparing reported cases to estimated cases in 2023 and 2024.

To estimate the number of COVID-19 cases in 2023 and 2024, we used an approach developed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) that has been used to estimate the burden of influenza in the USA for over a decade and has recently been used to estimate the burden of COVID-19 [6, 7]. The CDC method uses the ratio of cases to hospitalizations from a time period during which data were well collected and applies it to the number of hospitalizations from the current period to estimate the current number of cases [6].

We used this same approach for estimating COVID-19 cases by taking the number of COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths from 2022, when surveillance programs were still active during the pandemic period and Omicron sublineages were already predominant in circulation [4]. Although some jurisdictions had already begun scaling down testing and reporting in 2022, reporting was generally more complete and frequent in 2022 than in the postemergency period assessed in 2023 and 2024. Therefore, 2022 was used as a conservative baseline for estimating subsequent underreporting. We then calculated the ratios of cases to hospitalizations and cases to deaths for each country [2, 7].

The calculated ratios for each country were then applied to the reported hospitalizations and deaths in 2023 and 2024 to estimate the true number of COVID-19 cases in each year. The estimated COVID-19 cases were compared with the cases reported in surveillance data to assess how much underreporting has increased over time. Ethical approval was not applicable.

Data Extraction

Country-specific data for Latin America, Africa, Asia, and the Middle East regions were collected from country Ministry of Health (MoH) databases, the World Health Organization (WHO) [1], the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) [8], Our World in Data [9], and the John Hopkins University [10] for COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths for the years 2022, 2023, and 2024. A targeted literature search was conducted for data on countries that were not available in other sources (see Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). The targeted literature searches were conducted in OVID to identify peer-reviewed articles reporting data relevant to the research questions in the Embase, Medline, and Medline In-process databases. Search strategies included a combination of free-text search terms and controlled vocabulary terms, as recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration. Duplicates were removed from the results of the search before review. No conference abstracts were included as part of the search. Searches were restricted to studies conducted with human participants and published in English, without any geographic limitations. Studies were included for review if they included hospitalization- or mortality-specific terms. The included titles and abstracts were screened by a single researcher, and screened records were classified as either included or excluded. Despite the additional literature search, the regions explored showed that several countries reported only data from 2020 and 2021. Furthermore, the data were often collected from single-center hospitals, which limits the representativeness and may not accurately reflect the healthcare situation across the entire country.

COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths in Latin American countries were obtained from dashboards published by PAHO, which collates data reported by individual countries. However, data collection methods vary; some countries report data from universal COVID-19 case surveillance, whereas others report cases detected through integrated sentinel surveillance of respiratory viruses. In instances in which data were missing from PAHO, data were derived from John Hopkins University [10–15], and country-specific MoHs. This included data for Colombia, Brazil, Peru, Argentina, Guatemala, Puerto Rico, and Panama.

COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths for Asian, African, and Middle Eastern countries were derived from the WHO COVID-19 dashboard. The WHO stopped requiring daily basic reporting in August 2023; however, a few countries continued to report at a daily frequency. Data were missing for several countries in Asia, including Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Turkmenistan. To address these gaps, a targeted literature search was carried out on OVID to identify additional data (see Table 1). However, the search did not yield any articles that could fill the data gap given that reports from single-center hospitals on mortality and hospitalization rates were excluded from the analysis because of their lack of representativeness. For periods in which COVID-19 cases, hospitalizations, and deaths were incomplete (e.g., starting from April 2022), cases were estimated using linear projections to cover the full year.

Analysis

The base year used in the analysis to estimate ratios of cases to hospitalizations and deaths was 2022. This year was used because it was the first year in which Omicron was the predominant variant and because COVID-19 surveillance reporting was generally more frequent than in 2023 and 2024, before the formal end of the global health emergency. We do not assume that 2022 represented complete case ascertainment; rather, it was the most appropriate pragmatic reference year available for applying a consistent case-to-hospitalization and case-to-death ratio approach across regions. Discussion

Accurately estimating COVID-19 cases is crucial for understanding the true burden of COVID-19. Underreporting can lead to delays in getting vaccines to priority populations [19]. Underreporting can also lead to misleading trends, making it appear as though disease burden is decreasing when it is not. This can result in inadequate preparation for future waves of the virus and hold back vaccine distribution strategies. The results of this analysis demonstrate that COVID-19 cases are substantially underreported, and this trend has increased from 2022 to 2024 in most regions. This underreporting affects the accuracy of public health data and hampers effective response efforts. These disparities suggest potential variations in healthcare infrastructure, reporting accuracy, and access to medical care. These findings suggest that reductions in reported COVID-19 case counts may not fully reflect changes in community-level burden. Under ascertainment of cases can limit the usefulness of surveillance data for public health planning and may reduce the ability of decision-makers to interpret trends in disease activity. Although hospitalizations and deaths pose the highest burden among COVID-19 cases, nonhospitalized cases can still have a substantial direct and indirect impact on public health, including impacts on quality of life, productivity loss, and substantial societal burden. In addition, a clear understanding of community-level trends could inform and prepare healthcare systems in advance for new surges. Estimations of the true burden of COVID-19 are necessary to sustain public health measures and inform decisions around vaccination strategies and investment in healthcare systems in general.

Underreporting may be especially problematic in countries in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, where COVID-19 testing for hospitalizations and deaths is generally limited to high-risk or severe cases. It is no longer standard practice to test all viral infections associated with hospitalizations, especially in resource-constrained settings [20, 21]. Shortfalls exist in testing of COVID-19 and registrations and cause-of-death reporting, particularly in countries in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, where civil registration and statistics systems may be unable to provide accurate, complete, and timely data on births, deaths, and cause of death [22]. A study from 2021 showed that the registration of COVID-19 deaths was reported in only 10% of the African region, compared with 98% in European region [23]. This is unsurprising because of the lack of information, lack of tests, and testing inaccuracies during the early stages of the pandemic [18]. Furthermore, underreporting of deaths may occur when there are inconsistencies in what constitutes a COVID-19 death, when no test is done, or when testing results are false-negative [24]. Similarly, hospitalization is underreported because of asymptomatic infections [25] and limited testing capacity [26]. Hospitalization data are often underreported compared with death data because hospitals prioritize severe or critical cases for testing and reporting.

The high standard deviations observed within each region indicate substantial inconsistency in the reported data, which may be attributed to the differences in testing rates, healthcare capacity, and reporting among individual countries. For example, Egypt reported 27,334 hospitalizations out of 134,994 cases, suggesting better reporting or higher severity of cases. Conversely, Botswana reported only 14 hospitalizations out of 115,549 cases, indicating potential underreporting or differences in healthcare-seeking behavior. The discrepancies highlight that the true global burden of COVID-19 is likely to be substantially higher than indicated by available data. These findings have significant implications for public health policy, underscoring the need for improved data collection and reporting systems to ensure accurate health monitoring and response. International support and collaboration are essential to enhance the accuracy and completeness of health data in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America. Further research is recommended to understand the underlying causes of the disparities in case-to-hospitalization ratios, which will be crucial for developing effective strategies to address the underreporting of COVID-19.

This study has several limitations. First, to estimate the number of cases in 2023 and 2024, we assumed that the ratio of cases to hospitalizations and deaths was stable over time. However, if the ratio changes over time, estimates of underreporting will, in part, reflect these changes. This may happen because of changes in vaccination coverage or severity of the predominant variant or because of herd immunity due to vaccination or multiple infections. This approach aligns with methods used by the CDC [7], which rely on the same assumption. Second, the approach assumes that cases in 2022 were fully reported. If cases in 2022 were underreported, this underreporting will persist through to the estimates of cases in 2023 and 2024. Studies have shown that cases were underreported even in 2022 [2], so the estimates here are likely underestimates of the true burden in 2023 and 2024. Third, there is a lack of available evidence for several countries within each region. This lack of evidence is most apparent in the Middle East, and little data are available on hospitalizations. Finally, hospitalization data may also be underreported because hospitals may prioritize testing and reporting severe or critical cases, resulting in underreporting of less severe hospitalizations. The targeted literature searches were restricted to English-language publications. This may have excluded relevant local-language reports, surveillance summaries, or government documents from countries included in the analysis, which could contribute to selection bias and incomplete data capture. Despite these limitations, estimates from this study are comparable to other studies. For instance, Mustapha et al. [2] demonstrated that underreporting was highest during the predominance of Omicron; Africa and South American countries generally had the highest underreporting, and Europe had the lowest levels of underreporting. Similarly, the CDC has shown that the use of at-home COVID-19 tests and the prevalence of asymptomatic cases have contributed to the underreporting of COVID-19, leading to underestimation of the true number of COVID-19 cases [27].

Conclusions

This analysis offers valuable insights to assist decision-makers in critically assessing the burden of COVID-19. This information can help guide in decision-making and prevention policies. Adjusting burden estimates for underreporting is essential for effective public health surveillance because testing of COVID-19 cases is not routinely conducted. Despite testing improvements, there are discrepancies in the definitions of what constitutes a COVID-19 hospitalization or death, leading to inconsistencies in reported data. Underestimation of cases can lead to weaker responses and larger outbreaks. The US CDC has begun implementing this adjustment in influenza and COVID-19 [7], and the data suggest that countries in Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America should follow a similar approach.
