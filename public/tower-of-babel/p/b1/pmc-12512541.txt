A study of the impact of social presence on college students’ learning satisfaction in online learning contexts: a chain-mediated model
Authors: Yunzhen Liang, Liling Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnline learning is rapidly evolving and gaining popularity due to its flexibility in both time and space. College students’ learning satisfaction is regarded as a key criterion for assessing the learning effect of online courses, and it is closely related to the quality of online courses and learners’ behavior. This study recruited 206 college students with online course experience to examine the effect of social presence on learning satisfaction. Academic emotions and self-efficacy were incorporated into the research model to explore the underlying mechanisms linking social presence and learning satisfaction, leading to the following conclusions: social presence was significantly and positively associated with college student’ learning satisfaction; positive academic emotions and self-efficacy were significantly and positively associated with college students’ learning satisfaction; negative academic emotions was significantly and negatively associated with college students’ learning satisfaction; positive academic emotions and self-efficacy partially mediate the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction; academic emotions and self-efficacy chain mediate the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03386-5.Keywords: Online learning context, Social presence, Learning satisfaction, Academic emotions, Self-efficacy

Introduction

The adoption of online learning modes has grown substantially and has assumed an important role, particularly within higher education [1–3]. Previous research indicates that the actual effects of online learning have not yet met expectations, and that students are not sufficiently motivated to participate. They are also prone to problems such as anxiety, helplessness, and low learning efficiency [4]. Establishing and maintaining a learning community with social characteristics is considered valuable in improving learners’ learning effectiveness [5]. The Community of Inquiry (CoI) model is an important theoretical model in online learning [6]. As one of the three main elements of the model, social presence is an indicator for assessing the relationships and social atmosphere in the online learning community, which contributes to the creation, sharing, and dissemination of knowledge in the online learning community [7]. It is also effective in increasing participation in self-directed and collaborative learning processes [8]. As an important theoretical model in the context of online learning, the CoI model has been regarded by many scholars as needing to enhance its descriptive and explanatory power during its development. Some researchers have suggested that “learning presence” [9], which consists primarily of self-regulated learning and self-efficacy, should be included in this model. Meanwhile, some researchers proposed to include “emotional presence” in the model [10]. Furthermore, academic emotions and self-efficacy, as individual psychological factors of learning, both have a significant impact on learning effectiveness [11–14]. Online learning satisfaction is a visual representation of the effectiveness of online learning [15–17]. Therefore, exploring the influence mechanism of college students’ learning satisfaction in the online learning context is crucial. Several studies have explored social presence and learning satisfaction in online learning contexts [18–20]. However, there are few empirical studies from the perspective of integrating “learning presence” and “emotional presence” with the CoI model. Therefore, based on the complementary perspective on the elements of the CoI model, this study establishes a research model from the holistic view of the impact of social presence on college students’ learning satisfaction in the online learning context, incorporates academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence) and self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) as mediating variables to further explore the overall impact mechanism of the model. Aiming to provide theoretical references and empirical evidence for enhancing college students’ learning satisfaction in the online learning context and further improving the quality of college students’ online learning. The context of the present study is online learning, with the research questions presented as follows:

What is the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction?

What is the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction?

What is the mechanism of academic emotions and self-efficacy between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction?

What is the mechanism of academic emotions and self-efficacy between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction?

Theoretical framework

CoI theory

CoI theory, coauthored by Randy Garrison, Terry Anderson, and Walter Archer (1999) [6], plays a crucial role in promoting the development of learners’ critical thinking in online and blended learning environments. However, it has also been accompanied by skepticism during its development, with some arguing that it places too much emphasis on environmental factors in the learning process and ignores the intrinsic influence of the learners themselves, resulting in their over-reliance on standardized online learning communities in their practical application [21]. One of the initial criticisms of the CoI model was that it did not consider the learner’s confidence, goal orientation, beliefs, or the learner’s series of efforts such as planning, implementing, and monitoring learning [9]. They suggested the CoI model should add a new construct called “learning presence”. Its main components are self-efficacy and self-regulated learning [22], with self-efficacy playing a key role in learners’ initiative and knowledge construction [23]. Cleveland-Inne and Campbell [24] noted that the CoI model lacks an element of “emotional presence”, which is defined as the outward expression of emotions and feelings between and among individuals in a CoI environment. They argued that this emotion will emerge during the transition to online learning and throughout the learning experience itself. Similarly, Rienties and Rivers [10] added an “emotional presence” component to the original CoI model and designed a theoretical CoI model with four presence components. The emotions experienced by students in learning, classroom, and exam situations are defined as academic emotions [25]. These studies have separately underscored the importance of “emotional presence” and “learning presence” as components of the CoI model.

Social presence is the feeling, perception, and reaction that occurs when an individual connects with another subject in the mediated environment of text-based online discussions [26]. By promoting communication and social interaction among students, social presence plays an important role in the success of online learning. Relevant empirical research supports this view; in the study of Grieve et al. [27], they found that students with a higher sense of social presence in online environments experienced higher levels of learning satisfaction. Similarly, Lim and Richardson [28] noted that students’ perceptions and social presence were positively correlated with students’ satisfaction.

Therefore, considering the importance of students’ social presence on online learning satisfaction, as well as the suggestions to add new constructs to the CoI model. This study included academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence) and self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) in the research model for empirical research to reveal the relationship between social presence (a key element of the original CoI model) and learning satisfaction and its internal mechanism of action. Aiming to put forward suggestions for enhancing students’ learning satisfaction. In addition, we try, through the discussion of the internal mechanism of the relational model, to clarify the relationship among the relevant variables. In addition, provide insights to a certain extent for the inclusion of “learning presence” and “emotional presence” as independent factors in the CoI model.

Control-value theory

Pekrun(2006) [29] proposed the control-value theory, and its central assumption is the arousal of academic emotions. Control-value theory states that control of academic activities and outcomes and assessment of the value of the activities are the main sources of academic emotions. Control assessment refers to students’ assessment of their ability to complete the learning task and master the learning material, with the most significant factor being self-efficacy. In Pekrun’s control-value theory [29], there is a bi-directional relationship between emotions and their antecedents and their effects. Where control appraisal and value appraisal are considered antecedents of emotions, and emotions can in turn affect these assessments. The application of the control-value theory to academic contexts is that learners’ appraisal (including control appraisal of their own learning behaviors and value appraisal of their learning outcomes) and academic emotions interact with each other and are affected by external factors. And the learning effects produced by individuals can, in turn, affect students’ appraisal and academic emotions. Thus, it can be inferred that students’ academic emotions in an online learning context can also affect students’ self-efficacy in turn, and there are few studies in which self-efficacy is included in the perspective of academic emotions. Therefore, the present study starts from the route that academic emotions inversely affect self-efficacy and explores their mechanisms of action in the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction.

Social learning theory

First proposed by American psychologist Albert Bandura in the 1960s, social learning theory emphasizes the importance of cognitive functions, self-selection, and self-regulatory mechanisms. The main components of social learning theory include observational learning theory, self-regulation theory, self-efficacy theory, and ternary interaction theory. Among them, the ternary interaction theory is the cornerstone of the social learning theory [30]. It regards subject factors, environmental factors, and behavioral performance as theoretical entities that are independent of each other, interact with each other, and determine each other. According to Bandura’s social learning theory, in the process of online learning, students’ learning behaviors are not only influenced by internal factors, such as individual knowledge and skills, motivation, and cognitive beliefs, but also by external environmental factors, such as course content and the teacher’s teaching form, etc. Individual behaviors, internal factors, and external factors are constantly interacting with each other and influencing each other, and there are also different and complex interactions between the different influencing factors.

This study focuses on the relationship and mechanism of college students’ social presence, academic emotions, self-efficacy, and learning satisfaction in the online learning context. Learning satisfaction specifically means that students, as consumers of educational products, will be motivated to continue learning when their learning expectations are met; on the contrary, the motivation for continuous learning is low or even unwilling to learn. It partially reflects the students’ behavior of continuous learning. Furthermore, social presence reflects a supportive environment for emotional expression, open communication, and group cohesion that builds understanding [31]. College students’ academic emotions and self-efficacy are individual student factors. Therefore, according to social learning theory, it can be inferred that for the study of continuous learning behaviors (learning satisfaction) in online learning contexts, both internal factors (self-efficacy, academic emotions) and external influences (social presence) have an impact on it, and they are constantly interacting with each other and influencing each other.

Overall, this study combined three complementary theoretical perspectives to construct an integrated theoretical framework for exploring the relationships among social presence, academic emotions, self-efficacy, and learning satisfaction. Specifically, the suggestion to incorporate new constructs into the CoI theory provides a perspective for including academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence) and self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) in the research model. Control-value theory provides the theoretical foundation for the relationship between academic emotions and self-efficacy. Moreover, social learning theory offers an overarching theoretical perspective for examining the relationships among all variables in this study.

Literature review

Based on the analysis of the CoI model, control-value theory, and social learning theory, it can be found that academic emotions, self-efficacy, and the three elements of the CoI model are closely related; academic emotions may inversely affect self-efficacy; learning satisfaction to a certain extent reflects the motivation of students to carry out sustained learning behaviors, and social presence reflects the supportive learning environment. Therefore, it is deduced that both internal factors (self-efficacy, academic emotions) and external influences (social presence) have an impact on individual behavior (learning satisfaction), and they are constantly interacting with one another and impacting each other.

The relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction in the online learning context

For the definition of learning satisfaction, Tough (1968) [32] defined it as the feelings and attitudes of learners in the process of learning. This definition focused on learners’ emotional responses to their learning experience. While Martin [33] placed greater emphasis on the alignment between learning outcomes and expected goals and defined it from the perspective of expectation as an indicator to assess whether the actual effect of learners in learning has reached the expected learning result. With the deep integration of emerging technologies and education and the prosperous development of online learning, students’ online learning satisfaction has become an important indicator for evaluating the quality and effectiveness of online learning [34] and learning effectiveness. Drawing on previous definitions, the present study defines college students’ learning satisfaction in the context of online learning as the extent of learners’ engagement in the learning process and the degree to which their expectations are satisfied. This construct reflects learners’ subjective assessment and overall perception of their own learning experience.

In the later CoI model proposed by Garrison et al. [6], social presence is defined as the learner’s ability to project themselves socially and emotionally into the community, and it reflects a supportive environment for emotional expression, open communication, and group cohesion that builds understanding [31]. Social psychological effects are considered significantly correlated with satisfaction [35]. In the process of online learning, learners may be affected by the lack of timely communication and feedback. Social presence helps to eliminate the psychological distance between teachers and students [36] and enhances the sense of belonging of learners, which is an important factor to ensure the success of online learning [37] and a significant positive variable to predict the learning effect and course satisfaction [38].

Social presence is closely related to course satisfaction [39], and relevant empirical studies support this view. For example, Gunawardena et al. [8] utilized the text-interactive approach to investigate the link between learners’ social presence and their learning satisfaction and found that social presence is a key indicator for assessing learning satisfaction. Similar findings have also been observed in online learning contexts. For example, Richardson et al. [40] investigated learners’ use of the SUNY system for online course learning and found that learners’ social presence was positively correlated with satisfaction with their instructors. In addition, in a meta-analysis, social presence was found to be significantly and positively related to students’ satisfaction in the online environment [18]. These studies showed that social presence was significantly and positively related to learning satisfaction. The research context for this study is an online learning context. Based on the above discussion, we infer that the higher the level of social presence, the higher their learning satisfaction, and vice versa, and accordingly propose specific hypotheses.

The mediating role of college students’ academic emotions and self-efficacy in the online learning context

Emotions reflect people’s attitudes and reactions to external things, which is a higher function of the human brain and exists in a complex interaction with other mental activities. Emotions not only contribute to an individual’s life and growth but also have a profound impact on learning, memory, and decision-making. Although there were early theories about emotions, these theories were somewhat isolated and fragmented from each other, resulting in the “fragmentation” of academic emotional research [29]. In this context, Pekrun [29] proposed the control-value theory for the first time, which provided a complete framework for analyzing emotions experienced in learning situations. In control-value theory, academic emotions were defined as emotions that are directly related to the learning process or outcome, including pleasure, excitement, boredom, frustration, anxiety, etc. These emotions play an important role in learning activities. In the online collaborative learning environment, emotions are the individual’s response to internal and external events, which can be categorized into positive and negative emotions [31], and they are interconnected and constrained by each other. For example, positive emotions, such as hope, enjoyment, and pride [41], generated by learners in the learning process can control and regulate their negative emotions (e.g., anxiety, anger, frustration, etc.) [42]. Examining how positive and negative emotions coexist and interact is useful for better understanding the contribution of emotions to successful learning [43].

In the original definition of social presence, emotions were contained within the structure of social presence [44]. However, some studies have presented contradictory empirical evidence. Social interaction is significantly related to emotions [45–47] Molinillo et al.[48] found that emotions were involved in and mediated social presence; that is, emotions play a crucial role in social presence and learning outcomes. Kucuk and Richardson [49] found that teaching presence, cognitive presence, and cognitive engagement created an emotional presence. In addition, Stenbom et al. [21] found that emotional presence directly affected social presence. Furthermore, a study on the role of emotional correlates of online learning among nursing students on their satisfaction with online learning also found that social presence significantly and positively influenced positive emotions and further influenced overall online learning satisfaction [50]. All these studies indicated the rationality and necessity of the emotional factor independent of the social presence. However, research on the exploration of “emotional presence” and the original CoI model remains limited and requires further development. Therefore, this study includes academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence) to investigate the relationship between social presence (a key element of the original CoI model) and learning satisfaction.

Emotions are significantly related to satisfaction [51], and many studies have explored the relationship between academic emotions and learning satisfaction. For example, Luo et al. [52] found that students’ emotional engagement, such as enjoyment, strongly influenced their course satisfaction. Zhu et al. [53] surveyed 1088 college students participating in online and offline courses and found that positive academic emotions were positively correlated with subjective experiences such as satisfaction. In addition, the study by Lee et al .[54] examined the relationship between peer support, achievement emotions, and learning satisfaction among graduate nursing students and found that positive emotions were positively correlated with learning satisfaction, while negative emotions were negatively correlated with learning satisfaction. The above studies indicate that academic emotions, both positive and negative, are closely associated with students’ learning satisfaction. Based on the insights from social cognitive theory, for the study of sustained learning behavior (learning satisfaction) in online learning contexts, both internal factors (academic emotions) and external influences (social presence) have an impact on it, and they are constantly interacting with each other and influencing each other. Previous studies have shown that social presence affects academic emotions [55], which are closely related to learning satisfaction [52]. Building on the above discussion, we infer that academic emotions influence online learning satisfaction and that social presence may affect college students’ learning satisfaction through academic emotions, and we propose specific hypotheses accordingly.

Bandura first introduced the concept of self-efficacy and defined it as an individual’s beliefs in their ability to act as an agent or to make judgments [56]. Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy states that students’ expectations and judgments of their own abilities during learning activities affect their motivation, learning behavior, and academic performance [57]. The relevant empirical evidence supported this view. Artino [58] found that learners with higher self-efficacy were more likely to have a better learning satisfaction experience. Self-efficacy is closely related to the CoI model. Shea and Bidjerano [9] suggested the CoI model should add a new construct called “learning presence”, whose main components are self-efficacy and self-management. However, research on the exploration of “learning presence” and the original CoI model still needs to be further enriched. Therefore, this study includes self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) to investigate the relationship between social presence (a key element of the original CoI model) and learning satisfaction.

Social presence enhances social interaction between learning partners in asynchronous interactive learning environments, especially in online teaching practice. This interaction helps to establish learning identity among learners and subsequently facilitates learners’ self-efficacy. Shea and Bidjerano [9] found significant correlations between self-efficacy, teaching/social/cognitive presence and self-regulated learning effort. Particularly, the research findings indicated that self-efficacy and self-regulated learning effort were strongly associated with social presence and teaching presence, both of which play an important role in learning success.

Based on theoretical analysis and previous research, we infer that self-efficacy is also closely related to college students’ online learning satisfaction and that social presence may influence college students’ learning satisfaction through self-efficacy; accordingly, specific hypotheses are proposed.

Control-value theory suggests that the relationship between emotions and cognitive appraisal is bidirectional and that academic emotions can, in turn, act on a student’s self-efficacy. Positive emotions promote learners’ cognitive flexibility and improve their judgments about their learning behavior and ability to achieve academic success, while negative emotions reduce individuals’ assessments of their own learning ability. Studies have shown that positive academic emotions were significantly positively correlated with self-efficacy, while negative academic emotions were significantly negatively correlated with self-efficacy [14]. In the current study, we include academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence) and self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) in the research model for empirical research to reveal the relationship between social presence (a key element of the original CoI model) and learning satisfaction and its internal mechanism of action. Based on the control-value theory and appeal discussion, we infer that in the online learning context, academic emotions can also affect students’ self-efficacy and then affect college students’ learning satisfaction, and academic emotions and self-efficacy can work successively as the mediating variables of the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction, and the hypothesis is proposed accordingly.

Methods

Participants

This study adopted the questionnaire survey method and used the online questionnaire distribution platform to distribute questionnaires to college students. All participants have online course learning experience. A total of 240 questionnaires were distributed and recovered, and after deleting the invalid questionnaires with the same answer options and the answer events lower than the 60 s, a total of 206 valid questionnaires were obtained, with a validity rate of 85.83%. In addition, each scale in this study was analyzed using validated factor analysis, and the maximum number of items measured on a single scale was not more than 10. The sample size of this study was 206, which is larger than the suggested sample size of around 200 [59]. Among them, there are 133 female students, accounting for 64.6%, and 73 male students, accounting for 35.4%. In terms of grade, there are 7 freshmen students, accounting for 3.4%, 33 sophomores, accounting for 16.0%, 64 juniors, accounting for 31.1%, and 102 seniors, accounting for 49.5%; in terms of majors, there are 128 students from the sciences and engineering, accounting for 62.1%, and 78 from the humanities and social sciences, accounting for 37.9%.

Measures

Four questionnaires were used to investigate college students’ social presence, academic emotions, self-efficacy, and learning satisfaction in the online learning context. Items in all four questionnaires were administered on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1, “Completely Disagree”, to 7, “Completely Agree”). Specific scale items have been provided as supplementary materials.

The social presence scale for college students in the present study was adopted from the social presence subscale of Arbaugh et al. [60] and was revised to fit the online learning context, with revised items such as “In the online learning course, getting to know the other participants in the course gave me a sense of belonging in the course”. The reliability is good, including the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.914, and the results of the validation factors were: χ2/df = 1.670; IFI = 0.982; TLI = 0.976; CFI = 0.982; RMSEA = 0.057; SRMR = 0.0311.

The academic emotions scale for college students was based on the two-dimensional emotions scale of Abbott et al. [61] and revised to fit the online learning context with revised items such as “I found the online learning course interesting”. After deleting the items whose factor loadings did not meet the requirements through validated factor analysis, a total of 11 items were retained, including six items measuring positive academic emotions and five items measuring negative academic emotions. The reliability is good, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.906 and 0.828 for positive academic emotions and negative academic emotions, respectively. And the results of the validation factors were: χ2/df = 1.778; IFI = 0.974; TLI = 0.966; CFI = 0.973; RMSEA = 0.062; SRMR = 0.0377.

The self-efficacy scale for college students was based on the self-efficacy subscale of the MSLQ developed by Pintrich [62] and revised according to the online learning context. Revised items such as “In online learning course, I am confident that I will excel in this course”. Factor analysis retained a total of seven items after deleting the items whose factor loadings did not meet the requirements. The reliability is good, including the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.887, with the following results: χ2/df = 1.886; IFI = 0.982; TLI = 0.973; CFI = 0.982; RMSEA = 0.066; SRMR = 0.0302.

The online learning satisfaction scale for college students in this study was designed based on the online learning satisfaction scale of Kuo et al. [63] and revised according to the online learning context, with revised questions such as “In online learning course, I am generally satisfied with this course”. A total of four items were retained after deleting the items whose factor loadings did not satisfy the requirements through the validated factor analysis. The reliability is good, including the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which was 0.875, and the results were: χ2/df = 1.926; IFI = 0.996; TLI = 0.987; CFI = 0.996; RMSEA = 0.067; SRMR = 0.0162.

Analysis of the impact of social presence on college students’ learning satisfaction

Direct effects

Multiple hierarchical regression analysis is a statistical analysis method used to measure the effect of multiple variables on a particular variable. To measure direct effects between variables, this study constructed a model with online learning satisfaction as the dependent variable, social presence, positive academic emotions, negative academic emotions, and self-efficacy as independent variables, with gender, grade, and major as control variables. Model 1 in this study includes only control variables to account for confounding factors, whereas Model 2 builds upon this by incorporating independent variables. A significant increase in the explanatory power (R2) of Model 2 indicates that these independent variables make a meaningful contribution to the dependent variable, demonstrating significant direct effects. As shown in Table 4, Model 1 is the model whose dependent variable is college students’ satisfaction with online learning and contains only the control variables gender, grade, and major, and the model has an R2 of 0.286, an adjusted R2 of 0.082, and a change of R2 of 0.082, with an F value of 6.016, and is significant. Model 2 is a multivariate hierarchical regression model in which the dependent variable is college students’ learning satisfaction, and social presence, positive academic emotions, negative academic emotions, and self-efficacy are added on the basis of controlling variables of gender, grade, and major, and the R2 of the model is 0.902, the adjusted R2 is 0.813, the change of R2 is 0.731 compared with that of model 1, and the F value is 123.155 and significant, proving that model 2 has better explanatory power than model 1. The covariance test results of model 1 and model 2 show that the values of VIF range from 1.013 to 1.089 and 1.111 to 6.124, respectively, which are lower than the test standard of 10 [71]. The results of the covariance test of model 1 and model 2 show that there is no serious covariance problem between the variables of model 1 and model 2.

Discussion

In the online learning context, social presence significantly predicts college students’ learning satisfaction

The results of the direct effect analysis and the mediation effect analysis showed that social presence significantly and positively predicted college students’ learning satisfaction, which is consistent with previous research [72]. This suggests that the stronger the psychological perception of college students being together and interacting with others in an online learning situation, the higher the subjective evaluation and overall feelings about their learning process.

Although online learning environments provide abundant resources, it lacks some necessary social interactions and non-verbal cues compared with the traditional face-to-face lectures, which to a certain extent affects the interactions between students and teachers. Students often experience loneliness in online learning and fostering a sense of belonging and identity within the learning community is essential for effective engagement. Social presence emphasizes the ability to express one’s “true self” in social interactions, which affects the process and outcome of knowledge construction. It helps to improve the effectiveness of online learning, reduces the sense of isolation that may occur in the process, and encourages students to support each other [73]. Therefore, the greater the degree to which learners perceive the presence of others and interact with each other in the process of using the media, the lower the sense of loneliness in the learning process, which in turn positively affects the process of knowledge construction by the learners, leading to an increase in the effectiveness of learning, the formation of a positive learning experience, and a higher subjective evaluation of their own performance.

This finding suggests that schools should pay more attention to students’ feelings about learning, respect their personal and psychological needs, create a favorable atmosphere for interaction, and enhance their social presence through the introduction of interactive online platforms and technical support. Meanwhile, teachers could increase guidance strategies and conduct activities such as community discussions to promote interaction and cooperation among students.

In the online learning context, positive academic emotions and self-efficacy significantly predicted college students’ learning satisfaction

According to the results of the direct effect analysis and path analysis, positive academic emotions significantly positively predicted college students’ learning satisfaction, and negative academic emotions significantly negatively predicted college students’ learning satisfaction. This finding is consistent with previous research [74]. That is, the stronger the students’ positive academic emotions in the online learning context, the higher the level of learning satisfaction tends to be. Conversely, the stronger the students’ negative academic emotions, the lower the learning satisfaction tends to be. This is consistent with previous research that positive academic emotions positively predicted learning outcomes and negative academic emotions negatively predicted learning outcomes [75]. Positive academic emotions can expand the scope of students’ attention in the learning process, increase students’ learning motivation [76], and lead to self-management as well as controlled efforts to accomplish learning tasks. In contrast, negative academic emotions reduce students’ learning motivation and interest, which in turn affects their learning outcomes.

The results of the data analysis indicated that self-efficacy significantly positively predicted college students’ learning satisfaction. That is, the higher the level of students’ self-efficacy in the online learning situation, the higher their learning satisfaction. This is consistent with previous research that self-efficacy can positively predict learning outcomes, namely, the higher the students’ self-efficacy, the more engaged they will be in learning [77] and the higher their academic performance. Students’ self-efficacy refers to their confidence in their ability to effectively solve learning problems. Therefore, in the online learning context, the stronger the students’ confidence in completing the learning tasks and realizing the learning goals, the more they will tend to use deep learning methods and processing strategies to form a positive learning experience, and the higher the subjective evaluation of their own learning process and learning results.

Therefore, it is suggestes that schools could design strongly interactive online learning platforms (such as the incorporation of affective AI tools) that promote emotional interactions between students and students as well as teachers [78]. Based on these platforms, teachers could design online collaborative tasks with elements that stimulate positive emotions (e.g., color scheme and spatial layout) to enhance student interactions and stimulate positive academic emotions.

In the online learning context, positive academic emotions and self-efficacy partially mediate the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction

According to the analysis of mediating effects, positive academic emotions played a partial mediating role between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction in online learning contexts. That is, college students’ social presence significantly positively affects college students’ learning satisfaction by influencing college students’ positive academic emotions in online learning contexts, which is consistent with previous research [50] Social presence is a core element of a meaningful learning environment, which includes the online environment that may trigger emotions and affective processes [79]. It is through reducing students’ isolation in online learning environments and facilitating the formation of mutual support networks among students, making it easier for students to develop positive academic emotions in the learning process, which in turn further enhances motivation and facilitates cognitive processing, leading to a positive learning experience about the learning process and learning outcomes. However, the mediating effect of negative academic emotions between social presence and learning satisfaction is not significant. It may be because, although negative academic emotions significantly affect learning satisfaction, their effect size is quite small. Consequently, even if students experience a lower sense of loneliness and higher interactive support in the online learning context, the impact of their negative academic emotions remains limited, they do not significantly influence learning satisfaction.

The results of the mediation effect also indicated that self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction in online learning contexts. That is, college students’ social presence significantly positively affects college students’ learning satisfaction by influencing college students’ self-efficacy in online learning contexts, which is consistent with previous research [72]. Learning was recognized as a social process, and social presence encourages students to demonstrate their perspectives and skills and facilitates communication and socialization among students [80]. These interactions subsequently lead to students’ higher self-efficacy to achieve learning goals, more positive learning behaviors in learning, and higher satisfaction with the learning process and outcomes.

This finding recommends teachers should pay attention to students’ emotional experiences and design online learning activities that promote positive emotions’ development, such as designing collaborative group learning to promote students’ mutual support, reducing students’ sense of isolation, and helping students to reasonably make attributions [64]. In addition, teachers could also set up personalized online school activity contests to promote student interaction and give students the opportunity to perform successfully to increase their self-confidence to enhance their self-efficacy.

In the online learning context, academic emotions and self-efficacy play a chain mediating role between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction

According to the results of data analysis in this study, academic emotions and self-efficacy were confirmed to play a chain mediating role between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction. That is, social presence positively affects college students’ learning satisfaction by influencing positive academic emotions and further influencing self-efficacy in online learning contexts. Similarly, social presence in online learning contexts positively affects college students’ learning satisfaction by influencing negative academic emotions and further influencing self-efficacy. This is consistent with the control-value hypothesis that control assessments and value assessments, as antecedents of emotions, influence emotions while also being influenced by emotions, that is, they are reciprocal. And this finding aligns with prior research [14]. Although the mediating effect of negative academic emotions between social presence and learning satisfaction is not significant, the negative emotions and self-efficacy play a chain mediating role. This may be because despite the weak and non-significant mediating effect of negative academic emotions in the relationship between social presence and academic satisfaction, with the participation of self-efficacy, negative academic emotions, as part of the chain, still participated in the process of affecting learning satisfaction.

Social presence can reduce students’ loneliness in the online learning process, promote communication and socialization among students, and students will be more likely to have positive academic emotions. Consequently, it will affect students’ perception of learning activities, improve students’ judgment of their own learning behaviour and ability, and bring positive achievement expectations to students. Therefore, students’ self-confidence will be higher, which will lead to a high level of self-efficacy, the willingness to face challenges, and the willingness to devote time and energy to solving learning problems, thus generating a positive learning experience and a high level of satisfaction with the learning process and results. Negative academic emotions will make students doubt their own abilities and methods in the learning process, reduce their self-confidence, and thus lower their level of self-efficacy, which is not conducive to students’ learning.

Therefore, it suggests that teachers should pay attention to emotional interaction with students in the teaching process, provide timely help when students encounter difficulties, and awaken students’ positive academic emotions to enhance students’ self-efficacy and futher form a higher degree of learning satisfaction and academic achievement.

Implications

Emotional presence plays an independent and critical role in online collaborative learning environments [24]. In this study, academic emotion (a key element in emotional presence) was used as a mediating variable to explore the relationship between social presence and college students’ academic satisfaction in online learning contexts, and we found that social presence significantly affects academic emotion, in which positive academic emotion has a partially mediating effect between social presence and academic satisfaction. Therefore, the results of this study provided empirical evidence to elucidate whether and how social presence affects academic emotions, explained the relationship between social presence and “emotional presence” to a certain extent, and provided insights of the independent existence of “emotional presence” for exploring the CoI model.

Self-efficacy is critical for describing and explaining successful learning in inquiry communities [9]. In this study, self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) was used as a mediator variable to explore the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction in the online learning context. The results of the study found that social presence significantly affects self-efficacy, and self-efficacy plays a partial mediating effect between social presence and learning satisfaction. Therefore, this study explained the relationship between social presence and “learning presence” to some extent and provided insights for the existence of “learning presence” as an independent element in exploring the CoI model.

Building on the previous discussion on the development of the CoI model, “Emotional presence” is believed to play an independent and critical role in online collaborative learning environments, as well as being regarded as strongly linked to the original CoI elements [24, 81]. Meanwhile, “learning presence”, which includes two main subcategories of self-efficacy and self-regulated learning, is also considered to be closely related to the original CoI model [9, 82]. However, few studies have simultaneously explored the relationship between the elements of “learning presence”, “emotional presence”, and the CoI model. In the current study, we used social presence (a key element of the original CoI model) as the independent variable and simultaneously incorporated academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence) and self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence) as the mediating variables and conducted the empirical research to reveal their specific influence mechanisms. According to the path analysis, the positive academic emotions significantly positively affected self-efficacy, and the negative academic emotions significantly negatively affected self-efficacy. Positive academic emotions and self-efficacy respectively play a partial mediating role in the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction, and further play a chain mediating role. Therefore, the present study’s result explains the relationship among CoI model, “emotional presence”, and “learning presence” to a certain extent. It also provides insights indicating that “emotional presence” and “learning presence” exist as independent elements, thereby strengthening the CoI model.

Conclusions, innovations, and limitations

Conclusion

This study takes the online learning context as the research background, carries out empirical research under the theoretical support of the CoI theory, control-value theory and social learning theory, explores the influence of social presence on college students’ learning satisfaction. Meanwhile, based on the supplementation of the CoI model of the existing research, incorporates academic emotions and self-efficacy into the research model and reveals the relationship among social presence, academic emotions, self-efficacy and college students’ learning satisfaction. Finally, we found that: (1) social presence was significantly and positively associated with college students’ learning satisfaction in online learning contexts. (2) positive academic emotions and self-efficacy were significantly and positively associated with college students’ learning satisfaction, and negative academic emotions was significantly and negatively associated with college students’ learning satisfaction; (3) positive academic emotions and self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction; (4) academic emotions, self-efficacy acted as a chain mediator between social presence and college students’ academic satisfaction. Overall, this study enriches empirical research on the relationship between social presence and college students’ learning satisfaction in online learning contexts and provides reference for subsequent research on college students’ online learning satisfaction.

Innovations

There are several innovations in this study: Regarding the practical implications of this study, firstly, this study explored the relationship between social presence and learning satisfaction in the online learning context, validated the mediating role of academic emotions and self-efficacy, revealed the relationships among the variables. Meanwhile, it provides recommendations for enhancing college students’ online learning satisfaction. Additionally, it also provides insights for exploring the relationships among academic emotions (a key element in emotional presence), self-efficacy (a key element in learning presence), and social presence (a key element of original CoI model). Regarding the theoretical significance of this study, the findings of this study indicate that academic emotions work inversely on antecedents (self-efficacy), thereby validating the assumptions of control-value theory. Moreover, it also provides insights to some extent for expanding the CoI model (incorporating “emotional presence” and “learning presence”).

Limitations

However, this study still has some shortcomings. First, this study explored the relationship of academic emotions, self-efficacy and social presence. To some extent, it reflects the relationship among “emotional presence”, “learning presence” and “social presence”, but did not explore the relationship of “teaching presence” and “cognitive presence” in the CoI model. Therefore, it is suggested that subsequent research could consider exploring the relationship between “learning presence” and “emotional presence” and all the core elements of CoI from a holistic perspective to provide insights for the development of the CoI model. Second, the sample collection method used in this study is still the self-report scale. There is a certain degree of subjectivity in the traditional self-report scale, and it is recommended that more emerging technologies should be adopted for data collection in subsequent studies, such as multimodal data collection. In addition, the sample size selected for this study is relatively small and the study population is limited to college students. Future research can expand the sample size and sample background to improve the representativeness of the study. Furthermore, since this study utilized a cross-sectional design, future longitudinal studies are recommended to provide a deeper understanding of the relationships between these variables.
