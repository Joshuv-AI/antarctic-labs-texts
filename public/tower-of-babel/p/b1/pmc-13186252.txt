Shrew is an essential, recently evolved, Drosophila specific gene required for formation of the embryonic BMP activity gradient
Authors: MaryJane Shimell, Robert Connacher, Sangbin Park, Aidan J. Peterson, Kavita Arora, Michael B. O’Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patterning the dorsal surface of the Drosophila blastoderm embryo requires the rapid redistribution of a BMP heterodimer (Decapentaplegic/Screw) from lateral regions of the embryo to the dorsal midline as cellularization is completed. BMP redistribution creates a steeply graded signal with peak activity in the mid-dorsal region and requires four additional secreted gene products: Short gastrulation (Sog), Twisted gastrulation (Tsg), Tolloid (Tld), and Shrew (Srw). While the functions of Sog, Tsg, Tld, and their vertebrate homologs, in generating BMP activity gradients have been well-described, the role of Drosophila Srw remains an enigma. Here we show that Srw encodes a secreted, N-terminally truncated paralog of Tsg that, like Tsg, stimulates the cleavage of Sog by Tld. However, unlike Tsg, it does not form a stable interaction with any identified component of the patterning machinery. Structural modeling and mutant analysis suggest Srw transiently binds to the Sog/Tsg/BMP complex to facilitate proteolysis of Sog. We further demonstrate that the requirement for Srw can be bypassed by providing additional Tsg in srw mutant embryos prior to cellularization. Phylogenetic analysis suggests that Tsg and Srw emerged from separate duplications of the Crossveinless (Cv) paralog in higher Diptera, and then Drosophilid lineages respectively, thus providing components for progressive specialization of insect embryo patterning. Taking into consideration our genetic, biochemical and phylogenetic data, we present three possible mechanistic models for how Srw might accelerate Sog cleavage by Tld, to hasten BMP gradient formation in rapidly developing Drosophila embryos.

Introduction

Members of the Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP) family of secreted growth and differentiation factors modulate a wide range of developmental processes including stem cell self-renewal, organ growth and differentiation, apoptosis, and early axial specification (reviewed in (Katagiri and Watabe, 2016). In Drosophila, assignment of dorsal cell fate during early embryogenesis requires the activity of two BMP ligands, Screw (Scw) and Decapentaplegic (Dpp) (reviewed in (O’Connor et al., 2006; Upadhyay et al., 2017). These factors form both homo and heterodimers that signal through the type II receptor Punt and different complexes of the Type I receptors Thickveins (Tkv) and Saxophone (Sax) to specify early cell fate. Activation of the type I receptors by the type II receptor Punt in response to ligand binding, stimulates phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation of phosphorylated Mothers against Dpp (pMad), the only Drosophila BMP specific R-Smad, in a complex with the co-Smad Medea. The pMad/Medea complex regulates transcription of specific target genes in the dorsal half of the embryo to assign two primary cell fates within this region (Ashe et al., 2000; Raftery and Sutherland, 2003; Sutherland et al., 2003). High levels of BMP signaling specify the amnioserosa which is derived from approximately 200 cells that straddle the dorsal midline. In contrast, lower levels of BMP signaling in flanking dorsal-lateral cells are sufficient to specify the dorsal ectoderm. Overall, dorsal cell fate in Drosophila is governed by BMP ligands acting as classical morphogens to specify these unique fates in a concentration dependent manner (Ferguson and Anderson, 1992a; Wharton et al., 1993).

An unusual feature of this embryonic BMP patterning mechanism is that its output exhibits spatial bi-stability (Wang and Ferguson, 2005) where there is a sharp step-like transition zone in signal reception between the future amnioserosa and dorsal ectoderm cells. This step-like output results from a complex series of interactions between the two BMP ligands and several additional extracellular components (reviewed in (Umulis et al., 2009)). These components included a zinc metalloprotease Tolloid (Tld) and two BMP binding proteins: Short gastrulation (Sog) and Twisted gastrulation (Tsg). Sog and Tsg preferentially form complexes with Dpp/Scw heterodimers preventing them from binding to their receptors in dorsal lateral regions (Shimmi et al., 2005b). Tld cleaves Sog (Marques et al., 1997), releasing the heterodimer from the complex allowing it to either rebind another Sog/Tsg complex or their receptors. This reiterative cycle of binding and cleavage leads to a net flux of heterodimers to the dorsal midline, where the cleavage reaction dominates, and net ligand release occurs (Marques et al., 1997; Mizutani et al., 2005; Shimmi et al., 2005b). Ligand heterodimers then bind to a receptor complex containing Punt and a heterodimer of Tkv Sax, that in turn stimulates a high level of Mad phosphorylation and specification of amnioserosa. Dpp homodimers and Scw homodimers have a lower affinity for Sog/Tsg (Shimmi et al., 2005b). Consequently, they are not transported effectively to the midline and remain broadly distributed throughout the dorsal domain where they likely bind to Tkv and/or Sax homomeric complexes that are less effective in signaling.

In addition to the ligand transport process mediated by Sog, Tsg and Tld, the step-like boundary between regions of high and low pMad signaling is further enhanced by a positive feedback mechanism that involves BMP activation of the TNF ligand Eiger. Eiger signals via an incompletely characterized pathway to augment heterodimer binding to receptors in the dorsal-most cells (Gavin-Smyth et al., 2013). In addition, extracellular matrix components such as type IV collagen and integrin also play a role in the shuttling mechanism. These proteins provide an assembly and release platform for incorporating Sog, Tsg, Tld and the BMP heterodimer into various complexes through a series of distinct interactive steps (Sawala et al., 2012, 2015; Umulis et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2008; Winstanley et al., 2015).

A similar mechanism employing BMP heterodimer transport, complex cleavage and positive feedback has been shown to pattern a subset of Drosophila wing veins during pupal development. Specification of the posterior cross vein requires the BMP ligands Dpp and Glass bottom boat (Gbb) (reviewed in (O’Connor et al., 2006)). Dpp/Gbb heterodimers appear to be preferentially bound by Sog and the Tsg parlog, Cv whereupon Sog is targeted for cleavage by the Tld paralog Tolloid-related (Tlr or Tolkin) (Serpe et al., 2005; Shimmi et al., 2005a). These interactions produce a net flux of heterodimers away from the longitudinal veins into the presumptive posterior cross vein region and subsequent refinement of the BMP spatial signal through positive feedback. Low level BMP signals induce Crossveinless 2 (Cv-2), which acts as a cell surface localized BMP binding protein augmenting signaling, likely by facilitating transfer of BMPs to the signaling receptors (Serpe et al., 2008). An additional secreted factor, Crossveinless-d, further specifies wing patterning, although its mechanism of action has not been determined (Chen et al., 2012).

Homologs of these Drosophila patterning proteins are found in numerous other invertebrates and vertebrates where they are also involved in generating BMP activity gradients. However, in vertebrates the mechanistic roles played by the various BMP interacting proteins can be quite different (reviewed in (De Robertis and Tejeda-Muñoz, 2022)). In the “shuttling” model, exemplified by Drosophila, interactions of the BMP heterodimer with Sog, Tsg and Tld leads to a redistribution of the heterodimer over time. In contrast, in zebrafish a “source-sink” model best fits the available data, while in Xenopus a “counter gradient” model is proposed (reviewed in (Madamanchi et al., 2021)). These mechanisms arise from different spatial expression patterns of the various interacting components and their individual biochemical reaction parameters (binding affinities, cleavage rates, diffusion characteristics).

Although a great deal is known about the molecular mechanisms contributing to formation of a BMP gradient in the early Drosophila embryo, the role of the shrew (srw) gene product continues to be ill-defined. Mutations in srw were originally identified in loss-of-function screens for genes that affect embryonic patterning (Jürgens et al., 1984). Subsequently, srw was shown to act in the dorsal patterning process and presumed to augment Dpp signaling since its phenotype could be suppressed by additional copies of Dpp (Ferguson and Anderson, 1992b). Previous studies showed that srw encodes a truncated Tsg-like protein (Bonds et al., 2007). Tsg-like proteins normally contain a N-terminal BMP binding domain (Oelgeschläger et al., 2000) and a C-terminal domain that is capable of binding to one or more of the four von Willebrand factor (vWF) domains of Sog and its vertebrate homolog Chordin (Malinauskas et al., 2024; Moore et al., 2025). By contrast, Srw lacks the N-terminal domain, suggesting that it might bind Sog but not BMPs.

In this study, we show that srw is ubiquitously expressed in the early embryo and that loss of srw leads to very low uniform levels of BMP signaling throughout the dorsal domain, like sog and tsg mutants. Epistasis experiments in the embryo using double mutant analysis demonstrates that srw functions upstream of tld. Biochemical experiments suggest that although Srw does not appear to be a stable component of the Sog/Tsg/BMP complex, it nevertheless facilitates the cleavage of this complex under conditions of low Tsg. Consistent with this view, we find that injection of additional tsg mRNA or maternal deposition of tsg can bypass the requirement for srw in embryonic patterning. Previous phylogenetic data suggests that Srw arose from a Cv-like gene in Drosophilids (Nunes da Fonseca et al., 2010). We conducted a phylogenetic analysis of selected insects and found several species that contain three full length Cv-type molecules indicating two duplication events, while more basal phyla contained only one or two. Drosophilids have three Cv derived genes (Cv, Tsg and Srw) where Tsg and Srw both appear to have arisen as separate duplication events. Either in conjunction with a duplication or as an additional event, N-terminal truncation of one Cv-like copy yielded Srw. We discuss the implications of our biochemical observations and the phylogenetics and potential molecular mechanisms of Srw function.

Results

The srw gene was identified in embryonic lethal screens by Nüsslein-Volhard, Weischaus and Jürgens (Jürgens et al., 1984). Based on the loss of specific cuticular structures, and alterations in embryonic mitotic domains, srw was inferred to have a role in patterning the dorsal region of the early embryo (Arora and Nüsslein-Volhard, 1992; Ferguson and Anderson, 1992b). Cloning and molecular characterization of the srw gene revealed that it encodes an N-terminally truncated relative of Tsg/Cv. To further understand how Srw functions in early embryonic patterning, we isolated a genomic fragment containing the entire srw coding region and approximately 3 kb of upstream sequences. This fragment, and a modified construct containing a C-terminal His-tag, were used to generate transgenic lines for additional studies. Transgenic lines on the 2nd chromosome were paired with two different homozygous srw lethal mutants (srwB4, srwB18). The paired lines were then crossed to additional srw alleles to test if the genomic transgene could rescue transheterozygous mutant animals. We recovered Mendelian ratios of non-balanced adults using both the tagged and untagged versions of Srw. This result indicates that the genomic fragment contains all essential regulatory elements, and that the C-terminal tag does not interfere with Srw function, a critical consideration for subsequent tests of biochemical activity.

In the initial cloning study, srw expression could not be detected by in situ hybridization but was inferred to be expressed at low levels during early development since it could be PCR amplified from RNA prepared from 0 to 6 h embryos (Bonds et al., 2007). To determine if srw expression was spatially restricted in the early embryo, we carried out additional in situ hybridization experiments. We detected specific expression of srw in a uniform pattern in the cellular blastoderm and early gastrulation stage embryos (Fig. 1A and B), which fades during gastrulation (Fig. 1 C). We could not detect srw expression above background in any other embryonic tissue after germband extension or in third instar larval imaginal discs and the central nervous system. This is consistent with RNA-seq data across developmental timepoints (Brown et al., 2014; Pérez-Mojica et al., 2023).

The early uniform expression of srw is unusual since all other genes involved in specification of embryonic dorsal tissues, apart from scw (Arora et al., 1994), show localized expression along the dorsal-ventral (D/V) axis. Both dpp and tld are transcribed in the dorsal half of the embryo (Fig. 1D and E) – consistent with results published by (Shimell et al., 1991; St Johnston and Gelbart, 1987) - while tsg expression is restricted to a central dorsal domain (Mason et al., 1994). In contrast, sog is expressed in two ventral-lateral domains in the blastoderm embryo (Fig. 3F) (Francois et al., 1994). Although scw is ubiquitously expressed at the blastoderm stage like srw, it is presumed that the active Dpp-Scw heterodimer, can only form in the Dpp expressing cells of the dorsal domain (Arora et al., 1994; Shimmi et al., 2005b). The expression of srw is also unusual in that it is not observed in imaginal discs. This observation suggests that Srw function is likely only required at the cellular blastoderm stage to facilitate shuttling of the Dpp/Scw heterodimer during embryonic development.

The presence of a predicted signal peptide at the Srw amino terminus implies that it is a secreted protein. To test this, we generated several constructs for expression of C-terminally tagged Srw in S2 cells and found that Srw was secreted into the supernatant (Fig. S1 A), consistent with our expectation. To further aid in structural studies, we sequenced 12 available mutant alleles (Table 1). Note that several mutations resulted in identical substitutions and may represent either mutational hotspots or erroneous isolation of sibling flies derived from the same original mutational event. Next, we used AlphaFold to model the consequences of these mutations on the predicted three-dimensional structure (Fig. 2). A comparison of the predicted structures of Tsg, Cv, and Srw (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1), shows that putative Sog and BMP-binding domains form distinct globular structures separated by short unstructured regions. While, Srw lacks the N-terminal BMP-binding domain, the putative Sog binding domain folds similarly to Tsg and Cv (Fig. 2B). The srw missense mutants characterized in this study are found throughout the protein (Fig. 2C). Residues mutated in srwB3 (G94R) and srw110.18 (G110R) are buried within the presumed Sog binding domain. However, srw35.67 (E31K) is predicted to be solvent exposed. The cysteines of srwh2.19 (C123Y) and srw1 (C123S) are predicted to form one of two disulfide bonds that hold the C-terminal tail close to the Sog binding domain (Fig. 2D). An equivalent disulfide bond is predicted in Cv and Tsg and verified in the human Twisted gastrulation protein homolog 1 (TWSG1) crystal structure (Malinauskas et al., 2024). The C123S mutation at this location in srw1 strongly implies that this disulfide bond is critical for Srw activity.

Recently, it has been suggested that TWSG1 interacts with the vWF-4 domain of Chordin and mutations in Drosophila Tsg that disrupt the interaction interface showed altered dorsal embryonic patterning (Moore et al., 2025). Both Tsg and Srw are predicted to interact similarly with the equivalent region of Sog (Fig. 2E). Specifically, the Srw α2 helix (and Tsg α4) are directly apposed to a bundle of vWF4 β-sheets (Fig. 2F). As G94 and G110 are packed tightly behind this helix, their mutation to a bulky, hydrophilic residue likely disrupts the vWF4 binding.

Srw is required for Dpp shuttling and amnioserosa cell fate determination

To assess the role of Srw in the shuttling process more directly, we examined the expression of several embryonic dorsal tissue markers including rhomboid (rho), phosphorylated Mad, and Kruppel (Kr) that marks the amnioserosa (Gavin-Smyth et al., 2013). Loss of srw resulted in a failure to refine the dorsal pattern of both rho and pMad, like that seen in tsg and sog mutant embryos, but less severe when compared to tld (Fig. 3), scw and dpp mutants. Since refinement of the rho and pMad expression patterns require the proper shuttling of the Dpp/Scw heterodimer to the dorsal midline (Marques et al., 1997; Shimmi et al., 2005b), we infer that Srw also plays a role in this process. Failure of morphogen shuttling ultimately leads to the loss of amnioserosa fate and improper gastrulation in srw mutants (Fig. 3G), much like the phenotypes of tsg and sog mutants (Ferguson and Anderson, 1992a; Mason et al., 1994). Additionally, srw, tld double mutants lack dorsal expression of rho (Fig. 3F), just like tld mutants. Therefore, we infer that Tld function is epistatic to Srw. In contrast, the tsg, tld double mutant has the opposite epistatic relationship, where the pattern of the double mutant is like tsg mutants (i.e. retains broad expression of rho) (Shimmi and O’Connor, 2003).

Srw enhances Tld-mediated cleavage of Sog-Dpp or Sog-Tsg-dpp complexes

We have previously demonstrated that Tsg forms a tripartite complex with Sog and either Dpp homodimers or Dpp/Scw heterodimers (Shimmi et al., 2005b). A similar complex is formed by the vertebrate counterparts, although the exact stoichiometry of Tsg (1 or 2 per complex) in the Drosophila and vertebrate complexes is unclear (Oelgeschläger et al., 2000). Since the phenotype of srw loss is like that of sog and tsg loss (Fig. 3), we reasoned that Srw might also form a stable complex with one or more of the D/V patterning components. Using an S2 cell supernatant expressing a tagged form of Srw, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments using Dpp, Dpp/Scw, Sog, Tsg and Tld with unique combinations of tags on each protein. Surprisingly, in no case did we observe Co-IP of Srw with any of these proteins individually or in different combinations. Thus, we conclude that Srw function utilizes either a transient low-affinity interaction with one or more of these molecules or requires an unidentified intermediary component.

Despite lacking a stable interaction, we considered whether Srw could enhance the cleavage of Sog in various combinations with other D/V patterning components. Previous studies showed that the speed of Sog cleavage by Tld in the presence of Dpp or a Dpp/Scw heterodimer is augmented by increasing the Tsg concentration (Shimmi and O’Connor, 2003; Shimmi et al., 2005b). The similarity of Srw to the Tsg C-terminal domain suggested that it may also enhance Tld mediated Sog cleavage. We found that in the absence of Tld, there is no processing of Sog at any of the three known cleavage sites (Serpe et al., 2005), that generate fragments of 100, 50 and 25 kDa (Fig. 4A). Next, we identified relative concentrations of Dpp, Sog and Tld that led to slow cleavage of Sog at sites 1 and 2, and incomplete cleavage at site 3 (Fig. 4B). Addition of Tsg to the reaction causes somewhat faster (compare lanes 1, 2 in panels B and C), but no cleavage at site 3 given the absence of the 25 kDa product expected (Fig. 4C). Substitution of Srw for Tsg produced sustained cleavage of Sog at all three sites (Fig. 4D). Addition of Srw and Tsg together, resulted in even faster processing as evidenced by the presence of the 25 kDa fragment after only 2 h incubation in contrast to the 24 h required for cleavage with Srw alone (Fig. 4E compared with 4D). Finally, when Tld concentration was reduced by half, and equivalent amounts of Srw, Tsg and Sog are maintained (Fig. 4F compare with 4C, E), cleavage remained equally robust and complete. These results indicate that Srw enhances both the speed and effectiveness of Tld proteolytic activity, perhaps by inducing a transient conformation of the (Tsg)-Sog-Dpp complex that renders Sog cleavage sites more accessible to Tld.

Srw function is dispensable for viability in the presence of excess Tsg

Since the primary function of Srw appears to be enhancement of Sog cleavage, like that seen with excess levels of Tsg (Larraín et al., 2001; Shimmi and O’Connor, 2003), we hypothesized that the requirement for Srw might be bypassed by additional Tsg. We used two different approaches to address this possibility.

First, we injected mRNA corresponding to full length tsg or srw mRNA into srw or tsg mutant embryos and scored for rescue of dorsal structure by quantifying the number of Kr-positive amnioserosa cells formed. Injection of srw mRNA into srw mutant embryos rescued amnioserosa cell fate in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5C–G). Intriguingly, tsg mRNA injections could also rescue srw mutants (Fig. 5D–G). The few adults that eclosed showed no visible morphological defects, consistent with the absence of srw expression after early embryogenesis (Fig. 1). Crucially, injection of srw mRNA into tsg mutant embryos (even at a high concentration) did not rescue tsg mutants as may be expected given that Srw does not possess a BMP binding domain (Fig. 5E–H).

We also tested two chimeric Tsg-Srw mRNAs. TsgB-Srw had the N-terminal Dpp-binding domain of Tsg (TsgB) attached to the entire Srw sequence (minus the signal peptide). A second chimera had the same TsgB sequences attached to an N-terminally truncated version of Srw that does not contain the putative Sog binding domain (TsgB-ΔSrw). Interestingly, injected TsgB-Srw mRNA provided modest rescue of srw mutants at low but not high dose, and it did not rescue tsg mutants at either dose (Fig. 5G and H). Injected TsgB-ΔSrw mRNA did not rescue srw or tsg mutants at either dose (Fig. 5F, G, H). While tsg mRNA could rescue of srw mutants at high dose, TsgB-Srw mRNA rescue of srw mutants was better at low dose (Fig. 5G). One interpretation of this result is that while the presence of Srw reduces the need for a high dose of Tsg, the stoichiometry of the interacting proteins (and effective proteolytic processing) likely plays an important role as well.

In a second test, we examined whether supplying srw or tsg maternally would be sufficient not only for embryogenesis but also to produce fully developed fertile adults. We placed srw and tsg coding sequences in a UASp vector enabling high level maternal expression using suitable maternal promoters driving Gal4 or derivatives (Rørth, 1998). We found that loading srw mutant eggs with either tsg or srw, using mat tub Gal4 VP16 as a driver resulted in complete rescue of srw mutants. These crosses yielded Mendelian ratios of adult flies that were viable and fertile with no obvious morphological defects including normal crossveins (Fig. S2). We conclude that Srw is only required during early embryonic development and that this requirement can be bypassed if sufficient Tsg is present.

Srw is a recent Drosophila innovation

Beyond the clear homology between Srw and Tsg, the evolutionary relationship between Srw and its relatives has been previously analyzed. Both Srw and Tsg were suggested to be evolutionary novelties, found only in higher Dipterans, that arose by duplication of the more ancient Cv (Nunes da Fonseca et al., 2010). The idea that Tsg and Srw are likely derived from Cv, is supported by the observation that both have intron-less genes while Cv contains introns. This has been argued to have occurred because of retrotransposition (Nunes da Fonseca et al., 2010). It has been further noted that multiple members of the Screw-associated embryonic patterning system, including Tsg and Srw, are duplications of evolutionarily widespread genes, and that the co-opted products have evolved faster than the parental gene (Fritsch et al., 2010).

Taking advantage of additionally available insect sequences, we re-evaluated the expansion of Cv-related genes in Dipterans to gain further insight into the relationship between Cv, Tsg, and Srw. The phylogenetic tree shown in Fig. 6 is composed of Cv-related protein sequences from selected representative species. To match the inferred evolutionary history of the proteins, we assigned the term Cv to any of the proteins that is neither Tsg nor Srw. Outside of higher Dipterans, insects generally have a single gene with introns, that encode a Cv-like protein. Several isolated lineage specific duplications have been noted, exemplified here by Nvit_Cv and Nvit_Cvx. Widespread expansion of the protein family is evident in several higher Dipteran lineages (including Drosophilids and Calyptratids such as M. domestica and L. sericata), where each genome has three distinct Cv-like genes. All higher Dipterans have a Tsg homolog (deriving from genes with no introns). Drosophilids are unique in producing the truncated Srw protein, whereas the other flies have a duplicated full-length Cv product (Mdom_Cvx and Lser_Cvx).

Our results are consistent with earlier sequence analyses that Tsg and Srw are variant copies of Cv, but the inclusion of additional Dipteran proteins allows further resolution in the phylogenetic tree. The clustering of Tsg in a distinct branch from Cv and Srw is most consistent with Tsg arising from a duplication of Cv in the ancestor of all higher Dipterans. The Drosopholid-specific Srw likely emerged from Cv in a separate lineage-restricted event. Similarly, the branching pattern suggests that the Cvx proteins of the Calyptratae species (house fly and bottle fly) arose from yet another duplication event.

Discussion

Patterning the dorsal tissues of the Drosophila embryo requires core BMP signaling pathway components such as ligands, receptors and downstream signal transducers as well as an additional set of extracellular interacting components that modify the distribution and activity of the BMP ligand in time and space. These factors include the BMP binding proteins Sog and Tsg, the metalloprotease Tld, and components of the extracellular matrix (Matsuda et al., 2016; Sawala et al., 2015; Upadhyay et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2008). Together, these players enable net shuttling of the BMP ligand to the dorsal-most region generating a highpoint of signaling activity. Computational models have provided insights into the biophysical properties necessary to produce the experimentally observed patterns within the short 3-h time frame before early dorsal cell fates are established and gastrulation commences (Eldar et al., 2002; Shimmi et al., 2005b; Umulis et al., 2006, 2010). Homologous proteins perform similar functions to establish BMP activity gradients in several types of vertebrate embryos, but the expression and interaction details vary among the different components and result in unique patterning solutions that are adapted to the geometry and developmental tempo in different organisms (Madamanchi et al., 2021; Mörsdorf et al., 2024).

While BMP associated patterning molecules show remarkable sequence conservation across evolutionarily distant species, Srw stands out as a notable exception. Srw is an N-terminal truncated relative of Tsg (Bonds et al., 2007) that has not been described in vertebrates or in other insects outside the genus Drosophila. The restriction of srw to Drosophilidae genomes is consistent with the idea that it is a recent evolutionary recruit required for a specialized function – to enable the accelerated/rapid generation of the BMP signaling thresholds required to specify the amnioserosa, a novel embryonic tissue innovation seen in long germband or advanced dipteran insects (Schmidt-Ott and Kwan, 2022; Nunes da Fonseca et al., 2010). Our biochemical data (see Fig. 4) provide strong support for a role for Srw as a kinetic stimulator of BMP gradient generation.

A key biochemical finding relevant to the timing adaption hypothesis is that Srw enhances the Tld cleavage of Sog when it is in a complex with Dpp. This is also a property of high levels of Tsg and its vertebrate relatives, in tissue culture experiments (Scott et al., 2001; Shimmi and O’Connor, 2003; Xie and Fisher, 2005). Our finding that both Tsg and Srw can stimulate Sog cleavage by Tld fits well with our rescue data, both positive (Tsg rescues srw) and negative (Srw does not rescue tsg). This observation is also consistent with Tsg, and its vertebrate homologs, having the ability to bind BMPs via their N-terminal module that is absent in Srw.

Our observations raise the question of how Srw and Tsg accelerate Sog proteolytic cleavage by Tld and how this might fit with srw phylogeny. Since we were unable to detect a stable association between Srw and any known D/V patterning components, any model should be consistent with a weak or transient interaction with other patterning components. We propose three possible models (Fig. 7). The simplest model (Fig. 7A) is that Srw, or the C-terminal domain of Tsg, transiently interacts with Tld, perhaps through a Tld CUB repeat, to enhance its proteolytic activity at Sog cleavage sites. The fact that Procollagen C-protease enhancers (PCPE-1 and 2) stimulate BMP1/Tld and other Tld-related enzymes to process substrates other than Sog/Chordin lends some credence to this idea. However, PCPE-1 appears to function by binding the collagen substrate and altering its availability to the protease rather than by interacting with the protease itself (Moali et al., 2005). In addition, we strongly predicted interaction sites between Srw and Tld using AlphaFold.

A second model (Fig. 7B) is suggested by the fact that in vivo extracellular matrix components (ECM) are required for proper BMP gradient formation (Bornemann et al., 2008). Previous studies have shown that the Dpp/Scw heterodimer can bind to an N-terminal motif found in Drosophila type IV collagens present in the early embryo ECM, and that Sog can also be assembled with the BMP bound to collagen (Sawala et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2008; Winstanley et al., 2015). Furthermore, Tld can bind with collagen via CUB domains 1–3, while CUBs 4 and 5 interact with Sog. Furthermore, mutations in these CUB repeats alter proteolytic activity. An important finding from these studies was that Tsg can displace the Sog/BMP heterodimer complex from collagen, enabling Tsg to assemble into a multimeric complex through two associations: one with a BMP through its N-terminal BMP binding domain, and a second with a Sog vWR domain via its C- terminal Srw-like domain (Malinauskas et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2008; Winstanley et al., 2015). This displacement-integration process was found to stimulate Tld cleavage of Sog. It is possible that in vivo Srw performs this role of displacing the Sog/BMP complex from collagen to allow Tsg assembly (Fig. 7B). However, we note that this model does not readily explain our observation that Srw can accelerate Sog proteolysis even in the absence of added collagen (Fig. 4).

Instead, we favor a third alternative model for Srw function (Fig. 7C), that depends on the stoichiometry of Tsg within the Tsg-Sog-Dpp/Scw complex which has not been reported. One possibility is that two Tsg proteins might assemble into a complex which leads to a conformation change that promotes Tld-mediated Sog cleavage. However, the incorporation of two Tsg proteins into the complex may be sterically unfavorable, thus requiring either a high concentration of Tsg or a lower Tsg dose together with a transient interaction provided by Srw. The results of our chimeric rescue experiments are consistent with this model. The Tsg-Srw chimera that contains the Sog vWF binding domain can rescue srw mutants at low but not high mRNA concentrations while TsgB-ΔSrw which lacks the interaction domain cannot rescue Srw at any concentration. We propose that at high concentrations, the TsgB-Srw chimera acts in a dominant negative manner interfering with endogenous Tsg. Further, we suggest that the interference at high concentration results because the Srw C-terminal tail in the chimera is short and hinders assembly into a stable complex with Sog (Moore et al., 2025). This could explain why the chimera does not rescue tsg mutants since it cannot form a stable association with Sog. In contrast, the TsgB-ΔSrw chimera has neither a long C-terminal tail nor the Sog vWF binding domain to engage in productive interactions with Sog and consequently rescues neither tsg nor srw mutants.

While our data show that Srw is necessary for amnioserosa specification (Fig. 3) and acceleration of Sog cleavage (Fig. 4), the reason for this acceleration is not evident. The answer may lie in the evolutionary pressures that resulted in emergence of Srw specifically in the Drosophila genus. A previous phylogenetic analysis concluded that Srw likely arose from Cv in the Drosophila lineage and was retained due to the selective pressure for more rapid pre-gastrula development in Drosophilids (Nunes da Fonseca et al., 2010). The time to gastrulation in D. melanogaster is approximately 40 min while T. castaneum, (with a single Cv-like protein) is approximately two times slower (Nunes da Fonseca et al., 2010). A second difference between Drosophilids and more basal Diptera is that in this genus there is a single extraembryonic tissue (amnioserosa) while the amnion and serosa are distinct tissues in many insect orders (Schmidt-Ott and Kwan, 2022). The selective pressure(s) that lead to the fusion of these tissues is unknown but is coincident with rapid time to gastrulation.

Our expanded database search of selected insects found that only a single Cv-like protein exists in the genomes of species examined with the exception of parasitic wasps (Nasonia vitripennis) where two Cv-like proteins are found, and in the common house fly (Musca domestica) and a green bottle fly (Lucilia sericata), each of which had two Cv-like proteins and a Tsg relative lacking introns (Fig. 6). These observations are noteworthy since the house fly and blowfly develop with a very rapid time to gastrulation (~3 h) like Drosophila and each develops a single extraembryonic tissue (Blechert et al., 2011; Cantrell et al., 1976; Mellenthin et al., 2006; West et al., 1968). One can speculate that in these species one of the Cv-like proteins, along with Tsg, is needed to achieve a high enough “Tsg” concentration to drive rapid formation of the early BMP gradient formation. In this regard, it would be interesting to determine the expression profiles for each Musca or bottle fly Cv-like gene since we predict that one would be transcribed at the blastoderm stage, along with the Tsg relative, to achieve the high “Tsg” levels needed for rapid Sog cleavage. In the Drosophila lineages we propose that there were also two duplication events leading to three Cv-like derivatives. In this case however, one intronless copy arose from the Tsg duplication event, while the other intronless duplicate experienced an N-terminal truncation leading to Srw, which in turn led to even more efficient Tld mediated processing of the Sog-Tsg-BMP heterodimer complex. Future biochemical genetic and phylogenetic studies will be needed to confirm or refute this model.

Identification of the srw locus and transcript

At the turn of the century, after two steps of chromosome walking beyond the end of BAC(D471) using λ fix genomic library screens, we arrived near the srw locus concurrent with the release of the Celera sequence extending ~60 kb beyond BAC(D471) (Genbank: AC019525). Theorizing that srw would have some homology to tsg and a large hydrophilic ORF, we utilized BLAST to narrow down the putative srw region then designed primers that would include the region of interest. PCR showed an amplified region of 480 bps was on 3 of the λ phages from the walk (λ7, λ10, λ11) as well as in wild type Drosophila genomic DNA. The srw transcript was also identified via these primers in 0–4-h, 4–8 h, and 12–24-h Nick Brown cDNA libraries (Brown and Kafatos, 1988). cDNAs and genomic PCR products were the same size suggesting that any introns would be small. Sequencing of 12 srw mutants (Table 1) verified the identity of this gene. To define the functional limits of the srw locus, we designed transgenes to rescue the srw mutant phenotype. A 6.7 kb BamHI fragment from λ10 was inserted into pCaSpeR4 (Drosophila Genomics Resource Center RRID: 1213; https://dgrc.bio.indiana.edu//stock/1213; RRID:DGRC1213) yielding pMBO2145. Transgenic lines, designated P(Casper-genomic srw), were able to rescue transheterozygous srw (srwB4/srwB18) mutant animals.To create an epitope tagged Srw rescue transgene, the genomic srw fragment was reassembled with the 6XHis C-terminal tag of pMBO2156 in the pCaSpeR4 vector (pMBO2197). Effective rescue of a srw heteroallelic combination using the transgenic P (Casper-genomic srw 6XHis) lines indicated that C-terminal tagging of Srw does not affect function.

Isolation of a srw cDNA

The srw cDNA was obtained using the MACH method (Haerry and O’Connor, 2002) from the 0–4 h Nick Brown library (Brown and Kafatos, 1988). In brief, tail-to-tail divergent primer pairs to two different parts of the srw gene were employed to amplify the 0–4 h cDNA library as template yielding both strands of the entire plasmid containing the srw cDNA. After DpnI digestion, the two strands were isolated, annealed to each other and utilized to transform E. coli. Positive clones, named pMBO2153, were sequenced to verify the start of the srw gene, confirm the absence introns, and that our genomic isolates contained the entire srw locus.

Preparation of mRNA for injections and analysis of injected embryos

Capped mRNA for microinjection was generated using the mMessage mMachine T7 kit (Ambion Cat#1344). Dechorionated embryos were prepared as described previously (Ferguson and Anderson 1992; Nguyen et al., 1998), with the following modifications. Embryos at the pre-cellular blastoderm stage were injected by inserting the needle laterally and delivering approximately 34 pL (pl) of the indicated mRNAs using a programmable microinjector (Narishige IM-300). Following injection, embryos were incubated in a humid chamber at 18 °C until stage 14, then fixed in glutaraldehyde-saturated heptane for 4 min. After fixation, embryos were manually removed from the vitelline membrane and incubated in X-Gal activity staining solution at 22 °C for 16 h to visualize the Kr-LacZ reporter that is expressed in amnioserosa cells. srw mutant embryos were identified by the absence of a Ubx-LacZ reporter located on the III chromosome TM3 balancer, while tsg mutant embryos were identified by the absence of a ftz-LacZ marker located on the X chromosome balancer FM7c, P(Parvy et al.)YH1.

In situ hybridizations

Sense and anti-sense strands of digoxigenin-labelled srw, (made from cDNA clones in pNB40) were hybridized to early wild type (w1118) aged 0–6 h after egg lay as described by (Shimell et al., 1991). Only embryos probed with the srw antisense strand displayed expression. Anti-sense RNA strands of digoxigenin-labelled tld, sog, and dpp were prepared as above and hybridized to wild type embryos for comparison to the srw expression pattern. To unambiguously identify mutant embryos, a lac Z antisense probe was included that hybridized to the balancer-derived lacZ.

Digoxigenin-labelled rho anti-sense RNA was hybridized to wild type, tld, sog, tsg, srw, and tld-srw mutant embryos to assess expression in the dorsal midline.

Immunohistochemistry

Early embryos, <6 h old, were collected, dechorionated, fixed and devitellinized as described (Mitchison and Sedat, 1983). Embryos were incubated overnight at 4 °C in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) plus 0.1% Tween 80 (PBT) with a primary antibody (1/1000 α-pMad, gift from Peter ten Dike, 1/1000 α-LacZ, Promega, 1/100 α-Kr, gift from Claude Desplan) and then washed 4X for 30 min in PBT, incubated for 2 h with the appropriate secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit/mouse HRP) and washed 4X for 30 min in PBT. Color development (Vectastain Elite ABC kits, Vector Laboratories) was carried out according to the manufacturer’s directions, and the embryos mounted in 50% glycerol and photographed under bright-field optics.

Srw 6XHis transfections

Drosophila S2 cells were transfected with AcPA-srw 6XHis DNA using DDAB (dimethyldioctadecylammonium bromide, Avanti cat # 890810P) and cultured in Shields and Sang M3 insect medium (Sigma) with 10% insect medium supplement (Sigma). After 5 days, cells were physically dislodged from the plastic, gently spun down, and the supernatant employed in cleavage reactions. For the Western blots shown in Figure S1, primary antibodies were anti-pentaHis (Qiagen) and anti-α-Tubulin (Sigma). DL800 goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (ThermoFisher) was detected on an Odyssey M scanner (Li-Cor). Cells were transfected as above and cultured without serum for 4 days prior to collection of cells and supernatants.

AlphaFold predictions

Predicted structures for Srw (AF-Q9VZE5-F1-modelv4), Tsg (AF-P54356-F1-modelv4), and Cv (AF-Q9W494-F1-modelv4) were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Jumper, 2021; Varadi, 2021). Predicted interactions were generated using the AlphaFold Server (Abramson et al., 2024). The RefSeq sequences for Srw (NP_647886), Tsg (NP_511135), and Cv (NP_536786) were used as inputs. For visualization and modeling, the signal peptides of Srw (residues 1–18), Tsg (1–20), and Cv (1–25) were removed. To define the equivalent of CHRD vWF4 in Sog, the protein sequence of CHRD (Q9H2X0) was aligned to Sog (Q24025) via NEEDLE (Madeira et al., 2024). Uniprot defines the von Willebrand factor type C (vWF) domain 4 of CHRD as residues 872–972. The vWF4 domain of Sog was therefore defined as the aligned region in Sog, residues 941–1017.

The resulting models were aligned to visually confirm similarity. Sidechain clashes were visualized using show_bumps.py plugin in Pymol and compared with relaxed models developed with OpenMM/Amber (ColabFold) (Mirdita et al., 2022).

Phylogenetic analysis of Cv-related proteins

Protein BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) against full-length Dmel_Cv was conducted serially for candidate species. One isoform was selected for each corresponding gene, and the number of introns was deduced from Genome browser displays. The intron information is not considered in the phylogenetic tree building. The protein sequences were aligned with ClustalOmega (www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo). For the primary analysis, non-conserved sequences for Cv and Tsg homologs were trimmed from the N and C terminus, leaving 3aa + CNE and CPE+3aa respectively (relative to Dmel_Cv). Srw homologs were trimmed at the N-terminus leaving 3aa + MRKK (relative to Dmel_srw). For Srw-coverage comparisons, the N-terminus of all proteins were further trimmed to leave 2aa + MRKK. ClustalOmega alignments were repeated with the trimmed protein sequences. Tree building was conducted with the R packages phangorn (2.12.1) and ape (5.8–1) (Schliep, 2010; Paradis and Schliep, 2018). Alignment files from ClustalOmega were used to generate a tree using the Maximum Likelihood method, rooted with the non-insect crustacean Daphnia (Dcar_Cv) as the outgroup. The AA substitution model was determined by the modelTest function. Bootstrap analysis was conducted to provide confidence estimates for internal nodes. Within the Dipteran portion of the dendograms there was no meaningful difference between the NC-trimmed and Srw-coverage analysis; we present the NC-trimmed analysis.
