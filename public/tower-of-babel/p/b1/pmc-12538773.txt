CRISPR/Cas9 library screening reveals that STK19 has synergistic antitumor effects when combined with cisplatin on tongue squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Chunyan Li, Wen Peng, Zhaoming Zhong, Chao Zhang, Xuemin Wang, Rujia Qin, Qian Lei, Jialing Lv, Feineng Liu, Yinghong Zhao, Zengbo Lv, Chongxin Li, Shixuan Yang, Haixia Zhang, Zhi Tao, Chuanzheng Sun

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is a common oral cancer that has a high propensity for recurrence and metastasis. Therefore, TSCC has a 50% 5-year survival rate. Platinum-based chemotherapy is an effective treatment for squamous cell carcinoma, however, chemotherapy resistance remains a major issue. Therefore, innovative and effective drug combinations are needed to improve TSCC patient prognosis.MethodsIn this study, we conducted an in vitro CRISPR/Cas9 library screen using two TSCC cell lines (Tscca and Cal27) to identify specific genes that, when inhibited, synergize with cisplatin to effectively suppress tumor growth.ResultsWe identified STK19 as a potential drug target. Background

Tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is the most prevalent and aggressive type of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). It has a high propensity for recurrence and metastasis, leading to a poor prognosis [1–3]. The elevated mortality rate stems from two primary factors. Firstly, since this disease often presents no symptoms during its early stage, early diagnosis remains a challenge, resulting in many patients being diagnosed at an advanced stage where surgical intervention becomes less viable. Secondly, the main treatment methods primarily rely on platinum-based single-agent or combination chemotherapy [4, 5]. However, resistance to cisplatin, whether inherited or acquired, significantly contributes to chemotherapy failure [6, 7]. Consequently, surgical intervention remains the most efficacious option; however, it only accessible to a minority of early-stage patients. Advanced-stage patients who are unable to undergo surgery typically receive a standard treatment regimen comprising chemoradiotherapy and targeted therapy [8, 9].

Chemotherapy resistance remains a primary cause of treatment failure, and combination chemotherapy yields severe side effects, further exacerbating the already high mortality rate among advanced stage TSCC patients, with a mere 5-year survival rate of 50% [10, 11]. Hence, novel drug combinations with superior therapeutic efficacy to improve the outcomes of advanced TSCC patients are urgently needed.

Understanding the key drivers behind the progression of TSCC and its sensitivity to treatment is crucial for developing novel and more efficacious therapeutic approaches. Genomic sequencing of HNSCC has revealed recurrent gene alterations, with TP53 mutations detected in 55% of recurrent oral tumors [12]. Furthermore, mutations in tumor suppressor genes, including KMT2D, NOTCH1, KRAS, PIK3CA, CASP8, and PTEN, that inhibit their function are important genetic factors contributing to oral cancer relapse [13–16]. Currently, targeted therapy for these recurrence-associated genes is lacking. Mutations in RAS genes result in overactivation of the downstream MEK-ERK pathway, suggesting that targeting this pathway could be a promising therapeutic strategy. Nonetheless, inhibiting its activation in TSCC has not yet demonstrated significant clinical efficacy [17]. Hence, Identification of effective chemotherapeutic combinations remains a viable approach for improving the treatment outcomes of patients with TSCC. The advent of CRISPR/Cas9 libraries offers a potent tool to facilitate the discovery of effective drug combinations [18–20]. In this study, CRISPR/Cas9 library screening technology was utilized in TSCC cells to identify effective and potentially synergistic drug combinations. The goal was to discover new inhibitors that could enhance the chemosensitivity of the traditional chemotherapeutic drug cisplatin. Historically, cisplatin has been the primary chemotherapeutic agent for HNSCC and is commonly used alone or in combination with other chemotherapy drugs for the treatment of TSCC. Cisplatin has been an important drug in cancer treatment since 1978 and is widely used in the treatment of various malignant tumors, such as ovarian cancer, lung cancer, and testicular cancer.

While the chemotherapeutic efficacy of cisplatin is approximately 50% in TSCC, cisplatin chemoresistance remains a significant challenge in clinical treatment. Therefore, the search for agents that can sensitize TSCC cells to cisplatin is crucial for overcoming chemoresistance and improving patient outcomes. In this study, CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (KO) library screening technology was employed to identify potential targets with synergistic effects when combined with cisplatin in vitro. The focus of this study is to explore kinases associated with TSCC cell survival after cisplatin treatment, with the aim of identifying those kinases that, when inhibited, exert a synergistic cytotoxic effect on tumor cells when used in combination with cisplatin. A customized sgRNA library containing 5171 sgRNAs targeting 508 kinases was constructed and screened to explore drug targets that synergize with cisplatin in TSCC. The results highlighted serine/threonine kinase 19 (STK19) as a promising candidate with a significant synergistic lethal effect when combined with cisplatin. At the molecular level, deletion of the STK19 gene lead to cisplatin sensitization, potentially through depletion of the DNA repair protein MGMT. This depletion leads to cell death both in vitro and in vivo, which is caused by the accumulation of DNA damage. These findings highlight the potential of CRISPR/Cas9 library screening in identifying novel and more effective drug combination targets.

Methods

In vitro cell culture

The TSCC cell lines (Tscca, Scc-9, and Cal27) utilized in this study were acquired from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai, China. These cell lines were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin. The culture was carried out in an incubator with 5% carbon dioxide at a constant temperature of 37 °C.

Construction of CRISPR sgRNA kinase library, viral packaging and infection

The CRISPR/Cas9 kinase library, sourced from the State Key Laboratory of Oncology in South China, comprised 5171 sgRNAs targeting 508 kinases (with 10 specific sgRNAs per kinase) following a standard negative screening protocol. We packaged the library plasmids into lentiviruses using 293 T cells and the packaging plasmids psPAX2, and pMD2. G (library plasmids: psPAX2:pMD2.G = 3:2:1). To ensure sufficient sgRNA coverage at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) (~ 0.3), 5.2 × 106 Tscca or Cal27 cells were seeded in 5 × 10 cm dishes, with the aim of infecting each cell with at most 1 sgRNA. Lentiviruses containing sgRNA libraries were used to infect Tscca or Cal27 cells, followed by selection with 1 μg/ml puromycin for at least 7 days to eliminate uninfected cells. After reaching an adequate number of cells, Cal27 cells were seeded in 4 × 10 cm dishes, with one dish of cells serving as a control, and the remaining three dishes of cells being treated with cisplatin at concentrations of 0.125, 0.25, and 0.5 μg/ml. Tscca cells were seeded in 3 × 10 cm dishes, with one dish serving as a control and the other two dishes of cells being treated with cisplatin at concentrations of 0.5 and 1 μg/ml. The cells were then collected after 48 h of treatment. Genomic DNA was extracted, amplified via PCR, and analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS).

Validation of STK19 as a potentl cisplatin sensitization target in TSCC

To validate STK19 following initial screening, we designed and cloned sgRNA guide sequences (SgRNAs) for STK19 knockout in the Tscca and Scc-9 cell lines. The following sgRNA guide sequences were utilized: sgRNA1, CACCGGCACTGCCCCAGCATACGGT; and sgRNA2, CACCGAGAAGGAGCTTCAAGAGCAG. Oligonucleotides containing 5′-CACCG and 5′-AAAC sequences with sgRNA guide sequences were procured from Suzhou and hybridized to generate sticky-ended double-stranded DNA for ligation. The LentiV2 plasmid harboring the sgRNA backbone was digested with the Esp3I restriction endonuclease at 37 °C for 4‒6 h. The LentiV2 vector was purified via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, after which the vector was ligated to the target gene sequence using T4 ligase. The HEK293T cell line was used to produce a STK19 knockout lentivirus. The lentivirus was used to infect Tscca and Scc-9 cells to express STK19 sgRNA, Establishment of stable cell lines. Puromycin (1 μg/ml) was used to select TSCC cell lines with stable STK19 knockout, and western blotting analysis was performed to assess the efficiency of STK19 gene knockout.

Cell viability assay

Tscca and Scc-9 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3500 cells per well. Once the cells adhered to the bottom of the plate, they were treated with cisplatin and ZT-12-037-01 (a small-molecule inhibitor that specifically targets STK19). After a predetermined time (48 h), the medium in the 96-well plates was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium. Subsequently, 10 μl of CCK8 solution was added to every 100 μl of fresh medium, and the cells were further incubated for 3 h. The 96-well plates were then removed from the incubator, and the absorbance of the cells under different treatment conditions was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plate reader.

Colony formation assay

Tscca or Scc-9 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1500 cells per well (Corning). After 24 h, the cells were subjected to treatment with cisplatin or ZT-12-037-01 for 3 weeks. The culture medium containing the drugs was changed once a week. At the specified time points (about 3 weeks), the plates were washed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and then stained with crystal violet solution (0.5% crystal violet in methanol) for 30 min. Following staining, the plates were washed with water, allowed to dry, and subsequently scanned and counted.

Apoptosis assay

Tscca or Scc-9 cells were seeded in 6-well plates provided by Corning at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well. These cells were then subjected to treatment with cisplatin or ZT-12-037-01 for 48 h. After treatment, the plates were rinsed with cold PBS, digested with 0.2% trypsin without the addition of EDTA, and then suspended in Annexin V-binding buffer. After the addition of FITC-conjugated Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) (BD 556547), the cells were incubated at room temperature for a period of ten minutes. Afterward, dilutions were performed using binding buffer, and the results were analyzed by flow cytometry with a FACSCalibur instrument.

BeyoClick™ EdU cell proliferation assay

Tscca and Scc-9 cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The cells were then exposed to cisplatin or ZT-12-037-01(MCE: HY-122866) for 24 h. Afterward, a concentration of 10 μM EDU (Beyotime: C0075L) was introduced, and the cells were kept in a controlled environment for 2 h. The cells were then treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation, followed by washing with 3% BSA. The samples were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and incubated in the dark for 30 min with Click Additive solution. Hoechst 3342 was used for nuclear staining. The cells were then captured and documented using a fluorescence microscope, and statistical analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

Tscca and Scc-9 cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The cells were then exposed to cisplatin or ZT-12-037-01 for 48 h. After being washed with cold PBS, the cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling Technology). After 15 min of spinning at 14,000 RPM at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected, and the total protein concentration was measured using Thermo Fisher Pierce BCA protein assay reagent. Samples with the same protein concentration were loaded into each well of a gel, separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to Millipore PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% milk and incubated for 1 h at room temperature and overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies such as anti-γ-H2AX (Cell Signaling Technology:Cat# 9718S,1:1000), anti-STK19(Santa Cruz: Cat# SC100427,1:1000), anti-MGMT(ABclonal: Cat# A11151,1:1000), anti-cleaved caspase-3(Cell Signaling Technology: Cat# 9664S,1:500), anti-PARP(Cell Signaling Technology: Cat#9508,1:500),anti-Bim(Cell Signaling Technology: Cat# 2819,1:500),anti-Bak(Cell Signaling Technology: Cat# 12,105,1:500),anti-Bax(Cell Signaling Technology: Cat# 5023,1;500) and anti-cleaved caspase-9(Cell Signaling Technology: Cat# 20,750,1;500) antibodies. After being washed with 1 × TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) at room temperature for 1 h. A Millipore enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit was used to visualize the membranes containing attached antibodies.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells cultured on glass coverslips (VWR) were treated with ciaplatin or ZT-12-037-01 for specific time, Then washed with PBS and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 10 min. After fixation, the cells were treated for 10 min with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS. After being blocked with 4% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 1 h, the cells were treated with a γ-H2AX antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) for 8 h. After being washed with PBS, the cells were treated with a secondary antibody for 1 h (Alexa Fluor-488 goat anti-mouse IgG/IgM [heavy chain (H) + light chain (L)] [Invitrogen]). After washing with PBS, the coverslips were air-dried and mounted on Fisher Scientific slides in reverse using DAPI (Vector Laboratories) mounting media. ImageJ software was used for statistical analysis of the images captured using a fluorescence microscope.

In vivo xenograft experiments

Female 4–8-week-old BALB/cAnN-nu (nude) mice were used in the xenograft experiments. Xenograft tumors were created by injecting control sgRNA-infected WT (sgNC) and STK19 KO (sgSTK19) cells into the axillae of 6-week-old nude mice purchased from Hunan Slake King Laboratory Animal business. At 30mm3, the tumors were apparent, and the mice received abdomen injections of cisplatin/ZT-12-037-01. Weekly monitoring of tumor appearance and caliper measurements (volume = length × width2/2) were conducted. After 14 days of therapy, the mice were subjected to CO2 asphyxiation, and the tumors were removed for examination. The Kunming Medical University Experimental Animal Centre Animal Protection and Use Committee authorized all the animal operations.

RNA-Seq and library preparation

Control sgNC and sgSTK19 cells were exposed to cisplatin or ZT-12-037-01 for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA-Quick Purification Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library preparation and high-throughput sequencing were conducted by Mingma Technology. The quality of the RNA-seq data was assessed using FASTQC.

Multicolor immunofluorescence analysis

Fresh tissues were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Next, 5 μm-thick paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared and placed on slides. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene and ethanol after baking at 63 °C for 1 h. The hot-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) cycle was performed in Akoya Biosciences AR6 citrate buffer for antigen retrieval. After being blocked for 10 min with blocking buffer, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h in a humidified environment at room temperature. Secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase were added for 10 min. We used different opal detector fluorophores for each marker: STK19 (ABclonal, 1:2000, Cat# A13383)/Opal520, γ-H2AX (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:2000, Cat# 7631)/Opal570, and MGMT (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:2000, Cat# 86,039)/Akoya Biosciences supplied a 7-color manual kit (NEL811001KT, 1:800 dilution) including all opal fluorophores and DAPI. The slides were scanned and photographed using TissueFAXS following DAPI staining and sealing.

qRT-PCR

In order to verify the mRNA expression levels of MGMT in different groups, we collected control (sgNC) and STK19 KO cells were exposed to cisplatin or ZT-12-037-01 for 24 h, and detected the relative expression levels of MGMT by qRT-PCR. This study was approved by the Internal Review Committee of the Third affiliated hospital of Kunming medical University., Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the TRizol method. hifair® Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR was used to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). Servicebio® 2 × Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme) was used to detect the mRNA levels of target genes. Human gene primers were used as follows (5′-3′):

MGMT F primer:TTTTCCAGCAAGAGTCGTTCAC;

MGMT R primer:GGGACAGGATTGCCTCTCAT;

GAPDH F primer:GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT;

GAPDH was used as an internal reference gene to correct the relative expression level of MGMT, and the primers were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 for Windows. Unpaired Student’s t tests were used to compare two groups, unless otherwise specified. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Boxplots depict the median (center), 25th and 75th percentiles (bounds of the box), and minimum and maximum values (whiskers). Each experiment was replicated at least three times. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All grouped data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated.

Results

CRISPR library screening revealed STK19 as a critical gene for cisplatin resistance in TSCC

In this study, we conducted kinome CRISPR/Cas9 knockout library screening to identify critical genes implicated in cisplatin resistance in human TSCC. The TSCC cell lines Tscca and Cal27 were employed in vitro models. The kinome CRISPR library screening revealed 5171 unique sgRNAs targeting 508 protein-coding genes. Subsequently, the TSCC cells were treated with either vehicle or cisplatin for 48 h for positive and negative screening. We hypothesized that the sgRNA counts would significantly increase after drug treatment, indicating that knockout of the corresponding gene might enhance resistance to cisplatin chemotherapy. Conversely, a significant decrease in the number of sgRNAs was detected after cisplatin treatment compared with that in the control group, this suggests that target genes corresponding to sgRNA may serve as potential drug targets that exert synergistic effects with cisplatin (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1CRISPR screening identifies STK19 as a synergistic target for DDP in TSCC. We aimed to identify genes with a markedly reduced number of sgRNAs after cisplatin treatment. The deep sequencing results revealed that a total of 17 kinases in Tscca and Cal27 cells exhibited a significant decrease in sgRNA counts following cisplatin treatment (Fig. 1B). Genes with substantial differences are depicted in Fig. 1C. To expedite the clinical translation process, we imposed two specific conditions for gene screening: firstly, the kinase encoded by the gene should have corresponding and specific targeted inhibitors to facilitate its rapid clinical application; secondly, the sgRNA counts of the gene should significantly decrease in both cell lines with increasing cisplatin concentration. We subsequently conducted a validation screening with additional sgRNAs for each of these genes using the Cal27 cell line. The results demonstrated that depletion of STK19 synergistically increased cisplatin cytotoxicity (Fig. 1D). The number of sgRNAs corresponding to STK19 kinase decreased in a gradient manner with increasing concentrations of cisplatin (Fig. 1E).

STK19 (serine/threonine kinase 19), located in the major histocompatibility complex MHC class III region on chromosome 6, plays a crucial role in transcriptional regulation through its phosphorylation. Emerging evidence suggests its close association with tumorigenesis and its important role in DNA damage repair. However, its significance in the progression of HNSCC or TSCC remains unexplored, and its potential as a combined therapeutic target for HNSCC requires further investigation. Consequently, we investigated the potential role of STK19 in HNSCC progression to assess whether inhibiting STK19 has therapeutic potential for HNSCC. To achieve this goal, we analyzed the regulation of STK19 expression in HNSCC tumors and its prognostic value for patient survival. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the expression level of STK19 was significantly higher in cancer tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 1F, 1G). Our analysis of the cBioPortal online database revealed an approximately 8% mutation frequency for STK19 in HNSCC patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort (Fig. 1H). Compared with patients without STK19 mutations, patients with STK19 mutations exhibited shorter disease-free survival (Fig. 1I).

Transcriptional data analyses of HNSCC patients in TCGA dataset by GEPIA revealed that STK19 upregulation in tumors significantly reduced overall survival (Fig. 1J). These findings strongly indicate that STK19 plays a critical role and represents a promising therapeutic target in HNSCC.

Inhibition of STK19 sensitized TSCC cells to cisplatin treatment

To validate the outcomes of our CRISPR/Cas9 kinase knockout library screening, we established STK19 stable knockout (KO) subclones in Tscca/Scc-9 cells through infection with the two sgRNAs. Western blot analysis revealed that complete depletion of the STK19 protein was rare, particularly in Tscca cells (Fig. 2A, 2E). Nevertheless, the DNA synthesis ability and clone formation ability of some STK19-deficient TSCC cells were significantly attenuated under cisplatin treatment compared with the control group (Fig. 2B, 2F and supplementary Fig. S1), along with a notable increase in the number of apoptotic cells in the presence of cisplatin (Fig. 2D, 2H). Consistent with these findings, longer-term crystal violet colony formation assays revealed that, compared with control clones, STK19 KO clones presented significantly greater sensitivity to cisplatin (Fig. 2C, 2G). An STK19 inhibitor sensitized TSCC cells to cisplatin treatment

ZT-12-037-01 is a small-molecule inhibitor that specifically targets STK19. We determined the IC50 of ZT-12-037-01 in the Tscca/Scc-9 cell line (Fig. 3A, 3E) and observed that ZT-12-037-01 significantly inhibited cell proliferation in the presence of cisplatin (Fig. 3B, 3F). To assess whether ZT-12-037-01 could synergize with cisplatin to suppress TSCC growth, we treated Tscca/Scc-9 cells with 0.8/3 µg/ml cisplatin alone, 12 µM ZT-12-037-01 alone, or 0.8/3 µg/ml cisplatin combined with 12 µM ZT-12-037-01 for 48 h. Annexin V and PI staining revealed that cisplatin alone could induce apoptosis. Notably, ZT-12-037-01 significantly increased the number of apoptotic cells induced by cisplatin, although ZT-12-037-01 itself only slightly induced apoptosis (Fig. 3C, 3G). Consistent with these findings, ZT-12-037-01 significantly inhibited DNA synthesis, as determined by the 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay (Fig. 3D, 3H). STK19 depletion results in the excessive accumulation of damaged DNA

We also found that cisplatin treatment of STK19 KO cells altered the expression of genes involved in DNA repair. Our original approach included immunofluorescence (IF) experiments to identify phosphorylated gamma-H2AX (γ-H2AX), a histone modification observed in megabase chromatin regions near double-strand breaks. KO cells presented a significantly increased number of γ-H2AX foci at 4 h after cisplatin treatment, but control cells did not present a similar number of foci until 24 to 48 h after treatment with the same dose of cisplatin (Fig. 4A). STK19 KO cells had considerably greater amounts of γ-H2AX foci at 48 h after treatment, suggesting that more DNA double-strand breaks (DNA DSBs) formed after cisplatin treatment (Fig. 4A, lower). Pharmacological suppression of STK19 with small-molecule inhibitors, similar to genetic depletion, led to considerable accumulation of DNA damage, as shown by the formation of γ-H2AX foci (Fig. 4B, 4C). We used SDS‒PAGE to assess γ-H2AX protein levels, in addition to observing foci development. Deficiency in STK19 leads to impaired DNA repair by the repair enzyme MGMT

Next,in order to elucidate the specific mechanism of STK19 increases sensitivity to cisplatin chemotherapy. We initially investigated whether STK19-depleted cells exhibited a distinct transcriptional response to cisplatin. To this end, we conducted a comparative analysis of the transcriptional profiles of control and KO cells in two independent TSCC cell lines. Notably, KO cells displayed markedly different responses to cisplatin, with a substantially greater number of genes showing differential regulation.

Combination treatment synergistically inhibits tumor growth in vivo

We next examined whether the combination may decrease TSCC tumor development synergistically. We tested STK19 genetic depletion and pharmacological suppression in TSCC xenograft model. First, we compared the cisplatin sensitivity of control and STK19 KO tumors. To compare tumors from different genetic backgrounds, we injected 5 × 106 control Tscca cells into the left armpit and the same number of STK19 KO cells into the right armpit of each animal. We compared two tumors in the same animal using this method. After 1 week of tumor development, the mice were randomly allocated to the control and 8 mg/kg cisplatin groups. Cisplatin reduced control tumor volume, whereas STK19 KO tumor volume decreased the most (Fig. 6A–D). The cisplatin-treated STK19 KO tumors were considerably smaller than the control or untreated KO tumors from the third treatment on (day 6 of tumor growth). IHC revealed that the protein expression levels of STK19 significantly reduced in STK19 KO tumors (Fig. 6E). As shown in vitro, mIHC was performed to determine whether cisplatin caused greater DNA damage in STK19 KO cells in vivo. Similar to the in vitro results, cisplatin treatment resulted in increased γ-H2AX staining in STK19 KO tumors (Fig. 6F), suggesting more DNA damage in STK19 KO tumors than in control tumors. Additionally, in vivo xenograft tests were performed to determine whether cisplatin and STK19 suppression synergistically decreased TSCC tumor volume. After 3 days of tumor development, the mice were randomly separated into 4 groups. Importantly, only tumors treated with the combination treatment showed a statistically significant decrease in tumor volume at 6 days posttreatment. The STK19 inhibitor with cisplatin had a substantially greater therapeutic effect than either agent alone (Fig. 6G–J). Discussion

In this study, our primary objective was to identify new pharmacological targets that are capable of increasing the therapeutic efficiency of existing chemotherapy regimens in TSCC. This disease continues to have a poor prognosis despite enormous efforts, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 50% [21, 22]. Cisplatin is a foundational component of multi-agent chemotherapy,this study sought to elucidate novel combination targets that yield a synthetic lethal interaction with cisplatin. The CRISPR library screening method was used to accomplish this goal. This method allowed us to discover critical regulators whose depletion might result in conditional lethality when cisplatin is administered. The screening was carried out with the use of an in vitro cell model using a customized library of sgRNAs targeting approximately 500 genes that are involved in the control of kinases. Preliminary screening and subsequent validation tests revealed that inhibiting STK19 increased the sensitivity of TSCC cells to cisplatin.

STK19 plays a pivotal role in driving cancer progression, making it an increasingly important focus of research. Particularly noteworthy is its potential as a target for drug intervention. Wang et al. [23] revealed STK19 as a promising candidate influenced by NRAS in tumor development. Through meticulous structure‒activity relationship studies, they developed a specific small-molecule inhibitor, ZT-12-037-01, tailored to target STK19. While clinical trials for ZT-12-037-01 are pending, our own findings underscore the significance of STK19 as a therapeutic target, especially in TSCC patients typically treated with cisplatin chemotherapy. We demonstrated a clear correlation between elevated STK19 expression and poor patient survival in patients with TSCC, suggesting that STK19 inhibition could improve patient outcomes. Notably, our screening revealed that the STK19 inhibitor and cisplatin exerted synergistic lethal effects, suggesting the potential for enhanced efficacy and selectivity against TSCC cells through combined STK19 inhibitor and cisplatin treatment.

The mechanism underlying the synergistic cytotoxicity resulting from STK19 inhibition and cisplatin treatment remains unclear. The combination treatment-induced DNA damage offers some insights into the underlying processes involved. Our data support the notion that cells lacking STK19 are unable to withstand the DNA damage induced by cisplatin treatment. The inhibition of STK19 also leads to a substantial decrease in the expression of MGMT, a DNA repair enzyme located on the human chromosome 10 [24]. MGMT plays a crucial role in repairing O6-methylguanine nucleotides, preventing DNA damage and cell death caused by alkylating agents [25–27]. High levels of MGMT have been linked to resistance to cisplatin chemotherapy, with studies showing its involvement in regulating RAD51 expression and BRCA1 phosphorylation in nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Inhibiting MGMT effectively reduces homologous recombination repair activity, enhancing the ability of cisplatin and PARP inhibitors to induce DNA DSBs and cell death [28]. The combined depletion of STK19 and treatment with cisplatin results in considerable accumulation of DNA damage, which is attributed to the depletion of MGMT and reduced homologous recombination repair. While the precise molecular mechanisms have not been fully elucidated, we propose that STK19 inhibition downregulates MGMT and impairs homologous recombination repair, contributing to the enhanced DNA damage observed through γ-H2AX staining and Western blotting. Cisplatin, a widely used first-line chemotherapeutic agent for a variety of tumors, including ovarian cancer and lung cancer [29–32], has potential for enhancing cisplatin chemoresistance and chemo-insensitivity. However, further basic research is needed to explore the clinical application of ZT-12-037-01 in combination with cisplatin, and a comprehensive evaluation of its pharmacological and toxicological effects is crucial for ensuring safe and effective patient use. Cisplatin induces DNA damage primarily through DNA adduct formation, leading to cell death if the damage is excessive and irreparable. Therefore, investigating the potential synergistic effects of ZT-12-037-01 with other drugs, such as gemcitabine, 5-fluorouracil, and mitomycin, is important. Furthermore, the interaction of cisplatin with various kinases involved in DNA repair warrants further study to determine whether synergistic effects exist.

Moreover, metabolic reprogramming is a well-documented determinant of cisplatin efficacy and the chemotherapeutic response, influencing drug resistance mechanisms [33, 34]. Recent investigations have identified STK19 as a critical regulator of metabolic reprogramming, and succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit A (SDHA), an essential enzymatic component linking the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to oxidative phosphorylation, is markedly downregulated in STK19-knockdown (KD) cells [35].

Targeting STK19 in combination with cisplatin may disrupt tumor metabolic plasticity by simultaneously inhibiting DNA damage repair and intercepting compensatory metabolic adaptations that maintain redox homeostasis and nucleotide biosynthesis. This dual-targeting strategy could synergistically impair cancer cell survival by exacerbating metabolic stress while preventing adaptive resistance mechanisms. Investigating the impact of STK19 inhibition on cisplatin-altered metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation, could reveal novel mechanisms to overcome chemoresistance, providing a rationale for developing STK19-targeted adjuvants to use in platinum-based chemotherapy regimens.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified STK19 as a novel chemosensitising target for cisplatin in tongue squamous carcinoma cells based on CRISPR technology and the mechanism that targeted inhibition of STK19 inhibits the MGMT-mediated DNA damage repair pathway and enhances cisplatin-induced DNA damage and apoptosis.
