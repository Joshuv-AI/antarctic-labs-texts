Relationship typologies with online sexual partners and condomless anal intercourse among men who have sex with men: Evidence from latent class analysis and multilevel regression
Authors: Luyao Xie, Angela Yuen Chun Li, Alex Siu Wing Chan, Ho Hin Lee, Meiqi Xin, Zixin Wang, Joseph Tak Fai Lau, Phoenix Kit Han Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOnline platforms have become important contexts through which men who have sex with men (MSM) meet sexual partners, yet little is known about how relationship patterns with online partners cluster and how these patterns shape condomless anal intercourse (CAI).ObjectivesTo identify distinct relationship typologies between MSM and their online sexual partners and examine how the typologies are associated with CAI, considering participants’ attitude towards condom use as a moderator.MethodsA telephone-based, cross-sectional survey was conducted among MSM in Hong Kong between November 2023 and August 2024. Participants provided information on their demographics and condom attitudes, the relational features of their online sexual partnerships, and condom use.ResultsA total of 405 participants reported on 1,067 online sexual partners in the past year. Latent class analysis identified three relationship typologies among MSM and their online sexual partners: Sexual Desire-Oriented (62.5%), Relationally Driven (20.8%), and Sexually Adventurous (16.6%). Multilevel regressions showed that, compared with Sexual Desire-Oriented partnerships, Relationally Driven (odds ratio [OR]=3.11; 95% CI: 1.69–5.70) and Sexually Adventurous (OR=2.47; 95% CI: 1.31–4.65) partnerships were associated with higher odds of CAI, after adjusting for covariates. Negative attitudes towards condom use were also associated with a greater likelihood of CAI, and this association was significantly stronger in the Relationally Driven group (OR=1.13; 95% CI: 1.01–1.26).ConclusionsFindings highlight the relationship heterogeneity between MSM and their online sexual partners. Tailored interventions that consider different relational contexts are needed to reduce CAI and improve HIV prevention.Keywords: Condomless anal intercourse, Condom use attitude, Latent class analysis, MSM, Online sexual partners, Relationship typologies

Introduction

High prevalence of HIV and CAI among men who have sex with men (MSM)

Men who have sex with men (MSM) continue to represent a high proportion of new HIV infections and AIDS cases (HIV/AIDS, 2021). Condomless anal intercourse (CAI) remains one of the most important behavioural pathways of HIV transmission in this population and continues to be highly prevalent across settings (Fernandez-Rollan et al., 2019; García et al., 2014; Knox et al., 2021a). Sexual practices often do not occur in isolation and are enacted within relational contexts which may vary across partners. Understanding CAI among MSM therefore requires attention not only to individual attitudes and intentions, but also to the relationship contexts in which sexual encounters take place.

Online platforms for partner seeking and sexual risk behaviors among MSM

This relational perspective is particularly important in the context of online partner seeking. Online platforms, including geosocial networking sites (GSNS) such as Grindr, Jack’d, and Scruff, as well as broader social media platforms, have significantly transformed the ways in which MSM meet and connect with sexual partners. These platforms allow users to interact with a larger pool of potential partners and, in the case of GSNS, often use location-based technology to identify partners nearby, facilitating faster and more convenient connections (Licoppe et al., 2016). Beyond GSNS, social media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter are also increasingly used by MSM to seek sexual partners, often through private messaging or group communities, broadening the range of opportunities for partner-seeking behavior.

Online partner-seeking via these online platforms is widespread among MSM. Evidence from different settings shows that many MSM use these platforms regularly and engage sexually with partners met online. In Southern China, 89% of MSM reported ever using geosocial networking apps, and 63.8% had anal intercourse with app-met partners (Guo et al., 2023). In the United States, the proportion reporting dating app use to meet other men rose from 66.5% in 2018 to 84.9% in 2021 (Hecht et al., 2024). In Hong Kong, most MSM app users reported logging onto such apps at least weekly (Chan et al., 2024). In this digital era, online platforms are not merely occasional tools for partner seeking, but high-frequency contexts in which MSM form and maintain sexual partnerships.

A substantial body of research has investigated the relationship between online sexual partner seeking and sexual risk behaviors among MSM. Studies report that app users tend to have more sexual partners, more frequent anal intercourse, and higher rates of CAI or HIV/STI diagnosis (Card et al., 2016; Holloway et al., 2015a; Lewnard & Berrang-Ford, 2014). However, the findings were inconsistent. For example, some studies indicate that MSM are more likely to use condoms with partners met online compared with partners met offline (Rice et al., 2012), while others show that meeting sexual partners online is not significantly associated with sexual risk behaviors (Heijman et al., 2016; Wray & Monti, 2020b). The mixed findings regarding the associations with CAI suggest that online partner-seeking behaviors alone cannot fully account for CAI risk. Rather than treating online partner seeking as a single exposure, it may be more useful to examine the diversity of relationship characteristics formed through online platforms and how these relationship contexts shape condom use.

Complexity of online partner and relationship types: the need for a person-centered approach

Sexual partnerships are often multidimensional. An individual may have multiple online sexual partners, and these partnerships may differ in their duration, degree of emotional closeness, frequency of contact, sexual role, and motivations for sex (Bauermeister, 2015), contributing to the complexity of relationship patterns. Different configurations of these characteristics can give rise to distinct relationship types, within which condom use may differ depending on the relational context.

Existing research provides some evidence that characteristics of online sexual relationships matter for sexual practices among MSM. Studies have shown that sexual communication with online partners, such as discussing sexual preferences or disclosing HIV or PrEP status, can influence sexual behaviours (Birnholtz et al., 2020; Newcomb et al., 2016; Pantalone et al., 2020). Other studies suggest that MSM engage with different kinds of partners for different relational or sexual purposes, and that these partner types may be associated with different practices (Bauermeister, 2015; Goedel & Duncan, 2015a). For instance, a previous study indicated that MSM might classify their sexual partners into different types, such as friends with benefits, hooking-up, or romantic interests, which are linked to different sexual practices (Sang et al., 2021). Another study identifying online dating patterns among MSM and found that “date seekers” were more likely to engage in sexual sensational activities, whereas “instant lovers” were less likely to pursue committed romantic relationships but more likely to report CAI (Choi & Bauermeister, 2022). Research in other populations similarly shows that condom use varies by relationship type, often being more common in casual or newer relationships (Hock-Long et al., 2013) and less common in regular or emotionally committed partnerships (Manlove et al., 2014).

Together, these studies suggest that CAI is likely shaped by the heterogeneity of sexual partnerships rather than by single relationship characteristics considered in isolation. However, relatively little is known about how multiple relationship characteristics and sexual motivations cluster together within online sexual partnerships among MSM, and how these configurations relate to CAI. Examining one relational factor at a time may obscure the ways in which intimacy, duration, sexual intention, and other partnership features intersect in practice. A person-centred approach is therefore particularly valuable in this context, as it allows the identification of unobserved subgroups characterised by distinct combinations of relational and motivational features.

Latent class analysis (LCA) provides such an approach. Rather than assuming that relationship characteristics operate independently, LCA can model how multiple indicators co-occur and identify underlying relationship typologies that reflect real-world patterns (Collins & Lanza, 2013; McCutcheon, 1987). This is especially relevant for identifying relationship typologies with online sexual partners, where their relationship characteristics and motivations for having sex often intersect in complex ways. By modelling these indicators concurrently, LCA can capture heterogeneous relational patterns that may be more meaningful for understanding condom use than any single variable alone (Scotto Rosato & Baer, 2012). Although latent class or profile approaches have increasingly been applied in social, behavioural, and health research (Collins & Lanza, 2013), their application to MSM sexual partnerships remains limited. Existing studies have focused mainly on young MSM (Choi & Bauermeister, 2022) or on broader partner categories not specifically centred on online-met sexual partners (Janulis et al., 2018). To our knowledge, no studies have examined the relationship typologies between MSM and their online sexual partners by simultaneously considering a range of relational characteristics and sexual motivations. This study aims to fill in this research gap and examine how these typologies relate to CAI among MSM.

Attitude towards condom use as a potential moderator

In addition to partner-level characteristics, intrapersonal factors, such as condom use attitudes, can influence how individuals high-risk behaviors occur within different relational contexts. Health behavior theories, such as the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991) and the Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills (IMB) model (Fisher et al., 2009), have highlighted the important role of attitudes in behavioral intentions, which in turn guide actual behavior such as condom use (Jiang et al., 2019). Negative condom-use attitudes may weaken condom use intentions and increase the likelihood of CAI (Albarracín et al., 2001). Moreover, attitude towards condom use may differ by partner type. In emotionally close or committed relationships, using condom is likely to reduce their intimacy and implies a lack of trust (Manlove et al., 2014). Negative condom attitudes may reinforce relational pressures to forgo condoms, resulting in higher CAI risk. In contrast, in a casual or instrumental relationship, condom attitudes may exert a smaller effect, as the context itself encourages safer sex practices (Hock-Long et al., 2013). This perspective suggests that negative condom attitudes may moderate the association between relationship typologies and CAI among MSM and their online-met sexual partners, highlighting the joint role of individual attitudes and relational context in shaping sexual risk behavior. No studies to date have examined the moderating effect of condom use attitudes in this context. Understanding this is critical for explaining the heterogeneity in CAI across different relationship typologies. It would inform more targeted, context-specific HIV prevention strategies for MSM populations that consider both individual and partner-level determinants.

The present study

This study aimed to identify relationship typologies between MSM and their sexual partners met online in the past year by concurrently considering a range of relationship- and motivation-related characteristics using latent class analysis. In addition, this study examined whether these relationship typologies were associated with CAI and whether such associations were moderated by MSM’s attitudes towards condom use. By focusing on the interplay between relational context and individual attitudes, this study seeks to provide a more nuanced understanding of sexual risk within online sexual partnerships among MSM and to inform HIV prevention strategies that are sensitive to the diversity of partner relationships.

Methods

Study design and participants

A telephone-based, cross-sectional survey was conducted among MSM in Hong Kong from November 2023 to August 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Chinese who identified as male, (2) aged 18 or older, (3) having used the online platforms such as GSNS and social media for male sex partner seeking in the past year, and (4) having had anal intercourses with any male sexual partners whom first met online in the past year.

Data collection

In this study, MSM participants were recruited through convenience sampling via the referrals from local non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and peer networks. Potential participants were first approached through these NGOs or peers, and individuals who agreed to be contacted by the research team were included in the contact list. Personal contact information (i.e., telephone numbers) was obtained through the referring NGOs or peer networks, and only after individuals had provided prior consent to be contacted by the research team. Research assistants then telephoned these individuals to clearly explain the study purpose and procedures, the voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality arrangements. Participants were assured of anonymity and their right to withdraw at any time, and that declining participation would not affect their access to any services. For eligible participants who agreed to join this study, verbal informed consent was obtained. Structured telephone interviews were then conducted by experienced interviewers with prior experience working with MSM populations, during pre-scheduled timeslots. Each interview took around 20 min. If individuals did not respond to the initial call, up to three follow-up calls were made. Participants were assured of the anonymity and their right to withdraw at any time, and that declining participation would not affect their access to any services. As a token of appreciation, participants received a HKD $50 (∼$6.4 USD) supermarket coupon by mail after completing the survey. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the corresponding author’s affiliation (Ref no.: SBRE-23-0415).

Measures

Structured questionnaires were used to collect data at two levels: (1) MSM respondents’ socio-demographic information, HIV testing, and attitude towards condom use (Participant level), and (2) information about each of their most recent sexual partners (up to five) they met online in the past year (Partner-level).

Participant-Level

Demographics and HIV/STD test. Demographic characteristics of participants, including age, education, employment, marital status, sexual orientation, and history of HIV and STD testing were collected.

Negative Attitude Toward Condom Use was measured by a six-item scale that captured negative condom attitudes and was applied in previous studies involving MSM populations (Nakamura et al., 2011). Item examples included: “I believe that stopping to put on a condom ruins the moment” and “I believe that using condoms makes me less sexually desirable”. Response options ranged from “1= Strongly disagree” to “5= Strongly agree”, with a higher score indicating a more negative attitude towards condom use. Its Cronbach’s a was 0.910 in this study.

Partner-Level

At the sexual partner level, for each of their five most recent sexual partners met via online platforms, participants were asked to recall and answer an identical set of questions pertaining to that specific partner, including:

Age and HIV status. Partners’ age and their HIV status (negative, positive, and unknown) were asked.

Relationship Role. The nature of the relationship with each sexual partner, categorized as non-regular partner, regular partner, boyfriend (or lover), ex-boyfriend, or transactional (e.g., paid sex), was asked.

Sexual relationship duration. The length of the sexual relationship with each sexual partner was recorded.

Intimacy. Participants were asked to rate their perceived emotional closeness with each sexual partner on a 5-point Likert scale, from “1 = Not close at all” to “5 = Very close”.

Motivations for sexual engagement. These items were derived from previous literature (Bui et al., 2014; Griffin et al., 2018; Wray & Monti, 2020a) about motivations to have sex with sexual partners, including: “Seeking emotional closeness”, “Experiencing physical pleasure”, “Maintaining partner’s satisfaction”, “Affirming personal attractiveness”, “Pursuing novel sexual experiences”, “Exploring sexual competence”, “Alleviating mental distress”, “Fulfilling personal sexual needs”, and “Meeting partner’s sexual needs”. Responses were rated on a four-Likert scale, with “1= Not suitable at all” to “4= Very suitable”.

Condom use behavior. Participants were asked to rate their condom use frequency with each SP in the past year using a three-point Likert scale: “Never”, “Sometimes”, and “Every time”. Responses were rated separately for receptive and insertive roles. Participants who reported “Never” or “Sometimes” for either role were categorized as engaging in CAI.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the characteristics of participants and the relationship with their online sexual partners. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (for normally distributed variables), median with interquartile range (IQR) (for non-normally distributed variables), or frequencies and percentages (for categorical variables).

LCA was conducted to identify distinct classes of the relationships between MSM and their sexual partners based on multiple characteristics, including sexual partner’s relationship role, relationship duration, participants’ perceived intimacy and motives for sexual engagement with sexual partners. All variables were dummy coded for analysis. Specifically, partner’s relationship role was categorized as “1= Stable, including regular partner, boyfriend or lover, and ex- boyfriend” or “0=Non-stable, including non-regular partner and transactional partner”; relationship duration was classified as “1=Long-term (≥75th percentile of duration)” or “0= Not long”; intimacy was coded as “1=High intimacy (responses = “Close/very close”) or “0= Not high (responses = “Not close at all/not close/moderate”. Motives for sexual engagement were first grouped into several domains using the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), ensuring that they provided distinct contributions to the latent classes. Each motive construct was then coded as “1= High” (responses= “Suitable/very suitable”) or “0=Not high (responses= “Not suitable at all/not suitable”), respectively. Models were estimated iteratively, with two to six classes evaluated by 20 random starts and a maximum of 5000 iterations per model. Model was selected based on log-likelihood (LL), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), consistent Akaike information criteria (CAIC), with better fit indicated by higher LL and lower BIC, aBIC, and CAIC values, and theoretical interpretability, avoiding classes representing fewer than 5% of the sample (Hagenaars & McCutcheon, 2002). Participants were assigned to classes based on posterior probabilities. The LCA required complete data on all indicator variables. Therefore, only partner records with complete data on all variables included in the LCA were retained for analysis (Collins & Lanza, 2013). Missing data at the partner level referred to cases where responses to one or more LCA indicator variables were not provided and were not related to the number of partners reported by participants.

This study further examined the association between the LCA-identified relationship classes and their CAI. Given the nested nature of the data (multiple sexual partners within each participant), multi-level logistic regression models were employed to identify the predictors of CAI engagement. The models accounted for both participant-level (e.g., demographics, attitude towards condom use) and partner-level (e.g., partner’s age, HIV status, relationship typologies) factors, with a participant-level random intercept to control for clustering effects. First, each background variable was individually assessed in the univariate analysis for its association with CAI. Variables with p< 0.1 in the univariate analyses were retained for the multivariate analysis. Then, significant background variables from the univariate analysis were entered into multivariate hierarchical logistic regressions, as the covariates, to examine the influences of relationship typologies (LCA result) and attitude towards condom use on CAI, after adjusting for covariates. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were reported. In addition, the interaction term of relationship typologies and negative attitude towards condom use was further added in the multivariate analysis to examine whether the association between relationship typologies and CAI was moderated by MSM’s negative attitude towards condom use.

All the above analyses were performed using R software using “poLCA” (Linzer & Lewis, 2011) and “lme4” (Bates et al., 2015) packages, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Since referrals were made through NGOs and peer networks, the total number of individuals initially approached by referrers was not systematically recorded. The research team only received contact information for individuals who had agreed to be contacted. Among 1107 contacted by the research team, a total of 405 participants met our study criteria and completed the telephone survey and were included in the final analytic sample. As shown in Table 1, the majority were 31 to 50 years (60.0%), holding a university degree or higher (88.4%), employed full-time (81.0%), single (64.7%), and homosexual (88.1%). Majority (n=391, 96.5%) reported having ever taken HIV test, and 323 (79.8%) had ever taken STD test.

Latent classes of MSM’s relationship with their online sexual partners

The EFA result identified four key and distinct domains of the sex motives, that is (i) Relational fulfilment, including seeking emotional closeness, feeling physical pleasure, maintaining sexual partner’s satisfaction, and affirming personal attractiveness), (ii) Sexual exploration, including seeking novel sexual experiences and exploring sexual competence, (iii) Stress relief, including alleviating mental distress, and (iv) Sexual desires, including fulfilling personal and SP’s sexual needs. The relationship characteristics with partners regarding their relationship role, relationship duration, participants’ perceived intimacy and four domains of motives for sexual engagement with sexual partners were included in the LCA. After excluding partner records with missing data on variables included in the LCA, 1,012 partner records were retained for analysis, accounting for 94.8% of all eligible partner records. Given the relatively low proportion of missing partner-level data, complete-case analysis was used, and multiple imputation was not applied.

As shown in Table 3, the study compared LCA models with two through six latent to determine the optimal fit. Based on goodness-of-fit indices (BIC= 7985.14, CAIC = 8008.14, aBIC = 7912.09), parsimony, and interpretability, the three-class solution was identified as the most suitable. Although the four-class model demonstrated a slightly better fit on aBIC (7897.16 vs. 7912.09 for the three-class model), its higher CAIC value (8026.62 vs. 8008.14 for the three-class model) emphasized the superior parsimony of the three-class solution (Lin & Dayton, 1997).

Fig. 1 presents the 3-class model. In the inclusive model, 62.5% (n=633) were classified as “Sexual Desire-Oriented”, 20.8% (n=211) were classified as “Relationally Driven”, and 16.6% (n=168) were classified as the “Sexually Adventurous” relationship classes. As shown in Table 4, Sexual Desire-Oriented typology showed the least likely to have stable sexual partners (11%), long-term relationship duration with partners (19%), and reported the lowest levels of intimacy (3%) and sex motivation for relational fulfilment (13%) with their SPs among the three groups. Sexual desire appeared to be their primary motivation for engaging in sex, with 86% selected the motive. Members in the Relationally Driven typology were the most likely to have stable partners (e.g., boyfriend, ex-boyfriend, or regular partners, 74%) and long-term relationship durations (44%), and reported the highest probabilities of high intimacy (81%) among the three groups. Their primary motivations for sex included relational fulfilment (58%) and sexual desires (85%). The Sexually Adventurous typology was less likely to have stable partners than the Relationally Driven typology but more likely than the Sexual Desire-Oriented typology, with 29% showing to be stable sexual partners. They also showed a higher likelihood of having long-term relationship duration (26%) and greater intimacy (45%) compared to the Sexual Desire-Oriented class but lower than the Relationally Driven typology. This typology was motivated by relational fulfillment (86%), sexual exploration (80%), stress relief (69%), and sexual desires (100%), with sexual exploration and stress relief being especially prominent compared to the other two typologies.

Associations among relationship typologies, MSM’s attitude towards condom use, and CAI

As presented in Table 5, the univariate analyses revealed that Relationally Driven (ORu = 4.21, 95% CI: 2.27–7.82) and Sexually Adventure (ORu = 3.58, 95% CI: 1.85–6.93) typologies were significantly more likely to engage in CAI compared to those in the Sexual Desire-Oriented group (ps<.001). A more negative attitude toward condom use of MSM participants (ORu = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.24–1.41) was also positively associated with CAI engagement with their online partners. In addition, background variables, including living with, being married to, or having a male partner (ORu = 5.27, 95% CI: 2.48–11.20), and having ever taken an STD test (ORu = 3.03, 95% CI: 1.28–7.24), were positively associated with CAI engagement (ps<.05); participants who were unknown of their sexual partners’ HIV status were less likely to engage in CAI (ORu = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.30–0.84, p<.01) compared to those who knew their partners’ HIV status to be negative. Other variables, including sexual partners’ age (p=0.075) and participants’ age (p=0.083), education, employment, sexual orientation, and whether having taken a HIV test ever, were not significantly associated with their CAI engagement (ps>0.05).

Discussions

This study is among the first to identify relationship typologies between MSM and their online-met sexual partners and to examine how different typologies, together with participants’ condom-use attitude, influence CAI. It provides new evidence that CAI among MSM is structured by the relational contexts in which sexual encounters occur. The findings highlight substantial heterogeneity in how online sexual partnerships are organised and how CAI risk is enacted across these contexts. Importantly, the likelihood of CAI varied systematically across relationship types, and attitudes towards condom use was a signficant moderator. The findings highlight the need to move beyond focusing solely on individual MSM characteristics toward examining the relational and motivational contexts embedded in online-partner seeking.

Our findings indicate that 37.5% of MSM engaged in CAI with online newly met sexual partners, which aligns with previously reported patterns of risk behavior in China. A meta-analysis showed that the pooled prevalence of unprotected anal intercourse among MSM was 45% with regular partners and 34% with non-regular partners (Wu et al., 2014). Considering online-met partners are generally new and non-regular, the level of CAI observed in our study is consistent with the risk profile typically associated with casual partner contexts. Similarly, another Chinese study reported that 37.1% of MSM who used GSN apps for partner seeking engaged in CAI (Luo et al., 2023), demonstrating the comparability of our findings. Online encounters can vary widely in terms of sexual motivations and the types of relationships formed, ranging from primarily sexual interactions to more relationally oriented connections. These differences may influence sexual risk behaviors, including the likelihood of engaging in CAI.

Investigating the MSM’s distinct relationship patterns with online sexual partners considering a wide range of relationship factors and sex motivations, making an innovative contribution to the literature. Previous research examining barriers to condom use among MSM has been largely variable-centered, focusing on factors at the individual level (e.g., HIV/STI knowledge, attitudes, psychological factors), interpersonal level (e.g., condom stigma, partner influence), and environmental level (e.g., availability, accessibility, and affordability of condoms) (Hoff et al., 2016). For the first time, this study has identified three distinct classes of relationship among MSM and their online partners, considering a range of factors regarding their sex motivations and relationship characteristics. In the present study, most MSM individuals having relationships with online sexual partners categorized as (a) Sexual Desire-oriented, characterised as high level of sexual desire, moderate level of stress relief purpose, and low level of intimacy; (b) Relationally Driven, which is characterised as high level of intimacy, sexual desire and stable partner relationship; and (c) Sexually Adventurous, which is characterised as highest level of sexual desire, high level of relationship fulfilment (a sense of emotional closeness), fulfilling physical satisfaction of self and partners, and confirming one’s attractiveness.

Our finding regarding sexual desire-oriented relationship account for the highest proportion, which is aligned with prior work showing that MSM use online platforms more frequently for casual sexual encounters rather than for dating or forming romantic relationships (e.g., boyfriend) (Choi et al., 2021b; Goedel & Duncan, 2015b). Evidence also indicates that MSM tend to report higher condom use with GSNS-met partners compared with partners not met via GSNS (Holloway et al., 2015b; Knox et al., 2021b). These patterns suggest that online partner-seeking is more likely to occur within clearer relational boundaries, lower expectations of relational closeness, which may facilitate more explicit and consistent protective sexual practices. This aligns with the patterns and condom behaviors observed in the Sexual Desire-Oriented class of the present study. While some studies have found that hookup relationships are more likely to involve CAI, these studies primarily focused on young and single MSM (Bauermeister, 2015; Choi & Bauermeister, 2022). Given that younger age and being single have been consistently associated with higher CAI risk (Choi et al., 2021a; Johansson et al., 2018), it is possible that factors beyond relationship patterns contribute to the CAI risk observed in these populations.

The Relationally Driven class, representing nearly one-quarter of relationship patterns among MSM and their online sexual partners, showed the highest likelihood of engaging CAI. These individuals tended to experience stronger emotional connection, intimacy, and relational expectations with the partners. Our finding is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that higher emotional closeness, trust, or longer relationship are often associated with reduced condom use (He et al., 2018; Hoff et al., 2012; Siegler et al., 2014). In such trusted or committed relationship, partners may perceive lower HIV risk or prioritize intimacy, inhibiting condom use. Furthermore, stable partners are more likely to disclose HIV serostatus and STI diagnosis compared to casual partners (McMahan & Olmstead, 2024; Przybyla et al., 2013). Such disclosure, particularly when the partner reports being HIV-negative, can substantially lower perceived vulnerability, which in turn facilitates CAI (Truong et al., 2016). This may also be explained by the Risk Compensation Theory (Cassell et al., 2006), which posits that when individuals perceive a situation as safer or more trustworthy, they are more likely to reduce their protective behaviors and adopt greater behavioral risks to “compensate” for the perceived reduction in risks. Consistent with a risk compensation process, within the relationally driven partnerships, MSM are likely to interpret stable and longer relationship, intimacy, trust as a safe environment, leading to reduced HIV-risk perception and, consequently, increased CAI.

The Sexually Adventurous typology, although representing the smallest proportion of participants, was characterized by the highest probabilities across all types of motivations for sex, including sexual desire, sexual exploration, stress relief, and relational fulfilment. This class was also associated with elevated CAI compared to the Sexual-oriented group. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that MSM who use GSNS and report higher level of sexual sensation‑seeking were more likely to engage in CAI (Luo et al., 2023). The exploratory and affective nature of such partnerships may predispose individuals to higher engagement in CAI. They tend to be guided more by immediate emotional states or sexual arousal, which may lead them to prioritize subjective experience over safety and potentially reduce consideration of protective behaviors (Strong et al., 2005).

Another key contribution of this study is the identification of a moderating effect of condom-use attitudes within specific relational contexts. Our findings showed that negative attitudes towards condom use were associated with increased likelihood of CAI, but only within the Relationally Driven typology. This moderation pattern suggests that condom-use attitudes exert a stronger influence on CAI when online sexual encounters are embedded in an affective or relational context. Among individuals in the Relationally-driven typology, sexual behaviors are more likely to be interpreted through interpersonal communications, and negative attitudes towards condom use may therefore be more significant. Prior studies show that condom use can function as a form of relational communication, where using a condom may be perceived as an ‘interference’ with their intimacy (Golub et al., 2012). Consequently, when relationally-driven individuals hold more negative condom use attitudes, these relational interpretations may further amplify their likelihood of CAI. In contrast, the Sexual-Desire-Oriented and Sexually Adventurous groups appear to be primarily driven by sexual arousal-based or motivational factors, which might tend to override attitudinal influences on CAI.

Implications

These findings have important implications for HIV prevention. Interventions should be designed tailored to relationship profiles and psychosocial characteristics, rather than applying uniform strategies.

Specifically, for the Relationally Driven partnership, whose sexual risk is heightened by intimacy and trust with partners, programs may therefore benefit from a couples-focused approach, such as promoting joint HIV/STI testing, communication training about condom use. Additionally, enhancing positive attitudes toward condoms may help support safer practices without undermining relational closeness. Interventions can be implemented in this population to improve condom use without diminishing relational closeness through several evidence-based strategies. For example, pleasure-inclusive approaches, such as training on condom fit and feel, use of lubrication to enhance comfort, and eroticized safer-sex demonstrations, can be integrated to reduce perceptions of pleasure loss (Ford et al., 2021). Couples-based communication training can help integrate condom use into intimacy by improving communication scripts, shared decision-making, and partner-focused safety framing (Mitchell, 2014). Cognitive-behavioral approaches, including cognitive restructuring, condom-skills training, and motivational interviewing, may also be effective for fostering positive attitudes towards condom use (Parsons et al., 2018).

For the Sexually Adventurous relationship pattern, they also showed elevated CAI risk. Interventions adopt a harm-reduction and pleasure-informed approach that acknowledges MSM’s sexual exploration and sensation-seeking tendencies, including regular HIV/STI self-testing (Witzel et al., 2020), guidance on ensuring safer sex within adventurous-driven context, and delivering messages that aligns safer sex with sexual autonomy and exploration rather than framing it as restrictive.

Third, sexual Desire-Oriented subgroup exhibited comparatively lower CAI risk, likely reflecting shorter or less intimate encounters in which condom use may be more routinized. Prevention efforts could focus on reinforcing condom access and consistent use during casual encounters, supporting risk assessment skills when transitioning across partner types, and ensuring protection awareness or norms even in high-desire situations (Rodrigues, 2023).

Fourth, integrating an understanding of the relationship characteristics and sexual motivations into digital and clinical outreach can help identify high-risk MSM subgroups and deliver tailored interventions. Such an approach may improve engagement, acceptability, and effectiveness of HIV prevention strategies, supporting more context-sensitive public health efforts.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, with the widely use of online platforms for sexual partner seeking, the present study provides a novel, relationship-level perspective by examining online-met sexual partners per participant, allowing for a nuanced understanding of how partner-specific relationship characteristics influence their CAI. Second, the use of LCA enabled identification of distinct relationship profiles, capturing heterogeneity in partner-seeking motivations and patterns that would be obscured in aggregate analyses. Third, the study employed multilevel logistic regressions, accounting for the nesting of sexual partners within participants, which appropriately models the non-independence of observations and enhances the precision of effect estimates. Fourth, data collection using structured telephone interviews conducted by trained personnel will likely improve data quality, reducing missing data and misreporting.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Second, Potential recall and social desirability biases may also exist, although trained interviewers and structured telephone interviews likely mitigated some of these issues. Third, participants were recruited through convenience sampling via local NGOs and peer networks and therefore may not be representative of all MSM in Hong Kong or all MSM who seek sexual partners online. Nevertheless, this approach was considered feasible and suitable given the absence of a comprehensive sampling frame for this population and the sensitive nature of sexual behaviour research. The findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting patterns among MSM reached through the present recruitment channels, rather than the broader MSM population.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence that MSM’s relationship typologies with online-met partners meaningfully shape their engagement in CAI, and that negative condom use attitudes further heighten CAI risk for the Relationally oriented typology. These findings underscore the need to consider broader relational context, not just individual behaviors, when designing tailored interventions for different typologies.
