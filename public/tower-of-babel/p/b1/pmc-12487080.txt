Functional diversity of disorder-specific macrophages involved in various diseases
Authors: Yuichi Mitsui, Takashi Satoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMacrophages are highly plastic immune cells that adopt diverse functional states in response to the local microenvironment. The traditional M1/M2 polarization model that has long been used to describe macrophage activation is insufficient to capture the full spectrum of macrophage diversity observed in vivo. Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have revealed that macrophages exist in a continuum of transcriptional states formed by tissue-specific and disorder-specific cues. This insight has led to the recognition of disorder-specific macrophages, defined as macrophage subpopulations that emerge in response to pathological stimuli and play unique roles in disease progression. These macrophages exhibit distinct transcriptional signatures, epigenetic modifications, and functional properties shaped by their ontogeny and microenvironmental signals, arising from the reprogramming of resident macrophages or the differentiation of bone marrow–derived progenitors. Notable examples include macrophages in chronic infections (e.g., tuberculosis), immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages, lipid-associated macrophages in obesity, and disease-associated microglia in neurodegeneration. These subsets exhibit unique regulatory mechanisms, including enhancer remodeling driven by histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, CCAAT enhancer binding protein α-mediated differentiation in obesity, and Jmjd3-IRF4 axis control in allergic inflammation. Additionally, their function and fate are strongly influenced by their subtissular niche, as evidenced by crown-like structures in adipose tissue, tumor microenvironments, fibrotic lesions, and granulomas, where distinct microenvironmental cues shape macrophage behavior. Furthermore, interindividual heterogeneity in macrophage function, driven by genetic polymorphisms, is increasingly recognized, highlighting the role of host genetic background in disease susceptibility and macrophage-driven pathology. Here, we review the conceptual evolution of the disorder-specific macrophage, tracing its origins from the limited M1/M2 model to its refinement through scRNA-seq-based classification. We summarize the ontogeny, transcriptional regulation, and spatial heterogeneity of these macrophages across various disorders, emphasizing how the subtissular niche dictates functional specialization. Finally, we discuss potential therapeutic strategies targeting disorder-specific macrophage subsets, highlighting the need for integrative multi-omics approaches to refine their classification and functional characterization. Understanding the regulatory networks that govern disorder-specific macrophages will advance our knowledge of macrophage biology while facilitating the development of precision medicine for immune-related disorders.Keywords: M1/M2 polarization model, Multidimensional spectrum model, Disorder-specific macrophage, Interindividual diversity of macrophages

Background

The discovery that macrophages are phagocytes capable of engulfing foreign substances is attributed to Ilya Metchnikoff [1], who was awarded the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on their phagocytic function [2]. Over a century later, it is clear that macrophages with diverse functions are present in various bodily organs, recognizing pathogens and other foreign substances, coordinating adaptive immune responses through antigen processing and presentation, and contributing to the establishment of human diseases [3–5]. In the late 1990s, it was reported that macrophages have two distinct activation programs: “classical activation” by interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and “alternative activation” by interleukin (IL)−4 and IL-13 [6, 7]. A new classification of macrophages into M1 or M2, based on differences in arginine metabolism observed in macrophages from mice exhibiting distinct immune response profiles associated with helper T cell (Th)1 and Th2 pathways, was proposed in 2000 [8]. These findings formed the widely accepted theory that macrophages polarize into two main phenotypes: M1 (classically activated), which produces pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-12, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α upon stimulation with Th1-derived cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ), alone or in combination with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and exhibits inflammatory functions; and M2 (alternatively activated), which produces anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and exhibits anti-inflammatory functions upon stimulation with Th2-derived cytokines (e.g., IL-4 and IL-13) [9–11]. In 2004, Mantovani et al. further classified M2 macrophages into three subtypes according to the type of stimulus and immune response properties: M2a induced by IL-4 and IL-13, M2b induced by immune complexes (ICs) and Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, and M2c induced by IL-10 [12]. Thus, while the M1/M2 bipolarity theory has advanced macrophage research and has been highly reproducible in experiments performed in vitro, it is insufficient to explain the diversity and plasticity of macrophages in vivo [13–15].

Our understanding of in vivo macrophage diversity and plasticity has deepened through studies on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). TAMs were initially understood to have an M2-like phenotype that promotes tumor growth by influencing tumor invasion and metastasis through suppression of adaptive immunity and angiogenesis [16–19]. Additionally, TAMs reportedly co-express IFN-inducible chemokines associated with the M1 phenotype, including C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)9, CXCL10, and CXCL16 [20]. Duluc et al. reproduced this phenomenon in vitro, classifying it as the M2d phenotype based on M1/M2 theory [21]. While such studies indicated that TAMs are a single population with unique characteristics, several recent reports have revealed that TAMs comprise multiple subpopulations with distinct phenotypes [22–24]. Furthermore, with the recent development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology, TAM subtypes with independent functional characteristics have been identified on the basis of differences in gene expression profiles [25, 26]. Therefore, TAMs are now considered to represent a heterogeneous population of multiple functionally distinct subsets that are difficult to classify using the M1/M2 bipolarization theory [27, 28]. Multiple macrophage subtypes with different functional characteristics in vivo have been reported in other diseases, such as the Mox, Mhem, and M4 intraplaque macrophages involved in atherosclerosis [29–31]. These diverse macrophage profiles cannot be explained by the linear M1/M2 polarization model, which fails to account for the in vivo heterogeneity of macrophages.

An alternative spectral polarization model has been introduced that categorizes macrophage activation states on the basis of surface markers, gene expression profiles, and functional attributes [13, 32]. Furthermore, recent discoveries have shown that macrophages follow multiple differentiation pathways and undergo transcriptional and epigenetic modifications in response to tissue-specific signals, further altering their characteristics under disorder-specific stimuli. For example, resident tissue macrophages (RTMs) of the peritoneal cavity are broadly classified into large peritoneal macrophages derived from yolk sacs and small peritoneal macrophages derived from monocytes. Reportedly, tissue-specific retinoic acid stimulates large peritoneal macrophages to induce the expression of the transcription factor GATA-binding protein 6 (GATA6), which contributes to immunoglobulin A production through peritoneal B-1 cells [33, 34]. Resident adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) are classified into lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 (LYVE1)+ ATMs of embryonic origin (which are replaced by monocyte-derived ones in adults) and monocyte-derived ATMs [35]. In addition to these subtypes, a disorder-specific subtype of lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs) positive for triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is reportedly recruited in the obese state, contributing to the pathogenesis of the disease [35, 36]. Based on these findings, a multidimensional model has been proposed that considers the one-dimensional activation state of macrophages along with their differentiation pathways and the diverse signals that they receive in a tissue- or disorder-specific manner [37].

Here, we review the concept of disorder-specific macrophages based on the multidimensional theory, examining studies that show the importance of macrophage subtypes in human diseases, particularly infectious diseases, cancers, metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative disorders, fibrosis, and immune-mediated diseases, in which significant progress has been made within this conceptual framework.

Concept of disorder-specific macrophages

The notion of disorder-specific macrophages has emerged as an essential framework for understanding how macrophage populations adapt to distinct pathological environments beyond the conventional M1/M2 classification [36, 38]. Across all organs in the body, RTMs generally have two major developmental origins, but their functional characteristics are largely shaped by the local environment. For example, many RTMs, such as microglia and Kupffer cells, arise from embryonic progenitors, including yolk sac–derived erythromyeloid precursors [39–41]. In contrast, RTMs in tissues with high cellular turnover, such as the gut and spleen, can be continuously replenished by adult bone marrow–derived monocytes [39–41]. Notably, even bone marrow–derived macrophages can acquire tissue-specific enhancer landscapes when exposed to the local microenvironment, suggesting that niche-driven transcriptional programming can override ontogenetic differences [42, 43]. However, it remains unclear whether monocyte-derived RTMs can fully recapitulate the functions of their embryonic counterparts. This dual contribution from embryonic and adult origins adds an additional layer of complexity to macrophage biology in both health and disease. What is known, however, is that macrophages originally residing in healthy tissues—regardless of their embryonic or monocyte-derived origin—can be reprogrammed by pathological stimuli and transformed into new disorder-specific subtypes. At the same time, progenitors located outside the affected area perceive disorder-specific signals and subsequently accumulate at the site of pathology, further diversifying the macrophage populations [36, 44]. These disorder-specific macrophages exhibit distinct gene expression profiles and can often be identified through characteristic molecular markers; for example, TREM2+ macrophages have been highlighted as a representative subset. TREM2, a receptor expressed on innate immune cells, binds various ligands, including phospholipids of apoptotic cells and apolipoproteins, transmitting downstream signals through its interaction with DNAX-activation protein 12 (DAP12) [45, 46]. Importantly, TREM2+ macrophages do not represent a single entity, but a family of related subsets that emerge in distinct disease contexts. The TREM2+ macrophage subtype was first identified in Alzheimer’s disease (disease-associated microglial cells, DAMs) and has since been reported in other conditions, including obesity (LAMs), non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH; hepatic LAMs), and liver cirrhosis (scar-associated macrophages, SAMs) [36, 47–49]. Although these macrophage subsets share a common TREM2-dependent transcriptional program, they differ in developmental origin, molecular regulators, and functional roles, reinforcing the idea that macrophage identity is dynamically shaped by local tissue cues [50].

As outlined above, the development and differentiation of disorder-specific macrophages are currently understood to involve multiple factors, including their origin, migratory cues from progenitors (if derived from bone marrow), disorder-specific signals from different organ microenvironments, master regulators, lineage-determining factors, and distinct gene expression signatures that define their identity (Fig. 1). For example, DAMs in the brain originate from yolk sac–derived microglia and exhibit phagocytic and neuroprotective features, whereas LAMs and SAMs are mostly monocyte-derived and are involved in lipid metabolism and fibrosis, respectively [36, 47, 49]. These functional differences arise not only from distinct tissue-derived signals but also from variations in transcriptional regulators and epigenetic modifications. For example, Jumonji domain-containing protein 3 (Jmjd3)-mediated H3K27 demethylation plays a crucial role in interferon regulatory factor (IRF)4-driven macrophage differentiation in allergic diseases. By contrast, macrophages involved in obesity and metabolic dysfunction follow a tribbles pseudokinase 1 (Trib1)- constitutive photomorphogenic 1 (COP1)-CCAAT enhancer-binding protein α (C/EBPα)-dependent transcriptional program, independently of the Jmjd3–IRF4 axis that governs macrophage differentiation in allergic diseases [51, 52]. These findings suggest that disorder-specific macrophages operate under distinct gene regulatory networks tailored to unique pathological stimuli, with epigenetic modifications dynamically controlling macrophage plasticity. This underscores the need for comprehensive transcriptional mapping across various pathologies. In addition to these regulatory layers, disorder-specific macrophages interact closely with other immune and stromal cells, shaping and being shaped by the local immune environment. For example, SAMs interact with activated hepatic stellate cells, promoting fibrosis, while LAMs influence lipid metabolism and systemic insulin sensitivity through their interactions with adipocytes and other immune cells [36, 49]. Such interactions are pivotal for the maintenance or disruption of tissue homeostasis.

Model of the disorder-specific macrophage concept. Visual overview of the complexity of macrophage plasticity and the intricate interplay among their developmental origins, microenvironmental signals, transcriptional regulation, and disorder-specific adaptation, highlighting the mechanisms that govern macrophage heterogeneity in pathological contexts. Macrophages originate from yolk sac − derived or fetal monocyte-derived progenitors that give rise to embryonic-derived RTMs, or from bone marrow–derived monocytes that can differentiate into monocyte-derived RTMs under certain conditions (1). These macrophages settle in different tissues and maintain homeostasis, but their identity is shaped by signals from the local microenvironment. Within specific subtissular niches, RTMs respond to various cues, including tissue-specific signals that sustain homeostasis, disorder-specific signals that induce differentiation and activation, and additional signals that recruit monocytes to inflamed or damaged sites (2). Upon exposure to these signals, macrophages undergo transcriptional shifts orchestrated by master regulators and epigenetic modifications (e.g., PPARγ in LAMs and ATF3/LXRs in SAMs), which help define their functional identity (3). Through these mechanisms, macrophages acquire disorder-specific phenotypes characterized by distinct gene expression profiles, allowing adaptation to pathological microenvironments (4). These newly differentiated, specialized macrophages are identifiable by unique surface markers, including TREM2 positivity of LAMs in obesity, SAMs in liver fibrosis and NASH, and DAMs in neurodegeneration (5). Despite sharing some transcriptional programs, these macrophages display functional divergence based on subtissular niche, ultimately shaping their roles in lipid metabolism, tissue remodeling, or inflammation

Interestingly, LAMs and CD11c+ inflammatory ATMs, which share the same crown-like structure (CLS) niche in obese adipose tissue, represent a clear example of how different macrophage subsets coexist in a given microenvironment while executing divergent functions. While LAMs specialize in lipid scavenging and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, CD11c+ ATMs promote chronic inflammation and insulin resistance [53]. Despite coexisting within the same subtissular niche, these populations exhibit distinct gene expression signatures, reflecting their specialized roles in either tissue protection or inflammation-driven metabolic dysfunction. This suggests that macrophage diversity is not solely dictated by functional adaptation to disease-specific microenvironments, but may also be shaped by intrinsic properties of each subset, such as developmental origin and epigenetic regulation. [50]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which these factors contribute to macrophage heterogeneity are still not fully understood, and further elucidation is expected through future studies.

In summary, disorder-specific macrophages arise through a combination of tissue-derived signals, progenitor recruitment, subtissular niche specialization, and transcriptional regulation, ultimately acquiring unique gene expression profiles that define their pathological contributions (Fig. 1). Their exact differentiation mechanisms remain incompletely understood, but we anticipate that future advances in single-cell multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics will enable the construction of a comprehensive macrophage atlas that delineates their roles in health and disease. Understanding these regulatory mechanisms will enhance our fundamental knowledge of macrophage biology while facilitating the development of novel therapeutics targeting disorder-specific macrophage functions.

Infectious disease-associated macrophages

Pulmonary macrophages represent among the most intensively studied tissue macrophage systems, particularly in the field of infectious diseases, due to the lung’s continuous exposure to airborne pathogens. In the steady state, alveolar macrophages (AMs)—which are largely derived from embryonic progenitors—reside within the alveolar lumen and maintain homeostasis by clearing inhaled particles and modulating immune responses [54]. In contrast, interstitial macrophages (IMs) inhabit the lung parenchyma and consist of a heterogeneous population that includes both long-lived, self-renewing cells and monocyte-derived populations recruited during inflammation or infection [50, 55]. These two populations differ in origin, localization, surface markers, and functional properties, with AMs being more tolerogenic and IMs playing more active roles in immune surveillance and inflammation [56]. Given the lung’s vulnerability to microbial invasion, pulmonary macrophages are rapidly activated in infectious settings and exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity in response to local cues. Using advanced techniques such as flow cytometry and scRNA-seq, recent studies have highlighted the remarkable heterogeneity of pulmonary macrophages, identifying multiple disorder-specific macrophage subtypes across various infectious lung diseases [54].

In Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, circulating monocytes are recruited to the lungs, where they differentiate into IMs and increase in number. Among them, the CD11c+ IM subtype has been shown to expand progressively as the infection advances, serving as a crucial niche for long-term Mtb survival [57–59]. According to a study by Lee et al., this cell population increasingly expresses CD14, CD38, and ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 1 (ABCA1) as the infection progresses, enhancing its favorability as an Mtb reservoir. In fact, as the infection advances, ~ 75% of CD11c+ IMs become infected with Mtb, suggesting that they play a role in Mtb persistence [57]. Paradoxically, CD11c+ IMs—despite displaying a robust interferon-γ signature and elevated nitric oxide synthetase-2 (NOS2) activity indicative of T-cell engagement—serve as key reservoirs for Mtb. This unexpected immune evasion has positioned them as compelling targets for therapeutic strategies aimed at controlling latent Mtb infection [57].

Similar to tuberculosis, macrophages involved in viral infections can also display disorder-specific properties. Recently, scRNA-seq analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) patients identified a disorder-specific subtype of CD163+ and legumain (LGMN)+ macrophages that increases from the early stages of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) to become the predominant population as the disease progresses [60]. Within the same study, these CD163+ LGMN+ macrophages were shown to originate from ficolin-M (FCN1)+ inflammatory monocytes, which are actively recruited to the lung via chemokines such as C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2). Transcription factor enrichment analysis predicted PU.1 as a key regulator in FCN1+ monocytes, and identified transcription factor EC (TFEC) and glycosylated lysosomal membrane protein (GLMP) as transcriptional regulators potentially associated with CD163+ LGMN+ macrophages. This macrophage subset exhibits high expression of fibrosis-promoting genes, including those encoding secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β, and CCL18, and shares a transcriptional profile with macrophages involved in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Through interactions with fibroblasts, these macrophages activate signaling pathways mediated by fibrosis-promoting factors such as SPP1, TGF-β and fibroblast growth factor, contributing to fibrotic lesion formation in ARDS.

Collectively, infectious disease–associated macrophages are shaped through the dynamic interplay of monocyte recruitment, transcriptional reprogramming at the site of infection, and crosstalk with surrounding stromal cells such as fibroblasts. Recognizing these axes is essential for interpreting macrophage function and heterogeneity in infection and inflammation.

TAMs

TAMs are a key component of the tumor microenvironment (TME), traditionally recognized as immunosuppressive macrophages that support tumor progression. This classical view posits that TAMs are primarily derived from bone marrow–derived monocytes that infiltrate tumors and differentiate under the influence of tumor-derived factors. Based on this understanding, Duluc et al. have proposed that TAMs be considered M2d-type macrophages that are stimulated by IL-6 and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), and secrete low levels of IL-12, high levels of IL-10 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [21]. However, recent scRNA-seq studies have challenged this simplified model, revealing extensive heterogeneity within TAM populations and identifying multiple subtypes with distinct immunosuppressive roles [26, 61, 62]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests ontogenic diversity among TAMs, with certain subsets originating from tissue-resident macrophages—such as Kupffer cells in liver cancer and alveolar macrophages in lung cancer—rather than exclusively from monocyte-derived precursors [63]. For example, TREM2+ TAMs represent a distinct macrophage subset found across multiple cancer types, characterized by a unique transcriptional and functional profile that differentiates them from conventional TAMs [62, 64]. These cells are thought to originate from C–C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2)+ TREM2+ monocytes recruited under inflammatory conditions within the tumor microenvironment, rather than from tissue-resident macrophages, highlighting their distinct ontogeny and functional specialization [64, 65]. TREM2+ TAMs share some features with M2d macrophages, but exhibit high metabolic activity and a transcriptional signature associated with lipid metabolism, further supporting their role in promoting an immunosuppressive niche​. TREM2+ TAMs are characterized by high expression of arginase-1 (Arg1), a key enzyme that depletes extracellular arginine, thereby suppressing T-cell proliferation and activation [64]. These cells also express high levels of markers associated with immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic macrophage subsets, namely mannose receptor C-type 1 (MRC1), C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), and CD81 [62]. Functionally, TREM2+ TAMs have been shown to correlate with CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade therapies targeting anti-programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1), representing a critical barrier to effective immunotherapy [62, 66]​. Concurrently, scRNA-seq analysis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) identified folate receptor 2 (FOLR2)+ TAMs with a gene expression profile similar to FOLR2+ fetal liver macrophages [67]. These FOLR2+ TAMs are suggested to originate from RTMs, rather than bone marrow–derived monocytes, as indicated by their transcriptional resemblance to fetal-derived liver RTMs [63]. FOLR2+ TAMs may represent a subtype induced by plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein (PLVAP)+ onco-fetal endothelial cells, which reappear in HCC and trigger Notch signaling-mediated reprogramming of these macrophages [67]. Additionally, FOLR2+ TAMs express CXCL12, CXCL16, and CD86, and are suggested to interact with regulatory T cells (Tregs) at the tumor site, contributing to the formation of an immunosuppressive TME through immune inhibitory signals (e.g., CD86-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 [CTLA4]) [67]. These TAMs have also been identified in lung adenocarcinoma, where they may contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME by inducing dendritic cells to secrete chemokines such as CCL17, CCL19, and CCL22, thereby promoting the recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [68].

Despite significant progress in characterizing and classifying TAM heterogeneity, several questions remain unanswered. One major challenge lies in determining the ontogeny, or precise developmental origin, of different TAM subtypes. Understanding how these lineages contribute to specific TAM subsets is crucial for deciphering their plasticity and therapeutic potential. Additionally, the master regulators governing TAM differentiation and function have yet to be fully characterized. While TREM2+ TAMs are potentially induced by human lipids and by the local presence of granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and CSF-1, which are abundant in tumor tissues, their transcriptional and epigenetic regulation is still not comprehensively understood [62, 69]. Future research integrating lineage tracing, single-cell multi-omics, and spatial transcriptomics will be critical to refining the TAM classification and advancing precision macrophage-targeted therapies to overcome tumor immune suppression.

Metabolic disorder-related macrophages

Atherosclerosis

Macrophage subsets specific to atherosclerotic plaques, such as Mox, Mhem, and M4, have been reported in the context of atherosclerosis. These subsets, all derived from monocytes, exhibit distinct transcriptional programs and functional responses shaped by unique microenvironmental cues within atherosclerotic plaques, thereby differentially influencing plaque development, stability, and associated inflammation. For example, Mox macrophages, which develop in response to oxidative stress, are regulated by the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway and are induced by oxidized phospholipids in atherosclerotic lesions. This leads to the upregulation of redox-regulatory genes, such as heme oxygenase-1 (Hmox1), sulforedoxin-1 (Srxn1), and thioredoxin reductase-1 (Txnrd1), while also reducing the capacity for phagocytosis and chemotaxis, which may contribute to the persistence of inflammation in atherosclerotic plaques [29]. In contrast, Mhem macrophages reportedly differentiate in response to intraplaque hemorrhage, particularly upon exposure to hemoglobin–haptoglobin complexes. Activation of ATF1 in these macrophages induces the expression of HMOX1 and LXR-β, facilitating iron handling and cholesterol efflux. The resulting mitigation of oxidative stress and foam cell formation supports a protective role in plaque stability ​[30]. Induced by the platelet-derived chemokine CXCL4, M4 macrophages have been described as a pro-inflammatory subset characterized by the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-7 (MMP7) and S100 calcium-binding protein A8 (S100A8). Both of these molecules contribute to ECM degradation and vascular remodeling, potentially leading to plaque destabilization and an increased risk of rupture [31].​ In summary, while Mox macrophages may perpetuate chronic inflammation through impaired phagocytic function, Mhem macrophages are likely to exert an atheroprotective effect via regulation of iron metabolism and cholesterol homeostasis, whereas M4 macrophages appear to be involved in exacerbating plaque vulnerability. These findings highlight the diverse and complex roles of macrophage subsets in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

Obesity

ATMs are key immune cells in the regulation of metabolic homeostasis and inflammatory responses within adipose tissue, constituting ~ 5%–10% of the stromal vascular fraction in lean adipose tissue but accounting for up to 50% in severe obesity [70]. The role of ATMs was initially interpreted through the M1/M2 polarization model, in which obesity was linked to a shift toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, whereas ATMs in lean individuals were characterized by an anti-inflammatory M2-like profile [71]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that this dichotomy fails to accurately capture the diversity and functional complexity of ATMs [72]. Notably, metabolic activation of ATMs, particularly in obesity, has been shown to generate a phenotype distinct from classical M1 activation, in which peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) and sequestosome 1 (p62/SQSTM1) regulate lipid metabolism and inflammation independently [72]. The resulting metabolically activated macrophage phenotype is characterized by upregulation of lipid-handling genes, such as ABCA1, CD36, and perilipin-2 (PLIN2), rather than the classical inflammatory markers seen in bacterial infections [72]. Furthermore, recent research has led to the classification of ATMs into two or three major subtypes based on the expression of molecular markers including CD11c, CX3CR1, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II, T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 4 (Tim-4), and LYVE1. This has revealed distinct subpopulations that are influenced by their subtissular niche, which refers to the specialized microenvironments where macrophages reside within adipose tissues [50, 73, 74]. In lean mice, two distinct ATM populations have been identified: perivascular Tim-4+ LYVE1+ ATMs and perineural CX3CR1+ MHC class II+ ATMs, both of which are primarily of embryonic origin but are gradually replenished by circulating monocytes during adulthood [35, 50]. These niches provide unique signals that shape ATM function, with perivascular Tim-4+ LYVE1+ ATMs engaging in tissue remodeling and lipid homeostasis regulation while perineural CX3CR1+ MHC class II+ ATMs regulate neuronal signaling and angiogenesis [50]. In contrast, obese mice exhibit the substantial accumulation of monocyte-derived, CLS-associated CD11c+ ATMs, which localize to CLS and adopt an inflammatory phenotype in response to free fatty acids and necrotic adipocytes [35].

The advent of scRNA-seq has enabled the identification of a novel subset of CD11c+ ATMs called LAMs, which are functionally distinct from the classical inflammatory CD11c+ ATMs. Marked by the expression of TREM2, CD9, and glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), LAMs are primarily involved in lipid handling and ECM remodeling [36]. Fate-mapping and transcriptomic analyses have revealed that LAMs originate from circulating monocytes rather than from tissue-resident macrophage pools, and their differentiation is driven by cues within the obese adipose microenvironment. LAMs are specifically localized within a defined CLS subtissular niche of obese adipose tissue, where they function to clear lipids from necrotic adipocytes [36, 75]. Unlike classical CD11c+ inflammatory ATMs, which also accumulate in the CLS niche and express pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, LAMs exhibit a transcriptional program enriched in lipid metabolism genes and are regulated by TREM2 signaling, which facilitates lipid uptake and protects against excessive inflammation [36]. Despite sharing the same niche, LAMs and CD11c+ inflammatory ATMs display functionally divergent roles, with LAMs promoting lipid scavenging and ECM remodeling while CD11c+ inflammatory ATMs drive chronic inflammation and insulin resistance [53].

Although the signals that recruit monocytes and induce their differentiation into LAMs are not yet fully understood, several candidate factors have been reported. In murine epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), loss of the miR-23–27–24 cluster has been shown to impair LAM proliferation due to reduced suppression of Eif4ebp2, a negative regulator of translation [76]. In addition, in brown adipose tissue (BAT), metabolically stressed adipocytes release extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing oxidatively damaged mitochondrial and lipid contents, which induce LAM differentiation through CD36-dependent uptake [77]. In human obese adipose tissue, proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IL-12A, the Jun–Fos signaling pathway, and hypoxia-related factors including hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT), and endothelial PAS domain protein 1 (EPAS1) have been predicted—based on RNA-velocity and ingenuity pathway analyses—to act as upstream regulators of LAM differentiation [78].

Notably, similar TREM2+ lipid-associated macrophages have also been identified in other disease settings, including atherosclerotic plaques (referred to as plaque LAMs) and steatotic liver (hepatic LAMs), where they exhibit comparable roles in lipid metabolism and matrix remodeling, suggesting a conserved program of macrophage adaptation to lipid-rich, damaged tissue environments [79–81]. These findings highlight the evolving complexity of ATM biology and suggest that targeting LAM-associated pathways, including TREM2 and PPARγ modulation, may provide new therapeutic strategies for obesity-induced inflammation and insulin resistance [36, 53]. Understanding the interplay of ATM subsets, subtissular niches, and metabolic disease progression will be crucial for developing precision medicine approaches to treat obesity and its complications.

Neurodegenerative disorder-associated macrophages

In neurodegenerative disorders, scRNA-seq analysis has identified three intriguing disorder-specific macrophage populations: DAMs, disease-inflammatory macrophages (DIMs), and senescence-associated macrophages [47, 82–84]. These populations differ in their ontogeny, activation cues, functional roles, and temporal dynamics within the central nervous system (CNS). DAMs, first characterized by Keren-Shaul et al. (2017), represent a subset of microglia with a distinct transcriptional profile that is activated in response to pathological stimuli, such as amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation in Alzheimer's disease (AD) [47, 85–87]. In contrast, DIMs are monocyte-derived macrophages that infiltrate the CNS under pathological conditions, exhibiting a robust pro-inflammatory phenotype [82]. Recently, senescence-associated macrophages have been identified as a third subset, distinguished by their expression of p16Ink4a and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SAβG), and association with chronic sterile inflammation and aging [83, 84].

DAMs originate from yolk sac–derived microglia that populate the brain early in development and maintain their presence through self-renewal [82]. Their activation follows a two-step process: an initial TREM2-independent phase characterized by the downregulation of homeostatic microglial genes (e.g., P2ry12, Tmem119), followed by a TREM2-dependent phase involving upregulation of genes related to lipid metabolism and phagocytosis (e.g., Lpl, Cst7) [47, 88, 89]. This sequential activation enhances the ability of DAMs to clear neurotoxic aggregates and promote tissue repair. Although DAMs are initially neuroprotective, playing a crucial role in Aβ clearance and tissue repair, their prolonged activation can lead to chronic neuroinflammation [90–92]. In the early stages of AD, DAMs help remove toxic aggregates and maintain CNS homeostasis [92, 93]. However, as the disease progresses, activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain, leucine-rich repeat, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome has been suggested to contribute to the dysfunction of DAMs [91]. This, in turn, may promote the release of IL-1β and facilitate Aβ plaque deposition, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation and neuronal damage. Furthermore, in tauopathy models, DAMs have been reported to exhibit spatial proximity and potential ligand–receptor interactions with CD8+ T cells, forming immune hubs within the brain parenchyma. This interaction appears to be apolipoprotein E (ApoE)–dependent and is associated with enhanced inflammatory chemokine expression and progressive neurodegeneration. These findings suggest that DAMs not only respond to pathological stimuli but may also act as antigen-presenting cells, orchestrating CD8+ T-cell recruitment and activation within diseased CNS environments [94]. Although DAMs were initially identified in mouse models of AD, recent studies, including one describing the construction of the Human Microglia Atlas, have demonstrated that similar DAM-like subsets exist in human brain tissue [95]. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and scRNA-seq analyses have revealed transcriptional similarities between mouse and human DAMs, particularly among genes related to lipid metabolism and phagocytosis. These findings suggest that DAM-like microglia are conserved across species, underscoring the importance of translating insights from animal models to human neurodegenerative conditions.

In contrast, DIMs—defined by their expression of CD83—are derived from circulating monocytes that infiltrate the CNS in response to neurodegenerative conditions and aging. Unlike DAMs, DIM activation does not rely on TREM2 but rather on inflammatory signals within the CNS [82]. These cells express high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and contribute to sustained neuroinflammation, exacerbating neuronal damage in diseases such as AD and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [82]. In contrast to DAMs, which predominantly localize to the cortical subtissular niche, DIMs have been reported to accumulate in distinct subtissular niches such as the hippocampus and basolateral amygdala in mouse models [47, 82]. This spatial segregation suggests that DAMs and DIMs occupy non-overlapping niches within the brain parenchyma. Notably, while DIMs can be found near Aβ plaques, their presence is limited to regions with lower plaque density; in areas with high Aβ aggregation, microglia such as DAMs are more likely to engage with the plaques [82]. These findings highlight that DIMs preferentially localize to subtissular niches distinct from those of DAMs, potentially reflecting their unique ontogeny and functional roles in neuroinflammation.

Senescence-associated macrophages, meanwhile, accumulate in aged tissues, including the CNS, and are implicated in age-related inflammation, also known as "inflammaging." These cells express p16Ink4a, a key marker of cellular senescence, and exhibit increased SAβG activity, which has traditionally been used to identify senescent cells [83, 84].

Several key distinctions exist among these macrophage subsets. DAMs and DIMs originate from resident microglia and peripheral monocytes, respectively, while senescence-associated macrophages appear to be a senescent subpopulation that develops over time in response to aging and stress-related signals. Functionally, DAMs have a dual role in neurodegeneration, initially protective but potentially harmful with chronic activation, whereas DIMs primarily drive neuroinflammation and exacerbate tissue damage. Senescence-associated macrophages contribute to chronic inflammation by sustaining low-level immune activation over time, influencing multiple age-related pathologies, including neurodegeneration [82].

Deepening our understanding of the distinct roles of these macrophage subsets in neurodegenerative disorders could open new avenues for therapeutic strategies. Enhancing DAM function while preventing chronic activation could be beneficial for AD treatment. Meanwhile, limiting DIM infiltration or suppressing their inflammatory response may help mitigate ALS and other neuroinflammatory conditions. Targeting senescence-associated macrophages may require different strategies, such as selective elimination of senescent cells or modulation of the SASP to reduce chronic inflammation [83, 84].

Fibrosis-associated macrophages

In fibrosis, macrophages play a central role in regulating ECM remodeling and fibrosis progression [96–98]. Recent studies have identified distinct fibrosis-associated macrophage subsets, including SAMs, hepatic LAMs, and segregated-nucleus-containing atypical monocytes (SatM) [44, 49, 81, 99, 100]. These macrophage subsets are not homogeneous but instead represent distinct functional states derived from different progenitors in response to specific microenvironmental cues. SAMs and hepatic LAMs are monocyte-derived macrophages that emerge in response to chronic inflammation and tissue injury, whereas SatM arise from granulocyte/macrophage progenitors and exhibit a unique differentiation pathway under fibrotic conditions.

Initially, SAMs were reported as a TREM2+ CD9+ macrophage population localized within the fibrotic niche of cirrhotic livers, where these cells promote hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and excessive ECM deposition [49]. Unlike Kupffer cells, SAMs originate from circulating monocytes and exhibit a transcriptional profile marked by high expression of TNF ligand superfamily member 12 (TNFSF12), SPP1, and Galectin-3 (LGALS3), all of which are involved in fibroblast activation and fibrogenesis [49]. Hepatic LAMs have been identified as a distinct fibrosis subset associated with NASH, a condition now referred to as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [81, 99, 100]. Hepatic LAMs are characterized by high expression of TREM2, GPNMB, and CD9, and are strongly associated with dysfunctional lipid metabolism and chronic liver inflammation [81, 99, 100]. While hepatic LAMs are primarily linked to MASH pathology, their functions and key markers (e.g., TREM2 and CD9) overlap with those of SAMs in cirrhosis, suggesting that these macrophage subsets may represent different stages along a continuum from metabolic dysfunction to advanced fibrosis [49].

Although SAMs are often considered drivers of fibrotic progression, studies in liver injury models have demonstrated that SAMs also play an active role in fibrotic regression [101]. In the early recovery phases, SAMs significantly decline in number, but do not disappear entirely [101]. Residual SAMs remain localized within fibrotic areas, particularly in perisinusoidal regions, where they continue to influence tissue remodeling [101]. Importantly, the depletion of SAMs during recovery impairs the resolution of fibrosis, resulting in persistent collagen accumulation and delayed scar degradation [81]. These findings suggest that, while SAMs contribute to fibrosis development, they are also required for its resolution. Their role transitions from a fibrogenic phenotype (expressing TGF-β1 and TNF-α) in active disease to a pro-resolution phenotype characterized by decreased inflammatory gene expression and an enhanced capacity to mediate ECM degradation and HSC inactivation during recovery [81, 101]. Subsequent research demonstrated that SPP1+ macrophages, functionally similar to SAMs, are present in fibrotic tissues across multiple organs, including the lungs, heart, kidneys, skin, and uterus, leading to the broader definition of SAMs as a pan-fibrotic macrophage population [96, 102–104]. Originally identified in hepatic fibrosis, SAM-like macrophages have since been recognized as a conserved population in fibrotic lesions of diverse organs, highlighting their shared role in tissue scarring and reinforcing fibrosis-associated macrophages as central drivers of pathological ECM remodeling [49, 96, 105].

While SAMs are all involved in promoting fibrosis, hepatic LAMs have emerged as a phenotypically similar but functionally distinct subset of hepatic macrophages [81, 99, 100]. Hepatic LAMs, originally described in MASH, also express TREM2, CD9, and GPNMB, but differ from SAMs in their spatial distribution, functional roles, and disease phase specificity [48, 49, 81, 106]. Rather than concentrating within fibrotic scars, hepatic LAMs primarily localize around steatotic hepatocytes, where they form hepatic CLSs [81, 100]. Functionally, hepatic LAMs are involved in lipid handling, phagocytosis of lipid-laden debris, and the modulation of local inflammation [75, 81]. They exhibit a gene expression profile enriched for lipid metabolism-related genes, such as Cd36, Lpl, and Fabp4, as well as upregulation of genes encoding matrix-degrading enzymes, including Mmp9, Mmp12, and Mmp14 [75, 81]. Functionally, hepatic LAMs are responsible for the efferocytosis of lipid-laden apoptotic hepatocytes, thereby maintaining immune homeostasis in the fatty liver [107]. TREM2 expression is induced by sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) secreted from hepatocytes; however, prolonged hypernutrition elevates hepatic TNF-β and IL-1α levels, which promote disintegrin and metalloprotease domain 17 (ADAM17)-dependent TREM2 shedding [107]. Loss of TREM2 impairs efferocytosis, leading to excessive accumulation of apoptotic hepatocytes and a vicious cycle of cytokine release, ultimately inducing chronic liver inflammation and progression from simple steatosis to MASH [107].

From an epigenetic perspective, a recent study has identified the long-chain saturated fatty acid–Egr2 axis as a key driver of hepatic LAM differentiation, whereby Egr2 promotes the transcriptional programming of lipid-handling in infiltrating monocytes [108]. In addition, hepatic macrophages undergoing transformation in MASH exhibit histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) modification at the enhancer regions of hepatic LAM/SAM-associated genes. This modification promotes the coordinated binding of activating transcription factor (ATF)3 and liver X receptors (LXRs), thus facilitating their transcriptional shift towards a hepatic LAM/SAM-associated phenotype [106]. However, it remains unclear whether hepatic LAMs transition into pathogenic SAMs under certain conditions or whether these subsets represent entirely distinct macrophage lineages with separate differentiation pathways. Further research is warranted to elucidate the ontogeny and fate of these functionally divergent macrophage populations.

SatM, another subset of fibrosis-associated monocytes/macrophages that have been identified in pulmonary fibrosis and liver cirrhosis, are characterized by a distinct granulocyte-like morphology with high expression of carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 1 (​Ceacam1) and macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (Msr1) [44]. Unlike SAMs, SatM originate from granulocyte/macrophage progenitors rather than monocyte-derived macrophages, relying on C/EBPβ for differentiation. SatM accumulate in fibrotic lesions as fibrosis progresses, and their adoptive transfer in murine models has been shown to exacerbate fibrotic pathology [44]. While SatM do not directly produce TGF-β or SPP1, they contribute to fibrosis by secreting TNF-α, which in turn stimulates fibroblasts to produce SPP1, potentially contributing to fibroblast activation and tissue scarring [44]. Recent studies have further elucidated that SatM recruitment is driven by the chemokine Cxcl12, which is secreted by apoptotic non-hematopoietic cells in the fibrotic microenvironment [109]. The dysregulated expression of RNA binding protein 7 (Rbm7) in epithelial cells has been shown to promote apoptosis through nuclear degradation of nuclear-paraspeckle assembly transcript 1 (NEAT1), subsequently increasing CXCL12 production and recruiting SatM to pre-fibrotic regions, initiating fibrosis [109].

Understanding these macrophage subsets and their roles in fibrosis has significant therapeutic implications, as targeting fibrosis-associated macrophages may offer novel strategies for modulating disease progression. Potential approaches include blocking macrophage recruitment via chemokine–receptor signaling (e.g., CCL2–CCR2 or CXCL12–C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 [CXCR4]), modulating macrophage polarization by targeting transcriptional and epigenetic regulators such as Egr2, ATF3, and LXRs, and inhibiting key fibrogenic pathways such as TREM2, TNFSF12, and SPP1 to reduce fibroblast activation and ECM deposition [96]. The evolving understanding of SAMs, particularly the discovery of their presence in multiple fibrotic organs beyond the liver, highlights the complexity of fibrosis-associated macrophages and their pivotal role in disease progression. By further investigating the heterogeneity and functional specialization of these macrophages, future studies may develop targeted interventions to mitigate fibrosis while preserving essential immune functions.

Immune-mediated disease-related macrophages

Rheumatoid arthritis

Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics have revealed a highly heterogeneous landscape of synovial tissue macrophages (STMs), with distinct subsets showing divergent ontogeny, localization, and functional specialization in health, active disease, and remission stages of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Tissue-resident MER proto-oncogene, tyrosine kinase (MerTK)+ CD206+ synovial tissue macrophages represent the predominant macrophage subtype in healthy human and mouse synovium and are composed of at least two major subpopulations [110, 111]. The first is a TREM2+ cluster, which forms a barrier-like lining in the synovial membrane. This subset represents the human counterpart of the CD64+ MHC class II- CX3CR1+ macrophages described in mice and is characterized by the expression of tight junction molecules such as Zona occludens 1 (ZO-1) and Claudin-5 [111, 112]. These cells are locally self-renewing, derived from embryonic precursors, and provide a structural and immunological barrier that regulates neutrophil infiltration and maintains immune quiescence [111]. The second is a FOLR2+ LYVE1+ cluster (resistin-like molecule α [RELMα]+ MHC class II+ CX3CR1- in mice), which predominantly resides in the sublining layer. This population is suggested to be involved in ECM remodeling, heme metabolism, and vascular homeostasis [110, 111, 113].

During RA progression, a key immunological shift involves the expansion of at least three distinct CD48+ STMs with a MerTK- CD206- phenotype, which infiltrate the synovium and replace the protective TREM2+ lining macrophages [110]. These CD48+ STMs are monocyte-derived and comprise transcriptionally distinct subsets with specialized pathogenic functions. The CD48+ S100A12+ subset plays a key role in inflammatory amplification within the synovium through IL-1β release, activating monocytes and fibroblasts via alarmins such as S100A8, S100A9, and S100A12, and attracting neutrophils via CXCL8 [110, 113]. The CD48+ SPP1+ subset, characterized by high glycolytic activity, is suggested to contribute to bone and tissue destruction [110, 113]. The CD48+ interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15)+ subset displays a strong type I interferon signature, suggesting its contribution to interferon-mediated synovial inflammation and possibly contributing to therapy resistance in some patients [110, 113–115]. In contrast to the inflamed state, sustained remission in RA is characterized by a reduction in the number of MerTK- CD206- infiltrating STM subsets and concomitant re-establishment of the tissue-resident MerTK+ CD206+ macrophage compartment, including the TREM2+ and LYVE1+ subsets [110]. Together, these findings highlight the dynamic plasticity and functional polarization of STMs, which transition from a homeostatic, barrier-forming tissue-resident state to an inflammatory, monocyte-derived phenotype in active RA. The distinct molecular profiles and pathogenic mechanisms of CD48+ STM subsets highlight their critical roles in orchestrating synovial inflammation, tissue damage, and clinical outcomes, positioning them as potential targets for precision immunotherapy in RA.

Lupus nephritis

In the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, renal macrophages exhibit behaviors distinct from those in the synovial tissue. Lupus nephritis (LN), a severe renal manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), is driven by the deposition of autoantibody-derived IgG-containing ICs within the glomeruli [116]. These ICs activate the complement cascade and engage Fcγ receptors on resident macrophages, triggering inflammatory responses [116, 117]. According to recent studies, tissue-resident renal macrophages can be broadly categorized into embryonically derived macrophages and monocyte-derived populations, including MHC class II+ and MHC class II−, both arising from circulating monocytes [118]. Unlike other organs where MHCII expression is inversely correlated with markers such as LYVE1 and CX3CR1—delineating functionally and spatially distinct macrophage subsets—renal MHCIIhi and MHCIIlo subsets show comparable LYVE1 and CX3CR1 expression, and do not segregate anatomically with the vasculature or nerve fibers [50, 118]. Thus, the kidney lacks the canonical bifurcated macrophage differentiation trajectory observed in tissues like the heart, lung, and skin. Remarkably, during LN, not only are monocyte-derived macrophages recruited and expanded—as also seen in RA—but embryonic-derived macrophages also increase in number and contribute to disease progression [118]. Monocyte differentiation is skewed from the MHIIhi to the MHCIIlo subset, which exhibits elevated Fcgr4 expression and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activity, enabling efficient uptake of large IgG ICs and driving robust inflammatory responses [118]. In contrast, the expanded tissue-resident macrophages in LN, although limited in antigen presentation and IC phagocytosis, secrete high levels of chemokines such as CCL8 and cytokines like B-cell activating factor (BAFF), thereby promoting monocyte recruitment and establishing a proinflammatory B-cell niche [118]. Building upon this macrophage heterogeneity, a recent study identified a TREM2+ macrophage subset with an anti-inflammatory phenotype [119]. The induction of these macrophages appears to follow a two-step process. First, CSF2-producing NKp46+ group 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1s) were found to promote the expansion of monocyte-derived macrophages, especially the CSF2R-expressing MHCIIlo subset [119]. Then, prolonged interactions of these macrophages with injured epithelial cells have been proposed to lead to the upregulation of TREM2, driving a phenotypic skewing from proinflammatory monocyte-derived MHCIIlo subsets towards TREM2+ macrophages characterized by reduced NF-κB activity and an anti-inflammatory profile [119]. Notably, TREM2 deficiency resulted in the accumulation of proinflammatory MHCIIlo macrophages and impaired epithelial repair, underscoring the protective and reparative role of the TREM2 axis in the pathogenesis of lupus nephritis.

Inflammatory bowel disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) provides a representative setting to investigate how disrupted monocyte-to-macrophage maturation contributes to persistent inflammation at barrier surfaces [120]. Unlike in organs such as the brain, liver, and lung—where long-lived, self-renewing embryonically derived macrophages predominate—macrophages in the intestinal lamina propria are continuously replenished by circulating Ly6C+ monocytes under steady-state conditions [121]. These monocytes follow a well-defined stepwise differentiation trajectory, progressing from an initial Ly6Chi MHCII- CD64- stage to an intermediate Ly6Chi MHCII+ CD64- stage, and eventually maturing into Ly6Clo MHCII+ CD64+ CX3CR1hi macrophages [120, 122]. Multiple signaling pathways are involved in the regulation of monocyte-to-macrophage maturation. In particular, this maturation is critically regulated by CSF1–CSF1R signaling in a concentration-dependent manner, alongside contributions from growth factors such as FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3L) and CSF2 [123]. Advances in scRNA-seq have significantly deepened our understanding of intestinal macrophage heterogeneity. Mature Ly6Clo MHCII+ CD64+ CX3CR1hi macrophages are now recognized to encompass several transcriptionally and spatially distinct subsets. Among them, two major populations have been delineated: CD11c+ CD121b+ macrophages, which predominantly localize in the lamina propria, and CD11c- CD206hi CD169hi macrophages, enriched in the submucosa and muscularis layers [124]. The former subset shows the enriched expression of genes associated with antigen presentation and immune activation, whereas the latter expresses markers such as stabilin-1 (Stab1) and Lyve1, reflecting roles in tissue remodeling, phagocytosis, and vascular interaction [124]. This regional specialization is shaped by epithelial and stromal cues as well as microbiota-derived signals, contributing to compartmentalized immune regulation within the gut mucosa.

In IBD, however, the well-defined monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation trajectory described above is disrupted, leading to the accumulation of monocyte-derived immature macrophages with proinflammatory properties. These macrophages display heightened sensitivity to TLR signals and produce large amounts of proinflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-23, and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), while exhibiting diminished expression of regulatory cytokines such as IL-10, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation [120, 125, 126]. Furthermore, recent studies have further characterized a population of TREM1+ inflammatory macrophages enriched in both ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, which expressed high levels of IL-6, CXCL8, and IL-1β, and were shown to amplify inflammation through TREM1 signaling [127]. Notably, patients with IBD exhibit higher serum concentrations of soluble TREM1 than healthy controls, regardless of disease activity status, further supporting its involvement in disease pathogenesis [128, 129]. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of the TREM1 pathway significantly attenuates disease severity in murine models, highlighting its pathogenic relevance and positioning TREM1 as a promising therapeutic target for IBD [127].

Collectively, these studies highlight the dynamic remodeling of the intestinal macrophage compartment in IBD, characterized by the expansion of transcriptionally distinct, proinflammatory subsets that integrate microbial, stromal, and lymphoid signals to sustain chronic mucosal inflammation. Understanding the signals that govern their fate and function may yield novel therapeutic strategies for restoring immune homeostasis in IBD.

Future perspectives

Recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies have unveiled remarkable heterogeneity among macrophage populations across diverse disease contexts. In particular, TREM2+ macrophages—including LAMs, SAMarc, NAMs, DAMs, and TREM2+ TAMs—have emerged as key players in disease progression and resolution, each occupying distinct niches and fulfilling context-dependent functions (Table 1). Despite their shared expression of TREM2 and related markers such as CD9 and GPNMB, these macrophage subsets exhibit considerable divergence in ontogeny, activation cues, transcriptional programs, and interactions with surrounding cells, including fibroblasts, adipocytes, neurons, and T cells. Future studies should aim to dissect the molecular determinants that govern the context-specific differentiation and functional plasticity of these TREM2+ macrophages. Key questions include: What defines the balance between their protective and pathogenic roles? How do stromal cues and metabolic signals shape their phenotypes? Can we selectively modulate disease-promoting functions while preserving beneficial activities? Therapeutically, TREM2+ macrophages represent an attractive but challenging target. For example, agonistic anti-TREM2 antibodies are currently under investigation in neurodegenerative diseases, yet their impact on peripheral TREM2+ macrophages remains largely unknown. A deeper understanding of the spatial and temporal regulation of these subsets across tissues will be crucial for developing precise and safe interventions. Moreover, integrating multi-omic approaches with genetic lineage tracing and functional perturbation studies in both human and animal models will be essential to uncover the full spectrum of TREM2+ macrophage biology. By elucidating the common and divergent mechanisms that govern these cells in disease-specific microenvironments, future research may pave the way for macrophage-centered therapies tailored to the pathophysiological context. Building on this perspective, therapeutic strategies targeting TREM2+ subsets have attracted increasing attention as promising approaches in precision medicine. Recent advances have led to the development of novel interventions that go beyond theoretical potential. For example, anti-TREM2 agonistic antibodies have been explored in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, while their role in modulating tumor-associated macrophages is being evaluated in cancer immunotherapy [66, 130]. Additionally, pharmacological inhibition of the CCL2–CCR2 axis, which regulates monocyte recruitment and macrophage differentiation, is being tested as a therapeutic approach in liver fibrosis and other chronic inflammatory conditions [131, 132]. These examples underscore the clinical relevance of targeting macrophage subsets and highlight the importance of integrated translational research that bridges experimental models and human disease.

Future challenges for macrophage research include interspecies differences between humans and mice. For example, human and mouse macrophages exhibit different surface marker expression and gene expression profiles that serve as clues for identifying subtypes in each species [133]. Specifically, a cross-species scRNA-seq analysis of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in lung cancer tissues showed that, while subtypes of dendritic cells and monocytes are largely conserved between mice and humans, TAMs exhibit species-specific subtypes with no reported counterparts [25]. This indicates the potential divergence of macrophage characteristics and subtypes between humans and mice, posing an obstacle to translating macrophage research findings across species and underscoring a key unresolved issue.

In addition, the effects of human-specific genetic polymorphisms on macrophages that are not present in wild-type laboratory animals have recently been reported. For example, knock-in mice genetically engineered to carry single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in murine Tlr4, analogous to human TLR4 variants associated with susceptibility to inflammatory bowel disease, developed severe colitis attributed to an impaired capacity of macrophages expressing M2a marker genes [134]. Furthermore, our recent work showed that human alveolar macrophages harboring SNPs that lead to fusion of the adjacent IFNAR2 and IL10RB genes were associated with an elevated risk of severe COVID-19. Pronounced expression of the resulting readthrough transcript, which encodes the novel COVID-19 infectivity-enhancing dual receptor (CiDRE), notably enhances the IL-10 signaling pathway, promoting viral amplification while simultaneously sequestering type I IFN and thereby diminishing the antiviral response, leading to severe COVID-19 disease [135]. Thus, SNPs in macrophages that induce readthrough transcription near the variant site may contribute to individual differences in disease pathogenesis. Recent advances in long-read RNA-seq technology have enabled the identification of numerous previously unreported readthrough transcripts, drawing growing attention to their potential functions [136]. These functionally uncharacterized readthrough transcripts are suggested to modulate immune functions through yet-unknown mechanisms and may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of human diseases [135]. Going forward, human immunology research should aim to elucidate the intraindividual heterogeneity of macrophages using conventional approaches, while addressing the interindividual heterogeneity driven by genetic polymorphisms.

Conclusions

Recent technological advances in flow cytometry and scRNA-seq analysis have accelerated the progress of research on macrophage diversity in vivo. Although studies using mouse models have offered valuable insights into human macrophage subtypes, many challenges remain, particularly the interspecies differences in surface markers used to characterize the subtypes and the limitations of modeling genetic polymorphism-driven functional variation in mice. Future research approaches must aim to strategically integrate findings from mouse models with analyses of human clinical specimens.
