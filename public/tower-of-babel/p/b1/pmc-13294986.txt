Editorial: Pluripotent stem cells in discovery and mechanistic toxicology
Authors: David G Belair
Published: 2026

Background

Pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) have proven indispensable for studying developmental biology and are the current gold standard for assessing mechanisms of developmental toxicity in vitro. Mammalian PSC culture was unlocked by the pioneering work of Evans and Kaufman who in 1981 described a method for in vitro cultivation of PSCs from mouse embryos (Evans and Kaufman, 1981). This was followed nearly two decades later by James Thomson and colleagues who described a method for isolating and cultivating human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) (Thomson et al., 1998). The utility of stem cells expanded rapidly into other areas of research including toxicology due to enabling and foundational work on induced pluripotency of somatic cells (Takahashi et al., 2007, Yu et al., 2007). Development and commercialization of cells and reagents for directed differentiation of PSCs unleashed the potential of stem cell research in toxicology for organ systems that had been historically challenging to study in vitro due to lack of robust and ethical means of sourcing primary cells, for example human neurons (Zeng et al., 2010) and cardiomyocytes (Zhang et al., 2009). Commercially available induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cells and iPSC-based assays have been a boon for toxicological research spanning discovery toxicology and in vitro hazard identification to mechanistic toxicology, all in close alignment with the 3Rs principles (replacement, reduction, refinement) for animal usage. Validation and regulatory adoption of stem cell-based toxicology approaches have increased over the past decade, buoyed by financial support from the public and private sectors and motivated by regulatory adoption of new approach methodologies (NAMs) codified in the Frank R. Lautenberg Chemical Safety for the 21st Century Act and the FDA Modernization Act. The work is ongoing to develop and characterize new stem cell-based methods for toxicity assessment and to highlight contexts of use for stem cells in various toxicological applications. The purpose of this special issue is to highlight both the breadth and depth of PSC research applications in the fields of discovery and investigative toxicology (Fig. 1). Overview

The initial value proposition for PSC research in toxicology was the unparalleled access to the study of developmental processes in a dish, leading to the establishment of the embryonic stem cell test (Scholz et al., 1999) and other stem cell-based approaches for assessing developmental toxicity. Gamble and Deisenroth (2025) expand on decades of in vitro developmental toxicity work with PSCs and present a compelling use case of the DevTox germ layer reporter platform for assessing developmental toxicity. The authors profiled the RUES2-GLR cell line, which expresses fusion proteins of biomarkers representing each of the three germ layers (SOX2 for pluripotency and early neural ectoderm, BRA for mesoderm, and SOX17 for endoderm) (Martyn et al., 2018). The authors assessed the technical performance of the assay across a range of compounds and observed concordance between developmental toxicity outcomes and positivity in the assay with a reference set of chemicals. Ultimately the endoderm assay exhibited the best overall predictive accuracy among a set of 16 reference chemicals.

Human iPSCs (hiPSCs) have been particularly valuable to the field of neurotoxicology. Researchers over the past two decades have described detailed protocols for deriving many nervous system phenotypes from hiPSCs including neural progenitors, cortical neurons, and glial cells. In this special issue, three distinct avenues of research were conducted with hiPSC-derived neurons: (i) functional endpoints for safety and efficacy assessment, (ii) miRNA biomarkers of neurotoxicity, and (iii) the role of microglia in neurotoxicity. Fleming et al. present a detailed characterization of a human neuromuscular junction (NMJ) on a dish using hiPSC-derived myoblasts and motor neurons co-cultured with human dermal fibroblasts in the Mantarray 24 well plate platform (Fleming et al., 2025). Channelrhodopsin-2 expression in hiPSC-derived motor neurons enabled light-induced muscle contraction, which was measured in real-time by sensors embedded in the array. The engineered muscle tissue assay was validated with immunofluorescence staining and force measurements in response to a dose response treatment with botulinum neurotoxin complex A establishing a use case of the engineered muscle tissue for evaluating neurotoxin potency. Belair et al. characterized an in vitro phenotypic assay of seizure liability using hiPSC-derived glutamatergic neurons cultured on multi-electrode arrays. The assay was sensitive to the effects of known seizurogenic compounds and was profiled against drug candidates covering a range of pharmacological targets, revealing exquisite sensitivity to compounds with activity against the sodium channel among other off-target receptors. One limitation of the in vitro model pointed out by the authors is that it does not account for differential brain partitioning. To address this limitation, the authors coupled the in vitro assay with a mathematical model for central nervous system (CNS) penetrance, revealing that the assay concordance with in vivo seizure outcomes was improved with prediction of brain exposure (Belair et al., 2025).

A review article by Majumder and Murphy and an original research article by Yuan et al. highlight the usefulness of hiPSC-derived neural organoids for modeling neurotoxicity and the role of microglia and neuroinflammation triggered by compound exposure. Majumder and Murphy laid out the methods historically employed for neural organoid generation by self-assembly, guided assembly, or 3D printing, and summarized the predictivity of organoid models for neurotoxicity. The authors raised experimental variability and durability as key limitations in the field (Majumder and Murphy, 2025). Yuan et al. describe a method for generating self-organizing neural organoids using hiPSC-derived neural precursor cells, endothelial cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and microglia seeded on synthetic hydrogels. Neural organoids self-assembled, differentiated, and were sensitive to inflammatory stimulus, highlighting the role of microglia for induction of IL-8 production. Neurotoxicity assessment revealed unique responses elicited by bona fide neurotoxins with microglia playing a key role in the neurotoxicity of colchicine and lead acetate (Yuan et al., 2025).

Another application of hiPSC-derived neural organoids is the detailed study of potential circulating biomarkers of neurotoxicity, which is especially informative given the relative inaccessibility of the CNS for tissue biomarker analysis. Silver et al. generated hESC-derived cerebral organoids and isolated extracellular vesicles (EVs), which were profiled with NanoString and pathway analysis, revealing representation of myelination signaling, serotonin receptor signaling, axonal guidance, neuroinflammation pathways, and neurological disease pathways in the EV-derived miRNAs (Silver et al., 2025). The study paves the way for the future use of EVs and EV-derived miRNA as biomarkers of neurotoxicity and disease.

Since reprogramming of somatic cells to iPSCs preserves the genotype of the cells, hiPSC-derived cells offer the opportunity to explore the impact of patient genotype on cell function. This feature has proven useful for disease modeling, particularly in the CNS. In their study, Gao et al. used hiPSC-derived hepatocytes to explore whether donor variability can affect susceptibility of the liver to known hepatotoxicants. The authors differentiated hiPSC-lines to hepatocytes using established protocols and profiled the cells by immunofluorescence staining, bulk transcriptomic (RNAseq) analysis, albumin production assays, and cytochrome P450 activity via mass spectrometry analysis of CYP-specific metabolites. Hepatocytes derived from 9 unique donors exhibited differences in phenotype by RNAseq and P450 activity and were differentially sensitive to known hepatotoxicants acetaminophen, troglitazone, diglycolic acid, and usnic acid (Gao et al., 2025). Although donor ethnicity was not strongly correlated with increased or decreased sensitivity, the study exemplified the utility of hiPSC-derived cells to profile for donor susceptibility to toxicity.

Finally, hiPSC-derived cells serve as an important source of cells for off-target profiling of novel modalities including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells. Price et al. describe a workflow using cultured hiPSC-derived GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons, astrocytes, endothelial cells, hepatocytes, and cardiomyocytes to profile for off-target effects of an exemplary CAR T cell in co-culture. The case study for a CAR T directed against CD33 revealed on-target cell killing with no notable off-target toxicity in non-target expressing cells by impedance measurements, LDH release, and cytokine release assays (Price et al., 2025). For cell and gene therapies and biologics with limited or no cross-reactivity to preclinical species, assays for off-target binding and toxicity in the context of human cells (Lebrec et al., 2021) will become increasingly valuable for demonstrating safety of a candidate drug in advance of first-in-human clinical trials.

Concluding remarks

Stem cells have occupied an increasing percentage of toxicology publications since 1980, reaching an apex in 2016 (Fig. 2). The increase in PSC-related publications in toxicology around 2008 reflects the rapid adoption of induced pluripotent stem cell technology in toxicological research. Stem cells, both multipotent and pluripotent, will continue to find utility for toxicological applications given the strong impetus by researchers, industry, and regulators to develop and characterize NAMs consistent with the 3Rs principles. Discovery toxicology and hazard identification require robust cells and reagents for reproducible and high throughput characterization of a range of different toxicity challenges. Mechanistic toxicology requires the availability of tools for probing aspects of developmental biology on a background that is amenable to gene editing and non-integrating methods for assessing pathway susceptibility and demonstrating rescue of chemical effects.
