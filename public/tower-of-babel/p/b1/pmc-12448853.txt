Cervical cancer prevention and control in Nigeria: mapping and review of policies
Authors: Elvis Anyaehiechukwu Okolie, Kristen Beek, Bindu Patel, Chizoma Millicent Ndikom, Rohina Joshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is a significant public health issue in Nigeria and a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women. Equitable implementation of cervical cancer control programs alongside relevant policies and strategic plans is vital to reducing the burden of cervical cancer and improving the quality of life. Considering the role of policies in guiding program implementation, we reviewed Nigeria's cervical cancer policy landscape to identify strengths, limitations, and opportunities for improvement. This policy appraisal involved a literature review to understand related policy review frameworks, developing a modified framework containing six domains, systematically searching key databases and websites to identify relevant policy documents, data extraction and analysis, and synthesizing findings from reviewed documents. A total of five documents were reviewed in this study—three integrated cancer control plans, a cervical cancer policy, and a strategic plan for cervical cancer prevention and control. Two of the reviewed documents are current (2023–7), one is outdated, and two are expired. Key strengths identified in these documents include (i) a clear articulation of goals, (ii) a collaborative development process, (iii) the adoption of a phased implementation approach for proposed interventions, (iv) detailed intervention plans, and (v) monitoring and evaluation plans with performance indicators. In contrast, key limitations include (i) poor participation of subnational level stakeholders, (ii) absence of costing and funding approach in some plans, (iii) lack of baseline data on unmet needs and outcomes of previous plans, and (iv) absence of health system resource mapping. Addressing identified limitations is critical to improving the quality of policy and policy-informing documents, strengthening implementation across all levels, lowering the cervical cancer burden, and improving women's health outcomes.Keywords: cervical screening, health policy, health systems research, health workforce, cancer, policy analysis, reproductive health, women’s health, Nigeria

Introduction

Cervical cancer represents a substantial health challenge in Nigeria despite advancements in the prevention and control of the disease (Sowemimo et al. 2017, Ferlay et al. 2024). Nigeria ranks first for cervical incidence and mortality in the sub-Saharan African region, with an estimated 13 700 new cases and 7100 deaths annually (Ferlay et al. 2024). Cervical cancer is the second leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality among women in Nigeria (Ferlay et al. 2024). Even more concerning is that an estimated 61 million women and girls in Nigeria are at risk of developing the disease, and such risk is expected to increase if there are no strategic efforts to scale up prevention and control programs in the country (Bruni et al. 2023). The lack of a coordinated and integrated national cervical cancer prevention and control (CCPC) program has been identified as a major constraint and contributing factor to this burden (Sowemimo et al. 2017, Okolie et al. 2022a, 2022b).

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a National Cancer Control Program (NCCP) is an initiative developed

to reduce cancer incidence and mortality and improve quality of life of cancer patients, through the systematic and equitable implementation of evidence-based strategies for prevention, early detection, diagnosis, treatment, and palliation, making the best use of available resources. World Health Organization (2002)

Regardless of a country's socioeconomic status, the importance of comprehensive, costed, funded, and integrated national cancer control plans and policies as a foundation for NCCP cannot be overstated in cancer prevention and control efforts (World Health Organization 2002, 2005, 2014, Duncan et al. 2019). Resolution 58.22 on cancer prevention and control of the 58th World Health Assembly in 2005, which encouraged member states to establish cancer control programs and plans, stimulated national cancer policies and plans (World Health Organization 2005).

Following the WHO Resolution 58.22, there has been a constant increase in the number of cancer-related plans, as seen in the digital repository of the International Cancer Control Partnership (ICCP) (Romero et al. 2018, International Cancer Control Partnership 2025). In 2018, 224 cancer-related plans, including National Cancer Control Plans from 93 countries, were available on the ICCP portal (Romero et al. 2018). By 2025, this number had risen to 436 from 136 countries (International Cancer Control Partnership 2025). Beyond the steady growth of cancer policy documents, several global milestone moments, such as the adoption of the global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer by 194 WHO member states (World Health Organization 2020); the launch of the Cancer Moonshot Initiative by the Quad Countries (USA, Australia, India, and Japan) to reduce cancer burden in the Indo-Pacific including a 5-year commitment of $1.58 billion for human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines (The White House 2024); and the historic $600 m funding pledge from the first Global Cervical Cancer Elimination Forum in March 2024 (Almonte et al. 2024) represent significant commitments to global progress in CCPC.

Cancer policies and plans are expected to provide a clear roadmap for countries to prepare, coordinate, and implement targeted priority interventions to prevent and control cancer in a sustainable and evidenced-based manner (World Health Organization 2005, Dutta et al. 2018, Duncan et al. 2019, Akanda et al. 2022, Zubairu and Balogun 2023). Additionally, these plans must rely on empirical evidence that provides data on a range of cancer-related issues, such as disease burden, priority areas for intervention, resource availability, health system capacity, political commitment, and opportunities for collaborative action to address identified issues within the country's broader socioeconomic context (World Health Organization 2005, Dutta et al. 2018, Beddoe 2019, Duncan et al. 2019). Australia, which is on the verge of being the first country to eliminate cervical cancer by 2035, is a prime example of how robust policy development, strategic planning, and integrated interventions across the cervical cancer continuum can make a difference (Hall et al. 2019).

There is a dearth of research evidence that provides insight into the cervical cancer policy landscape in Nigeria. However, such research evidence is key for identifying strengths and limitations of policy content, improving plans and policies, and situating gaps and recommendations within the health system and policy environment (Dutta et al. 2018, Romero et al. 2018, Horton et al. 2020, Akanda et al. 2022). For instance, while recommendations such as training health personnel, funding, HPV vaccination, and purchasing vital equipment are critical for cancer prevention and control, they may miss prevailing issues across the health system (e.g. absenteeism, power supply, personnel welfare, caregiver burden, quality of life, and other direct vs. indirect vs. social burdens of disease) that can affect desired outcomes even when these interventions are implemented (Albreht et al. 2015, Horton et al. 2020). Hence, it is important for recommendations to be drawn from holistic health system evaluations including the policy environment, to ensure the success, scale-up, and sustainability of proposed recommendations (Albreht et al. 2015, Horton et al. 2020).

Previous studies that reviewed cancer control plans and policies globally and in Africa observed significant variability in content, depth of information on key components, and comprehensiveness of analyzed documents (Dutta et al. 2018, Romero et al. 2018, Akanda et al. 2022). These studies observed misalignment between the objectives and activities in national plans and standards outlined in global guiding documents (Dutta et al. 2018, Akanda et al. 2022). As part of the efforts to assess Nigeria's trajectory toward meeting national and global cervical cancer prevention targets (World Health Organization 2020), it is imperative to evaluate the cervical cancer control plan and policy landscape since the establishment of its NCCP in 2006 (Federal Ministry of Health 2024). Therefore, this study involved the review of relevant policies and plans for CCPC in Nigeria. The review will provide insight into the strengths and limitations of the policies and identify opportunities to improve future policy recommendations and plan updates.

Materials and methods

This policy review involved a literature review to understand frameworks used in previous studies that appraised national cervical cancer policies and plans. Following the literature review, we conducted a systematic search to map and identify relevant cervical cancer-related policies and plans in Nigeria. Policies refer to documents that contain clear commitment from the government and partners and highlight goals for CCPC, priority intervention areas, resources, and strategies to achieve specified goals (World Health Organization 2002). National cancer control plans refer to strategic documents that provide a roadmap of intended activities to be implemented across priority areas of the cancer control continuum to achieve specified goals within a stated time frame (Oar et al. 2019, Union for International Cancer Control 2022). We used a modified framework adapted from studies by Dutta et al. (2018), Akanda et al. (2022), and key WHO documents (World Health Organization 2002, World Health Organization and International Atomic Energy Agency 2011) as a lens to extract and analyze data from the identified cervical cancer-related policy and plans in Nigeria. Finally, we synthesized key findings from the results across the six domains and associated subsets in the modified framework.

Search strategy

To obtain relevant cervical cancer–related policy and plan documents in Nigeria, we searched key databases in October 2024. Two databases, namely the WHO Noncommunicable Disease Document Repository (https://extranet.who.int/ncdccs/documents/) and the ICCP database (https://www.iccp-portal.org/), were searched by applying “Nigeria” as a search filter on either the country or national plan sections of these databases. Following the application of “Nigeria” as a search filter, we retrieved three national cancer control plans—2008–13 (FMOH 2007), 2018–22 (FMOH 2018), and 2023–7 (NICRAT and FMOH 2023a). To broaden our search for relevant cervical cancer–related documents, we employed Boolean operators (OR and AND) to search Google and Google Scholar by combining relevant terms such as “Nigeria,” “national,” “cancer,” “cervical cancer,” “cervix uteri,” “NCD,” “plan,” “strateg*,” “guideline*,” “polic*,” and “action.” Additionally, we searched relevant websites of Nigeria's Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH; https://www.health.gov.ng/), National Institute for Cancer Research and Treatment (NICRAT; https://www.nicrat.gov.ng/), and the Nigerian Documents Blogspot (https://nigeriandocuments.blogspot.com/). Results from the search of these websites produced the existing cervical cancer–specific policy and strategic plan—2010 policy (FMOH 2010) and the 2023–7 strategy (NICRAT and FMOH 2023b). Furthermore, we contacted FMOH and NICRAT and received confirmation that our list of documents was exhaustive and up to date since the establishment of Nigeria's NCCP.

Development of a framework for the review of cervical cancer–related prevention and control policies and plans in Nigeria

Currently, there is no global consensus on the adoption of specific standards or frameworks for the comprehensive assessment of cancer-related policies and plans (Romero et al. 2018). However, there are published reviews of cervical cancer–related policies using different frameworks. These frameworks, including those used by Dutta et al. (2018) and Akanda et al. (2022), were adapted from relevant policy models and WHO's key requirements for national cancer control programs, including plans and policies. Importantly, any framework adopted for the review of cancer-related policies and plans must be aligned with the specified research objectives. The adapted framework for this policy review focused on the policy development timeline, comprehensiveness, use of evidence, and existence of fundamental components of cervical cancer policies, such as strategy for service delivery, health system resource mapping across the WHO building blocks, and funding plans. Additional focus areas for this review included health workforce strategies, entities responsible for implementing these strategies, and indicators for progress.

We reviewed two policy analysis approaches and two WHO documents (see Supplementary Table S1) and adapted the guiding principles to develop a suitable framework for this policy review (World Health Organization 2002, World Health Organization and International Atomic Energy Agency 2011, Dutta et al. 2018, Akanda et al. 2022). Based on the frameworks and principles mentioned above, this review will rely on six domains and a subset of measures—overview and goals of the plan or policy, ownership and participation, comprehensive approach for CCPC, evidence use, health system resource mapping, and health workforce development initiatives (see Table 1). The first five domains focus on the quality of the contents of the identified document in view of identifying strengths and limitations. The sixth domain on health workforce initiatives is focused on understanding policy direction for strengthening the health workforce for CCPC.

Data extraction and analysis

We reviewed all the identified policies and implementation plans and extracted data using the modified framework (Table 1). Relevant sections containing information on the key focus areas of the six domains and subsets were reviewed, and appropriate data were extracted and entered into the data table. We analyzed the extracted data across the six domains to identify the strengths and limitations of the plans and policies. The results of the analysis are presented in summaries according to the domains.

The above domains and subset of measures will serve as a foundation for the review of the cervical cancer–related plan and policy landscape in Nigeria. Specifically, this review will seek to identify a clear articulation of the framework's key focus areas across the plans and policies.

Results

Overview and goals of the plan and policy documents

Five relevant cervical cancer–related documents were reviewed in this study (see Fig. 1 and Table 2). The documents include three national cancer control plans—National Cancer Plan (2008–13, 2018–22) and National Strategic Cancer Plan (2023–7), and two cervical cancer-specific documents—National Cervical Cancer Control Policy (2010) and National Strategic Plan for Prevention and Control of Cancer of the Cervix in Nigeria (2023–7). Of the five documents reviewed, two plans are current, and their implementation will end in 2027. Each document has a range of seven to ten goals or guiding principles targeted at reducing cancer incidence and mortality. Various approaches to achieve these goals include vaccination, screening and treatment, palliative care, data management, research, funding, service integration, innovation, partnerships, health system strengthening, and evidence-based decisions.

Ownership and participation

The formulation of plans and policy documents was government-led, with varying levels of collaboration with NGOs, civil society, multilateral organizations, the private sector, and representatives from academia. Subnational government participation in the development of these documents was largely missing. The planned implementation of the plans and policy activities involved partnerships between specific government agencies, NGOs, multilateral organizations, civil society organizations, and academia. The key government agencies responsible for leading implementation were the Federal and State Ministries of Health, the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA), and NICRAT. However, only two documents, the National Cancer Control Plan (2018–22) and the National Strategic Cancer Control Plan (2023–7), indicated a collaborative funding approach between the government and proposed partners for implementing the plans. While there was no substantial evidence of the participation of community stakeholders including religious leaders and women groups who play a critical role in service uptake in the formulation of the reviewed plans and policy documents, the importance of engaging communities in the implementation phase was recognized.

Comprehensive approach for cervical cancer prevention and control

Of the five reviewed documents, the National Cervical Cancer Control Policy (2010) and National Strategic Plan for Prevention and Control of Cancer of the Cervix in Nigeria (2023–7) provided more details on the comprehensive approaches for prevention and control, such as HPV vaccination, screening and treatment, and palliative care. For instance, the current cervical cancer–specific plan included details on target age groups for HPV vaccination (girls aged 9–14 years) and screening (women aged 25–49 years), settings for vaccination, screening interval, treatment methods for precancer and invasive cervical cancer, and palliative care approaches. Detailed intervention plans were present in two integrated cancer control plans (2018–22) and (2023–7). While there was an intervention plan in the current strategic plan for cervical cancer control, key areas such as risk and mitigation components were missing. Costing or funding plan was present in two integrated cancer control plans (2018–22 and 2023–7) but was missing across other documents. All the reviewed documents contained elements of sustainability with service integration mentioned as a critical approach.

Evidence use

The burden of cancer was described across the three cancer control plans. The cervical cancer burden in Nigeria was highlighted in the 2010 policy and 2023–7 strategic plan for CCPC. While health system gaps were mentioned, data on unmet service delivery needs for the prevention and control of cervical cancer were largely missing across these documents. The recent plans did not have an empirical review of progress from previous plans nor specific baselines upon which the results of proposed activities could be measured. Monitoring and evaluation components with key performance indicators were present only in three recent plans, including the National Strategic Plan for Prevention and Control of Cancer of the Cervix in Nigeria (2023–7).

Health system resource mapping

Information about health system resources using the WHO's health system building blocks varied across the five documents (see Table 3). For the health information system block, two cancer control plans (2018–22 and 2023–7) provided information on the number of cancer registries in the country. The current specific plan for CCPC in Nigeria only covered areas of medical products and technologies by providing information on the number of nuclear medicine facilities, linear accelerator treatment machines, and facilities offering morphine for pain management. Information on existing resources for service delivery, health workforce, and health financing blocks were not contained in the documents.

Health workforce development initiatives

The plan to improve health workers’ capacity for the prevention and control of cancer, including cervical cancer, was present in all the documents (see Table 3). While training was the central approach to improving health workforce capacity, other strategies included developing the curriculum, establishing oncology specialities in postgraduate medical institutions, and leveraging continuing professional development schemes. Indicators to monitor progress toward strengthening health workforce capacity were present in only two reviewed plans: the National Cancer Control Plan (2018–22) and the National Strategic Cancer Control Plan (2023–7). For instance, an annual 15% increase in the number of skilled health workforce in cancer care was mentioned in both plans. All the reviewed documents mentioned the key agencies responsible for achieving specified health workforce objectives.

Discussion

We reviewed Nigeria's policy landscape for CCPC to assess the strengths and limitations of relevant documents and identify opportunities across key domains to improve future policy updates. This study determined the existence of relevant cervical cancer–related policy and plan documents, mixed outcomes regarding areas of strengths and limitations across the domains, and variability in quality between recent and older documents.

At the core of any cervical cancer policy or plan is to provide information on the status of cancer burden, goals and priority areas, and what can be done with the available resources (World Health Organization 2002, 2006, Romero et al. 2018, Oar et al. 2019). Generally, we observed considerable improvements in quality, especially in the level of detail across key domains in recent documents compared with previous versions. Observed improvements in quality may be due to evolving knowledge on evidence-based approaches, increasing number of policy development guidelines and tools, and improved technical and financial support from development partners (Romero et al. 2018, Oar et al. 2019, NICRAT and FMOH 2023a, 2023b).

Reviewed documents provided a clear overview with goals or guiding principles, priority areas, and deadlines. Additionally, the indication of a phased implementation of proposed interventions with progressive milestones for each year represented a key strength. For resource-limited settings, policies and plans that adopt a phased implementation of cost-effective interventions will likely achieve desired outcomes (World Health Organization 2002, 2006). While there is a current strategic plan for CCPC, the overarching policy enacted in 2010 is now outdated as it is more than 10 years old and no longer consistent with current recommendations (World Health Organization 2006). A policy update is needed to ensure consistency in national strategy as part of Nigeria's governance efforts for CCPC.

Developing policies and plans through a participatory process improves accountability, ownership, and implementation outcomes. Specifically, a bottom-up approach in planning ensures that stakeholders who understand the prevailing context and will implement the plan are involved throughout the process (World Health Organization 2002, 2006). With regard to ownership and participation, the key strengths documented in this review include the significant involvement of key stakeholders, including national government agencies, NGOs, the private sector, and academia; recognition of collaborative implementation as a key strategy; and suggestion of a shared funding approach between government and partners in two cancer plans (2018–22 and 2023–7). Beyond promoting ownership and accountability, collaboration allows for the optimization of available stakeholder resources and better coordination of implementation (World Health Organization 2002, 2020, Almonte et al. 2024).

Notably, evidence of substantial participation of subnational-level stakeholders, including state and community representatives, in developing policy and plans was largely missing. Our finding highlighting poor participation of subnational-level representatives is corroborated by an activity in the National Strategic Cancer Control Plan (2023–7) targeted at making the planning phase more inclusive (NICRAT and FMOH 2023a). While the importance of community engagement in the implementation phase was recognized across these documents, poor engagement in the planning phase may lead to poor buy-in and failure to achieve results (World Health Organization 2002, 2006, Okolie and Nwadike 2023). To demonstrate real intent for policy implementation, future planning efforts must prioritize the participation of subnational representatives who understand the local context, priority areas, and perspectives on what could work (World Health Organization 2006, Union for International Cancer Control 2022).

Similar to previous studies, we found considerable variability in the depth of details on comprehensive strategies for CCPC across reviewed documents (Dutta et al. 2018, Romero et al. 2018, Akanda et al. 2022). As expected, integrated cancer plans did not elaborate on key strategies compared with the specific cervical cancer documents. While the 2010 cervical cancer policy provided detailed information on the approaches across the cervical cancer continuum, some of these are now outdated. However, the strategies in the current plan for CCPC are comprehensive and consistent with recent global recommendations. In addition to comprehensive strategy across the cervical cancer continuum, documents with detailed intervention plans are more likely to be effectively implemented (World Health Organization 2006, Romero et al. 2018). In view of the key strategies for cervical cancer prevention contained in reviewed documents, such as HPV vaccination, screening, and treatment, Nigeria has recorded some progress. Still, more remains to be done to position the country on the path to sustainable results. For instance, in October 2023, Nigeria launched a two-phase national HPV vaccination campaign that has reached over 12 million girls aged 9–14 years via a single-dose schedule (UNICEF 2024). However, screening and treatment rates remain poor, with <12% of eligible women screened for cervical cancer (Bruni et al. 2022). The establishment of a National Taskforce to facilitate the achievement of the audacious target of screening 50% of eligible women and treating 100% by 2027 as contained in the 2023–7 strategic plan offers some hope (NICRAT and FMOH 2023b, Vanguard News 2024). Importantly, reaching such targets will rely on systematically addressing policy and program challenges.

Detailed intervention plans were present in recent documents, except for the current cervical cancer-specific plan, where risk and mitigation components were missing. Ensuring detailed intervention plans that include risk and mitigation measures is critical to successful implementation as potential threats are identified, mitigation measures deployed, and decisions consider the best direction to enhance outcomes (Romero et al. 2018, Union for International Cancer Control 2022). We found that three reviewed documents lacked costing and proposed funding approaches, including the current strategic plan for cervical cancer. Costing outlined activities and funding approaches in cancer control plans is a critical process in the development phase that has implications for implementation success or failure (World Health Organization 2006, Eguzo et al. 2020). This calls for attention to ensuring that activities in the current strategic cervical cancer plan are costed, and funding mechanisms are identified.

In the evidence-use domain, reviewed documents highlighted health system gaps and cancer burden. Similarly, we found detailed monitoring and evaluation plans with key performance indicators across the priority areas in recent plans. Despite the presence of monitoring and evaluation plans, a significant weakness across reviewed documents was the absence of baseline data to track progress and the lack of data on unmet service delivery needs and specific achievements of previous plans. For instance, recent documents failed to provide information on the extent of implementation of prior plans and policy, the proportion of targets reached, including service delivery outcomes, and resources expended. This lack of baseline data on the performance of previous plans has meant that newer plans did not build effectively on those before them. Also, subsequent plans may not allocate resources effectively to areas with the greatest needs, and their targets may be unrealistic (World Health Organization and International Atomic Energy Agency 2011, Romero et al. 2018). Hence, creating a robust and coordinated monitoring and evaluation system that appraises the development and implementation of policies and plans, including HPV vaccination, screening, and treatment programs, is vital for improving cervical cancer outcomes (World Health Organization 2006, Romero et al. 2018).

Despite the importance of aligning plans and policies with existing resources, we observed that health system resource mapping was neglected across reviewed documents. This should raise significant concern for stakeholders as a knowledge of available resources, including community structures relevant to the delivery of cervical cancer prevention interventions, is critical in setting priorities, selecting cost-effective interventions, and successfully implementing plans (World Health Organization 2002, 2006). Notably, we found a strong emphasis on strengthening the health workforce capacity through several strategies, especially training across the policy landscape. However, this strong emphasis has not translated into results, as evidence demonstrates that the shortage of skilled cancer workforce remains a significant challenge in Nigeria (Okolie et al. 2022a, 2022b, NICRAT and FMOH 2023b).

Furthermore, if Nigeria is to implement these policies and plans successfully, attention must be paid to addressing the implementation challenges highlighted in this paper. These include weak health system capacity, lack of evidence to inform decisions, poor buy-in from subnational levels, resource limitations and failure to align interventions with existing resources, ineffective stakeholder coordination, and inadequate political will to fully implement policies and plans (NICRAT and FMOH 2023a, 2023b).

We therefore provide the following recommendations: (i) Given Nigeria's resource constraints, it may be more practical to concentrate on a few priority interventions with a high chance of success and leverage results as entry point to mobilize additional resources to scale programs (World Health Organization 2006, Romero et al. 2018), (ii) It is imperative to move beyond costing and proposing a funding approach for priority areas to developing accompanying resource mobilization plans that capitalize on existing resources and highlight additional resource needs, potential funding sources, and strategies targeted at these sources (World Health Organization 2002, Union for International Cancer Control 2022), (iii) In addition to leveraging bottom-up participatory processes involving subnational-level stakeholders, developed policies and plans should be disseminated widely to ensure accessibility, broader understanding of national priorities, and use (World Health Organization 2006, Tumwesigye et al. 2013), (iv) Special attention needs to be directed at strengthening coordination and providing technical and funding support for subnational levels to develop and implement cancer plans that are consistent with national priorities and feasible to achieve successful implementation (Eguzo et al. 2020), and (v) Deliberate steps must be taken to strengthen governance and mutual accountability among government and partners at all levels to promote greater commitment and effective implementation to achieve the desired impact of these policies and plans.

Study limitations and strengths

Study limitations must be considered in the interpretation of the findings of this review. The depth of the findings in this study would have been enhanced by additional insight into the policy environment through interviews with policymakers, as we were limited to the content of reviewed plans and policy documents. However, conducting interviews was out of the scope of this review. In contrast, the rigorous methods employed in conducting this review strengthen the reliability of the findings. These include a systematic search of relevant policy documents, engagement with Nigeria's leading cancer policy agency (NICRAT), and the use of a structured framework to ensure a comprehensive review.

Conclusion

This review provided insight into the strengths and limitations of the policy landscape for CCPC in Nigeria. While there are several limitations and opportunities to improve the policy landscape, the government's commitment to developing these policies and progress in quality observed across successive plans must be acknowledged. With careful planning and resource allocation, Nigeria's cervical cancer policies and plans can be translated into effective implementation to address the high disease burden (NICRAT and FMOH 2023a, 2023b).
