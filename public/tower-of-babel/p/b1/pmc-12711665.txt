System analysis links SMARCD3 regulons to growth signaling and MEK inhibitor response in everolimus-resistant ER+ breast cancer cells
Authors: Eric F Medina, Elena Farmaki, Jason I Griffiths, Andrea H Bild, Aritro Nath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEstrogen receptor-positive breast cancer (ER+BC) accounts for ∼70% of all breast tumors, and 20%–40% of patients develop metastases. Everolimus is an mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor used in combination with exemestane for metastatic ER+BC. However, resistance remains common and leads to poor survival outcomes. To uncover resistance mechanisms, we analyze transcriptomic profiles from everolimus-sensitive and -resistant ER+BC cell lines. Our study uncovers persistent activation of a growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype in resistant cells involving IGF1R, ESR1, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways. We identify SMARCD3 regulons linked to this meta-phenotype. Additionally, we find SMARCD3 regulon activity elevated in everolimus-refractory patient tumors. Importantly, we show that SMARCD3 regulon activation was correlated with sensitivity to several known MEK1/2 inhibitors, including trametinib. Combining trametinib with everolimus treatment significantly reduces resistant cell growth. Our results demonstrate that everolimus-resistant ER+BC cells evade therapy via alternate growth-factor signaling linked to activation of SMARCD3 regulons, which can be therapeutically targeted using MEK1/2 inhibitors.Keywords: ER+ breast cancer, everolimus resistance, SMARCD3 regulon, growth-factor signaling, transcriptomics, MEK1/2 inhibitors, therapeutic targeting

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the most common diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide.1 In the United States alone, 310,720 new invasive BC cases were diagnosed in 2024, of which over 42,000 women are expected to succumb to the disease.2 The estrogen receptor-positive (ER+)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−) molecular subtype makes up roughly 70% of all BC cases.2 Early detection and improvements in first-line treatment regimens with endocrine therapy alone or in combination with cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) have increased progression-free survival (PFS) in patients diagnosed with ER+BC.3,4,5 However, 20%–40% of patients ultimately develop resistance to first-line endocrine therapy and develop distant metastases.6,7,8

The activation of phosphoinoside 3 kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway has emerged as an important mechanism of endocrine therapy resistance. This pathway is activated in roughly 30%–40% of patients with advanced ER+BC who no longer respond to first-line regimens.9,10,11,12,13,14 Consequently, drugs targeting the mTOR complex were approved for use against advanced ER+BC.15,16 The randomized phase 3 breast cancer trials of oral everolimus-2 (BOLERO-2) clinical trial17 evaluated mTOR allosteric inhibitor everolimus, also known as RAD001, in post-menopausal patients with advanced ER+BC in combination with the steroidal aromatase inhibitor exemestane. The trial demonstrated clinically significant improvements in PFS in patients treated with everolimus plus exemestane (7.8 months) compared to exemestane plus placebo (3.2 months).18 However, the trial showed no difference in overall survival between treatment arms.19

The nutrient sensing mTOR is a serine/threonine protein kinase essential to the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.20,21 Given the key roles of PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling in cell growth and survival, cancer cells rapidly evolve and develop mechanisms of resistance to mTOR inhibition.22,23 Potential mechanisms of resistance include upregulation of otherwise mTOR-inhibited signal transduction pathways, such as with insulin signaling.24 Additionally, the activation of RAS/ERK pathway was shown to compensate PI3K/mTOR signals in cancer,25,26 and mTORC1 inhibition led to hyperactivation of pERK in ER+BC cells.27,28 Considering the importance of everolimus therapy in advanced ER+BC, multiple studies have focused on developing predictive biomarkers for everolimus response.29,30 However, the precise phenotype that drives everolimus resistance in advanced ER+BC remains elusive.31 Importantly, there are no specific treatment strategies available to overcome everolimus resistance.

In this study, we develop a systems-level framework to characterize the everolimus-resistant phenotype in ER+BC cells and to identify associated phenotypic vulnerabilities that can serve as therapeutic targets. We analyze transcriptomic data from three matched pairs of everolimus-sensitive and -resistant ER+BC cell lines, with and without treatment, using linear mixed-effects models to uncover meta-phenotypes linked to sensitivity and resistance. Next, we implement a network-based protein activity inference algorithm to reconstruct state-specific regulon networks and identify a resistance regulon signature linked with the dysregulated meta-phenotypes.32,33 Finally, we identify pharmacologic vulnerabilities associated with the resistance regulon signature and demonstrate that add-on therapy against these vulnerabilities overcomes everolimus resistance.

Results

Systems-level framework to define mechanisms of everolimus resistance and identify therapeutic vulnerabilities

The objective of this study was to develop a systematic approach to characterize the transcriptional state associated with everolimus resistance in ER+BC cells and reveal targetable phenotypic vulnerabilities. To this end, our lab previously developed isogenic in vitro models of acquired everolimus resistance using CAMA1, MCF7, and T47D ER+BC cell lines.33 We selected the CAMA1, MCF7, and T47D cell lines to model everolimus resistance because they are well-established ER+BC models that represent distinct molecular and phenotypic subtypes with ER + cancers. The inclusion of these cell lines provides a broader view of resistance by capturing shared resistance mechanisms in response to mTORC1 inhibition, which enhances the generalizability of our findings. We obtained gene expression profiles of the cells with or without everolimus treatment and calculated phenotypic activity using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)34 scores based on the Hallmark and C2 (curated) Molecular Signature Database35 gene sets (Figure 1A).

To convert the phenotypic ssGSEA scores into a more interpretable form, we condensed the vast number of gene sets into meta-phenotypes defined through systematic annotation of the gene sets using pathway nomenclature and prior knowledge describing closely related biological pathways with similar function (Figures 1C and S1C). We define a meta-phenotype as an aggregate biological feature emerging from the coordinated activity of functionally related pathways. Meta-phenotypes represent distinct cellular states such as growth-factor signaling or cell-cycle activation and are composed of multiple related individual phenotypes, for example, EGFR, IGF1R, and ESR1 signaling within the growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype. Meta-phenotypes capture broad transcriptional states that may not be captured by individual pathways. With respect to drug resistance, meta-phenotypes help reveal the underlying regulatory architecture by grouping related pathway signatures into interpretable and biologically meaningful modules.

We identified significant gene sets by using linear mixed-effects models, which identify important features across ER+BC cell lines by accounting for both fixed effects, such as treatment condition or resistance status, and random effects, such as variability between cell lines. We were able to identify shared transcriptional changes associated with resistance while controlling for baseline expression differences between cell lines. Thus, this approach allowed us to improve the accuracy and generalizability of differentially enriched features detected across our model system. To obtain a test statistic for each meta-phenotype, we combined the model outputs of significant gene sets within each meta-phenotype using two methods: (1) Fisher’s method36,37 for combining p values of independent experiments sharing the same null hypothesis and (2) Cochrane’s method38,39 for combining estimates from multiple experiments (Figures 1B, 1C, and S2B).

In addition to identifying the meta-phenotypes associated with the resistant state, we used differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified by mixed-effects models between cell states to reconstruct transcriptional regulatory networks32,33,40 and reveal resistance regulon signature candidates (Figures 1B and 1D). We further linked the resistance regulon gene members to meta-phenotypes in resistant cells by labeling genes based on their presence in Hallmark or C2 gene sets annotated with a meta-phenotype. To evaluate if the regulon signatures correlate with phenotypic vulnerabilities against anti-cancer drugs, we implemented an approach leveraging pharmacogenomic profiles of 926 cancer cell lines and 395 anti-cancer drugs from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC)41 database (Figures 1A and 1E). Finally, we preformed viability assays to validate candidate anti-cancer drugs as monotherapy or in combination with everolimus in sensitive or resistant cells (Figure 1F).

Everolimus-resistant cells maintain higher levels of PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling after treatment

To confirm resistance to everolimus in ER+BC cells, we performed dose-response assays and analyzed curves using 4-parameter log-logistic models (p value ≤ 0.05) (Figure S1A). We next obtained transcriptional profiles from cell states before or after 10 nM everolimus monotherapy. In total, thirty-six samples were used for downstream analyses. To confirm if mTORC1 signaling was downregulated in both sensitive and resistant cells after treatment, we analyzed ssGSEA scores for two mTOR-related gene sets using linear mixed-effects models (Figure S1B). This analysis confirmed downregulation of mTORC1 signaling independent of cell state (p value ≤ 0.05) (Figure S1B). However, enrichment scores for the HALLMARK_PI3K_AKT_MTOR pathway remained elevated in post-treatment resistant cells relative to sensitive counterparts (p value = 0.031), indicating sustained activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway in resistant cells despite mTOR inhibition (Figure S1B).

Growth-factor signaling is the predominant meta-phenotype activated in resistant cells

To identify phenotypic differences between the cell states at baseline, we analyzed pre-treatment ssGSEA scores using linear mixed-effects models and identified 288 differentially enriched gene sets (false discovery rate [FDR] ≤ 0.05) (Figure S2A). After assigning meta-phenotypes, we performed hierarchical clustering of the ssGSEA scores within meta-phenotypes and observed a strong activation of growth-factor signaling gene sets in resistant cells (Figures 2A and S2C). Meta-analyses showed several notable meta-phenotypes enriched in resistant cells, including growth-factor signaling, immune response, GPCR signaling, and anti-apoptosis (Figure 2B). Growth-factor signaling (meta-FDR = 3.88e−63) was the predominant meta-phenotype enriched in resistant cells, which consisted of several pathways associated with IGF1R/insulin, estrogen, RAS, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/ERK signaling (Figures 2A and 2B). The growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype contained a total of 44 gene sets, of which 40 were activated in the resistant cell state while only 4 were in sensitive (Table S1). Immune response (meta-FDR = 4.75e−52) was the second most elevated meta-phenotype in resistant cells (Figure 2B), which is consistent with previous reports showing that everolimus treatment leads to immunosuppression.42,43 The GPCR meta-phenotype (meta-FDR = 7.03e−19) contained individual gene set ssGSEA scores entirely elevated in resistant cells, which included activation of several cell surface GPCR receptor pathways, such as F2R and ADORA2B signaling (Figure S2C; Table S1). Moreover, previous reports demonstrate GPCR signaling crosstalk with several growth-factor signaling pathways in ER+BC, including estrogen and IGF1R.44,45,46

Further examination of apoptosis signatures revealed two distinct meta-phenotypes: anti-apoptosis and apoptosis (Figure 2B). We observed anti-apoptosis enriched in resistant cells (FDR = 1.24e−05) and involved intrinsic apoptotic mechanisms (Figure S2C). In contrast, apoptosis (meta-FDR = 3.79e−11) was enriched in sensitive cells with gene sets involved in tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling via nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), caspase, and FAS activation (Figure S2C). The metabolism (meta-FDR = 7.90e−46), cell cycle (meta-FDR = 1.85e−36), and Rho-GTPase signaling (meta-FDR = 1.09e−15) meta-phenotypes were the topmost enriched in sensitive cells under no treatment (Figure 2B). Sensitive cells displayed several metabolism gene sets involved in amino acid, fatty acid, and xenobiotic metabolism while cell-cycle gene sets were associated with G2/M transitions and mitosis (Figure S2C). Moreover, the strong activation of cell cycle in sensitive cells may reflect differences in growth rates between sensitive and resistant cell states under no drug pressure and is indicative of the cost of resistance displayed by drug-resistant cells.47 Rho-GTPase signaling gene sets involved activation of several effector gene families contributing to actin polymerization and cytoskeleton organization, such as PKNS, ROCKS, and FORMINS.48 Thus, these results suggest that resistant cells activate growth-factor signaling while decreasing apoptotic and proliferation signals relative to sensitive cells.

Resistant cells maintain activation of growth-factor signaling upon everolimus treatment

To complement the aforementioned analyses of pre-treatment differences, we next determined how meta-phenotypes change between cell states under selective pressure. For this analysis, we compared transcriptional profiles of sensitive or resistant cells after everolimus treatment using a linear mixed-effects model fitted to the ssGSEA scores. We identified 209 differentially enriched gene sets (FDR ≤ 0.05) (Figure S2B). Meta-phenotypes were assigned to all gene sets, and meta-analysis parameters were calculated as described previously (Figures 1C, S1C, and S1D). Hierarchical clustering of ssGSEA scores revealed that most gene sets within the growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype were elevated in resistant cells, consistent with pre-treatment analyses (Figure 2C). Out of the 45 gene sets assigned to the growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype, 27 were enriched in resistant cells while 18 were in sensitive (Table S2). Next, we analyzed meta-phenotype enrichment in post-treatment samples and observed that resistant cells maintained strong enrichment of growth-factor signaling (meta-FDR = 1.64e−74) (Figure 2D). Of note, we observed a downshift in the proportion of growth-factor signaling gene sets enriched in resistant cells in post-treatment compared to pre-treatment analyses (Tables S1 and S2). The relative downshift in growth-factor signaling in resistant cells may reflect their transcriptional stability after long-term everolimus exposure, in contrast to sensitive cells, which promptly upregulate these pathways in an ultimately unsuccessful attempt to bypass treatment. Nonetheless, resistant cells continued to display strong activation of insulin/IGF1R, estrogen, Ras, and MAPK signaling (Figures 2C and S2C). Interestingly, sensitive cells showed upregulation of several pathways involved in fibroblast growth factor receptor and ERBB signaling (Figures 2C and S2C), suggesting resistant cells do not depend on these pathways for cell growth.

Resistant cells preserved strong enrichment of GPCR signaling (meta-FDR = 1.77e−12) and anti-apoptosis (meta-FDR = 1.76e−07) meta-phenotypes after everolimus treatment (Figure 2D). All GPCR signaling gene sets again showed ssGSEA scores elevated in resistant cells while anti-apoptosis was reflective of intrinsic apoptosis mechanisms (Figures 2C and S2C). As expected, apoptosis (meta-FDR = 3.89e−08) was enriched in sensitive cells after everolimus treatment, consisting of FAS, caspase, and TNF signaling via NF-κB (Figure S2C). We further observed a shift in the cell cycle, which was originally enriched in sensitive cells in pre-treatment samples (Figure 2B). However, no difference in post-treatment samples was observed (Figure 2D), indicative of the effect everolimus extends on the sensitive cell proliferation and the persistence of resistant cells to continue to proliferate under selective pressure. In addition, sensitive cells continued to display strong enrichment of metabolism (meta-FDR = 3.74e−75) and Rho-GTPase (meta-FDR = 1.35e−14), with metabolism gene sets involved in fatty acid and cytochrome p450/xenobiotics metabolism while Rho-GTPase gene sets continued to represent activation of effector families (Figures 2D and S2C). Moreover, sensitive cells showed activation of the post-translation modification (meta-FDR = 3.44e−22) meta-phenotype with gene sets primarily involved in ubiquitination and SUMOylating, suggesting their downregulation in resistant cells (Figure S2C). Again, these results support the conclusion that resistant cells conserve activation of a growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype while decreasing apoptosis notwithstanding everolimus treatment.

Transcriptional activation of IGF1R/ESR1 receptors and ligands in resistant cells

Given that resistant cells show strong activation of IGF1R and estrogen pathway-level signals (Figures 2A and 2D), we hypothesized that resistant transcriptomes should show upregulation of these receptors and corresponding ligands. To confirm this hypothesis, we first fit transcriptomic profiles to linear mixed-effects models, revealing 3,394 DEGs pre-treatment and 3,436 DEGs post-treatment (FDR ≤ 0.05). Next, we examined the differential expression status of IGF1R and ESR1 and observed their upregulation in resistant cells before or after treatment (Figure S3A). IGF1R expression level was significantly upregulated in resistant cell states independent of treatment condition. Strikingly, ESR1 expression was similar in resistant cell states across cell lines while expression in their sensitive counterparts varied, suggesting that estrogen signaling may be part of a shared regulatory phenotype activated in resistant cells. In addition, we observed modest upregulation of INSR and IGF2R RNA expression in post-treatment resistant cell samples (Figure S3A).

Next, we sought to identify putative ligand-receptor pairs that may contribute to the enhanced growth-factor signals displayed by resistant cells. To this end, we utilized a meticulously curated ligand-receptor pair database49 and searched ligand pairs for IGF1R, IGF2R, and INSR (Figure S3B). We observed consistent upregulation of IGF2 (FDR ≤ 0.001) in resistant cells independent of everolimus treatment. This finding agrees with previous reports demonstrating IGF2/IGF1R in promoting tumor growth and anti-cancer drug resistance.50,51 Resistant cells displayed strong activation of HRAS (FDR ≤ 0.001), which is well known for its crosstalk with insulin/IGF1R signaling and activation of MAPK/ERK.52,53 This database49 also noted GNAI2 and CDH1 as interactors with IGF1R. Resistant cells showed consistent upregulation of GNAI2 (FDR ≤ 0.01) but downregulation of CDH1 (FDR ≤ 0.05) before and after everolimus treatment (Figure S3B). These observations agree with previous reports showing GNAI2 activation and CDH1 downregulation in enhancing insulin/IGF1R signaling.54,55 The upregulation of IGF1R and ESR1 receptors aligns with pathway-level analyses identifying IGF1R and estrogen signaling as the predominate upstream growth-factor signaling mechanisms activated in resistant cells before or after everolimus treatment.

Given the upregulation of IGF1R in resistant cells, we examined the expression of the IGF1R protein by western blot. IGF1R is known to undergo autophosphorylation after binding with IGF2. CAMA1- and MCF7-resistant cells showed increased IGF1R expression relative to their respective sensitive counterparts, whereas T47D-resistant cells showed reduced IGF1R (Figures S3C and S3D). Interestingly, phosphorylation of IGF1R at Tyr1131 was elevated in resistant cells across all cell lines, including T47D (Figures S3C and S3D). Thus, these results strongly suggest enhanced IGF1R activation in resistant cells despite variable total IGF1R protein levels.

Identification of SMARCD3 regulon signatures linked to multiple meta-phenotypes

The efficacy of anti-cancer treatments can be limited by the emergence of acquired resistance mechanisms, such as activation of growth-factor signaling.56 Previous reports suggest the existence of context-specific regulon network signatures influencing the shift between two conditions, such as drug-sensitive and -resistant states.57,58 We thus hypothesized that meta-phenotypes in resistant cells may be associated with the existence of such regulon signatures, the activity of which may contribute to non-genetic resistance mechanisms essential to the resistant cell state. To identify potential regulon signatures, we first identified DEGs (FDR ≤ 0.05) between the sensitive and resistant cell states, along with two curated lists of transcription factors (TFs) and co-factor gene names from the Gene Ontology databases “DNA binding transcription factor activity” (GO:0003700) and “Transcription co-activator activity” (GO:0003712). We then implemented the algorithm for reconstruction of accurate cellular networks with adaptive partitioning (ARACNe-AP) to reverse-engineer transcriptional networks.32,40 Next, we used the network modules to calculate normalized enrichment scores inferring regulon signature activity using an integrative probabilistic framework as implemented by the virtual inference of protein-activity by enriched regulon analysis (VIPER) algorithm.33

Using this approach, we identified two regulon signatures enriched in resistant cells in the pre-treatment setting centered around HNF4G and SMARCD3 (Figure 3A). Post-treatment transcriptomes revealed HNF4G, ZFX, and SMARCD3 signatures enriched in resistant cells (Figure 3B). Notably, HNF4G and SMARCD3 signatures were consistently enriched in resistant cells independent of treatment condition. However, only SMARCD3 RNA gene expression was consistently enriched in resistant cells (Figure 3C). In addition, we evaluated the gene expression of each candidate in patient samples collected from a cohort of post-menopausal women diagnosed with ER+BC treated with 5 mg everolimus for 14 days29 Patients in this clinical trial were classified as responders or non-responders based on two distinct classifications following treatment: changes in cytoplasmic phosphorylated AKT (pAKT) or Ki67 expression by immunohistochemistry. Interestingly, SMARCD3 RNA expression was the only candidate activated in both pre-treatment and post-treatment non-responder patient tumors, independent of tumor classification method (Figures 3D, 3E, S3E, and S3F).

To reveal relationships between the pre- or post-treatment SMARCD3 signatures and meta-phenotypes, we assigned meta-phonotype categories to gene members present in the signatures. This analysis overlaid a variety of meta-phenotypes, including growth-factor signaling (Figures 4A, S4A, and S4B). Interestingly, several notable genes implicated in BC progression were observed in the signatures (Figure 4A). Specifically, ESR1 and IGF2 genes were upregulated in the SMARCD3 signatures, consistent with the strong activation of the growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype observed in resistant cells. Conversely, CASP7 was consistently downregulated, aligning with the deactivation of the apoptosis meta-phenotype in resistant cells. We also observed consistent upregulation of CDKN1A and KRT13, which have been implicated in anti-apoptosis and BC progression.59,60 These results revealed context-specific SMARCD3 signatures, with gene members linked to meta-phenotypes in resistant cells, including growth-factor signaling. The consistent enrichment of these signatures may indicate their critical role in maintaining the resistant state.

SMARCD3 regulon signatures are enriched in patients with ER+BC refractory to everolimus

To evaluate if the SMARCD3 signatures are clinically relevant, we examined their enrichment in tumor samples collected from patients diagnosed with ER+BC treated with everolimus described earlier.29 We performed ssGSEA using the SMARCD3 signatures and the microarray patient profiles. Patient samples were split based on pAKT or KI67 responder/non-responder classification methods, and enrichment scores for the two signatures were evaluated in pre- or post-treatment conditions.

Interestingly, we observed elevated ssGSEA scores for the two SMARCD3 signatures in non-responding patient tumors (Student’s t test, p value ≤ 0.05). Although the pre-treatment signature was consistently elevated in non-responding tumors across conditions (Figures 4C and 4D), ssGSEA scores derived from the pAKT marker did not reach statistical significance in the post-treatment setting (p value = 0.101). We also observed elevated post-treatment signature scores in non-responding tumors (Figures 4C and 4D) but did not reach statistical significance when stratified by the KI67 marker (p value = 0.102). Elevated SMARCD3 signature scores in non-responding patient tumors mirrored patterns observed in everolimus-resistant cell lines, indicative of their clinical and therapeutic relevance (Figure 4B). Moreover, we applied ARACNe-AP and VIPER analyses to DEGs from pre-treatment patient samples, classified using either the pAKT or KI67 markers. Notably, SMARCD3 signatures were consistently enriched in non-responding patient tumors, regardless of classification method (Figures S4C and S4D).

Inhibition of SMARCD3 and the SWI/SNF complex impacts genes involved in MAPK and insulin/IGF1R signaling

To further validate the regulatory role of SMARCD3, we analyzed previously published microarray profiles of immortalized human mammary epithelial cells treated in duplicates with three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) pools targeting SMARCD3.61 The original study used a GenePix 400B fluorescent scanner to measure ratio intensity measurements (siRNA-treated cells vs. empty vector). To confirm SMARCD3 knockdown, we averaged duplicate measurements across the three siRNAs and observed variable reductions in SMARCD3 expression (Figure S5A). We next performed one-class significance analysis of microarrays (SAMs) and identified 973 downregulated genes, which were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology database. Notably, we observed enrichment of several pathways involved in MAPK signaling, insulin signaling, and apoptosis (Figure S5B). Further, SMARCD3 knockdown cells displayed altered expression of key genes, including IGF1R, CASP7, CDKN1A, MAP2K1/2 (MEK2), and MAPK1/3 (ERK1/2), which correlated with the level of SMARCD3 expression (Figure S5C).

SMARCD3 is an integral component of the SWI/SNF complex. While the SWI/SNF complex is key epigenetic regulator controlling state-specific cellular programs, it is not known whether SMARCD3 possesses direct DNA-binding capacity and ability to function as transcriptional regulator beyond its role in the SWI/SNF complex.62,63 Therefore, we focused our effort on analyzing another gene expression dataset with SWI/SNF perturbation. We analyzed transcriptomic profiles from prostate cancer64 and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)65 cells treated with two distinct SWI/SNF inhibitors (AU-15330 or BRM014, respectively). In both cancer cell models, SWI/SNF inhibition led to consistent downregulation of IGF1R and MAP2K1 and upregulation of CASP7, independent of cancer type or SWI/SNF inhibitor (Figures S5D–S5G). Further, pathway analysis revealed deactivation of RAS/MAPK, IGF1R, and PI3K/AKT signaling upon inhibitor treatment (Figures S5D–S5G). Of note, enrichment of the SMARCD3 pre-treatment signature was reduced in BRM014-treated NSCLC cells (Figure S5G). These findings reinforce the association between SMARCD3 expression and activation of growth-factor signaling pathways observed in our everolimus-resistant cells (Figures 2A, 2B, and 3C).

SMARCD3 regulon signatures reveal vulnerability to MEK1/2 inhibitors

Previous reports demonstrate that experimentally generated pathway signatures can elucidate underlying mechanisms essential to oncogenic state development and predict sensitivity to anti-cancer drugs.10,30,66,67,68 We sought to determine whether the two SMARCD3 signatures could reveal vulnerability to existing anti-cancer drugs. Thus, we incorporated an approach to identify anti-cancer drug sensitivity in resistant cells by leveraging the SMARCD3 signatures and pharmacogenomic profiles of 926 cancer cell lines from the GDSC dataset,41 which is outlined in Figure 5A.

We computed ssGSEA scores using the RMA-normalized microarray GDSC cell line data and the SMARCD3 signatures. To assess associations between pathway activity and drug response, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between SMARCD3 signature scores and area under the curve (AUC) values across all cell lines for each anti-cancer drug. After correcting for FDR, anti-cancer drugs were ranked based on Pearson coefficient and significance. Drug candidates with top negative Pearson coefficients were of interest, representing patterns of cell lines with high SMARCD3 signature phenotype and sensitivity to a particular drug. The pre-treatment signature revealed several known MEK1/2 inhibitors within the top 10 drug rankings (Figure 5B). These included trametinib, PD0325901 (mirdametinib), refametinib, and selumetinib.69,70,71,72 Interestingly, the post-treatment signature again showed the same MEK1/2 inhibitors (Figure 5C). Figures 5D and 5E show GDSC cancer cell line drug sensitivity profiles against ssGSEA scores for the two SMARCD3 signatures. The consistent high rankings of MEK1/2 inhibitors across the SMARCD3 signatures indicate potential sensitivity to these inhibitors.

To assess the robustness and generalizability of our findings, we analyzed pharmacogenomic data from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE).73,74,75,76 The CTRP_v1 dataset included 222 CCLE cell lines treated with 338 compounds, while CTRP_v2 contained drug sensitivity profiles for 803 cell lines treated with 536 compounds. Pearson correlations were computed between ssGSEA scores of the SMARCD3 regulon pre-signature and drug AUC values. In CTRP_v1, selumetinib ranked among the top 15 compounds with negative correlations (p = 0.02, Figure S6J), indicating potential sensitivity of everolimus-resistant cells to this MEK1/2 inhibitor. This result is promising given that selumetinib was the only MEK1/2 inhibitor in this dataset. Of note, sirolimus (rapamycin) showed positive correlations, suggesting cross-resistance in everolimus-resistant cells. In CTRP_v2, the B-RAF inhibitor PLX-4032 (vemurafenib) ranked among the top candidates (p = 5.45 × 10−5, Figure S6J), consistent with B-RAF acting upstream of MEK1/2. Selumetinib and trametinib also displayed negative correlations, though these did not reach statistical significance. Temsirolimus, another rapamycin derivative, showed positive correlations, indicating that resistance may extend to related mTOR inhibitors.

Trametinib, a MEK1/2 inhibitor, overcomes everolimus resistance in ER+BC cells

Our drug sensitivity approach strongly suggested that enrichment of the SMARCD3 signatures exposes vulnerability to MEK1/2 inhibition (Figure 5). Thus, we hypothesized the following: (1) MEK1/2 inhibitor monotherapy decreases proliferation relative to everolimus alone in resistant cells, and (2) everolimus and MEK1/2 inhibitor combination treatment further decreases resistant cell proliferation. To test these hypotheses, we validated the effectiveness of trametinib monotherapy or in combination with everolimus in CAMA1-, MCF7-, and T47D-sensitive cells labeled with Venus yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) or resistant cells labeled with mCherry (Texas Red) fluorescent protein using clonogenic assays. Briefly, cells were treated with everolimus, MEK1/2 inhibitor, or everolimus in combination with MEK1/2 inhibitor. Media containing drug combinations were replaced every 3 days, and viability and fluorescence signal intensity measurements were taken after 14 days (Figures 6 and S6A–S6I).

Viability and fluorescence intensity measurements indicated that resistant cells displayed a significant decrease in proliferation after trametinib treatment compared to everolimus alone across all cell lines (Welch t test, p ≤ 0.01) (Figures 6B and 6C). In contrast, sensitive cells were less affected by trametinib when compared to everolimus single agent. However, both sensitive and resistant cell lines showed no difference or increased resistance to mirdametinib or refametinib compared to everolimus alone (Figures S6A–S6I). Next, we examined the effect of everolimus in combination with MEK1/2 inhibitor on cell proliferation. Strikingly, the combination of everolimus and trametinib significantly decreased resistant cancer cell proliferation with respect to everolimus alone (Welch t test, p ≤ 0.01) (Figure 6). This decrease in resistant cell proliferation was also observed in drug combinations with mirdametinib or refametinib (Figures S6A–S6I). Further, CAMA1- and T47D-sensitive cells showed modest decreases in cancer cell proliferation when treated with everolimus plus MEK1/2 inhibitor combination when compared to everolimus alone, which is explained by the degree of sensitivity of these cells to everolimus monotherapy (Figures 6 and S6A–S6I). However, MCF7-sensitive cells did show a strong decrease in proliferation under the same conditions (Figures 6 and S6A–S6I). Finally, both sensitive and resistant cells experienced decreased proliferation when treated with everolimus in combination with any of the three MEK1/2 inhibitors relative to MEK1/2 inhibitor alone (Welch t test, p ≤ 0.05) (Figures 6 and S6A–S6I). These results demonstrate that resistant cells exhibit sensitivity to trametinib compared to everolimus single agent, and sensitivity is partially restored with everolimus and MEK1/2 inhibitor combination.

Everolimus and trametinib combination treatment decreases proliferation of ER+BC patient-derived cells

To validate the effects of everolimus and trametinib combination therapy, we analyzed primary patient-derived tumor samples from four metastatic ER+BC patients (P1, P2, P3, and P5; Figure S6K). Patient-derived cells serve as a more suitable model for evaluating drug responses while enhancing both biological relevance and translational value.77,78 We selected these samples based on progression on hormonal therapy and subsequent treatment history that included everolimus.79 3D spheroid assays were performed by treating samples with everolimus, trametinib, or their combination for 4 days, followed by cell proliferation measurements (Figure S6K). We observed two distinct everolimus-response patterns emerged: P2 and P5 were more sensitive, with everolimus monotherapy reducing proliferation by 56.7% and 42.7%, respectively, while P1 and P3 showed modest reductions of 22.6% and 26.3%, respectively (Figures 6D and 6E). These patterns mirrored our in vitro models, where P2 and P5 resembled everolimus-sensitive cells, and P1 and P3 aligned with resistant profiles. Trametinib monotherapy had little effect on P2 and P5 but reduced proliferation in P1 and P3 by 31.9% and 22.1%, respectively. Importantly, the combination of everolimus and trametinib outperformed everolimus alone in P1 and P3, consistent with our in vitro observation that resistant cells show enhanced sensitivity to trametinib, either alone or in combination.

Discussion

Patients diagnosed with advanced ER+BC who progressed on non-steroidal aromatase inhibitor treatment exhibited prolonged PFS after everolimus plus exemestane combination regimens.17,18,80 However, these patients did not benefit in overall survival.19 This outcome highlights the importance of identifying resistant signaling traits that emerge with mTOR treatment and in knowing which patients would benefit from mTOR inhibitors before starting treatment. To date, there are no systematic guidelines or effective treatment strategies for patients who become refractory to everolimus.22,29,30,31 Therefore, our objectives were to perform a detailed systems-level characterization of the everolimus-resistant state and reveal key context-specific regulon signatures linked to dysregulated meta-phenotypes, which may elucidate novel molecular vulnerabilities.

The use of cell-context-specific transcriptomic information to reconstruct relationships between key regulon networks and meta-phenotypes is becoming increasingly promising in understanding aberrant transcriptional programs distinct between cell states.57,81,82 Importantly, our analyses revealed pre- and post-treatment SMARCD3 signatures enriched in resistant cells and in tumors of ER+BC patients refractory to everolimus treatment. SMARCD3, also known as BAF60C, is a non-catalytic subunit of the large switch/sucrose-nonfermentable (SWI/SNF) complex responsible for chromatin remodeling, thereby controlling state-specific cellular programs depending on unique SWI/ZNF assemblies.62,63 Ferguson and colleagues identified high SMARCD3 expression in advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tumors, which was responsible for regulating biological programs involved in resistance to chemotherapy.83 However, a previous study found decreased mRNA expression in malignant tumor samples relative to normal and a marker of recurrence in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Breast Invasive Carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) dataset.84 These findings contrast with our context-specific analyses demonstrating increased SMARCD3 expression and activity in everolimus-resistant cells, which may be explained by the comparison of breast tumors to normal samples and the diversity of histologic subtypes included in the TCGA-BRCA dataset.85 The observation that everolimus-resistant cells display increased SMARCD3 regulon activity in our study may be a distinct response to everolimus resistance, highlighting the importance of incorporating precision oncology methods for patient stratification.

Interestingly, we observed IGF2 and ESR1 as gene members of both SMARCD3 signatures and their upregulation in resistant cells independent of treatment conditions. These results agree with previous reports showing IGF2 and IGF1R overexpression involved in anti-cancer drug resistance in various cancers, including PI3K/mTOR pathway inhibitors.51,86,87 Further, we observed enrichment of RAS/MAPK/ERK pathway in resistant cells, which are reportedly activated in response to mTORC1 inhibition and well known to be downstream of IGF1R signaling.28,88 These findings agree with reports demonstrating horizontal inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling enhancing anti-cancer effects (summarized in Figure S6L).89,90

The role of ESR1 activation is strongly associated with acquired resistance to endocrine therapy and cancer progression,91 as well as activation of PI3K/Akt92 and MAPK/ERK93 signaling. Through immunoprecipitation pull-down assays, SMARCD3 was found to interact with various nuclear receptors and TFs, including ESR1.94 Although nuclear-initiated steroid signaling (NISS) between 17b-estradiol (E2) and ESR1 binding is responsible for the majority of ESR1 signaling, several reports discuss non-genomic E2-induced membrane-initiated steroid signaling (MISS) events that quickly activate several molecules including IGF1R and HRAS.95,96,97,98,99,100 Previous studies reported synergistic receptor signaling crosstalk between ESR1 and IGF1R leading to cancer cell proliferation.101,102 Thus, it may be speculated that resistant cells, apart from the classical E2-mediated ESR1 NISS, utilize non-genomic MISS E2-ESR1 signals to quickly activate IGF1R to enhance growth-factor signals through PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways thereby contributing to everolimus resistance.

Importantly, we identified vulnerability in the SMARCD3 signatures to several known MEK1/2 inhibitors, which agrees with a previous report showing increased pAKT and pERK in gastric cancer cells overexpressing SMARCD3.103 We show that resistant cells are more sensitive to trametinib relative to everolimus monotherapy and that everolimus and trametinib combination partially restores sensitivity. This additive effect is more apparent in the CAMA1- and T47D-resistant cell lines, where the effect of the combination treatment in resistant cells is notably still less than the effect of everolimus monotherapy on their sensitive counterparts. Interestingly, the effect of the combination treatment on MCF7 resistant cells was greater than the effect of everolimus monotherapy on MCF7-sensitive cells, suggesting additive and synergistic drug effects between mTOR and MEK1/2 inhibitor combination treatment.104,105

A phase 1b study (NCT00955773) investigating safe and effective dose regimens for everolimus and trametinib combination in advanced solid tumors reported partial response in only 5 of the 67 patients.106 However, this trial did not consider molecular characterization with respect to patient stratification and was not specific to ER+BC. While concurrent and intermittent combinations were examined, the study did not address sequential regimen strategies for the two targeted therapies, which our results suggest may be beneficial for patients that develop resistance to everolimus. Although we recognize that phase 1 clinical trials typically do not use molecular stratification strategies, phase 1b trials may leverage molecular data to explore biomarker-response relationships to identify responsive subgroups for guiding patient stratification in phase 2 stages.107,108

Further, we found that everolimus in combination with mirdametinib or refametinib also decreased resistant cell proliferation. These inhibitors are currently not Food and Drug Administration approved for anti-cancer treatment, and their potential as single-agents or in combination with everolimus in the preclinical setting remains scarce with respect to ER+BC. Still, a recent phase 2 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02096471) and several preclinical studies found mirdametinib and refametinib treatment effective in cancer.109,110,111 Our findings support further study of trametinib alone or with everolimus, and of everolimus with mirdametinib or refametinib, in ER+BC resistant to everolimus.

In all, our approach demonstrates the use ER+BC cell transcriptomes in elucidating how resistant cell states circumvent everolimus-driven mTORC1 inhibition through meta-phenotype and regulon network analyses. Our study strongly suggests that everolimus-resistant cells shift toward MAPK/ERK pathway activation, which we targeted after identifying SMARCD3 signature vulnerabilities. Our approach demonstrates the value of context-specific transcriptomic profiles leading to MEK1/2 inhibitor phenotypic sensitivity in ER+BC cells resistant to everolimus. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the SMARCD3 signatures could be used to identify patients that are likely resistant or non-responsive to everolimus. Furthermore, patients with high SMARCD3 signature scores could be candidates for receiving MEK1/2 inhibitors as an add-on therapy.

Limitations of the study

Important limitations include reliance on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) from only three ER+BC cell lines (CAMA1, MCF7, and T47D) sensitive or resistant to everolimus and a small patient dataset of 21 matched pre- and post-treatment profiles.29 Patients were post-menopausal with operable early-stage ER+BC: 12 patients were ER+/PR+/Her2−, 2 patients were ER+/PR+/HER2+, 3 patients were ER+/PR−/HER1, and 4 patients were ER+/PR−/Her2+. Although subtypes varied, SMARCD3 expression and regulon activity were consistently elevated in the non-responding tumors. Markers such as Ki67 and pAKT remain important surrogates for therapeutic response.112,113 Additional ER+BC cell lines sensitive or resistant to everolimus may identify regulons with increased clinical relevance. Moreover, we identified SMARCD3 regulons in both cell lines and patient transcriptomes. Machine learning approaches combining both data sources may enhance prediction of response.30 Our findings strongly implicate SMARCD3 activity in everolimus resistance, with resistant cells showing vulnerability to MEK1/2 inhibitors. Whether MEK1/2 inhibitor treatment inverts SMARCD3 phenotypes remains clear.114 Further transcriptomic profiling following MEK1/2 inhibition may clarify its role. Of note, compensatory mechanisms such as EP400/TIP60 may compensate for SWI/SNF loss.65

Experimental model and study participant details

Development of everolimus-resistant ER+BC cell lines

CAMA1 (ATCC HTB-21) and MCF7 (ATCC HTB-22) cell lines were maintained in Dulbeco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. 12306C) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution at 37°C and 5% CO2, while T47D (ATCC HTB-133) cells were maintained in Roswell Park memorial Institute (RPMI) media (Gibco, Cat. No. 11875-093) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were authenticated via STR profiling (Laragen, Inc) and tested for mycoplasma contamination with a Mycoplasma detection kit (Myco Alert Kit, Lonza). MCF7 and T47D everolimus resistant cells were developed through long-term culture by treating the respective parental cells with 100nM continuous dosages of everolimus (Selleckchem, Cat. No. S1120) until resistance was achieved. Similarly, CAMA1 resistant cells were generated with continuous dosages of 50nM everolimus. Fresh media containing everolimus was changed every 3 days. We observed resistance to the drug roughly 6–12 months after initiation of everolimus treatment, which was confirmed by comparing the everolimus dose-response with respective parental cells via CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega Corporation Cat. No. G7573). Resistance cells were maintained in complete culture medium complemented with 100nM everolimus for MCF7 and T47D while 50nM everolimus for CAMA1 resistant cells. Sensitive cells were labeled with Venus (LeGO-V2 addgene Cat. No. 27340) and resistant cells were labeled with mCherry (LeGO-C2 Addgene Cat. No. 27339) fluorescent proteins, as described previously.79,115,116

Clinical patient data processing and analysis

We leveraged micro-array gene expression profiles of pre-treatment and post-treatment tumor samples collected from a cohort of 21 post-menopausal women diagnosed with ER+BC treated with 5mg everolimus.29 We used the R package GEOquery (v2.69.1) to obtain the expression data using NCBI GEO accession GSE119262. The patient samples were profiled in duplicates using the Illumina HumaneRef-8 v2 Expression BeadChip technology and classified as responder or non-responder based on two distinct classification methods: changes in cytoplasmic phospho-Akt (pAKT) and MKI67 marker expression via immunohistochemistry. Non-normalized gene expression profiles were filtered for protein-coding genes using the annotables package (v0.2.0). We examined gene-level expression profiles by taking the sum expression for gene symbols mapped to multiple probe IDs to account for probes targeting different gene isoforms. Micro-array profiles were quantile normalized using the R package beadarray (v2.50.0). Low quality probes were removed, and the data was batch-adjusted using the sva package (v3.48.0). The resulting matrix contained 13,700 genes and 84 samples (21 patients with pre- and post-treatment samples in duplicate).

To identify differentially expressed genes between responder and non-responder in pre-treatment or post-treatment tumor samples for each of the two classification methods (pAKT and MKI67), we implemented Significance Analysis of Microarrays (SAMs) as described in the original study using the R package siggenes (v1.74.0). A Wilcoxon signed rank statistic was used as the test statistic and Delta values with FDR ≤0.05 were chosen to identify differentially expressed genes. With respect to the pAKT classification method, we chose a Delta value of 0.960541 with corresponding FDR = 0.0459 resulting in 1096 differentially expressed genes between responder and non-responder samples in the pre-treatment setting. In the post-treatment analysis, we chose Delta = 0.93386 with FDR = 0.0456 resulting in 471 differentially expressed genes. In terms of the MKI67 classification method, we chose Delta = 0.991241 with FDR = 0.0498 giving rise to 1737 differentially expressed genes between pre-treatment responder and non-responder groups. For post-treatment samples, we chose Delta = 0.940408 with FDR = 0.0438 generating a list of 687 differentially expressed genes. Further, we calculated ssGSEA scores for the SMARCD3 resistance regulon signature using the patient quantile-normalized micro-array expression profiles with the R package gsva (v1.48.3). To determine a significant difference in ssGSEA scores between responder and non-responder groups, we used Student’s t test. Shapiro-Wilk’s test was used for normality confirmation and F-test was used to confirm equal variance.

Pharmacogenomics cell line data processing

We retrieved micro-array expression and drug sensitivity (AUC) profiles from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC)41 Website (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/downloads/bulk_download). The CEL files corresponding to non-normalized micro-array expression profiles for 1018 GDSC cell lines were stored in the working directory where the files were read and inserted into an AffyBatch and RMA-normalized using the affy package (1.78.2). To convert the Affymetrix Human Genome U219 probe names to gene symbols, we used the DAVID117 bioinformatics Gene ID conversion tool (https://www.davidbioinformatics.nih.gov). For genes with duplicate symbols, we took the mean expression. The cell lines present in the expression data were matched to corresponding cell lines present in the area-under-the-curve (AUC) profiles, which resulted in 926 cell lines with matching micro-array expression and AUC responses across 395 anti-cancer drugs. Anti-cancer drugs with multiple AUC response datasets were treated as separate entries and missing AUC values were imputed with nearest neighbor averaging as implemented in the R package impute (v1.74.1). CCLE RNA-seq data was downloaded from the depmap portal (https://depmap.org/portal/data_page/?tab=allData) and the CTRP v1 (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v1/) and CTRP v2 (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v2/) drug sensitivity profiles were downloaded from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal website.

Patient sample collection

We obtained malignant fluid (patient #’s 1, 2 and 5) or thoracentesis (patient # 3) samples from 4 female patients diagnosed with ER + breast cancer whose treatment history included progressing while on everolimus treatment. The experiments in this study were conducted using samples exclusively from female subjects diagnosed with breast cancer, which reflects the most commonly affected population. As a result, the influence of sex or gender on the study outcomes could not be assessed. The sample numbers for this study were not predetermined; instead, all eligible samples meeting the criteria of treatment with everolimus were utilized to maximize the use of existing resources. These samples were collected under informed consent and ethical compliance under Institutional Review Board (IRB) #07047 and #17334 at City of Hope. Demographic information for these patients was not used for selection criteria.

Patient-derived cancer cell isolation

First, cells were pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Next red blood cells were removed from the samples by lysis in Tris-ammonium chloride buffer (17 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 135 mM ammonium chloride); previously described by Nath et al. 2021, or by magnetic depletion with the EasySep Red Blood Cell Depletion Reagent (StemCell Technologies) and diluting cell pellets in 2% FBS in phosphate-buffered saline. Samples were then incubated with 50 μl Depletion Reagen/mL for 5 min at room temperature followed by two 5-min rounds of incubation on EasyEight magnet (Stem Cell Technolgies). Primary human breast cancer cells were purified by magnetic depletion with the EasySep Dead Cell Removal kit (Annexin V, Stem Cell Technologies #17899) to remove dead cells. Then the EasySep CD45 Depletion Kit II (StemCell Technologies #17898) was used to remove immune cells, as previously described.79

Method details

RNA sequencing of sensitive and resistant cells

Parental (sensitive) or resistant CAMA1, MCF7 and T47D cell lines were plated in triplicates at ∼500,000 cells/well in 6-well plates in triplicates (Table S3). After 24 h of incubation, cells were treated with 10 nM everolimus or DMSO (Sigma, Cat. No. D2650) for 12 h. We used 10nM everolimus for RNA-sequencing experiments based on IC50 values determined from in-house viability assays (Figure S1A). Although the IC50 for our cell lines was approximately 1nM, we selected the higher concentration of 10nM to ensure sufficient treatment efficacy and capture robust transcriptional response. Next, cells were trypsinized and washed to collect the. RNA was isolated using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 74136) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA-seq libraries were generated with an Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In total, 36 samples were sequenced on an Illumina Nova Seq6000 with 2 × 150 pair-end reads, giving rise to ∼25 million reads per sample. Sequencing was performed at the High Throughput Genomics Core at Huntsman Cancer Institute of University of Utah. Samples were pre-processed using BETSY,118 which aligned reads to human genome 19 (hg19) using STAR aligner (v2.7.0) and quantified transcripts using RSEM (v1.3.1). Next, we removed genes with low variance and accounted for differences in library depth across samples with trimmed mean of M-values normalization.119

Differentially expressed genes

The initial count matrix after alignment and quantification contained 19371 genes, which was filtered for known protein-coding genes within the GRCH37 assembly using the R package annotables (v0.2.0), leaving 18,362 protein-coding genes. We next filtered out genes with low variance across samples and controlled for differences in library depth using edgeR (v3.42.4).120 Genes with low variance were removed with the “filterByExpr” function using phenotypic state and treatment condition as grouping factors, leaving 11,680 genes in the dataset. Trimmed-mean of M values (TMM) was used to control differences in library depth between samples using the “calcNormFactors” function. Counts were then extracted from the edgeR object and log2 Count-per-million (log2CPM) transformed.

For statistical testing, the log2CPM gene matrix was first converted to long format were each sample was pivoted with respect to the number of genes and expression values, resulting in a data frame with 420,480 rows (36 samples ∗ 11,680 genes) and two columns (“Gene” and “Sample”). Sample names were assigned to generate additional columns for each sample. For example, “CAMA1_everR_DMSO_rep_1” sample name was partitioned into “Cell line”, “phenotypic state”, “treatment condition”, and “replicate”. To account for differences between cell lines, we implemented linear mixed-effects models using the R package lme4 (v1.1–35.5). The linear mixed-effects model equation for pre-treatment and post-treatment comparisons was expressed in the following form:

yi = log2-transformed counts-per-million value of the i-th, β0 = intercept, β1(state1) = effect of state1 on yi, β2(treatment2) = effect of treatment2 on yi, β3(state ∗ treatment3) = state1 and treatment2 interaction effect on yi, (1|cell line) = random intercept for each cell line, εi = residual error for yi. To control for multiple comparisons, we adjusted p-values using the Benjamini & Hochberg (FDR) method and genes with FDR ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. For pre-treatment comparisons, we set parental sensitive cells treated with DMSO as references in the model, allowing for identification of 3,394 differentially expressed gene between sensitive and resistant cells across cell lines at baseline. In contrast, for post-treatment comparisons reference levels were set to the resistant phenotypic state and everolimus for treatment condition, which revealed 3,436 differentially expressed genes between sensitive and resistant cells after everolimus treatment. To identify differentially expressed ligand-receptors pairs, we utilized the curated dataset generated by Ramilowski and colleagues.49

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis

To perform Single-sample Gene set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA), we used the GSVA (v1.48.3) package.34,121 We used the processed count matrix containing 11,680 protein-coding genes as input for the analysis. Gene signatures for 50 Hallmark and 6,366 curated (C2) gene-sets were retrieved from MSigDB using the msigdbr package (v7.5.1). For ssGSEA, we used method = “ssgsea” and kcdf = “Gaussian” arguments. To identify statistically significant enriched gene-sets in both pre-treatment and post-treatment settings, long-format data preparation was performed and implemented linear mixed-effects models with fixed-effects and random-effect identical to the differentially expressed gene analyses described above. Here, however, the dependent variable, yi, represents ssGSEA scores for the i-th gene-set. Statistical significance was also adjusted for FDR and gene-sets with FDR ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. Next, we included gene-sets derived from Hallmark, Biocarta, REACTOME, and KEGG databases for downstream analyses. Pre-treatment comparisons between sensitive and resistant cells resulted in 288 differentially enriched gene-sets while 209 gene-sets were identified post-treatment. Heatmaps showing clustering of statistically significant gene-sets were generated using the R package ComplexHeatmap (v2.16.0).

Identifying meta-phenotypes

To condense the large amount of phenotypic information generated by ssGSEA and reveal meta-phenotypes differentially active between sensitive and resistant cell states, we performed meta-analyses in the pre-treatment and post-treatment settings to generate one overall test of significance and estimate for each meta-phenotype. First, we generated a repository consisting of 1185 manually annotated gene-sets based on biological process or signaling pathway, which we refer to as meta-phenotypes. We leveraged background information provided by the Hallmark, Biocarta, REACTOME, or KEGG databases for each gene-set when assigning meta-phenotypes. Using this repository, we then assigned meta-phenotypes to 288 statistically significant differentially enriched gene-sets between pre-treatment samples and 209 gene-sets between post-treatment samples identified after fitting ssGSEA scores to linear mixed-effects models. Meta-phenotypes with only one gene-set were removed from further analyses. Further, we considered meta-phenotypes with meta-estimates above the 10th percentile of the distribution for further downstream analyses.

Second, to generate a single test statistic for each meta-phenotype, we combined the FDR values of all constituent gene-set in a meta-phenotype using Fisher’s method.36 Here, the adjusted FDR values obtained from linear mixed-effects models were converted from two-sided to one-sided using the R package metap (v1.8) with the “two2one” function. Next, the converted FDR values were combined by applying Fisher’s sum of logs method with the metap package, generating one chi-squared statistic and the associated FDR value (meta-FDR) for each meta-phenotype. Identification of SMARCD3 resistance regulon signatures

To identify RSS candidates, we first reconstructed transcriptional regulatory networks using ARACNe-AP (Algorithm for the Reconstruction of Accurate Cellular Networks with Adaptive Partitioning) as previously described in the original study.32 Briefly, we used statistically significant differentially expressed genes in pre-treatment or post-treatment samples, and a pre-defined list of transcription factors and co-activators obtained from the GeneOntology database (GO:0003700: “DNA binding transcription factor activity” and GO:0003712 “Transcription co-activator activity”) as input for the algorithm. The list contained a total of 1757 transcription factor gene symbols, however, only those found differentially expressed in pre-treatment or post-treatment settings were used for further analyses: 318 transcription factors for pre-treatment and 323 transcription factors post-treatment. The ARACNe-AP40 standard pipeline was implemented, which involves three major processes (1) estimating mutual information (MI), (2) reconstruction of mutual information networks with bootstrapping, and (3) building a consensus network. ARACNe-AP differs from the original implementation in that it implements an Adaptive Partitioning estimator, instead of a Fixed Bandwidth estimator, when determining the number of discrete bins to divide the gene expression space into. Rather than specifying a pre-set partition size, the AP-estimator divides the space in an adaptive fashion depending on the local distribution of the mean, which is accomplished when a uniform distribution is reached, as given by a chi-squared test. The MI between a transcription factor candidate and a potential target gene is determined by pairwise MI with a Gaussian kernel estimator. The p-value threshold for MI was set at 1e−08 and 100 bootstraps were performed for reconstructing networks. Next, we used the transcription regulon network modules obtained with pre-treatment or post-treatment samples as input for the R package viper (v1.32.0)33 to predict resistance regulon signature activity in resistant cells.

We selected the SMARCD3 resistance regulon signature for further analysis based on the following findings: (1) SMARCD3 RNA-seq expression was the only transcription factor candidate consistently elevated across all resistant cell lines, and in non-responding tumors of post-menopausal women diagnosed with ER+BC refractory to everolimus.29 (2) The SMARCD3 resistance regulon signature was the only signature with ssGSEA scores elevated in both resistant cell lines and non-responding patient tumors. (3) Analyses using patient micro-array expression profiles also revealed SMARCD3 signatures. To link signature gene members to meta-phenotypes, we first annotated genes with meta-phenotypes assigned to their parent gene-set present in the meta-phenotype repository described earlier. For example, all gene members constituting gene-sets within the growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype were assigned to the growth-factor signaling meta-phenotype. Similarly, all gene members of gene-sets within the immune response meta-phenotype were assigned to the immune response meta-phenotype. Genes appearing in growth-factor signaling and immune response were labeled with both meta-phenotypes. We then filtered for gene members observed in the SMARCD3 signatures.

Identification of MEK1/2 inhibitors extending sensitivity to SMARCD3 regulon signature

To identify phenotypic vulnerabilities in the SMARCD3 resistance regulon signature, we first leveraged expression profiles of 926 GDSC cancer cell lines described above to perform ssGSEA against the SMARCD3 signature identified in pre-treatment or post-treatment samples using GSVA (v1.48.3). Next, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between SMARCD3 signature scores in the GDSC cancer cell lines and their corresponding AUC responses to a specific anti-cancer drug. To accomplish this, we generated a long-format data frame containing pre-treatment or post-treatment SMARCD3 signature scores, AUC response and corresponding anti-cancer drug for each GDSC cancer cell line. We then looped through the data frame on a per-drug-basis to calculate linear correlations between ssGSEA scores and AUC responses. p-values were adjusted for family-wise error rate (FWER) using the Bonferroni correction method as implemented in the stats R package (v4.3.0). Anti-cancer drugs with FWER ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. Drug candidates were then ranked according to Pearson coefficients and the top candidates with negative correlations were considered for further analyses, which revealed several known MEK1/2 inhibitors. Top negative correlations represented patterns where cancer cell lines displayed high enrichment of the SMARCD3 signature and sensitivity (low AUC) to the candidate anti-cancer drug.

Cell maintenance

CAMA1 and MCF7 cells were maintained in 15 mL Dulbeco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) media (Gibco, Cat. No. 11995-065), 10% FBS (Sigma, Cat. No. F4135) and 1× anti-antimycotic solution (Gibco, Cat. No. 15240). T47D cell lines were maintained in 15 mL of Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) media (Gibco, Cat. No. 11875-093), 10% FBS, and 1× anti-antimycotic solution. Cells were kept at 37°C and 5% CO2 until confluency was reached, at which time culture media was removed and cells washed with 2 mL trypsin/EDTA (0.25%, Gibco, Cat. No 25200). Cells were treated with 2 mL of fresh trypsin and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. Culture media was added to neutralize trypsin, and cells were transferred to 10 mL tubes and centrifuged at room temperature and 300 × g for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in culture media solution at 0.5 - 1M cells/mL. Maintenance was performed on a weekly basis or as needed.

Western blot

For Western blotting, cells were first washed with PBS and then lysed with ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Scientific). BCA (Pierce) was used to determine protein concentration. Total protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)/4–20% Tris-glycine Gel (Bio Rad). Proteins were then transferred to Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using the iBlot Dry Blotting system (Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Next, membranes were immunoblotted over-night with antibodies against anti-IGF1R (#9750) and anti-phosphor-IGF1R Try1131 (#80732) purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Experiments were performed in triplicates and representative results are shown.

Cell viability assay

To confirm resistance to everolimus, we performed cell viability assays to measure the difference in dose-response between parental sensitive and resistant cells after increasing dosages (0–1000 nM) of everolimus (Selleckchem, Cat. No. S1120) treatment for 4 days. To this end, we split cells 2 days before plating to ascertain cells were captured in the logarithmic (log) growth phase. Roughly 2000 cells were plated in each well of a 384-well flat bottom, black walled plate (Corning, Cat. No. 3764) using Tecan Fluent 780 and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 overnight. The next day, cells were treated with increasing drug dilutions of everolimus using a Tecan Fluent 780 and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 4 days. A CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega Corporation, Cat. No. G7573) was used to measure drug response according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Measurements were normalized to respective cells treated with DMSO control. Dose curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism (10.2.3) software and differences in slopes were determined by fitting the data to 4-parameter log-logistic models as implemented in the drc (v3.0-1) package. Cell viability experiments were performed in triplicates and representative plots are shown.

Clonogenic assay

We validated several known MEK1/2 inhibitor drug candidates as 1 μM monotherapy or in combination with 1nM everolimus for 14 days in both sensitive and resistant cell states for CAMA1, MCF7 and T47D ER+BC cell lines. To achieve this, we leveraged ER+BC cell lines previously labeled in our lab; sensitive cell states express YFP (Venus fluorescence) while resistant cells express Texas Red (mCherry fluorescence). We purchased MEK1/2 inhibitor candidates from Selleckchem: trametinib (Cat. No. S2673), PD0325901 (Cat. No. S1036) and Refametinib (Cat. No. S1089). Cells were plated at a density of 160 cells/well in a 96-well flat, clear bottom microplate (Corning, Cat. No. 3904) and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 overnight. To confirm cells were plated properly, 96-well plates were imaged the following morning using a Cytation 5 imager (Biotek Instruments), which measured signaling intensity from brightfield, YFP and Texas Red channels. Images of the entire area of each well were taken. Next, drug solutions containing either DMSO (control), 1nM everolimus alone or in combination with 1 μM MEK1/2 inhibitor were added to the cells and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. Media containing respective drug solutions was replaced every 3–4 days. On day 14, cells were again imaged as described above, capturing the entire area of each well. We used the Gen5 3.14 software, as previously described,116 to process the raw fluorescence data. In brief, for each well, 5 × 4 montage images were stitched followed by Z-projection using focus stacking with size of maximum filter parameter set to 11 pixels. The total intensity of the image was then calculated. Once imaging was complete, we also obtained growth measurements using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega Corporation, Cat. No. G7573) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Measurements were then normalized to respective cells treated with DMSO control. Clonogenic assay experiments were performed in triplicates and representative images are shown.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Experiments performed in this study were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using Rstudio 4.4.2 for comparing expression profiles using linear mixed-effects models to identify differentially enriched features, Student’s or Welch’s t-test when comparing cell viability between sensitive and resistant states in clonogenic assays or fitting log-logistic models to dose-response curves. Dose-response curve graphs were generated with Graphpad Prism 10.4.1. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Detailed description of the statistical approach used for the analyses can be found in the method details section, while statistical tests used in the experiment, number of samples, definition of center, dispersion and precision measures are included in figure legends.
