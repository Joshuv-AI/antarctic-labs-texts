Immunotherapy in Peanut Allergy: The Present and the Future
Authors: Kangmo Ahn
Published: 2025

Peanut allergy (PA) represents one of the most significant food allergies globally, with marked geographical differences in prevalence. In Western countries, PA prevalence increased significantly from the 1990s to the early 2000s. For example, in a study comparing 2 birth cohorts conducted at different time periods in the same region of the Isle of Wight, UK, the prevalence at age 3 years rose from 0.5% in the 1989 birth cohort to 1.2% in the 2001 cohort, and at age 10 years from 0.6% to 1.4%.12 In the United States, the prevalence of PA among children under 18 years increased from 0.4% in 1997 to 1.4% in 2008, with subsequent studies by Gupta et al. 4 reporting 2.2% in 2018.3 In Australia, 2 cohort studies indicated that the prevalence of PA in infants was 3.1% in 2007–2011 and 2.6% in 2018–2019, with no statistical difference between them.5 In contrast, the prevalence of PA in the Asia-Pacific region remains substantially lower than in Western countries, with reported rates of 0.22% in Korean schoolchildren, 0.1%–0.3% in Singaporean infants, 0.3%–0.5% in children from Hong Kong.678 Despite these regional differences, peanuts are a major cause of food-induced anaphylaxis worldwide, including Korea.79101112 Owing to the potential for fatal reactions from accidental ingestion and the low likelihood of natural resolution, there is a clear need for effective treatment of PA.

Oral immunotherapy (OIT) has been extensively investigated as a therapeutic option for PA. In a phase 3 trial (PALISADE clinical trial) involving children aged 4–17 years, 67.2% of whom received AR101 (a peanut-derived investigational biologic OIT drug) passed a 600-mg peanut protein challenge, as compared to 4.0% in the placebo group.13 The IMPACT trial administered 2,000 mg/day of peanut protein to children aged 12 to < 48 months for 134 weeks, with 71% achieving desensitization at a 5,000-mg challenge dose.14 In the POSEIDON trial, children aged 1 to < 4 years who were administered a maintenance dose of standardized peanut allergen powder-dnfp 300 mg/day (Palforzia®; Aimmune Therapeutics, Inc., Brisbane, California, USA) for approximately 12 months resulted in 73.5% of the participants achieving desensitization at a 600-mg challenge dose.15 In contrast, the clinical remission rate following OIT in PA remains much lower than desensitization rate. For example, in a double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized phase 2 study involving participants aged 7–55 years (POISED study), peanut OIT for 104 weeks successfully desensitized 85% of peanut-allergic individuals to 4 g of peanut protein. However, after 52 weeks of discontinuation, the proportion of participants passing double-blind placebo-controlled food challenges declined significantly to 13%, indicating a recurrence of clinical reactivity to peanut in most desensitized individuals.16 In the IMPACT trial, only 21% maintained remission 26 weeks after stopping OIT.15 Regarding safety, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 trials showed that OIT for PA significantly increased anaphylaxis risk and frequency during build-up and maintenance phases compared to no OIT. OIT also increased serious adverse events and non-anaphylactic reactions.17

Epicutaneous immunotherapy (EPIT) was developed as an alternative to OIT due to its ease of use and reduced risk of systemic allergic reactions. It uses skin patches containing peanut protein (Viaskin® Peanut patch; DBV Technologies, Bagneux, France) and was designed to exploit the resident dendritic cells in the skin for efficient processing of low-dose allergens. In the PEPITES trial involving children aged 4–11 years, EPIT for 12 months resulted in improved desensitization to oral peanut challenge compared to placebo (35.3% vs. 13.6%), although the predefined statistical threshold for efficacy was not met.18 The EPITOPE trial in toddlers aged 1–3 years demonstrated that children in the intervention group achieved significantly higher desensitization rates by 12-month daily EPIT than those in the placebo group (67.0% vs. 33.5%). Younger children appear to show more favorable response to EPIT.19 Importantly, both trials in different age groups showed that EPIT is safe and well-tolerated, with most adverse events being localized skin reactions rather than systemic (< 5%).

Transdermal immunotherapy (TDIT) is a noninvasive therapeutic option for food allergies like EPIT. It uses a biodegradable microneedle array patch (MAP) to deliver allergens through the skin. Yun et al. 20 investigated the efficacy of TDIT in a murine model of peanut anaphylaxis. In that study, treatment with MAPs containing peanut protein extract that was incorporated with hyaluronic acid significantly reduced clinical anaphylaxis scores and lowered serum levels of peanut-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) in peanut-sensitized mice compared to controls. TDIT significantly reduced the recruitment of Th2 cells, while increasing Treg and Th1 cells in both the spleen and the jejunum. TDIT reduced Th2 cytokines (interleukin [IL]-4, IL-5, IL-13) and enhanced transforming growth factor-β production in stimulated splenocytes. These findings suggest that TDIT could be a promising treatment option for PA. The therapeutic efficacy of TDIT has also been demonstrated in a murine model of gliadin-induced anaphylaxis.21 In addition, TDIT provides the theoretical advantage of direct allergen delivery via microneedle insertion over EPIT, which delivers allergens via passive diffusion through intact skin. Nonetheless, clinical data directly comparing the delivery efficiency of TDIT with EPIT are currently lacking, and evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of TDIT in humans remains limited. Further clinical validation is needed to confirm its long-term efficacy, safety, tolerability, and real-world adherence.

As an adjunct to OIT, omalizumab (OMA), a humanized anti-IgE monoclonal antibody, can be used to support high-dose desensitization and higher maintenance doses by raising the threshold of peanut protein-tolerated.22 Recently, the OUtMATCH trial demonstrated that OMA monotherapy enabled 67% of multi-food allergic patients to tolerate ≥ 600 mg of peanut protein, compared with only 7% in the placebo group.23 These findings underscore the potential of OMA to enhance both the safety and efficacy of treatment for children with moderate-to-severe PA, either as monotherapy or in combination with OIT.

A recent systematic review by the GA2LEN Food Allergy Guideline Group concluded that sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) may increase tolerance during therapy (RR, 4.7; 95% confidence interval, 1.6–13.8), but the certainty of evidence was low.24 A recent open-label study involving 54 children aged 1–11 years, 70% of children aged 1–11 years achieved desensitization to ≥ 800 mg of peanut protein after 48 months of 4 mg/day SLIT. Adverse events were primarily mild and localized, with no systemic reactions requiring epinephrine.25 In a randomized trial of peanut-allergic children aged 1–4 years (n = 25), those receiving 4 mg/day of peanut SLIT for 36 months had significantly higher rates of desensitization (60% vs. 0%) compared to placebo (n = 25), with a median tolerated dose of 4,443 mg vs. 143 mg. SLIT also showed good efficacy compared to placebo in achieving remission assessed 3 months after treatment discontinuation. The highest rate of desensitization and remission was seen in 1–2-year-old, followed by 2–3-year-old, and 3–4-year-old participants.26 Although SLIT appears safe and effective, more evidence is needed to establish its clinical utility relative to OIT.

In conclusion, the treatment for PA has evolved from sole avoidance to various immunotherapies. While OIT remains the most efficacious, concerns about safety persist. Alternative approaches, including EPIT, TDIT, biologics like OMA, and SLIT offer safer or more tolerable options. The choice of immunotherapy should be individualized based on patient age, disease severity, treatment goals, and risk tolerance. Further studies in diverse populations will help refine treatment strategies and optimize long-term outcomes.
