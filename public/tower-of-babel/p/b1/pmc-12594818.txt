Simultaneous antimicrobial and adsorptive capabilities of a novel surface-modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) for sustainable wastewater remediation
Authors: Batoul Roustaei, Ghodsieh Bagherzade, Hamid Beyzaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are an emerging class of porous materials with remarkable surface area, tunable pore structures, and diverse chemical functionalities. In this study, we reported the green synthesis and comprehensive characterization of a novel modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) derived from 2-aminoterephthalic acid, followed by post-synthetic modification with terephthalaldehyde to improve its adsorption capabilities. The synthesized Cu-MOF exhibited a very high specific surface area (2037.65 m2·g–1, BET), a total pore volume of 0.7465 cm2·g–1 and mesoporosity with an average pore diameter of 29.06 nm. SEM and TEM images showed uniform polyhedral particles with an average particle size of ≈ 85 ± 10 nm, while XRD patterns displayed well-defined diffraction peaks with the most intense reflection at ~ 2θ = 28–29°, confirming high crystallinity and preservation of the MIL-101 topology after modification. Under optimized conditions (10 mg adsorbent, 10 mL solution, room temperature and appropriate pH), the material exhibited high adsorption capacities of 230.1, 165.2, and 187.4 mg·g–1 for crystal violet, methyl orange, and rhodamine B, respectively, attributable to its large porosity and functional surface groups. A plausible mechanism involving electrostatic interaction, π–π stacking, and coordination bonding is proposed for adsorption of dyes onto the modified MOF. The Cu-MOF maintained excellent structural stability and reusability, retaining over 92% of its initial adsorption capacity after five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles, as confirmed by XRD patterns showing no noticeable framework collapse. This highlights its robustness and potential for sustainable wastewater remediation. In addition to dye removal, the material demonstrated antimicrobial activity, with MIC values of 4, 8, 32 and 128 µg/mL for P. aeruginosa, C. albicans, E. coli, and A. fumigatus, respectively, while no inhibition was observed against Gram-positive strains at concentrations up to 4096 µg/mL. The antimicrobial effect is likely attributed to Cu2+ ion release and electrostatic interactions leading to membrane disruption and ROS generation. These results highlight the potential of the synthesized Cu-MOF as a multifunctional and eco-friendly candidate for both wastewater treatment and biomedical applications.Keywords: Adsorption, Antimicrobial evaluation, Copper based-MOF, Dye removalSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Water pollution caused by organic dyes from textiles, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries poses a severe threat to aquatic ecosystems and human health1,2. Conventional dye removal methods such as coagulation, photodegradation, and biological treatments, while widely applied, each suffer from key limitations, including that coagulation and flocculation generate large volumes of secondary sludge requiring further photolysis and photocatalysis, more energy and sophisticated equipment, in addition to the fact that biological treatments are often resistant to synthetic dyes, slow and ineffective3,4. Other industries also release dye-rich effluents into water bodies. These effluents discharge large amounts of synthetic dyes into natural water media, leading to reduced light penetration, inhibition of photosynthesis, and disruption of aquatic biodiversity. Many dyes and their breakdown products are toxic and carcinogenic, for example, azo dyes are degraded into harmful aromatic amines5,6. Therefore, there is an urgent need for advanced alternatives that can overcome these shortcomings by offering high efficiency, low energy demand, and minimal secondary pollution. In recent years, copper-based materials and their composites have been extensively explored for catalytic and adsorptive pollutant removal due to their high redox activity and tunable surface chemistry7–9. MOFs offer high surface area, tunable porosity, and chemical versatility for environmental applications10. Unlike conventional methods, MOFs act as reusable solid adsorbents with minimal sludge formation, and their modular structures allow for targeted functionalization to improve selectivity toward diverse pollutants. Importantly, beyond dye removal, certain MOFs also display intrinsic antimicrobial activity, which makes them attractive for dual applications in water purification11,12. For example, copper-based MOFs have shown inhibitory effects against clinically relevant pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Candida albicans, underscoring their potential for addressing both chemical and biological contamination in a single system. Among MOFs, copper-based organic linkers are especially attractive for dye adsorption, attributable to the redox activity of copper centers and the strong affinity of Cu ions for nitrogen- and oxygen-containing pollutants13. Although Cu-based MOFs and amino-functionalized MOFs have shown promising adsorption efficiency, challenges such as long-term stability, regeneration capacity, and performance in real wastewater still remain critical issues that need to be addressed14–17. Functionalization of MOFs via post-synthetic modification (PSM) enables the incorporation of additional reactive groups, thus improving selectivity and adsorption performance. Despite advances in wastewater treatment, there remains a significant research gap in designing Cu-MOFs that simultaneously integrate multiple functional groups to achieve both efficient dye adsorption and antimicrobial activity. Most previous studies have either focused on adsorption performance or biological activity, but rarely on their combination within other material, especially through the synergistic incorporation of amine and imine functionalities18,19. Recent studies on hybrid materials, such as MgFe₂O₄@CuO nanocomposites5 and MOFs@COFs composites6 highlight their potential for dye removal and multifunctional applications, though challenges in stability and scalability remain.

In this study, a novel Cu-MOF was synthesized using 2-aminoterephthalic acid (NH2-BDC) and further functionalized its surface with terephthalaldehyde (TPA) to introduce imine groups for enhancement of dye binding20,21. The material’s efficacy in removing both cationic and anionic dyes including crystal violet (CV), rhodamine B (RhB), and methyl orange (MO) was assessed under optimized conditions. Additionally, the antifungal and antibacterial activities of the prepared Cu-MOF were tested against a range of pathogenic strains. To the best of our knowledge, very few studies have systematically combined amine and imine functionalities within a Cu-MOF structure, aiming to achieve both enhanced adsorption and antimicrobial activity. Although some reports have demonstrated the potential of NH₂-functionalized Cu-MOFs for antibacterial applications22 and Schiff-base modified Cu-MOFs for improved adsorption and bioactivity23, the simultaneous integration of these dual functionalities within a single framework remains largely unexplored. This dual functionality highlights the novelty and practical significance of the present work24,25.

Experimental

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification. The following reagents were employed for the synthesis, modification, adsorption, and antimicrobial studies: copper(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O) as the copper source for the MOF synthesis; 2-aminoterephthalic acid as the organic linker for the Cu-MOF framework; terephthalaldehyde for post-synthetic modification to introduce imine functional groups; ethanol (C2H5OH) as solvent for modification, washing, and dispersion; cationic dyes including crystal violet and rhodamine B as well as anionic dye of methyl orange for adsorption experiments (Fig. 1); hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solutions for adjusting the pH of dye solutions; Mueller-Hinton and Sabouraud dextrose media (broth and agar) for bacterial and fungal cultures in antimicrobial assays; gentamicin and fluconazole as positive controls for antibacterial and antifungal testing. All reagents were purchased from reputable chemical suppliers and used as received without further purification.

The molecular structure of dyes: (A) Crystal violet, (B) Methyl orange, and (C) Rhodamine B.

The modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) was thoroughly characterized to assess its structural, morphological, textural, and chemical properties. The chemical structure of the samples was examined using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, carried out on a Shimadzu FT-IR-8300 spectrophotometer using KBr pellets. X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were collected using Rigaku SmartLab and Philips PW1730 diffractometers equipped with a Cu-Kα radiation source. Nitrogen adsorption/desorption measurements were performed on a Micromeritics Tristar II 3020 surface area analyzer at 77 K. Prior to analysis, all samples were degassed at 200 °C for 6 h to eliminate moisture and surface impurities. The pore size distribution was calculated from the desorption branch using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model. The morphology of the samples was investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on a Hitachi HT7700 instrument operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Elemental analysis via energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was conducted using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), Hitachi SU8010, which was also employed for imaging the surface morphology. UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectra (UV-vis DRS) were recorded using a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu), with H2O used as the reference standard.

Bacillus cereus (PTCC1665) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (PTCC1435) as Gram-positive bacterial strains, Escherichia coli (PTCC1399) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PTCC1310) as Gram-negative bacterial strains, Candida albicans (PTCC5027) and Aspergillus fumigatus (PTCC5009) as fungal strains were prepared from the Persian Type Culture Collection (PTCC). The absorption spectra of bacterial and fungal suspensions were recorded using a UV-2100 RAY Leigh UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Synthesis of NH2-MIL-101 (Cu)

The NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) was synthesized via a solvothermal method. First, 1.66 g of NH2-BDC was dispersed in 30 mL of deionized water. Separately, 4 g of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water. The copper salt solution was then added dropwise to the ligand solution under continuous stirring at room temperature. The molar ratio of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O to NH2-BDC was approximately 1.8:1. The resulting mixture was stirred for 3 h at ambient conditions, then transferred to a teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and heated at 150 °C for 24 h. The resulting green precipitate as NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with water and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 8 h26.

Modification of NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) with terephthalaldehyde

Initially, 0.78 g of NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) and 0.39 g of TPA were each dispersed separately in 30 mL of ethanol. The TPA solution was then added dropwise to the Cu-MOF suspension under continuous stirring at room temperature. The mixture was subjected to reflux at 78 °C for 24 h. The resulting solid as the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ethanol, and finally dried in an oven at 80 °C for 8 h27,28.

Adsorption studies

The adsorption performance of the synthesized Cu-MOF was evaluated using three representative dyes: CV, RhB and MO. A concentrated dye stock solution (1000 mg/L) was prepared in deionized water and stored at 3 °C to ensure stability. Preliminary tests confirmed that no significant degradation occurred during the storage period. Alternatively, the stock solutions can be stored at ambient temperature, protected from light, without affecting the adsorption results.

Working solutions in the concentration range of 200–500 mg/L were freshly obtained through appropriate serial dilutions of the stock solution prior to each experiment. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted by adding 10 mg of the adsorbent to 10 mL of dye solution, typically with an initial concentration of 200, 400, and 450 mg/L for RhB, MO and CV, respectively. The solution pH was adjusted within the range of 2 to 12 by adding dropwise dilute hydrochloric acid (0.1 M) or sodium hydroxide (0.1 M), depending on the experimental conditions, and measured using a calibrated pH meter. The suspensions were magnetically stirred at ambient temperature (~ 25 °C) for 30 min to allow sufficient interaction between dye molecules and the adsorbent surface. Preliminary kinetic experiments confirmed that 30 min was sufficient to reach adsorption equilibrium for all three dyes, consistent with previous studies29–31. These experimental conditions (10 mg adsorbent in 10 mL solution; 30 min contact) follow standard batch adsorption protocols reported in the previous literatures and were chosen to ensure rapid equilibration while minimizing adsorbent usage32–34. After the interaction period, the mixtures were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min and the resulting supernatants were filtered through 0.45 μm PVDF syringe filters to remove residual particulates prior to UV–Vis analysis. These separation steps are widely adopted in dye adsorption studies to ensure accurate measurement and to avoid colloidal interference35,36. The concentration of unadsorbed dye remaining in solution was determined spectrophotometrically at λmaxs of 584, 554 and 464 nm for CV, RhB, and MO, respectively. Adsorption equilibrium was defined as the state at which no further net uptake of dye occurred, indicating dynamic balance between adsorption and desorption processes. The amount of dye adsorbed at equilibrium (qe mg/g) was calculated and used to assess the adsorption capacity of the materials, as well as to fit the data to appropriate adsorption isotherm models. Results and discussion

Chemistry

The Cu-based MOF was synthesized via a solvothermal method (Scheme 1). An aqueous solution of NH2-BDC was mixed with a solution of copper(II) nitrate under continuous stirring at room temperature. The reaction mixture was stirred for several hours and subsequently transferred to a teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, where it was heated under solvothermal conditions to promote crystallization. In this step, the NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) was prepared as a green powder. Then, it was reacted with TPA in ethanol under reflux conditions to condense its amine groups with carbonyl groups of TPA. Finally, the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu), which consists of Schiff base ligands and copper ions, was synthesized and spectrally analyzed.

Synthesis of the modified NH2- MIL-101 (Cu).

To identify the most favorable operational parameters for efficient dye removal, a series of controlled experiments were conducted to investigate the influence of four key variables: solution pH (2–12), initial dye concentration (100–500 mg/L), adsorbent dosage (6–30 mg), and contact time (10–30 min). A one-variable-at-a-time (OVAT) approach was adopted for the optimization study, wherein one parameter was varied while the others were held constant to isolate its individual effect on adsorption performance. All three dyes were independently studied to account for their differing chemical structures and adsorption behaviors. In each step, the optimal condition was determined by analyzing UV-Vis absorption spectra at the dye’s λmax. The condition that produced the lowest absorbance peak corresponding to the highest dye removal was selected as optimal. To further confirm that dye removal occurred through adsorption rather than degradation, UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded in two stages. First, spectra of the original dye solutions were measured before adding the Cu-MOF. After adsorption, the Cu-MOF was separated and washed, and the desorbed dye solution was analyzed again by UV–Vis spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2, the characteristic λmax peaks for CV (584 nm), RhB (554 nm), and MO (464 nm) remained at the same wavelengths, with only a decrease in absorbance intensity, demonstrating successful surface adsorption without decomposition or structural changes. FT-IR analysis of the Cu-MOF before and after adsorption (Fig. 3) also showed no formation of new bonds corresponding to degradation products. These results indicate that the dyes retained their molecular structures and were removed primarily through surface adsorption rather than chemical degradation. In addition, Cu2+ leaching doesn’t occur from the adsorbent during the adsorption process.

Comparative analysis of the three dyes shows that RhB achieved the highest overall optimized removal efficiency (92%), followed by CV (89%) and MO (83%). Various acidic, neutral, and alkaline media were suitable for maximizing the adsorption process of CV, RhB and MO dyes, respectively. These differences can be attributed to the nature (anionic or cationic) and the molecular size of dyes as well as how they interact with the Cu-MOF surface. The optimized parameters determined for each dye system will be subsequently adopted in all further adsorption studies, including isotherm modeling, kinetic analysis, and thermodynamic evaluation, to ensure consistency, reproducibility, and relevance across all performance assessments. These findings provide crucial insights into the interaction mechanisms between the adsorbents and different classes of dye molecules, enabling the tailored application of the synthesized materials for selective dye removal in wastewater treatment processes.

The synthesized Cu-MOF was further tested under conditions simulating real wastewater, demonstrating promising reusability and stability over multiple adsorption cycles, highlighting its practical potential for environmental applications39. The reusability of the modified NH₂-MIL-101(Cu) was evaluated over five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles for CV removal. After each cycle, the adsorbent was separated by centrifugation, regenerated by washing with an ethanol/deionized water mixture, and dried at 80 °C for 2 h before reuse. As shown in Fig. 4, the dye removal efficiency remained relatively high, decreasing slightly from 89% in the first cycle to about 75% after the fifth cycle, likely due to partial pore blockage or incomplete desorption of dye molecules.

Reusability performance of the adsorbent in CV, RhB and MO removal over consecutive adsorption desorption cycles.

To assess the structural stability and potential leaching of the adsorbent, the XRD pattern of the regenerated Cu-MOF was recorded after the fifth cycle (Fig. 12), as well as the concentration of Cu2+ in the dye solutions before and after adsorption was measured using UV–Vis spectroscopy. The diffraction peaks retained their original positions with only a minor decrease in intensity, and no detectable change in Cu2+ concentration was observed, confirming no leaching and excellent structural stability. These results collectively indicate that the synthesized Cu-MOF possesses good reusability and structural stability, making it a promising candidate for practical dye removal applications in wastewater treatment.

To evaluate the practical applicability of the synthesized Cu-MOF, a comparative analysis was conducted against previously reported adsorbents, including unmodified MOFs and conventional materials such as activated carbon, zeolites, and graphene oxide composites. The synthesized Cu-MOF exhibited a specific surface area of 2037.65 m2/g, comparable to or exceeding many known MOFs and surpassing most conventional adsorbents. As summarized in Table 5, the synthesized Cu-MOF achieved 83–92% removal efficiency for CV, RhB, and MO dyes, while showing its excellent performance compared to activated carbon, graphene oxide composites and zeolite-based materials. It was almost as effective at absorbing these dyes as ZrNDC MOF, PWA/SrMOF, Ni-DAP MOF, Fe-MOF, Fe-BTC, Tragopogon dubiu, Anchote peel. In addition, unmodified MIL-101(Cr) (1500–2100 m2/g, 60–88% removal) offers relatively similar performance, but the synthesized Cu-MOF benefits from larger pore diameter and richer functional group diversity, enhancing dye–adsorbent interactions. Its mesoporous structure with an average pore diameter of approximately 29.06 nm facilitates the accessibility of large organic molecules, such as dyes, into internal adsorption sites. FT-IR and EDX analyses confirmed the presence of abundant functional groups (–OH, C = O, and nitrogen-containing moieties), offering multiple interaction pathways including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and electrostatic attraction for efficient dye capture. Furthermore, the designed Cu-MOF showed a high BET constant (C = 290.46), indicative of a strong adsorbate-adsorbent affinity, consistent with its high adsorption performance. Overall, the combination of exceptional surface area, favorable mesoporous architecture, and abundant surface chemistry positions the synthesized Cu-MOF as a highly efficient adsorbent for the removal of a wide range of organic dyes, clearly outperforming or matching most reference materials in both structural and functional parameters.

Bibliometric analysis of Cu-MOFs

A bibliometric analysis using VOSviewer was performed on recent studies concerning Cu-based MOFs in environmental applications. Four major keyword clusters were identified: (I) Cu-MOF and adsorption, highlighting extensive research on dye removal; (II) dye removal, covering various classes of organic pollutants; (III) antimicrobial evaluation, reflecting growing biomedical interest; and (IV) the intersection of adsorption and antimicrobial activity, which remains underexplored. This work specifically targets the latter by integrating both functionalities through amine–imine modification, emphasizing its novelty and dual environmental–clinical significance.

Proposed adsorption mechanism of dyes on modified NH₂-MIL-101 (Cu)

The adsorption mechanism of dyes onto modified NH2-MIL-101(Cu) can be attributed to a combination of physical and chemical interactions (Fig. 5). Based on the structural and surface chemistry analyses (FT-IR, BET, and EDX), the following key mechanisms are proposed: (I) Electrostatic interactions: The surface charge of Cu-MOF is strongly pH-dependent. Under acidic conditions, protonated –NH2 and –OH groups enhance the adsorption of anionic dyes, while under alkaline conditions, deprotonated –COO⁻ and –NH groups favor the adsorption of cationic dyes. (II) π–π stacking interactions: Aromatic rings in the MOF linkers interact with the aromatic structures of dyes (e.g., CV, RhB, MO), contributing to the stabilization of adsorbed molecules. (III) Hydrogen bonding: Functional groups such as –OH, and –COOH can act as both hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, interacting with polar groups (–OH, –NH2, –SO2–, etc.) of dye molecules, thereby stabilizing their attachment to the surface. (IV) Coordination bonding: Open Cu2+ metal sites coordinate with electron-donor atoms (O, N) from dye molecules, resulting in stronger and more specific interactions that enhance adsorption capacity. (V) Pore-filling effect: The mesoporous structure and high surface area provide sufficient space for the penetration and physical trapping of bulky dye molecules, thereby increasing overall adsorption capacity. Overall, the synergy among these mechanisms explains the high adsorption efficiency and multifunctional performance of modified NH2-MIL-101(Cu) toward a wide range of cationic and anionic dyes in aqueous media.

Possible mechanisms for adsorptive removal of dyes over MOFs.

It should be noted that in this study, our primary objective was to demonstrate the adsorption efficiency of the designed NH₂-MIL-101 (Cu) toward different organic dyes. Detailed kinetic and thermodynamic analyses of the adsorption process, while highly important, were beyond the scope of this work. These aspects require independent and systematic investigation, as reported in previous studies65, and will be addressed in our future research to complement the current findings.

Advantages and limitations of the synthesized adsorbent

The designed Cu-MOF demonstrates several advantages as an adsorbent, including exceptionally high surface area, mesoporosity with a large pore diameter, and the coexistence of amine and imine functionalities, which collectively enhance adsorption through electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking and coordination interactions. In addition, the material exhibits dual functionality by combining high dye removal efficiency with antimicrobial activity, expanding its potential applications in wastewater treatment. Its reusability under simulated real wastewater conditions further confirms practical stability. However, certain limitations should also be considered. The material may exhibit reduced stability under highly acidic or alkaline conditions, and partial loss of adsorption capacity can occur after multiple regeneration cycles. Furthermore, the synthesis cost and energy consumption are relatively higher compared to low-cost adsorbents such as activated carbon or agricultural wastes, which may limit large-scale applications. Addressing these limitations through process optimization and hybrid material development could enhance its practical viability.

Structural and morphological characterization

A comprehensive characterization was performed to evaluate the structure, morphology, elemental composition, porosity, and stability of the synthesized MOF.

The FT-IR spectra of the prepared Cu-MOF before and after functionalization showed broad bands at ~ 3339, 3184, 3091 and 3040 cm− 1 assigned to surface –OH and N–H/–NH₂ stretching (Fig. 6). Peaks in the 3000–2800 cm− 1 range indicated C–H stretching from organic linkers. Strong absorptions near 1652 and 1567 cm− 1 are attributed to ν(C = O)/ν(C = N) (or conjugated aromatic modes) and asymmetric COO⁻ stretches, respectively. Bands at ~ 1248 and ~ 1030 cm− 1 are assigned to C–O/C–N stretching and aromatic skeletal modes, while peaks at ~ 885 and ~ 760 cm− 1 correspond to aromatic out-of-plane C–H bending. Signals in the ~ 650–500 cm− 1 range (e.g., ~ 628, 573, 535 cm− 1) are attributed to Cu–O stretching and metal–oxygen lattice vibrations, confirming coordination of Cu centers with oxygen-containing linkers in the MOF70.

FT-IR spectra of (a) NH2-MIL-101 (Cu); (b) the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu).

To confirm the formation of imine bonds after modification with TPA, the FT-IR spectra of the pristine (a) and modified (b) samples were compared. The broad band assigned to ν(N–H)/–NH2 vibrations at ~ 3339–3040 cm− 1 decreased noticeably in the modified sample, while a new/stronger band emerged at ~ 1652 cm− 1 corresponding to ν(C = N). These spectral changes indicate the conversion of amine groups into imine linkages (Schiff-base formation). In addition, EDX analysis revealed an increase in the relative carbon content after modification, consistent with the incorporation of TPA units. Taken together, these results confirm the successful formation of the imine-functionalized MOF without destruction of the framework.

Based on the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms measured at 77 K, the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) exhibits notable values for specific surface area, total pore volume, and average pore diameter, as summarized in Table 8. These parameters were obtained using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method for surface area calculation, and the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model from the desorption branch for determining pore volume and diameter. The specific surface area (in m2/g) represents the total available area for adsorption per unit mass. A high surface area indicates a highly porous structure, offering numerous active sites for interaction with guest molecules. This characteristic is particularly important in applications such as catalysis, adsorption, and molecular storage. The total pore volume, measured to be 0.7465 cm3/g, reflects the overall internal space within the porous framework. This relatively large pore volume suggests excellent capacity for accommodating guest species, which is beneficial in processes involving molecular diffusion or adsorption of organic compounds. The average pore diameter was determined to be 29.06 nm, placing the material firmly in the category of mesoporous materials (2–50 nm), according to IUPAC classification. The presence of such mesopores facilitates the diffusion and accessibility of larger organic molecules into the internal structure, enhancing the material’s functionality in applications requiring transport and interaction with bulky species. Together, the combination of high specific surface area, substantial pore volume, and mesoporous architecture highlights the potential of the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) for use in fields such as adsorption of large organic molecules, heterogeneous catalysis, and environmental remediation. SEM and TEM techniques were employed to investigate the morphology and particle size distribution of the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) (Figs. 9 and 10). SEM images reveal agglomerated polyhedral-like crystallites with relatively uniform morphology and limited surface irregularities. TEM micrographs further confirm the presence of well-defined nanosized particles with a tendency to aggregate. Quantitative particle size analysis was performed using ImageJ software based on TEM images, calibrated with the corresponding scale bar. The average particle size was determined to be approximately 85 nm with a standard deviation of ± 10 nm, while the majority of the particles were distributed within the range of 70–100 nm. These findings confirm the formation of nanoscale particles with a relatively narrow size distribution. Moreover, the observed particle agglomeration in certain regions suggests interparticle interactions, which could influence the accessible surface area, porosity, and potential performance of the material in practical applications.

The XRD pattern of the synthesized NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) (Fig. 11) displays multiple well-defined diffraction peaks, confirming the crystalline nature of the material. The most intense reflection is observed around 2θ = 28–29°, while several weaker peaks appear near 10°, 16°, 18°, 23°, and 25°. A broad low-angle band in the 10–20° range is also evident, which is characteristic of the mesoporous framework and large unit cell typically associated with MIL-101-type materials. Peak broadening and the reduced intensity of several reflections suggest a partial loss of long-range order, indicating slight amorphization that likely arises from nanoscale disorder or strain introduced during post-synthetic modification. These diffraction features are in good agreement with previously reported NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) derivatives, confirming that the MIL-101 topology was preserved after modification. Although a simulated XRD pattern of the ideal NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) framework was not generated in this study, the experimental diffraction features—particularly the broad low-angle band (10–20°) and the presence of weak high-angle peaks—are consistent with the reported MIL-101 structure. The observed partial amorphization and mesoporous characteristics inferred from the broadened peaks align with earlier reports for similar MOF derivatives, validating the successful synthesis and structural integrity of the modified framework.

Structural stability of absorbent after five adsorption–desorption cycles

The XRD pattern of the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) after five adsorption–desorption cycles (Fig. 12) retains the main diffraction peaks observed in the fresh sample, although with slightly reduced intensity. The preservation of the characteristic reflections indicates that the crystalline framework remains largely intact after multiple reuses. The moderate decrease in peak intensity can be attributed to minor structural disorder, partial blockage of pores by residual adsorbate, or slight aggregation of particles during the regeneration process. These results demonstrate that the modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) adsorbent possesses good structural stability and reusability, maintaining its crystalline framework even after repeated use.

Stability of the adsorbent under different thermal and pH conditions

After the adsorption process, the structural stability of the designed NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) under acidic (pH = 2), alkaline (pH = 12), and neutral (pH = 7) conditions was further examined at different temperatures (room temperature, 50 °C, and 100 °C) after 24 h, as shown in Figs. 13, 14 and 15. In the acidic environment, the XRD patterns at all temperatures exhibit similar diffraction positions, indicating that the framework remains stable. Only a slight decrease in peak intensity is observed at elevated temperatures, suggesting minor surface modification without collapse of the framework. In the alkaline environment, a more pronounced reduction in peak intensity is detected, especially at 50 °C and 100 °C. This decrease implies partial degradation or disorder of the MOF, possibly due to the attack of hydroxide ions on the Cu–O coordination bonds. Nonetheless, the presence of residual characteristic peaks confirms that the framework is not completely destroyed. In the neutral environment, the diffraction patterns remain nearly unchanged up to 100 °C, with only small variations in intensity. This behavior indicates excellent structural integrity and thermal stability of modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu).

Overall, the XRD results confirm that modified NH2-MIL-101 (Cu) maintains its crystallinity and structural framework after multiple adsorption–desorption cycles and under various environmental conditions. Although a slight reduction in long-range order is observed under strong alkaline or high-temperature conditions, the material exhibits remarkable resistance to framework collapse. This structural stability, combined with its high adsorption capacity and pronounced antimicrobial activity, highlights the dual functionality and reusability of the synthesized Cu-MOF, positioning it as a robust and sustainable candidate for wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusion

This study successfully reports the post-synthetic functionalization of a copper-based MOF (NH2-MIL-101 (Cu)) using terephthalaldehyde to introduce imine functionalities. Structural characterization through XRD, FT-IR, and BET confirmed the formation of a highly crystalline framework with a large surface area (2037.65 m2/g), uniform mesoporosity (average pore diameter of 29.06 nm). These features enhanced the adsorption behavior of the modified Cu-MOF, which showed high capacities of 230.1, 187.4 and 165.2 mg/g for crystal violet, rhodamine B and methyl orange, respectively, achieving up to 92% removal within 30 min.

Beyond its adsorption performance, the modified Cu-MOF demonstrated significant antimicrobial potential, particularly against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, C. albicans, and A. fumigatus, with MIC values ranging from 4 to 32 µg/mL. This dual functionality highlights the material’s potential as an efficient adsorbent for wastewater treatment while simultaneously acting as an antimicrobial platform to mitigate biological contamination. The integration of amine and imine groups within the MOF framework contributed to its strong interaction with both dye molecules and microbial cells, underscoring its multifunctional nature and practical environmental relevance.

Although the results confirm the promising performance of the modified NH₂-MIL-101 (Cu), further studies are necessary to evaluate its long-term stability, regeneration efficiency, and copper ion leaching in real wastewater environments. Detailed antimicrobial mechanism analysis, cytotoxicity testing, in vivo evaluations and clinical trials are also required to ensure biosafety and support biomedical applicability. Future research should focus on scaling up synthesis, enhancing selectivity for diverse pollutants, and exploring hybrid MOF-based composites for broader applications in catalysis, drug delivery, and biosensing.
