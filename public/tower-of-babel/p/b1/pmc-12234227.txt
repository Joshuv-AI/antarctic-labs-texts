Refining estimation of the instantaneous reproduction number during early pandemic stages: addressing case-reporting variability and serial interval uncertainty
Authors: Gary Hettinger, David Rubin, Jing Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring infectious disease outbreaks, estimates for the instantaneous reproduction number, , are essential for understanding transmission dynamics. This study develops and analyzes new methodology to improve estimation of when observed case counts are subject to reporting patterns and available serial interval estimates are subject to uncertainty and nonrepresentativeness. Specifically, we developed a Bayesian time-since-infection model with layers to adjust for reporting measurement error, integrate multiple candidate serial interval estimates, and estimate transmission with an autoregressive time-series model incorporating factors relevant to transmission. Additionally, we provide practical tools to identify reporting patterns and determine when to smooth case counts for more usable estimates. We evaluated model performance relative to widely adopted methodology by simulating outbreak data, finding improved estimation with the proposed methodology. We also used 2020 COVID-19 data to analyze transmission trends and predictors, identifying strong day-of-week and social distancing effects that subsequently reduced estimate volatility. In addition to new approaches for addressing serial interval uncertainty and incorporating transmission predictor information, this study provides an alternative approach for addressing case-reporting patterns without delaying detection or smoothing over relevant transmission signals. These tools and findings may be used or built upon for current and future outbreaks.Keywords: Bayesian, COVID-19, infectious disease, measurement error, time-series analysis

Introduction

During infectious disease outbreaks, decision-makers require timely, accurate monitoring of disease transmission to inform public health responses. An important quantity for infectious disease surveillance is the instantaneous reproduction number, , which represents the average number of secondary cases an individual infected at time t could expect to infect should current conditions persist.1,2 The quantity has many applications and was heavily used during the COVID-19 pandemic to monitor and project disease spread,3-5 evaluate intervention efficacy,6-9 and identify transmission risk factors.10,11 Despite this, statistical modelers face several challenges when estimating , particularly in the early stages of outbreaks, due to data availability, quality, and representativeness.12

Methodologists have developed several estimators for using the time-since-infection (TSI) epidemiologic model,13 with many summarized in a report by White et al.12 Generally, TSI-based models estimate by linking newly identified cases to previously identified cases at an aggregate level, using estimates of the serial interval distribution, which refers to the distribution of time between case definitions of a primary and secondary case.1,14 Compared with compartmental models like the susceptible-infected-recovered model, which do not incorporate information about the time between infections, TSI-based models are particularly useful early in outbreaks when serial interval information from small contact-tracing studies and biological experiments is more available than the number of susceptible or recovered individuals in the population.1,15 However, neither the true interval between primary and secondary infections nor the true daily infection count is known; therefore, robust estimation of requires flexibility in the proposed relationship between observed cases and the serial interval distribution.16 Methodologists have addressed this uncertainty by estimating probabilistically with Bayesian approaches, leveraging sampling algorithms for parameter estimation under complex likelihood functions.17-20

Despite these methodological advances, inherent measurement error in case-notification data often renders estimates overly volatile.2 To mitigate this, practitioners commonly use supplementary data, such as seroprevalence. When additional data are not available, they often aggregate case counts or estimates over a sliding window of pre-determined length.2,18 However, coarse smoothing techniques require arbitrary smoothing interval selection and may delay trend detection or obscure transmission signals necessary to understand transmission dynamics and evaluate intervention efficacy. Instead, known drivers of such variation, like day-of-week reporting patterns, should be exploited for targeted measurement correction.21,22

Time-since-infection models also must incorporate uncertainty regarding the serial interval distribution.2 Especially early in outbreaks, available serial interval estimates may lack precision or representativeness because samples may be limited by size, affected region, or viral mutation.23 Practitioners must then choose between multiple imperfect estimates to estimate .24-26 A few approaches have accommodated serial interval uncertainty, either by resampling serial interval parameters over a user-specified range or integrating accurate, representative contact-tracing data into a 2-step estimator.18,19 However, neither approach directly integrates multiple candidate serial interval estimates often available early in an outbreak.

Our objectives are 3-fold. First, to address limitations in available methodology, we introduce a unified Bayesian framework to estimate in the presence of case-reporting measurement error without requiring additional data such as seroprevalence. Our methodology also considers nonrepresentative serial interval estimates and available transmission predictors (eg, social distancing metrics). We also provide tools to identify relevant reporting patterns and select a proper adjustment approach. Second, we assess estimator performance in simulation studies modeled after COVID-19 outbreak patterns. Finally, we apply our method to analyze COVID-19 transmission in 10 major US counties.

Methods

COVID-19 data

We collected reported cases from March 25, 2020, to November 10, 2020 (230 days) for 10 counties containing large US cities: Austin, TX, Travis County; Chicago, IL, Cook County; Dallas, TX, Dallas County; Houston, TX, Harris County; Los Angeles, CA, Los Angeles County; Miami, FL, Miami-Dade County; New York, NY, New York County; Philadelphia, PA, Philadelphia County; San Diego, CA, San Diego County; and San Francisco, CA, San Francisco County. We also collected metrics for daily visitation difference, which measures the difference in visits to nonessential businesses between the current day and a prepandemic baseline, and wet bulb temperature, which measures the complex thermodynamic relationship between temperature and humidity. These measures, provided by Unacast and the National Ocean and Atmospheric Administration Local Climatological Data, respectively, were associated in a prior study with transmission.10

To account for the unknown lag period between transmission factor and case identification, we used the average metric between 2 and 15 days prior to a given day as our predictor. Given the absence of serial interval studies on target counties, we selected 3 serial interval distribution estimates from a systematic world review25; a study of Dublin, Ireland26; and an analysis of California counties27,28 to borrow information from serial interval estimates on cities with comparable demographic and metropolitan characteristics (Appendix S1).

Model implementation

We implement our approach using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampler in the JAGS program.31 Accordingly, our Bayesian approach enables information sharing between modules to improve overall estimation while allowing for principled uncertainty quantification.32 We used posterior means for parameter point estimates and the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles for a 95% credible interval. For simulations, we implemented the sampler using 8 chains with 2000 burn-in and 4000 total iterations per chain, thinning every other draw. For the longer study period in our COVID-19 analysis, we tripled the number of burn-in (6000) and total (12000) iterations per chain. We included 4 lag terms for autoregressive models in the application (2 in simulations), which we selected by investigating partial autocorrelation functions but that can alternatively be selected by optimizing model information criterion.33-35 Convergence of key parameters was assessed by the Gelman-Rubin statistic () provided by JAGS.36,37 Statistical analyses were performed in R, version 4.0.2 (R Group for Statistical Computing).

Reporting pattern specification

In certain settings, practitioners may know, a priori, county-level reporting patterns, . More often, they will know the general structure of (eg, day-of-week) but want to data-adaptively cluster periods with similar reporting patterns for efficiency gains. Therefore, we developed an algorithm (Table 2) to cluster days of the week with similar reporting patterns by extending standard time-series decompositions. Intuitively, our approach first identifies general temporal trends (eg, 7-day averages) and uses these to identify reporting variability, measured by the ratio of reported cases to general trends.38 We then cluster days of the week with similar reporting variability over the study period and select the clustering that best explains all reporting variability by minimizing the Akaike information criterion of a linear model with cluster-specific intercepts. The estimated intercepts provide reasonable prior means for . A step-by-step visualization of this method using COVID-19 data from New York is provided in Figure 1.

Evaluation

We simulated 1200 50-day outbreaks for 3 different outbreak patterns (growth-lockdown-equilibrium, growth-lockdown-growth-lockdown-equilibrium, and a smooth decay from the growth stage) and 1200 100-day outbreaks for the growth-lockdown-growth-lockdown-equilibrium pattern. We discarded simulated outbreaks with fewer than 10 cases for more than 20% of days because these generally indicated outbreaks that did not persist for the entire study length, leaving 989 of 1200 50-day outbreaks and 804 of 1200 100-day outbreaks, on average. The Supplemental Material includes sample code and a simulated data set.

We evaluated performance of our method by calculating the mean squared error (MSE), bias, and coverage probability of and associated credible intervals from day 8 onward. We first evaluated estimator performance when the reporting periods are considered known and then when they require estimation. For the latter, we additionally evaluated our clustering algorithm performance using a weighted F-measure () and volatility test performance by assessing the fraction of simulations that the test selected over .40

We compared performance with the highly used method of Cori et al. implemented using the EpiEstim package.18,41 This method played a key role in recent epidemics, including the Ebola outbreak in 2014-201642,43 and the recent COVID-19 outbreak,44-47 either through direct applications or extensions of the method. Notably, their method does not address reporting measurement error, include a transmission model for potential autocorrelation or covariate predictors, or allow for multiple serial interval estimates. Thus, their method largely resembles the assumptions of V0 outbreaks and provides a baseline for evaluating the importance of different contributions. To improve performance, their method offers 2 additional options: (1) to aggregate estimates for over a user-specified window of length for improved stability and (2) to resample serial interval means and variances over a user-specified range of values for improved uncertainty quantification. In our main results, we present comparisons with baseline method estimates denoted using to fit our priority of capturing granular transmission signals, and under no serial interval uncertainty, because inclusion resulted in poor simulation performance. Comparisons between window size and uncertainty options are provided in Table S2.

Figure 5 visualizes the association between daily visitation difference and transmission rate for San Diego and New York City. We estimated that a 1 SD increase in daily visitation (reduced social distancing) was associated with an average 76% increase in between the counties. Discussion

When estimating , practitioners must use noisy case-notification data subject to reporting patterns and nonrepresentative external serial interval estimates. Through extensive simulation studies and an analysis of COVID-19 data, we demonstrated the importance of allowing for serial interval uncertainty when estimating transmission and modeling, rather than smoothing, case-reporting patterns even when additional data resources are not available during early stages of outbreaks. We proposed a Bayesian framework that shares information and uncertainty between intuitive modeling layers, allowing for principled but flexible parameter exploration. Our approach has benefits for monitoring and parameterizing models reliant on , where coarse smoothing approaches may delay detection of transmission trends and wash out relevant signals in transmission rates. We further provided objective methods to determine the day-of-week model and select between reporting error adjustment approaches by using time-series decompositions and smoothness measures. These methods offer practitioners data-adaptive tools for practical challenges of such analyses without requiring user subjectivity. Furthermore, our framework enables direct exploration of transmission predictors, which revealed substantial effects of social distancing on transmission in our study.

Our framework targets measurement error due to case reporting and can be viewed as a new approach to smoothing, where our “smoothing” technique incorporates knowledge that reporting behavior differs substantially across days of the week. When day-of-week reporting variation does not sufficiently capture overall reporting variation, coarser methods like rolling averages or spline smoothing may be beneficial.2 In these cases, we proposed smoothing cases, but smoothing or averaging is also reasonable.18 When prioritizing model interpretability and responsiveness to recent transmission changes over estimate stability and smoothness, our proposed autoregressive model may be preferential. Otherwise, models with more smoothing effects, like the autoregressive moving average may be more appropriate.29 As opposed to directly weighting cases by an estimate for day-of-week variation, our Bayesian model allows for uncertainty in reporting-adjusted cases on a particular day and shares information from other modeling layers, like that from the serial interval mixture, to better inform estimates of weights and .

Our approach does not address unidentified cases resulting from factors like asymptomatic transmission and testing guidelines. When the rate of case identification is constant over time, our estimates for are unbiased because the identification rate cancels out in the renewal equation.1,12 However, estimates will be biased if the identification rate changes over time.49 Other methods have attempted to account for identification rate by incorporating additional data on seroprevalence, hospitalizations, or deaths.20,50-52 Scaling cases by seroprevalence measures, hospitalization rates, or death rates may adjust for such challenges and allow investigation of underreporting within the scope of our model (Appendix S5), but they come with additional assumptions and may further delay detection of transmission changes.53 Reporting patterns, themselves, may change during a study period and trigger changes in the serial interval over time.54,55 Our model can be readily adapted to such situations by specifying the reporting period map, , and/or the serial interval mixture weights, , to vary over time (eg, ) (Appendix S5). Future work may consider principled integration of case data from multiple regions to improve statistical power, although these efforts pose significant challenges.

An important contribution of our work is the autoregressive transmission model, which dually improves estimation accuracy and enables exploration of factors associated with transmission. Previous works have demonstrated the predictive power of autoregressive transmission models in practice, suggesting that similar models may also perform well for forecasting, a feature worth assessing in future work.56,57 In long study periods, exploration of transmission factors like social distancing and weather can easily be extended to include time-varying indicators representing new mutations or policy changes. However, we caution against strong interpretations of the associations between predictors and transmission, because latent estimates are influenced by predictor information in the Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampler.32 Furthermore, Lipsitch et al. advocate for using the case reproduction number, , when analyzing causal effects of policies, because includes additional data after day by reparameterizing the renewal equation as .58 Still, the relative strength of associations between standardized covariates may provide valuable insights into their relative importance.

Conclusion

Our research expands upon prior methodology to estimate transmission during infectious disease outbreaks by integrating epidemiologic and statistical models within a Bayesian framework. We propose intuitive modules to address data limitations during outbreaks and link them with advanced statistical techniques to optimally balance model flexibility and efficiency. Our work provides a new tool for infectious disease researchers and public health decision makers and also highlights focus areas for studies adopting different approaches. Our findings are relevant for current and future outbreaks.
