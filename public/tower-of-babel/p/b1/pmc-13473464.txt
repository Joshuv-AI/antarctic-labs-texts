Selenium atom transfer enables selective bridged alkene-arylamine cycloaddition
Authors: Jiefeng Hu, Kun Zhang, Jiahao Zhang, Daojing Li, Yongkai Su, Shuangru Chen, Qinqi He, Die Deng, Jing Zhang, Guoqiang Wang, Su Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCycloaddition reactions are central to building cyclic molecular architectures for drug discovery and materials science. However, controlling single-atom-bridged cycloaddition remains elusive. Herein, we describe a selenium-atom transfer [3 + 2 + 1] heterocycloaddition of readily available arylamines, alkenes and diselenides, enabling rapid, modular access to diverse selenium- and nitrogen-containing heterocycles, including selenomorpholines and polycyclic 1,4-selenazinanes. This metal-free method operates under mild, simple conditions with scalability, showcasing broad substrate generality and functional group tolerance, is amenable to scalable synthesis, and demonstrates utility in the late-stage skeletal diversification of complex molecules. Mechanistic and computational studies reveal a Se-bridged cycloaddition pathway involving alkene aminoselenylation, followed by electrophilic selenocyclization and deprotonative rearomatization. This heteroatom-driven annulation opens opportunities for the development of reactions for the construction of multi-heteroatom cyclic scaffolds and broader application in selenium-based medicinal chemistry.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

Nitrogen-containing (N-) heterocycles constitute fundamental structural motifs that are ubiquitous in natural products, functional organic materials, and pharmacologically active compounds1. Strategic incorporation of secondary heteroatoms (e.g., O, S, B) into N-heterocyclic scaffolds, allows fine-tuning of their electronic effects, steric profiles, and biophysical properties, thereby unlocking unexpected opportunities for biological targeting enhancement and optoelectronics advancement2–5. Among these substitutions, selenium stands out: its high polarizability and redox activity endow motifs with functions not accessible to lighter congeners (oxygen, sulfur)6–8. Thus, considerable effort has focused on embedding selenium (Se) into N-heterocycles to access Se, N-heterocyclic architectures9–12. Selenazinanes are particularly appealing owing to their captivating physicochemical and biological properties, such as favorable solubility, permeability, and biocompatibility, enabling them to be widely utilized in pharmaceutical and material science (Fig. 1a)13–16. Yet despite this potential, their synthesis remains largely underdeveloped. Current synthetic approaches have predominantly relied on double SN2 nucleophilic substitutions17–20 and hetero-Diels-Alder reactions21. However, these methods often suffer from inherent limitations such as prolonged reaction sequences, laborious pre-functionalization requirements, limited structural diversity, and scarce opportunities for post-functionalization, thereby impeding progress in the discovery and design of advanced drugs and materials incorporating selenium heterocycles. Therefore, the development of general, efficient, and modular strategies for direct assembly of structurally diverse selenazinanes from simple feedstocks is valuable.

The increasing demand for chemical biology and medicinal research drives the need for efficient annulation methods to rapidly build heterocyclic libraries from readily available starting materials. Catalytic intermolecular cycloaddition22–25 between alkenes and N-containing partners has emerged as a straightforward and powerful tool for constructing N-heterocyclic scaffolds, offering exceptional atom- and step-economy (Fig. 1b)26–29. Substantial advances in [n + 2] cycloadditions enable selective C–C and C–N bond formation through metallacyclic intermediates, affording diverse five- and six-membered N-heterocycles such as pyrrolidines, indolines, benzolactams, and tetrahydroquinolines30–36. However, despite these advances, multicomponent cycloaddition for the direct construction of multi-heteroatom ring systems remains an elusive challenge due to complex reactivity and unsatisfactory control of chemo- and regioselectivity.

Single-atom transfer (SAT) annulations, where a single atom is transferred from a donor into the newly formed ring, represent an appealing strategy for building cyclic scaffolds due to their high atom utilization and flexible molecular restructuring (Fig. 1c). Recent studies demonstrate that introduction of a carbon37,38, nitrogen39–41, oxygen42,43, sulfur44,45 or boron46–48 atom into ring-forming events can directly construct privileged heterocyclic structures49,50. Inspired by the above concepts, we asked whether the insertion of a bridging atom into an alkene [n + 2] cycloaddition could unlock a general route to multi- heteroatom ring systems (Fig. 1d). However, realizing this multicomponent SAT cycloaddition approach presents major obstacles: (i) competitive reaction pathways such as undesired alkene difunctionalization, two-component C–H functionalization, and Heck-type side reactions, and (ii) difficulties in controlling both chemo- and regioselectivity.

In this work, we report a Se-atom-transfer strategy that enables bridged [3 + 2 + 1] cyclization of readily available arylamines, alkenes, and diselenides via an unprecedented mechanism (Fig. 1e). This method offers a straightforward and versatile platform for the synthesis of diverse selenomorpholine derivatives. We further show that preassembled olefin-amine substrates undergo SAT cycloaddition with diselenides to construct two rings in a single operation, providing rapid access to polycyclic 1,4-selenazinanes, which are challenging structures that were previously inaccessible.

Results

Reaction development

With the concept in mind, we initiated our investigation using N-methylaniline (1) and styrene (2) as model substrates (Fig. 2), exploring various selenium sources under transition-metal-catalyzed conditions for the multicomponent cyclization. Unfortunately, these preliminary attempts proved unsuccessful, as the system preferentially favored two-component reactivity rather than the desired transformation. Recognizing selenium’s versatile redox behavior, we next examined selenium ion-mediated processes51–54. Screening of selenium ethers and oxidants revealed that commercially available benzyl diselenide, in combination with 1,3-diiodo-5,5-dimethyl-2,4-imidazolidinedione (DIDMH) in toluene, provided the desired cyclic product in 11% yield, accompanied by C(sp²)–H selenation by-products 4. Systematic optimization ultimately identified a combination of dimethyl diselenide (S1), N-fluorobenzenesulfonimide (NFSI), and sodium formate (HCO₂Na) in THF as the optimal system, affording the desired product in 80% yield (entry 1). Elemental selenium and disodium diselenide (Na₂Se₂) were entirely inactive (entries 2 and 3). Other primary diselenides (S2 − S5) provided the products in moderate to good yields (entry 4). The secondary diselenide (S6) remained reactive, giving the 1,4-selenazinane in 47% yield alongside vinylamine by-products 5. In contrast, diphenyl diselenide (S7) failed to promote the three-component cyclization, instead favoring C(sp²)–H selenation. Further examination of alternative selenium sources, including ferrocene triselenide (S8), monoselenide (S9), and selenosuccinimide (S10), confirmed their ineffectiveness in Se-atom transfer annulation, leading only to oxidation of the aniline substrate (entries 5–7). The essential role of selenium was underscored by a control experiment with methyl disulfide (Me₂S₂), which was completely inactive (entry 8). Systematic evaluation of oxidants revealed that common reagents, including PhI(OAc)₂, PyF⁺BF₄⁻, Selectfluor, and N-bromosuccinimide (NBS), either provided negligible yields or failed to initiate the cyclization (entries 9 − 12). Similarly, replacing HCO₂Na with other bases, such as K₃PO₄ or Na₂CO₃, did not improve the outcome (entries 13 and 14), and omission of HCO₂Na reduced the yield of 3 to 61% (entry 15). Finally, solvent screening identified THF as optimal for both yield and selectivity (Supplementary Table 4).

Substrate scope exploration

With the optimized reaction conditions identified, we examined the scope and limitations of this three-component Se-doped annulation reaction. As shown in Fig. 3, a broad range of alkenes bearing electron-rich and electron-deficient substituents on the aromatic rings proved suitable, affording the benzo[b][1,4]selenazines in moderate to good yields. For instance, substrates containing methyl or methoxy groups at various ring positions reacted smoothly, delivering the corresponding products (6–9) in 68–84% yield. Single crystals of compound 8 were grown by recrystallization from ethyl acetate/nhexane (1:10) at room temperature. X-ray diffraction analysis (solved using SHELXT and refined with SHELXL within Olex2) unambiguously confirmed its structure. Aryl alkenes bearing halogens and tosylates were also compatible with this transformation, resulting in corresponding products (10 − 13) in yields ranging from 61–72%. Strongly electron-withdrawing groups (–CF₃, –CO₂Me, –CN, –NO₂) were compatible but gave lower yields (38–51%, 14–17) compared with electron-rich analogues (18–20), underscoring the influence of electronic effects. Moreover, aryl olefins bearing sensitive functional groups, including alkylsilane and boronic ester moieties, also successfully participate in this reaction (21 and 22), confirming the robustness and synthetic utility of this method. Polyaromatic substrates such as 2-vinylnaphthalene and 4-vinyl-1,1’-biphenyl underwent efficient annulation with 1 and S1 to afford the corresponding products (23 and 24) in good yields. Remarkably, the protocol was also applicable to conjugated dienes, affording product 25 in 55% yield and extending the scope to π-extended systems. The reaction tolerated polysubstituted and internal alkenes, although with reduced efficiency, providing 26 and 27 in 32% and 23% yield, respectively. More importantly, 27 was obtained as a single diastereomer. These results highlight the versatility of the method while revealing steric and electronic challenges associated with more hindered alkenes. For aliphatic alkenes such as but-3-en-1-ylbenzene, the cyclization proceeded efficiently but generated a mixture of two regioisomers, as confirmed by GC–MS and NMR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, limitations emerged with particularly strained (bicyclo[2.2.1]hept-2-ene) or electron-deficient (N,N-dimethylacrylamide) systems, as well as with heterocyclic alkenes (2,3-dihydrofuran), where only complex mixtures from decomposition of the starting materials were observed (28 − 30). Next, our endeavor shifted to evaluate the amine component scope to further demonstrate the versatility of this Se-doped annulation in accessing structurally diverse selenomorpholine derivatives. For instance, aryl amines bearing electron-donating groups at different ring positions underwent smooth cyclization to afford the desired products (31 − 33) in good yields (66 − 75%). Notably, the meta-substituted analogue furnished product 34 in 66% yield with a site-selectivity ratio of 70:30. Aryl amines featuring synthetically valuable functional groups, including fluorine (35), chlorine (36), bromine (37), iodine (38), cyano (39), and nitro (40), were all amenable to the reaction. Furthermore, the protocol proved equally effective for alkyl-substituted amines (41–43) as well as cyclic secondary amines of varying ring sizes (44–47), affording the corresponding selenomorpholine products in 66–78% yields. Diphenylamines could also be engaged and gave the product 48 in 62% yield. Ethers, alkyl chlorides, carboxylic acid esters, thiophenes, furans, and cyclohexenes were well tolerated under the optimized conditions (49–54). The methodology demonstrated good regioselectivity for substrates containing multiple potential C(sp²)–H reaction sites. For instance, dibenzo[b,d]furan- and 9-ethyl-9H-carbazole-derived amines underwent selective cyclization, affording products 55 and 56 with site-selectivity ratios exceeding 90:10. The robustness of the method was further validated with polycyclic secondary amines such as indoline, 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroquinoline, and 3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]oxazine, which smoothly furnished the corresponding Se, N-heterocycles (57–59) in synthetically useful yields. Finally, the protocol could be extended to α-amino acid derivatives, as demonstrated by the reaction of methyl phenyl-D-alaninate, which produced the 1,4-selenazinane 60 as a 1:1 diastereomeric mixture.

Synthetic diversification and applications

To demonstrate the reaction’s potential for expanding accessible chemical space, we evaluated its compatibility with diverse pharmaceutical fragments and complex molecular architectures (Fig. 4a). Olefin-modified derivatives of indomethacin, adapalene, and estrone underwent efficient conversion to the corresponding selenomorpholine products (61 − 63) while retaining their core structural frameworks. Likewise, amines derived from natural products and functional organic molecules efficiently participated in the three-component cyclization, furnishing the corresponding 1,4-selenazinanes (64 and 65). Furthermore, through simple olefin derivatization of oxaprozin, tolmetin, and febuxostat, the Se-atom transfer annulation enabled direct incorporation of a selenomorpholine motif into these drug-like scaffolds (66–68). We next introduced chain alkenes into aryl amines and subjected them to reaction with S1 in the presence of NFSI. Notably, the Se-atom-transfer annulation proceeds efficiently, rapidly constructing a 5/6 fused bicyclic skeleton (69) that is challenging to access by conventional methods (Fig. 4b). The reaction exhibited broad substrate scope, accommodating both electron-rich (70) and electron-deficient (71–73) aryl amines. Even carbonyl groups, typically prone to undesired side reactions, remained intact under the optimized conditions (74). Single crystals of compound 71 were grown by recrystallization from ethyl acetate/nhexane (1:10) at room temperature. X-ray diffraction analysis (solved using SHELXT and refined with SHELXL within Olex2) confirmed its structure. Extending the carbon chain of aryl amines enabled access to 6/6 bicyclic products (75–80) in 51–73% yields, while a coumarin-derived polycyclic amine underwent cycloaddition with excellent site-selectivity to furnish 81. Further expansion to 7/6 fused systems was also achieved, yielding 83, albeit with diminished efficiency. The practicality of the protocol was validated on a 10-mmol scale, and its synthetic versatility was highlighted through representative post-functionalizations (Fig. 4c). For example, oxidation of 8 with 3-chloroperbenzoic acid (mCPBA) afforded selenium oxide 85 in 77% yield55, while visible-light-mediated C–H functionalization with methyl 2-bromo-2,2-difluoroacetate provided access to fluorinated derivative 86 in 46% yield56.

Mechanistic studies

The mechanism of the Se-transfer [3 + 2 + 1] annulation was investigated using a combination of experimental studies and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The addition of radical inhibitors, including 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO), 9,10-dihydroanthracene (DHA), or butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), did not hamper the reaction (Fig. 5a), and the N-cyclic product 8 was obtained in good yield. These observations exclude the involvement of radical species in the transformation. To identify possible intermediates, a series of control experiments was conducted (Fig. 5b). Treatment of 1 with S1 in the presence of NFSI and HCO₂Na for 3 h afforded the C–H selenylated product 4a in 23% yield (probably formed through nucleophilic selenylation of the electron-rich aniline)57. However, when isolated 4a was resubjected to the standard conditions in the presence of arylalkene 84, no annulated product was detected. Furthermore, when alkene 84 and S1 were subjected to the standard reaction conditions, the olefinic derivative 87 was obtained in 19% yield. This product may form via competitive capture of the alkene-selenonium ion complex by the N(SO₂Ph)₂ anion present in the system, leading to a selenoamidation intermediate, in which the methylselanyl group is subsequently oxidized by NFSI followed by reductive β hydride elimination51. However, when 87 was subjected to the standard reaction conditions, it failed to yield the target product 8. These results suggest that 4a and 87 are unlikely to be intermediates and are better regarded as off-cycle by-products. To gain further insight into the possible pathway, we further examined the reaction under modified conditions. Notably, reducing the loading of NFSI to 0.5 equiv led to the detection of trace amounts of an aminoselenylation adduct 88 by GC–MS. To probe the relevance of this type of species, the related analogue 88a was prepared and found to undergo conversion to the corresponding 1,4-selenazinane 89 under the standard conditions. This result supports the involvement of an alkene aminoselenylation process. In addition, to exclude the possibility that by-product 5 serves as an alternative intermediate, 5 was subjected to the standard conditions, but no target product was detected by GC–MS. Further mechanistic support for a nucleophilic pathway was obtained from a competition experiment between an electron-rich and an electron-poor arylamine (90 vs 91), in which cycloaddition of 84 occurred exclusively with the electron-rich methoxyaniline (Fig. 5c). This outcome is consistent with preferential participation of the more nucleophilic arylamine. The experiment for calculating the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) was carried out using d1-1 as substrate, giving a value of kH/kD ≈ 1 (Fig. 5d). In a parallel experiment, 84 was allowed to react with a 1:1 mixture of N-methylaniline and N-methylaniline-d5 under standard reaction conditions, affording a product with 50% deuterium incorporation. These results indicate that aryl C–H bond cleavage should not be involved in the rate-limiting step.

a Addition of TEMPO, DHA, or BHT did not inhibit the reaction. b Control experiments explored possible intermediates in this transformation. c Competition experiments indicated that the reaction proceeds via a nucleophilic pathway, as suggested by its exclusive cycloaddition with electron-rich anilines. d KIE experiments indicate that cleavage of the C–H bond is not the rate-limiting step. 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy = TEMPO, 9,10-dihydroanthracene = DHA, or butylated hydroxytoluene = BHT.

Based on the results of these mechanistic experiments, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations [B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVP/PCM(THF)//B3LYP-D3/def2-SVP/PCM(THF)]58–62 to disclose the detailed pathway of Se-atom transfer annulation (See the Supplementary Information for the computational details and the Source Data file for the Cartesian coordinates of the optimized structures). The formation of the electrophilic selenium species and the subsequent aminoselenylation of the alkene were investigated using the combined molecular dynamics and coordinate driving methods developed in our laboratory63–65 (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). Although HCO₂Na had a beneficial effect on the yield of selenomorpholine, product formation was still observed in its absence (c.f., Fig. 2 80% versus 61%). Control experiments reveal that in the presence of HCO₂Na, the reaction mixture darkened at a significantly slower rate compared to the additive-free reaction. In addition, the consumption of N-methylaniline was slower in the presence of HCO₂Na, whereas the yield of the desired product was higher at the same reaction time. Monitoring of the reaction by ¹⁹F NMR spectroscopy revealed characteristic signals attributable to fluoride (-109.4 ppm), suggesting the possible generation of NaF during the reaction. Although these experiments do not allow a definitive assignment of its precise function, they support the conclusion that HCO₂Na plays a beneficial and non-trivial role in promoting the reaction. Consequently, to simplify the computational model, HCO₂Na was not explicitly included, and the system comprising N-methylaniline (1), styrene (2), dimethyldiselenide (S1), and NFSI was used for the theoretical study. As shown in Fig. 6, the reaction begins with the electrophilic fluorine transfer from NFSI to dimethyldiselenide, generating the ionic intermediate IMS1 (Supplementary Fig. 10)66. Subsequent cleavage of Se–Se bond in IMS1 furnishes two electrophilic selenium species: MeSe–N(SO2Ph)2 (IM1) and MeSeF (IM2). This fragmentation is strongly exergonic by 37.6 kcal mol–1 overall (and 15.5 kcal mol–1 relative IMS1) and proceeds with a modest fluorine-cation-transfer barrier of 15.0 kcal mol–1. DFT analysis further revealed that IM1 is appreciably more electrophilic than IM2 as evidenced by the lower activation barrier for the subsequent aminoselenylation of styrene (Supplementary Fig. 11). Accordingly, IM1 was identified as the electrophile for the following steps.

Computed energy profile of single-selenium atom transfer annulation reaction.

Calculations were performed at the B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVP/PCM(THF)//B3LYP-D3/def2-SVP/PCM (THF) level of theory. 3D structures of the seleniranium intermediates derived from styrene and 4-phenyl-1-butene highlight their distinct electronic structure (C–Se distances are in Å and natural charges on carbon atoms are in a.u.).

Electrophilic attack of IM1 toward the styrene affords the seleniranium ion intermediate IM3 via transition state TS2, with an activation barrier of 14.3 kcal mol–1. Subsequent addition of N-methylaniline with IM3 generates ionic adduct IM4 (via TS3), and deprotonation of the resulting quaternary ammonium ion by (PhSO2)2N– affords the aminoselenylation adduct IM552,53. The formation of IM5 is consistent with control experiments (c.f., Fig. 5b, e.g., the intramolecular cyclization reaction of 88a). Notably, the exclusive formation of a single diastereomer of product 27 from (Z)-prop-1-en-1-ylbenzene indicates a stereospecific Se-amination process. This stereochemical outcome is consistent with the formation of the proposed alkene-selenonium ion complex IM3, followed by nucleophilic attack of aniline through TS3, and thus supports the mechanistic model shown in Fig. 5. IM5 readily undergoes electrophilic fluorination with NFSI, forming the ionic intermediate IM6 via TS4 (∆G‡ = 16.9 kcal mol–1; ∆G = –23.7 kcal mol–1). The low barrier for this process explains the experimental observation that when the amount of NFSI was increased to 1.0 equiv, the alkene selenoamidation product became barely detectable, indicating that IM5 is not a steady‑state intermediate. A conformational rearrangement converts IM6 into IM7, setting the stage for an intramolecular selenocyclization of the aniline to afford phenonium ions IM8. This cyclization requires a modest barrier of 20.4 kcal mol–1 (via TS5). IM8 readily associates with HCOONa to form the corresponding complex IM8’. This process is exergonic by 12.6 kcal mol–1. In this complex, HCO₂Na promotes deprotonative aromatization accompanied by the elimination of NaF to generate the selenonium salt IM9 (exothermic by 33.1 kcal mol–1). The activation barrier for this process is only 2.9 kcal mol–1 (2.6 kcal mol–1 with the N(SO₂Ph)₂ anion, see Supplementary Fig. 13). Given that HCO₂Na is present in excess (3 equiv) in the reaction system, this pathway represents a potentially favorable competitive channel. The rather low activation barrier for this rearomatization process reveals that C–H bond cleavage is not the rate-limiting step, which is consistent with experimentally observed KIE values (c.f., Fig. 5d, kH/kD ≈ 1.1). Subsequently, the selenonium salt IM9, with N(SO₂Ph)₂ as the nucleophile, undergoes an SN2 reaction to afford the corresponding Me–N(SO₂Ph)₂ (∆G‡ = 23.8 kcal mol–1). Furthermore, the formation of Me(NSO₂Ph)₂ was detected by NMR, confirming the involvement of SN2 reaction for the selenonium salt IM9. Similar selenonium salts have been independently prepared and applied in nucleophilic substitution reactions67. Overall, the nucleophilic ring-opening of the seleniranium intermediate IM3 and the intramolecular selenylation step are the two kinetically decisive transformations, exhibiting nearly identical barriers (20.3 and 20.4 kcal mol–1, respectively). These results are in good accordance with the competition experiments performed with electronically divergent aryl amines.

Further computational analysis of the key seleniranium intermediates provides a clear rationale for the distinct reactivity observed between aryl- and alkyl-substituted alkenes. In the styrene-derived seleniranium (Fig. 6), the markedly disparate C–Se bond lengths (2.177 vs 1.996 Å) and the pronounced charge difference at the olefinic carbons (–0.120 a.u. vs –0.494 a.u.) indicate a significant unsymmetrical three-membered bridge. This electronic polarization favors nucleophilic attack at the more positively polarized carbon, thereby directing the aminoselenylation toward the Markovnikov addition pathway. Consistently, DFT calculations show a 6.2 kcal mol–1 lower activation barrier for the Markovnikov transition state (TS3) relative to the anti-Markovnikov alternative (TSS3). In contrast, the seleniranium intermediate derived from 4-phenyl-1-butene displays nearly equivalent C–Se bond lengths, indicative of a more symmetric bridging interaction and a reduced charge difference between the carbons. This electronic feature weakens regioselective control, increasing the propensity for anti-Markovnikov addition and leading to the observed mixture of regioisomeric products with alkyl-substituted alkenes. For the aminoselenylation reaction of chain alkene N-(pent-4-en-1-yl)aniline, it was found that 5-exo-trig cyclization of seleniranium intermediate is predicted to be kinetically more favorable over the 6-endo-trig cyclization by 5.7 kcal/mol. This result can account for the experimental observation of the formation of the 5/6 fused bicyclic product.

Discussion

In summary, we report a metal-free Se-atom transfer annulation that unites readily available amines, alkenes, and selenides to deliver a broad array of six-membered Se, N-heterocycles. This multi-component cyclization is distinguished by its wide substrate scope, high regioselectivity, and compatibility with complex molecular frameworks, offering new opportunities for late-stage functionalization. Mechanistic studies combining experimental evidence and DFT calculations elucidate the key role of a transient ionic Lewis base-selenium(II) species, providing a fundamental understanding of the Se-atom transfer cycloaddition process. Given its operational simplicity, scalability, and mechanistic distinctiveness, we anticipate that this work will open new avenues for heteroatom-transfer-bridged cycloaddition, providing a robust foundation for the design of polyheteroatomic cyclic molecules.

Methods

General procedure 1 (GP1) for the synthesis of Se,N-heterocycles

To an oven-dried Schlenk tube (10 mL), the corresponding amine (0.45 mmol, 1.5 equiv.), HCO₂Na (0.9 mmol, 61.2 mg, 3.0 equiv.), alkene (0.3 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), THF (1.0 mL), dimethyl diselenide (0.36 mmol, 67.7 mg, 1.2 equiv.), and NFSI (0.6 mmol, 189.2 mg, 2.0 equiv.) were sequentially added in the air atmosphere. The resulting solution was stirred at 40 °C for 3 h and then cooled to room temperature. The reaction mixture was diluted with ethyl acetate (10 mL) and transferred into a separatory funnel. The mixture was washed with a 5 wt% aqueous NaCl solution (3 × 10 mL). The organic phases were dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was purified by flash silica gel column chromatography with nhexane/EtOAc to give the desired product.

General procedure 2 (GP2) for the synthesis of Se,N-heteroatom polycyclic molecules

To an oven-dried Schlenk tube (10 mL), the corresponding amine (0.3 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), THF (2.5 mL), dimethyl diselenide (0.36 mmol, 67.7 mg, 1.2 equiv.), and NFSI (0.6 mmol, 189.2 mg, 2.0 equiv.) were sequentially added in the air atmosphere. The resulting solution was stirred at 40 °C for 3 h and then cooled to room temperature. The reaction mixture was diluted with ethyl acetate (10 mL) and transferred into a separatory funnel. The mixture was washed with a 5 wt% aqueous NaCl solution (3 × 10 mL). The organic phases were dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was purified by flash silica gel column chromatography with nhexane/EtOAc to give the desired product.

Crystallographic data for the structure reported in this study have been deposited at the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center, under deposition numbers CCDC 2403213 (8) and CCDC 2445709 (71). A copy of the data can be obtained free of charge via https://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/structures/. The Cartesian coordinates of DFT-calculated geometries labeled as xyz-coordinates in this study are provided in the “Source Data” file. All data are available from the corresponding author upon request. Source data are provided with this paper.
