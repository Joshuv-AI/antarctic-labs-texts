Endoscopic therapy versus surgical resection for rectal gastrointestinal stromal tumors: a population-based comparative analysis of long-term survival outcomes
Authors: Jiayi Ding, Luojie Liu, Ye Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRectal gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are relatively rare, and the long-term survival outcomes of endoscopic therapy (ET) versus surgical resection (SR) remain uncertain. This study aims to assess the long-term survival in patients with rectal GISTs treated with either ET or SR.MethodsPatients were selected from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Propensity score matching (PSM) was implemented to reduce selection bias in the comparative analyses. The impact of demographic and clinical features on overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) was evaluated through Kaplan-Meier analyses and the use of multivariate Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsA total of 239 patients were included, with 146 undergoing ET and 93 undergoing SR. After PSM, 164 patients were evenly matched for comparison. There was no significant distinction between ET and SR regarding long-term OS (P = 0.592) and CSS (P = 0.832). Furthermore, subgroup analysis considering factors like age, tumor size, year of diagnosis, and sex also found no variance in OS and CSS outcomes between the groups. The multivariate Cox analysis identified advanced age as the sole independent risk factor impacting the prognosis of patients with rectal GISTs.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that ET and SR offer comparable long-term survival outcomes for patients with rectal GISTs, indicating that ET could be considered a viable treatment option.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03720-8.Keywords: Rectal gastrointestinal stromal tumors, Endoscopic therapy, Surgery resection, SEER, Survival

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are mesenchymal tumors found in the gastrointestinal tract, believed to originate from the neoplastic transformation of either the intestinal pacemaker cells (interstitial cells of Cajal) or their precursors [1]. Most countries report an annual incidence of approximately 1.2 cases per 100,000 individuals for GISTs [2]. GISTs predominantly occur in the stomach (60–65%) and small intestine (20–25%), with a low occurrence in the rectum, comprising around 5% of the overall GIST cases [3, 4]. However, GISTs located in the rectum exhibit a higher degree of malignancy compared to those in other sites, contributing to an unfavorable prognosis [5, 6]. Therefore, the guidelines provided by the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) stress the importance of surgical resection for all rectal GISTs, irrespective of the tumor’s size [7].

At present, surgical intervention continues to be the primary approach for treating GISTs [8]. However, when dealing with GISTs located in the rectum, especially those in close proximity to the anal region, surgical intervention frequently requires the simultaneous excision of both the tumor and the anus, which can adversely affect the patient’s overall quality of life. The rapid advancement in endoscopic therapy (ET) has positioned it as a promising alternative for GIST treatment [9]. Numerous studies indicate that ET offers benefits such as shorter operating times, minimized trauma, and quicker postoperative recovery [10–12]. Although ET may result in a higher occurrence of positive margins compared to surgical resection (SR), the majority of cases can achieve a cure through subsequent endoscopic resection [13–15]. However, current research primarily focuses on ET for gastric GIST, and there is a shortage of studies examining population-based ET for rectal GISTs. Moreover, there is a significant lack of comparative studies on the long-term prognosis outcomes between ET and SR for rectal GISTs. Hence, this study utilized the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to analyze the overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) of ET and SR for rectal GISTs, with the aim of providing guidance for surgical strategies in patients with rectal GISTs.

Methods

Patient selection

Patients diagnosed with GIST between 2000 and 2019 were chosen from the SEER database (http://seer.cancer.gov). GIST identification and primary tumor site were based on the third edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) with a specific histologic subtype code of 8936 and a primary site code of C20.9. Exclusion criteria included: [1] unknown survival information; [2] unknown surgery-related information; [3] patients who did not undergo surgery. Covariates extracted included age, race, sex, year of diagnosis, grade, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, clinical M stage, marital status, tumor size, chemotherapy, follow-up time, cancer-specific death, and vital status. RX Summ-Surg Prim Site (1998+) codes were used to distinguish between various surgical techniques, such as ET (codes 20–28) and SR (codes 30–90). The objective of the study was to assess the OS and CSS of patients with rectal GISTs who underwent either ET or SR. OS and CSS were calculated from the time of rectal GISTs diagnosis to the date of death or death from cancer or the most recent follow-up. Since the patient information obtained from the database is anonymous, ethical consent was not required for this study.

Multiple imputation

Missing data was observed in several variables during the study, including race (0.4% of cases), tumor grade (73.2%), T stage (33.9%), N stage (30.5%), M stage (25.1%), tumor size (25.1%), and marital status (5.9%). To handle this issue, a polytomous regression model was applied, integrating the multiple imputation (MI) technique using R software (version 4.1.0). The adoption of this method aimed to enhance the statistical analysis of the study.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons conducted using either the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables displaying non-normal distributions were represented by medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for comparative analyses. One-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to align ET and SR groups. The propensity model included age, race, sex, year of diagnosis, grade, T stage, N stage, M stage, marital status, tumor size, chemotherapy, and follow-up time, with a caliper width set at 0.01. OS and CSS were computed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and group comparisons were made using the log-rank test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were utilized to evaluate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.0), while GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, California, USA) was used for generating Kaplan-Meier curves and performing log-rank tests. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was established to identify noteworthy differences between groups.

Results

Patients characteristics

A total of 239 patients with rectal GISTs meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled, with 146 (61.1%) undergoing ET and 93 (38.9%) undergoing SR. The study protocol is illustrated in Fig. 1 as a flowchart. Prior to PSM, there existed a significant difference (P < 0.05) in patient age, T stage, M stage, tumor size, and chemotherapy between the ET and SR groups. The median (IQR) follow-up period for the ET and SR groups was 63.5 (22.8-111.8) months and 82.0 (35.5–140.0) months, respectively (P = 0.051). After PSM, the analysis comprised 82 matched pairs of patients, with no significant differences observed in any baseline characteristics between the two groups (Table 1). Detailed demographic and clinical data for the patients are presented in Table 1, while Supplementary Table 1 provides a comprehensive comparison of the demographic and clinical features of the two groups prior to MI. The number of rectal GISTs patients treated endoscopically increased from 24 cases (2000–2004) to 56 cases (2015–2019), while the number of rectal GISTs patients treated with SR remained approximately 23 cases every 5 years (Fig. 2).

Survival analysis of rectal gists patients received ET or SR

The average OS rates for the ET group at 1-year, 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year intervals were 94.7%, 87.0%, 79.0%, and 51.0%, respectively. Correspondingly, the CSS rates were 97.3%, 90.9%, 86.2%, and 65.8%. In contrast, patients undergoing SR exhibited mean OS rates of 94.7%, 88.2%, 77.0%, and 54.3% at the same intervals. Their mean CSS rates were 100.0%, 98.4%, 88.7%, and 69.4%, respectively, as presented in Table 2. This table underscores the 5-year survival rate, a critical metric for evaluating the impact of chemotherapy on patient survival. Across both the ET and SR groups, there were no discernible differences in the 5-year OS and CSS rates between patients who received chemotherapy and those who did not (P > 0.05).

Comparison between the ET and SR groups on OS and CSS

During the median (IQR) follow-up period of 69.0 (26.0, 123.0) months, 38 cases in the ET group and 33 cases in the SR group died, with 23 and 20 cases attributed to rectal GISTs, respectively. Prior to PSM, Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank tests indicated that the OS (HR 0.932, 95% CI 0.584–1.489, P = 0.766) and CSS (HR 0.945, 95% CI 0.518–1.723, P = 0.853) were comparable between the ET and SR groups (Fig. 3A and B). After PSM, the ET and SR groups continued to exhibit comparable OS (HR 1.151, 95% CI 0.688–1.4926, P = 0.592) and CSS (HR 1.194, 95% CI 0.604–2.363, P = 0.832) (Fig. 3 C and D).

Multivariate COX regression

The results of the multivariate Cox analysis are presented in Table 3. According to the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, the type of surgical strategy demonstrated no significant impact on OS (HR 0.988, 95% CI 0.566–1.725, P = 0.967) and CSS (HR 0.969, 95% CI 0.451–2.082, P = 0.935). Furthermore, our investigation identified advanced age as the sole independent risk factor influencing the prognosis of patients with rectal GISTs. In comparison to individuals aged ≤ 60 years, those aged > 60 years exhibited inferior OS (HR 6.593, 95% CI 3.363–12.924, P < 0.001) and CSS (HR 6.930, 95% CI 2.741–17.521, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

The results of the multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that age is an independent risk factor influencing patient prognosis. Therefore, we separately compared the OS and CSS of rectal GISTs patients treated with ET and SR in different age groups. The findings revealed that in the group aged ≤ 60 years, there was no significant difference in OS (HR 0.585, 95% CI 0.226–1.515, P = 0.285) and CSS (HR 0.607, 95% CI 0.186–1.979, P = 0.420) between ET and SR treatments (Fig. 4AC). Similarly, in the group aged > 60 years, the OS (HR 1.394, 95% CI 0.755–2.573, P = 0.294) and CSS (HR 1.600, 95% CI 0.693–3.693, P = 0.282) for rectal GISTs patients treated with ET and SR were also comparable (Fig. 4B and D). At the same time, we also analyzed the OS and CSS of rectal GISTs patients treated with ET and SR within different subgroups based on sex, tumor size, and year of diagnosis. The results revealed that the ET group exhibited comparable OS and CSS outcomes to the SR group across these various subgroups (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, 3).

Discussion

Surgical intervention is currently the preferred treatment approach for GIST [8]. The primary surgical guidelines focus on achieving tumor-free resection margins through complete excision while minimizing any compromise to organ functions [16]. However, GISTs in the rectum region are uncommon. The removal of these tumors is often complex due to their anatomical location. In specific situations, when excising the tumors, it may be necessary to include the removal of the anus, resulting in the creation of a stoma and consequently impacting the patient’s quality of life. With the advancement and widespread adoption of endoscopic techniques, an increasing number of endoscopists are using endoscopic methods to treat rectal GISTs [17–19]. Simultaneously, our research indicates a rising trend in the number of rectal GISTs patients undergoing ET, while the number of patients undergoing SR remains relatively stable (Fig. 2). This could be linked to the lesser trauma and quicker postoperative recovery associated with ET. Nevertheless, due to the relatively low incidence of rectal GISTs, there is currently no research evaluating the long-term effects of ET or SR on the survival of these patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the inaugural study examining the long-term impact of ET and SR on the survival of patients with rectal GISTs.

Our findings indicate no significant difference between ET and SR in terms of long-term OS (HR 1.151, 95% CI 0.688–1.926, P = 0.592) and CSS (HR 1.194, 95% CI 0.604–2.363, P = 0.832). Additionally, the study reveals that there is no substantial distinction in the 1-year, 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year OS and CSS rates between the two groups, as detailed in Table 2. Regarding tumor clinical characteristics, our investigation demonstrates that the SR group has a higher proportion of tumors exceeding 5.0 cm compared to the ET group (50.5% versus 27.4%, P < 0.001). This observation may be attributed to the capability of SR to provide a more extensive field of vision and direct accessibility, facilitating the thorough removal of larger tumors. Furthermore, larger rectal GISTs may involve a broader tissue area, necessitating a more extensive excision, and SR typically allows for a more comprehensive tumor removal, ensuring the preservation of surrounding tissue integrity and minimizing the risk of recurrence. In a study by Yan et al. [20], comparing ET and SR treatments for duodenal GISTs, it was also noted that the SR group exhibited a larger tumor size (63.0 mm versus 45.8 mm, P = 0.030). However, our study highlighted that the OS and CSS of ET and SR remained comparable in tumor size groups less than 5.0 cm and greater than 5.0 cm (P >0.05). Subgroup analysis, considering factors such as age, year of diagnosis, and sex, also revealed no variance in OS and CSS outcomes between the groups.

In a multicenter study, Jia et al. [21] analyzed the clinical outcomes of laparoscopic versus open surgery in the treatment of rectal GISTs. The results revealed that laparoscopic surgery provides a safe and feasible alternative for the radical resection of primary localized rectal GISTs. Simultaneously, substantial research has been conducted on endoscopic treatment (ET) for gastric GISTs [22–24]. However, reports on ET for rectal GISTs are relatively limited, presumably owing to the lower incidence in this anatomical location. Due to the exceptionally low likelihood of lymph node metastasis in GISTs, the standard approach typically entails excising the tumor without the necessity for lymph node dissection [25]. Consequently, minimally invasive endoscopic approaches have progressively found application in the management of rectal GISTs. Currently, a variety of ETs are utilized for treating rectal GISTs, including endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR), submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection (STER), and transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM) [23, 26, 27]. ESD is mainly suitable for superficial GISTs, whereas EFTR techniques are applied to tumors originating from the muscularis propria. There are also documented cases of using STER for rectal GISTs treatment [17, 18]. TEM is commonly employed in rectal GISTs to preserve anal sphincter function [26, 27]. Previous studies have indicated that ESD demonstrates comparable resection rates, side effects, and recurrence rates to TEM, but with shorter treatment and hospitalization periods [28, 29].

Our research reveals that advanced age is the exclusive factor determining the prognosis of rectal GISTs. In line with our results, Kramer et al.‘s research also demonstrates a worse prognosis in elderly GIST patients [30]. Khan et al. [31] and Güller et al. [32], using data from the SEER database, observe a poorer prognosis in GIST patients aged over 60. While analyzing metastatic patterns and prognosis in GIST patients, Yang et al. [33] point out that being over 65 is a significant risk factor affecting outcomes. Wu et al., in their investigation of the prognosis of GIST patients with metastasis, find that age remains a pivotal factor influencing results [34]. The diminished prognosis observed in older GIST patients can be ascribed to the interplay of various factors. To begin with, as individuals age, the physiological functions of the body tend to decline, encompassing a reduction in the effectiveness of both the immune system and organ functions. This poses a greater challenge for elderly individuals in effectively managing diseases like GIST. Furthermore, individuals in the older age brackets may concurrently grapple with other chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, amplifying the intricacy of treatment and impacting its overall effectiveness. Additionally, variations in the biological characteristics of tumors across different age groups may contribute to this phenomenon, with rectal GISTs in older patients potentially manifesting more aggressive features.

The emerging role of immunotherapy in rare rectal malignancies, particularly anorectal melanoma, offers valuable insights for rectal GISTs management. While surgical resection remains the cornerstone for localized rectal GISTs, recent studies highlight immunotherapy’s potential in anorectal melanoma, where neoadjuvant approaches (e.g., ipilimumab/nivolumab) have shown partial responses, enabling sphincter-preserving strategies [35]. Similarly, adjuvant immunotherapy demonstrated comparable survival benefits to chemotherapy in metastatic anorectal melanoma, underscoring its systemic efficacy [36]. For rectal GISTs, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like imatinib dominate adjuvant therapy, but immunotherapy’s success in mucosal melanomas prompts exploration of combined modalities—especially for high-risk or metastatic GISTs. Future trials should evaluate immune checkpoint inhibitors in GISTs, leveraging lessons from anorectal melanoma to refine multidisciplinary paradigms. However, current evidence prioritizes surgery and TKIs for GISTs, while immunotherapy remains investigational.

Our study has several notable limitations. Firstly, this study is a retrospective analysis based on the SEER database, which inherently carries the risk of data omissions and biases. Notably, a substantial proportion of cases had missing values for key covariates, such as tumor grade (over 70% missing) and other critical factors (25–34% missing). Although we employed MI and PSM techniques to mitigate the impact of missing data and selection bias, these methods cannot completely eliminate such limitations. Secondly, while our findings suggest a comparable prognosis between the ET and SR groups, the choice of treatment for rectal GISTs should ultimately be guided by the patient’s specific clinical condition and preferences. Thirdly, we acknowledge a significant limitation of our study stemming from the inherent constraints of the SEER database. Specifically, the database lacks detailed information on postoperative complications, recurrence rates, margin status, mitotic rate, as well as genetic mutations of KIT or PDGFRA. These factors are critical and could potentially influence the long-term prognosis of patients with rectal GISTs. The absence of such data precludes a more comprehensive assessment of the comparative effectiveness of ET and SR in these aspects, and limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the superiority or equivalence of one procedure over the other. Future prospective studies are warranted to incorporate these variables and provide a more nuanced understanding of the treatment outcomes for rectal GISTs. Additionally, rectal GISTs are relatively uncommon, and surgical resection remains the predominant treatment approach. Despite our extensive retrospective analysis spanning two decades and involving 239 cases from the SEER database, PSM resulted in only 82 matched pairs for both the ET and SR groups. This limited sample size, particularly in subgroup analyses of more advanced disease (e.g., tumor size > 5 cm or T3–T4 stage), significantly reduces the statistical power to detect clinically relevant differences between the two treatment modalities. Importantly, a non-significant result in these subgroups does not confirm equivalence or non-inferiority of ET compared to SR, as a formal non-inferiority analysis was not conducted, and sample size calculations suggest that a larger cohort would be required to detect such differences with adequate power. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first exploration into the long-term effects of ET and SR on the survival of individuals with rectal GISTs, providing valuable insights that warrant further investigation in larger, prospective studies.

In summary, our study, based on the SEER database, serves as a preliminary exploration into the long-term survival outcomes of ET versus SR for rectal GISTs. Our findings indicated similar long-term survival rates between ET and SR, suggesting that ET could be a viable surgical option for addressing rectal GISTs. Nevertheless, we recognize the limitations of our study, including its retrospective nature and the absence of a formal non-inferiority design. Therefore, while our findings provide valuable insights, they should be interpreted with caution, and larger-scale studies are necessary to establish more robust evidence regarding the enduring impacts of endoscopy on patients with rectal GISTs.
