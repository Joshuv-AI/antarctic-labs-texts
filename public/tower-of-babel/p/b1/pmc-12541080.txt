Advanced machine learning models for predicting unconfined compressive strength from point load strength index of rock samples from Chennai and Bangalore
Authors: Sowmya Kochukrishnan, Premalatha Krishnamurthy, Chintaluri Venkata Prasad, M Navaneethan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the effectiveness of point load tests (PLI), specifically both diametral (PLId) and axial tests (PLIa), in forecasting various rock types’ Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS). Additionally, it examines the implementation of Regression learner app with five machine learning (ML) models to enhance prediction accuracy. These models include Linear Regression, Stepwise Linear Regression, Support Vector Machine, Gaussian Process Regression, and Neural Network. The investigation focuses on rock samples sourced from Pallavaram in Chennai and Panathur in Bangalore. To ensure the reliability of the developed ML models and to assess the best predictive model, performance metrics such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Coefficient of Correlation (R²) were employed for validation. The findings suggest that all tested models exhibited commendable performance correlating the parameters through the training and testing phases. Notably, the Neural Network and Gaussian Process Regression models surpassed the performance of the other methods, demonstrating their superior predictive capabilities. The study’s results signify a robust correlation between PLI and the UCS of rocks sampled from Pallavaram and Panathur, indicating that the PLI can serve as a valuable metric for anticipating the UCS of rocks. Moreover, an analysis of the correlation across various models revealed that the PLI test performed in the axial direction shows a stronger relationship with UCS, yielding a Coefficient of Correlation (R²) of 0.996, in contrast to the diametral point load index which resulted in an R² of 0.991, applicable for both Pallavaram and Panathur rock samples.Keywords: Unconfined compressive strength, Point load index, Regression learning app, Machine learning models, Chennai rocks, Bangalore rocksSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The rocks’ uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) constitutes the paramount geomechanical parameter, typically assessed in the laboratory under confined or unconfined conditions. This parameter is critical in various design formulas and is occasionally employed as an index property for selecting suitable excavation techniques.

The process of obtaining rock core samples is influenced by several field factors, especially the drilling machine’s parameters and the rock mass’s geotechnical attributes. These machine parameters are determined by the drilling technique and the drill bit’s characteristics, while the geological features are influenced by the rock mass’s structure, mechanical behavior, and mineral composition1. The alignment of the rock mass sample concerning the applied stress direction influences the UCS test results. The most dependable strength index is achieved when samples are drilled perpendicular to the bedding planes. Drilling and sample preparation in soft rocks present more challenges than hard rock materials, suggesting that sample preparation and laboratory testing methods for soft rocks resemble those used for soils. Issues have arisen while preparing mudstone samples for direct shear testing in the laboratory2. The American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM)3 require a minimum l/d ratio of 2.0 for performing UCS tests on rock core specimens. However, collecting sufficient samples of the required dimensions is uncertain because it relies on various factors. Researchers are looking for correlation equations to determine the UCS of rocks. Having more samples would enhance the correlation and define the boundary lines better. A larger dataset would yield a more precise simple linear regression line, suggesting a more accurate correlation.

Many researchers have indicated relationships between different rock types to estimate UCS using easily measurable lab parameters4,5. One frequently utilized parameter for these correlations is the point load strength index, a simple test that requires minimal specimen preparation and can handle irregular samples6. The portable equipment allows the test to be conducted under very low loads. The (ASTM) suggests four methods for obtaining the Point load Index of rocks7. The correlation most frequently utilized in rock mechanics and geotechnical engineering to estimate the UCS is by Brock and Franklin8. The correlation was obtained from fifteen samples measuring 50 mm in diameter, and the authors suggest that the value of 24 should be considered approximate due to the limited number of rock types tested. This value may not accurately reflect all rock types or conditions9. It was recommended that mining engineers and practitioners limit the application of empirical equations to the same rock types from which they were derived. The statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate the performance of these equations and determine the effectiveness of index and physical tests in providing accurate UCS estimations for three rock types: granite, schist, and sandstone from India10. Singh et al.11 proposed a conversion factor of 21–24 for harder Indian rocks and 14–16 for softer ones. They emphasized using these conversion factors cautiously, noting that varying factors should apply to different rock types to prevent over- or under-estimation of the UCS value for design purposes.

The feasibility of substituting the point load strength index test with the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) for mudstones was studied12. The aim was to guide scientific choices related to roadway planning, selection of support techniques, and mining engineering. It was found that the UCS values of Shale and Marlstone from Iran correlate well with the point load index and align with earlier correlation equations13. Jamshidi and Sousa14 presented straightforward correlation equations derived from multiple regression analysis, highlighting porosity’s influence on the accuracy of UCS predictions based on the point load index of limestone and sandstone. Mohamad15 proposed correlation equations relating UCS to the point load index for Penang Island’s granites, also classifying the rocks based on UCS values. Wang et al.16 examined the point load test’s effectiveness in estimating UCS for dolomite rocks in both dry and saturated conditions, finding that logarithmic fitting yielded a higher coefficient of correlation compared to linear fitting.

Most studies identified a linear relationship between the UCS and point load index, although power functions were occasionally noted. Kahraman17 examined the correlations between the UCS and point load index specifically for pyroclastic rocks, comparing the obtained results to existing literature. Regression analysis performed with Centurion XV software indicated that an exponential function more accurately represents the relationship between UCS for dry and saturated rocks.

ASTM D5731-167 has recommended various approaches for conducting the point load strength index test. Research reveals that the diametral method for point load is more user-friendly and straightforward than the two alternative methods (axial and irregular) when applied to gypsum rocks18.

Aladejare10 recommended utilizing machine learning and soft computing techniques when various input data types are accessible at a rock or project site, especially when multiple regressions are needed to estimate UCS. Li et al.19 developed a prediction SVM model using a machine learning algorithm for the correlation between UCS and point load from various researchers. They tested 30 limestone samples to establish correlations and created a highly accurate computational model for predicting uniaxial compressive strength with the support vector machine algorithm. Due to its simplicity, Niu et al.20 developed a predictive model using K-nearest neighbour (KNN). They employed two optimisation techniques, resulting in an optimised KNN model with R2 values over 0.98 in training and testing. However, while effective for UCS prediction in specific rock types, its performance may vary across different geological formations. Lei et al.21 conducted a study to predict UCS using five input parameters and six optimized BP neural networks with swarm intelligence algorithms. Their findings indicate that the hybrid models significantly outperform traditional models, with the FA-BP hybrid model demonstrating the best performance among all tested models. Kochukrishnan et al.22 developed two machine learning models—simple linear regression and stepwise linear regression—in Python to predict the UCS of rocks. The stepwise model achieved higher accuracy with lower mean absolute and root mean square errors. The UCS and point load for charnokite rocks from Pallavaram correlated at 0.85. Researchers examined how effectively machine learning models like Random Forest, Relevance Vector Machine, Support Vector Machine, and Artificial Neural Network can predict the UCS of rocks23. Petrographic properties were analyzed to predict UCS through advanced machine learning methods. This study presents a novel indirect strategy utilizing machine learning models, including Multiple Linear Regression, Support Vector Regression, Bi-directional Long Short-Term Memory, along with two hybrid models: artificial neural networks combined with teaching learning-based optimization (ANN-TLBO) and particle size optimization (ANN-PSO), all employing petrographic properties. Additionally, the authors found that quartz mineral significantly affects UCS prediction24. Random forest regression was suitable for estimating rocks’ uniaxial compressive strength, achieving minimal absolute error and a higher correlation coefficient than other machine learning algorithms. The authors estimated the UCS of rocks using point load strength, porosity, Schmidt rebound hardness, block punch index, and specific gravity25. The study26 indicates that integrating AI and digital technologies in tunnel surveillance represents a major shift, offering significant advantages over traditional methods. AI-driven monitoring systems can improve safety, extend infrastructure lifespan, and reduce operational costs, shaping the future of subterranean management. The study27 analyzed ensemble neural network models, including bagging neural network (BaggingNN), model averaging (MA), separate stacking (SS), and integrated stacking (IS) algorithms, for predicting failure modes in flat slabs. The results show that the IS algorithm outperformed BaggingNN, MA, and SS, as well as prior models.

This study was initiated with the primary objective of investigating the effectiveness of the Point Load Index (PLI) in estimating the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) of rocks in the Pallavaram region. This investigation arises from the challenge of obtaining core samples necessary for conducting UCS tests in various scenarios, where access to such samples is limited or unfeasible. To enhance the study’s rigor, both PLIa and PLId tests were conducted to compare the performance of these different PLI tests. Furthermore, the scope of this research was expanded to include rock samples from the Panathur area, thereby broadening the geographical context of the findings. Linear Regression is a widely recognized and frequently employed statistical technique for correlating various parameters within geological studies. In this research, however, an innovative approach was taken by implementing five distinct machine learning (ML) models to examine the potential for improving the accuracy of the correlation between UCS and PLI. The ML models investigated in this study encompass Linear Regression, Stepwise Linear Regression, Support Vector Machine, Gaussian Process Regression, and Neural Networks. These analyses were conducted using a MATLAB application (Regression learning app), effectively harnessing advanced computational tools to enhance the predictive capabilities of understanding rock strength properties.

Materials and methods

Significant developments, such as metro projects, IT corridors, double-decker flyovers, and elevated corridors, are on the horizon in major cities like Pallavaram and Panathur. These key projects necessitate rock formations as their foundational support. Therefore, rocks for this study were obtained from borehole sites in Pallavaram and Panathur.

Sampling area

As part of the Geotechnical Investigation, boreholes were drilled at specific locations in Zamin Pallavaram, a suburb in the southern region of Pallavaram, and Panathur in the eastern part of Panathur. Collection and Preparation of rock core samples

This study employed rotary drilling as the primary method for obtaining rock samples from various locations. This drilling technique entails rotating a drill bit at high speeds, which can be paired with significant torque and thrust levels to penetrate rugged geological formations. Alternatively, the drill can be operated at lower speeds while still applying high torque and thrust, allowing versatility in different drilling scenarios. The specific characteristics of the drill bit—such as its material composition and geometric shape—are critical determinants of drilling effectiveness and efficiency. In this research, a diamond drill bit, renowned for its exceptional hardness and durability, was utilized to facilitate the continuous sampling of the rock formations. This approach ensures that samples are collected in a manner that preserves their integrity for subsequent analysis. Such careful sampling is essential for accurate geological assessment and understanding of the subsurface materials.

Boreholes were drilled at ten locations in Pallavaram and 35 in Panathur. The collected rock samples’ depths varied between 5.0 m and 20.0 m at Pallavaram and from 2.0 m to 10.0 m in Panathur. The chosen rock quality ranged from good to excellent, with RQD values between 75 and 90 in Pallavaram and 90 to 100 in Panathur, respectively27. At each depth, samples were extracted for Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) and point load testing (axial and diametral).

The authors supervised most field operations, sampling, and laboratory analyses. Additionally, sample preservation protocols were rigorously followed, with samples stored in core boxes and handled carefully to maintain integrity (Fig. 2).

Laboratory testing

The rock samples meeting good and excellent criteria for the RQD value were chosen for the UCS, PLIa, and PLId tests. A total of ninety-three garnetiferous granite rock samples from Pallavaram and one hundred and forty-nine samples from Panathur underwent testing.

Upon retrieval, core samples carefully stored in core boxes were subjected to immediate testing to determine their water content.

All tests conducted during this study were meticulously performed in strict adherence to the ASTM standards, ensuring the reliability and validity of the results. Specifically, for the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) test, the specimens were prepared with a precise length-to-diameter ratio of 2, utilizing samples with a diameter of 54 mm. The preparation and testing processes followed ASTM guidelines3, ensuring consistency and accuracy throughout the testing procedure. The machine used for compression testing was an automatic CTM with a capacity of 200 T. All the rocks tested were free from fractures and were evaluated under dry conditions. In addition to the UCS test, point load tests were also executed, encompassing both axial and diametral testing methods, again adhering to ASTM protocols7 for proper execution and measurement. Predictive models in regression learner application

The Regression Learner app was used to train regression models for data prediction. The dataset variable was selected to operate the regression learner app, and the values for the Response and Predictors fields were set according to our needs. The app first trained and validated the data, then tested it to present a comparison between the actual and predicted results. The percentage of data for testing was specified, a test dataset was set aside, and the start button was clicked.

The Models pane was accessed to select the desired models. Upon selection, the models appeared on the left side of the status bar. Next, the data from the specified file was trained by clicking the Train All button, which trained the chosen model. The models for this predictive analysis included Linear Regression, Stepwise Linear Regression, Support Vector Machine Regression, Gaussian Process Regression, and Neural Networks. After training, each model’s status changed to Validation. 70% of the dataset was used for training. The models were then tested by clicking the Test All button to test either the selected or all models. 30% of the dataset was used for testing. Once tested, the status for each model changed to Test. The results were reviewed by clicking the drop-down in PLOT and INTERPRET and selecting the desired result fields. To view the summary of results for all tested models, the Results Table was clicked, and to export the results to a file, the third icon on top of the results table was clicked, as highlighted below. Models tested

Linear regression

A linear regression model illustrates the association between a response (output) and predictor (input) variables. In this model, the response variable is defined by a linear equation in terms of the regression coefficient of the predictor variable.

Stepwise regression

Stepwise linear models construct a linear model by automatically adding or removing terms. To create a small model, start with a constant model. In contrast, a large model begins with multiple terms. A large model often shows lower errors based on its fit with the original data, but may not predict new data better.

Support vector machine regression (SVM)

A support vector machine (SVM) model should be trained using fitrsvm to ensure the accuracy of low- to medium-dimensional data sets. A linear regression model, including a linear SVM model, should be trained with fitrlinear for faster computation on high-dimensional data sets.

Gaussian process regression

Nonparametric, kernel-based probabilistic models are Gaussian process regression (GPR) models. Users can interactively train a GPR model using the regression learner app. Once training is complete, predictions for new data can be made by passing the model along with the new predictor data to the predict object function.

Neural networks

In neural network models, layers are designed to replicate how the brain processes information. The regression neural networks in the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox are categorized as fully connected feedforward networks, allowing users to customize layer sizes and activation functions. The Regression Learner app or the fitrnet function can enhance flexibility and initiate training. After training, predictions for new data can be made by supplying the model with new predictors to the predict function.

This study utilized the Regression Learner application to train five distinct predictive models on a laboratory-tested rock samples dataset. The models included Linear Regression, Stepwise Linear Regression, Support Vector Machine Regression, Gaussian Process Regression, and Neural Networks.

The dataset was divided to ensure robust training and evaluation, with 70% allocated for training the models and 30% reserved for testing their performance. The effectiveness of these regression models in accurately predicting the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) of rocks based on Point Load Index (PLI) measurements was assessed using several regression metrics. Specifically, Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of correlation (R²) were employed to evaluate the models’ accuracy and reliability. The foundational database for this research was developed through systematic laboratory testing of various rock specimens, ensuring comprehensive data for model training and validation.

In the study examining rocks sourced from the Pallavaram and Panathur regions, the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) was predicted utilizing two key indices: the diametral point load index and the axial point load index. These indices provide valuable insights into the mechanical properties of the rocks under study. The analysis revealed that all the predictive models employed were statistically significant in forecasting the UCS of these geological samples. Among the various models assessed, the Gaussian model and the Neural Network emerged as the most effective, demonstrating superior accuracy in predicting the UCS of rocks. This indicates that these advanced modeling techniques may be particularly well-suited for geological studies where precise strength predictions are critical.

Conclusion

The study conducted laboratory research and developed a dataset featuring UCS and PLI measurements (axial and diametral) for rocks from Pallavaram and Panathur. Approximately five machine learning models were used to forecast UCS based on these regions’ axial and diametral PLI data. The performance of the models was assessed using regression metrics, including RMSE, R², and MAE. The following conclusions were drawn from the study:

The axial point load test has been observed to outperform the diametral point load test when it comes to predicting the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of garnetiferous rocks found in the Pallavaram region, as well as granite rocks located in the Panathur region.

The axial point load test has been observed to outperform the diametral point load test when it comes to predicting the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of garnetiferous rocks found in the Pallavaram region, as well as granite rocks located in the Panathur region.

Five models—Linear Regression, Stepwise Regression, SVM, Gaussian Process Regression (GR), and Neural Networks (NN) were utilized to predict the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) of rocks based on Point Load Index (PLI) measured in the diametral and axial directions. In samples collected from Pallavaram, the correlation between UCS and PLIa indicated that Gaussian Process Regression was the most effective, achieving a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.86, a Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of 1.06, and an R² value of 0.996. Conversely, the Linear Regression model demonstrated the poorest performance, with a MAE of 3.22, an RMSE of 4.58, and an R² of 0.933.
