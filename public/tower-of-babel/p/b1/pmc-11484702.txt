Aphidicidal activity of nano-emulsions of spearmint oil and carvone against Rhopalosiphum maidis and Sitobion avenae
Authors: Partha Chandra Mondal, Rajna Salim, Vijay Kumar, Parshant Kaushik, Najam Akhtar Shakil, Pankaj, Virendra Singh Rana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Different species of aphids, responsible for severe yield losses of cereal crops including wheat, (Triticum aestivum L.) are managed by insecticides, which are harmful to organisms and the environment under field conditions. Therefore, an environment friendly aphidicidal product of plant origin is required. Mentha spicata oil was found to be rich in carvone (81.88%), but the use of its oil and carvone in crop protection is lacking due to their volatility, poor solubility, and stability. A nanoformulaton not only solves these problems but also improve the efficacy and dose of the bioactive compounds. Thus, nano-emulsions of the oil and carvone prepared were characterized, and evaluated against Rhopalosiphum maidis (corn aphid) and Sitobion avenae (wheat aphid) The average droplet size of nano-emulsions of the oil and carvone was found to be 22.1 and 41.21 nm. Nano-emulsion of carvone exhibited higher aphid mortality (LC50 = 0.87–1.94 mg/mL) at 24 h and acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity (IC50 = 0.07–3.83 mg/mL) compared to the nano-emulsion of the oil (LC50 = 2.87–2.81 mg/mL; IC50 = 1.66–5.34 mg/mL). The repellence index (RI) in nano-emulsion of essential oil was found to be higher (84.73 and 81.72%) at the highest concentration (0.05 µL/cm2) than that of carvone (77.59 and 80.98%) for R. maidis and S. avenae. Further, in silico studies also revealed the favourable binding energy (− 6.6 to − 8.5 kcal/mol) of the main compounds in the oil with acetylcholinesterase, facilitated by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. This study suggests that the nano-emulsions of the essential oil and carvone can be explored under field conditions to establish efficacy for their utilization as aphidicidal and repellent products against aphids. In the present study, aphidicial and repellent activities of its essential oil and carvone were reported for the first time against R.maidis and S.avenae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74149-2.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), belonging to the family Poaceae, is the principal cereal crop of about 36% of the population worldwide1. It is reported to be first cultivated about ten thousand years ago during the Neolithic revolution2,3, and is rich in carbohydrates, protein (gluten), minerals, vitamins, and dietary fibres1. In India, wheat is the second most important cereal crop after rice and is a staple food for Northern India4. During the various stages of its growth and development, the wheat crop is attacked by many insect pests under field conditions, including a complex species of aphids5. In wheat, different aphids cause direct damage by sucking the cell sap, especially during the milky grain stage, resulting in the development of sooty mold on plant leaves6,7 causing about 3.53–21.05% yield loss in different wheat varieties8. Climate change has been a major factor in further intensifying the damage caused by wheat aphids9. Among these, Rhopalosiphum maidis (corn aphid) and Sitobion avenae (wheat aphid) are the most economically important aphids infesting wheat crop along with other species10. Currently, aphids are controlled by the application of synthetic insecticides under field conditions, but their prolonged and indiscriminate use has resulted in an adverse impact on the natural enemies and environment11. The resistance in aphids against insecticides belonging to organophosphate, neonicotinoids, synthetic pyrethroids and macrolides has also been reported12–15. Thus, the identification of an eco-friendly and safer alternative of plant origin for aphid management in crops is urgently needed.

Botanical extracts and their bioactive compounds have been used as the pesticidal agents against various pests and diseases for centuries16. However, with the advancement of research and development in synthetic pesticide chemistry, synthetic pesticides gained popularity due to their immediate knock-down effect on insect pests and thus have largely substituted the use of botanicals worldwide17. The use of synthetic pesticides together with improved crop varieties, agricultural practices, plant nutrients has dramatically increased agricultural production globally18. However, continuous, and unscientific use of toxic synthetic pesticides has given rise to newer challenges such as pest resistance and resurgence, toxic residues hazardous to non-target and beneficial organisms, the environment, and human beings, which are now of major concern. To overcome these issues, there is a need to identify target specific, effective, biodegradable and environment friendly pest control agents of natural or synthetic origin. The search for a safer alternative of biological origin has received a major push over the last few decades19. Plant secondary metabolites have always been a rich source of useful and model molecules for synthetic pesticide research and development. They also have the potential to be a sustainable alternative for toxic and persistent synthetic pesticides either alone or as a component of integrated pest management strategies20.

Plants are known to be a rich source of bioactive secondary metabolites responsible for protection of plants from biotic and abiotic stresses21. Of these, essential oils from different parts of the plants reported to have pest control, antimicrobial, nematicidal, and antifungal properties, which can be investigated for development of an environmentally benign and effective alternatives to harmful synthetic chemicals for management of pests and diseases22. However, their volatility, lipophilicity, water insolubility and instability, limit their uses in crop protection. The use of nanotechnology driven techniques for development of nano-formulation can solve these major issues23–25. Nano-emulsions are transparent to slightly translucent dispersion solutions of active ingredients in aqueous medium that are stabilized by surfactants and other adjuvants increasing their water miscibility26. Also, nano-sizing of essential oils helps in increasing their bioefficacy by many folds, owing to the improved dispersibility and high diffusivity in aqueous medium. The bioavailability of the active ingredients is also increased resulting from the increase in surface area owing to the reduced particle size27,28. Nano-emulsions are prepared by using both high energy and low energy methods29, of which the high energy methods like high-pressure homogenization and ultrasonication are much effective in bringing the droplet size into the nano range30.

Mentha spicata, an aromatic herb and commonly known as spearmint, is one of the most important species of the genus Mentha (Family: Lamiaceae). It is commercially cultivated in different parts of India for the production of essential oil31,32. The different parts of the plants are also used in cold, cough, nausea, flatulence, bronchitis, liver complaints, and colitis and are considered stimulant, antiemetic, diaphoretic, carminative, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, emmenagogue, antispasmodic, and anti-catarrhal32–34. The essential oil from M. spicata, has been investigated by many workers worldwide but the yield of essential oil and composition vary with the geographical location, season, and even different varieties within the same species35. The oil can be isolated by hydrodistillation36, steam distillation and supercritical fluid extraction methods37,38. Its oil has been widely used in aromatherapy, medicine, cosmetic and flavouring purposes31–34. The essential oil is also reported to possess insecticidal activity against stored-grain insects, mosquitoes, houseflies besides acaricidal, nematicidal, fungicidal, and antibacterial activities39. The essential oil showed potent activity against storage pests, Rhyzopertha dominica (LD50 = 6.1 µL/mL)40, Sitophilus oryzae (LC50 = 2.86–10.75 µL/L)41, Callosobruchus chinensis (LC50 = 0.94 µL/mL)42, Sitophilus granarius (LC50 = 0.94 µL/mL)43 and against Culex quinquefasciatus (LC50 = 92 ppm), Aedes aegypti (LC50 = 56.08 ppm) and A. stephensi (LC50 = 49.71 ppm) insects44.

Earlier studies showed that the essential oil is primarily dominated by monoterpenoids such as carvone, limonene, 1,8-cineole, linalool, α-pinene, β-myrcene, dihydrocarvone, piperitone, and piperitone as well as some sesquiterpenoids, β-bourbonene, caryophyllene, elemene, Z-β-farnesene, germacrene-D, and caryophyllene oxide31,33. Carvone has been reported as a major compound in spearmint essential oils from different parts of India31–33,45−47. However, the oils from Brazillian spearmints were found to contain piperitone (81.18%) and piperitone oxide (94.8%) as the major compounds48,49. Linalool (86.7%) was found to be a major compound in a Greek spearmint50 while pulgeone (56.28%) and limonene (48.0%) were main compounds in two Iranian spearmints51,52. These studies showed a great variation even in major compounds of the oil of M. spicata growing in India and abroad. Although, significant work on the chemical composition of its oil and use in perfumery, medicinal and cosmetic purposes have been done but its oil has not been explored against aphids in agriculture. Nano-formulations have attracted the attention of many researchers for drugs and pesticides delivery applications53–55. Studies showed that the nano-formulations of the bioactive compounds have more advantages over conventional formulations that improve solubility, bioavailability, control release, efficacy, low toxicity with reduced dose and particle size30,55–56. Thus, the aphidicial and repellent activities of the nano-emulsions, prepared from the essential oil of M. spicata and its major compound, carvone, was carried out against R. maidis and S. avenae with a focus on understanding the underlying mechanism of action through biochemical as well as in silico molecular docking studies.

Results

Yield and composition of essential oil

The yield of essential oil isolated from the dried leaves of M. spicata was found to be 1.35% on a dry weight basis. The volatile composition of the essential oil was analyzed using GC-FID and GC-MS. Results showed the presence of twenty-seven compounds, accounting for 96.26% of the oil. The oil was found to be rich in monoterpenoids with a minor amount of sesquiterpenoids (Table 1; Fig. 1). In the monoterpenoid group of compounds, carvone (81.88%), limonene (8.03%), and eucalyptol (1.29%) were identified as the main compounds. Sesquiterpenoids (3.62%) were found to be relatively less abundant in the essential oil and β-bourbonene (1.22%), and caryophyllene (0.94%) were the main compounds.

Characterization and stability of nano-emulsions

Triton X-100 was found to be the most suitable surfactant with a surfactant to oil/ carvone ratio (1:1), based on the solubility, compatibility, and stability studies of the nano-emulsions of essential oil and carvone. The properties of nano-emulsions are summarized in Table 2. The average droplet size of nano-emulsion of the essential oil was found to be 22.1 nm with a Polydispersity Index (PDI) value of 0.51, while that of carvone’s nano-emulsion, the average droplet size and PDI values were 41.21 nm and 0.53, respectively (Fig. 2). The narrow PDI range indicates a uniform size distribution of the droplets, an essential feature for the stability of nano-emulsion. No phase separation, droplet aggregation or any other form of instability were observed during freezing and thawing tests and the nano-emulsions remained transparent. The zeta potential values for the nano-emulsions of essential oil and carvone were − 0.33 and − 9.98 mV, respectively (Fig. 2), indicating that the nano-emulsions were primarily stabilized through steric interaction between the surfactant molecules. TEM analysis of the nano-emulsions revealed the spherical-shaped nanodroplets of essential oil and carvone (Fig. 3) with droplet diameters ranging from 17.7 to 51.7 nm and 24.2 to 67.7 nm, respectively. The kinetic stability of the nano-emulsions exhibited no aggregation of droplets in TEM analysis.

Aphidicidal activity

The effect of different concentrations of nano-emulsions of essential oil and carvone on the mortality of R. maidis and S. avenae is given in Fig. 4. The analysis of variance at p < 0.05 followed by the post-hoc test using Tukey’s HSD confirmed the significant difference between the mean of mortality at different concentrations of the essential oil and carvone. Results showed the mortality of both aphids increased with increase in concentration and time of exposure (Tables 3 and 4). The nano-emulsion of carvone was found to be more effective, causing 100% mortality of R. maidis after 48 h at 0.5% concentration, compared to the nano-emulsion of essential oil in which 90.21% mortality at the highest concentration (0.75%) was recorded in similar duration of exposure. Similarly, nano-emulsion of carvone showed promising action against S. avenae at 0.5% concentration, resulting in 96.16% mortality after 48 h (Table 5). The LC50 of the nano-emulsions was calculated by probit analysis, which ranged between 0.87 and 2.87 mg/mL for R. maidis and 1.64–2.81 mg/mL for S. avenae (Table 5).

Repellent activity of nano-emulsions

Nano-emulsion of essential oil from M. spicata displayed more repellent activity against R. maidis and S. avenae, than the nano-emulsion of carvone. The repellent activity of nano-emulsions increased with increase in concentration of the essential oil and carvone. The repellence index (RI) was found higher (84.73% and 81.72%) at highest concentration (0.05 µL/cm2) in nano-emulsion of essential oil for S. avenae and R. maidis, compared to carvone (77.59 and 80.98%), respectively. RD50 values were calculated by probit analysis, which ranged between 0.014 and 0.021 µL/cm2 for R. maidis and 0.019–0.025 µL/cm2 for S. avenae (Table 6).

Acetylcholinesterase inhibition activity of nano-emulsions

Nano-emulsions of the essential oil and carvone exhibited variable but promising acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity against R. maidis and S. avenae. IC50 values ranged from 0.05 to 5.34 mg/mL (Table 7). The inhibitory activity of nano-emulsions increased with increase in concentration of the essential oil and carvone. The nano-emulsion of carvone showed higher inhibition than the essential oil. However, AChE inhibition was more in R. maidis as compared to S. avenae.

Molecular docking study

Molecular docking studies provided valuable understanding about the inhibition of acetylcholinesterase enzyme, a target enzyme by the major compounds identified in the essential oil of M. spicata. Analysis showed the carvone, β-bourbonene, β-caryophyllene, eucalyptol and limonene, the major components of the essential oil, were found to bind favourably with the target enzyme (S1). The strong binding was mainly attributed towards the hydrophobic interactions such as π-σ, π-alkyl, alkyl, π-anion, π-π stacked, and π-π T shaped (Figs. 5 and 6). In eucalyptol, the bonding was mainly due to the 6 hydrogen bonds (3 conventional and 3 C-H hydrogen bonds), and was stabilized by the hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 7). TYR 133, TRP 359, PHE 368, TYR 408, and PHE 409 were major amino acid residues involved in the hydrophobic interactions with compounds whereas HIS 514, SER 124, ASP 126, and SER 174 were key amino acid residues involved in hydrogen bonding with eucalyptol (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). The binding energy of the major components varied between − 6.6 and − 8.5 kcal/mol and β-bourbonene was found to have the strongest binding among the major components. Since carvone is the most dominant component of the essential oil, it plays a major role in the acetylcholinesterase enzyme inhibitory activity as well. However, the results from the in silico molecular docking studies suggest that the AChE inhibitory activity of the essential oil may be attributed to the synergistic action of the major compounds present in the oil.

Discussion

The essential oil from M. spicata, has been investigated by many workers worldwide31,36–38. The yield of essential oil and composition were found to vary with the geographical location, season, and even among the different varieties of the same species31,35. Earlier studies showed monoterpenoids constitute the major portion of the essential oil with carvone, piperitone, piperitone oxide, linalool, pulegone, limonene, and 1,8-cineole as the most abundant compounds, together with sesquiterpenoids, β-bourbonene, caryophyllene, elemene, Z-β-farnesene, germacrene-D, and caryophyllene oxide31,33. The essential oils from Brazillian spearmint varieties contained piperitone (81.18%) and piperitone oxide (94.8%)48,49, linalool (86.7%) in Greek spearmint50 and pulgeone (56.28%) and limonene (48.0%) from Iranian spearmint varieties as the major compounds51,52. Carvone (48.60-76.65%) has been reported as a major compound in Indian spearmint followed by limonene (9.57–25.59%) besides other minor compounds31,33,45–47. In this study, carvone (81.88%) was identified as the most abundant compound in the essential oil followed by limonene (8.03%). The amount of two major compounds, carvone and limonene in its oil was found to be similar to that reported earlier from Indian spearmint31.

Nano-sized essential oil based formulation was found to be an effective technique to improve its stability, shelf-life, and bioefficacy of formulation with physicochemical properties57. High energy techniques such as high-pressure homogenization, ultrasonication, and microfluidization have been more commonly employed to achieve droplet size in the nanometre ranage58. Alternatively, low energy methods such as spontaneous emulsification and phase inversion temperature can also be used to achieve the desirable droplet size and stability59. In this study, a low energy spontaneous emulsification method was used to prepare stable nano-emulsions of M. spicata oil and carvone. Results showed that the average droplet size of the nano-emulsion of M. spicata oil was 22.1 nm with PDI value of 0.51, while that of carvone was 41.21 nm with PDI value of 0.53, respectively. These results were found to be similar as observed in the preparation of nano-emulsion of lime essential oil by using same technique having droplet size (21 nm) with PDI value of 0.4460, while nano-emulsion from the essential oil of Rosmarinus officinalis had a small droplet size (50 nm) and a narrow particle size distribution (PDI = 0.506)61. Another spontaneous emulsification study also showed the stable nano-emulsions of neem oil and citronella oil with droplet size of 11.23–17.84 nm30. Nano-emulsion of lemongrass oil was also prepared by a low energy spontaneous emulsification method and droplet size of 116.4 nm bearing PDI of 0.215, good stability with zeta potential ranged between − 0.0173 and − 8.05 mV were recorded62. These studies displayed the usefulness of a low energy spontaneous emulsification technique for the preparation of nano-emulsions of essential oil with high stability and desirable physicochemical properties. Nano-emulsions were found stable despite having a relatively lower zeta potential value due to steric stabilization since the formulations were prepared using non-ionic surfactants63.

In this study, the nano-emulsions of essential oil and carvone showed promising aphidicidal activity against R. maidis (LC50 = 0.87–2.87 mg/mL) and S. avenae (LC50 = 1.64–2.81 mg/mL) with potent repellence action. Earlier, the essential oils from Cuminum cyminum L., Hyssopus officinalis L., Tanacetum balsamita L., Levisticum officinale W.D.J. Koch, Lavandula angustifolia Mill. and Thymus vulgaris L. showed repellent action against Rhopalosiphum padi64. The essential oil from Mentha rotundifolia also showed insecticidal activity against Rhopalosiphum padi (LC50 = 0.16 mL/L) and Sitobion avenae (LC50 = 0.2 mL/L), respectively65. The insecticidal activity of the essential oil and the major compounds from Stevia rebaudiana66, and extracts of neem seed and moringa leaf against Schizaphis graminum (R.), Rhopalosiphum padi (L.) and Sitobion avenae (F.) was carried out, which showed variable bioefficacy10. These results showed that the essential oil and carvone from M. spicata have the potential to be used against these aphids.The essential oil is mixture of volatile compounds, and its repellent activity is generally attributed to monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes and other compounds67–69. However, sometime the minor constituents found in low percentages in the essential oil, may act as synergists, enhancing the effectiveness of the major constituents through a variety of mechanisms, compared to the isolated pure compounds69–70 .

Acetylcholinesterase enzyme plays a crucial role in the insect nervous system by breaking down acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter responsible for transmitting nerve signals through synapses71. Inhibition of this enzyme leads to accumulation of acetylcholine in excess, resulting in restlessness, hyperexcitability, tremor, convulsion, and eventual paralysis and death72. Earlier, acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity of the essential oils from Mentha aquatica, M. gentilis and M. arvensis was established with IC50 = 26–32 ppm73. The essential oil from M. spicata also showed acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity74 and was due to the presence of carvone75,76. The present study also confirmed the findings that both essential oil and carvone strongly inhibited the acetylcholinesterase enzyme.

In silico molecular docking studies provide insights about the interactions between proteins and ligands and their modes of binding77. A molecular docking study of carvone, a major compound as well as three minor compounds, limonene, β-bourbonene, and β-caryophyllene identified in the essential oil from M. spicata was carried out with the acetylcholinesterase enzyme to explain their modes of inhibitory activity. The major compounds are known to play key role in the biological activity of essential oil67,70. In vitro and molecular docking studies also confirmed the key role of carvone as an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor in aphidicidal activity. The results also showed the hydrophobic interaction as the major driving force behind strong interactions between enzyme and major compounds studied, however, in eucalyptol, hydrophobic interaction, and hydrogen bonding, both played crucial roles. These results were in accordance with a previous study, which showed the acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity of carvone supported by molecular docking studies that revealed the favourable binding of carvone with acetylcholinesterase as SER487 played a crucial role in the interaction78. Also, hydrophobic interactions involving the residues PHE, TYR, and TRP were reported as crucial for binding of limonene with AChE79. These results suggest that the hydrophobic interaction has played a crucial role in inhibiting acetylcholinesterase enzyme activity by the major compounds of the essential oil of M. spicata besides hydrogen bonding.

Conclusions

Mentha spicata, an aromatic herb, is commercially cultivated in different parts of India for the production of spearmint oil used in pharmaceutical, flavouring and cosmetic purposes. This study showed the plant is a rich source of essential oil and carvone. The nano-emulsions of the essential oil and carvone formulated using a low-energy spontaneous emulsification method were found stable and showed promising concentration and duration of exposure dependent aphidicidal activity against R. maidis and S. avenae. The oil and carvone showed potent acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity, supported by in silico molecular docking studies in which hydrophobic interactions were found to be the major driving force for strong interactions besides hydrogen bonding. This study suggests that the nano-emulsion of carvone has better aphidicidal activity than that of essential oil and can be useful to control R. maidis and S. avenae. However, effective nano-emulsions of the oil and carvone need to be further evaluated to confirm their efficacy under field conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The fresh and shade dried leaves of M. spicata used in present study, were purchased from M/s Creation Biotech, Bareilly (28.2699° N, 79.1605° E), Uttar Pradesh, India.

Isolation of essential oil

The essential oil from the leaves (400 g) was obtained by hydrodistillation method using Clevenger type apparatus for 4 h. The oil from the distillate was separated using diethyl ether and the ethereal layer was collected and dried over anhydrous sodium sulphate. The purified oil was then stored in glass vials at 4 °C till further use. The yield of essential oil extracted was calculated as follow:

Gas chromatography-flame ionisation detector (GC-FID) analysis

The essential oil was analysed using a Gas Chromatography-Flame Ionisation Detector, (Shimadzu model 2010 Plus) equipped with a capillary column DB-5 (30 m × 0.25 mm, film thickness 0.25 μm) and autosampler. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature was held at 60 °C, then programmed at 3 °C/min to 230 °C with split mode (1:20) and the injection volume was 0.3 µL. The injector and the detector temperatures were kept at 250 °C and 260 °C, respectively. The relative percentages of the individual components were calculated based on GC peak area (FID response) without using correction factors.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis

The analysis of essential oil was performed on a Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (Thermo DSQ) equipped with a capillary column DB-5 (30 m × 0.25 mm, film thickness 0.25 μm) and autosampler. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: helium as carrier gas at a flow-rate of 1 mL/min (split mode, 1:20); injection volume was 0.3 µL; injector temperature was 250 °C, respectively. The column temperature was held at 60 °C, and then programmed at 3 °C/min to 230 °C. The ion source temperature was kept at 200 °C. The column was coupled directly to the quadrupole mass spectrometer at EI mode at 70 eV with the mass range of 28–400 a.m.u range at 1 scan/s. The identification of individual compound was carried out based on retention time and retention index using a homologous series of n-alkanes (C8-C20, Sigma-Aldrich) and by comparing their mass spectra with NIST Mass Spectral Library (Ver. 2, 2005), co-injection with authentic samples and literature80.

Preparation of primary emulsions

The emulsions (20 mg/mL) of the essential oil and carvone, a major compound of the oil, were prepared separately, by mixing the essential oil or carvone with surfactant in a ratio of 1:1 using a magnetic stirrer with different surfactants (Tween 80, Span 80 + Tween 80, Triton X-100) at 500 rpm for 15 min. The stability of the emulsions was evaluated by keeping them at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h. The best performing surfactant was chosen for further standardization of the surfactant to oil or carvone ratio. Among the three combinations (1:1, 1:2, and 2:1) of surfactant to oil ratio tested, the most stable ratio (1:1) was chosen for the preparation nano-emulsions.

Preparation of nano-emulsions

Nano-emulsions of the essential oil and carvone were prepared by adding water dropwise (4 mL/min) to the primary emulsions with constant stirring at 500 rpm for 30 min, separately. The clear nano-emulsions (10 mg/mL) of essential oil and carvone were obtained as stock solutions and stored at room temperature till further studies.

Determination of stability of nano-emulsions

The stability of the nano-emulsions was determined at room temperature (25 °C) after 24 h of storage. The stability was further evaluated after freezing and thawing, for which the nano-emulsions (5 mL) were kept at – 20 °C overnight for freezing followed by thawing at room temperature after 24 h to check for any creaming, sedimentation, oil layer separation or other instabilities along with particle size.

Determination of physicochemical properties of nano-emulsions

The average particle diameter, polydispersity index and zeta potential of the nano-emulsions were determined by a Zetasizer (Microtrac, Germany) equipped with Microtrac FLEX data analysis program following the principles of dynamic light scattering.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis

The droplet size and morphology of the nano-emulsions were measured using Transmission Electron Microscopy (JEOL JEM-1400) at 120 kV, equipped with TEMCON software. In this method, a copper coated grid was impregnated with the nano-emulsions followed by drying for 15 min and staining with uranyl acetate. Micrographs were obtained at a magnification range of 6000-20,000× at 100–600 nm under TEM.

In vitro insecticidal activity

Rearing of aphids

Two aphids, R. maidis and S. avenae, were collected from infested fields of maize and wheat and were identified by Dr. Rajna, Division of Entomology, ICAR-IARI, New Delhi. The colonies of aphids were established from single apterous virginopare adults. Offsprings were used to infest more seedlings of maize inside a fine-net green house to protect insects from parasites and predators under laboratory conditions (25 ± 5 °C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity and 16 L, 8 D photoperiod).

Leaf-dip bioassay

The bioassay was carried out on wingless adult aphids using healthy and freshly harvested leaves of maize. The stock nano-emulsions (10 mg/mL) of essential oil and carvone were serially diluted with a mixture of surfactant-water to obtain different concentrations of the test solutions (0.5–7.5 mg/mL for R. maidis and 0.625–5 mg/mL for S. avenae) for the leaf-dip bioassay after the preliminary screening. A mixture of surfactant-water (10 mg/mL) served as a negative control. The bioassay was undertaken using the IRAC (Insecticide Resistance Action Committee) susceptibility test method 019.

The freshly harvested leaves were cleaned and soaked in different concentrations of test solutions for 1 min and allowed to air dry for 5 min to evaporate water from their surfaces. 20 adult aphids at fourth-stage of development from the infested maize plants were transferred to the treated leaves, kept on blotting paper moistened with water, using a thin brush. Each experiment was carried out in triplicates and performed under controlled conditions. The observation for the mortality of the aphids was taken after 24 h under microscope. Insects were considered dead upon failing to react to touch with a thin haired brush to their sensitive area, such as the underside of the abdomen. The corrected mortality rates were calculated according to the formula81.

Acetylcholinesterase inhibition activity

The acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition activity of the nano-emulsions was determined using a BioTek microplate reader equipped with Gen 3.5 software using earlier method75 with slight modification. The lethal concentrations (LC50) and sub-lethal concentrations (LC10, LC20, LC30) of the nano-emulsions were sprayed on aphids (n = 40), separately. Ten aphids from each treatment were homogenized separately in 0.5 mL of ice-cold 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) containing 1 mM of EDTA, 0.1 mM of DTT, 1 mM of PTU, 1mM of PMSF, and 0.1% glycerol. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ºC and the supernatant was carefully pipetted out into Eppendorf tubes for subsequent enzyme assay. The transparent 96-well plate was filled with 10 µL of 10 mM 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) and 30 µL of 100 mM acetylcholine iodide (AChI) prepared in 100 µM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and enzyme source (100 µL) was added. The equal volume of the buffer was added in place of the enzyme source in blank. Molecular docking study

The molecular docking analysis offers insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the interactions between the ligands and the target proteins77. In the present study, acetylcholinesterase (AChE) was chosen as a potent target for studying the in silico binding of the major components of M. spicata essential oil. The protein sequence was obtained from the NCBI protein database, and suitable templates for the target protein were identified using the blastP server. Based on the degree of similarity, one or more proteins were selected for further modeling using Modeller 10.4 software. The generated models were evaluated using DOPE scores, and the best-fitting model was further refined using Galaxy refine web server. The best fitted models were then chosen for molecular docking studies. The selected proteins were prepared using AutoDock Vina software, involving deletion of additional water molecules, addition of polar hydrogen atoms, and assignment of necessary charges. The binding sites were identified using AADS active site prediction tool. The 3D sdf files of the major components were acquired from the PubChem website and converted to pdb format using PyMol software. Further ligand preparation was conducted with AutoDock Vina. Following preparation, the ligand molecules were docked onto the target proteins, and their affinities were ranked based on binding energy. Protein-ligand interactions were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio software to identify interacting amino acid residues and characterize the type of interaction between residues and ligands.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained were analysed using IBM SPSS (version 20.0) and R Studio software. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD test with p ˂ 0.05 was done for testing the significant difference between variables. The average lethal concentration (LC50, RD50, and IC50) values of the essential oil and carvone were determined by probit analysis85 using POLO Plus 2.0 equipped with LeOra software.
