Toward general AI harmonization with real mammogram imaging breast cancer dataset
Authors: Aymen M Al-Hejri, Muneer Fazea, Archana Harsing Sable, Riyadh M Al-Tam, Mugahed A Al-antari, Sultan S Alshamrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis dataset was specifically collected to support the development and evaluation of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems for breast cancer detection. The dataset, Ma’amon Diagnostic Center Mammogram Imaging for Breast Cancer (MDCMI-BC), was created to provide high-quality mammogram images with expert annotations to improve AI-based diagnostic tools. This data is intended to aid in the validation of CAD systems, ensuring they can more effectively detect breast cancer by integrating with other available multimodal datasets.Data descriptionThe MDCMI-BC dataset contains 133 mammogram cases with 266 images, categorized by the BI-RADS (Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System) classification. These include 100 normal, 66 benign, and 100 malignant cases, with images captured from both left and right sides using craniocaudal (CC) and mediolateral oblique (MLO) views. The dataset includes detailed annotations made by expert radiologists, which provide ground truth (GT) for training and validating CAD systems. The images are converted from DICOM format to PNG, de-identified to ensure patient privacy, and made available for public access at the Mendeley repository. The data will assist in refining diagnostic algorithms and fostering AI technologies in breast cancer detection.Keywords: Breast cancer, Mammogram images, BI-RADS, CAD systems, General AI, Data harmonizations

Objective

The primary objective of the MDCMI-BC dataset is to facilitate the development, training, and validation of CAD systems tailored for breast cancer detection. By providing high-quality mammogram images along with expert annotations, the dataset aims to support the creation of more accurate and generalized AI models for breast cancer diagnosis.

This initiative contributes to the global effort of data harmonization for AI in healthcare, particularly in the realm of medical imaging. The dataset was specifically collected from the Al-Ma’amon Diagnostic Centre in Yemen, and it includes 133 cases with 266 mammogram images. These images were classified according to the BI-RADS classification system and tumor type (benign, malignant, normal). It serves as a foundation for AI models that require diverse and high-resolution data to effectively detect and classify breast cancer, ensuring applicability across various global demographics.

The data were collected as part of a dedicated research effort to improve breast cancer detection using AI, rather than being a byproduct of another study. This dataset has been used in a related research paper, ETECADx [1], which utilized an ensemble self-attention transformer encoder for the diagnosis of breast cancer through the analysis of full-field digital X-ray breast images. It demonstrates the use of this data for evaluating AI-based breast cancer diagnostic systems. The dataset is now publicly accessible and will contribute to improving the robustness and accuracy of AI-based diagnostic tools, particularly those designed for early breast cancer detection.

Data description

The standard tool of the mammographic device, a Senographe 800T High-Frequency X-Ray Generator from GE in Florida, USA, was used to scan the patient’s breasts. Two local expert radiologists in Yemen prepared detailed clinical reports for each case, offering comprehensive explanations of the patient’s condition, diagnostic annotations, tumor size, location, and more. The experts consistently followed the BI-RADS scale to determine whether the breast cancer was benign or malignant. A total of 166 mammograms, encompassing both benign and malignant cases, and 100 mammograms (normal) were collected with their assistance, as they supervised the scanning of the mammogram images and the preparation of the medical reports. In addition, two external radiologists from India conducted the annotation for the regions of the lesion.

Images were collected and stored in a de-identified dataset, with each image formatted in DICOM [2]. Accompanied by a structured report categorizing the case as normal, benign, or malignant. The Pydicom library in Python [3] It is used to read all images in DICOM format with their metadata and convert them to JPG image format, ensuring that all personal data records are kept confidential.

According to the radiologist’s report accompanying each case, the images were categorized as either benign, malignant, or normal. The images are grouped based on the BI-RADS classification. Only significant categorizations from the structure report are extracted and placed into a CSV file, as shown in Table 1 [4], while the original reports are not shared to ensure patient and health privacy.

Expert radiologists typically perform annotations in mammography to identify and outline suspicious areas. The mammogram with annotated regions, likely highlighting regions of interest (ROI) or abnormalities in breast tissue, such as benign or malignant, which could potentially indicate breast cancer. The annotations, marked by blue outlines by the computer vision annotation tool (CVAT), delineate specific areas within the mammogram images that may contain suspicious findings. These outlined regions serve as GT for evaluating CAD systems, providing a reference standard for training and testing machine learning models in breast cancer detection.

The generated CSV file contains the metadata for each image, such as patient’s encoded identifier, breast view (L or R), breast projection view (CC or MLO), BI-RADS assessment, lesion classification (benign, malignant, or normal), and the specific image name incorporating the patient ID along with the side and view data. All images have a width of 3540 pixels and a height of 4740 pixels. Table 1 shows the description and the details of the MDCMI-BC dataset.

Limitations

The sample size is still small, but we intend to improve and increase the second version of this data with more images, along with US and medical reports for the same patients. Also, to address this constraint, we recommend employing data augmentation techniques and integrating our dataset with multimodal and breast cancer risk datasets [5]. Moreover, the annotation process demands considerable time and effort from the radiologist.
