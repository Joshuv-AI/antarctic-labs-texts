Effect of prior intravitreal injections on the risk of posterior capsule rupture during cataract surgery: a single-surgeon matched case-control study
Authors: Cagatay Caglar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the impact of a history of intravitreal injections (IVI) for various retinal diseases on the risk of posterior capsule rupture (PCR) during cataract surgery in a single-surgeon setting.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, matched case-control study included 93 eyes of 74 patients who underwent phacoemulsification after prior IVI (anti-VEGF and/or dexamethasone) and 93 control eyes without prior IVI, matched for age, gender, laterality and axial length. All surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgeon. The primary outcome was the incidence of PCR. Statistical analyses was performed using Chi-square tests and logistic regression.ResultsAll eyes in the IVI group had received at least two injections (mean: 5.71 ± 3.5). PCR occurred in 3 eyes (3.2%) in both the IVI and control groups (p = 1.00). There was no statistically significant difference in overall intraoperative complication rates between the two groups (8.6% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.79). Logistic regression analysis revealed no significant association between PCR and the number of injections, injection agent (anti-VEGF or dexamethasone), time from first injection to surgery, or retinal disease subtype (p > 0.05 for all).ConclusionIn this study, a history of IVI was not associated with an increased risk of PCR or other intraoperative complications during cataract surgery. These findings suggest that cataract surgery following IVI can be safely performed by an experienced surgeon without an increased risk of PCR.Keywords: Cataract surgery, Intravitreal injection, Posterior capsule rupture, Anti-VEGF, Dexamethasone implant

Introduction

Intravitreal injections (IVI) of vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors (anti-VEGF) and dexamethasone implants (DEX) have had an increasingly important impact on the ophthalmology practice over the recent years. They have become essential in the treatment of common retinal disorders, such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and macular edema caused by retinal vein occlusion (RVO) and diabetic macular edema (DME) [1–3].

IVIs have become the most frequently performed invasive procedure in the ophthalmology clinics. As the intravitreal half-life of these drugs is relatively short, many patients need multiple injections. While the patients’ vision has improved satisfyingly after treatment, each intravitreal injection poses a risk of vision-threatening ocular complications [4, 5]. Besides these serious ocular side effects, cataract formation can be observed after IVI during the follow-up period [6]. Cataracts may occur due to direct lens trauma during the intravitreal injection. In addition, after intravitreal injection of steroids, development of cataract due to the cataractogenic effect of steroids may be seen. IVIs are performed frequently, especially in the elderly and diabetic population. It is known that these conditions are risk factors for cataracts. Therefore, some of these patients have already developed cataracts before the intravitreal injection treatment started. For all these reasons, cataract surgeries are frequently performed during the treatment period in patients undergoing intravitreal injection therapy. However, several complications can occur during the cataract surgery in these patients [7–9]. The existing literature on this topic presents inconsistent findings. Some studies reported a significantly higher rate of PCR in eyes with prior anti-VEGF injections, attributing it to unidentified iatrogenic lens trauma [8–10]. Conversely, Wozniak found no increase for PCR [11].

Much of this discrepancy may stem from variability in surgical skill and technique across studies involving multiple surgeons. Therefore, to eliminate surgeon-specific factors as a major confounder, we conducted a retrospective matched-case control analysis where all procedures (both injections and cataract surgery) were performed by a single surgeon. This design allows for a more isolated examination of the effect of prior IVI on surgical outcomes. In this study, complications and results of cataract surgery were investigated in patients who had previously undergone intravitreal injections. The purpose of this study is also to investigate the hypothesis that previous intravitreal treatment is associated with an increased risk of PCR during cataract surgery.

Methods

This retrospective, single-center, comparative study was conducted by reviewing the medical records from the clinical practice of a single retina specialist. Patients were identified through a diagnostic database search, including those diagnosed with DME, RVO, AMD, who underwent phacoemulsification surgery after receiving intravitreal anti-VEGF agents (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept) or DEX implants between January 2016 and January 2022.

The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgery. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Hitit University Faculty of Medicine (Date: January 6, 2016; Approval No: 2016.52).

All eyes had received a minimum of two intravitreal injections prior to cataract surgery. The control group comprised an equal number of eyes undergoing cataract surgery for senile cataract without a history of IVI, matched by age, gender, laterality, and axial length using computerized matching techniques. Collected data included demographic variables (age, sex, diagnosis), clinical parameters (treated eye, type of cataract, type and number of prior IVIs, indication for IVI, type of agent), and surgical details (complications, best-corrected visual acuity [BCVA] before and after surgery). All intravitreal injections were performed in the superotemporal quadrant, 4 mm posterior to the limbus. Except for the dexamethasone implant injection (which uses a 22-gauge applicator needle), all intravitreal injections were performed using a 30-gauge needle. Only patients aged ≥ 50 years, diagnosed with DME, RVO, or AMD, who had undergone phacoemulsification and IOL implantation with at least three months of postoperative follow-up, were included. Exclusion criteria were: history of intraocular surgery (e.g., pars plana vitrectomy, glaucoma surgery), ocular comorbidities, cataract surgery by any technique other than phacoemulsification, and patients with anatomical features increasing risk for complications (e.g., small pupil, axial length < 22 mm or > 24 mm, zonular dialysis, phacodonesis, or iridodonesis).

Statistical analyses was conducted using SPSS software (Version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical comparisons. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was applied for continuous variables depending on distribution. ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare more than two groups. Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc analyses. The primary outcome was the incidence of PCR and was treated as a binary dependent variable. To assess the association between PCR and potential risk factors, univariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Candidate covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included age, sex, eye laterality, number of prior intravitreal injections, interval from first injection to cataract surgery, history of dexamethasone implant, indication for injections, and intravitreal agent type. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 93 eyes from 74 patients met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 60 eyes were diagnosed with DME (42 patients), 19 eyes with RVO (18 patients), and 14 eyes with AMD (14 patients). All patients had received IVI for DME, macular edema due to RVO, or choroidal neovascularization due to AMD. The same number of eyes (93) from 74 control patients, matched by age, sex, eye laterality and axial length, who underwent cataract surgery without prior IVI, were included as the control group. All procedures were performed by the same surgeon between January 2016 and January 2022.

The demographic data and characteristics of the patients are given in Table 1. The postoperative BCVA was significantly better in DME patients compared to those with RVO (p = 0.01) and AMD (p = 0.001), with no significant difference between the RVO and AMD groups (p = 0.41). Cataract surgery resulted in a statistically significant improvement in BCVA in all three retinal disease groups (p < 0.05 for each). As expected, postoperative BCVA was significantly better in the control group (0.11 ± 0.11 logMAR) compared to the IVI group (0.54 ± 0.43 logMAR, p < 0.001), reflecting the absence of underlying macular pathology in the control eyes.

Postoperatively, 48 of 60 DME eyes, 13 of 19 RVO eyes, and 8 of 14 AMD eyes required re-injection due to worsening macular pathology. The time to recurrence was shorter in DME patients (mean 8.53 weeks) compared to RVO (20.67 weeks) and AMD (12.5 weeks). History of DEX implant was significantly more common in RVO patients (89.5%) than in those with DME (36.6%) (p < 0.01).

A comparison between the patient and control group is given in Table 2. The PCR occured in 3.22% in both the IVI and control groups (3 of 93 eyes in each group). In the IVI group, all PCRs occurred during the phacoemulsification phase. Anterior vitrectomy was required in all three cases. In two patients, three-piece IOLs were placed in the sulcus; in the third case, IOL implantation was deferred due to uncertain capsular support. This patient, who belonged to the DME group and had high myopia, later received sulcus IOL implantation one month postoperatively. In the control group, two cases required anterior vitrectomy with sulcus IOL implantation, while the third received an in-the-bag IOL since the PCR was limited and the vitreous was intact.

Of the 60 DME eyes, 22 had received previous DEX implants—none of which experienced PCR. Among the 19 RVO eyes, 17 had prior DEX implants, including the one case with PCR. In one other RVO patient, no intraoperative complication occurred, but a vitreous strand was detected postoperatively, possibly due to unrecognized zonular dialysis. No cases of dropped nucleus occurred in either group. Nine DME patients received anti-VEGF injections during the cataract surgery session; no adverse events were noted during follow-up.

Discussion

This matched case-control study, unique for its single-surgeon design, found no association between a prior history of intravitreal injections and an increased risk of posterior capsule rupture during cataract surgery. The identical PCR rate of 3.2% in both the IVI and control groups, coupled with the absence of significant risk factors in regression analysis, challenges the premise that IVI inherently elevates surgical risk.

Intravitreal injection is one of the major routes for the treatment of many vitreous and retinal diseases. An increasing number of patients require intravitreal therapies for many retinal diseases [12]. The number of IVIs has increased rapidly worldwide during the past 15 years [13]. IVIs are also the most common ophthalmic procedures performed in the United States [14]. The proper injection technique has been described in the ophthalmology literature [7, 15]. The injection site is critical to avoid damaging important tissues, including the crystalline lens of the eye. Lens damage caused by the needle tip is avoided by injection at the proper location [15]. Many patients receiving IVI are phakic. These patients are likely to have cataract surgery in the future. An inappropriate needle angle (not perpendicular to the vitreous cavity) or a needle path too close to the lens can cause iatrogenic trauma. Such subclinical trauma may later predispose the eye to PCR and zonular damage during cataract surgery [7] Although there is no visible evidence of damage to the lens or posterior capsule on biomicroscopic examination before cataract surgery, previous studies have shown an increased risk of PCR during cataract surgery in patients with a history of IVI [8, 14–16]. Moreover, the underlying mechanism remains unclear in these studies, it has been speculated that there may be local distortion of the sclera during the administration of the intravitreal agent, causing inadvertent zonular trauma, but there may also be biochemical factors. Hahn and colleagues [16] investigated clinical data and compared rates of intraoperative complications (PCR and/or anterior vitrectomy and/or unplanned 3-piece intraocular lens placement) during cataract surgery. In this clinical study, specific cataract surgery-related complications were directly assessed via a review of clinical records, which revealed a significantly increased rate in eyes with a prior history of intravitreal injection compared with eyes without a history of injections (3% vs. 0%, p = 0.03). In our study, eyes with a history of previous IVIs did not demonstrate an increased risk of PCR or vitreous loss during cataract surgery.

In our study, no evidence of lens trauma caused by the injector needle was detected in the crystalline lens examination performed before cataract surgery in any of the patients. At least 2 injections, an average of 5.71 ± 3.5 injections, were performed in the patients included in this study. The injection technique requires excess pressure on the globe in patients who are injected with DEX implants. Rarely, the implant can even be misdirected into the crystalline lens [17]. In our study, no statistically significant difference was found between the history of dexamethasone implantation and PCR (p = 0.680). In a patient with DME who developed PCR during cataract surgery, there were no DEX implants among the 8 IVIs performed. Among the patients with RVO who developed PCR during cataract surgery, 3 of the 11 preoperative injections were dexamethasone implants.

In some previous studies, the rate of PCR that occurred during cataract surgery increased as the number of IVIs increased. Nagar et al. reported a dose-dependent increase of 8.6% relative risk per injection (OR, 1.086) in patients [8]. Lee et al. reported a dose response, with a 4% increased risk of PCR with each injection [18]. In contrast to these findings in our study, we did not find an increased PCR risk when the number of previous injections was analysed. Eyes that underwent PCR were subjected to 5, 8 and 11 injections, and the mean number of preoperative injections for these patients was greater than the mean of all eyes (8 versus 5.71). However, the comparison was limited by the small number of patients who underwent PCR, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.170). Some studies have reported similar results. Shalchi et al. reported that a history of intravitreal injection was associated with an increased risk of PCR and that the number of prior injections was not associated with an increased risk of PCR [15]. Hahn et al. reported that the mean numbers of injections prior to cataract surgery were 11.83 in eyes with PCR (range 2–41) and 5.37 in eyes without PCR (range 1–49), but these differences were not statistically significant [16]. In our study, no relationship was found between PCR and other factors, including age, sex, laterality, preoperative injection number, interval from the first exam to cataract surgery, history of dexamethasone implant, indication for injections, or type of actual injection agent (p > 0.05 for all factors, Table 4).

PCR results during routine cataract surgery are between 2% and 5.2% [19–23]. Many studies have shown that PCR normally occurs at a relatively high rate by less experienced surgeons and trainees. Studies focusing on surgery trainees have reported that the PCR rate is higher than 3.5% in this group [24–28]. Another study analysing risk factors for PCR during cataract surgery reported that trainee surgeons were a risk factor [21]. In all studies in which a high rate of PCR was found in previously injected eyes [8, 18], all cataract surgeries and injections were performed by different surgeons. Moreover, the surgeons all had different degrees of surgical experience. In these studies, it has been shown that trainees perform more PCRs when risk analysis is performed. In Hahn’s study, although the surgeons were experienced, four different surgeons performed cataract surgery [16]. In this study, the rate of PCR was 3% in the eyes that were injected previously, and PCR was not performed in the eyes in the control group during cataract surgery. However, in this study, IVI was performed by trainees. Lens or zonular damage caused by these injections, which were performed by less experienced trainees, may have contributed to this high rate. In our study, all IVI and cataract surgeries were performed by a single surgeon. This surgeon has high experience in intravitreal injection (performing 1200 IVIs per year) and cataract surgery (performing > 500 cataract surgeries per year). Specifically, needle-induced lenticular trauma may be a result of variability in the technique or skill of lens trauma associated with ophthalmologists with various levels of experience. Similarly, the fact that surgeons who perform surgeries with different levels of experience in cataract surgery may have contributed to the differences in complication rates. Given that all intravitreal injections and cataract surgeries in our study were performed by a single surgeon, we conclude that a history of intravitreal injection is not a risk factor for PCR in these patients undergoing cataract surgery.

There were several limitations in this study. The study is limited by its retrospective design. We were not able to match controls according to lens status, which was graded subjectively. We cannot exclude the possibility of effects caused by any of these factors. In the present study, one experienced surgeon performed all IVI and cataract surgeries and followed all patients before and after surgery. Thus, the most important variable in the study has been eliminated. Additionally, because PCR events were rare (n = 3), we acknowledge that the model is underpowered and at risk of overfitting; therefore, we additionally performed univariable logistic regression. In our study, although the mean number of injections was numerically higher in patients who experienced PCR, we did not find a statistically significant association between the cumulative number of injections and the risk of PCR. However, this lack of statistical significance should be interpreted with caution. Given the low number of PCR events (n = 3), our study may be subject to a Type II error, as it possesses limited statistical power to detect such an association. Despite this limitation, our findings align with the hypothesis that cumulative mechanical stress on the lens apparatus may increase surgical vulnerability, a point further supported by the high risk observed in trainee-led studies such as that by Hahn et al. While the single-surgeon design is a strength for internal validity by eliminating inter-surgeon variability, it limits the generalizability of our findings. The outcomes may not be directly applicable to less experienced surgeons or different surgical settings. Third, our assessment of preoperative lens and zonular integrity relied on biomicroscopic examination. Since more sensitive imaging modalities, such as ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), were not utilized, the presence of subclinical trauma cannot be entirely ruled out. Cataract density or nuclear hardness was not formally graded (e.g. LOCS III), which represents another potential limitation of the study.The present study is the first study with a single surgeon in this patient group and is the most important strength of the study.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that a history of multiple intravitreal injections is not associated with an increased risk of PCR or other intraoperative complications during subsequent cataract surgery when performed by an experienced surgeon. While previous literature suggests a potential for lens or zonular trauma, we believe that the consistent surgical technique in our single-surgeon series contributed to these favorable outcomes. Although a meticulous preoperative evaluation remains essential to rule out any clinical trauma, our findings suggest that cataract surgery can be safely undertaken in these patients without anticipating a higher complication rate. This should provide significant reassurance to both surgeons and patients, indicating that a history of IVIs should not deter from proceeding with cataract surgery when clinically indicated.
