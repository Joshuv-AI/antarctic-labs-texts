A systematic review and meta-analysis of the prevalence of reflux symptoms after esophagectomy: patient-reported outcomes and management
Authors: Zhao Xue, You Tingting, Liu Jiarui, Cheng Yuying, Wang Xiaowei, Luo Lei, Miao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal cancer predominantly affects middle-aged and older adults, and postoperative reflux is one of the most common long-term complications after curative esophagectomy. It can persist for years and impair quality of life. However, no previous review has quantified the incidence of postoperative reflux or its related factors. This study aimed to summarize the prevalence and time course of reflux after esophagectomy and to explore associated determinants.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and other major databases from inception, without time limits. We included original studies reporting postoperative reflux symptoms or reflux esophagitis after curative esophagectomy for primary esophageal cancer. Patient-reported reflux symptoms were the primary outcome, and endoscopically confirmed reflux esophagitis was the secondary outcome. We used random-effects models to pool prevalence, restricted cubic splines to model time trends, and meta-regression to explore heterogeneity and publication bias.ResultsWe included 28 studies with 5,276 patients. Pooled prevalence was 38% for symptomatic reflux and 33% for reflux esophagitis, with high heterogeneity. Symptoms peaked at about 30 months, while reflux esophagitis peaked at about 35 months and then plateaued. Mean age, questionnaire threshold, medication coverage, and the timing of endoscopic assessment explained part of the heterogeneity.DiscussionReflux is a common long-term problem in survivors after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. The trajectory of patient-reported symptoms does not fully mirror changes in endoscopic mucosal injury.Conclusions and implicationsReflux is common after esophagectomy and may affect long-term recovery. Symptom patterns do not fully match mucosal injury, so symptoms alone cannot reflect esophageal damage well. Follow-up should focus on the first 3 years after surgery, with standardized symptom assessment, planned endoscopic monitoring, and clear recording of acid-suppressive therapy and other key clinical factors to support risk stratification and targeted care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07561-0.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, Postoperative complications, Reflux, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Esophageal cancer predominantly affects middle-aged and older adults [1]. Esophagectomy with gastric conduit reconstruction remains the standard treatment for resectable disease, and advances in minimally invasive techniques and perioperative care have markedly reduced early postoperative mortality and improved long-term survival [2]. However, functional outcomes and symptom control have not kept pace with these survival gains. This gap may be especially important in patients with multimorbidity, postoperative frailty, and limited physiological reserve, who are more vulnerable to treatment-related sequelae [3].

Esophagectomy alters the anatomy and physiology of the upper gastrointestinal tract, and many patients experience persistent gastroesophageal reflux after surgery. Contemporary studies report that about 20%–65% of survivors develop chronic reflux symptoms after esophagectomy, making reflux one of the most frequent and challenging survivorship problems after curative surgery [4–6]. Ongoing reflux exposes the esophageal remnant to acid and bile, leading to reflux esophagitis and anastomotic stricture [7, 8], and may also precipitate severe aspiration pneumonia [9]. In addition, symptom burden does not always parallel objective mucosal injury, which further complicates postoperative assessment and follow-up [10]. Thus, postoperative reflux increases the complexity of survivorship care and places sustained demands on patients, caregivers, and health systems.

Definitions and assessments of reflux vary across studies. Common approaches include patient-reported symptom scores, endoscopic evaluation, and 24-hour pH or impedance monitoring, yet no unified diagnostic standard or recommended testing pathway exists [11]. Some studies report only the frequency of symptoms, whereas others use endoscopically confirmed mucosal injury as the endpoint. Differences in methods and follow-up time points lead to wide variation in reported incidence and hamper direct comparison. At present, there is a lack of systematic quantitative description of reflux burden and its temporal pattern after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer, which leaves clinicians without clear guidance on when and how to assess reflux during postoperative survivorship follow-up.

Against this background, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the incidence of reflux symptoms and reflux esophagitis after esophagectomy. Patient-reported reflux symptoms were treated as the primary outcome, and reflux esophagitis as the mucosal injury outcome. Our aims were to generate pooled estimates of postoperative reflux and to examine whether its prevalence varies by study design, geographic region, reconstruction method, and other factors, so as to inform symptom management and survivorship follow-up after esophagectomy, with particular implications for older and clinically vulnerable survivors, in whom postoperative functional burden may be greater.

Methods

Protocol and reporting guidelines

This review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024617458). Reporting adhered to the PRISMA 2020 checklist [12] (Supplementary Material 1).

Search strategy and inclusion/exclusion criteria

We systematically searched PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from database inception to May 2025. Search strings combined subject headings and free-text terms related to esophageal cancer, esophagectomy, the postoperative setting, and reflux; full strategies are provided in Supplementary Material. No explicit age threshold was prespecified in the search strategy or eligibility criteria. After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and then assessed the full text of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer.

Inclusion criteria were original studies that reported postoperative reflux symptoms or reflux esophagitis after curative esophagectomy for primary esophageal cancer. Patient-reported reflux symptoms were treated as the primary outcome, and reflux esophagitis was treated as a secondary outcome to complement the symptom-based outcome assessment. Exclusion criteria were studies involving non-curative esophagectomy, non-primary esophageal cancer populations, duplicate reports from the same cohort, non-English publications, and non-original articles such as reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted study characteristics and outcome-related data, including reflux outcome type, study design, setting, follow-up, patient and surgical characteristics, and key clinical, management, and assessment variables. These included selected factors relevant to postoperative reflux interpretation, such as preoperative reflux, delayed gastric emptying, bile reflux, neoadjuvant or drug therapy, PPI exposure or withdrawal status, pyloric interventions, assessment timing, and assessment tools. Risk of bias was assessed independently using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data, which is appropriate for proportion outcomes [13]. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Based on key JBI items on representativeness, outcome measurement validity, follow-up, and response rate, studies were classified as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias and these ratings informed the descriptive summaries and sensitivity analyses. We also recorded reporting features that could affect prevalence interpretation, including incomplete surgical details, missing endoscopic grading, and inadequate reporting of medication or assessment protocols.

Data analysis

To account for differences in follow-up timing and to describe temporal patterns, follow-up was grouped into three windows: <1 year, 1–3 years, and 3–5 years. Within each window, pooled prevalence of reflux symptoms and reflux esophagitis (RE) was estimated using random-effects generalized linear mixed models, with 95% confidence intervals and prediction intervals [14]. For studies reporting multiple follow-up time points, we used multilevel random-effects models to account for clustering within studies and modeled follow-up time as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic [15]. Based on data availability and clinical relevance, subgroup analyses and study-level meta-regression were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Factors were analyzed separately for each outcome. For both symptomatic reflux and reflux esophagitis, study-level variables including age, anastomotic site, and reconstruction route were examined in the relevant studies. Outcome-specific factors were assessed separately, including assessment tool, questionnaire type, and symptom threshold for symptomatic reflux, and endoscopy-related factors for reflux esophagitis. Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed with funnel plots, using the Peters test [16] as the main test and the Egger test [17] as a sensitivity check.

Sensitivity analysis

We derived study-level risk-of-bias variables from the JBI items, including an overall grade and three key domains: q2 (recruitment), q6 (outcome measurement validity), and q9 (handling of response rates). Robustness was assessed by restricting analyses to low-risk studies, excluding studies that failed any key domain, adding a low-risk indicator as a moderator in random-effects models, and repeating domain-specific exclusions. We also performed design-based sensitivity analyses by repeating the pooling after restricting to cohort studies only and after excluding cross-sectional studies. In addition, leave-one-out analyses were performed for all included studies. When studies reported multiple follow-up time points, only the observation closest to 12 months was retained to avoid double-counting, and the random-effects model was refitted after sequential omission of each study.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

We identified 3,795 records through systematic searches of MEDLINE, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. After de-duplication, 2,232 records remained. Title and abstract screening yielded 41 records; exclusions at this stage were due to ineligible design, outcomes, or populations. Full-text assessment resulted in 28 included studies. The selection process followed the PRISMA flow [18] and is shown in Fig. 1. The 28 studies enrolled 5,276 patients, including 3,952 men; the mean age was 65 years. Thirteen studies reported reflux symptoms only [4–6, 9, 19–27], six reported RE only [7, 28–32] and nine reported both [33–41]. By design, twenty were cohort studies [5, 7, 19–22, 25, 27–29, 31–34, 36–41], including eight prospective [5, 7, 20, 27, 32–34, 36] and twelve retrospective [19, 21, 22, 24, 25, 28, 29, 31, 37–41] and eight were cross-sectional [4, 6, 9, 23, 24, 26, 30, 35]. Geographically, most studies were conducted in Asia, with fewer from Europe and North America. Study methods varied substantially. Symptomatic reflux was assessed using different instruments and thresholds, including esophageal cancer–specific questionnaires, generic reflux tools, and non-standardized symptom definitions. For RE, endoscopic diagnosis was used in all relevant studies, most commonly with the Los Angeles classification. Surgical characteristics and postoperative management were also inconsistently reported across studies, particularly for operative approach, anastomotic site, conduit type, pyloric interventions, and medication use. Risk of bias and quality assessment

Qualitative risk-of-bias assessment of the 28 included studies identified several reporting concerns. Among studies reporting symptomatic reflux, 11 (39.3%) did not specify a validated symptom instrument or a scoring threshold. Among studies reporting both outcomes, 6 (21.4%) did not report severity, and 5 (14.3%) reported only the Los Angeles (LA) grade for reflux esophagitis (RE). Only two studies reported preoperative reflux, delayed gastric emptying, and bile reflux together.

Important clinical and methodological details were also incompletely reported. Six studies (21.4%) had sample sizes < 50, and six (21.4%) lacked some or substantial surgical details. Six studies (21.4%) did not report anti-reflux medication use, and 15 (55.5%) did not report neoadjuvant therapy. Study-level characteristics and key methodological variables are summarized in Table 1.

Management- and assessment-related factors were also variably reported. Four studies (14.9%) reported no PPI use, six (21.4%) treated all symptomatic patients with medication, four (14.9%) had > 50% of patients on medication, and six (21.4%) had < 50% on medication. Five studies (15%) did not specify the timing of endoscopic assessment, and six (21.4%) reported pyloric interventions in all patients. Two studies (7.4%) documented postoperative lifestyle counselling, and two (7.4%) reported preoperative reflux. Eleven studies (67%) reported bile reflux; among these, two reported bile reflux in all patients and two reported no bile reflux. Treatment- and assessment-related factors are summarized in Table 2.

Meta-analysis and publication bias

The pooled prevalence of symptomatic reflux was 38% (95% CI 29–47%; 95% PI 9–79%), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=96.3%). Across follow-up windows, the pooled prevalence was 39% at < 1 year, 40% at 1–3 years, and 26% at 3–5 years, with no significant subgroup difference (p = 0.372). The pooled prevalence of reflux esophagitis was 33% (95% CI 24–44%; 95% PI 7–76%; I²=88.8%). Corresponding estimates were 20%, 35%, and 42% across the three follow-up windows, respectively, again without a significant subgroup difference (p = 0.352) (Fig. 2). When RE was pooled by Los Angeles grade, mild RE was 19% (95% CI 12–29%; 95% PI 4–57%; I²=82.6%) and severe RE was 11% (95% CI 6–19%; 95% PI 2–46%; I²=75.6%; p = 0.09) (Supplementary Fig. 1). By study design, pooled symptomatic reflux prevalence was 36% (95% CI 18–59%; 95% PI 5–86%) in prospective cohort studies, 30% (95% CI 18–46%; 95% PI 6–75%) in retrospective cohort studies, and 48% (95% CI 29–68%; 95% PI 9–90%) in cross-sectional studies (p = 0.21). By region, pooled symptomatic reflux prevalence was 38% (95% CI 26–52%; 95% PI 7–83%) in Asia, 29% (95% CI 9–62%; 95% PI 3–86%) in Europe, and 45% (95% CI 5–93%; 95% PI 0–99%) in North America (p = 0.48) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Restricted cubic spline analyses showed nonlinear time trends (Fig. 3). RE increased over follow-up and then plateaued. Symptomatic reflux was highest in the early period, decreased at approximately 10–15 months, increased again at approximately 30–40 months, and then declined slightly (Fig. 4).

For publication bias, the funnel plot for symptomatic reflux was broadly symmetrical; the Peters test gave p = 0.613 and the Egger test p = 0.513. For RE, the funnel plot was also broadly symmetrical; the Egger test gave p = 0.621 and the Peters test p = 0.076.

Meta-regression and subgroup analyses

Study-level meta-regression showed that, for symptomatic reflux, only mean age was significantly associated with pooled prevalence. For every 5-year increase in mean age, symptomatic reflux prevalence increased (β = 1.004, 95% CI 0.291–1.718, p = 0.006). Publication year and the retrosternal reconstruction route showed only borderline associations, and no clear association was found for the other study-level variables. For reflux esophagitis, none of the included study-level covariates was significantly associated with pooled prevalence.

Outcome-specific subgroup analyses showed that between-group differences in symptomatic reflux were mainly related to age, medication background, and outcome definition. Studies with a mean age ≥ 65 years had a higher pooled prevalence than those with a mean age < 65 years (47.7% vs. 24.4%, p = 0.011). By medication coverage, the pooled prevalence was highest in the 30%–70% group (58.5%), compared with 24.2% in the < 30% group and 22.2% in the > 70% group (p < 0.001). The OG-series threshold also had a marked effect, with a higher pooled prevalence at ≥ 2 than at ≥ 3. Studies with endoscopy scheduled within ± 1 month of symptom assessment also showed a higher prevalence than those using a uniform 12-month postoperative assessment. Other subgroup factors showed numerical differences only.

For reflux esophagitis, subgroup analyses were limited by the small number of studies. Except for scheduled endoscopy time, which showed borderline significance (p = 0.081), no significant between-group differences were found for study design, region, age, male proportion, or anastomotic site. Detailed results are shown in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses showed that the main findings were robust to study-level risk of bias, influential studies, and study design. Excluding studies that failed any key risk-of-bias domain yielded a refitted model with τ² = 0.547, close to the baseline value of 0.525, and similar results were observed in domain-specific analyses. In leave-one-out analyses using the observation closest to 12 months for each study, pooled symptomatic reflux ranged from 36.2% to 39.8%, and pooled reflux esophagitis ranged from 32.5% to 37.4% (Supplementary Fig. 3). Restricting analyses to cohort studies or excluding cross-sectional studies led to only modest changes in pooled prevalence. For symptomatic reflux, the pooled prevalence was 38.1% (95% CI 29.8% to 47.3%) overall, 33.5% (95% CI 25.0% to 43.2%) in cohort-only analyses, and 33.5% (95% CI 25.0% to 43.2%) after excluding cross-sectional studies. The corresponding estimates for reflux esophagitis were 31.1% (95% CI 24.6% to 38.4%) and 34.4% (95% CI 29.6% to 39.6%), respectively (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

Our pooled results showed that reflux is a common complication after curative esophagectomy for primary esophageal cancer, with a pooled prevalence of 38% for symptomatic reflux and 33% for RE. Time-window analyses and spline models suggested that these two outcomes did not follow the same postoperative pattern. Symptomatic reflux showed a nonlinear course, with a high burden early after surgery, a decline at around 10–15 months, a second rise at around 30–40 months, and then a slight decrease. In contrast, RE increased more gradually over time and remained at a relatively high level during longer follow-up. This difference is biologically plausible. After esophagectomy, reduced early gastric acid secretion, persistent hypergastrinemia, and bile reflux may lessen typical acid-related symptoms, while still promoting non-acid reflux and mucosal inflammation, thereby contributing to RE progression even when symptom burden is not at its peak [32, 35]. Differences in the timing of endoscopic assessment may also shape this pattern: early examination may miss established mucosal injury, whereas later examination is more likely to detect chronic reflux-related damage, but is also more affected by selective follow-up and loss to follow-up [42]. The very wide prediction intervals indicate that the pooled prevalence should not be taken as a fixed benchmark for all clinical settings. In random-effects meta-analysis, the prediction interval reflects the range in which the true effect may lie in a comparable new study or setting. When this interval is wide, the pooled mean is better interpreted as an average estimate of overall burden rather than a precise value for any single center [43, 44]. Therefore, the main clinical message of these findings is that reflux burden after esophagectomy is high, and that symptom burden and mucosal injury may not be synchronized across follow-up stages. These findings support follow-up strategies that take into account dynamic changes in gastric motility and acid secretion, regard the first 1–3 postoperative years as a key period for symptom monitoring, and recognize that mucosal injury may appear or persist after the symptom peak [11].

A major source of the extreme heterogeneity in symptomatic reflux was inconsistency in outcome definition and measurement. Nearly 40% of the included studies did not clearly report whether a validated symptom tool or a prespecified threshold was used. Some studies defined the outcome only by single symptoms such as heartburn or reflux, which broadened case identification and made prevalence estimates highly sensitive to how reflux was defined. Our subgroup analysis supported this finding: within the OG-series questionnaires, pooled prevalence was clearly higher when symptomatic reflux was defined as ≥ 2 than as ≥ 3. In the EORTC QLQ-OES18/QLQ-OG25 reflux items, scores from 1 to 4 represent “not at all” to “very much” [45, 46]. Therefore, a cutoff of ≥ 2 includes mild symptoms, whereas ≥ 3 is closer to a clinically meaningful moderate-to-severe symptom burden. This suggests that threshold choice alone can substantially affect prevalence estimates in postoperative reflux research. By contrast, neither questionnaire use nor questionnaire type showed a significant between-group difference, suggesting that the main issue may lie less in the tool itself than in the lack of standardized and reproducible scoring rules. Still, the EORTC QLQ-OG25 and QLQ-OES18 were developed for patients with esophageal and esophagogastric junction cancers and remain suitable for postoperative symptom monitoring in this population, whereas GerdQ was originally designed for typical GERD and may be less well aligned with the altered physiology after esophagectomy [34]. In contrast, assessment of reflux esophagitis was relatively consistent, as most studies used endoscopy and the Los Angeles classification. Overall, symptomatic reflux prevalence largely reflects subjective symptom burden under a given measurement framework and cannot directly replace objective mucosal injury [10].

A second important source of heterogeneity was more likely related to differences in assessment setting and postoperative management than to any single outlying study. Both leave-one-out and design-based sensitivity analyses showed only limited changes in pooled prevalence after excluding studies one by one, restricting to cohort studies, or removing cross-sectional studies, suggesting that between-study variation was not mainly driven by a single large study or by study design itself. By contrast, the treatment and assessment context appeared more relevant to symptomatic reflux. Our subgroup analyses showed significant differences in pooled prevalence across study-level medication coverage groups, suggesting that treatment background may affect symptom burden. When stratified by medication status at assessment, off-therapy studies showed a numerically higher prevalence than studies allowing on-demand or regular medication use, although the difference was not significant. Previous studies support PPI treatment, and guidelines recommend assessment after PPI withdrawal when objective evidence of reflux is needed [20, 47, 48]. The lack of significance in our study may reflect the small number of studies, inconsistent off-therapy definitions, and the fact that post-esophagectomy reflux is not driven by acid exposure alone. Assessment timing may also affect prevalence estimates. Studies with endoscopy scheduled within 1 month before or after symptom assessment showed a higher prevalence of symptomatic reflux than those using a uniform 12-month postoperative assessment. Although based on few studies, this finding suggests that symptom surveys and objective testing should not be separated in postoperative follow-up. Previous studies have shown that routine endoscopy may detect reflux esophagitis and other lesions that patients may not notice [41, 49]. Therefore, symptomatic reflux should be interpreted in the context of objective testing time, and future studies should better align subjective and objective assessments to improve comparability. Some residual heterogeneity may also come from clinically plausible but hard-to-test postoperative factors, such as bile reflux, delayed gastric conduit emptying, pyloric interventions, and feeding recovery pathways [7, 11, 21, 30, 40, 50, 51]. These variables were inconsistently reported or variably defined, which limited formal comparison, but previous studies suggest that they may affect reflux symptoms, mucosal injury, and postoperative recovery. Overall, the extreme heterogeneity in symptomatic reflux prevalence likely reflects the combined effects of assessment conditions, treatment background, and postoperative physiology.

Beyond measurement and assessment differences, some between-study variation may reflect real differences in reflux biology, patient vulnerability, and reconstruction strategy after esophagectomy. In our study-level analysis, higher mean age was associated with a higher prevalence of symptomatic reflux, possibly reflecting differences in functional status, comorbidity burden, and physiological reserve in older patients [52, 53]. However, this study-level signal should not be interpreted as a patient-level causal effect. Publication year was not significantly associated with symptomatic reflux prevalence, although a borderline positive trend was observed, which may reflect temporal changes in symptom recognition, follow-up intensity, and postoperative management [6, 54]. Park et al. reported a higher risk of symptomatic reflux with the retrosternal route [9]. In our meta-regression, however, this factor showed only a borderline association, with the direction suggesting a possible lower prevalence of symptomatic reflux. Current evidence is therefore insufficient to support a stable independent effect. Cho et al. also noted inconsistent findings on whether the posterior mediastinal route reduces postoperative reflux [55]. These differences may relate to symptom definitions, follow-up windows, postoperative medication use, and differences between study-level and patient-level analyses [11]. Overall, reconstruction-related factors are more likely to act together than alone, and their clinical meaning should be interpreted cautiously. Anastomotic site has also been considered important [11, 56]. Ao et al. reported no significant difference between procedures in patient-reported reflux scores or long-term quality of life [57]. In our arm-level analysis, symptomatic reflux and reflux esophagitis showed different directional patterns, but neither between-group comparisons nor study-level meta-regression reached significance. This may reflect limited power and the small number of directly comparable studies and suggests that any effect of anastomotic level is likely intertwined with reconstruction route, conduit configuration, pyloric management, and postoperative medication status. Overall, these findings suggest that postoperative follow-up should not rely on a single fixed approach but should be stratified by patient vulnerability and surgical features to identify high-risk patients earlier and improve individualized management.

From a clinical perspective, the key message of this review is not that any single symptom tool can replace objective testing, but that reflux after esophagectomy should be monitored with a standardized symptom-based strategy and interpreted together with mucosal findings. Based on our results, no symptom-based patient-reported outcome tool or cutoff showed a sufficiently stable and consistent relationship with objective mucosal injury, and these measures should not be used as surrogate endpoints for reflux esophagitis after esophagectomy. Still, when symptom monitoring is needed, the reflux items in the EORTC QLQ-OES18/QLQ-OG25 remain a practical option because they are cancer-specific, widely used in this field, and more suitable for between-study comparison. Given the marked effect of threshold choice on pooled prevalence, we suggest using ≥ 3 as the main cutoff for clinically meaningful symptomatic reflux when a binary definition is required, while also reporting continuous scores or using ≥ 2 in sensitivity analyses [58, 59]. For objective assessment, a more appropriate strategy is to pair endoscopy with standardized symptom monitoring at prespecified time points, while recording acid-suppressive treatment status and time since surgery to improve interpretability and comparability [33, 35, 56]. Endoscopic assessment should use the Los Angeles classification and clearly document acid-suppressive treatment status at the time of assessment, ideally distinguishing off-therapy from on-therapy evaluation [60, 61]. To improve future comparability, a minimum follow-up and reporting framework should also record the exact time from surgery to assessment, the PRO tool and recall window, the prespecified symptom threshold, current acid-suppressive treatment, and, where possible, delayed gastric conduit emptying, bile reflux, pyloric interventions, anastomotic site, reconstruction details, and feeding recovery (Supplementary Table 7). This framework may help better distinguish symptom burden from mucosal injury and reduce avoidable heterogeneity in future studies.

This study has several major limitations. First, reporting in the original studies was often incomplete, particularly for medication exposure, lifestyle counseling, bile reflux, delayed gastric conduit emptying, pyloric interventions, and key surgical details, which increased the risk of bias and limited exploration of heterogeneity. Second, symptomatic reflux was not measured uniformly across studies; several reports did not use validated questionnaires or prespecified thresholds, which may have led to over- or underestimation of pooled prevalence. Third, despite subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses, substantial residual heterogeneity remained, and some exploratory analyses were based on only a small number of studies. Finally, meta-regression findings were study-level, and the included studies were predominantly conducted in Asia, which may limit patient-level interpretation and generalizability to other settings.

Conclusion

In summary, this review shows that reflux is a frequent and clinically important late complication after esophagectomy, with about one third to one half of patients experiencing persistent symptoms or reflux esophagitis. Follow-up should monitor reflux for at least 3–5 years after surgery, with the first 3 years being the key period for assessment and intervention. Because symptom and mucosal trajectories differ and are influenced by non-standardized symptom tools, variable PPI use, and incomplete reporting of delayed gastric emptying, bile reflux, and pyloric procedures, symptoms alone cannot reliably reflect esophageal injury. Structured, time-sensitive follow-up combining validated symptom scales, planned endoscopy, and routine documentation of medication status at assessment, surgical details, feeding, and lifestyle counseling may improve risk stratification and long-term symptom management, particularly in clinically vulnerable survivors.
