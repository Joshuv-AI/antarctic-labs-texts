Methodology and baseline characteristics of the China Migraine registry (CHIME): a nationwide, multi-center, prospective cohort study of 11,814 patients
Authors: Ye Ran, Zizi He, Xun Han, Hui Su, Mingjie Zhang, Shuhua Zhang, Longteng Ma, Shengyuan Yu, Zhao Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is the leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) in young and middle-aged populations globally. Despite strategic initiatives to standardize headache care in China, large-scale real-world evidence regarding clinical management patterns remains sparse. The China Migraine Registry (CHIME) was established to bridge this gap. This study aims to delineate the CHIME methodology and report the baseline clinical characteristics, diagnostic accuracy, and therapeutic patterns of its initial cohort.MethodsCHIME is a nationwide, multi-center, prospective, longitudinal cohort study involving over 100 specialized headache centers across 28 provinces in China. Initiated in May 2024, the study utilizes a proprietary digital health platform integrated with a clinical decision support system (CDSS 2.0) for headache disorders. Data collection encompasses structured clinical profiles, patient-reported outcomes, and specialized sub-study cores. This baseline analysis details the cross-sectional characteristics of patients enrolled through February 15, 2026.ResultsAmong 11,814 participants (79.43% female; mean age 37.42 ± 10.94 years), 10,123 (85.7%) had episodic migraine (EM), 1,019 (8.6%) had chronic migraine (CM), and 672 (5.7%) had probable migraine. A profound diagnostic-therapeutic gap was identified: only 31.0% of patients had received a correct prior diagnosis. Paradoxically, 91.43% had undergone auxiliary investigations, primarily cranial imaging (87.36%). Acute treatment relied heavily on non-specific analgesics (NSAIDs: 59.77% EM, 51.03% CM). Among patients meeting clinical criteria for prophylaxis, only 26.59% were receiving preventive therapy, and the treatment maintenance rate was critically low (12.56% for ≥3 months). CM patients exhibited significantly higher disability (MIDAS Grade IV: 52.2% vs. 26.9%) and psychiatric comorbidities compared to EM patients (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe CHIME study reveals a paradoxical “treatment odyssey” in China characterized by a low correct diagnosis rate, pervasive diagnostic over-investigation, alongside a systemic under-utilization of evidence-based therapies. Introduction

Migraine remains a formidable global health challenge, ranking as the eighth leading cause of disability worldwide according to the latest Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data. In 2023, migraine accounted for an estimated 40.9 million years lived with disability (YLDs), with an age-standardized YLD rate of 487.5 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 323.0–678.8). The age-standardized prevalence rate is estimated at 11,420 per 100,000 (95% UI: 9,841–12,927), and this rate for individuals aged 20 years and older is significantly higher at 14,612 per 100,000 (95% UI: 12,395–16,875) [1]. In China, the socioeconomic impact is equally profound. A nationwide investigation estimated that direct and indirect costs associated with migraine constituted approximately 1.1% of the national Gross Domestic Product (GDP) [2]. Despite its high prevalence and burden, significant gaps persist in clinical management. Current evidence reveals that only 52.9% of affected individuals seek professional medical consultation, and diagnostic accuracy remains alarmingly low (ranging from 13.8% to 27.3%). Furthermore, only a fraction of eligible patients (16.5%) receive evidence-based preventive pharmacotherapy [3, 4].

The recent decade has witnessed remarkable advancements in migraine therapeutics. However, many patients continue to endure a substantial disease burden [4]. This discrepancy stems from structural inequities in healthcare resource allocation and the inherent clinical heterogeneity of migraine, which leads to significant inter-individual variability in treatment responses. Recognizing these challenges, China has launched several strategic initiatives to bolster headache care. These include the establishment of over 700 specialized headache centers and clinics, the translation and implementation of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), and the development and dissemination of evidence-based clinical guidelines [5].

Nevertheless, the absence of standardized, large-scale prospective cohorts remains a critical bottleneck. Such registries are indispensable for monitoring diagnostic paradigms, assessing real-world maintenance of prophylactic treatment, and identifying disparities in clinical practice across diverse regions. To bridge these critical knowledge gaps and advance the clinical understanding of migraine within the Chinese population, we established the China Migraine Registry (CHIME)—a comprehensive observational registry structured around three synergistic modules: (1) a nationwide cross–sectional analysis of contemporary diagnostic and therapeutic patterns; (2) a prospective longitudinal evaluation of pharmacological treatment outcomes; and (3) a multimodal biomarker investigation incorporating biofluid analysis, electroencephalography (EEG), and advanced neuroimaging. The present article delineates the methodological framework of CHIME, encompassing multidimensional data collection protocols, the implementation of unified electronic case report forms (eCRFs), robust quality assurance mechanisms, and the baseline characteristics of the initial cohort.

Methods

Study oversight

The study protocol was developed by the Chinese PLA General Hospital in accordance with the International Headache Society (IHS) Guidelines for Clinic-Based Headache Registries [6]. The study is conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the ethics committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (Approval No. 2022-565; No. 2023-459-01; No. 2023-460-01; No. 2023-815-02; No. 2024-347-01), as well as by the respective ethics committees of all participating centers. All participants provide informed consent prior to enrollment. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Study design and setting

The CHIME study is a nationwide, multi-center, prospective, longitudinal cohort registry for migraine, conducted within the China Primary Headache Real-World Study framework. The study aims to recruit a target population of at least 16,500 patients with migraine from over 100 certified headache centers across China between May 2024 and December 2027. The registry is formally registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200064494 and ChiCTR2400090162).

The target sample size was calculated to ensure sufficient statistical power for estimating key clinical indicators with high precision. Based on previous literature, a conservative expected proportion of 10% was utilized for key clinical parameters. With an allowable error of 1%, a significance level (ɑ) of 0.05, and a 95% confidence interval, the required sample size was determined to be 13,161 using PASS 15.0 software. Accounting for a potential 10% non-response rate and a 10% rate of invalid questionnaires, the final target enrollment was set at 16,500 participants. This substantial sample size also facilitates robust subgroup analyses across different geographic regions and clinical phenotypes.

Participants

Eligible participants include adults (age ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with migraine or probable migraine by headache specialists at the participating headache centers according to the criteria of ICHD-3. To minimize selection bias, each center performs consecutive recruitment of eligible patients. All participating headache centers are certified by the Chinese Research Hospital Association and adhere to standardized diagnostic and operational protocols to ensure inter-center consistency [5]. Detailed information regarding the headache centers is accessible at https://www.headache.org.cn/.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) secondary headache disorders, with the exception of medication overuse headache (MOH); (2) other primary headache disorders (e.g., tension-type headache, cluster headache) in the absence of a migraine or probable migraine diagnosis.

Study procedures and data collection

All enrolled participants are integrated into the Clinical Core for baseline and longitudinal assessment. Depending on the site’s capabilities and patient’s consent, participants may optionally enroll in additional sub-study modules, including the Biobank, Environmental Factors, MRI, and Neuroelectrophysiology Cores (see Fig. 1 for a structural overview).

Organizational framework and modular structure of the CHIME study. The CHIME registry adopts a multi-dimensional architecture centered on the clinical core, in which all participants are enrolled for standardized baseline and longitudinal assessments. Surrounding this foundation are four optional, specialized modules: (1) Biobank core for biospecimen storage; (2) Environmental Factors core for exposome analysis; (3) MRI core for multimodal neuroimaging; and (4) Neuroelectrophysiology core for functional brain evaluation. Participants undergo a structured clinical interview and eligibility screening at the initial visit. For those enrolled in optional modules, biospecimen collection (blood/saliva) and specialized examinations (MRI/EEG) are performed at the baseline time point

At the baseline visit, patients complete a comprehensive electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) with assistance from trained clinical staff provided as necessary. To ensure high–fidelity data collection, the process integrates a proprietary clinical decision support system (CDSS 2.0) for headache disorders. This system was developed by our research team and has been previously validated for its diagnostic accuracy and clinical efficiency [7]. The digital interface employs automated logic checks and mandatory fields to minimize missing data and prevent implausible entries [8, 9]. The standardized baseline assessment is structured into four functional modules: (1) Socio-demographic Profile: Including age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index, educational attainment, occupational status, marital status, and annual household income. (2) Clinical Characteristics and Therapeutic History: Covering disease course, symptom profiles, attack frequency and duration, aura characteristics, triggers, and a comprehensive record of current and prior treatments. (3) Headache-Related Burden: Assessing healthcare resource utilization, impact on daily life and productivity (using a validated questionnaire adapted from the Global Campaign against Headache “Lifting The Burden” initiative [2]), and willingness–to–pay for treatment. (4) Patient-Reported Outcomes: A battery of standardized instruments including the Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS), Migraine-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire version 2.1 (MSQ v2.1), 8-item World Health Organization Quality of Life questionnaire (WHOQoL-8), Office for National Statistics 4-item well-being scale (ONS-4), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (detailed in Table 1).

Data acquisition is facilitated by the CDSS 2.0 for headache. Initially, patients complete a comprehensive digital questionnaire in the waiting room to provide their demographic and headache history. During the subsequent consultation, the attending neurologist reviews this pre-filled data as a foundation for formal history-taking. Data verification occurs in real-time; if discrepancies were identified—such as a patient misreporting a prior diagnosis or medication—the physician corrects the entries on their digital terminal based on clinical judgment and a review of the patient’s medical records. Consequently, the registry only captures finalized data that have been confirmed and validated by a headache specialist.

Following the baseline visit, longitudinal data are captured through a patient-facing WeChat mini-program (“Headache Supervisor”). This digital tool serves as an electronic headache diary for real-time recording of headache days, pain characteristics, and acute/preventive medication use. Although the CHIME registry maintains a one-year follow-up period to assess long-term outcomes, the present analysis focuses strictly on the cross-sectional baseline data. (The data collection overview is shown in Fig. 2).

Workflow of digital data acquisition and longitudinal follow-up in the CHIME study. Data collection is partitioned into three phases: (1) Pre-visit: patients input structured clinical profiles, including demographics and headache characteristics, via the CDSS 2.0 WeChat interface; (2) Clinical consultation: neurologists review patient-reported data through a dedicated physician terminal, establish formal diagnoses according to ICHD-3 criteria, and obtain written informed consent from eligible participants; (3) Longitudinal follow-up: enrolled patients maintain electronic headache diaries and record medication use via the “Headache Supervisor” WeChat mini-program for 12 months to assess long-term clinical outcomes

Study modules and specialized cores

Biobank core: Participants opting into the Biobank Core provide blood samples at enrollment. To ensure sample integrity for future multi-omics analysis, all biospecimens are collected, processed, and aliquoted under standardized pre-analytical conditions and stored in ultra-low temperature freezers (−80 °C) at the central biobank.

Environmental Factors Core: This module employs a comprehensive “exposome” approach. Participants undergo detailed, validated assessments of lifestyle and environmental exposures at enrollment, including structured evaluations of dietary patterns, ethanol and tobacco consumption, physical activity levels, digital media exposure, and ambient noise levels.

MRI and Neuroelectrophysiology Cores: Participants in these specialized cores undergo advanced neuroimaging and functional brain mapping. Imaging is performed using 3.0-Tesla or 7.0-Tesla MRI scanners, following a standardized protocol that includes T1-weighted morphometry, blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) functional MRI, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). In Parallel, scalp electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings are captured to evaluate neural oscillations. Crucially, the migraine phase (ictal or interictal) at the time of examination is documented to account for cyclicity-related brain state variations.

Outcome measures

The CHIME study evaluates a multidimensional set of outcomes across its various cores. While primary, secondary, and exploratory outcomes will be detailed in specific future publications, the general framework is as follows:

Clinical Core: Primary and Secondary Outcomes: These focus on longitudinal clinical evolution, including changes from baseline in monthly migraine days (MMDs) and monthly headache days (MHDs); Therapeutic Efficacy: Assessments include acute treatment outcomes (e.g., pain-free at 2 hours, time to relief, and patient satisfaction) and prophylactic treatment maintenance; Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROs): Changes in standardized scores for disability (MIDAS, MSQ v2.1), quality of life and subjective well being (WHOQoL-8, ONS-4), and psychological comorbidities (PHQ-9, GAD-7).

Biobank Core: Outcomes involve the quantification of circulating biomarkers related to migraine pathophysiology, such as inflammatory cytokines, neuropeptides (e.g., CGRP, PACAP), and metabolomic/proteomic signatures.

Environmental Factors Core: The primary outcome is the longitudinal association between multi-domain environmental exposures and migraine frequency trajectories.

MRI/EEG Cores: Outcomes include group-level and longitudinal differences in structural morphometry, functional connectivity networks, and neural oscillations patterns, aimed at identifying quantitative neuroimaging biomarkers for disease progression and treatment response.

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes for the specialized sub-studies (Biobank: 3,000; Environmental Factors: 8,000; MRI: 3,000; EEG: 5,000) were determined based on logistical feasibility and the statistical requirements typical of biomarker and neuroimaging investigations. For the present baseline cross-sectional analysis, all eligible patients enrolled on or before February 15, 2026, were included.

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) based on their distribution. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Missing data were minimal (<1%) and occurred completely at random; thus, a pairwise deletion was employed to maximize the utility of the datasets.

Differences in clinical characteristics and disease burden across migraine subtypes were assessed using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey-Kramer adjustment or Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.3.0.

Results

Demographic Profiles and Disease Severity across Migraine Subtypes

Demographic Profiles and Disease Severity across Migraine Subtypes

A total of 11,814 patients from 113 headache centers across 28 provinces and municipalities in China were included in the baseline analysis (Supplementary Table 1). The cohort demonstrated a strong female predominance (79.43%), with a mean age of 37.42 ± 10.94 years. Detailed socioeconomic characteristics, including education level, employment status, annual household income, and ethnicity, are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Regarding clinical classification, 85.69% (n = 10,123) of the participants were diagnosed with episodic migraine (EM), 8.63% (n = 1,019) with chronic migraine (CM), and 5.69% (n = 672) with probable migraine (PM). Clinical severity and disease course varied significantly across these three subgroups. CM patients exhibited a substantially higher headache frequency than the other two groups (24.73 ± 5.92 MHDs vs. 4.22 ± 3.12 MHDs for EM and 5.25 ± 5.67 MHDs for PM; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the mean disease duration was notably longer in the CM group (16.14 ± 10.45 years) than in the EM (10.66 ± 9.17 years) and PM (9.87 ± 8.61 years) groups (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

2. Diagnostic Gaps and Utilization of Auxiliary Investigations

Diagnostic Gaps and Utilization of Auxiliary Investigations

Migraine without aura (MO) was the most prevalent subtype (77.09%), followed by CM (8.63%), migraine with aura (MA, 8.60%), and probable migraine (PM, 5.69%). Notably, nearly half of the CM patients concurrently met the criteria for MOH, accounting for 3.79% (n = 448) of the total study population.

A profound diagnostic gap was identified: prior to enrollment, only 31.00% of participants had received a correct prior diagnosis of “migraine”. The remaining 69.00% were either undiagnosed (42.94%) or misdiagnosed (26.06%). Among those misclassified, non-standard terms such as “neurogenic headache” (10.50%) and “normal” (9.09%) were the most frequent incorrect labels (Fig. 3).

Of the 11,814 participants, 51.91% (n = 6,133) had sought prior medical consultation for their headaches. To ensure the stringency of the diagnostic accuracy analysis, patients who reported being unable to recall their previous consultation history or specific diagnoses were categorized into the “uncertain/not consulted” group. The proportion of patients who had sought care from a specialist (including neurologists or headache specialists) prior to enrollment was 28.25% (n = 3,337), while 23.67% (n = 2,796) had consulted non-specialist providers (Supplementary Table 3). As illustrated in the Sankey diagram for the consulted cohort (Supplementary Figure 1), specialist consultation was associated with a significantly higher diagnostic accuracy compared to non-specialists (48.07% vs. 37.41%, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Table 3). Notably, the rate of correct diagnosis among these consulted patients remained suboptimal.

Despite the suboptimal rate of pre-enrollment diagnostic accuracy, historical auxiliary over-investigation—ordered as part of routine clinical management prior to study enrollment—was strikingly pervasive. It is important to note that these utilization rates exclusively reflect prior clinical care and strictly exclude any protocol-driven investigations performed post-enrollment as part of the optional CHIME sub-study modules (such as the MRI or Neuroelectrophysiology Cores). Notably, 91.43% (n = 3,348) of patients with a correct prior diagnosis and 92.17% (n = 2,838) of those who had been misdiagnosed had undergone at least one auxiliary investigation. Cranial imaging was the most frequently performed investigation, utilized by 87.36% of the correctly diagnosed group and 86.65% of the misdiagnosed group. Other diagnostic modalities also showed high utilization across both groups (Fig. 4). Specifically, the misdiagnosed group exhibited a higher rate of cervical imaging compared to the correctly diagnosed group (30.95% vs. 27.80%, p < 0.01). Conversely, patent foramen ovale (PFO) screening (25.72% vs. 12.24%, p < 0.01) and electroencephalography (EEG; 20.13% vs. 16.56%, p < 0.01) were more frequently performed in those who had received an accurate diagnosis prior to study entry. These findings, detailed in Supplementary Table 4, underscore a significant reliance on technical examinations regardless of diagnostic precision (Fig. 4A).

Both specialist and non-specialist groups demonstrated a high propensity for ordering pre-enrollment diagnostic investigations, with cranial neuroimaging being the most prevalent (72.28% in the specialist group vs. 75.18% in the non-specialist group). Notably, screening for PFO was significantly more frequent among patients who consulted specialists compared to those managed by non-specialists (21.22% vs. 18.81%, p = 0.0193) (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table 5).

3. Acute and prophylactic treatment patterns

Acute and prophylactic treatment patterns

Regarding acute treatment, NSAIDs were the primary medication class utilized across both groups (59.8% in EM vs. 51.0% in CM). Conversely, the utilization of migraine-specific abortive therapies, such as triptans, remained notably low, accounting for only 14.2% of EM and 17.2% of CM patients. Of particular concern was the high reliance on non-specific compound preparations—primarily consisting of analgesic powders or caffeine-containing compounds—which was observed in 31.9% of CM patients (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Table 6).

A significant gap was identified in preventive management. According to the Chinese Guideline on the Diagnosis and Management of Migraine (2022 Edition)—the national clinical standard—prophylaxis is indicated for patients experiencing more than two monthly migraine days (MMD > 2), headache duration exceeding 72 hours, or severe disability (MIDAS grade IV) even with a low frequency (MMD ≤ 2 days). In our study, 7,763 (65.71%) patients met these clinical criteria; however, only 2,064 (26.59%) were actively receiving preventive treatment (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, the proportion of patients maintaining established, long-term preventive therapy (≥3 months) was critically low: only 12.56% (n = 128) of CM patients and 8.63% (n = 544) of EM patients reached this threshold at the time of the survey (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table 7). In terms of specific medications, flunarizine (8.63%) and Traditional Chinese Patent Medicine (TCPM) (8.85%) were identified as the most frequently utilized preventive agents (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table 8).

Stratification of medication patterns by physician specialty revealed substantial discrepancies. Patients managed by headache specialists were significantly more likely to receive migraine–specific abortive therapies, including triptans (21.91% vs. 15.06%, p < 0.001) and gepants (6.95% vs. 3.04%, p < 0.001). A similar trend was observed in prophylactic management. Compared to non-specialists, headache specialists prescribed evidence-based preventives at significantly higher rates: calcium channel blockers (14.08% vs. 10.94%), anti-epileptic drugs (8.75% vs. 3.04%), anti-depressants (4.55% vs. 1.97%), and CGRP-targeted therapies (3.45% vs. 1.32%) (all p < 0.001). Conversely, patients consulting non-specialist providers exhibited a greater reliance on non-specific compound preparations for acute treatment (18.13% vs. 14.14%, p < 0.001) and were more likely to utilize TCPM for migraine prophylaxis (12.80% vs. 10.76%, p = 0.013) (Fig. 5 E & F; Supplementary Table 9).

4. Disease Burden and Comorbidities

Disease Burden and Comorbidities

The progression toward migraine chronification was associated with a profound physiological and psychological burden. Severe disability, defined as MIDAS Grade IV, was present in more than half of the CM cohort (52.21%, n = 532), a prevalence significantly higher than that observed in the EM group (26.89%, n = 2,722; p < 0.001). This physical impairment was further compounded by a heightened burden of psychiatric comorbidities. Compared to patients with EM, those with CM exhibited a substantially higher prevalence of moderate-to-severe depression (29.64% vs. 17.21%, p < 0.001) and anxiety (14.62% vs. 8.89%, p < 0.001). Furthermore, sleep disturbances were pervasive across the study population, with 62.00% of the total cohort reporting poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5). Consistent with these findings, the prevalence of both severely impaired quality of life (WHOQoL-8 Index < 3) and diminished subjective well-being (ONS-4 score < 5) was significantly higher in the CM group than in the EM group (poor quality of life: 26.92% vs. 17.23%; low well-being: 12.77% vs. 8.31%; all p < 0.001) (Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 10).

Discussion

The baseline data of 11,814 patients in the CHIME study—the largest prospective migraine registry in China to date—delineates a protracted “treatment odyssey” faced by Chinese migraineurs. Despite significant national initiatives to standardize headache care through the establishment of specialized centers, our findings reveal a persistent and paradoxical triad of a profound diagnostic gap, rampant over-investigation, and systemic under-treatment.

The diagnostic gap and International context

When contextualized within the global migraine landscape, the CHIME study reveals striking disparities in management paradigms between China and Western nations. The diagnostic accuracy observed prior to specialist referral (34.54%) remains considerably lower than the approximately 60% reported in the U.S. OVERCOME study [10], and mirrors the widespread diagnostic barriers historically identified across European primary care settings in the Eurolight project [11]. Although the correct diagnosis rate in China has improved from 13.8% in 2013 [3, 12] to 27.3% in 2020–2021 [4], it remains suboptimal for routine clinical practice. A substantial proportion of patients continue to be misclassified with obsolete or vague terms such as “neurogenic headache” or “neurovascular headache,” reflecting an insufficient penetration of ICHD-3 criteria among primary care physicians. Our data confirms that consultation with a headache specialist remains the strongest predictor of diagnostic accuracy, highlighting the imperative for a tiered headache care system and the decentralization of specialist knowledge to primary care settings—a strategy strongly advocated by recent global consensus reports on optimizing headache service organization [13]. These observations are highly consistent with our previous investigations regarding the development and challenges of headache centers within the Chinese healthcare system [5].

However, it is noteworthy that even among specialists, the captured diagnostic accuracy appears relatively low (48.70%). It is plausible that this figure represents an underestimation attributable to recall bias inherent in a cross-sectional study design; patients often recall receiving symptomatic relief (e.g., “painkillers”) but may fail to accurately remember or report the formal “migraine” diagnosis communicated during prior specialist consultations. A more probable explanation lies in the clinical practice of providing provisional symptomatic diagnoses (e.g., ‘headache to be investigated’) during initial work-ups. Patients may undergo various investigations but fail to receive a definitive ICHD-3 diagnosis if they do not attend follow-up visits after the tests are completed. This highlights a critical ‘leakage’ in the diagnostic pipeline, where the transition from symptom-based investigation to specific primary headache diagnosis is not finalized.

The paradox of over-investigation and defensive medicine

While international and Chinese guidelines consistently discourage routine neuroimaging for primary headache disorders lacking “red flags” [14, 15], the over-utilization of diagnostic investigations remains a formidable global challenge in neurological practice [16]. Within the CHIME cohort, this phenomenon is particularly pronounced. The rates of cranial and cervical imaging were staggeringly high, exceeding 70 and 25%, respectively. Notably, these elevated rates were observed independent of prior diagnostic accuracy, suggesting that a correct diagnosis does not necessarily preclude redundant testing. This finding echoes American Headache Society’s recommendations against performing routine neuroimaging in patients with stable headaches meeting migraine criteria [17].

A paradoxical finding was that the utilization of contrast-enhanced transcranial Doppler (cTCD, or “bubble tests”) and echocardiography was significantly higher among patients who had already received a correct diagnosis compared to those who had not. This trend underscores a widespread clinical misconception regarding the pathogenic significance of PFO in migraine management. Although current international consensus statements strictly advise against routine PFO screening for all migraine patients or closure for migraine prevention except in highly selected patients [18, 19]. This practice remains stubbornly entrenched in certain clinical settings.

For a primary headache disorder like migraine, clinical history remains the gold standard for diagnosis; however, the systemic reliance on MRI, CT, TCD, and EEG reveals a deep-seated dependency on instrumental tests when diagnostic confidence is lacking. This “defensive medicine” or “exclusionary diagnostic logic” not only fails to improve diagnostic yield but also imposes a substantial and avoidable socioeconomic burden on the healthcare system, highlighting an urgent need for targeted educational interventions and stricter adherence to evidence-based neuroimaging stewardship.

Inefficient acute care and the prophylactic treatment gap

The treatment gap in China is further characterized by a “low-specificity” pharmacological profile and a significant misalignment with evidence-based guidelines. Our triptan utilization rate (14.2%–17.2%) is remarkably lower than the 89.3% reported in the Danish REFORM study or the 40–50% reported in European registries [11, 20]. This disparity underscores a critical “poor alignment” between pre-enrollment clinical practice and international standards, which advocate for migraine-specific medications as first-line acute therapy [21]. Our analysis reveals that physician specialty is a pivotal determinant of prescription quality: patients consulting specialists were significantly more likely to receive migraine-specific medications, whereas those seen by non-specialists predominantly received non-specific analgesics or TCPM. This mirrors findings from European studies, where triptan utilization was substantially higher among patients managed by specialists compared to those managed by general practitioners (51.3% vs. 35.9%) [11]. The “suboptimal alignment” observed at baseline reflects a lack of specialized knowledge or “treatment inertia” in pre-referral settings. This is particularly evident among CM patients, where inexpensive and highly accessible compound analgesics remain dominant; their usage rate (31.9%) is nearly double that of specific treatments (17.2%). This widespread reliance on non-standard acute therapy is likely a key driver of migraine chronification and the high prevalence of MOH

The gap in preventive therapy is even more pronounced. Among patients meeting the clinical criteria for prophylaxis, only 26.6% were receiving preventive treatment, and less than 10% of individuals were maintained on established, long-term preventive therapy (≥3 months). This indicates that the current management status suffers not only from a quantitative gap in “access” but also a a profound challenge in long-term “maintenance”. This low continuation rate is not unique to China; it reflects a universal clinical hurdle where the discrepancy between patient expectations for rapid relief and the delayed onset of oral preventives frequently drives premature discontinuation globally [22, 23].

Notably, flunarizine (8.63%) and TCPM (8.85%) were the most consistently prescribed preventive options across both EM and CM groups, effectively constituting the “first-line defense” for migraine prophylaxis in China. This pharmacological landscape stands in sharp contrast to Western clinical practice, where CGRP-targeted therapies, topiramate, or beta-blockers are the standard of care [24, 25]. The current underutilization of novel CGRP-based therapies in our cohort closely mirrors the initial post-market access barriers—such as delayed insurance reimbursement and high out-of-pocket costs—previously observed during the early rollout phases in European healthcare systems [26]. Furthermore, the prominent role of TCPM as a preventive strategy reflects a unique cultural therapeutic preference absent in the German DMKG or American ARMR registries [27, 28], necessitating further investigation into its long-term efficacy and safety relative to internationally recognized prophylactic standards. By documenting this baseline “misalignment,” the CHIME registry establishes a necessary benchmark to evaluate how future interventions—supported by CDSS 2.0 and specialized headache services—can bridge the gap between real-world practice and idealized guideline recommendations.

Multidimensional disease burden and comorbidities

Our results demonstrate a profound disability burden, with over half (52.2%) of CM patients classified as having MIDAS Grade IV disability—nearly double the proportion observed in the EM group (26.9%). This striking escalation in severe disability among CM patients closely aligns with findings from large-scale Western registries, such as the CaMEO and OVERCOME studies, reinforcing the universal functional devastation associated with migraine chronification [29, 30].

This escalated disability is intricately linked to a high prevalence of comorbidities, particularly sleep disorders (62.0%) and significant psychological distress. Specifically, CM patients exhibited substantially higher rates of moderate-to-severe depression (29.6% vs. 17.2%) and anxiety (14.6% vs. 8.9%) compared to their EM counterparts. Rather than viewing these merely as secondary consequences of chronic pain, contemporary neurobiological frameworks emphasize a bidirectional relationship. Unmanaged psychiatric comorbidities and poor sleep quality are established independent risk factors that drive the transition from episodic to chronic migraine [31]. This suggests that the current “pain-centric” treatment model must evolve into a multidisciplinary management approach that integrates psychological support and sleep intervention alongside pharmacological prophylaxis.

Study significance and the role of digital Health integration

While established registries such as American ARMR [28], and the German DMKG [27] or NeuroTransData [32] have provided invaluable insights into migraine management in western population, China has historically lacked a comparable large-scale.platform. The CHIME study was initiated to fill this void. As reflected in our baseline data, this registry offers a high-resolution mapping of the current migraine landscape in China, exposing critical disparities in diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic access across various healthcare tiers.

Beyond its cross-sectional utility, the CHIME registry’s architecture facilitates a transition toward longitudinal and translational precision medicine. By integrating real-time digital headache diaries with multimodal biological and neuroimaging cores, CHIME provides a unique opportunity to characterize the “phenotypic evolution” of migraine. Specifically, the longitudinal tracking of this massive cohort will enable the identification of robust predictive biomarkers for migraine chronification, remission, and treatment response. This integrated digital approach not only enhances data fidelity but also establishes a sustainable framework for evaluating long-term socioeconomic impacts and optimizing healthcare resource allocation within the Chinese healthcare system.

A primary strength of the CHIME registry lies in its integration of a validated digital health ecosystem (the CDSS 2.0 for headache disorders and the “Headache Supervisor” mini-program) into routine clinical workflow. By leveraging China’ s most ubiquitous social platform, WeChat, we addressed a common bottleneck in large-scale registries: the burden of manual history-taking and the risk of recall bias in headache diaries [7].

This “pre-visit entry + physician verification” model not only enhances diagnostic precision but also ensures high data granularity and standardization across the multi-center nationwide cohort [33]. Furthermore, the transition from conventional paper diaries to a seamless electronic interface likely facilitates sustained patient engagement and follow-up adherence, which are critical for the longitudinal robustness of the registry.

Limitations

Despite its scale, this study has several limitations. First, the referral bias inherent in tertiary headache centers may skew the cohort toward patients with higher disease severity and chronicity. Second, recall bias may affect the accuracy of pre-enrollment data, particularly regarding prior clinical diagnoses and treatment history. Because patients provided this information retrospectively—often without access to previous medical records—there is a possibility that the rates of ‘undiagnosed’ status or ‘non-specific’ treatments are slightly inflated. Third, the cross-sectional nature of this analysis precludes causal inferences. Fourth, despite rigorous training, inter-center variability in clinical practice across 28 provinces may exist. Fifth, the lack of granular data on the diagnostic yield of historical investigations is a limitation. While we focused on utilization rates to highlight over-investigation, the fact that all participants were ultimately confirmed as having primary headache disorders suggests that prior tests rarely yielded findings that altered the diagnosis. Sixth, the digital divide may introduce selection bias, potentially underrepresenting digitally marginalized populations.

Conclusions

The CHIME study, representing the largest prospective migraine registry in China to date, provides critical evidence of the significant gaps in migraine management at a national scale. Our baseline findings highlight a paradoxical landscape where a profound diagnostic deficit coexists with rampant over-investigation. Furthermore, the severe under-utilization of specific abortive therapies and prophylactic treatments—coupled with poor long-term adherence—underscores a systemic failure in translating clinical guidelines into real-world practice. Addressing these challenges requires a strategic shift toward strengthening primary care training in ICHD-3 criteria, curbing non-essential diagnostic tests, and fostering a multidisciplinary management model that integrates psychological interventions. As the longitudinal phase of CHIME progresses, it will offer a unique platform for identifying predictive biomarkers and optimizing precision treatment strategies for the Chinese migraine population.
