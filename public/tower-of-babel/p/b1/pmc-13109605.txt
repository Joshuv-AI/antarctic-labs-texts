Episiotomy and Obstetric Anal Sphincter Injury Among Unassisted Vaginal Births: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Meghan G. Hill, Aimee Humphries, Jessica Wilson, Amanda D. Harvey, Robin Cronin, John M. D. Thompson, Lynn C. Sadler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMediolateral episiotomy (MLE) is a common procedure. Whether it modifies the risk of obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) in nulliparous and parous people is unclear.AimsWe performed a retrospective cohort study to assess if MLE was associated with decreased OASI.Materials and MethodsA retrospective cohort study including all unassisted vaginal births from 36 weeks' from 2008 to 2021 was assembled. Analysis was separated for first and subsequent vaginal births, adjusting for maternal height, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, prior caesarean birth (if no previous vaginal birth) or pre‐delivery parity (if there was a previous vaginal birth), smoking status, augmentation of labour, induction of labour, length of second stage, caregiver type, socioeconomic deprivation quintile, gestational age, baby head circumference, baby birthweight and use of epidural using Generalized Linear Models.ResultsThere were 49 363 unassisted vaginal births. Of these, 20 201 were first vaginal births (19 082 nullipara and 1119 with one prior caesarean birth), 27 825 subsequent vaginal births, and 1337 unknown vaginal birth status. Episiotomy and OASI were recorded in 28.6% and 3.6% of first and 9.1% and 0.8% of subsequent vaginal births, respectively. Among first vaginal births, there was no association between episiotomy and OASI in univariable (RR 1.00 (0.86, 1.17)) or multivariable analysis (RR 0.91 (0.77, 1.09)). Among subsequent vaginal births, episiotomy was associated with increased odds of OASI in univariable (RR 2.21 (1.57, 3.13)) and multivariable analysis (RR 1.55 (1.08, 2.22)).ConclusionOur findings do not support the use of MLE for first births and suggest harm for subsequent births.

Introduction

Episiotomy rates vary by region, with up to 90% of patients undergoing a first birth having the procedure performed in some locations [1], despite the World Health Organization reporting a lack of evidence to support episiotomy during routine care [2]. International societies frequently discourage the use of routine episiotomy. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (ACOG) recommends ‘restricted use’ [3] while the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists details situations in which episiotomy is not found to decrease the Obstetric Anal Sphincter Injury (OASI) rate, such as in patients with a prior OASI and recommends clinicians consider the procedure for instrumental but not unassisted births [4]. New Zealand's Accident Compensation Corporation has released guidance neither recommending for or against episiotomy in the setting of unassisted birth, but detailing that ‘selective’ episiotomy may be indicated [5]. The recommendation by this body is also that if an episiotomy is to be performed, it should be mediolateral [5]. For this reason, if an episiotomy is performed in New Zealand, the procedure is always mediolateral, with trainees only learning this approach. The recommendation for ‘selective’ or ‘restricted use’ seems to indicate that a birth attendant can accurately predict which patients will benefit from the procedure, despite a lack of evidence that this is the case. To this end, we sought to perform a large, retrospective cohort study to determine the independent association between episiotomy and OASI in patients giving birth in one tertiary maternity unit which provides care to birthing people in Auckland, New Zealand's largest city. The population treated on the unit receive care from hospital‐employed and private midwives and obstetricians.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective cohort study included all vaginal births from 36 weeks' gestation, at a single tertiary maternity unit with approximately 7000 births per year between 2008 and 2021. A sample size calculation was not performed as there was no way to increase the number of cases within the cohort timeframe, though with the anticipated number of births it was expected that the study would be adequately powered with 14 years of data.

Cases were ascertained from the hospital maternity clinical database. Only unassisted vaginal births were included, hence all instrumental births were excluded from this study (these are reported separately in the companion paper). Cases were excluded if there were no data available to confirm occurrence of the primary outcome OASI. Births were also excluded from analysis if they were a subsequent birth, and the mode of delivery of prior births could not be determined from the database.

Manual chart checking by authors was carried out in cases with missing data regarding birthweight, demographics, onset of second stage or classification of perineal trauma. Episiotomy is routinely recorded in patient charts. In New Zealand, if an episiotomy is to be performed it is always mediolateral (with trainees taught to approximate an angle of 60 degrees laterally from the midline) as this is considered the standard of care with medial episiotomies not being taught or supported by governing bodies. Therefore, all episiotomies were assumed to be a mediolateral episiotomy (MLE). All cases recorded as third or fourth degree tears were manually reviewed to ensure correct classification of perineal tear and to further characterize third degree tears into subtypes 3a, 3b and 3c [6]. Where a grade of tear was not specified in the operative report as current correct categorization of 3a, 3b or 3c, the reviewer assigned a grade from the descriptors in the operative report. Buttonhole tears with an intact anal sphincter complex were removed from the OASI analysis. A sample of cases from the entire birthing cohort (prior to instrumental vaginal births being excluded) recorded as second degree (n = 50) and first degree/intact perineum (n = 50) were also manually reviewed from all births (including unassisted (n = 44 and 45)) to check accuracy of data recording.

Manual data checking was performed for the extremes of baby head circumference (for < 30 cm or > 40 cm) to confirm the accuracy of outliers.

Birthing people were categorized according to whether or not they had undergone a prior vaginal birth, the no prior vaginal birth group including nullipara and parity 1 patients who had one prior caesarean. Parous patients had undergone at least one prior vaginal birth. Patients who were parity ≥ 2 with previous caesareans were excluded from the analysis as it was not known whether they had a prior vaginal birth.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons of categorical variables were undertaken using chi‐squared tests and generalized linear models. Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐tests. Parity, previous caesarean, maternal height, body mass index (BMI) category, maternal age and ethnicity, parity (for prior vaginal birth), smoking status, augmentation of labour (defined as occurring at 4 or more centimetres of cervical dilation), induction of labour (defined as occurring at less than 4 cm of cervical dilation), length of second stage, health professional type (private obstetrician, community midwife or general practitioner, hospital midwife), socioeconomic deprivation quintile (measured using NZDepscore [7]), gestational age, baby head circumference, baby birthweight and use of epidural in labour were included for a priori reasons in logistic regression models using SAS software. Variables were removed in a backwards fashion if they were not statistically significantly associated with the outcome and were not obviously confounders of the association between episiotomy and OASI. Final models were fitted using generalized linear models in STATA to provide Relative risks with 95% confidence intervals, and linearity of continuous variables checked using generalized additive models. Statistical significance was assumed at p < 0.05, and missing data was treated as such and not imputed.

Ethical approval was granted by the Auckland Health Research Ethics Committee on 16/09/2021 (AH1140) and locality approval by the Te Toka Tumai Auckland Research Governance Group on 16/09/2021 (A+ 9256). No written consent was obtained from patients as there is no patient‐identifiable data included. A STROBE checklist was employed to ensure reporting criteria were adhered to for a retrospective cohort study.

Results

Between the years 2008 and 2021, inclusive, there were 48 026 unassisted vaginal births with known previous vaginal birth and OASI status from a cohort of 61 384 births (including both unassisted and instrumental vaginal births) (Table 1). Regarding unassisted births, there were 20 201 first vaginal births and 27 825 subsequent unassisted vaginal births (Tables 2 and 3) included in the analysis. Prior birth status was unknown in 1337 cases. There was an episiotomy performed in 28.6% of first and 9.1% of subsequent vaginal births (Tables 2 and 3). In patients with no prior vaginal birth having an unassisted birth, episiotomy was not associated with OASI in univariable or multivariable analysis (aRR = 0.91 95% CI (0.77, 1.09), Table 2). In patients having a first vaginal birth who did not undergo an episiotomy (n = 14 421), 24% (n = 3413) had an intact perineum and 18% (n = 2642) had a first‐degree laceration (Table 4). After adjusting for episiotomy and all confounders in the model, prior caesarean among people with no prior vaginal birth did not significantly elevate the risk of OASI.

In patients with a prior vaginal birth having an unassisted birth, episiotomy was associated with an increased risk of OASI in univariable and multivariable analysis (aRR = 1.55 95% CI = (1.08, 2.22), Table 3). In patients having a subsequent vaginal birth who did not undergo an episiotomy (n = 25 294), 46% (n = 11 560), had an intact perineum and 23% (n = 5698) had a first‐degree laceration (Table 4).

Overall, when an episiotomy was not performed, nulliparous people had a 42% chance of an intact perineum or a first degree laceration only, as did 68% of parous people (Table 4).

Discussion

Main Findings

We found no association between episiotomy and risk of OASI in patients having a first unassisted birth in our multivariable analysis and an associated increase in OASI for people having a subsequent vaginal birth. This is highly relevant to harm minimization in obstetric practice. Patients who have an episiotomy cannot, by definition, have an intact perineum or a first degree perineal laceration as an episiotomy is an incision into the perineal muscles. When assessing first vaginal births that occurred without an episiotomy, 42% of patients had less perineal damage (an intact perineum or a first degree tear) than would have occurred had an episiotomy been performed. This was true for 68% of patients having a subsequent vaginal birth. This amounted to 23 313 patients who avoided an unnecessary episiotomy (and thus 100% chance of a second degree incision into the pelvic floor) without any evidence that these episiotomies would have prevented OASI in nulliparous patients and evidence of an increased OASI rate in parous patients. This consideration is very important as many consider the intentional injury caused by an episiotomy as a ‘trade off’ against the risk of OASI. This belief is clearly prominent in New Zealand considering the comparatively high rates of episiotomy on our unit; 28.6% in unassisted first vaginal births and 9.1% in unassisted subsequent vaginal births.

The increased rate of OASI in parous patients undergoing episiotomy in unassisted births was a somewhat unexpected finding. One candidate mechanism is that the episiotomy incision is the initiation of perineal damage that then extends posteriorly towards the anal sphincter. Another potential mechanism is the performance of a mediolateral episiotomy that is not ‘lateral enough’ with inadvertent iatrogenic sphincter injury.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had several strengths, including the size of the dataset, and hence substantial contribution to the existing data. We had a single reviewer access every chart where an OASI occurred. This makes us confident that we are correctly reporting on the type of perineal laceration and whether or not an episiotomy was performed. The same reviewer also accessed a random sample of 89 charts of patients who did not experience OASI to confirm the accuracy of the data. Likewise, data checks via direct chart review in relation to extremes of head circumference (for < 30 cm or > 40 cm) were performed to confirm the accuracy of outliers.

The study had several limitations. The most obvious is that we did not perform direct chart review for every patient included in the cohort. We did not believe that it was feasible for individual review of the 61 384 charts of all people identified to have birthed vaginally, or for the group studied here; the 48 026 people with OASI and parity data who had unassisted births. For this reason, we chose to perform chart review for every noted OASI injury and a random sample of patients without an OASI injury, along with a random sample of patients with exposure to episiotomy.

Our study adjusted for confounding factors with multivariable analysis, highly relevant here as the baseline characteristics differed between those who did and did not undergo a mediolateral episiotomy. Irrespective, this was a retrospective study with inherent limitations of such a study including the inability to control for all potential risk factors for an OASI. Likewise, there was an inability to control for the reasons that a mediolateral episiotomy was performed or not performed in individual cases. While we question whether a birth attendant can predict which patients will benefit from a procedure (meaning the performance of ‘selective episiotomy’), it is possible that there are physical perineal factors that are predictive for OASI which cannot be investigated or controlled for retrospectively.

Our study only assessed immediate outcomes from birth, specifically, OASI. While this is an important outcome which is correlated with long term morbidities (chronic perineal pain, prolapse and incontinence) [8, 9], a better outcome may be function, rather than injury. Follow up studies will be important, considering the existing evidence that episiotomy in patients undergoing unassisted birth increases the rate of anal incontinence [9, 10].

Interpretation

Our findings are consistent with those from a French national database analysis of 623 003 people undergoing a vaginal birth (both nulliparous and parous), indicating that for those having an unassisted birth, episiotomy did not provide a benefit in preventing OASI [8]. Moreover, episiotomy was associated with a higher chance of OASI in patients having an unassisted birth whether or not they had given birth before [8]. Concerningly, the French data pertaining to mediolateral episiotomy also identified that the procedure in patients without risk factors was associated with anal incontinence [9]. Multiple other studies have indicated that episiotomy does not modify the risk of OASI in nulliparous patients undergoing unassisted births [11, 12] or may actually increase the risk [13] (a concerning outcome, but one only identified for parous patients in our study).

The above research findings conflict with that of an Italian study which reported a lower incidence of OASI in unassisted births with mediolateral episiotomy. However, it should be noted that there were several dissimilarities between this study and ours, including an exceptionally low OASI rate of 0.3% in unassisted vaginal births [14].

On balance, we would suggest that there is no benefit to episiotomy in the setting of a first unassisted birth and potential harm when the procedure is performed in people with a prior vaginal birth. ‘Selective’ use of episiotomy is variously defined and seems to be a term utilized to justify the procedure based on provider belief or anecdotal experience. For this reason, we have made efforts to control for factors we thought likely to be provider justifications for the procedure in the New Zealand population, resulting in no effect in relation to the chance of OASI in nulliparas. Our finding, coupled with the evidence that mediolateral episiotomy is associated with more post‐birth perineal pain, more dyspareunia, and higher rates of both urinary and faecal incontinence, should give providers pause when they consider performing the procedure during the process of unassisted vaginal birth [9, 10, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Apart from emergency situations where episiotomy might be used to expedite vaginal birth, clear discussion as to why the provider wants to perform an episiotomy should occur as it is not a necessary procedure for most births. Perineal protection techniques and controlled vaginal birth appear to be important factors that decrease the risk of OASI and of episiotomy itself [2, 19].

Though our data indicates that MLE does not prevent OASI in nulliparas and is associated with an increased risk of OASI in parous people, we have not identified additional strategies (apart from omission of MLE in unassisted births) to decrease risk. This is an area for further study. There is an indication from our data that some populations are at a higher risk than others and causal mechanisms (e.g., health literacy, nutritional status and its effect on tissue integrity) need to be investigated to decrease rates in the highest risk populations. Our group is currently performing work assessing iron deficiency and its association with OASI.

In summary, we identified no association between episiotomy and OASI for patients having their first unassisted vaginal birth and an increase in the occurence of OASI for patients having an episiotomy during a subsequent unassisted vaginal birth. Our findings do not support the use of episiotomy for unassisted vaginal births.
