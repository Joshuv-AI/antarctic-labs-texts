Recent advances in reprogramming lactate metabolism in polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Jing Xu, Wei-hong Chen, Ming-na Huang, Ling-tao Zheng, Si-Yan Huang, Shu Lin, Qi-rong Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent and complex endocrine disorder affecting 4–20% of women of reproductive age worldwide. It is characterized by dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, weight gain, and increased risk of cardiovascular disease. An imbalance in the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is an important pathophysiological basis of PCOS. Lactate, a glycolytic product and an important energy source, also regulates gene transcription through histone lactylation, a novel post-translational modification. Emerging evidence highlights the crucial role of lactate and lactylation in cancer, inflammation, and metabolic reprogramming, although the exact mechanisms remain unclear. Based on the manifestations of PCOS, we hypothesized a potential link between lactate levels, lactylation, and PCOS. This review explores the role of lactate in PCOS pathophysiology, examines the regulatory effect of metabolic reprogramming on various pathophysiological scenarios, and discusses the therapeutic potential of targeting lactate metabolism in PCOS management.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a major endocrine disorder affecting women of reproductive age. Deswal et al. reported an average PCOS prevalence of 21.27% in 2020, affecting 4 − 20% of women of reproductive age worldwide [1]. Despite its high prevalence, PCOS remains unrecognized as a serious global health issue. Amenorrhea, hirsutism, obesity, and infertility were categorized by Stein and Leventhal in 1935 as the main disorders constituting the Stein-Leventhal syndrome. PCOS is characterized by hyperandrogenism, sustained anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology. Its main clinical manifestations are irregular menstrual cycles, infertility, hirsutism, acne, and obesity [2]. The Rotterdam criteria acknowledge the heterogeneity of PCOS and permit the diagnosis of different phenotypes of the syndrome, even in women without obvious hyperandrogenism or chronic anovulation. These phenotypes include: (A) hyperandrogenism, chronic anovulation, and polycystic ovaries; (B) hyperandrogenism and chronic anovulation; (C) hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovaries; and (D) chronic anovulation and polycystic ovaries without clinical or biochemical evidence of hyperandrogenism[3]. The pathophysiology of PCOS primarily involves hormonal imbalance, chronic low-grade inflammation, hyperandrogenism, and insulin resistance, which impair oocyte production and increase the risk of complications such as endometrial cancer (EC), cardiovascular disease (CVD), gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, and type II diabetes [4–6].

An imbalance in the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis is an essential pathophysiological basis of PCOS, suggesting that central regulation, particularly the aberrant activation of hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) neurons, critically influences PCOS development. Increased GnRH pulse frequency leads to elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) secretion, which may result in ovarian dysfunction and abnormal sex steroid synthesis. Peripheral sex steroids modulate GnRH neuron activity through disrupted feedback in PCOS, creating a vicious circle. Notably, hypothalamic neuropeptide Y (NPY) has been implicated in both reproductive axis modulation and metabolic regulation through direct GnRH neuron interactions and appetite control mechanisms [7, 8].

Emerging evidence highlights metabolic reprogramming as a key modulator of cellular plasticity through epigenetic modifications [9, 10]. Particularly, lactate - formerly regarded as a glycolytic byproduct - has been recognized as a signaling molecule capable of regulating gene expression via histone lactylation [11]. Studies associate lactate with various diseases, including cancer, metabolic disorders, and inflammation [12, 13]. Additionally, central lactate infusion can suppress energy intake by reducing NPY expression [14]. These findings suggest potential intersections between lactate metabolism and PCOS pathophysiology that remain underexplored. Sun et al. [15] verified the presence of disturbed glucose metabolism in patients with PCOS, resulting in significant elevation of lactate and heterogeneity of pathogenesis. Zhao et al. [16] analyzed plasma metabolites of four phenotypes in 217 patients with PCOS. Compared to controls, patients with PCOS showed disrupted carbohydrate metabolism, with elevated lactate levels in all phenotypes except C phenotype. Lactate was closely linked to insulin resistance but was not affected by hyperandrogenism. There was no significant correlation between insulin resistance and PCOS phenotypes [17]. These findings suggest that differences in lactate metabolism between PCOS phenotypes warrant further investigation. In contrast to the trend of elevated plasma lactate levels, patients with PCOS had significantly lower lactate concentrations in hair follicular fluid [18]. This suggests that lactate metabolism may be regulated between different body fluids in a tissue-specific manner, further emphasizing the complexity and heterogeneity of PCOS at the metabolic level.

Despite these advances, the specific mechanisms through which lactate metabolism contributes to PCOS development have not been systematically investigated. This review aims to (1) synthesize current evidence on lactate’s regulatory roles in PCOS-associated metabolic and reproductive dysfunctions, and (2) evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting lactate metabolic reprogramming for PCOS management. By bridging existing knowledge gaps, this analysis seeks to provide novel insights for developing mechanism-based interventions to improve clinical outcomes in PCOS patients.

Involvement of lactate in the pathophysiology of PCOS

Insulin resistance (IR)

IR and PCOS

Studies have shown IR is present in approximately 35 − 80% of patients with PCOS [19]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the effects of IR remain unclear. Therefore, exploring the role of IR in the pathogenesis of PCOS is critical for its prevention and treatment.

IR and hyperinsulinemia are common symptoms of PCOS. Elevated insulin levels can disrupt ovarian function and sinus follicle development by affecting the HPO axis [20]. Hyperinsulinemia triggers the production of large amounts of androgens by follicular membrane cells [21]. Wallace et al. [22] found that high insulin levels decrease hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin levels and increase free testosterone concentrations. Additionally, insulin’s effect on adipose tissue and inflammation contributes to PCOS pathogenesis. Insulin promotes adipogenesis and inhibits adipose degradation, leading to fat accumulation [23, 24]. These conditions are crucial in PCOS development and are associated with several metabolic and reproductive abnormalities.

IR and lactate

Numerous studies indicate a connection between lactate and IR; lactate can induce IR [25, 26]. Elevated plasma lactate levels inhibit glycolysis and insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, leading to IR (Fig. 1). Lactate-induced IR is associated with impaired insulin signaling pathway and reduced insulin-stimulated glucose transport in skeletal muscle [27] (Fig. 2.). In 1992, DiGirola et al. [28] found a negative correlation between lactate levels and insulin sensitivity, hypothesizing that fasting lactate levels may cause IR. This inference was validated by Santos et al. [29] and Chondronikola et al. [30], who concluded that fasting lactate concentration could be a biomarker for reduced insulin sensitivity.

Lactate also promotes insulin secretion and hyperinsulinemia. Under insulin-resistant conditions, insulin receptor substrate (IRS)-1 serine 636 is hyperphosphorylated [28]. Maschari et al. [31] found that lactate increased IRS-1 serine 636 phosphorylation. Furthermore, they observed that insulin sensitivity was negatively correlated with muscle lactylation levels, and lactylation was positively correlated with lactate levels. Lactylation, a novel lactate-induced histone modification, suggests insulin sensitivity can be improved by altering muscle lactate levels. Lactate also alleviates IR despite promoting it through multiple pathways. Cai et al. [32] found that moderate l-lactate levels could ameliorate IR by inhibiting pro-inflammatory M1 polarization of adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) through the activation of the GPR132-protein kinase A(PKA)-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)α1 signaling pathway. Impaired cellular autophagy has been observed in both IR and PCOS [33]. Pirani et al. [34] observed that high-intensity interval training activates the ERK/P90SRK pathway and inhibits the mTOR/70pS6K pathway, promoting cellular autophagy in a lactate-dependent manner. This finding suggests that lactate accumulation corrects impaired cellular autophagy, reducing IR and lowering blood glucose levels. Lactate in adipose tissue links obesity with IR [35]. Additionally, Liang et al. [36] found that high insulin levels lead to abnormal glycolysis in granulosa cells, resulting in decreased lactate levels and reduced cell viability. Liu et al. [37] further confirmed that the increased production of lactate within granulosa cells plays a significant role in improving follicular development disorders associated with PCOS. Therefore, lactate metabolism and its role in granulosa cells may provide new directions for the treatment of PCOS, suggesting that modulating lactate levels could enhance follicular development in patients with PCOS.

Obesity

Obesity and PCOS

PCOS affects women with and without obesity, with current estimates suggesting that approximately 30% of patients with PCOS have obesity. Previous studies have shown that various degrees of obesity are significantly associated with PCOS [38]. Clinical symptoms, such as irregular menstruation and infertility, can be reduced by moderate weight loss (starting at 2 − 5%) [39].

Weight gain and obesity may contribute to IR by affecting adipokine release [40–47] (Table 1). As shown in the table, adipokines can directly or indirectly influence IR by affecting the expression of IR-related genes, insulin signaling pathways, and the release of inflammatory factors. Dresner et al. [48] observed that increased plasma-free fatty acid concentration could decrease phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase activity associated with IRS-1, reducing insulin’s ability to transport glucose and leading to IR. PCOS exacerbates obesity’s deleterious effects on IR, as reported in a meta-analysis by Samira et al. [49]. In rodent models, diet-induced obesity (DIO) increases IR levels in metabolic tissues (e.g., adipose, liver, and muscle) and the reproductive axis [50], decreasing fertility and suggesting that obesity-related metabolic dysfunction may lead to PCOS [51–54]. Obesity induces IR and hyperinsulinemia, which can promote adipogenesis and inhibit lipolysis in adipocytes, suggesting bidirectional effects between the two [55, 56]. The mechanism by which obesity causes PCOS may be genetically related in addition to its effects on metabolism. Genome-wide association studies first revealed that fat mass and obesity genes (FTO) are positively associated with human obesity [57]. A meta-analysis by Liu et al. [58] demonstrated that the rs9939609 A/T polymorphism of FTO correlates with PCOS development. Obesity itself can trigger PCOS, with obesity and insulin acting in a complementary and interconnected manner in its pathogenesis. In addition to IR, obesity contributes to PCOS progression by inducing hyperandrogenemia and infertility [59].

Obesity and lactate

Plasma lactate concentration is higher in individuals with obesity than in those of normal weight, with adipose tissue being a significant source [61]. In obese animals, adipocytes appear hypertrophied and are susceptible to hypoxia, which stimulates glucose uptake and increases lactate production in adipose tissue [62]. Insulin has been found to convert most of the glucose to lactate rather than fatty acids in brown adipose tissue isolated from the interscapular region of 6-week-old male mice. Lactate is an important substrate in adipose tissue production, increasing the rate of adipogenesis compared to that by glucose alone [63]. Lactate also inhibits adipose tissue catabolism. The GPR81 receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor in adipose tissue, binds to Gi-type G proteins to mediate lactate’s antilipolytic effects [64]. Two signaling pathways are involved in this process. One is the cAMP-PKA signaling pathway, where GPR81 binds to Gi proteins, activating Gi signaling, which inhibits adenylyl cyclase activity and decreases cAMP levels and PKA activity. This decreases the activity of hormone-sensitive lipase and adipose triglyceride lipase, inhibiting lipid degradation [65]. The other pathway is the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) signaling pathway, which is abundant in brown adipose tissue and favors lipid storage. GPR81 is a direct target of PPARγ, and studies show a conserved PPARγ binding site near the GPR81 gene promoter. In mature adipocytes, GPR81 receptor expression significantly increases compared to that in preadipocytes. In mature 3T3-L1 adipocytes, PPARγ knockdown significantly reduces GPR81 protein expression, leading to increased lipolysis in differentiated adipocytes [66]. This finding suggests that enhancing PPARγexpression boosts lactate’s anti-lipolytic effect by increasing GPR81 receptor levels.

A large body of evidence suggests that adipose tissue inflammation is the primary cause of obesity-induced IR [67–69]. Lactate is crucial in mediating obesity-induced inflammation and IR. Lin et al. found that deletion of the Slc16a1 receptor elevated intracellular lactate levels in mice fed a high-fat diet. The Slc16a1 gene encodes monocarboxylic acid transporter 1(MCT1), the major lactate transporter protein in adipose tissue. In contrast to lactate’s previously described inhibition of adipocyte degradation, the accumulation of large amounts of lactate in cells triggered by SLc16a1 deletion (possibly causing a decrease in the cellular pH) can lead to adipocyte degradation and cytokine release. Pro-inflammatory cytokines released into circulation by locally recruited macrophages amplify inflammation and lead to IR [35].

Chronic inflammation

Chronic inflammation and PCOS

A case-control study has suggested that PCOS is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation [70]. This condition is characterized by mildly elevated levels of inflammatory markers, which places PCOS within this category [71]. C-reactive protein (CRP) is a classic marker of low-grade inflammation, and its levels correlate positively with IR and obesity in patients with PCOS [72, 73]. Other markers, such as IL-18, IL-17, and tumor necrosis factor-α, are also significantly increased in PCOS [74, 75]. In addition to these markers, leukocytes (especially lymphocytes and monocytes) and their classical markers are remarkably increased in patients with PCOS and are strongly associated with IR [76]. Ruan et al. [77] measured and compared white blood cell counts, CRP, sex hormone levels, and fasting insulin levels in 74 patients with PCOS and 51 healthy women of similar age and body mass index (BMI). Their analysis showed significantly higher serum CRP levels and leukocyte counts in patients with PCOS than in healthy women. Serum CRP levels positively correlated with BMI and negatively correlated with insulin sensitivity. Similarly, Kelly et al. [66] found a positive correlation between CRP concentration, degree of obesity, and level of IR. Individuals with obesity have elevated serum inflammatory cell levels [78] and altered lymphocyte function [79]. Obesity is a classic chronic mild inflammatory state characterized by an imbalance of pro- and anti-inflammatory adipocytokines expression in adipose tissues [80]. This imbalance disrupts insulin signaling, leading to IR development [81]. Most markers indicating mild inflammation in PCOS are associated with IR and obesity. Moreover, abnormal inflammatory regulation affects ovarian function, oocyte quality, and the ability of the uterus to tolerate pregnancy. Therefore, infertility is associated with inflammation [82].

Chronic inflammation and lactate

In inflammatory diseases, lactate triggers intracellular signals that promote chronic inflammation [13]. It contributes to chronic inflammation by driving T-cell immune dysfunction and increasing inflammatory cytokine production [83]. Lactic acid inhibits T-cell migration, causing a loss of lysogeny and elevated IL-17 levels [12]. High lactate levels also increase signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) phosphorylation and IL-17 expression by inducing ectopic translocation of PKM2, a pyruvate kinase isoform implicated in immunity and tumors, to the nucleus of CD4+ T-cells [13, 84]. Additionally, PKM2 acts as a substrate for lactylation, which correlates with lactate levels. Wang et al. [85] found that chronic lactate exposure in macrophages activates PKM2, inhibits glycolysis, and converts it into a repair phenotype through the lactylation of K62 on PKM2. This process prevents the onset of inflammatory metabolism in pro-inflammatory macrophages, highlighting the need for further investigation into the effects of lactate on PKM2 expression.

Furthermore, lactate produced by adipocytes plays a key role in the pro-inflammatory milieu, inducing a pro-inflammatory response by facilitating intercellular communication between adipocytes and ATMs [86]. Beyond stimulating the release of inflammatory cytokines, lactate binds to GPR81, thereby reducing inflammation by inhibiting cytokine maturation and secretion through activation of the intracellular adapter protein arrestin beta 2 and attenuating NF-kB activity [87, 88]. These findings suggest that lactate plays an important role in the association between obesity, IR, and chronic inflammation. However, the exact mechanisms underlying the role of lactate in this process remain unclear. Therefore, further studies on lactate levels through additional experiments are necessary to explore its potential in the prevention, detection, and treatment of PCOS by influencing lactate levels.

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)

AMH and PCOS

Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) is mainly secreted by granulosa cells in ovarian sinusoids and presinusoidal follicles, reflecting the reserve and developmental status of small follicles [89]. AMH regulates follicular response to follicle- stimulating hormone (FSH), thereby regulating folliculogenesis and ensuring controlled progression of follicular development [90]. AMH is significantly elevated in PCOS patients and promotes anovulation by inhibiting FSH sensitivity and follicular maturation [91, 92]. Moreover, AMH concentration was positively correlated with oxidative stress levels in follicular fluid. This suggests that AMH may influence ovulation by affecting the follicular microenvironment [93, 94].

Hyperandrogenemia, insulin resistance, and obesity are significant characteristics of PCOS that influence the process of folliculogenesis [49]. In individuals diagnosed with PCOS, elevated serum AMH concentrations are positively correlated with androgen levels (including serum testosterone and androstenedione) [95]. Moreover, AMH levels are also positively associated with fasting insulin and various indices of β-cell function and insulin sensitivity, with this correlation exhibiting an age-dependent pattern [96]. This relationship appears to be influenced by the effect of reactive hyperinsulinemia on androgen secretion by follicular membrane cells. In contrast, obesity is associated with reduced AMH production, which may be due to the inhibitory effect of leptin (a hormone associated with obesity) on the expression of AMH and its receptor genes in granulosa cells [97]. This observation highlights the complex relationship between hormonal regulation and metabolic factors in the context of PCOS.

AMH and lactate

In contrast to the elevated plasma lactate levels observed in patients with PCOS, lactate levels in follicular fluid were decreased. Zhang et al. [98] conducted a study and found that lactate concentrations in follicular fluid were significantly reduced in patients with PCOS compared to healthy controls. This finding was accompanied by similar decreases in pyruvate and alanine levels, suggesting that glycolytic flux may be impaired or that lactate clearance and utilization within the follicle have increased. These observed changes may indicate redox imbalance within the follicular fluid microenvironment. The production of lactate is associated with the conversion of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) to NAD⁺, and a decrease in lactate concentration implies an accumulation of NADH. This accumulation may exacerbate oxidative stress in the follicular fluid [99], thereby creating conditions that enhance AMH synthesis.

Research findings indicate a noteworthy negative correlation between AMH levels and lactate concentrations in the follicular fluid of individuals with PCOS [100]. This relationship may be influenced by the stage of follicular development. In patients diagnosed with PCOS, the presence of small follicles in the ovaries often increases, leading to elevated AMH levels. However, these smaller follicles exhibit lower glycolytic activity, resulting in decreased lactate production. The reduction in lactate (an important metabolic product for oocyte maturation in the follicular microenvironment) may reflect developmental arrest, a characteristic of interrupted follicular development [101]. The high expression of AMH further exacerbates this situation. Furthermore, the relationship between lactate metabolism and AMH forms a complex pathological network in PCOS, significantly impacting reproductive function. First, ovulation disorders are the primary cause of infertility in PCOS patients. Disruptions in lactate metabolism, combined with elevated AMH levels, may disrupt normal follicle development and hinder ovulation, posing significant challenges to conception. Second, the combined effects of lactate-induced oxidative stress and elevated AMH levels may lead to a decline in oocyte quality. This reduction in oocyte quality further decreases the likelihood of successful conception in PCOS patients. Finally, the interaction between lactate metabolism and AMH may exacerbate hormonal imbalances, including hyperandrogenism and estrogen deficiency, thereby further impairing reproductive function.

Reproduction

Lactate is a key metabolite produced through glycolysis in granulosa cells and oocytes and is an essential energy metabolite during oocyte maturation [102]. In contrast to glucose, early embryos show a preference for lactate as the primary energy source and substrate of NAD + regeneration that supports mitochondrial function and cell viability. Additionally, lactate modulates intracellular pH and the overall metabolic state, thereby influencing blastocyst formation rates and developmental potential. During the pre-implantation stage, the appropriate lactate concentration help to replicate the in vivo physiological environment, thereby enhancing implantation success [103]. These findings suggest that lactate metabolism may be used as a non-invasive indicator for assessing embryo quality in assisted reproductive technology, thus improving the precision and success of embryo selection and transfer.

Cao et al. [104]demonstrated a significant reduction in the expression of key glycolytic enzymes in granulosa cells associated with PCOS. This reduction was accompanied by a decrease in lactate level, a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential and an accumulation of reactive oxygen species. The resulting metabolic imbalance triggered an increase in granulosa cell apoptosis, along with a significant decrease in oocyte maturation and embryo quality. These findings suggest that impaired lactate metabolism may indirectly diminish embryonic developmental competence by disrupting cellular energy homeostasis and exacerbating oxidative stress.

Lactate significantly influences epigenetic regulation through histone lactylation, thereby impacting gene expression in granulosa cells. Research conducted by Wu et al. [105] demonstrated that the luteinization of granulosa cells was correlated with an increase in lactate production and enhanced lactylation. Notably, inhibition of lactate synthesis or pharmacological blockade of lactylation resulted in significant impairment of hCG-induced luteinization process in vivo, suggesting a functional interdependence between metabolic and epigenetic interactions. In patients with PCOS, lactate levels in follicular fluid are often reduced. This reduction may impair normal granulosa cell luteinization by disrupting lactate/emulsification-related mechanisms. Granulosa cell luteinization regulates the secretion of key hormones such as progesterone and estrogen, and these hormonal changes are essential for establishing a uterine environment that supports embryo implantation and early pregnancy [106]. The interaction between metabolic and epigenetic disorders significantly contributes to impaired hair follicle development, reduced embryo quality, and reduced in vitro fertilization success in patients diagnosed with PCOS. This highlights an important link between lactate metabolism, lactylation and specific reproductive outcomes in PCOS, emphasizing the potential role of metabolic interventions in enhancing fertility management.

Lactate involvement in metabolic regulation via the central nervous system

Numerous studies have found that NPY neurons in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus are among the most abundant peptides in the nervous system and form a powerful group of orexigenic neurons in the brain. NPY stimulates appetite and is associated with energy expenditure, metabolism, and reproduction [107–109].

NPY and PCOS

GnRH

In addition to its unique metabolic profile, PCOS has a distinctive neurosecretory feature, such as sustained rapid LH pulsation [110]. GnRH neurons, located at the apex of the HPO axis, are responsible for regulating reproductive functions. GnRH peptide pulses are secreted by long projections from these neurons towards the median eminence into the pituitary hilar system, where they stimulate the release of gonadotropins (e.g., LH and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)) [111, 112]. The different pulse frequencies partly determine which gonadotropins are initially synthesized and secreted. Fast GnRH pulses facilitate LH release, while slower GnRH pulses promote FSH release [113, 114]. Both hormones are associated with ovarian androgen secretion and follicular development. Silva et al. [115] demonstrated that GnRH hyperactivity, that is, the secretion of abnormally high levels of LH, is a major factor contributing to reproductive and hormonal disturbances in PCOS. Steroid hormones have a negative feedback effect on the HPO axis and inhibit the activity of GnRH neurons [116], but this homeostatic feedback mechanism is impaired in PCOS.

A large body of literature suggests that NPY acts directly on GnRH neurons and influences their metabolism and reproductive functions. NPY controls the release of GnRH prior to ovulation, which stimulates the LH surge during ovulation. Advis et al. [117] reported that the interaction between NPY and GnRH affects LH release prior to ovulation. Evidence suggests that NPY affects the binding of GnRH to anterior pituitary receptors and influences the response of anterior pituitary cells to GnRH, thereby increasing LH release [118]. Similarly, Evans et al. supported the notion that NPY can directly regulate LH release at the pituitary level [119]. NPY both stimulates and inhibits GnRH neuronal activity. Klenke et al. [120] found that NPY directly inhibits the activity of early-developing GnRH neurons through the G protein-coupled Y1 receptor. Manfredi-Lozano et al. [121] reported that NPY inhibits GnRH neuronal activity and reduces LH secretion in the presence of metabolic deficits (Fig. 3. ).

Obesity

NPY neurons are the most fundamental group of neurons and are closely associated with eating behavior, regulation of energy homeostasis, and lipid storage [122]. Chronically elevated NPY-ergic activity is associated with obesity, a response to peripheral energy excess signals (e.g., leptin) [123]. Tiesjema et al. [124] demonstrated that NPY overexpression in the paraventricular nucleus leads to obesity by stimulating food intake before reaching a certain body weight. NPY stimulates appetite through different NPY G protein-coupled receptors [125]. For example, Shi et al. [126] found that NPYY1 and NPYY5 double-knockout rats exhibited increased food intake, obesity, and weight gain. Additionally, Tamura et al. [127] demonstrated that oral administration of an NPYY5 antagonist suppressed food intake in DIO mice and led to long-term weight loss. Baranowska et al., in a case-control analysis, found that NPY levels were increased in patients with PCOS, with or without obesity. However, in obese women without PCOS, NPY levels were positively correlated with body weight [128].

IR and hyperinsulinemia

Schmitz et al. [129] showed that NPY inhibited insulin secretion by mediating its action through to Gi/o protein-coupled receptors. Similarly, Moltz et al. [130] demonstrated that while NPY directly inhibits insulin release, central NPY indirectly promotes elevated plasma insulin levels. Sainsbury et al. [131] found that chronic injection of NPY into the ventricles of normal rats resulted in hyperinsulinemia, muscle IR, and insulin hyperresponsiveness in white adipose tissue. Additionally, central NPY impaired insulin’s inhibitory effect on endogenous glucose production (EGP). Hoek et al. [132] found that NPY injection into the lateral ventricle activated sympathetic output from the liver and significantly inhibited the inhibitory effect of insulin on EGP, leading to IR development in a sham-denervated rat model. In addition to the direct effects of central NPY on insulin secretion and sensitivity, Ruud et al. [133] reported that acute regulation of feeding and IR by agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons was dependent on NPY. Notably, the effect of NPY on the insulin level is not unidirectional. Studies have shown that chronic injection of NPY into rat ventricles stimulates the release of insulin from central NPY, whereas negative insulin feedback decreases central NPY levels [134]. This regulation may involve insulin-mediated inhibition of NPY expression in the arcuate nucleus via the GABAergic system [135].

NPY and lactate

The central effects of lactate have been shown to suppress appetite [136]. Monocarboxylic acid transporters (MCTs) transport lactate and are widely found in anorexic neurons. Lactate is transported into cells with the assistance of MCTs. Once inside the cell, lactate activates the STAT3 and protein kinase B (PKB) and rapidly inactivates AMPK. These molecular mechanisms are implicated in the regulation of energy intake. Following lactate treatment, NPY/AgRP mRNA expression was reduced, leading to appetite suppression [137]. Ou et al. [138] demonstrated that elevated lactate levels activated PKB and STAT3 signaling in hypothalamic neurons, leading to the suppression of AgRP/NPY neuronal secretion. Additionally, Cha et al. [14] reported that central lactate significantly reduces NPY expression by inhibiting the hypothalamic AMP kinase/malonyl coenzyme A signaling pathway. These findings reveal a potential link between lactate and neuroendocrine function. In PCOS, IR and alterations in energy balance are common, and understanding how lactate influences the levels of NPY and AgRP could provide new insights into individual appetite changes and weight management studies. Elevated circulating ghrelin levels promote hypothalamic NPY and AgRP expression to stimulate appetite [139]. However, lactate inhibits ghrelin entry into the circulation. This interaction between lactate and ghrelin suggests that lactate may serve as a key regulator of energy intake in the context of PCOS. Elevated levels of circulating ghrelin may be modulated by lactate concentrations, adding complexity to the neuroendocrine regulation of PCOS.

Overall, this finding underscores the significance of lactate in the neuroendocrine regulation of appetite, which may be related to the pathophysiology of PCOS. Further studies into these mechanisms could enhance our understanding of the interactions between lactate and neuroendocrine signals, potentially leading to more effective interventions for managing PCOS and its associated symptoms.

Involvement of lactylation in long-term complications of PCOS

As previously mentioned, lactate plays an essential role in the pathophysiology of several diseases and is no longer considered merely a metabolic waste product. Additionally, lactate acts as a donor for the lactylation of histone lysine residues, which then regulates gene transcription or protein function. Recently, lactylation has been shown to play a role in various diseases, including cancer [140], CVDs, and IR [31]. While most current studies related to PCOS focus on the pathophysiology and treatment of the existing symptoms, the long-term risks associated with PCOS, such as CVDs, EC, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, are also important. In this section, we describe the roles of lactate and lactylation in PCOS complications.

Lactylation in CVD

PCOS is linked to several endocrine and metabolic disorders, such as obesity, IR, and metabolic syndrome, which contribute to a higher risk of CVD [141]. Numerous studies have suggested that PCOS is associated with an increased risk of CVD, including hypertension [142] and acute myocardial infarction (MI) [143]. The most common dyslipidemias in patients with PCOS include elevated levels of triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and very-low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, as well as lowered levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [144]. A retrospective cohort analysis in the UK showed that the incidence of MI and angina pectoris was significantly higher in patients with PCOS than in women of the same age, both in the perimenopausal and postmenopausal periods [143].

Lactate levels are strongly linked to CVD, and evidence shows that lactate is associated with various pathological processes [145]. Macrophages are the main immune cell population in CVD and play a crucial role in disease development [146]. Lactate promotes macrophage polarization to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype through different cell signaling pathways [147] (Table 2). Furthermore, lactylation modifications in macrophages promote their transition to M2 anti-inflammatory macrophages, facilitating tissue repair [148]. Wang et al. [149] reported that histone lactylation creates a favorable environment for tissue repair by activating early remote repair genes in monocytes following MI. However, lactate levels negatively affected MI. Additionally, Fan et al. [150] found that lactate upregulates cardiac endothelial-mesenchymal transition after MI by inducing Snail1 lactylation, increasing myocardial fibrosis, and exacerbating cardiac dysfunction.

Conversely, Zhang et al. [159] found that elevated lactate concentrations promoted lactylation of α-MHC K1897, which improved α-MHC-Titin interactions and alleviated heart failure. In addition to its role in cardiac pathology, lactate influences the development of atherosclerosis. LDL and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) are known to contribute to atherosclerosis, and the presence of lactate further exacerbates this process. Lactate promotes LDL aggregation and modification and induces a shift from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype in VSMCs, thereby increasing the risk of atherosclerosis [148]. Furthermore, lactate affects endothelial cells and influences the progression of atherosclerosis. Wang et al. [149] demonstrated that both exercise and exogenous lactate inhibit endothelial cell epithelial regulatory protein expression via MeCP2 lysine lactylation. This lactylation modification altered the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway and reduced the release of inflammatory factors, thereby inhibiting the progression of atherosclerosis. Overall, growing evidence indicates that lactate plays a vital role in the development and prognosis of CVD. Its effects on macrophages, cardiac function, atherosclerosis, and endothelial cells highlight its importance in pathological processes and provide potential targets for therapeutic interventions. However, the dual role of lactate underscores its complex involvement in cardiovascular pathophysiology, especially in patients with PCOS, suggesting that more in-depth studies are necessary to elucidate its specific mechanisms.

Lactylation in EC

High expression of insulin-like growth factor 1, insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1, and phosphatase and tensin homolog in the endometrium of patients with PCOS, along with lactylation of EC, are involved in the insulin signaling pathway, which explains the increased risk of EC in women with PCOS [160]. According to a recent meta-analysis, the risk of EC is elevated in patients with PCOS across all age groups, particularly in postmenopausal patients with PCOS, although the overall risk of ovarian and breast cancers is not significantly increased [5]. Metabolic reprogramming in cancer is crucial for the tumor microenvironment (TME), and lactate metabolites have a significant impact on cancer development and immunosuppression in the TME [161]. Lactate is considered a biomarker of malignancy, most classically exemplified by the Waborg effect, where tumor cells undergo intense glycolysis under aerobic conditions to produce and secrete large amounts of lactate [162]. Shi et al. [163] demonstrated that TIMM50, a gene related to lactate metabolism, promotes EC cell proliferation, migration, and lactate metabolism. Similarly, Latif et al. [132] found that MCTs, acting as lactate transporters, are expressed in the cytoplasm and plasma membrane of EC cells, and high expression of MCT1 may be an important marker of poor EC prognosis. Additionally, anterior gradient 2, a protein regulating cancer progression, was reported by Gong et al. [164] to induce lactate production at high levels, promoting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of EC cells. Overall, these findings suggest that lactate and lactylation may be key factors in the development of EC. However, the exact role of lactylation modifications in the progression of EC remains unclear, necessitating further studies to elucidate its potential mechanisms.

Conclusion and prospects

PCOS is established as a complex endocrine disorder driven by HPO axis dysregulation, obesity, IR, and chronic inflammation. Lactate has been identified as a central mediator connecting these pathological processes, functioning through dual mechanisms of metabolic regulation (via GPR81 and MCT receptors) and epigenetic modulation (via lactylation). The therapeutic potential of targeting lactate metabolism is underscored by its ability to disrupt the interplay between IR, adipogenesis, and inflammatory cascades. Notably, lactate’s role as a biomarker for insulin sensitivity warrants validation in PCOS-specific cohorts to enable risk stratification and precision interventions. Three priority research directions are proposed to advance this field: Biomarker discovery: (1) Longitudinal investigations are needed to characterize tissue-specific lactate dynamics (e.g., ovarian follicular fluid versus systemic circulation) as prognostic indicators for PCOS progression. The clinical utility of advanced imaging modalities, such as lactate-MRI mapping, should be explored for non-invasive quantification of metabolic activity in reproductive tissues. (2) Therapeutic innovation: Selective modulation of lactate signaling pathways, including GPR81 modulators and MCT isoform-specific inhibition, must be prioritized to achieve tissue-selective effects while minimizing systemic adverse outcomes. Combinatorial approaches integrating lactate metabolism regulators with existing insulin sensitizers (e.g., metformin) warrant systematic preclinical evaluation. (3) Mechanistic elucidation: Advanced multi-omics strategies (e.g., lactylomics coupled with single-cell transcriptomics) are essential to decode spatiotemporal patterns of lactylation modifications in ovarian granulosa cells and hypothalamic nuclei. The crosstalk between lactate-driven epigenetic reprogramming and androgen biosynthesis pathways remains to be delineated. Although lactation-targeted therapies have shown clinical potential in oncology, their application in PCOS is still in the exploratory phase. There is an urgent need for preclinical validation of lactation-modifying modulators in animal models of PCOS to identify novel mechanism-based interventions.

By addressing these knowledge gaps, future research may establish lactate-centric therapeutic paradigms that concurrently ameliorate reproductive dysfunction and mitigate long-term cardiometabolic risks in PCOS. The integration of lactate metabolism profiling into standardized PCOS management protocols could ultimately enable personalized therapeutic strategies tailored to individual metabolic signatures.
