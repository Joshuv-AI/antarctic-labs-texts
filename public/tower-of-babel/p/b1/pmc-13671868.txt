Sebaceous Adenoma of the Caruncle: A Case Report
Authors: Ji Sun Park, Namju Kim
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We would like to introduce a rare case of sebaceous adenoma of the caruncle. To the best of our knowledge, two cases of caruncular sebaceous adenoma have previously been reported in South Korea [1,2], one in the pathology literature and another in the head-and-neck oncology literature; however, neither has been reported in the ophthalmic literature. Therefore, the present case represents the first South Korean report of caruncular sebaceous adenoma published in an ophthalmology journal. A 54-year-old male patient was referred to our clinic with a complaint of a left caruncular mass. The mass had gradually enlarged over 2.5 years. At the initial visit, the patient reported no known systemic disease; however, detailed family history regarding internal malignancy was not available. His uncorrected visual acuity was 20 / 32 in both eyes. Intraocular pressure was normal in both eyes. A round, semihard, movable, yellowish mass measuring approximately 0.8 cm × 0.4 cm × 0.3 cm was visible at the left caruncle (Fig. 1A, 1B). Anterior segment examination was unremarkable except for the caruncular lesion. Preoperatively, benign caruncular lesions such as papilloma, nevus, epithelial inclusion cyst, oncocytoma, and pyogenic granuloma were considered. The yellowish color and gradual enlargement of the lesion raised the possibility of a sebaceous gland lesion. Therefore, excisional biopsy was performed for histopathologic confirmation. Written informed consent for publication of the clinical images and medical details was obtained from the patient. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (No. B-2604-1037-701).

The mass was removed en bloc. The gross specimen was a round mass measuring 0.8 cm × 0.4 cm × 0.3 cm. Pathologic examination revealed a sebaceous gland proliferative lesion (Fig. 1C, 1D), consistent with sebaceous adenoma. Histopathologic examination showed well-circumscribed lobules composed of peripheral basaloid germinative cells and centrally located mature sebocytes. No infiltrative growth pattern, marked cytologic atypia, necrosis, or frequent mitotic activity was observed. These findings favored sebaceous adenoma over sebaceous carcinoma.

Immunohistochemical staining was not performed. After the histopathologic diagnosis of sebaceous adenoma, systemic evaluation for Muir-Torre syndrome (MTS) and associated internal malignancy was considered necessary. However, additional systemic workup, referral, genetic counseling, and malignancy surveillance were not performed because no further clinical follow-up was conducted after completion of ophthalmologic management. Consequently, long-term recurrence and additional systemic findings could not be evaluated.

The caruncle is composed of stratified squamous epithelium, containing hair follicles, accessory lacrimal glands, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands. Although the caruncle contains diverse tissues including conjunctival, lacrimal, and cutaneous elements, neoplasms in this area are uncommon. The majority of caruncular lesions are benign, most frequently nevi or papillomas [3]. Malignant neoplasms of the caruncle are even rarer; Luthra et al. [4] reported that among 112 caruncular lesions reviewed over a 52-year period, only four were malignant.

Sebaceous tumors of the caruncle are rare conditions, as they are more frequently found in the eyelids. Sebaceous adenoma accounts for approximately 1% to 3.8% of caruncular lesions and is even rarer among conjunctival lesions. Clinically, it can mimic other benign tumors such as papilloma or pyogenic granuloma, making clinical diagnosis difficult. Kaeser et al. [3] reported that the clinical diagnosis matched the histological diagnosis in only 37.4% of cases; thus, histopathologic confirmation is essential.

Clinically, the diagnosis of sebaceous adenoma is significant due to its strong association with MTS, which occurs in approximately 50% of cases. MTS is a rare autosomal-dominant condition characterized by at least one sebaceous skin tumor and a visceral malignancy, such as gastrointestinal, genitourinary, or breast cancer. Therefore, although the lesion itself is benign, its diagnosis warrants a systemic workup to rule out associated internal malignancies. A limitation of this report is that systemic evaluation for MTS was not completed because additional follow-up after diagnosis was not performed. Therefore, although this case highlights the importance of systemic evaluation after the diagnosis of sebaceous adenoma, we could not determine whether this patient had an associated internal malignancy or MTS.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of sebaceous adenoma of the caruncle. Despite its rarity, sebaceous adenoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of caruncular masses. Given the potential association with MTS, accurate diagnosis through excisional biopsy and histopathologic review is mandatory to ensure appropriate systemic evaluation.
