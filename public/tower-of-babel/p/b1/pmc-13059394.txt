Nasolabial morphological changes following anterior vs. subapical maxillary segmental osteotomy: a comparative study
Authors: Yaoran Liu, Guangwei Chen, Zhengguo Piao, Junwei Zhang, Luo Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the effects of subapical anterior maxillary segmental osteotomy (SAMSO) and anterior maxillary segmental osteotomy (AMSO) on nasolabial morphology using three-dimensional (3D) cephalometric method.Materials and methodsA total of forty-five cases of maxillary protrusion who underwent AMSO or SAMSO from January 2019 to December 2023 were retrospectively included. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) was taken before and ≥ 1 year after surgery. Eleven distances (nasal height, upper lip protrusion, length of philtrum, etc.) and four angles (nasolabial angle, nasal tip angle, inclination of nasal tip, etc.) were measured on the lateral view, and four distances (width of alae nasi, width of alae nasi, nostril width, etc.) and one angle (alar nasi break angle) were measured on the frontal view. Changes in the anterior maxillary hard-tissue and nasolabial soft-tissue before and after surgery were analyzed. Correlations between hard-tissue movements and nasolabial soft-tissue changes were also evaluated.ResultsBoth AMSO and SAMSO achieved significant horizontal posterior displacement of point A and U1, with definite efficacy in correcting maxillary prognathism. Compared with SAMSO, AMSO presented a more obvious vertical impaction of U1, with a vertical decrease of 1.9 ± 1.13 mm (P = 0.002). Both groups exhibited significant retraction of upper lip protrusion, and the retraction amplitude was larger in the AMSO group (5.10 mm vs. 0.05 mm, P = 0.002). The AMSO group showed significant widening of the alar base width and width of the alae nasi, with increases of 2.57 ± 1.95 mm (P < 0.001) and 1.29 ± 1.64 mm (P = 0.01), respectively. In contrast, the SAMSO group showed a significant increase in nasal height and nasal tip angle, with increases of 5.65 ± 6.28 mm (P = 0.04) and 12.63 ± 6.89°(P = 0.001), respectively. Although posterior changes and lingual inclination of the maxillary anterior teeth strongly correlated with posterior changes of the upper lip in both groups, there was a significant correlation between changes of vertical position of the anterior maxilla and changes of width of alar base in the AMSO group.ConclusionAMSO and SAMSO can assist patients with maxillary prognathism in reducing upper lip protrusion and enhancing their profiles. Introduction

Maxillary protrusion, characterized by excessive overbite and anterior lip prominence, often results in a gummy smile, convex facial profile, and difficulty in achieving lip closure. This condition negatively affects oral function, facial aesthetics, and the psychological well-being of patients [1]. Maxillary protrusion is a relatively common dentofacial deformity in Asian populations, presenting with varying degrees of severity. Clinically, maxillary protrusion is managed based on its severity, using either orthodontic treatment alone or in combination with orthognathic surgery. Anterior maxillary segmental osteotomy (AMSO) is a commonly employed surgical technique for the correction of maxillary protrusion [2]. This approach not only addresses the underlying skeletal deformity but also aims to improve functional outcomes and facial aesthetics [3]. However, AMSO often involves extensive manipulation of the bony nasal base, which may lead to unintended and irreversible changes in nasolabial morphology. These include widening of the alar base, an increase in the nasolabial angle and upper lip length, and counterclockwise rotation of the nasal tip [4–8]. To minimize such complications, subapical anterior maxillary segmental osteotomy (SAMSO) was developed as a more conservative alternative [9]. While both techniques aim to achieve similar skeletal outcomes, the absence of controlled comparative studies makes it difficult to conclusively evaluate their differential effects on soft tissue morphology.

Three-dimensional (3D) analysis provides a reliable means of assessing changes in both soft and hard tissues, particularly in the frontal view, which is often the focus of patient concerns and a key criterion for evaluating surgical success. The purpose of this study was to evaluate and compare the postoperative nasolabial changes following SAMSO and AMSO using 3D analysis. After controlling for covariates, this study aimed to determine whether significant differences exist in nasolabial soft tissue outcomes between the two surgical approaches.

Materials and methods

Patients and group selection

This study was designed as a retrospective comparative cohort study and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Approval No. LCYJ20250430001 [Blinded for Review]). All procedures involving human participants strictly adhered to the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and all its subsequent revisions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to surgery, which clearly informed them the use of their clinical and imaging data for scientific research. A pre-test sample size calculation was performed prior to study initiation to ensure adequate statistical power. Based on the primary outcome and previous literature data, a sample size of 45 participants was determined using “G Power version 3.1.9.7” (Heinrich Heine University, Dusseldorf, Germany) with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.91, which was sufficient to detect the clinically meaningful difference in the study.

The cohort was divided into two groups based on the surgical approach: the SAMSO group and the AMSO group, with group allocation determined by the distance between piriform aperture and canine apices as evaluated by the same senior maxillofacial surgeon team. Patients with the distance between piriform aperture and canine apices greater than 8 mm were assigned to the AMSO group, while those with the distance less than 8 mm were placed in the SAMSO group.The primary indication for inclusion was Angle Class I malocclusion with excess of the maxillary anterior segment and significant dental protrusion that was uncorrectable by orthodontic treatment alone and thus required surgical correction.

The strict inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) No history of congenital oromaxillofacial malformations (e.g., cleft lip and palate, craniofacial syndromes); (2) Symmetrical facial morphology confirmed by preoperative clinical examination and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) (facial midline deviation < 2 mm); (3) No prior history of facial trauma or orthognathic treatment; (4) No significant pre-operative lip deformation (e.g., cleft lip repair sequelae, severe lip ptosis or asymmetry) that could interfere with post-operative nasolabial soft tissue assessment; (5) Availability of complete preoperative and postoperative CBCT data that meet the timing requirement: preoperative CBCT scanned 1 month before surgery and postoperative CBCT scanned at least 1 year after surgery, which could ensure postoperative imaging data met the long-term stability requirement; (6) Availability of complete clinical follow-up records for at least 18 months.

Surgical procedures

All surgeries were performed using the classic Epker technique [10]. Bilateral maxillary first premolars were extracted, and osteotomies were performed above the level of the tooth apices without palatal incisions or mucosal undermining. A vertical osteotomy line was created through the alveolar sockets of the extracted premolars bilaterally. Anteriorly, the cartilaginous nasal septum was elevated from the vomerian groove, and the nasal mucoperiosteum was elevated from the floor of the anterior nasal cavity. A horizontal osteotomy cut was then made from the piriform aperture (Fig. 1). An undulating horizontal osteotomy line was created 5 mm above the canine apices and 3 mm below the piriform aperture, with care taken to avoid stripping the nasal mucoperiosteum (Fig. 2). Alar base cinch sutures were applied in both groups to minimize soft tissue widening.

Computed tomography (CT) measurement

All patients received standardized cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) before and after orthognathic surgery. CBCT data were imported into Proplan CMF 3.0 software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for standardized 3D reconstruction of craniofacial hard and soft tissues. The scanning parameters for CBCT were standardized: 120 kV, 8 mA, voxel size 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm, and the field of view (FOV) covered the entire craniofacial region (from the frontal sinus to the mandibular symphysis) to ensure that all landmarks were included. Soft tissue segmentation was performed by adjusting the Hounsfield unit (HU) threshold (hard tissue: 226–3071 HU; soft tissue: −1024–225 HU) with manual correction for overlapping structures to ensure segmentation accuracy.

A standardized three-dimensional cephalometric reference system was established based on the Frankfort horizontal (FH) plane (defined as the plane passing through the orbitale and porion bilaterally) and the midsagittal plane (MSP, defined as the median plane bisecting the crista galli and mandibular symphysis), with a coronal frontal plane perpendicular to both FH and MSP extracted for frontal view measurement (Table 1). After set-up of 3D cephalometric planes, virtual definition of thirteen cephalometric landmarks (four hard-tissue and nine soft-tissue landmarks; Table 1) were identified by the same trained observer according to established criteria [11, 12]; to ensure reliability, landmark identification was repeated once with a 1-week interval. The following 3D cephalometric hard, soft and teeth measurements can be automatically calculated in the 3D virtual scene. In the lateral view, 4 angular and 11 linear variables were measured (Table 2; Fig. 3). In the frontal view, 1 angular and 4 Linear variables measured (Table 2; Fig. 4). Unified directionality criteria were established for all measured hard and soft tissue change. Detailed directionality definitions are provided in the notes of Tables 3 and 4. Three independent observers measured cephalometric parameters to evaluate nasolabial hard and soft tissue changes [11, 12]. Additionally, to investigate the nasolabial morphology changes, Geomagic Freeform 12.0 (Geomagic, Morrisville, NC, USA) was used for preoperative and postoperative soft tissue superimposition analysis. Preoperative 3D models were set as the reference, and postoperative models were rigidly superimposed based on stable cranial base landmarks (crista galli, basion, and porion) to eliminate head position differences. The surface displacements of the nose and upper lip were calculated using 3D surface-to-surface matching with a least-mean-squared algorithm. Subsequently, the reconstructed images were automatically fitted based on the coordination between the registration areas for superimposition-based 3D displacement measurement.

For interobserver and intraobserver reliability assessment, three independent observers (two maxillofacial surgeons and one oral radiologist, all trained in 3D cephalometric measurement) independently measured all cephalometric parameters twice. Interobserver reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC values were all greater than 0.8, demonstrating the high reliability of these measurements.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For each measurement parameter, preoperative and postoperative means and standard deviations were calculated. Paired t-test was used to assess intragroup differences in anterior maxillary hard-tissue and nasolabial soft tissue measurements before and after surgery, and the Welch t-test was used to compare the intergroup data. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between maxillary hard tissue movements and corresponding changes in nasolabial soft tissues. A p-value of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of forty - five consecutive Chinese patients who underwent SAMSO or AMSO in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between January 2019 and June 2023 were retrospectively enrolled. The final study cohort consisted of 45 participants, which met the sample size requirement determined by pre - test calculation. The mean age of the patients was 28.9 ± 9.28 years. Regarding surgical procedures, six-teen cases involved AMSO and twenty-nine cases involved SAMSO. Of the included patients, thirty-two additionally underwent anterior mandibular subapical osteotomy and 9 underwent simultaneous genioplasty.

Changes in hard tissues of the anterior maxilla

To evaluate the skeletal changes, several anatomical landmarks were analyzed including point A and upper incisor (U1) point. Both groups exhibited statistically significant posterior movement at point A and U1 (Tables 3 and 4). In terms of vertical changes, SAMSO group maintained vertical stability of anterior maxillary hard-tissue (both A(v) and U1(v)). In contrast, AMSO group achieved extremely significant vertical depression, which showed that U1(v) changed from 54.09 ± 2.68 to 52.19 ± 3.39 mm with a decreased mean value of 1.9 ± 1.13 mm (P = 0.04) and A(v) changed from 28.55 ± 2.52 to 26.87 ± 2.52 mm with a decreased mean value of 2 ± 1.53 mm (P = 0.002) (Table 4). Moreover, Table 5 compared the preoperative and postoperative changes of anterior maxillary hard-tissue between AMSO and SAMSO group, with only U1(v) presenting significant difference of preoperative minus postoperative measurements. This indicates that the vertical depression amplitude of anterior maxilla is much larger in AMSO group.

Changes in soft tissue around the nose and upper lip

Intragroup comparisons of nasolabial soft tissue changes between the preoperative and postoperative stages are presented in Tables 3 and 4. In the sagittal plane, significant changes were observed in upper lip protrusion and nasolabial angle in both groups. Both surgeries achieved extremely significant upper lip retraction, mainly attributed to passive remodeling of upper lip after losing excessive support from anterior maxillary hard-tissue horizontal posterior shift. Neither changed philtrum length, preserving the integrity of lip physiological morphology. The horizontal and vertical positions of Sn had no significant changes, indicating that both surgeries did not interfere with the basic support of subnasal soft-tissue with excellent safety. Concerning the nasolabial angle, both surgeries achieved significant increase with postoperative values, which is in line with upper lip retraction amplitude and direction. Although both groups exhibited upward and forward rotation of the nasal tip, the AMSO group demonstrated greater alterations at the Pn point. We found that it vertically decrease 1.05 ± 1.55 mm (p < 0.05) and horizontally increase 1.24 ± 1.77 mm (p < 0.05), which indicated more pronounced nasal tip rotation (Table 4). But the nasal height and nasal tip angle in SAMSO showed apparent increase while remained stable in AMSO (Tables 3 and 4). Furthermore, the alteration of nasal tip angle exhibited an extremely significant intergroup difference (−0.07 vs. −12.63°, P = 0.006), with a far greater increase extent in SAMSO group (Table 5). The change of upper lip protrusion also showed extremely significant difference between AMSO and SAMSO groups (5.10 mm vs. 0.05 mm, P = 0.002) (Table 5). These results indicate that these two types of surgeries have different effects on the profile of the nose and upper lips.

Nasal width was assessed using the transverse plane measurements. In the AMSO group, all nasal parameters except nostril width increased significantly (Table 4), indicating a general widening of the nasal base. In contrast, the SAMSO group showed no statistically significant changes in nasal width, with the exception of a significant reduction in nostril width (Table 3). The results of the matching maps were consistent with the quantitative analysis (Fig. 5). The alar nasi break angle, which reflects structural changes in the nasal base and alar contour, increased significantly in the AMSO group—from 105.14 ± 11.86° to 112.04 ± 10.64° (p < 0.05)—suggesting a widening and flattening of the alar base. However, in the SAMSO group, this angle remained unchanged. Thus it can be seen a more obvious widening amplitude of nasal width indicators in AMSO group. Regarding the comparison of the changes in nose width between the two groups, only the width of alar base and the alar nasi break angle showed significant differences (Table 5), which means postoperative effect of alar base will vary more apparently depending on which of these two surgeries is performed.

Correlations between maxillary hard tissue movements and soft tissue changes in the nasolabial region

Correlations between postoperative changes in hard tissues and corresponding changes in the nasolabial soft tissues are summarized in Table 6. Both groups demonstrated horizontal displacement of point A (A(h)) was significantly negatively correlated with length of philtrum and positively correlated with nasolabial angle. The horizontal displacement of U1 (U1(h)) was solely significantly positively correlated with upper lip protrusion in the AMSO group. While for the SAMSO group, it showed that horizontal movement U1 (U1[h]) was not only significantly positively correlated with upper lip protrusion, but also negatively correlated with inclination of nasal tip and nasal height. Regarding vertical movements, vertical displacement of point A (A(v)) presented significant negative correlations with inclination of nasal tip and vertical coordinate of Pn in both groups. Furthermore, the vertical displacement of A (A(v)) demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the length of the philtrum in the AMSO group. The U1(v) showed a significant positive correlation with the nasolabial angle in the SAMSO group. Moreover, in the AMSO group, it was significantly negatively correlated with Pn(v) and positively correlated with the vertical coordinate of Sn (Sn(v)). As for incisor inclination, both groups demonstrated significant correlation between U1-to-FH angle and nasolabial angle, as well as upper lip protrusion. Notably, in the SAMSO group, a significant relationship was also observed between U1-to-FH angle and horizontal movement of the subnasale point (Sn[h]).

Discussion

AMSO is perhaps the most commonly used surgical approaches for correcting isolated dentoalveolar protrusion, particularly in patients with class I or II molar occlusions [8]. Conventional AMSO has been shown to be effective in repositioning the anterior maxilla in posterior, inferior, and superior directions, thereby improving both function and facial aesthetics. Historically, Cupar (1954) [13] introduced an incision design similar to the Le Fort I circumferential vestibular approach to improve surgical access and visibility. This labial approach allowed osteotomies to be performed from the labial to the palatal side under direct visualization. Later, in 1977, Epker [10] modified the procedure by refining the sequence of osteotomies and introducing the concept of “downfracturing” the anterior maxilla, thereby enabling more direct exposure of the nasal septal structures. Despite the functional benefits of these techniques, both approaches typically involve vertical osteotomies that extend through the alveolar sockets (pos-premolar extraction) toward the lateroinferior aspect of the piriform aperture. This trajectory may affect the nasal septum and anterior palatal region. As a result, structural changes in the anterior nasal spine, the inferior rim of the piriform aperture, and the skeletal nasal base can occur, often leading to noticeable postoperative alterations in nasal morphology. To address these issues, a modified horizontal osteotomy technique has been proposed, designed to preserve the inferior piriform aperture and the anterior nasal spine, thereby minimizing unintended changes to nasal structure and nasolabial soft tissues. However, existing literature on nasolabial soft tissue outcomes following AMSO remains limited, consisting mostly of case series or observational cohort studies. The lack of controlled comparative studies makes it difficult to draw robust conclusions regarding the soft tissue effects of different segmental osteotomy approaches. In the present study, we sought to fill this gap by directly comparing nasolabial soft tissue parameters before and after surgery using two anterior maxillary setback techniques—AMSO and SAMSO.

The current study showed that the AMSO technique led to significant posterior and superior movement of the anterior maxilla, while the SAMSO technique produced posterior and slight superior displacement. Both groups exhibited posterior movement and lingual inclination of the maxillary anterior teeth, but significant vertical (upward) movement occurred only in the AMSO group. The difference may be attributed to the modified horizontal osteotomy in the SAMSO technique, which is positioned below the anterior nasal spine and the piriform aperture. The limited distance between the osteotomy line and tooth apices restricts the upward repositioning of the dentoalveolar segment. Moreover, the vertical changes of U1 between the two groups presented more obvious differences compared with the sagittal changes, which proves that the two interventions have diverse abilities to move the maxilla in intrusion. AMSO has a wide range of indications because it can treat vertical excess of the maxilla. In this situation, this study can provide theoretical reference for the specific surgical planning.

Previous studies have reported marked soft tissue changes following anterior maxillary osteotomy. Okudaira et al. [6] noted significant upper lip changes; Wu et al. [9]and Park and Hwang [7] reported reduction in lip thickness and increases in philtrum length. In contrast, our findings showed clear retraction of upper lip protrusion in both groups but minimal change in philtrum length. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the magnitude and direction of anterior maxillary movement. Morphological changes in the nasolabial region also involve alterations in the nasolabial angle. Jayaratne et al. [14] reported that reduced labial prominence is typically accompanied by an increased nasolabial angle, especially when horizontal maxillary setback is less than 4 mm and incisor angulation plays a greater role [15]. In our study, both groups showed an increase in the nasolabial angle accompanied by a decrease in the U1-to-FH angle, suggesting a relationship between incisor inclination and soft tissue change.

The nasolabial soft tissue response follows the “hard tissue-driven” principle for both procedures. but the adaptive changes show distinct characteristics due to differences in hard tissue remodeling patterns, mainly reflected in nasal morphology and alar soft tissue changes. Notably, significant upward and forward rotation of the nasal tips was observed in the AMSO group, while minimal change occurred in the SAMSO group. This difference suggests that AMSO has a more obvious indirect traction effect on the nasal tip, while SAMSO is more conducive to maintaining the original nasal tip position, which is suitable for patients who need to preserve the native nasal tip morphology. Conversely, SAMSO induced a significant increase in nasal height and a marked increase in nasal tip angle, which significantly enhanced nasal stereoscopic appearance. In contrast, AMSO showed no significant change in nasal height or nasal tip angle. This unique advantage of SAMSO may be related to the specific traction effect of hard tissue displacement on the nasal dorsal soft tissue, which is particularly beneficial for patients with flat nasal bridge combined with anterior maxillary protrusion.These results suggest that SAMSO may have a more pronounced effect on the lateral contour of the nasal soft tissues, whereas AMSO exerts a stronger influence on tip rotation. Regarding frontal nasal morphology, previous studies have reported nasal base widening following maxillary osteotomy [16]. Wu et al. [9], however, found no change in nasal soft tissue width after SAMSO. The extent of alar base or alar width change after maxillary setback remains debated. In our study, nasal width parameters—including the alae nasi, alar base, and nasal alar tangent points—were more significantly altered in the AMSO group, despite both groups undergoing alar cinch suturing [17, 18]. Interestingly, the alar nasi break angle increased significantly in the AMSO group and decreased in the SAMSO group. The significant expansion of alar soft tissue in AMSO may be due to the vertical depression of hard tissue leading to soft tissue relaxation around the nose, while the stable alar morphology in SAMSO reflects its superior soft tissue stability, which can avoid the risk of nasal contourbloatingaftersurgery. .

In addition above findings, significant differences were observed in the changes of the upper lip protrusion and the nasal tip angle between the two groups.This might be due to the varying extent of changes at the Sn point. The major difference between the SAMSO and AMSO lies in whether the lower edge of the piriform aperture is retained or not. Therefore, different degrees of elevation of the anterior maxilla result in different degrees of changes in the nasal floor soft tissue relative to the fixed osseous sites during operation. Furthermore, the changes in width of alar base and alar nasi break angle also showed significant differences between AMSO and SAMSO. AMSO requires extensive soft tissue dissection around the piriform aperture and maxilla during surgery, whereas SAMSO better preserves the muscular attachments to the inferior piriform aperture and anterior nasal spine, limiting such change.The possibility of good blood circulation and rapid muscle reattachment guarantees narrowing the alar base width and the greatest alar width. The significant expansion of alar soft tissue in AMSO may be due to the vertical depression of hard tissue leading to soft tissue relaxation around the nose, while the stable alar morphology in SAMSO reflects its superior soft tissue stability, which can avoid the risk of nasal contour bloating after surgery.

We also examined the correlation between postoperative hard and soft tissue changes. Among the hard tissue variables, changes in the position of U1 showed the strongest association with soft tissue alterations. Specifically, U1(h) was significantly correlated with upper lip protrusion, while U1(v) was associated with changes in the nasolabial angle. Prior studies have shown that the nasolabial angle is more influenced by incisal angulation when horizontal maxillary movement is less than 4 mm [15]. Consistent with this, our findings revealed that lingual inclination of the anterior teeth (U1to FH) correlated with posterior displacement of the upper lip in both groups. The strongest correlation was observed between U1(h) and upper lip protrusion, suggesting that the position of the anterior maxillary teeth plays a central role in shaping the postoperative upper lip profile. Bhagat et al. [19] proposed that nasal tip changes may result from posterior lip rotation around the subnasale. In our study upward movement of the anterior maxilla (point A) correlated with decreased nasal tip inclination, Primarily due to elevation of the Pn with minimal movement of the Sn. Furthermore, vertical displacement of anterior maxillary hard tissues (U1 and A) showed strong correlation with the changes in Sn(v), Pn(v), and the alar base width in the AMSO group. These findings indicate that AMSO has more pronounced effect on nasal soft tissues compared to SAMSO. This may be attributed to preservation of the piriform aperture in the SAMSO approach, which appears to limit soft tissue disruptions and maintain nasal morphology.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations that need full elaboration. First, as a retrospective study, it suffers from inherent selection bias and uncontrolled confounding factors (e.g., patient age, preoperative orthodontic duration, soft-tissue thickness, baseline condylar status), which may reduce outcome comparability between AMSO and SAMSO groups; prospective studies with strict inclusion/exclusion criteria are required to improve result reliability.

Second, we only conducted a one-year follow-up post-operation, leaving uncertainty about the long-term effects of post-operative orthodontic therapy on patient outcomes. Longer follow-up is warranted in future research.

Third, we have additional unaddressed limitations: limiting precise surgical planning for high-risk patients; individual soft-tissue biological traits (e.g., elasticity, thickness) were not considered, which may affect nasolabial remodeling consistency; and no control group of other surgical techniques (e.g., total maxillary posterior impaction) was set, weakening the comparative validity of our results. These limitations provide key directions for future optimized research, while not affecting the core conclusions of this study.

Conclusion

Nasolabial changes following anterior maxillary retraction are complex and technique-specific. AMSO and SAMSO both effectively correct upper lip protrusion and optimize facial profile in maxillary prognathism, yet their nasal effects diverge: AMSO exerts prominent sagittal and frontal nasal soft tissue impacts, whereas SAMSO primarily modifies sagittal contours with negligible frontal nasal width alterations. This evidence aids individualized surgical planning and technique selection based on patient needs and aesthetic goals, and facilitates better doctor-patient communication and informed consent in clinical practice.
