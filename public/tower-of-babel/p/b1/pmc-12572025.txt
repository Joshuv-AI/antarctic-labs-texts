Taiwan society of colon and rectal surgeons’ consensus on the management of hemorrhoidal disease
Authors: Tsung-Kun Chang, Tao-Wei Ke, Pao-Shiu Hsieh, Shih-Ching Chang, Wan Hsiang Hu, Ching-Wen Huang, Feng-Fan Chiang, Hsiang-Lin Tsai, Jin-Tung Liang, Chien-Kuo Liu, Jaw-Yuan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEstablishing statements backed by strong scientific evidence with the intention of standardizing and guiding Hemorrhoidal Disease (HD) diagnosis and treatment in both the general population and in specific patient groups is the aim of this consensus.MethodsA consensus panel of 11 surgical specialists from the Taiwan Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons was convened from July 2023 to October 2024. Using a modified Delphi methodology, the panel addressed the diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and special situations of HD. Experts responded to a pre-meeting survey, and a literature search of English-language studies on clinical judgment, pharmacologic treatments, and surgical interventions was conducted. Draft statements were developed, discussed, and refined during the first meeting in July 2023. In April 2024, the panel reviewed updated studies, finalized recommendations, and voted anonymously. An online meeting in October 2024 validated and approved the recommendations, which were graded using the GRADE system.ResultsA total of 9 consensus statements have been developed covering the diagnosis and management of HD. The general principle of this consensus statement is to follow most international guidelines. To tailor the local clinical practice, we also discussed the alternative treatment strategy for sclerotherapy, the use of traditional herbs/medicines, and the consideration of CRC screening test.ConclusionsThese suggestions may represent a positive start toward recognized guidelines for improved hemorrhoidal disease treatment for all Taiwan healthcare professionals. As the first point of contact with patients, general practitioners, family medicine physicians, young surgeons, and pharmacists will find them to be quite valuable. Introduction

Hemorrhoidal disease (HD) is the most common pathology of the anorectal region in adults worldwide [1, 2]. It is associated with major socioeconomic and medical problems. The incidence of HD varies widely between countries, depending on the documentation method. Reports from the UK suggest that 13% to 36% of the general population have hemorrhoids [3]. Similarly, reports from the USA in 1990 suggest that >10 million individuals, representing 4.4% of the entire population, have HD [1]. Many studies have been conducted worldwide to determine the incidence of and contributing factors to HD. Among all countries, Australia has been reported to have the highest rate of HD (38.93%), followed by Israel (16%) and South Korea (14.4%) [4, 5]. In a study of data obtained from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, 14.4% of the respondents claimed to have HD, with 7.2% reporting a confirmed diagnosis [6]. An epidemiological study examined HD in Taiwan by using data from the National Health Insurance Research Database, which contains the claims data of 1 million patients randomly selected in 2000, 2005, and 2010. The results indicated that 1.37% to 2.01% of Taiwanese adults have HD, with approximately 30% of them receiving surgical treatment [7]. Internal hemorrhoids may be identified in up to 86% of patients undergoing proctoscopy, although most are asymptomatic [8]. HD may be asymptomatic or may present with a wide range of clinical symptoms, including rectal bleeding. The majority of patients with HD are asymptomatic and do not require surgical treatment. The potential risk factors for HD are male sex, age between 45 and 65 years, low-fiber diet, stress, depression, high body mass index, abdominal obesity, pregnancy, prolonged sitting, and high intra-abdominal pressure [9].

Due to the lack of developed consensus guidelines for the management of HD in Taiwan, we aim to make a positive start toward recognized guidelines for improved management of HD for all healthcare professionals in Taiwan. This consensus guideline is an expert consensus tailored to local clinical practice based on current scientific evidence.

Methods

The consensus panel, consisting of 11 surgical specialists from the Taiwan Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons, was convened from July 2023 to October 2024 using a modified Delphi methodology to provide recommendations that ensure the best possible diagnosis and treatment of HD in Taiwan (Fig. 1). Experts responded to a pre-meeting survey on four topics, including diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and special situations of HD, and then, the survey results were discussed at the first consensus meeting. Statements were drafted based on expert feedback and a literature search of electronic medical databases (including PubMed, Cochrane Review Library) conducted in July 2023 for relevant studies published after 2010. We conducted a literature search for original English-language studies on clinical judgment, efficacy of pharmacologic treatments, and effectiveness of surgical treatments published between 2010 and 2023.Fig. 1Flow diagram of the consensus forming process and members of the expert consensus guidelines meeting on hemorrhoidal disease

The included studies were used to draft statements that were discussed and modified by the experts during the first consensus meeting in July 2023. In the second consensus meeting, experts discussed the statements, made recommendations, reviewed newly published studies, and voted anonymously in April 2024.

A 5-point Likert scale was used to record the level of expert agreement for each statement: a, fully agree; b, agree with minor reservations; c, agree with major reservations; d, disagree with reservations; e, disagree completely. Consensus was reached when a statement received agreement (a, b, or c) from ≥75% of the expert group. A strong recommendation was given when a statement received ≥90% agreement.

An online meeting was then held in October 2024 to validate and approve the recommendations. The recommendations were defined and graded based on the current levels of evidence and in accordance with the GRADE system (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) [10].

Result

A total of 9 consensus statements were formed.

To achieve positive patient outcomes, HD should be appropriately diagnosed and managed. Hemorrhoids can be divided into three types based on their location in relation to the dentate line: internal (above or proximal), external (below or distal), and mixed [2]. Internal hemorrhoids are further divided into four grades based on the degree of prolapse from the anal canal [11]. Patients with hemorrhoids often present with bleeding, swelling, prolapse, pain, and itching, with anemia observed in severe cases.

HD is typically diagnosed by taking the patient’s medical history and performing a physical examination. Performing an appropriate physical examination and additional diagnostic workup is essential before attributing painless rectal bleeding to HD.

The first step in diagnosing HD involves taking the patient’s medical history, identifying symptoms suggestive of HD, and examining risk factors such as pregnancy, low-fiber diet, constipation, and sedentary lifestyle. Reviewing the patient’s treatment history can provide valuable insights into their symptoms and general health. History-taking usually involves questions regarding the patient’s food and bowel habits. In addition, the patient’s frequency of exercise and drug use and whether any blood relatives of the patient have colon polyps or cancer can be inquired. A history of chronic or uncontrolled portal hypertension in a patient can differentiate between HD and anorectal varices. In addition, a history of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and any symptoms associated with decreased anal continence can inform the optimal course of action.

Physical examination should be conducted to rule out other anorectal diseases and confirm HD. Physical examination should include anorectal digital examination, perianal tissue inspection, and evaluation of the degree of hemorrhoidal prolapse during strengthening. Anoscopy could also be conducted to identify internal hemorrhoids or anorectal pathology during the examination. After patients experience painless rectal bleeding, and anoscopy examination reveals unremarkable findings, diagnostic colonoscopy could be performed as a standard procedure.

Acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding is a common complication of HD [12]. In addition to being a sign of colon cancer, rectal bleeding is a common early sign of diverticular disease, angiodysplasia, IBD, and other colorectal conditions. Because performing hemorrhoid surgery on patients with IBD or colorectal cancer (CRC) can be devastating, colonoscopy could be indicated in patients who experience rectal bleeding. In addition, due to the high incidence of CRC in Taiwan, CRC screening tests should be considered in patients with unusual clinical presentations, such as bowel habit changes, refractory or unusual bleeding sites, abdominal pain, or body weight loss.

Depending on its grade, HD can be treated with either invasive surgical procedures or noninvasive medical strategies. Noninvasive medical strategies cannot reverse the underlying structural changes in hemorrhoidal tissue, and invasive surgical procedures are associated with high risks of complications and recurrence. In addition, if not appropriately treated, the severity and symptoms of HD often worsen over time. Therefore, the basic treatment approach for HD involves active prevention and early intervention, including components related to dietary modifications, medical attention, and changes to potential risk factors.

HD is influenced by sociodemographic factors such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, and educational level [13]. Many other factors also contribute to the development of HD, such as family history of HD, extended straining, delayed evacuation, constipation, delayed bowel movement, persistent cough, dietary habits, insufficient water intake, lifestyle habits, and overweight [14, 15]. For patients with HD, their preferences and characteristics should be accounted for during treatment and decision-making.

For any grade of HD, lifestyle modifications should be recommended as the main treatment strategy and as a preventative measure against exacerbation. These modifications include returning to a normal diet, eating more foods rich in dietary fibers, drinking sufficient water every day, refraining from lifting heavy objects, and adopting a sedentary lifestyle. Unhealthy lifestyle habits, such as drinking, eating spicy food or nuts, standing and walking for more than 4 h, spending excessive amounts of time on the toilet seat, and irregular defecation, can increase the risk of HD [16]. One key goal of the conservative treatment of HD is to maintain a balanced diet with sufficient fiber and fluid intake for improving the consistency of stools. Recommendations for lifestyle modifications should be provided to each patient during the treatment course, and these recommendations can also mitigate the risk of recurrence in case additional intervention is required.

Currently, dietary modifications and increased fiber intake are recommended as general guidelines for the conservative treatment and prevention of HD. Alonso-Coello et al. [17] reported that consuming diets rich in fibers is associated with a reduction of 50% in the clinical symptoms of HD. However, consuming diets rich in fibers has no effect on patients with grade III or IV hemorrhoids and prolapse [18].

HD can cause mild symptoms, ephemeral symptoms, or severe persistent symptoms. Treatment is not required if the hemorrhage is not symptomatic. Generally, the clinical treatment provided for HD depends on its severity. Specifically, patients with grade I or II internal HD could receive conservative treatment, whereas patients with grade III or IV internal HD could receive radical surgical treatment. Treatment plans range from simple conservative measures to medical therapy, in-office procedures, and ultimately surgical treatment, depending on the severity of symptoms, grade of HD, patient age, and comorbidities. In terms of medical care, irritation and pain can be temporarily relieved by using creams and suppositories containing astringents, analgesics, and steroids.

Sitting in a warm sitz bath at 40 to 42 °C for 3 min is a conventional and often recommended treatment for several anal conditions, including HD [19]. Despite the positive effects of this treatment approach, few randomized controlled trials have determined its effects on pain associated with HD.

HD can be topically treated with ointments, creams, or rectal suppositories. Topical treatments primarily include local anesthetics, corticosteroids, antibiotics, and anti-inflammatory medications, which ameliorate symptoms. Despite its promising effects, topical therapy is not sufficiently supported by scientific research [15]. Anti-inflammatory suppositories are often used to treat HD, primarily because inflammation causes pain, perianal skin irritation, and ultimately bleeding [10, 11, 20]. However, their effectiveness for treating HD remains to be confirmed [8–11].

Flavonoids are orally administered herbal drugs that have anti-inflammatory properties, and they can increase vascular tone, improve lymphatic drainage, and stabilize capillary permeability. Phlebotonics such as MPFFs are primarily used for the management of HD. These medications mitigate the risk of recurrence because they address the metabolic aspects of chronic venous insufficiency associated with HD [21]. In clinical practice, MPFFs consisting of 90% diosmin and 10% hesperidin are the most commonly used. Their small size (less than 2 μm) facilitates their rapid absorption. A recent clinical trial demonstrated that MPFFs are effective against chronic venous diseases and hemorrhoid symptoms. The trial obtained positive outcomes in patients with all grades of HD who received MPFF-based treatment before irreversible degenerative changes in their hemorrhoidal plexus ligaments [22]. In a recent meta-analysis for a wide variety of phlebotonics, including MPFFs, for the treatment of HD, phlebotonics were associated with statistically significant improvements in bleeding and symptom management of acute HD and in bleeding after hemorrhoidectomy [23]. Multiple randomized controlled trials have indicated that MPFFs have positive effects on pain, tenesmus, pruritus, and bleeding after hemorrhoidectomy [19, 24].

Traditional herbal medicines are widely available as over-the-counter drugs in Asian countries. Huai Hua San is a common traditional Chinese medicine used to regulate blood components, clear the intestine, and stop bleeding. It is widely used in clinical settings to treat diseases such as HD, colitis, or other fecal and blood-borne diseases [25]. Bromelain is a cysteine protease found in pineapples. Because of its anti-inflammatory properties, it is often used for the treatment of HD and ulcerative colitis [26, 27]. In short, traditional herbs/medicines may relieve certain patients’ hemorrhoidal symptoms temporarily, but long-term efficacy and safety lack evidence from large-scale randomized clinical trials. The use of traditional herbs/medicines in clinical practice should be done with caution.

Decreasing the likelihood of constipation can prevent the recurrence of HD and can alleviate the current discomfort associated with HD. The ingestion of foods that are rich in dietary fiber and an adequate intake of water are fundamental for the mitigation of the risk of recurrent HD and for the enhancement of bowel function.

For patients with HD, the primary goal of engaging in physical activity is to promote regular bowel movements, improve circulation, and strengthen the muscles in the pelvic area and lower back. Insufficient physical activity may increase the risk of constipation and thereby adversely affect the current HD, which presumably leads to its recurrence.

In a meta-analysis comparing phlebotonics with no therapy or placebo, the risk of recurrence was statistically significantly lower for patients receiving MPFFs [28].

The common outpatient procedures performed for internal hemorrhoids are RBL, bipolar diathermy (Bicap, HET bipolar system), laser photocoagulation, infrared coagulation, sclerotherapy, and cryosurgery. The choice of the procedure primarily depends on local availability.

For patients with grade I, II, or III internal hemorrhoids, RBL is recommended because it is more effective and involves fewer treatments than sclerotherapy and infrared coagulation [29, 30]. For patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs, patients with a compromised immune system, and patients with portal hypertension, sclerotherapy is recommended, if available, because it is associated with a lower risk of delayed bleeding compared with RBL [9]. Surgical suture ligation may be considered as an alternative to sclerotherapy, if it is unavailable.

RBL is a commonly used technique because it is effective [29–33], inexpensive, and easy to perform, and it is rarely associated with severe complications [32, 34]. If multiple sessions of RBL are required, repeat banding may be performed at intervals > 14 days, so that any swelling or ulceration can subside. The most common complication of RBL is pain, which occurs in approximately 8% of cases [32, 34, 35]. Other complications include delayed hemorrhage, hemorrhoidal thrombosis, local infection, and sepsis [36–38].

In laser hemorrhoidoplasty, a laser is used to coagulate the blood vessels in hemorrhoids, leading to their shrinkage. This technique can be used for patients with grade II and III HD, and it is associated with minor postoperative pain and minimal perianal wounding, generating negligible postoperative discomfort [39]. When laser hemorrhoidoplasty is used to treat patients with grade IV hemorrhoids, additional caution should be exercised in terms of postoperative complications [40]. In addition, the external part of the hemorrhoids may still need to be surgically removed after laser therapy. In summary, laser hemorrhoidoplasty offers a minor advantage over other approaches in terms of postoperative pain and wound healing; a disadvantage is the expensive equipment required for laser hemorrhoidoplasty.

The HET Bipolar Ligator System uses bipolar energy to treat grade I or II internal bleeding hemorrhoids that are refractory to medical therapy [20, 41]. This system can be used as an alternative to RBL.

Infrared coagulation involves the direct application of infrared light to hemorrhoidal tissue. These infrared light waves are converted into heat, which causes protein necrosis within hemorrhoids. This procedure is effective for grade I, II, or III internal bleeding hemorrhoids. Compared with RBL, infrared coagulation is associated with a higher rate of recurrence but fewer complications and less immediate postoperative discomfort [42, 43].

Injection sclerotherapy is used to treat patients with grade I or II internal bleeding hemorrhoids, with a reported success rate of 75% to 89% [44, 45]. Compared with RBL, this procedure is associated with a higher rate of recurrence but a lower risk of rebleeding [30]. Therefore, sclerotherapy is particularly useful for patients with coagulopathies or portal hypertension because of the lack of eschar formation as well as for immunocompromised patients because of the lower risk of anorectal sepsis compared with RBL or hemorrhoidectomy. The most validated agents include 3% polidocanol foam [46] and ALTA (aluminum potassium sulfate and tannic acid) [47].

Cryosurgery involves the use of special probes cooled with liquid nitrogen to achieve freezing, necrosis, and subsequent fixation of the hemorrhoidal cushion. This procedure is associated with a high rate of complications and low patient satisfaction [48].

The methods of internal hemorrhoidectomy differ primarily in terms of how the hemorrhoidal tissue is removed and whether the rectal mucosa is closed. Conventional hemorrhoidectomies are primarily divided into two types: closed (Ferguson) and open (Milligan–Morgan, excision and ligation without mucosal closure). Closed hemorrhoidectomy is commonly associated with a low rate of infection [49], whereas open hemorrhoidectomy is preferable for acute gangrenous hemorrhoids because mucosal closure cannot be conducted without undue tension due to tissue edema and necrosis [50]. Overall, no definitive advantage is observed between open and closed hemorrhoidectomy [49, 51]. All three internal hemorrhoidal columns are usually simultaneously treated. Utilizing energy equipment such as advanced bipolar devices and harmonic scalpels in conventional hemorrhoidectomies may reduce intraoperative bleeding and postoperative pain, but these devices have high costs [52, 53].

Doppler-guided hemorrhoidal artery ligation (DGHAL) and transanal hemorrhoidal dearterialization can be performed with or without mucopexy [54–56]. These techniques can be used in patients with grade II or III HD and are effective and safe. Compared with transanal hemorrhoidal dearterialization, DGHAL is associated with a lower rate of postoperative complications, including pain, but a higher rate of recurrence [57, 58]. In the treatment of low-grade symptomatic HD, hemorrhoidal artery ligation revealed a similar recurrence rate compared to repeated RBL, but it was more expensive and associated with more early postoperative pain[59].

Stapled hemorrhoidopexy is a time-saving alternative to conventional internal hemorrhoidectomy [60, 61]. Although this technique does not directly treat external hemorrhoids, it is used to treat patients with bleeding or prolapsing grade II to IV internal hemorrhoids who do not respond to RBL and patients who seek a less painful alternative to conventional surgery but are willing to accept a higher risk of recurrence [62, 63]. No significant differences are typically observed in the majority of postoperative complications between conventional hemorrhoidectomy and stapled hemorrhoidopexy, although some severe complications have been reported in patients undergoing stapled hemorrhoidopexy, such as rectal perforation, pelvic sepsis, and rectovaginal fistulas [64].

External HD is usually managed conservatively, unless patients have a skin tag large enough to cause skin irritation, itching, pain, or inability to maintain proper hygiene, in which case excision may be beneficial. For patients with thrombosed external hemorrhoids who present with persistent pain (typically within 72 h of onset), hemorrhoidal excision provides immediate relief [65]. Complete excision of thrombosed hemorrhoids is associated with a lower recurrence rate of 5% to 19% compared to 30% for simple incision and thrombus evacuation [66–68]. After 48 to 72 h, the thrombus typically contracts, ameliorating the patient’s symptoms and eliminating the need for surgical treatment.

Statement 8. The following are the recommendations for special situations:

(100% agreement for all sub-items)

Special situation 1: Pregnant women

Symptomatic HD has a prevalence of 25% to 35% in pregnant women, and it is particularly common in the last trimester of pregnancy and immediately after delivery [69]. Its symptoms range from mild (e.g., itching, discomfort) to severe (e.g., prolonged bleeding). Because these symptoms typically decrease after delivery, conservative treatment is primarily administered during pregnancy. Their treatment includes conservative local therapy, dietary modifications, lying on the left side, and preventive measures [70]. The utilization of warm sitz baths may be more effective than anorectal creams for recovery from hemorrhoidal symptoms [71]. Surgical treatments such as hemorrhoidectomy are rarely necessary and are used only in cases of strangulated or extensively thrombosed hemorrhoids or intractable bleeding. If necessary, surgical procedures such as thrombectomy, local excision, or hemorrhoidectomy can be performed on pregnant women without compromising the safety of the fetus [72, 73]. During pregnancy and immediately after delivery, hemorrhoidal surgery should be reserved for cases with severe complications that are refractory to medical treatment.

Special situation 2: Immunocompromised patients

In immunocompromised patients, HD should be conservatively managed because of the risks of sepsis and poor wound healing associated with anorectal procedures [74]. In this patient population, sclerotherapy is regarded as a safe alternative treatment for hemorrhoidal bleeding, when necessary [75, 76]. If sclerotherapy is not feasible, conservative treatment could be considered for patients with grade I, II, and III symptomatic hemorrhoids, whereas hemorrhoidectomy could be considered for patients with grade IV or complicated hemorrhoids.

Special situation 3: Patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs

Patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs often present with bleeding hemorrhoids. However, the bleeding episodes are usually self-limiting and do not require drug discontinuation or reversal of their medications. The majority of patients with low-grade (grade I or II) HD respond to conservative treatment. In cases of persistent bleeding, in addition to drug discontinuation, sclerotherapy also could be considered. Rubber band ligation (RBL) is relatively contraindicated in patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs because of the risk of catastrophic rectal bleeding after the eschar is cast off. If RBL or hemorrhoidectomy is required for high-grade or complicated HD, anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications should be reviewed and preferably discontinued in the perioperative period, depending on the indications for treatment and the risk of discontinuation [9].

However, there is a risk of so-called rebound hypercoagulability in patients who undergo cessation of anticoagulants and then restart them, thus increasing the risk of adverse thrombotic events. In patients suffering from refractory hemorrhoidal bleeding who are unable to stop anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs, injection sclerotherapy with polidocanol foam [46] or ALTA [47], or sclerobanding [77] may be safe and effective treatment options, although further high-quality evidence is needed. Transanal hemorrhoidal dearterialization [78] has been proven safe in a cohort of patients mainly taking acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), so it cannot be recommended for patients taking other anticoagulants.

Special situation 4: Patients with portal hypertension

Patients with portal hypertension should be differentiated in diagnosis between rectal variceal bleeding and hemorrhoidal bleeding [79]. In patients with portal hypertension, rectal variceal bleeding is commonly treated with suture ligation or medications, whereas hemorrhoidal bleeding is commonly treated conservatively. Patients who do not respond to conservative treatment are usually treated using sclerotherapy. In these patients, RBL is also contraindicated because of its risk of delayed bleeding. Patients who do not respond to sclerotherapy often require suture.

Recent studies suggest that treatments including RBL, injection sclerotherapy with various agents, hemorrhoidopexy, and the emborrhoid technique [80] are effective and safe for this population [81, 82]. The findings of these studies may necessitate a shift in our approach to the care of these patients; however, further investigation is required to substantiate these claims.

Special situation 5: Patients with IBD

In patients with IBD, hemorrhoidal surgery is often associated with major complications, including sepsis, stenosis, fistulas, fecal incontinence, nonhealing wounds, and even a need for proctectomy. Because of the high complication rate, the majority of patients with IBD who are present with symptomatic HD are conservatively managed with a high-fiber diet, oral hydration, warm sitz baths, and oral phlebotonics. In addition, IBD flares should be controlled. Some patients who are present with symptoms refractory to medical therapy may undergo surgical treatment if their IBD is under control. These patients should be informed of the high risks of any intervention. For this patient population, no optimal surgical treatment is yet available, and the majority of findings are from small retrospective studies conducted many years ago [83]. Surgeons often prefer conventional hemorrhoidectomy or minimally invasive techniques such as RBL or hemorrhoidal artery ligation. Hemorrhoidopexy may be associated with life-threatening complications [84].

So far, no consensus has been reached regarding the exact indications for surgery in patients with IBD and HD. D’Ugo et al. [83, 85] described that the first-line management for these patients should involve medical therapy given that spontaneous healing is possible. Despite the high risk of complications in patients with IBD, in nonresponsive patients, surgical options in selected patients can be considered for achieving acceptable results [86]. In cases of IBD, surgery is recommended only during the remission phase.

Conclusions

Consensus on the management of HD

Figure 2 is the schematic of Taiwan Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons HD consensus. Conservative treatment is appropriate for the majority of patients with low-grade HD. Although oral phlebotonics may ameliorate the symptoms of HD, dietary and lifestyle modifications remain the first step of treatment for achieving regular defecation with soft stools. Patients in Taiwan may also try using traditional herbs/medicines to relieve hemorrhoidal symptoms. In clinical practice, the use of these agents is not recommended based on current evidence. According to the clinical data of patients with HD, oral MPFFs are suitable and effective for all grades of HD. Surgical treatment for HD is often reserved for patients with severe conditions or patients who do not respond to conservative treatment. Postoperative MPFFs in patients with advanced HD who have undergone surgery may be effective in eliminating associated discomfort. In Taiwan, sclerotherapy is rarely performed due to lack of sclerosing agents and national insurance coverage. In this consensus, we recommend alternative treatment strategies, especially for immuno-compromised or cirrhotic patients. In addition, due to the high incidence of CRC in Taiwan, CRC screening tests should be considered in patients with unusual clinical presentation, such as bowel habit changes, refractory or unusual bleeding sites.

Overall, the treatment outcomes of patients with HD are often complicated by certain clinical conditions, such as pregnancy, IBD, thrombosed HD, cirrhosis, and strangulated HD. Therefore, caution should be exercised when performing any outpatient procedures on these patients to avoid potentially fatal outcomes, and conservative treatment should be used as the first line of treatment.
