Seasonal variation in climate, air pollution, and the incidence of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo
Authors: Tzong-Hann Yang, Shiu-Dong Chung, Herng-Ching Lin, Tsai-Ching Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) represents the most prevalent etiology of vertigo encountered in clinical practice. This study aims to evaluate the associations of seasonality in climate, air pollution, and the incidence of BPPV in Taiwan from 2002 to 2021. Data on BPPV cases were extracted from the Longitudinal Health Insurance Database 2010. We calculated the monthly incidence rates of BPPV episodes per 100,000 participants over a 240-month period, categorizing the data by gender and age groups: 18–44, 45–64, and 65 and above. Meteorological variables (temperature, atmospheric pressure, humidity, precipitation, sunshine duration) and air pollutants (NO2, SO2, CO, O3, PM10) were obtained from national monitoring networks. Auto-Regressive Moving Average (ARMA) models were employed to assess associations between environmental variables, seasonality, and BPPV incidence. BPPV incidence increased significantly over two decades across all age and sex groups. The ARMA regression models suggested that elevated temperature, atmospheric pressure, and NO₂ levels were significantly correlated with higher BPPV incidence, whereas sunshine duration showed no effect. Moreover, the ARMA regression models consistently show that Spring exhibited a greater monthly incidence of BPPV compared to winter across different sex and age categories. BPPV incidence shows significant population-level associations with climatic and air-quality factors. Elevated temperature, atmospheric pressure, and NO₂ exposure may be associated with otoconial instability, emphasizing the need to consider environmental conditions in BPPV prevention and clinical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52205-3.

Introduction

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) represents the most prevalent etiology of vertigo encountered in clinical practice, with an incidence reaching approximately 446.4 cases per 100,000 individuals1. Although it may manifest across all age groups, the highest incidence occurs in advanced age. A female predominance is consistently observed, with a male-to-female ratio of roughly 1:21. Clinically, BPPV is characterized by brief but often profoundly disabling episodes of vertigo, typically accompanied by positional nystagmus and nausea, which are precipitated by changes in head or body position relative to gravity2.

The precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying BPPV remain incompletely elucidated; however, two predominant hypotheses—cupulolithiasis and canalithiasis—are widely accepted to account for its clinical manifestations2. Under normal vestibular physiology, rotational acceleration is sensed when endolymphatic flow within the semicircular canals deflects the ampullary cupula, a gelatinous structure containing mechanosensitive hair cells. The cupulolithiasis theory posits that otoconial debris adheres to the cupula, thereby increasing its mass and rendering it abnormally sensitive to gravitational forces. This abnormal deflection of the cupula generates erroneous vestibular input, leading to the perception of motion. In contrast, the canalithiasis theory suggests that free-floating otoconial particles within the endolymph of a semicircular canal are displaced by gravity during head movements. The resulting endolymphatic currents induce pathological cupular deflection, giving rise to vertigo and nystagmus. The dislodged particulate matter is believed to consist of otoconia originating from the utricular otolithic membrane. Among the different subtypes, posterior canal BPPV is the most common, and canalithiasis is considered the principal underlying mechanism.

Despite the well-established pathophysiology of canalithiasis and cupulolithiasis, the underlying triggers for otoconial detachment remain largely idiopathic. In recent times, an increasing amount of attention has been directed towards environmental factors. Nevertheless, the impact of climate and air pollution on the incidence of BPPV remains a subject of debate. A plethora of epidemiological studies have yielded discordant results concerning the seasonality of BPPV3,4. A number of investigations have indicated a heightened incidence in winter, attributable to vitamin D deficiency or respiratory infections. In contrast, other studies have documented peaks in spring or an absence of significant seasonal variation. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests a correlation between air pollutants such as NO₂ and inflammation, as well as otoconial instability. However, the majority of extant studies are constrained by several limitations. These include small hospital-based samples, short observation periods, and the inability to control for confounding meteorological variables simultaneously.

The objective of this study is to address these knowledge gaps by conducting a nationwide, population-based analysis in Taiwan over a 20-year period (2002–2021). In contrast to the preceding small-scale studies, the present study utilized the Longitudinal Health Insurance Database 2010 (LHID2010) to ensure the procurement of a substantial sample size, employing Auto-Regressive Moving Average (ARMA) models. This approach facilitates a robust evaluation of the dynamic associations between climate factors (temperature, pressure), air pollution (NO2, PM10), and BPPV incidence, thereby providing more precise insights for clinical prevention and public health strategies.

Materials and methods

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo data

We obtained outpatient claim data from the LHID2010, a component of the National Health Insurance Research Database, to examine the trends of BPPV in Taiwan across two decades. This research encompassed patients (aged ≥ 18 years) whose principal diagnosis was BPPV from January 2002 to December 2021, recognized via ICD-9 386.11 or ICD-10 code H81.10. Exclusion criteria were strictly applied as follows: (1) patients aged < 18 years were excluded to ensure a focus on adult BPPV trends; (2) individuals with missing or incomplete demographic data, such as age or sex, were removed; and (3) patients with inconsistent enrollment history that prevented accurate person-month calculation were excluded. While the LHID2010 database encompasses clinical encounters across all specialties, the cohort under scrutiny includes all patients diagnosed with BPPV, thus reflecting real-world healthcare-seeking patterns in Taiwan. In order to circumvent the inflation of incidence rates engendered by subsequent visits, multiple claims within a 30-day period were consolidated into a single episode. As stated in the AAO-HNSF Clinical Practice Guideline, the active phase of BPPV is known to typically span weeks. Visits occurring within 30 days are considered to be most likely to represent follow-up care for an unresolved episode or a recurrence within the same active period5. Therefore, the decision was taken to group all outpatient visits occurring within 30 days into a single episode, with a view to preventing multiple visits from being treated as distinct episodes. No cases were excluded based on clinical follow-up duration; however, the model dynamically adjusted for attrition (e.g., death or disenrollment) by updating the active beneficiary denominator each month.

Three patient age groups were recognized: individuals aged 18–44, 45–64, and 65 and older. This classification was employed to determine the monthly incidence rates of BPPV per 100,000 people nationally, while considering age and gender specifics, derived from the annual demographic information provided by the LHID2010 Registry for Beneficiaries over the same twenty-year period. A thorough grasp of the demographic distribution and effects of BPPV on different Taiwanese population segments was made possible by this approach.

This study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University (approval number 202412EM030) and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the study’s retrospective design, the Research Ethics Committee at National Taiwan University waived the requirement for informed consent.

Air pollution data

Taiwan’s Environmental Protection Administration (EPA) manages air quality monitoring databases that provide statistics on the average monthly levels of different air pollutants. The EPA operates 66 monitoring sites throughout the island to perform daily evaluations of air quality. We opted for monthly average data because it offers a more consistent depiction of air quality and mirrors exposure levels in surrounding regions. The main pollutants that are being observed are NO2, O3, CO, SO2, and PM10. Stations located in isolated or high-altitude areas, or those that did not provide data on all five pollutants, were omitted from our analysis. To determine the monthly average values for these major air pollutants, we utilized data from the 55 other sites. The daily average concentrations were aggregated to compute the monthly average values used in our time-series models.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data, such as air pressure, precipitation, sunshine duration, relative humidity, and ambient temperature, was collected from the 23 monitoring stations managed by the Central Weather Bureau. We first calculated the daily average across all stations and then aggregated these into monthly mean values for the analysis. This method allowed for a constant understanding of environmental aspects during the study period and provided a comprehensive picture of climatic conditions in numerous regions.

Statistical analysis

Due to the retrospective, population-based design of this study covering the entire insured population of Taiwan over a 20-year period (2002–2021), a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. However, the study encompassed 240 months of data with a substantial number of observed BPPV events, featuring an average monthly incidence of 42.57 per 100,000 participants. This large-scale dataset ensures sufficient statistical power to detect the associations between environmental factors and BPPV incidence reported in this study. We calculated the monthly incidence rates of BPPV episodes per 100,000 participants over a 240-month period, categorizing the data by gender and age groups: 18–44, 45–64, and 65 and above. The NHI program covers > 99% of the Taiwanese population, ensuring minimal loss to follow-up. In our analysis, we calculated incidence rates based on the active beneficiary population for each month. Beneficiaries who exited the insurance program during the study period were naturally removed from the denominator (the population at risk) for subsequent months, ensuring the incidence calculation remains accurate. This calculation dynamically adjusts for attrition (e.g., disenrollment or death) and naturally reflects the changing age structure of the cohort over time.

This approach allowed us to treat climate and pollution variables as external regressors while simultaneously adjusting for the long-term trend and seasonality. We utilized SAS software to select the optimal lag structure (p, q) for each ARMA model based on the minimization of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Schwarz Criterion (SC). This model selection process ensures parsimony and adequately addresses serial correlation in the residuals6,7. To ensure the validity of the ARMA models, residual diagnostics were performed using the Ljung–Box test to confirm that the residuals approximate white noise. A non-significant p-value (p > 0.05) in the Ljung–Box test, alongside the inspection of residual Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) plots, was used to verify that no significant serial correlation remained in the model residuals.

The large number of monthly observations (240 months) and the high number of BPPV events ensure adequate statistical power to detect seasonal associations. All meteorological and air pollution variables (e.g., ambient temperature in °C, atmospheric pressure in hPa, and NO₂ in ppb) were analyzed in their original continuous units without standardization. Consequently, the estimated γj coefficients for these covariates (Xt) reflect the absolute change in the monthly BPPV incidence rate per 100,000 people for every 1-unit increase in the respective environmental factor. We considered the findings significant at a two-tailed p-value of 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of the present study and the inherent correlation between meteorological and pollution variables, we did not apply Bonferroni corrections in order to avoid inflating Type II errors. However, the primary associations reported in this study yielded p-values < 0.001, indicating robust statistical significance even under stringent thresholds.

Results

Monthly incidence rates of BPPV

During this study period, spanning from 2002 to 2021, the monthly incidence rates of BPPV (per 100,000 people) varied between a low of 19.24 and a high of 64.56, resulting in an average of 42.57 and a standard deviation of 10.77 (as presented in Table 1). The average monthly BPPV incidence rate per 100,000 people for males was 28.25 (± 6.78), while the average rate for females was 56.17 (± 14.73). The monthly incidence rate of BPPV per 100,000 people in the age groups of 18–44, 45–64, and ≥ 65 years were 17.63 (± 5.02), 49.28 (± 10.03), and 113.16 (± 16.60), respectively. Furthermore, the average monthly values recorded were CO at 0.48 ppm, NO2 at 15.77 ppb, SO2 at 3.62 ppb, PM10 at 50.85 µg/m3, O3 at 29.49 ppb, ambient temperature at 21.99 °C, relative humidity at 78.21%, atmospheric pressure at 979.57 hPa, rainfall of 188.41 mm, and monthly sunshine of 148.87 hours.

The outcomes from ARMA regression models, sorted by sex and age group, are shown in Tables 2 and 3, which analyzed the effect of the seasons on the monthly incidence rates of BPPV. These models incorporated climate and air pollution factors along with the calendar seasons to evaluate their influence on monthly incidence rates of BPPV. As indicated in Tables 2 and 3, there was a statistically significant increase in the monthly incidence rates of BPPV among different sex and age groups over the 20-year study period. Furthermore, the ARMA regression models revealed that temperature and atmospheric pressure were significantly related to the monthly occurrence of BPPV episodes across various sex and age groups, even when considering trends, seasonal fluctuations, climatic factors, and other air pollution variables. Moreover, the ARMA regression models consistently show that Spring exhibited a greater monthly occurrence of BPPV episodes compared to winter across different sex and age categories. In other words, seasonality exists in the monthly occurrence rates of BPPV after adjusting for BPPV trends, along with climate and air pollution influences.

Residual diagnostics indicated that the final models for all demographic groups achieved white noise residuals. For the total population and the ≥ 65 years age group, the model orders were refined to ARMA(1,1) and ARMA(2,0), respectively, to satisfy the white noise assumption (Ljung-Box p = 0.9446 and 0.1245, respectively). The updated regression coefficients for these refined models remained consistent with our primary findings, showing that temperature and atmospheric pressure are significantly associated with BPPV incidence.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated a notable upward trend in the incidence of BPPV between 2002 and 2021, encompassing all sexes and age groups (18–44, 45–64, and ≥ 65 years). Ambient temperature was found to have a significant positive correlation with BPPV across the total population, both sexes, and all age categories. Likewise, atmospheric pressure exhibited a significant positive association with BPPV incidence, particularly among individuals aged 45–64 years and ≥ 65 years. In addition, NO₂ exposure was significantly associated with an increased risk of BPPV across all patients, with a stronger effect observed in females and the middle-aged and elderly subgroups, indicating that air pollution may represent an important environmental factor associated with the occurrence of BPPV. Analysis of seasonal variation further revealed that spring was consistently associated with a significant increase in BPPV incidence across all demographic groups, whereas summer showed a smaller but significant rise limited to the total population and males.

Association between ambient temperature and BPPV

Our study demonstrated that higher ambient temperatures were significantly associated with an increased incidence of BPPV across the total population, both sexes, and all age groups. In contrast, several previous investigations—including those by Korpon et al.8, Saeed and Omari9, Mariani et al.10, and Cao et al.11—reported a significant negative correlation between temperature and BPPV occurrence. Conversely, studies by Zach et al.12 and Sideris et al.13 found no significant relationship between these variables. These discrepancies may reflect regional climatic differences, population characteristics, or variations in study design and temporal exposure assessment.

It is hypothesized that high ambient temperatures may contribute to the development of BPPV through multiple interacting mechanisms. Heat may induce dehydration and electrolyte imbalance in the endolymph and subsequent cause fragments and remnants of otoconia14, while fluctuations in vitamin D and calcium metabolism may weaken otoconial stability15, predisposing to their dislodgement from the utricle. Additionally, vascular changes and transient labyrinthine hypoperfusion under thermal stress, along with oxidative and inflammatory effects of temperature-related air pollutants, may further compromise inner ear integrity. Combined with behavioral and postural changes common in hot weather, these factors can collectively facilitate otoconia migration into the semicircular canals, triggering BPPV episodes.

Association between atmospheric pressure and BPPV

Our study found that higher atmospheric pressure (AP) was significantly associated with an increased incidence of BPPV across all groups, except for individuals aged 18–44 years, in whom the association was not significant. This finding is consistent with previous studies by Korpon et al.8, Saeed and Omari9, and Cao et al.11, which also demonstrated a positive correlation between atmospheric pressure and BPPV incidence. In contrast, Sideris et al. reported no significant relationship between atmospheric pressure and the occurrence of BPPV13.

The underlying mechanism behind the positive association between atmospheric pressure and the incidence of BPPV remains unclear and not yet fully elucidated. Both Korpon et al.8 and Cao et al.10 proposed that elevated AP might affect inner ear pressure, potentially contributing to otoconial dislodgement and subsequent vertigo. Their theory draws on earlier reports describing pressure-sensitive ion channels and valve-like structures that regulate inner ear pressure in response to external barometric changes16–18. However, while these prior studies discuss the anatomical and physiological plausibility of such valves, they do not provide conclusive evidence that increased AP directly elevates inner ear pressure or causes BPPV. Thus, the relationship remains biologically plausible but speculative. Moreover, Cao et al. and Korpon et al. noted that migraine and Ménière’s disease—both linked to fluctuations in atmospheric pressure—share overlapping mechanisms with BPPV. The coexistence of BPPV with these pressure-sensitive disorders may support the hypothesis of common pathophysiological pathways mediated by changes in inner ear pressure19,20. Although the proposed relationship between AP and BPPV remains biologically plausible, further research is warranted to clarify the mechanistic basis and determine whether pressure variations directly contribute to otoconial instability and canalith migration.

Association between NO2 and BPPV

Among air pollutants, NO₂ showed the strongest association with an increased incidence of BPPV, which is in line with Mun et al.21. Prior studies have demonstrated that airborne NO₂ can reach the middle ear via Eustachian tube22 and diffuse through the round window membrane into the inner ear23, a structure permeable to small molecules such as NO₂21. Once dissolved in the perilymph and endolymph, NO₂ may form acidic compounds that could potentially lower the pH of the inner ear environment. Experimental evidence suggests that such acidification may be associated with otoconia instability and weakens their attachment to the otolithic membrane, leading to otoconial detachment14. While speculative in the context of this epidemiological study, this process may underlie the pathophysiological mechanism linking elevated NO₂ exposure to increased BPPV incidence, although further studies are warranted to confirm this hypothesis.

Seasonality

Our study found that the incidence of BPPV increased significantly during the spring season across all patients, both sexes, and all age groups. In contrast, summer was associated with an increased incidence only in the total population and in males. These findings are consistent with earlier reports by Whitman and Baloh et al., who observed a markedly higher number of cases in March, April, and May compared with other months25. Similarly, Saeed and Omari et al. noted significant seasonal variation, with the highest number of cases in March and the lowest in July9, while Meghji et al. reported significantly more cases from January to June than from July to December, with a clear peak in March through May26.

However, other studies have presented differing seasonal trends. Zach et al. found the highest incidence in winter, followed by spring, with summer showing the fewest cases12. Zuma e Maia et al. reported significantly more cases in winter and autumn than in summer or spring27, and Shu et al. observed a winter peak compared with summer, though the difference between winter and autumn was not significant28. Mariani et al. also documented a winter predominance, peaking in January and declining through spring and summer, reaching a minimum in August10. Meanwhile, Sideris et al. noted an increase during autumn13, whereas Jeong et al. and Seidel et al. found no significant seasonal variation in BPPV occurrence29,30. While our findings support a spring predominance, seasonal variation in BPPV may differ across studies due to geographic, climatic, and demographic factors, as well as differences in study design and exposure timing.

The seasonal predominance of BPPV in spring is likely driven by an interplay of metabolic, environmental, and inflammatory mechanisms that destabilize otoconia and alter vestibular homeostasis. During winter, reduced sunlight exposure has been demonstrated to reduce vitamin D synthesis, leading to subtle disturbances in calcium metabolism and otoconia mineralization31. Vitamin D plays a pivotal role in calcium metabolism, which is critical for the maintenance of otoconial density and stability. The onset of spring has been demonstrated to result in a rapid restoration of vitamin D levels. Such fluctuations in calcium equilibrium have been demonstrated to render individuals susceptible to otoconia detachment from the utricle. The “spring peak” observed in this study may be indicative of the delayed effect of winter Vitamin D deficiency, resulting in compromised otoconial integrity that manifests as BPPV in the subsequent months.

Concurrently, allergic inflammation—common during the pollen season—may involve the Eustachian tube and middle ear, disrupting pressure regulation and labyrinthine microcirculation. These effects are often compounded by frequent springtime barometric and temperature fluctuations, which perturb endolymphatic pressure balance and enhance mechanical stress on the inner ear32. Moreover, postviral vestibular irritation following winter infections and increased physical activity or head movement in spring may further facilitate otoconial migration into the semicircular canals33, collectively contributing to the elevated incidence of BPPV during this season.

This study demonstrates several notable strengths. Foremost, it benefits from the use of a large, real-world, nationwide population-based dataset, providing a highly representative sample of patients and enhancing the generalizability of its findings to the broader population. The study also capitalizes on the strengths of Taiwan’s comprehensive NHI system, which offers universal, affordable, and easily accessible healthcare with low copayments. This system minimizes potential bias related to socioeconomic status or geographic location, thereby ensuring an inclusive and diverse study population. Moreover, the use of NHI claims data allows for the systematic capture of all BPPV cases across various levels of healthcare—from primary clinics to tertiary hospitals—without the socioeconomic barriers often seen in other healthcare settings. This design minimizes underreporting and avoids the recall bias commonly associated with self-reported surveys, thereby enhancing data accuracy and reliability.

Nonetheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the diagnosis of BPPV in this study relied on ICD codes without confirmation via the Dix-Hallpike maneuver, which is the diagnostic gold standard. This introduces potential misclassification bias. Specifically, other vestibular disorders listed in the differential diagnosis of BPPV, such as vestibular neuritis, may present with vertigo and could be miscoded as BPPV, especially by non-specialists. Since vestibular neuritis often occurs following viral respiratory infections which exhibit seasonal patterns, this overlap could potentially overestimate the magnitude of the “spring peak” observed in our study. However, given that BPPV is the most prevalent cause of vertigo and the 30-day episode grouping was applied, we believe the overall seasonal trends remain representative of the general BPPV population. Furthermore, the study population included patients who may have comorbid peripheral vestibular disorders, such as Menière’s disease or vestibular migraine. These conditions have been shown to share certain pathophysiological pathways with BPPV, including sensitivity to atmospheric pressure and potential seasonal fluctuations. The incorporation of these cases may introduce a potential confounding factor. Although BPPV remains the most prevalent cause of vertigo encountered in clinical practice, further studies utilizing more precise clinical data are warranted to identify idiopathic BPPV cases and confirm whether the observed environmental sensitivities persist in the absence of other vestibular comorbidities.

Second, the LHID2010 database does not include laboratory data (e.g., serum Vitamin D levels, bone mineral density scores (osteoporosis), and history of minor head trauma) or lifestyle information (smoking, physical activity). Consequently, direct adjustment for these potential confounders was not feasible, although age and sex stratification may partially account for the risks associated with osteoporosis.

Third, a significant limitation is that the LHID2010 identifies ‘healthcare-seeking events coded as BPPV’ rather than clinically confirmed incident cases verified by specialized vestibular testing or specific physician specialties. While procedure codes for vestibular tests exist in the database, relying solely on them might underestimate the incidence in primary care settings. Consequently, the observed seasonal trends may partially reflect patterns in healthcare-seeking behavior. For example, the ‘spring peak’ could be influenced by increased accessibility to clinics after winter or a higher propensity to seek care following seasonal respiratory infections. However, the consistent correlation with objective environmental variables like temperature and NO₂ suggests that these trends are driven by more than just behavioral shifts.

Fourth, the LHID2010 is known to mirror the national population, which underwent significant aging during the study period. The reported time trend may be indicative of population ageing, changes in healthcare-seeking behaviors, coding practices, or access, rather than a true change in incidence. To account for this confounding by population aging and other secular changes (e.g., coding practices), a deterministic time trend variable was included in all ARMA models. This approach, consistent with standard time-series methodology6,7, whereby long-term drifts are statistically partitioned, ensuring that the estimated environmental associations reflect short-term incidence fluctuations independent of the aging population structure.

Fifth, it is acknowledged that there is a high correlation between meteorological factors and air pollutants (e.g. temperature and ozone). Our collinearity analysis revealed high VIF values for temperature and atmospheric pressure, indicating a degree of redundancy between these meteorological factors (supplement). Such collinearity can inflate the variance of coefficient estimates, potentially complicating the interpretation of each factor’s independent effect. Therefore, these coefficients reflect population-level associations within the context of the overall meteorological environment rather than isolated, independent biological impacts.

Finally, this study utilized national average data for meteorological and pollution variables, which inherently introduces exposure measurement error by not accounting for urban-rural gradients or individual indoor-outdoor activity patterns. However, this non-differential misclassification is expected to bias the risk estimates toward the null, suggesting that the significant associations observed in this study may be conservative underestimates of the true effects.

In conclusion, this nationwide, population-based analysis revealed that the incidence of BPPV in Taiwan from 2002 to 2021 was significantly influenced by climatic and environmental factors, exhibiting distinct seasonal variation. The incidence of BPPV increased steadily over two decades and was positively correlated with higher ambient temperature, elevated atmospheric pressure, and increased NO₂ levels, whereas sunshine duration showed no significant association. A pronounced peak in BPPV occurrence was observed during the spring season across all age and sex groups, suggesting that temperature fluctuations, barometric pressure changes, and seasonal allergic or inflammatory responses may be associated with factors leading to otoconial destabilization and canalith migration. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of BPPV pathogenesis and highlight the importance of considering environmental and seasonal influences in disease prevention and clinical management. Further research integrating environmental exposures, metabolic regulation, and vestibular physiology is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms linking climate dynamics and BPPV, and to develop predictive and preventive strategies for susceptible populations.
