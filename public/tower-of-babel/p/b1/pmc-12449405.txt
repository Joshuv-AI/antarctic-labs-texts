Therapeutic potential of Bacillus sonorensis PMC204 membrane vesicles against drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Youngkyoung Lee, Hoonhee Seo, Soyeon Lee, Dongsic Choi, Sukyung Kim, Md Abdur Rahim, Ho-Yeon Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis remains a severe global health threat, exacerbated by the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Despite the urgent need for effective interventions, the development of anti-tuberculosis drugs has been slow, and the emergence of pan-drug-resistant strains underscores the critical need for innovative therapeutic strategies. This study introduces Bacillus sonorensis PMC204, a novel probiotic strain with potent anti-tuberculosis properties identified through extensive screening. PMC204 significantly reduced M. tuberculosis H37Rv and XDR strains within Raw 264.7 macrophage cells. Moreover, membrane vesicles (MVs) derived from this strain exhibited superior inhibitory effects against both standard and XDR strains of M. tuberculosis. Proteomic analysis of the isolated MVs revealed a high abundance of flagellin proteins, which are hypothesized to play a pivotal role in the observed anti-tuberculosis effects. These findings also suggest a close link between the therapeutic efficacy of PMC204 and autophagy activation. Safety assessments further demonstrated the feasibility of PMC204 as a potential anti-tuberculosis therapeutic. The anti-tuberculosis activity of bacterial MVs represents an innovative approach in microbiome therapeutics, positioning PMC204 as a next-generation probiotic distinct from conventional strains. This study contributes to advancing the field of microbiome-based therapeutics and presents promising avenues for managing drug-resistant tuberculosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00430-025-00851-1.Keywords: Bacillus sonorensis PMC204, Next-generation probiotics, Drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Membrane vesicles (MVs), Autophagy

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that in 2023, approximately one-quarter of the global population was estimated to be infected with tuberculosis [1]. The COVID-19 pandemic continues to impact access to TB diagnosis and treatment negatively, exacerbating the burden of TB disease [2]. Drug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) remains a significant global health issue, posing an ongoing threat to human life [3]. The need for tuberculosis elimination is particularly critical in developing countries and regions with high rates of tuberculosis [4]. Furthermore, developing new drugs is crucial due to the limited treatment options available for those infected with drug-resistant tuberculosis [5]. Although recent decades have seen the introduction of new treatments for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, the options for patients remain limited [6]. This issue is particularly urgent given the rapid increase in antimicrobial resistance among pathogens that compromise existing microbial treatments [7]. The growing antimicrobial resistance further exacerbates challenges in managing tuberculosis effectively [8]. For these reasons, developing novel concept pharmaceuticals is essential, with probiotics emerging as a promising alternative.

According to the WHO, probiotics are live microbes that can benefit the host’s health when consumed sufficiently [9]. Typically located in the digestive tract or gut, probiotics support healthy digestion, promote intestinal balance, and enhance the immune system, contributing to health benefits [10]. Probiotic therapies have gained prominence in recent years as potential promoters of host health. Probiotics secrete various metabolites during their lifecycle and interact with the host, possibly yielding beneficial health effects [11]. They may positively impact human health by influencing inflammation-related diseases or microbial infection risks and inhibiting pathogen growth within the host's microbial community via adherence to host cell receptors or specific surfaces in target niches [12]. Ongoing research aims to utilize probiotics to address diseases that pose threats to human health, treating a range of metabolic diseases [13] and bolstering the immune response in allergic diseases [14], particularly in difficult-to-treat, antibiotic-resistant forms of tuberculosis [15]. Information on the microbiome facilitates early disease detection and prognosis in at-risk populations, while microbiome modulation enables safe, effective, personalized treatments [16]. Previous studies have investigated the anti-tuberculosis properties of probiotics, but this research is still in its early stages, and the mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear.

This study aims to explore innovative microbiome-based therapeutic strategies for combating drug-resistant tuberculosis. We introduce Bacillus sonorensis PMC204, a novel probiotic strain identified for its potent anti-tuberculosis properties through comprehensive screening. By investigating its effects on Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains, including multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) variants, as well as analyzing the role of its membrane vesicles (MVs), this research aims to establish PMC204 as a next-generation probiotic distinct from conventional strains. Furthermore, the study delves into the mechanistic basis of its therapeutic efficacy, particularly its link to autophagy activation, and assesses its safety profile. Altogether, these findings advance the field of microbiome therapeutics and highlight PMC204 as a promising candidate for managing drug-resistant tuberculosis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of probiotics

The Probiotics Library was supplied by the School of Medicine at Soonchunhyang University, along with the Human Microbiome Medical Research Center (HMMRC) and Probiotics Microbiome Commercialization Center (PMC) at the Korean Gut Microbiome Bank (KGMB), Korea. All probiotic strains were retrieved from their stock vials and stored at -80 °C. Probiotics were inoculated at 0.1% into de Man Rogosa Sharpe (MRS) broth (BD Difco) and incubated at 37 °C overnight in a shaking incubator (BioFree) or an Anaerobic microaerophilic chamber (Concept 400 M, Baker Ruskinn, UK). Post incubation, bacterial growth was confirmed by measuring the optical density at 600 nm using a portable spectrophotometer (DR 1900, Hatch). The cultures were then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min (Combi R515, Hanil Scientific, Inc. Korea), the supernatants were removed, and the pellet was washed three times with a 0.85% NaCl solution, resulting in a concentrated solution of approximately 1 × 109 CFU/ml. Finally, the samples were transferred to a lysing matrix B tube (MP Biomedicals, USA) containing 0.1 mm zirconia/silica beads for bacterial cell lysis through bead-beating (FastPrep-24 5G, MP Biomedicals, USA). A sterile syringe filter with a pore size of 0.22 μM (Corning, Germany) collected cell extracts devoid of visible particles.

M. tuberculosis strains and cell lines

M. tuberculosis H37Ra (ATCC 25177) and M. tuberculosis H37Rv (ATCC 27294) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). H37Ra-GFP was developed using the prescribed methods [17]. Extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strain (KMRC 00203–00197) of M. tuberculosis was acquired from the Korean Mycobacterium Resource Center (KMRC) (Chungbuk, Korea). Experiments involving M. tuberculosis were conducted at the Soonchunhyang University Animal Biosafety Level 3 Laboratory (ABSL-3, KDCA-20–3-04). M. tuberculosis was cultivated in a shaking incubator at 37 ℃ in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (BD Difco, USA) enriched with 10% OADC (oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase) (BD Difco, USA) and 0.5% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Raw 264.7 cells (KCLB 40071) were sourced from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Korea) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1% antibiotics (100 U/ml of penicillin and 100 µg/ml of streptomycin) (HyClone, USA) in a CO₂ incubator at 37 ℃. The anti-tuberculosis screening medium was devoid of antibiotics.

16S rRNA gene sequencing-based identification

Bacterial growth was monitored using a portable VIS spectrophotometer at an optical density (OD) of 1.0 at 600 nm. Total DNA was extracted from each sample using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was prepared and sent to Biofact (Korea) for bacterial species identification through 16S rRNA sequencing. The 16S rRNA gene was sequenced following standard laboratory protocols at Biofact (Korea). Genomic DNA was isolated using a protocol involving repeated heating and ice cooling. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out using a Hushrun PCR cycler (Biofact, Korea) with primers 27F (5'-AGA GTT TGA TCC TGG CTC AG-3') and 1492R (5'-GGT TAC CTT GTT ACG ACT T-3'). The PCR products were purified and sequenced using an ABI PRISM 3730XL DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sequence data were analyzed and matched to sequences in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database using the BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) algorithm.

Phenotypic characterization based on metabolic profiling

The ability of the isolates to produce acid from various carbon sources was evaluated using the API 50 CHB test (BioMérieux SA, Lyon, Marcy l’Étoile, France), conducted per the manufacturer’s directions. Bacterial suspensions were prepared and inoculated into API 50 CHB strips housing 50 different carbohydrates. The strips were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, and outcomes were recorded based on color changes that indicated acid production from each carbon source. This assessment allowed the phenotypic characterization of the isolates based on their metabolic profiles.

Whole-genome sequencing

To identify new drug candidate PMC204 at the strain level, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analysis was performed to identify, compare, and classify microorganisms. Before proceeding with WGS sequencing, PMC204 probiotics were cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h, centrifuged to obtain a bacterial pellet, and sent to a commercial DNA sequencing service (ChunLab) for WGS. PacBio sequencing data were assembled using PacBio SMRT Analysis 2.3.0 with the HGAP2 protocol (Pacific Biosciences). The resulting contigs derived from PacBio sequencing data were circularized using Circlator version 1.4.0 (Sanger Institute). Sequence reads and assemblies have been stored in the NCBI database under Bio-Project ID: PRJNA735892. Predicted coding sequences (CDSs) were categorized according to the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) functional classification system [18].

Confocal-based screening for anti-tuberculosis efficacy

Imaging was conducted using an LSM800 confocal microscope (43X objective; Carl Zeiss, Germany). Raw 264.7 cells were cultured on a glass-bottom 96-well plate (Glass bottom black plate, Cellvis, USA) at a cell density of 5 × 105 cells/ml and were infected with Green fluorescent protein (GFP)-H37Ra (MOI 1:3). Following a 2-h infection, the cells were washed thrice and treated with various concentrations of probiotic extract samples, then incubated for 72 h. Post-incubation, the cells were washed three times with 0.85% NaCl solution. SYTO59 red-fluorescent nucleic acid staining solution (5 µM/ml; SYTO59; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was then added, the sample incubated for 30 min while protected from light, and subsequently, the staining solutions were removed, and the cells were rewashed thrice with 0.85% NaCl solution. SYTO59 red-fluorescent nucleic acid stain (5 µM/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added, and cells were incubated for 30 min in the dark. The staining solution was removed, and the cells were rewashed three times before imaging. Two separate confocal imaging experiments were performed. The preliminary screening was designed to visually assess the probiotic candidates’ relative intracellular anti-tuberculosis efficacy. Standard antibiotic controls were not included in these experiments, as the primary goal was to compare probiotic-treated groups prior to dose–response validation. Comparative imaging and the use of antibiotics.

A parallel confocal imaging experiment was conducted using the same infection and staining protocol to provide a visual comparison with standard anti-tuberculosis drugs. Isoniazid (INH), rifampicin (RIF), and pyrazinamide (PZA), purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), were used to treat infected macrophages. Finally, the glass-bottom 96-well plate was mounted on a motorized stage.

Intracellular anti-tuberculosis activity and MIC₉₀ assay

Raw 264.7 cells were cultured overnight in a 96-well plate (Glass bottom black plate, Cellvis, USA) at a cell density of 5 × 105 cells/ml and were infected with M. tuberculosis XDR (MOI 1:5) at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 2 h. Following infection, cells were washed three times with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) to remove residual extracellular M. tuberculosis. Infected cells were then treated with various concentrations of probiotic extract samples and drugs and incubated for 72 h. Post-treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and lysed using sterile distilled water. The number of viable bacilli was determined by plating serially diluted bacilli lysates on 7H10 agar plates. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC₉₀) was the lowest concentration that resulted in a ≥ 1.0 log₁₀ CFU/mL reduction compared to the untreated control. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Isolation of membrane vesicles derived from probiotic strain PMC204

We adhered to previously established methodologies and references to isolate membrane vesicles (MVs) derived from bacteria and to study the anti-tuberculosis effects of MVs from the probiotic strain PMC204 [19, 20]. The PMC204 strain was cultured in a food-grade medium (FGM) composed of edible ingredients deemed safe for human and animal consumption. This FGM medium, modeled after standard MRS medium, included components such as soy peptone, yeast peptone, glucose, Tween 80, magnesium sulfate, and pH-adjusted conditions. The initial experiments began with a 30 mL culture, which was scaled up using a fermenter system (FMT-ST-S07, Fermentec, Korea). The cultures were incubated in a 7L fermentation system (Fermentec, Korea) at 37 °C for 24 h, connected to a gas cylinder containing 5% H₂, 5% CO₂, and 90% N₂. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.8–0.9 or entered the stationary phase, cells (≥ 7.6 × 10⁸ CFU/mL of PMC204) were harvested by pelleting via centrifugation at 3515 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, repeated twice using a general-purpose centrifuge. The culture supernatant of PMC204 (≤ 1L) was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane filter (Bottle Top Filter, Millipore, USA). The concentrated conditioned medium was centrifuged using an ultracentrifuge (Optima MAX-XP, Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, USA) with an MLA-55 rotor at 150,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. The pelleted MVs were resuspended in PBS, and protein content was quantified using the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Characterization of PMC204-derived MVs

The purified MVs of B. sonorensis PMC204 were analyzed using linear gradient denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on a 4 to 12% Bis–Tris gel (Invitrogen, USA), with all samples prepared at the same protein concentration. Before gel loading, Bacteria Whole Cell lysate (WCL) samples were denatured by boiling in a reducing antioxidant agent (Nupage). The gel was subsequently stained with Coomassie Blue Stain Reagent (SumplyBlue SafeStain, Invitrogen, USA). MVs were examined using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) after staining with 2% uranyl acetate for negative staining. Uranyl acetate was applied to the grid, and the excess solution was removed with filter paper. The samples were carbon-coated on copper and plasma-cleaned for 30 s before being dried and viewed under a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) operated at 120kV (Bio-TEM, Tecnai G2 Spiri TWIN, FEI). Particle counts of MVs were confirmed using a Nano Sight NS300 system (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK), which measured the size and concentration of the particles. Samples were prepared in 1 × PBS buffer with measurements conducted at 25 °C across three 60-s sessions for each sample, using camera settings of level 16 and a threshold parameter of 5.

Proteomic analysis of MVs derived from B. sonorensis PMC 204

All analyses were repeated in three biological replicates. A total of 200 µg of MVs underwent methanol/chloroform precipitation, as described in previous research [21]. As indicated in the prior study, these precipitated proteins underwent in-gel digestion with trypsin [22]. The tryptic peptides were dissolved in 20 μL of buffer A (0.1%formic acid in water) and 5 μL was injected onto a trap column, Acclaim PepMap C18 nano Viper 100 (75 μm × 2 cm, 3 μm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 5 μL/min with 95% buffer A for 4 min, then analyzed in LC–MS/MS with an Orbitrap EclipseTM TribridTM mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, USA) coupled with an UltimateTM 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Loaded peptides were separated on an analytical column, PepMap RSLC C18 ES803A (75 μm × 50 cm, 2 μm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using a 180-min gradient from 5 to 90% of solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent Top 20 scans mode, alternating between MS and MS2. Analysis parameters included 10 ppm mass accuracy, 1850 V ion spray voltage, 275 ºC capillary temperature, m/z 375–1575 full scan resolution, and 120,000 higher-energy collisional dissociation activation scans with 35% normalized collision energy. The quadrupole isolation window was set at 1.4 Da. MS/MS spectra were detected on Orbitrap with a resolution of 30,000. The MS raw files were searched using the MaxQuant program (version 2.2.0.0) (Max Planck Institute, Germany) against the B. sonorensis strain’s protein database (release 2023_04) from UniProt. Tolerance settings were 20 ppm for both precursor and fragment ions. The trypsin digestion allowed for two potential missed cleavages. The employed modifications included a fixed carbamidomethylation of cysteine (57 Da) and variable modifications for deamidation of asparagine and glutamine (1 Da), oxidation of methionine (16 Da), and acetylation of the N-terminus (42 Da). All identified proteins were statistically analyzed using the Scaffold program (version 5.3.0), selecting only proteins with a Protein threshold ≥ 0.99 and a minimum of one peptide for further analysis. Normalized weighted spectra in the Scaffold program facilitated protein quantitation. Protein sequences were analyzed for interactions using String (v12.0) and Cytoscape (version 3.8.2), then mapped to the Gene Ontology (AmiGO 2) and KEGG database to predict biological processes and pathways. The results indicated significant enrichment of protein–protein interactions (PPI) (p < 1.0ⅹ10–16) and a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤ 0.05. Protein–protein interaction networks and protein sequences were analyzed using STRING (v12.0) and Cytoscape (v3.8.2).

Detection and analysis of autophagy

Acidic vacuoles were detected using Acridine Orange (AO), a pH-sensitive dye that accumulates in acidic compartments such as lysosomes and emits red fluorescence in these environments to investigate autophagy. Confocal imaging of AO-stained cells was performed with an LSM800 microscope (43X objective; Carl Zeiss, Germany), and fluorescence was quantified using Image J software. The excitation lasers were set at 502 nm for green fluorescence and 606 nm for red fluorescence, with emission filters at 525 nm and 659 nm, respectively. Additionally, autophagy gene expression was analyzed in PMC204-treated RAW 264.7 cells. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA protection bacteria reagent kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol, and quantified with a Qubit Fluorometer and Qubit RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA). RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Per the manufacturer's instructions, real-time PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green Supermix Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR detection system (Applied Biosystems, USA). RAW 264.7 cells were treated with M. tuberculosis or PMC204 cell extract at two different concentrations, and mRNA levels of autophagy-related genes (ATG5, ATG7, ATG12, and ATG16) were measured. The comparative Ct method normalized expression levels to the endogenous control gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

Assessment of cytotoxicity and acute toxicity of probiotic extracts in cell and animal models

Cytotoxicity and acute toxicity tests were conducted to evaluate probiotics’ safety. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the EZ-Cytox reagent (Dogen, Korea) through a Water-Soluble Tetrazolium Salt (WST) assay. Macrophages (1.5 × 105 cells/ml) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ overnight. Various probiotic extracts were added to each well and washed with 1 × PBS. EZ-Cytox reagent (20 μl) was added, incubated for 30 min, shaken for 1 min, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Toxicity in Animal Models was assessed by administering probiotics orally to animals once a day, 5 days a week, for 2 weeks. Animals were housed under controlled conditions (20–25 °C, 30–70% relative humidity, 12-h light–dark cycle). Two groups were compared: one treated with probiotics cultivated in MRS broth and the other with sterile saline as a control. Animals were monitored for clinical signs, mortality, and changes in body weight throughout the treatment period.

Measurement of nitrite and intracellular reactive oxygen species levels

Nitric oxide (NO) secretion was quantified by measuring nitrite (NO₂⁻) levels, indicative of NO synthesis, using the Griess reagent (Promega, USA). RAW 264.7 cells were cultured overnight at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL on a 96-well plate. The cells were exposed to M. tuberculosis H37Rv at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 2 h, followed by three washes with 1 × PBS and subsequent treatment with probiotic extract for 72 h. L-NG-mono-methylarginine (L-NMMA) was used as a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor. After incubation, 50 μL of the medium supernatant was transferred to new plates, mixed with 50 μL of Griess reagent solution, and the nitrite concentration was measured using a microplate reader after further incubation. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were determined in RAW 264.7 cells using the ROS H₂O₂ assay kit (Promega, USA). Cells were seeded in 80 μL monolayers on a 96-well plate (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Upon reaching 75 – 85% confluency, the cells were washed with warmed phosphate-buffered saline (1 × PBS) and exposed to M. tuberculosis H37Rv for 2 h. Menadione (Sigma Aldrich, USA), used as a positive control, was applied to the cells. The infected cells were then washed, treated with fresh medium containing probiotic extract, and incubated for 18 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of H₂O₂ substrate solution was added and incubated for 2 h. Finally, 100 μL of ROS-Glo detection solution was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at room temperature, and luminescence was measured using a Victor Nivo Multiplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons or Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons, as appropriate. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Confocal microscopy-based screening for anti-tuberculosis probiotic activity

A total of 56 probiotic strains from the HMMRC library, obtained from various sources, were utilized for the anti-tuberculosis activity screening (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1). The probiotic strains were cultured in fresh media under aerobic or anaerobic conditions, adjusted to 1 × 10⁹ CFU/ml, and tested in extract form using confocal microscopy. In the confocal results, green fluorescence indicated the survival of GFP-expressing Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and these values were quantified (Fig. 1A, C, E, G). The corresponding images are shown in Fig. 1B, D, F, and H. The results for probiotic extracts at a concentration of 1–2 × 10⁷ CFU/ml showed reduced GFP signals corresponding to M. tuberculosis survival, accompanied by cytotoxicity (Fig. 1A, B). Other strains also exhibited cytotoxicity, further diluting the probiotic extracts for additional testing. At 5 × 10⁶ CFU/ml, the extracts displayed similar patterns, but notably, the PMC204 strain demonstrated the most potent anti-tuberculosis activity without cytotoxic effects (Fig. 1C, D, E, F). For other strains, further dilution to a final concentration of 1.25–2.5 × 10⁶ CFU/ml was performed, but these continued to show GFP signal reduction along with cytotoxicity (Fig. 1G, H). Through this screening, the PMC204 strain was identified as the most effective probiotic with intense anti-tuberculosis activity and minimal cytotoxicity. This strain was selected for further experiments to investigate its anti-tuberculosis effects and therapeutic potential.

Identification of the PMC204 strain

The PMC204 strain, exhibiting superior anti-tuberculosis activity, was identified using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and BLAST analysis against the NCBI database (Fig. S1). Phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that PMC204 shares the highest similarity with Bacillus sonorensis (Fig. S1A), a finding further supported by sequence similarity analysis (Fig. S1B). Whole-genome sequencing of the strain was performed (Fig. 2), revealing a circular chromosome of 4,827,387 base pairs with an average GC content of 45.1% (Fig. 2A). Codon tree analysis using the PATRIC PGFam tool and PAxML program aligned single-copy genes for protein and coding DNA analysis. Results from the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) confirmed PMC204 as B. sonorensis (Fig. 2B). The genome annotation revealed 4880 coding sequences (CDS), encoding 4227 proteins categorized by COG functional classification (Fig. 2C). Of these, 2899 proteins exhibited identifiable biological functions. At the same time, 4296 CDSs showed homology with conserved proteins of unknown functions in other organisms. Additionally, 1069 virtual proteins showed no significant similarity to known proteins in current databases. The genome also contained 83 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes and 24 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. Comparative genomic analysis with other B. sonorensis strains (e.g., J41TS2, AB6, MGYG, HGUT-00227, AF04-49–2, and KCTC13918) revealed notable differences in genome size, GC content, predicted CDSs, and the number of rRNA and tRNA genes (Fig. 2D). These findings confirm PMC204 as a novel probiotic strain distinct from previously identified B. sonorensis strains.

Anti-tuberculosis effects of PMC204

The anti-tuberculosis effects of PMC204 were evaluated against M. tuberculosis strains (Fig. 3). Macrophages infected with the M.tuberculosis strain were treated with various concentrations of PMC204 probiotic extract, and the results were compared to anti-tuberculosis drugs and controls. The intracellular anti-tuberculosis effects of PMC204 against H37Rv and XDR strains were further confirmed through AFB staining and CFU assay, highlighting significant reductions in bacterial growth. Macrophages infected with the H37Rv strain were treated with various concentrations of PMC204 probiotic extract, and the results were compared to anti-tuberculosis drugs and controls. Treatment with PMC204 at 10 × 10⁶, 5 × 10⁶, 2.5 × 10⁶, and 1.25 × 10⁶ CFU/ml resulted in 86.0%, 86.4%, 75.6%, and 41.6% inhibition of H37Rv growth, respectively, compared to the untreated group. In contrast, isoniazid (INH) at 2.5 and 1.25 μg/ml achieved 53% and 7% inhibition, respectively. Control groups at 2.5 × 10⁶ and 1.25 × 10⁶ CFU/ml exhibited no anti-tuberculosis activity, with bacterial growth increasing by 17% and over twofold, respectively (Fig. 3A). Notably, PMC204 extract at 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL significantly reduced the intracellular bacterial load of XDR M. tuberculosis (log₁₀ CFU = 4.18, 95% CI 3.76–4.60), compared to INH at 25 μg/mL (log₁₀ CFU = 4.67, 95% CI 3.61–5.29). This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.048, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test) (Fig. 3B). These results support the superior intracellular anti-tuberculosis activity of PMC204 at higher concentrations compared to standard drug treatment. INH at 100 and 25 μg/ml reduced bacterial growth by 89.4%, 83.6%, and 66.9%, respectively. PMC204 extract at 1 × 10⁷, 5 × 10⁶, and 2.5 × 10⁶ CFU/ml reduced XDR strain loads by 89.4%, 84.7%, and 68.5%, respectively. However, no anti-tuberculosis effects were observed at 1.25 × 10⁶ and 0.6 × 10⁶ CFU/ml. Lower INH concentrations (12.5–3.12 μg/ml) also inhibited XDR strains compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3B). The intracellular anti-tuberculosis effects of PMC204 against H37Rv were further confirmed through AFB staining, highlighting significant reductions in bacterial growth (Fig. 3C). Confocal fluorescence quantification of GFP-H37Ra strains corroborated these findings, showing similar trends to those observed with AFB staining (Fig. 3D). These results collectively demonstrate PMC204’s potent anti-tuberculosis effects, particularly at higher concentrations, against both drug-sensitive and drug-resistant strains.

Minimum inhibitory concentration of PMC204 and PMC204-derived membrane vesicle

To assess the intracellular antimicrobial efficacy of PMC204 and its membrane vesicles (MVs), MIC assays were performed against extensively drug-resistant (XDR) TB clinical isolates. The MIC of PMC204 extract against H37Rv was 1.25 ± 0.21 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, while MVs displayed greater potency with a MIC of 1.0 ± 0.15 × 105 particles/mL. Although the MICs of isoniazid (0.2 µg/mL) and rifampicin (0.1 µg/mL) were lower in absolute terms against H37Rv, PMC204-based treatments exhibited measurable intracellular activity. Against the XDR strain, isoniazid and rifampicin showed MICs exceeding 1.6 µg/mL, consistent with a resistant phenotype. In contrast, PMC204 extract and MVs retained partial inhibitory effects, with MICs of 2.5 ± 0.30 × 10⁶ CFU/mL and 2.0 ± 0.25 × 105 particles/mL, respectively. The MIC₉₀ threshold, defined as a ≥ 1.0 log₁₀ CFU/mL reduction, was met at 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL for the extract and 1 × 10⁶ particles/mL for MVs (Table 1). Statistical analysis revealed significant reductions in intracellular bacterial counts in treated groups compared to the untreated control (p < 0.01). All results are based on triplicate independent experiments.

Isolation and anti-tuberculosis effects of membrane vesicles derived from PMC204

Membrane Vesicles (MVs) derived from PMC204 were successfully isolated. Their anti-tuberculosis effects were tested (Fig. 4). Using FGM, a food-grade medium, MVs were separated from the PMC204 strain, and the workflow is illustrated in Fig. 4A. The MVs isolated from PMC204 were characterized and quantified using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 4B). The analysis confirmed that the MVs consistently exhibited a nanoparticle structure. Furthermore, images of the vesicles obtained through ultracentrifugation (UC) and visualized using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) demonstrated a higher purity and more distinct visualization of capsid-shaped vesicles. The MVs’ mean size was 165.2 nm, with a concentration of 8.46×10⁸ particles/ml.

Notably, PMC204 extract at a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/ml and PMC204 MVs s at a concentration of 9 × 10⁶ particles/ml showed significant anti-tuberculosis effects against extensively drug-resistant (XDR) M.tuberculosis (Fig. 4C). While the inhibition rate of the extract was 77.8%, the efficacy of the MVs was particularly noteworthy, achieving an inhibition rate of 92%, surpassing that of 25 µg/ml INH (86%). The anti-tuberculosis activity of the MVs was dose-dependent (Fig. 4D). PMC204 extracts at concentrations of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/ml (85.3% inhibition) and 2 × 10⁷ CFU/ml (94.1% inhibition) exhibited concentration-dependent effects. Similarly, PMC204 MVs showed dose-dependent anti-tuberculosis activity, with 6 × 10⁶ particles/ml achieving 89.5% inhibition and 25 × 10⁶ particles/ml achieving 98.7% inhibition.

Given the XDR nature of the tuberculosis strain, inhibition with high concentrations of isoniazid (INH) and rifampin (RIF) was relatively low.

Proteomic analysis of PMC204-derived membrane vesicles

Proteomic analysis was conducted on the membrane vesicles (MVs) derived from PMC204 (Fig. 5). Proteins were isolated from the MVs and analyzed, revealing 186 common proteins identified across three distinct types of MVs (Fig. 5A). Key proteins detected include flagellin, peptidoglycan DL-endopeptidase CwlO, flagellar hook-associated protein 2, and autolysin inhibitor IseA (Table S3). KEGG pathway analysis showed that the MV-associated proteins are involved in pathways related to peptidoglycan biosynthesis, flagellar assembly, ribosome function, and RNA polymerase activity (Fig. 5B). In terms of Cellular Components, the identified proteins were associated with cellular anatomical entities, intracellular anatomical structures, protein-containing complexes, the extracellular region, and membrane rafts (Fig. 5C, Table S4). Regarding Biological Processes, the proteins were primarily related to cellular processes, metabolic processes, organic substance metabolic processes, cellular metabolic processes, and primary metabolic processes (Fig. 5D, Table S5). The proteins identified from PMC204-derived MVs exhibit a comprehensive network of interactions, highlighting their potential functional diversity.

Autophagy-inducing effects of PMC204

The autophagy-inducing effects of PMC204 were confirmed (Fig. 6). To detect lysosomes involved in autophagy, such as acidic vesicular organelles (AVOs), M. tuberculosis was treated with the PMC204 strain, and confocal microscopy was used for image analysis (Fig. 6A). The results showed that, as previously reported, INH and PRZ increased lysosome formation in a concentration-dependent manner. Similarly, PMC204 also induced lysosome formation in a dose-dependent manner. The relative fluorescence intensity and quantified values were presented (Fig. 6B). To further investigate autophagy-related changes, mRNA expression of autophagy-inducing genes, including ATG5, ATG7, ATG12, and the ATG16 complex, was found to be upregulated following PMC204 treatment (Figs. 6C–F).

Safety evaluation of PMC204 as a therapeutic agent

The safety of PMC204 as a therapeutic agent was evaluated through cytotoxicity and oral toxicity studies (Fig. 7). Cytotoxicity was assessed in RAW 264.7 macrophages using the WST assay (Fig. 7A). No cytotoxic effects were detected even at the highest tested concentration (1 × 107 CFU/mL). Furthermore, no changes in cell viability or morphology were observed under an inverted microscope after methylene blue staining (Fig. 7B). In a two-week repeated oral toxicity study, PMC204 was administered at doses of 5 × 109 CFU/mL/day to guinea pigs and 4 × 10⁸ CFU/mouse/day to Balb/c mice (Figs. 7C, D). No significant clinical signs, mortality, or weight loss were observed during treatment. Both treated and control groups showed similar general appearance and clinical signs. These findings support the preclinical safety of PMC204 for therapeutic applications.

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of PMC204

The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of PMC204 were evaluated in macrophages infected with M. tuberculosis (Fig. S3). Nitrite production was measured in M. tuberculosis-infected RAW 264.7 cells treated with varying concentrations of PMC204 extract (1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, 5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, 2.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, and 1.25 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) (Fig. S3A). After 3 days of infection, macrophages infected with M. tuberculosis showed significantly increased nitrite levels. Treatment with PMC204 extract markedly reduced nitrite levels compared to the untreated group. The antioxidant effect of PMC204 was assessed by measuring reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in M. tuberculosis-infected RAW 264.7 macrophages using a ROS H₂O₂ assay kit (Fig. S3B). ROS levels were significantly elevated in cells treated with menadione (positive control) or infected with H37Rv alone. However, treatment with PMC204 extract reduced ROS levels to levels close to the control, with no significant difference compared to the control group.

Discussion

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a pressing global health issue, exacerbated by the emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and extensive disruptions in TB control caused by the COVID-19 pandemic [23]. According to WHO reports, TB mortality, which had been declining by nearly 2% annually over the past two decades, surged by 3.6% in recent years, highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies [24]. Current treatments focus on inhibiting M. tuberculosis growth and inducing metabolic alterations through drug interventions; however, the growing prevalence of drug resistance severely hampers these efforts [25–27]. Resistant strains adapt by decreasing growth rates, enhancing efflux pump activity, and shifting metabolic pathways, particularly lipid metabolism, contributing to reduced drug efficacy and cell wall thickening [28, 29]. The widespread resistance to isoniazid and rifampicin, the cornerstone anti-TB drugs, underscores the critical need for new approaches to address MDR-TB [30]. Recent research highlights the microbiome’s potential in improving TB treatment outcomes. Evidence suggests probiotics may modulate immune responses and be biomarkers for diagnosis and control [31]. This study introduces Bacillus sonorensis PMC204, a novel strain isolated from Cheonggukjang, a traditional Korean fermented food, as a groundbreaking microbiome-based therapeutic agent. Whole-genome sequencing revealed unique chromosomal features of PMC204, distinguishing it from previously identified strains and supporting its designation as a new probiotic candidate [32]. This study presents a comprehensive initial characterization of Bacillus sonorensis PMC204, a newly identified strain with a genome size of 4,827,387 bp, confirmed through whole-genome sequencing [33]. The strain’s unique chromosomal features, distinct from those of other PMC204 strains, position it as a potential next-generation probiotic [34]. Beyond its genomic novelty, PMC204 demonstrates robust anti-tuberculosis effects, potentially mediated by its interaction with cellular metabolites, proteins, and biological processes to induce autophagy [35]. Although PMC204 and its membrane vesicles exhibited quantifiable intracellular anti-tuberculosis activity, they did not reach MIC₉₀ thresholds in broth culture, in contrast to rifampicin and streptomycin [36, 37]. This supports the notion that their direct bactericidal effects are limited in acellular environments, consistent with previous reports on similar probiotic-derived agents [38]. Interestingly, both PMC204 extract and MVs achieved ≥ 90% inhibition in macrophage infection models, suggesting a host-dependent mechanism such as autophagy activation or immune modulation [39, 40]. The identification of immunogenic proteins, including flagellin, in PMC204 further supports this hypothesis, as flagellin is known to activate innate immune signaling pathways such as TLR5 and NLRC4 inflammasome activation [41–43]. Since intracellular MIC values for clinical XDR isolates have not been standardized, and resistance profiles vary depending on the specific strain and patient origin, direct comparison with established critical concentrations should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the preserved intracellular efficacy of PMC204-based treatments against drug-resistant strains highlights their potential as adjunctive therapeutics [44]. The strain’s ability to combat drug-resistant TB underscores its significance as a next-generation probiotic and reinforces the growing importance of microbiome-derived pharmaceuticals [45]. PMC204-derived membrane vesicles (MVs) and lysate demonstrated potent anti-tuberculosis effects [46]. Proteomic analysis revealed that the efficacy of these vesicles is primarily attributed to their protein content [47]. Research on probiotic-derived exosomes is a burgeoning field, and this study provides critical insights into the role of these biologically active components in combating infectious diseases [48]. The use of PMC204-derived MVs represents an innovative approach to TB treatment, emphasizing the potential of probiotics as a source of therapeutic exosomes [49]. Proteomic analysis and TEM imaging identified flagellin as an abundant protein in PMC204-derived MVs, with significant anti-tuberculosis activity [50]. Previous studies highlight flagellin as a potent immunomodulatory agent and adjuvant for TB vaccine development [51]. While M. tuberculosis lacks flagella, flagellin’s ability to activate toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5) enhances innate immunity and induces robust adaptive immune responses [52]. Strategies such as the fusion of TB antigens with flagellin have demonstrated the potential to elicit strong immune responses [53]. These findings support the role of flagellin as a critical component in PMC204’s therapeutic efficacy [54].

To investigate PMC204’s potential to induce autophagy, cells were treated with varying concentrations of PMC204 extract [55]. The expression levels of autophagy-related genes (ATG5, ATG7, ATG12, and ATG16) were significantly elevated, and lysosomal activity increased in a concentration-dependent manner, mirroring the effects of isoniazid and pyrazinamide [56]. This indicates a strong association between PMC204 and autophagy induction [57]. Flagellin, a key protein in PMC204 MVs, may be crucial in triggering autophagy through TLR5 activation [58]. These findings establish a mechanistic link between PMC204, flagellin, and autophagy, offering insights into its anti-tuberculosis properties [59]. The anti-tuberculosis effects of PMC204 are further supported by its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which are closely tied to autophagy induction [60]. By reducing oxidative stress and modulating inflammatory responses, PMC204 enhances bacterial clearance and contributes to immune system regulation [61]. The interplay between flagellin, autophagy, and these protective mechanisms suggests a multifaceted approach by which PMC204 combats MDR-TB, providing additional therapeutic benefits [62, 63, 64].

PMC204 was cultured in a food-grade medium (FGM), a modified composition of the typical MRS medium designed for human and animal consumption [65]. The strain’s safety profile and robust anti-tuberculosis activity underscore its potential as a valuable candidate for probiotic-based drug development [66]. These findings highlight the broader implications of probiotics as safe, effective, and innovative therapeutic agents in addressing the challenges of drug-resistant TB. The PMC204 demonstrated no deleterious effects on cell viability and morphology. Furthermore, the compound exhibited no adverse impact on the overall health of animal models, as evidenced by stable body weight and normal physiological conditions during repeated oral administration [67, 68]. These findings highlight the potential of PMC204 as a safe candidate for potential therapeutic applications. Membrane vesicles (MVs), including those derived from PMC204, are rich in biologically active proteins and exhibit significant immunomodulatory and therapeutic potential [69]. This study underscores PMC204's promise as a novel therapeutic platform, offering an innovative microbiome-focused solution to the challenges posed by multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) [70]. Recent advancements have also reflected the growing interest in microbiome therapeutics, such as REBYOTA, which became the first microbiome-based therapy to receive US FDA approval in 2022 for recurrent Clostridium difficile infections [71]. These developments, combined with PMC204's potential effectiveness against drug-resistant TB, highlight the immense promise of microbiome-based pharmaceuticals as sources of new therapeutic agents [55, 72]. Furthermore, research into probiotics and extracellular vesicles (EVs) continues to support the utility of microbiome-based interventions in addressing infectious diseases.

Conclusion

Our study aimed to investigate the potential of the PMC204 strain as a next-generation probiotic with anti-tuberculosis efficacy. Specifically, the proteome of membrane vesicles (MVs) derived from the PMC204 strain was isolated and analyzed, demonstrating effectiveness in eradicating antibiotic-resistant M. tuberculosis (TB). The findings from the proteomic analysis, protein interactions, and biological processes are detailed in this report. The PMC204 extract and MVs exhibited remarkable anti-TB effects, particularly with pronounced reductions against extensively drug-resistant M. tuberculosis. Further investigation of the potential of the PMC204 strain as an anti-TB therapeutic is recommended, including in vivo experiments. This research should explore several avenues meriting further pursuit.
