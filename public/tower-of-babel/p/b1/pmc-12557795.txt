Mortality and associated factors among hospitalised COVID-19 patients in Lira regional referral hospital, a cross-sectional study
Authors: Augustine Ongoli, Bosco Opio, Marc Sam Opollo, Anne Ruth Akello, Francis Kiweewa, Bernard Omech
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) caused a global public emergency between 2020 and 2022 with various morbidity and mortality across the regions. While the impact in sub-Saharan Africa appeared relatively limited, data from regional referral hospitals remain scarce.ObjectiveTo determine the in-hospital mortality rate, risk factors and clinical characteristics of COVID-19 patients admitted to the COVID-19 treatment unit (CTU) at Lira Regional Referral Hospital (LRRH) in northern UgandaDesignCross-sectional study with the use of secondary dataSettingThis study was conducted at LRRH between January 2023 and December 2023. The data used were for patients admitted from May 2020 to March 2022.ParticipantRecords of 490 patients admitted with laboratory confirmed COVID-19 were collected and analysed. Selection was by simple census sampling technique. Inclusion criteria were moderately to critically ill patients and those with mild/asymptomatic infection but with comorbidities.ResultsOf the 490 participants, 52% were females and 41% were aged ≥60 years. The most common symptoms were cough (89.6%), difficulty in breathing (78.8%) and chest pain (69.3%). Hypertension (30%), diabetes mellitus (19.5%) and human immunodeficiency virus (10%) were the leading comorbidities. Severe and critical illness was observed in 40% and 7% of cases, respectively. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 29%. Factors significantly associated with reduced mortality included normal oxygen saturation (SPO2) (adjusted odds ratios (aOR) 0.11, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.44), normal body temperature (aOR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.99), absence of chronic liver disease (aOR 0.01, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.46) and younger age (31–45 years; aOR 0.14, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.74).ConclusionThe study revealed a high in-hospital mortality rate of 29% among COVID-19 patients admitted to the CTU at LRRH, primarily driven by severe disease presentation and limited access to critical interventions such as oxygen therapy. Independent predictors of survival included younger age, normal oxygen saturation, absence of chronic liver disease and normal body temperature at admission. These findings underscore the urgent need for early identification of high-risk patients and improved access to supportive care, particularly oxygen delivery systems, to reduce mortality in future outbreaks. Strengthening diagnostic capacity, clinical monitoring and preparedness for respiratory pandemics—alongside prospective studies capturing broader patient data—will be essential to refine response strategies and improve outcomes.

Background

COVID-19 is a respiratory disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus type 2 (SARS CoV 2), which led to a global pandemic with substantial morbidity and mortality between 2020 and 2022.1 In 2020, when mortality was at its peak, the overall pooled global case fatality rate (CFR) was approximately 10%, though this varied widely by setting. It was estimated at 1% in the general population, 13% among hospitalised patients, and up to 37% among those admitted to intensive care units.2

Certain preexisting conditions significantly increased the risk of severe illness and death from COVID-19. Mortality was particularly high among patients with cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory diseases, liver diseases, renal diseases and cancer.3 Globally, the CFR steadily declined as public health responses and clinical management improved, falling below 0.3% by August 2022.4 In sub-Saharan Africa, the CFR was approximately 3.0% during the same period.5

Treatment options for COVID-19 remain limited. For mild to moderate disease, antiviral agents such as nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and remdesivir have been recommended. In hospitalised patients with severe disease, immunomodulatory agents such as baricitinib and tocilizumab are used.68 Nevertheless, public health interventions—including mass vaccination campaigns, physical distancing, use of face masks, frequent hand washing and isolation of confirmed cases—remain the cornerstone of COVID-19 prevention and control.9

It has been observed that 75% of the people hospitalised with COVID-19 have at least one associated risk factor.10 The risk of adverse outcomes, including death and postacute sequelae of COVID-19, is significantly higher in older adults, pregnant women, males and individuals with comorbidities such as hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), sickle cell disease (SCD)/sickle cell anaemia (SCA), chronic liver disease (CLD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), cancer and chronic respiratory disorders.11 12

However, some studies have reported contradictory findings. For example, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection was not associated with worse COVID-19 outcomes in certain populations.13 Additionally, some reports indicated that women experienced higher COVID-19-related morbidity than men.14 This study intended to understand the scenario of COVID-19 at Lira regional referral hospital (LRRH) in Uganda.

Main objective

To determine the mortality rate, describe the clinical characteristics and factors associated with mortality of patients who were admitted with COVID-19 in the CTU at LRRH.

Specific objectives

(1) To determine the mortality rate of the patients hospitalised to the CTU at LRRH between May 2020 and April 2022. (2) To describe the clinical characteristics of COVID-19 patients admitted to the CTU at LRRH between May 2020 and April 2022. (3) To establish the factors associated with mortality among COVID-19 patients hospitalised in CTU at LRRH between May 2020 and April 2022.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cross-sectional study using secondary data obtained from patients records at LRRH between May 2020 and March 2022.

Study settings

The study was conducted at the CTU of LRRH located in Lira city east division, Lira city, northern Uganda. LRRH is a government-owned tertiary hospital that serves as the primary referral centre for nine districts in Lango sub-region. The CTU has a 30-bed capacity and managed a total of 795 patients with confirmed COVID-19 during the study period. Data abstraction was carried out between January and December 2023.

Target population

Patients suffering from COVID-19.

Study population

The study population consisted of all patients diagnosed with COVID-19 who were admitted to the CTU at LRRH during the specified study period.

Inclusion criteria were as follows:

Patients with a confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis by either reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction or COVID-19 rapid diagnostic test

Age 18 years and above

Admission to the CTU

Presence of COVID-19-related symptoms

Presence of at least one comorbidity

Exclusion criteria included:

Medical records missing 25% or having less than 75% of predefined variables in the data abstraction tool

Records with illegible handwriting, impairing accurate data extraction

Asymptomatic patients with no comorbidity

Data collection

Patient files were retrieved from the hospital records archive by the principal investigator, and eligibility was assessed individually. Trained research assistants (OA, WD and OE) supported data abstraction using a standardised tool. Data collected included:

Sociodemographic information

Age, sex, occupation, tribe and religion

Clinical presentation

Vital signs (blood pressure, pulse rate, temperature, blood oxygen saturation (SPO2)) and symptoms (cough, fever, myalgia, malaise, dyspnoea, general body weakness (GBW), etc.)

Comorbid conditions

Including HTN, DM, HIV, cancer, malaria, pregnancy, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, CKD, CLD, SCD and others

Disease severity classification

Asymptomatic—no symptoms; mild—constitutional symptoms without pneumonia on imaging; moderate—radiological evidence of pneumonia or tachypnoea; severe: pneumonia with low SPO2 or need for non-invasive oxygen therapy; critical—acute respiratory distress syndrome, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome, sepsis, septic shock or requirement of invasive ventilation

Treatment and outcomes

Interventions received and discharge status (recovered or deceased)

Strict confidentiality and data protection protocols were observed throughout the data collection process.

Data management and analysis

Anonymised data were entered and cleaned using STATA (version XX). Data entry included logic checks to minimise entry errors, and incomplete or inconsistent data entries were reviewed and corrected before final analysis.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics. Categorical variables (eg, outcome at discharge) were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations.

The independent associations between the dependent variables (recovered vs deceased) and the independent variable (eg, age, sex, comorbidities, smoking, use of corticosteroids) were initially tested using χ2 and the χ2 values reported.

To identify predictors of mortality, a bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, with results reported as crude odds ratios (cOR). Factors with p-values <0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to assess the strength and significance of associations. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by Gulu University Research Ethics Committee (GUREC-2022–357). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Reporting

The study findings are reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.15

Results

Recruitment and selection

Out of the 795 patients who were admitted to the CTU at LRRH, data were extracted from 490 eligible patient charts. A total of 150 patients below 18 years were excluded. Additionally, 25 records with less than 75% of the stipulated information in the data abstraction tool were excluded, as were 130 patients who were asymptomatic and did not have any documented comorbidity. The final analysis included 490 patients, 253 females and 237 males (see table 1 and figure 1).

The most common presenting symptoms were cough (369; 89.56%), difficulty in breathing (DIB) (293; 78.76%), chest pain (237; 69.3%), GBW (199; 63.38%) and fever (179; 61.3%). Notably, 179 patients (47.73%) presented with SPO 2<92%, which represented the lowest recorded SPO2 during hospitalisation (see online supplemental table 1).

Bivariate analysis of risk factors

Bivariate logistic regression was performed to assess the association between various clinical factors and in-hospital mortality. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05, and results are presented as cOR with 95% CI.

Patients aged ≥60 years were significantly more likely to die (cOR=6.47; 95% CI: 2.64 to 15.80; p<0.001).

Female patients had a lower likelihood of mortality (cOR=0.78; 95% CI: 0.53 to 0.61; p<0.001).

Normal SPO₂ (92–100%) was associated with significantly reduced mortality (cOR=0.21; 95% CI: 0.13 to 0.33; p<0.001).

A normal respiratory rate (12–18 breaths/min) was associated with reduced mortality (cOR=0.19; 95% CI: 0.12 to 0.32; p<0.001).

Patients who did not receive oxygen therapy had lower mortality (cOR=0.12; 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.21; p<0.001).

Non-use of corticosteroids was associated with reduced mortality (cOR=0.23; 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.37; p<0.001).

Patients without DM had lower mortality (cOR=0.41; 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.67; p=0.001).

Absence of HTN was also associated with lower mortality (cOR=0.46; 95% CI: 0.29 to 0.71; p=0.001) (see online supplemental table 2).

Multivariate analysis of data shows

A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to adjust for potential confounders. The following factors remained significantly associated with mortality:

Normal SPO₂ (92–100%) was strongly associated with lower mortality rate compared with low SPO2 (aOR=0.11; 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.44; p=0.002).

Normal body temperature was associated with lower mortality rate compared with abnormal temperatures (aOR=0.22; 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.99; p=0.049).

Absence of CLD was associated with a significantly decreased mortality rate (aOR=0.01; 95% CI: 0.001 to 0.46; p=0.017) (see online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

Clinical outcomes

Of all the patients admitted to the CTU of LRRH, the in-hospital mortality rate was 29%. This rate is considerably higher than that reported at Gulu regional referral hospital (GRRH), which recorded a mortality rate of 4.8% between March 2020 and October 2021.16 The lower mortality rate at GRRH could be attributed to their broader inclusion criteria, such as patients aged 12 years and above, including those with asymptomatic infection. In contrast, our study excluded children under 18 and asymptomatic individuals without comorbidities, who tend to have better outcomes. A study from a private hospital in Kampala reported an 11% mortality rate during June 2020 and September 2021, which is still lower than our findings.17 However, our mortality rate was lower than the 37% mortality rate reported at Mulago National Referral Hospital (MNRH) during Uganda’s second wave (May-July 2021),14 likely due to their focus on critically ill patients admitted to the high dependency unit.

These differences in mortality rate across Uganda’s CTUs could be explained by the difference in population characteristics and the period under consideration. Uganda experienced three waves of COVID-19, each dominated by different variants—Delta in the second wave, which was the most fatal, and Omicron in the third wave, known for high transmissibility but lower mortality.9 18 Our study spanned all three waves, providing a broader temporal snapshot of COVID-19 outcomes, but it is possible that mortality peaked during a specific period.

Of all the patients who were admitted to the CTU, 31% had mild diseases, 22% had moderate disease, 40% had severe disease and 7% had critical disease. The high mortality rate (29%) could also be attributed to the fact that many patients admitted to the CTU had severe cases of COVID-19 who were more likely to die. Notwithstanding, the availability and utilisation of lifesaving interventions at the facility at the time may have impacted on the outcomes of the patients.

Factors associated with mortality

The admission rates were 52% for females and 48% for males; this could be because women are naturally fearful and generally have better health-seeking behaviour compared with males. However, sex was not found to be associated with mortality, as opposed to other studies that reported that males have a higher mortality rate than females.19 20

Most patients (69%) were ≥46 years, with 41%≥60 years. Age above 60 was significantly associated with mortality. Individuals aged 45 years and below had an 86% decreased likelihood of death, consistent with existing literature, as younger patients generally have more robust immune responses and less comorbidity.14

The majority of patients presented with classic COVID-19 symptoms such as cough (90%), DIB (79%), chest pain (69%), GBW (63%) and fever (61%). These align with established clinical patterns. Ageusia and anosmia are some rare symptoms of COVID-19 with symptom sensitivities of less than 50% but almost everyone who presented with them without any other systemic disease had COVID-19, symptom specificities of over 90%.21

Notably, only 18% had documented hyperthermia (>37.5°C) at admission, although 61% reported fever. This discrepancy likely reflects the difference between recorded vital signs and self-reported symptoms across the disease course. Studies from China similarly noted that only 44% of hospitalised patients had fever on admission, despite nearly 90% developing it later. Fever at presentation was associated with worse outcomes, likely due to its role as a marker of cytokine storm and systemic inflammation.22

SPO2 was a strong predictor of mortality. Patients with normal SPO₂ ≥92% throughout hospitalisation had an 89% lower risk of death. This finding mirrors observations from Mulago Hospital and underscores the role of hypoxia in disease progression. During the second wave in Uganda, overwhelmed health systems and frequent breakdowns in oxygen supply, including at LRRH, significantly hindered patient care and likely contributed to poor outcomes.14

Tachypnoea (respiratory rate >18 breaths/min) and tachycardia (heart rate >100 bpm) were recorded in 34% and 37% of patients, respectively. Although tachypnoea was associated with mortality on bivariate analysis, it did not retain significance in multivariate analysis, possibly due to confounding factors such as SPO2. Normal respiratory rate was not independently predictive after adjusting for other variables. Tachycardia, a nonspecific sign, showed no significant association with mortality, likely due to its presence in anxiety and other non-lethal conditions related to COVID-19.

HTN was the most prevalent comorbidity (30%), followed by DM (19%), HIV (10%) and malaria (9%). HTN and DM were associated with increased mortality in bivariate analysis, but this association disappeared in multivariate analysis. This may be due to sample size limitations or confounding from other risk factors such as age and SPO2. While DM and HTN have been shown in other studies to worsen COVID-19 outcomes, our findings did not support an independent association.23 Studies have also shown that COVID-19 worsens and directly causes DM due to impaired glycaemic control and direct viral damage to pancreatic β-cells. Conversely, DM has been found to worsen COVID-19 due to impaired immunity and increased susceptibility to infection.24 25 HIV infection was also not associated with increased mortality, contrary to several global reports, possibly reflecting effective antiretroviral therapy coverage or limitations in sample size.2628 Malaria coinfection was observed in 9% of patients but was not associated with mortality, diverging from some reports suggesting a protective effect29 and others worse outcomes.30 31

However, CLD, though rare, emerged as a significant independent predictor of mortality. Patients without CLD had a 99% reduced likelihood of death. This aligns with previous studies indicating that SARS-CoV-2 can exacerbate liver dysfunction through direct cytopathic effects, systemic inflammation or drug-induced liver injury during treatment. Conversely, liver disease may impair immune responses, further compounding the severity of COVID-19.32 33

Other comorbidities were infrequent and did not show significant associations with mortality, likely due to insufficient statistical power.

Strengths of the study

The comprehensive timeframe, encompassing all three waves of the pandemic in Uganda, and its exclusion of asymptomatic patients who were mainly admitted for isolation purposes to prevent community spread of COVID-19, provide a more accurate picture of hospital-based clinical outcomes.

The inclusion of only symptomatic and clinically relevant cases enhances the applicability of findings to healthcare settings.

There was no bias since all the patients admitted to the CTU were considered for selection; only those who did not meet the eligibility criteria were left out.

Limitations of the study

Being a retrospective cross-sectional study, we took information on a particular point in time, which means other times the data may be different. The accuracy of recorded data may be variable. To mitigate this, the data were reviewed by experienced clinicians to ensure accurate interpretation. Some charts with incomplete data were excluded, and although a large sample size was used to address this limitation, generalisation of findings was difficult. Data regarding COVID-19 vaccine status, insurance status, education levels and pneumonia and tuberculosis testing were not collected.

The patient population was skewed towards moderate to severe cases, particularly during the second and third waves, potentially inflating the observed mortality rate.

Conclusion

The study revealed a high in-hospital mortality rate of 29% among COVID-19 patients admitted to the CTU at LRRH, primarily driven by severe disease presentation and limited access to critical interventions such as oxygen therapy. Independent predictors of survival included younger age, normal oxygen saturation, absence of chronic liver disease and normal body temperature at admission. These findings underscore the urgent need for early identification of high-risk patients and improved access to supportive care, particularly oxygen delivery systems, to reduce mortality in future outbreaks. Strengthening diagnostic capacity, clinical monitoring and preparedness for respiratory pandemics—alongside prospective studies capturing broader patient data—will be essential to refine response strategies and improve outcomes.
