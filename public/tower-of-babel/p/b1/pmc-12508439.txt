Lane-change risk prediction model based on traffic context and driving styles
Authors: Yingwei Ren, Fangzheng Li, Zihe Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLane-change risk prediction models are critical for enhancing driving safety and reducing traffic accidents. However, existing studies have not sufficiently considered the effects of traffic context and driving styles on lane change risk, limiting their adaptability in complex traffic environments. This study proposes a lane-change risk prediction model that integrates traffic context and driving styles. The traffic context model identifies different lane changing types using Attention-LSTM by analyzing traffic levels and vehicle types; The LSTM-Stacked Denoising Autoencoder (LSTM-SDAE) identifies and further characterizes distinct driving styles. The Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) algorithm predicts lane change risk using the Lane Change Risk Index derived from driving styles (LCRI_DS), and applies Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) to analyze feature importance across different LC types. The results show that incorporating traffic context improves the model’s prediction accuracy from 88.79 to 95.05%. The LGBM algorithm, combined with traffic context and driving style features, outperforms other algorithms. Feature importance analysis reveals that the risk of left lane changes is primarily influenced by the vehicle’s lateral velocity, the relative longitudinal velocity to the preceding vehicle in the target lane, and the driving style, whereas the risk of right lane changes is more strongly associated with the relative longitudinal velocities to following vehicles in both the current and target lanes, along with the driving style. This work provides valuable insights for improving driving safety and developing decision-support systems in complex traffic environments.Keywords: Lane-change risk prediction, Traffic context modeling, Driving style recognition, Lane-change type classification, Light gradient boosting machine (LGBM)Subject terms: Civil engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Classical driving behaviors primarily consist of car-following (CF) and lane-changing (LC), Among these, lane-changing involves more complex factors and presents greater challenges. Dangerous lane-changing behavior may result in erroneous driving decisions, thereby increasing the likelihood of traffic accidents1. Accurate prediction of LC risk is one of the key factors in the development of advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS)2. Based on this, it is both important and urgent to develop a safe and reliable model for identifying lane-change risk.

The value of reliable accident risk prediction and proactive prevention in traffic safety is well recognized. Prior studies have primarily assessed collision severity using statistical crash data in combination with economic models3. However, the limited data availability and the inability to obtain relevant parameters in real time, these approaches are difficult to apply to real-time risk prediction. With the advancement of computing technologies, researchers can now utilize more precise trajectory data to conduct diversified collision risk analyses. Currently, LC risk analysis methods are commonly categorized into two types. The first is econometric analysis models, such as random-effects logistic regression4 and Bayesian logistic regression5, which can explain the relationships between variables and risk, and are suitable for policy making and macro-management. However, these methods heavily depend on feature selection and data volume, which limits their applicability in high-dimensional and complex scenarios, and their real-time performance remains insufficient.

The second category involves machine learning algorithms. Li et al.6 employed Support Vector Machines (SVM) to predict collision risks using microscopic traffic variables, but the feature dimension was relatively limited. Xue et al.7 integrated vehicle trajectories and traffic context features to identify hazardous driving behaviors; however, the influence of driving style factors was not deeply explored. Shangguan et al.8 applied a Light Gradient Boosting Machine model to predict proactive lane-change risks, but further improvements are needed in model interpretability and generalization capability. Compared with traditional models, machine learning methods demonstrate superior performance in both prediction accuracy and computational efficiency, making them more suitable for lane-change collision risk analysis9. Although existing research has achieved notable progress based on trajectory data, the effects of factors such as traffic context and driving styles remain underexplored, presenting opportunities for further optimization of prediction models.

In recent years, researchers have gradually realized that solely relying on trajectory data is insufficient for comprehensive lane-change risk analysis; external and individual factors such as traffic context and driving style also play a critical role10. Li et al.6 highlighted the vital role of traffic context in autonomous driving systems, primarily focusing on overall contextual variables but lacking exploration of individual differences and microscopic behaviors. Bejani and Ghatee11 compared behavioral differences among various vehicle types, which provides a reference for modeling vehicle heterogeneity, but did not systematically integrate individual driving style with risk determination mechanisms. Ding et al.12 demonstrated that lane-change risk exhibits significant differences under different traffic states. However, most current models fail to dynamically adjust risk assessment strategies according to traffic context, resulting in insufficient generalizability in practical applications. Meanwhile, Sagberg et al.13, through questionnaires and on-road experiments, systematically elaborated on the profound impact of driving style on risk decisions and safe behaviors, but did not jointly model real trajectory and contextual features. Although previous studies have separately analyzed traffic context or driving style, systematic joint modeling and application to lane-change risk prediction remain limited. Therefore, identifying driving styles and providing personalized driver assistance can help reduce hazardous behavior and enhance road safety14. Integrating traffic context with driving styles offers a promising approach to address existing gaps in lane-change risk prediction.

In summary, current lane-change risk analysis research faces two primary limitations: (1) an overreliance on trajectory and kinematic features, with a lack of systematic modeling of the impact of traffic context; and (2) insufficient integration of individual driving style modeling with risk discrimination, which constrains the model’s generalization capability and practical application value. To address these issues, this study proposes a lane-change risk prediction model that comprehensively integrates traffic context and driving styles. The main innovations of this work include:

For the first time, systematically incorporating both traffic context and driving style—two key influencing factors—into the risk prediction framework, enabling collaborative modeling of multi-source heterogeneous data and enhancing the model’s adaptability to complex driving environments;

Data preprocessing

In this study, the HighD dataset is utilized. It is based on video data captured by unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), with vehicle trajectories extracted using computer vision algorithms at a frequency of 25 Hz. The dataset covers approximately 16.5 h of highway traffic scenes and includes trajectory information for over 110,000 vehicles15.

Figure 2 illustrates the road structure within the HighD dataset and two types of lane-change maneuvers: left lane change (LLC) and right lane change (RLC). In this study, the vehicle performing the lane change is defined as the Subject Vehicle (SV). The vehicle directly ahead in the original lane is referred to as the Preceding Vehicle (PV), and the one behind is the Following Vehicle (FV). In the adjacent left lane, the corresponding vehicles are denoted as the Left Preceding Vehicle (LPV) and the Left Following Vehicle (LFV). Similarly, in the adjacent right lane, the Right Preceding Vehicle (RPV) and the Right Following Vehicle (RFV) are defined accordingly.

To ensure accurate information extraction, this study collects a range of features, including frame ID, vehicle ID, vehicle position, lane width and length, speed, and acceleration. During the lane-change process, driving behavior is influenced not only by individual driver habits but also by interactions with surrounding vehicles16. Therefore, information about neighboring vehicles is also taken into account. A data model is constructed based on the trajectories of the target vehicle and its surrounding vehicles, with a 10-second time window selected for each lane-change sample17. After data cleaning (i.e., removing samples with missing values, abnormal speeds/accelerations, or insufficient window length), a total of 1,073 valid lane-change (LC) events were retained for risk analysis, including 521 left lane changes (LLC) and 552 right lane changes (RLC). Traffic context module

Existing studies have shown that macroscopic traffic flow characteristics not only determine the overall operational state of the roadway, but also directly influence drivers’ lane-changing intentions and maneuvering risks. Significant differences exist in driving behavior characteristics and lane-change safety under different traffic contexts such as free flow and congested flow. Therefore, incorporating traffic flow states as features into the model helps improve the accuracy and adaptability of lane-change risk identification.

Traffic flow state is a key factor influencing driving behavior and is typically categorized into free-flow and congested traffic conditions. Under free-flow conditions, drivers can independently adjust their speed without being influenced by surrounding vehicles, whereas in congested traffic, they are more likely to decelerate and engage in frequent lane-changing maneuvers18. Since traffic flow cannot be directly extracted from raw data, this study utilizes traffic density as a proxy and determines traffic states based on traffic volume per unit time, average density, and speed19. Lane-change classification model

Lane-changing behavior represents a typical multivariate time-series modeling problem, influenced by the actions of surrounding vehicles in a dynamic environment22. This study proposes an Attention-LSTM model, which integrates a long short-term memory (LSTM) network with an attention mechanism to identify and predict vehicle lane-changing behavior. The model input combines trajectory features of the subject vehicle (SV) and its surrounding vehicles7, and incorporates traffic state classification and vehicle type recognition to enhance traffic scenario modeling. The model outputs lane-change classification results, including left lane change (LLC), right lane change (RLC), and lane keeping (LK). The attention mechanism emphasizes key influences by computing importance weights over the hidden states. The output of the underlying LSTM is passed to the attention layer, which generates weight vectors through a fully connected layer followed by a Softmax function, and applies element-wise multiplication with the input features. Subsequently, a tiling layer is employed to expand the multidimensional data, and the final prediction is generated through a fully connected layer activated by a Softmax function.

We selected the LSTM-Attention model for lane-change behavior prediction because it can effectively capture complex patterns and nonlinear dependencies in time-series data. The LSTM network is well suited for modeling long-term dependencies, while the attention mechanism enables the model to focus on key moments during the lane-change decision process. This architecture is particularly advantageous for representing the dynamic characteristics of driving behavior, especially in scenarios involving continuous interactions.

To represent stable individual driving preferences, this study selected a continuous trajectory of T = 30 s and downsampled the original 25 Hz data to 5 Hz before inputting it into the model, so as to reduce redundancy and noise interference. The window length (30 s) and the number of clusters were jointly determined using internal clustering indices, in order to balance intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separability.

Traditional methods typically rely on statistical features (e.g., mean, variance) for modeling but are vulnerable to noise. In contrast, feeding the raw time series directly into an LSTM network enables more effective capture of fine-grained temporal variations and facilitates low-dimensional embedding of high-dimensional behavioral features.

A Denoising Autoencoder (DAE) extends the standard Autoencoder (AE) by adding noise to the input layer, which helps prevent overfitting and enhances the robustness of the encoder network23. This study aims to obtain discriminative feature encodings that reflect different driver styles. To improve the encoding’s discriminative capacity, Gaussian noise (µ = 0, σ = 0.01) is added to the input data. The model architecture consists of two LSTM networks serving as the encoder and decoder, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Feature extraction with LSTM-SDAE.

For the trained LSTM-SDAE model, only the encoder is retained, which is then used to encode driving time series into feature vectors representing driver styles. The resulting low-dimensional features are clustered to determine the optimal number of clusters. Subsequently, the clustering performance of PCA, AE, LSTM-AE, and LSTM-SDAE is evaluated using the K-means + + algorithm, in order to identify the optimal model-clustering combination.

In existing studies, clustering performance evaluation methods are generally categorized into internal and external assessments24. Due to the absence of ground-truth labels for driving styles, this study adopts internal evaluation metrics to assess clustering performance. Specifically, three internal metrics are employed: Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)25, Silhouette Coefficient (SC)26, and Calinski–Harabasz Index (CH)27. SSE measures the total squared distance between each data point and its corresponding cluster center; lower values indicate better clustering compactness. SC ranges from − 1 to 1, with higher average values indicating better-defined clusters. Higher CH values correspond to better clustering performance.

Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) is a decision tree-based gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft Research Asia and introduced at the NIPS conference31. It adopts a leaf-wise growth strategy instead of traditional level-wise expansion and builds decision trees step by step based on the maximum gain criterion8. In the lane-change risk prediction task, which relies on aggregated statistical features such as speed, acceleration, and time headway, LightGBM is particularly suitable due to its outstanding performance and its ability to process structured, high-dimensional data. Compared to sequential data, risk prediction focuses more on the integrated representation of features. This model provides both high predictive accuracy and strong interpretability. To further enhance model interpretability, the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method is introduced to analyze feature importance in the model’s output.

To compare the applicability of different algorithms in risk prediction, four models—Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM—are employed to model left and right lane changes separately. The experimental procedure is as follows: First, the dataset is split into training and test sets in an 8:2 ratio, and feature standardization is applied using the standard deviation method. Second, minority classes in the training set are oversampled using the SMOTE algorithm to address class imbalance. Third, random search combined with five-fold cross-validation is used to identify the optimal set of hyperparameters. Finally, model performance is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Feature importance is then analyzed and ranked using the SHAP method.

Evaluation of the traffic context model

Traffic flow data were extracted from a specific road segment within the HighD dataset and normalized to hourly units. Figure 5 presents the clustering results obtained under different traffic conditions using a clustering algorithm. The results indicate a clear distinction between free-flow and congested traffic conditions, with a threshold at 75.6 km/h. Under free-flow conditions, the average speed reaches 106 km/h and the maximum speed approaches the legal limit, whereas under congested conditions, the average speed significantly declines to 51 km/h. Traffic volume peaks in the speed range of 80–100 km/h at approximately 6300 vehicles/hour, and ranges from 1200 to 6500 vehicles/hour under free-flow conditions. Free-flow conditions occur approximately 24 times more frequently than congested states, indicating a relatively low level of congestion. In congested traffic environments, drivers tend to show stronger lane-changing intentions, different risk preferences, and distinct operational strategies compared to those in free-flow environments. Therefore, incorporating traffic state into lane-change behavior modeling not only helps improve the accuracy of risk identification but also enhances the model’s adaptability and practical value in complex traffic scenarios.

Results of lane change risk prediction and contributing factors

In this study, the Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) clustering algorithm is employed, and the optimal number of clusters for lane-changing risk is determined to be four using the Wilks’ Lambda variation method proposed by Oh et al.34. Using the number of clusters and real-time LCRI_DS values from 1,073 lane-changing events as inputs, in the risk prediction stage, raw time-series data are not directly input; instead, statistical aggregation of multi-source features is performed in the event neighborhood [t1 − 2 s, t2 + 1 s], and these are combined with traffic context and driving style embeddings before being input to LightGBM. This study compares the performance of four algorithms: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, and LightGBM. Figure 10 presents the overall precision and recall of each model in left- and right-lane-change scenarios. The results indicate that for left-lane-change risk prediction, LightGBM achieves the highest overall precision and recall, followed closely by XGBoost. For right-lane-change prediction, both XGBoost and LightGBM also perform well, exhibiting higher precision and recall than SVM and RF. Overall, the precision for right-lane-change prediction is generally higher than that for left-lane changes, while differences in recall across algorithms are minimal, except for the relatively lower performance of RF. This phenomenon may be attributed to the higher speeds and more complex interaction behaviors typically associated with the left lane, which increase the difficulty of risk prediction and render left-lane-change prediction more challenging.

In the left-lane-change model, lateral speed is identified as the most influential feature of the target vehicle. In the right-lane-change model, the relative longitudinal speeds of the vehicle behind in the home lane and vehicles before and after in the target lane are highly influential, indicating that drivers should pay particular attention to rearward dynamics. Feature importance ranking for lane change risk level prediction.

We further evaluated SHAP interaction values derived from the LightGBM model and visualized the leading interactions using heatmaps, as shown in Fig. 13. In the left lane change scenario, the strongest interactions were observed between the subject vehicle’s lateral velocity and the longitudinal or distance-related features of the lead vehicle in the target lane, (e.g., SV_vy × SV&LPV_dx and SV_vy × SV&PV_vx). These effects were further amplified when coupled with the driving style factors of adjacent vehicles (e.g., PV_DS × SV_vy, FV_DS × SV_vy), underscoring the critical role of both maneuvering and behavioral traits—consistent with previous findings that left lane changes rely heavily on lateral control and coordination with forward vehicles, with aggressive driving styles exacerbating risk.

In contrast, in the right lane change scenario,, the top interactions primarily reflected rearward traffic constraints, notably between the relative speed or distance of the following vehicle in the current lane or the rear vehicle in the target lane and the subject vehicle’s lateral movement (e.g., SV&FV_vx × SV_vy, SV&RFV_vx × SV_vy). These were also modulated by the driving styles of rear vehicles (e.g., RFV_DS × SV&RFV_dx, FV_DS × SV&FV_dx), highlighting the pronounced influence of rearward dynamics on right lane change risk. These insights further clarify the mechanism underlying lane-change risk and provide robust support for the design of advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS)38.

Conclusion

This study proposes a lane change risk prediction and analysis model that integrates traffic context and driving style. An integrated framework was developed, consisting of three core modules: traffic context perception, driving style recognition, and risk prediction based on LightGBM-SHAP. The proposed model can effectively identify risk states during lane changes and enhance the systematic and precise analysis of lane-changing safety.

The experimental results demonstrate that the model exhibits strong predictive capability for left-lane change (LLC) and right-lane change (RLC) risk assessment. The introduction of the traffic context perception module significantly improves the accuracy of lane change type identification, confirming the critical role of contextual information in lane change recognition. Incorporating driving style into the risk prediction model further enhances performance by capturing behavioral differences among drivers. The LCRI_DS indicator, developed using the Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) method and quantified via Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) clustering, enables effective grading of lane change risk. Using the LightGBM-SHAP framework, the model achieves over 90% recognition accuracy for medium- and high-risk events in both LLC and RLC scenarios. Feature importance analysis reveals that for LLC, attention should be paid to the dynamics of the leading vehicle to avoid longitudinal conflicts within the current lane. In RLC, drivers should focus on the approach behavior of rear vehicles, maintain safe following distances, and ensure lateral stability to reduce lane change risks.

While the proposed model demonstrates robust performance, there remain several promising directions for future research and real-world application. First, additional traffic features such as road type, weather conditions, and real-time traffic control measures can be integrated to further improve model accuracy and adaptability39. Future studies will also consider fusing multi-source heterogeneous data, including connected vehicle data and infrastructure-based sensing, to build a more comprehensive risk prediction framework. Second, we plan to explore the use of ensemble and hybrid deep learning approaches to enhance the model’s generalization and robustness across diverse traffic environments. Regarding practical deployment, the proposed model can be embedded in intelligent driver assistance systems (ADAS) and real-time traffic management platforms to provide dynamic risk warnings and decision support for lane-change maneuvers.
