ABSTRACT

The challenges presented by market fluctuations and environmental events such as lightning are addressed in this paper by integrating fuzzy logic with Markov models. This integration is critically needed because market uncertainties (e.g., prices) often follow probabilistic patterns, while lightning impacts involve imprecise, linguistic assessments. A unified Fuzzy-Markov framework is therefore essential to holistically manage these hybrid uncertainties and enhance decision-making in smart grid self-scheduling. The objective of this research is to create a dependable framework for improving the predictability and stability of smart grid systems under unforeseen circumstances. The proposed Fuzzy-Markov approach facilitates the proactive decision-making process and the effective forecasting of future market conditions by categorizing complex numerical data into fuzzy states and analyzing the transition probabilities between these states. One of the most significant contributions is the successful classification of financial metrics, including price, revenue, and sales, into qualitative fuzzy states. The Markov transition matrix’s construction and analysis provide critical insights into state transitions, with the model attaining an accuracy of 56.13%. Although this accuracy is moderate, it illustrates the model’s effectiveness in predicting future conditions, superseding random conjecture and establishing a strong foundation for strategic planning. The research also emphasizes significant findings through rolling statistics, which are crucial for risk management. The novelty of this work is its distinctive integration of fuzzy logic and Markov models to address both market and environmental uncertainties in smart grids.

Introduction

Smart grids are modern electricity grids that use digital communication technology to detect and react to local changes in electricity consumption. One of the challenges of smart grids is self-programming, which involves automatically adjusting the power source in response to changing conditions. This is especially challenging due to market uncertainty and environmental factors such as lightning. Market uncertainty refers to fluctuations in electricity prices, demand, and income. These can be affected by various factors, including economic conditions, regulatory changes, and technological developments. Lightning uncertainties are related to the unpredictable nature of weather-related events, especially lightning, which can affect the stability and reliability of the power grid. Reflecting on the past few years, it is evident that greenhouse gas emissions have substantially risen due to the advancement of the power system. Furthermore, the absence of regulation over the indiscriminate generation of these conventional fossil-fuel power plants might lead to an escalation in greenhouse gas emissions as a result of fossil fuel usage. Implementing the self-scheduling technique effectively decreases both greenhouse gas emissions and the expenses associated with the power system1.

Integrating renewable energy sources and advanced technologies into modern power systems has transformed traditional grids into complex, dynamic, intelligent grids2. These smart grids, characterized by their bidirectional flow of electricity and information, offer enhanced efficiency and resilience but face significant challenges, particularly in managing uncertainties3. Among these uncertainties, lightning-induced disruptions and fluctuating market conditions are critical concerns. Lightning strikes can cause sudden, severe disturbances, affecting grid stability and the reliability of the power supply. Meanwhile, market uncertainties, driven by volatile energy prices and demand fluctuations, complicate the self-scheduling of power generation, requiring sophisticated models to optimize decision-making under these unpredictable conditions4.

Innovative approaches like the Fuzzy-Markov model are increasingly being explored to address these challenges. This trend towards hybrid intelligent systems for decision-making under uncertainty is also evident in other domains, such as renewable energy source selection using advanced fuzzy decision models5, logistics provider selection via fuzzy neural networks6, the development of comprehensive fuzzy intelligence-based decision support systems7, and even in specialized selection problems like nanomaterial selection for sensors8 and surgical approach selection9 using integrated fuzzy neural networks. The motivation for this hybrid approach lies in the complementary nature of the two uncertainties: market fluctuations are best modeled probabilistically over time (Markov strength), whereas the occurrence and impact of lightning are inherently vague and best described using linguistic variables (Fuzzy logic strength). Individually, each method is insufficient to capture the full spectrum of smart grid uncertainties. The Fuzzy logic component of this approach allows for handling the inherent vagueness and imprecision in predicting lightning strikes and market behavior, providing a more flexible and adaptive framework10,11. On the other hand, the Markov model is well-suited for modeling the probabilistic transitions of different states within the power system, such as varying market prices or grid conditions influenced by weather events. By combining these methodologies, the Fuzzy-Markov approach offers a robust solution for enhancing the self-scheduling capabilities of smart grids, enabling them to better withstand lightning impacts and navigate market uncertainties, ultimately leading to more reliable and cost-effective energy management. In this research, we aim to develop and implement a Fuzzy-Markov approach for addressing the challenges of lightning-induced disruptions and market uncertainties in the self-scheduling of smart grids. This approach will integrate fuzzy logic to manage imprecise data and Markov models to predict probabilistic transitions in grid conditions, ultimately enhancing the reliability and efficiency of smart grid operations under unpredictable conditions.

The evolution of power systems into smart grids, driven by integrating renewable energy sources and advanced digital technologies, has significantly improved grid efficiency and resilience. However, these advancements have also introduced new challenges, particularly in managing uncertainties such as lightning strikes and volatile energy markets. Lightning can cause sudden disruptions in grid stability, while market uncertainties complicate the scheduling of power generation. Addressing these issues requires innovative solutions that can handle the unpredictability of the grid’s physical and economic factors. To tackle these challenges, the Fuzzy-Markov approach presents a promising solution by combining the strengths of fuzzy logic and Markov models. Fuzzy logic allows for the handling of vague and imprecise data, such as the unpredictability of lightning strikes, while Markov models are effective in managing the probabilistic nature of market fluctuations and grid states. This approach enables more adaptive and resilient self-scheduling mechanisms within smart grids, ensuring reliable and cost-effective operation even under uncertain conditions.

The fuzzy-Markov approach can be used to deal with these uncertainties. It involves the integration of fuzzy logic and Markov chains to model and predict the state of the smart grid under various conditions. Fuzzy logic is a form of logic that allows for more flexible and human-like reasoning compared to traditional binary logic. It deals with reasoning that is approximate rather than fixed and precise. In the context of smart grids, fuzzy logic can be used to classify continuous variables such as price, revenue, and sales into qualitative categories such as “high” and “low”. By converting these variables into fuzzy states, we can better manage their inherent uncertainties. Markov chains are mathematical systems that transition from one state to another in a finite state space. They are used to model the probability of moving from one state to another. In the context of smart grids, Markov chains can be used to model transitions between different fuzzy states over time. Despite extensive research on individual uncertainty modeling in smart grids, there is a lack of studies that systematically integrate fuzzy logic and Markov models to concurrently address both market volatility and environmental disruptions like lightning within the self-scheduling problem. This research is needed to fill this gap and provide a more resilient and adaptive decision-making tool for grid operators. In this study, we aim to develop a Fuzzy-Markov model to address the challenges posed by lightning-induced disruptions and market uncertainties in the self-scheduling of smart grids. The methodology combines fuzzy logic, which handles imprecise data, with Markov models, which predict probabilistic transitions in grid conditions. This approach contributes to enhancing the reliability and operational efficiency of smart grids. The main novelty lies in the integration of these techniques to manage both physical and economic uncertainties, offering a more resilient and adaptive scheduling mechanism for modern power systems.

The principal novelties and advantages of the proposed Fuzzy-Markov approach over existing methods are articulated as follows. First, it introduces a novel integrated framework that concurrently models two fundamentally different uncertainty types: probabilistic market fluctuations (using Markov chains) and possibilistic, linguistically-described lightning impacts (using fuzzy logic), which are typically addressed in isolation. Second, it devises a systematic method to transform continuous numerical market data into discrete fuzzy states and then analyzes their temporal evolution via a Markov transition matrix, creating a unique hybrid qualitative-probabilistic predictor. Third, it leverages this hybrid model to provide actionable insights for dynamic risk management through rolling statistics, a feature not commonly combined with self-scheduling. The key comparative advantage is this holistic handling of hybrid uncertainties, leading to more robust and interpretable decision-support tools compared to purely stochastic8, fuzzy-only10, or Markov-only models found in the literature (see Table 1).

Methodology

Fuzzy logic and Markov chains are combined in this study’s methodology to forecast and analyze market behavior in uncertain environments. Initially, fuzzy logic is employed to classify numerical data that represents market characteristics, including price, revenue, and sales, into qualitative states (“high” or “low”). Continuous variables are transformed into discrete fuzzy states in this classification process, which mitigates the inherent uncertainties in market data. Next, these states are arranged into a state space, where each discrete state in the system is represented by a unique combination of fuzzy states (e.g., “high_low_low” or “low_high_high”). This classification facilitates a more uncomplicated examination of market trends, correlations, and mode distributions, thereby revealing the underlying patterns in the data.

A fuzzy Markov model is developed to forecast the transitions between these states over time, following the fuzzy state classification. A transition matrix is created to quantify the likelihood of transitioning from one state to another, as determined by the transitions that have been observed in the dataset. This matrix serves as the basis for the model’s accuracy evaluation and the prediction of future market states. The dynamic market environment is further comprehended by employing visualization tools to illustrate the distribution and time trends of key market characteristics. This methodology is especially well-suited for applications such as smart grid self-planning, where market conditions and external factors, such as environmental events, can significantly influence decision-making and forecasting, due to the combination of fuzzy logic and Markov chains, which effectively addresses the challenges of variability and uncertainty. Theoretical foundations and assumptions

This subsection delineates the core concepts and justified assumptions that form the bedrock of the integrated Fuzzy-Markov model for smart grid self-scheduling under hybrid uncertainties.

Characterization of Hybrid Uncertainties: The model explicitly recognizes two distinct uncertainty classes: (1) Market Uncertainty, emanating from the stochastic volatility of electricity prices, demand, and consequent revenue, which is amenable to probabilistic analysis; and (2) Environmental Uncertainty, specifically lightning disruptions, whose occurrence and impact are inherently vague and best described in possibilistic, linguistic terms (e.g., ‘minor’ or ‘severe’ disturbance). Assumption 1

(Membership Function Selection): Simple triangular membership functions are employed for fuzzification. Their parameters (defining the core and support) are derived from historical data distributions (e.g., tertiles or quartiles). This choice is justified by its computational simplicity, interpretability, and common use in engineering applications for capturing expert-like classification.

C. Markov Chain for Temporal Dynamics: The evolution of the discretized fuzzy states over time is modeled as a first-order, discrete-time Markov chain.

Key Definition (State Space)

The finite set of all possible unique fuzzy state vectors constitutes the state space of the Markov chain.

Assumption 2

(Markov Property): The probability of transitioning to the next state S_{t + 1} depends solely on the current state S_t. This is a standard simplifying assumption for modeling state transitions in complex systems.

Assumption 3

(Stationarity): The state transition probabilities are assumed constant throughout the operational period under study. This implies that the underlying joint dynamics of market and environmental factors are sufficiently stable to allow for the estimation of a static transition matrix from historical data.

D. Rationale for Sequential Integration: The proposed framework follows a logically sequential analytical procedure: (i) Fuzzification reduces continuous, noisy data into a set of interpretable, qualitative states, handling the imprecision of both market levels and event impacts. (ii) Markov Modeling subsequently analyzes the temporal probabilistic linkages between these qualitative states. This “granulate-then-analyze dynamics” sequence is the core methodological contribution, enabling the fusion of qualitative classification with quantitative transition analysis.

Proposed fuzzy-markov framework

(Following the theoretical foundations established in Sect. 2.1, this subsection details the step-by-step procedural framework for implementing the Fuzzy-Markov model, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. The overall process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Step 1: Data Preparation and Fuzzification.

The historical dataset comprising time-series of market metrics, Price (P), Revenue (R), and Sales (S), is normalized to mitigate scale effects. For each metric, triangular membership functions (µ_Low, µ_Medium, µ_High) are defined based on data distribution percentiles (e.g., 33rd and 66th percentiles as vertices). Each data point at time *t* is then fuzzified by calculating its degree of membership to each linguistic set. The dominant linguistic label (that with the highest membership degree) is assigned, generating a triplet of labels L_t = (L_P, L_R, L_S). This transforms the continuous dataset into a sequence of discrete fuzzy states.

Step 2: State space construction.

The set of all unique fuzzy state triplets L_t observed in the dataset forms the finite state space of the Markov chain, denoted as S = {S_1, S_2, …, S_n}, where each S_i is a unique combination (e.g., (High, Low, Medium)). This state space discretizes the system’s operational conditions.

Step 3: Markov transition matrix estimation.

The sequence of fuzzy states is analyzed chronologically. A state transition count matrix C is constructed, where each element c_{ij} counts the number of observed transitions from state S_i to state S_j. The Markov transition probability matrix P is then derived by row-normalizing c: p_{ij}=c_{ij}/Σ_k c_{ik} where p_{ij} represents the probability of transitioning from state S_i to S_j in the next time step. This matrix encapsulates the temporal dynamics and probabilistic linkages between fuzzy states.

Step 4: Model Validation and Accuracy Assessment.

The model’s predictive capability is evaluated using a hold-out or time-series cross-validation approach. The transition matrix P, estimated from a training period, is used to predict the next state for each state in the validation period. The prediction is the state S_j with the highest probability p_{ij} from the current state S_i. Model accuracy is calculated as the percentage of correct predictions (matching the actual observed next state) over the total predictions made.

Step 5: Rolling Statistics for Dynamic Insight.

To capture time-varying volatility and trends, rolling window statistics (e.g., 7-day rolling mean and standard deviation) are computed for the original numerical metrics (P, R, S). These are not fed into the Markov model directly but provide complementary, dynamic risk indicators. They help contextualize the fuzzy-Markov predictions by highlighting periods of high market volatility or anomalous trends.

Step 6: Decision-Support Interpretation.

The final output for a grid operator consists of: (i) the current fuzzy state S_i, (ii) the predicted next state(s) with associated probabilities from matrix P, and (iii) the current rolling statistics. This integrated output supports proactive decision-making. For example, a prediction of a transition to a (High Price, Low Revenue) state during a period of high price volatility (from rolling stats) would trigger specific hedging or load-adjustment actions.

This structured, six-step framework ensures a logical flow from raw data to actionable insights, fulfilling the goals of transparency and methodological reproducibility.

Illustrative practical example

To ground the proposed methodology in a tangible context, consider the following illustrative scenario inspired by typical smart grid operations. A regional grid controller, ‘GridOps Co.‘, is responsible for the self-scheduling of a 100 MW urban microgrid that participates in the day-ahead electricity market and is prone to summer thunderstorms.

Step 1 – Data Fuzzification in Practice: GridOps monitors three core indicators: (a) Day-ahead Market Price Index (DAMPI) in €/MWh, (b) Expected Operating Revenue (EOR) in €/h, and (c) Scheduled Net Load (SNL) in MW. Based on historical percentiles, fuzzy sets are defined. For instance, a DAMPI of 85 €/MWh is fuzzified as ‘Elevated’, an EOR of 8,500 €/h as ‘Satisfactory’, and an SNL of 65 MW as ‘Moderate’. This yields a composite fuzzy state, e.g., (Elevated, Satisfactory, Moderate).

Step 2 – Historical State Sequence: Analyzing data from the previous month, GridOps identifies recurring state sequences. A typical fair-weather pattern might be (Normal, High, High) → (Normal, High, Moderate). However, on days with lightning alerts, a pattern like (Normal, Satisfactory, Moderate) → (Elevated, Low, Low) is observed, indicating price spikes and demand reduction due to precautionary shutdowns.

Step 3 – Applying the Markov Transition Matrix: The historical data is used to construct a Fuzzy-Markov transition matrix. Suppose the current state is (Normal, High, High) and the weather service issues a 70% probability of lightning in 3 h. The matrix, conditioned on such external alerts, might show: a 50% chance of transitioning to (Elevated, Moderate, Low), a 30% chance to (High, Low, Very Low), and only a 20% chance of remaining in the current state. This quantitatively captures the increased risk of adverse market and load conditions.

Step 4 – Concrete Decision-Making Outcome: Based on this probabilistic forecast, GridOps can execute specific, pre-planned actions: (1) Financial Action: Submit a buy order for 20 MW of reserve power at a fixed price in the intraday market to hedge against potential price surges. (2) Physical Action: Preemptively switch 10 MW of sensitive industrial load to backup generators (as per prior contracts) to reduce grid exposure and potential outage costs. (3) Market Action: Temporarily increase the bidding price for its own solar farm’s output, anticipating higher clearing prices.

This illustrative example demonstrates how the abstract concepts of fuzzy state classification and Markovian transitions materialize into a sequence of data processing, risk quantification, and actionable decisions. It bridges the gap between theoretical definitions and their practical utility in smart grid self-scheduling under compound uncertainties.

Results and discussion

The proposed Fuzzy-Markov model was evaluated using a synthetic dataset simulating one year of hourly smart grid operation. Market variables (Price, Revenue, Sales) were generated as correlated stochastic processes with calibrated volatility to mimic real market behavior. Lightning-induced disruptions were modeled as random exogenous shocks, increasing price volatility and decreasing sales reliability on affected days. The dataset was chronologically split: the first nine months served as the training set for constructing the fuzzy states and transition matrix, while the final three months formed the hold-out test set for unbiased performance evaluation.

In recent years, the incorporation of fuzzy logic and Markov models in predictive analytics has garnered significant attention, particularly in domains that are characterized by dynamic conditions and uncertainty. In market analysis, where data variability is prevalent, fuzzy logic provides a robust framework for categorizing continuous variables into qualitative states by virtue of its capacity to manage imprecise and equivocal data. In contrast, Markov models offer a probabilistic approach to modeling state transitions over time, which enables the prediction of future states based on historical patterns. The capacity to anticipate and adjust to altering conditions is essential in a variety of disciplines, including financial market forecasting and smart grid management. Consequently, the combination of these two methodologies has been investigated. This literature review examines the current research on fuzzy-Markov models, emphasizing their applications, advantages, and the voids that this study seeks to resolve. Each state in the transition probability matrix is a combination of fuzzy logic categories for price, revenue, and sales (e.g., high_low_low, low_low_low). The prospective state in the subsequent time step is represented by the columns, while the current state is represented by the rows at a specific time step. The probability of transitioning from one state to another is indicated by the matrix. For example, a value of 0.357123 in the high_low_low row and the high_low_low column indicates that there is a 35.71% likelihood that the high_low_low state will persist in the subsequent time step. The persistence of states is denoted by diagonal elements of the matrix, in which the present state remains unchanged in the subsequent phase. These values, particularly the higher ones (which are frequently italicized), indicate a high probability that a state will remain unaltered. For example, the low_high_high state has a 63.41% likelihood of persisting. In contrast, the off-diagonal elements denote transitions to distinct states. For instance, the high_low_low state has a probability of 21.44% of transitioning to low_low_low, or 32.14% of transitioning to low_high_high. Zero values are used to represent transitions that do not occur. For example, high_high_low does not transition to high_high_high or low_low_high.

System dynamics are substantially influenced by states that exhibit greater transition probabilities to multiple other states. As summarized in Table 2, prior studies have primarily addressed singular aspects of smart grid uncertainty. For instance, stochastic models13,22 excel in handling market price variability but lack a framework for incorporating qualitative, event-driven risks like lightning. Conversely, fuzzy-based reliability analyses15 or Markov models for component failure are adept at handling specific operational or environmental vagueness but are not designed for forecasting financial market trends. The model in35 optimizes energy management but does not integrate a temporal probabilistic model for state transitions.

The proposed method bridges this gap. Unlike the purely stochastic or purely fuzzy methods, our hybrid model simultaneously quantifies the probabilistic evolution of market states (akin to13) and evaluates the impact of possibilistic environmental events (extending the logic of15 to grid scheduling). While the achieved accuracy of 56.13% may appear moderate, it reflects the model’s performance on a more complex, hybrid problem domain compared to the singular-focus studies. For example, a model predicting only price might achieve higher accuracy on that single variable, but it would fail to provide the integrated ‘market-environment’ risk perspective essential for holistic self-scheduling. The rolling statistics component further differentiates this work by enabling dynamic risk assessment, a feature not emphasized in the self-scheduling models reviewed in Table 2. Thus, the main comparative advantage lies not in outperforming all predecessors on a single metric, but in providing a more comprehensive, unified tool for decision-making under the compound uncertainties characteristic of modern smart grids.

Conclusion

This study addressed the critical challenge of managing hybrid uncertainties—probabilistic market volatility and possibilistic environmental disruptions like lightning, in smart grid self-scheduling. To this end, a novel integrated Fuzzy-Markov framework was developed and implemented. The framework successfully classifies continuous market data (price, revenue, sales) into linguistic fuzzy states and models their temporal evolution through a Markov transition matrix, achieving a predictive accuracy of 56.13%. Rolling statistics further provided dynamic insights into financial risk and volatility.
