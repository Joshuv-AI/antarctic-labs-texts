Activation of the human β3 GABAA receptor by combinations of receptor protonation with chemical agonists and gain-of-function mutations
Authors: Allison L. Germann, Spencer R. Pierce, Joe Henry Steinbach, Gustav Akk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Native GABAA receptors are continuously exposed to numerous active compounds, including protons, whose net activating and modulatory effects determine receptor function and underlie the inhibitory tone in the brain. Here, we have investigated the interactions and energetic additivity between activation of the homomeric β3 GABAA receptor due to protonation and its activation by standard chemical agonists or gain-of-function mutations. Energetic additivity results from combined actions of agonists or mutations with independent sites and mechanisms, and manifests as apparent synergistic potentiation. It is therefore a clinically important mode of manipulation of receptor function. Our findings indicate a lack of strict energetic additivity in nearly each test, although the underlying mechanisms were different. Activation of the wild-type and tested mutant β3 receptors by the orthosteric agonist histamine was associated with reduced energetic contribution at higher [H+]. Mutational studies revealed that protonation of the β3(H107) residue, located within ~ 8 Å of its binding site, impairs activation by histamine. Mutation of this residue preventing its protonation revealed energetic additivity between activation by histamine and protonation at β3(H267). Activation by the allosteric agonist propofol exhibited reduced energetic impact at an increased level of background activity regardless of extracellular pH. Finally, the gating efficacy of H+ was reduced in two of the tested gain-of-function mutants. Overall, the results indicate a complex interplay between protonation and activation of the β3 receptor by other agents or mutations. It can reflect apparent direct interactions between protonation- and activation-sites, interactions mediated by changes in baseline activity or energetic activity.

Introduction

Native GABAA receptors in the central nervous system are continuously exposed to multiple activating and modulatory compounds whose net functional effect defines receptor activity and ultimately contributes to the inhibitory tone in the nervous system. The combined effects of the transmitter GABA and a number of allosteric agonists and modulators have been studied in considerable detail previously. The available evidence generally supports the idea that receptor function upon coapplication of orthosteric and allosteric agonists acting through distinct sites conforms to the rules of energetic additivity where each agonist acts independently of others and the total free energy change in the presence of an agonist combination is the sum of free energy changes contributed by each agonist separately. In practice, energetic additivity is akin to potentiation where the agonist combination generates a response that is, usually, many times greater than the response to either agonist alone. Energetic additivity has been reported for the combinations of GABA and propofol [1,2] (but see [3]), etomidate [4], benzodiazepines [5,6] (but see [7]), and the steroids alfaxalone and allopregnanolone [1,8]. Similarly, energetic additivity is expected, and has been shown, for combinations of an agonist and several gain-of-function mutations [9,10]. One advantage that results from these studies is the ability to mechanistically predict receptor function under a variety of experimental conditions.

Previous studies have demonstrated sensitivity of native and various recombinant GABAA receptors to H+ [11,12,13,14]. The polarity of the effect can, however, differ depending on the subunit composition of the receptor. In the homomeric β3 GABAA receptor, exposure to low pH (high H+-containing) bath solution enhances constitutive activity [15]. Further study has demonstrated that the observed positive response reflects the net effect of the activating action mediated by protonation of the H267 residue that overpowers the weaker inhibitory effect mediated by protonation of the H107 residue [16].

Changes in extracellular pH in the brain have been observed in several disease states as well as during normal synaptic activity. During brain ischemia, the pH drops to below 6.0 (>1 μM H+), which can be further worsened by concomitant hyperglycemia [17,18]. Less dramatic changes in pH have been reported in the synaptic cleft during regular synaptic activity [19,20].

Here, we investigated whether activation of the GABAA receptor by protonation acts independently of its activation by a standard chemical agonist, and whether protonation and chemical ligation follow the principles of energetic additivity. Confirmation of this would enable a more straightforward understanding of GABAA receptor function under conditions where extracellular pH deviates from normal. We employed the β3 homomeric receptor here because its protonation sites are known and mutation of specific residues enables removal of H+-mediated activation or inhibition or both. While native neuronal expression of β3 homomeric receptors has not been confirmed [21], it may be expected that protonation of the individual β3 subunits modifies the function of heteromeric β3-containing GABAA receptors.

Our findings indicate that in most cases, activation mediated by receptor protonation and activation by chemical ligands are not energetically additive in the β3 receptor. We observed that in the β3 and β3 (H267A) receptors, reduced pH was associated with reduced energetic contribution by histamine. This was mediated by protonation of the β3 (H107) residue, mutation of which to non-protonatable glycine revealed energetic additivity between protonation of β3(H267) and the action of histamine. Activation by propofol was diminished at high levels of background activity. This effect was not strictly dependent on pH because similar results were observed in β3 (or β3(H107G)) where constitutive activity is increased at low pH and in β3(H267A) where activity is increased at high pH. Analogously, H+-generated activity was not energetically additive with increased constitutive activity in gain-of-function mutants.

Methods

The experiments were conducted on human β3 wild-type (Genbank accession number: NM_000814.5) and mutant GABAA receptors. The mutant clones were purchased from Twist Bioscience (South San Francisco, CA). The receptors were expressed in Xenopus laevis oocytes, obtained from Xenopus 1 (Dexter, MI). The oocytes were injected with 5–40 ng of cRNA per oocyte and maintained at 15°C for 2–3 days before conducting electrophysiological recordings. In some cases, the oocytes were moved to room temperature for the last 16–24 hrs before recordings. Anecdotally, we have found this to increase surface expression of the β3 receptor. All experiments were conducted at room temperature. Additional details on oocyte handling, injections, and receptor expression can be found in a previous report [22].

Electrophysiological recordings were done using standard two-electrode voltage clamp. Bath and drug solutions were gravity-applied (flow rate: ~5 ml/min) from 30-ml glass syringes through Teflon tubing (ID: 1.07 mm). Solutions were switched manually using four-port bulkhead switching valves (model: V-101D) and medium pressure six-port bulkhead valves (model: V-341) purchased from IDEX Health & Science (Bristol, CT). The electrophysiological responses were amplified with OC-725C (Warner Instruments, Holliston, MA) or Axopatch 200B (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) amplifiers, digitized with Digidata 1200 or 1322A digitizers (Molecular Devices), and stored using Clampex software (pClamp, Molecular Devices).

Receptor activity was recorded in episodic stimulation mode with one-second long sweeps. The traces were low-pass filtered at 30–50 Hz and sampled at 10–20 ms sampling intervals. The holding potential was −60 mV for the first 150 ms of a sweep, followed by −70 mV for 700 ms, and −60 mV for 150 ms. Conductance was calculated from the difference in current amplitudes between the −70 mV and the initial −60 mV holding potentials as g = ΔI/ΔV. The traces showing receptor function are presented as conductance responses. More details on recording conductance responses are available in our recent publication [16].

The standard, control bath solution in two-electrode voltage clamp recordings was ND96 (96 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM HEPES) adjusted to pH 7.4. Additional HEPES-based ND96 bath solutions were made at pH 7.0, 7.5, 8.0, and 8.5. Bath solutions containing the same salts but using 5 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES) as the buffering agent were made at pH 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, and 7.0. In H+ concentration–response measurements, the pH 7.0 point was measured twice using bath solutions containing either HEPES or MES whose average response amplitude was used in data analysis. The pipette solution was 3 M KCl. Solutions containing the chemical agonist (histamine, pregnanolone, propofol, or pentobarbital) or the antagonist (picrotoxin) were made in the appropriate bath solutions, followed by adjustment of pH when needed. The buffering agents, GABAA receptor agonists and modulators, and most salts were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). MgCl2 was obtained from VWR Chemical (Solon, OH).

The probability of being in the active state (PA) was calculated by comparing the amplitude of the test conductance response to reference conductance responses to 1 mM pentobarbital and 200 μM picrotoxin that were expected to fully activate and inhibit, respectively, the receptors. In most experiments, the pentobarbital and picrotoxin applications were done at pH 7.4. Given a previous finding that activity of the β3 receptor in the presence of pentobarbital is increased at low pH [16], we compared in additional experiments responses to 1 mM pentobarbital at pH 5.5 and 7.4, the ratio of which was used as a conversion factor to estimate a response with PA of 1. The conversion factor was 1, i.e., no difference in responses to pentobarbital at pH 5.5 and 7.4, in the β3 (H267A) and β3(H107G + H267A) receptors, 1.2 in β3 wild-type, 1.6 in β3(H107G + F221W), 3.0 in β3(H107G), 4.4 in β3(H107G + Y284F), and 4.6 in β3(H107G + F301S).

We tested the effect of 1 mM histamine on the response to 1 mM pentobarbital (1 mM) in the β3(H107G + F301S) receptor. At pH 5.5, histamine was without effect on the response to pentobarbital (98 ± 17% of control), whereas at pH 7.4 it increased the response to 180 ± 83% of control. Therefore, we adopted the conductance at pH 5.5 as representing a PA of 1. The remaining conductance in the presence of picrotoxin was minimal in each tested receptor. Accordingly, the actions of picrotoxin and the level of PA of 0 were considered to not be sensitive to pH.

The data from each oocyte were analyzed separately. The data are presented as mean ± S.D., from at least five oocytes per data point or experiment. Curve fitting was done using the Origin 2022 software (OriginLab Corp, Northampton, MA). The concentration – response relationships were fitted with the Hill equation yielding low- and high-concentration asymptotes, and the EC50 and Hill coefficient of a curve.

Quantitative analysis was done by fitting the data on the probability of being in the active state (PA) with the state equation of the co-agonist model [23,24]: (1) PA=11+L×1+[agonist]/Kagonist1+[agonist]/Kagonist×cagonistNagonist where L is the level of background activity calculated from constitutive activity as (1-PA,const)/PA,const, Kagonist is the equilibrium dissociation constant for the agonist (H+ or histamine) in the resting receptor, cagonist is the ratio of the equilibrium dissociation constant for the agonist in the active receptor to Kagonist. Nagonist is the number of identical binding sites for the agonist, constrained to five throughout the study. In the co-agonist model, a mutation or a drug that acts independently of the agonist influences the PA of the response by modifying background activity, i.e., the value of L.

When all binding sites are occupied, the free energy change contributed by the agonist towards stabilization of the active state is related to cagonist as: (2) ΔGagonist=NagonistRT×lncagonist

We also calculated the energy difference for receptors in the active state relative to the resting state from the Boltzmann distribution. This relationship calculates the energy difference from the relative probabilities of being in the two states, as: (3) G=-RT×lnPAPR where R is the universal gas constant (1.987 cal·K−1·mol−1), T is the absolute temperature (assumed to be 295 K), and PA and PR are the probabilities of being in the active and resting states, respectively.

The energetic consequence of a change in experimental conditions (for example, addition of an agonist) was calculated from the change in G compared to the baseline state (for example PA in the absence of agonist) as: (4) ΔG=Gagonist-Gconst=-RT×lnPA,agonistPR,agonist--RT×lnPA,constPR,const=-RT×lnPA,agonist/PR,agonistPA,const/PR,const

This can be obtained from the experimental data as: (5) ΔG=-RT×lnPA,agonist/PR,agonistPA,const/PR,const where PA,const and PA,agonist are the probabilities of being active in the absence and presence of agonist, and PR,const and PR,agonist are the probabilities of being in the resting state in the absence and presence of agonist. More details on the approach are available in our recent publication [25].

Statistical analyses were done using Student’s unpaired t-test in Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). The P values are given in the text as appropriate.

Results

Activation of the β3 GABAA receptor by H+

Oocytes expressing the β3 GABAA receptor were exposed to pH 8.5 (0.032 μM H+) to 5.0 (10 μM H+) bath solutions. Each oocyte was additionally exposed to 200 μM picrotoxin and 1 mM pentobarbital at pH 7.4. Picrotoxin was assumed to inhibit all receptors, i.e., generate a conductance level with PA = 0. The PA of the response to pentobarbital at pH 7.4 was estimated by comparing, in separate experiments, the magnitudes of responses to pentobarbital at pH 7.4 and 5.5. The latter was considered to activate all receptors, i.e., generate a conductance level with PA = 1. In the β3 receptor, the ratio of responses to 1 mM pentobarbital at pH 5.5 and 7.4 was 1.23 ± 0.14 (n = 5).

Sample responses to bath solutions with different pH are shown in Fig. 1A. The H+ concentration–response relationship is given in Fig. 1B. Fitting the PA data with the Hill equation yielded a PA,min (low-concentration asymptote) of 0.053 ± 0.018 (n = 5), PA,max (saturating PA) of 0.83 ± 0.03, an EC50 of 0.53 ± 0.12 μM (pH 6.20 ± 0.10), and a Hill coefficient of 1.35 ± 0.28. We also analyzed the PA data using the co-agonist model (Eq. (1) to obtain estimates of the affinity of protons to the resting state of the receptor (KH = 0.55 ± 0.07 μM) and the affinity to the active receptor relative to that for the resting receptor (cH = 0.397 ± 0.058).

Insights into energetic additivity between protonation and activation of the β3 receptor by the orthosteric agonist histamine

We explored the energetic additivity for the combination of receptor protonation and activation by histamine. Histamine is a partial agonist of the β3 receptor with binding sites in the extracellular domain at positions homologous to the orthosteric binding sites in the heteromeric GABAA receptor [26]. As the first step, we compared histamine concentration–response relationships in the β3 receptor at pH 8.5 and 7.0 (Fig. 2A). Both bath solutions were made using HEPES as the buffering agent. The receptor was activated by applications of 3 μM to 10 mM histamine separated by washouts in bath solution of the same pH. The PA values of the histamine-responses were estimated by comparing the conductance levels in the presence of histamine to those produced by 1 mM pentobarbital (PA = 1) or 200 μM picrotoxin (PA = 0). Curve-fitting the histamine concentration–response data with the Hill equation yielded an EC50 of 338 ± 123 μM (n = 5) at pH 7.0 and 767 ± 292 μM (n = 5) at pH 8.5. The fitted low-concentration asymptotes (PA,min) in the presence of histamine were 0.21 ± 0.03 at pH 7.0 and 0.03 ± 0.02 at pH 8.5. The fitted maximal PA (PA,max) in the presence of histamine were 0.49 ± 0.04 and 0.55 ± 0.07 at pH 7.0 and 8.5, respectively. The Hill coefficients were 0.73 ± 0.09 at pH 7.0, and 0.60 ± 0.17 at pH 8.5.

The co-agonist model predicts that increased background activity generates an increase in PA,max and a left-shift in the concentration–response relationship [9]. These effects fully arise from a change in L (see Methods) while the affinity (Kagonist) and efficacy (cagonist) parameters for a given agonist are expected to remain constant at all levels of background activity. Fitting Eq. (1) to the histamine concentration–response data yielded similar KHis (262 ± 72 μM at pH 7.0 and 331 ± 123 μM at pH 8.5; P > 0.3, two-sample t-test) but dissimilar cHis values (0.801 ± 0.016 at pH 7.0 and 0.629 ± 0.047 at pH 8.5; P < 0.001). The calculated (Eq. (2)) free energy changes in the presence of histamine were −0.65 ± 0.06 kcal/mol at pH 7.0 and −1.37 ± 0.21 kcal/mol at pH 8.5 (P < 0.001). The smaller energetic contribution by histamine at pH 7.0 accounts for the concentration–response curves saturating at similar levels despite the background activity (PA,min) being greater at pH 7.0.

The concentration–response data presented in Fig. 2A were obtained from different sets of oocytes. To confirm the observations more directly, we compared the effects of histamine at different pH on the same oocyte. First, we compared activation of the β3 receptor by histamine at pH 7.4 and 5.5. The PA,const was 0.04 ± 0.01 (n = 6) at pH 7.4. Exposure to 1 mM histamine generated a response with PA of 0.19 ± 0.03. In the same oocytes bathed in pH 5.5 solution, the constitutive PA was 0.47 ± 0.07 and the PA,1 mM His was 0.51 ± 0.06. A sample trace showing the responses to histamine at pH 7.4 and 5.5 is given in Fig. 2B.

From these PA estimates, we calculated the free energy change contributed by 1 mM histamine using Eq. (5). The ΔG1 mM His was −0.95 ± 0.18 kcal/mol at pH 7.4 but only −0.10 ± 0.05 kcal/mol at pH 5.5 (P < 0.001). In other words, histamine activated the β3 receptor by 0.85 kcal/mol less efficaciously at pH 5.5 than what was predicted by the observations made at pH 7.4. To illustrate the functional consequences of this, we calculated the predicted PA,1 mM His at pH 7.4 and 5.5 assuming constant ΔG values at both pH. The predicted PA,1 mM His at pH 7.4 is 0.05 (rather than the observed 0.19) assuming a ΔG1 mM His of −0.10 kcal/mol, and, conversely, the predicted PA,1 mM His at pH 5.5 is 0.82 (rather than 0.51) assuming a ΔG1 mM His of −0.95 kcal/mol. The calculated PA,1 mM His levels are shown in Fig. 2B as red dotted lines.

The pKa of the nitrogen atom in the imidazole ring in the histamine molecule is 5.8. While at pH 7.4 only a minor fraction (~2%) of histamine is protonated, at pH 5.5 the majority (~65%) of histamine molecules are protonated. To test if protonation of this nitrogen underlies the weak activation at pH 5.5, we conducted an analogous experiment comparing the activating effects of 1 mM histamine at pH 8.0 and 6.5. The fraction of protonated histamine molecules is < 1% at pH 8.0 and ~ 15% at pH 6.5. In five oocytes, the PA at pH 8.0 was 0.06 ± 0.03, which was increased to 0.24 ± 0.11 in the presence of histamine. At pH 6.5, the background PA was 0.31 ± 0.16, and the PA in the presence of histamine was 0.52 ± 0.14. The calculated ΔG1 mM His was −1.03 ± 0.16 at pH 8.0 and −0.57 ± 0.13 at pH 6.5 (P < 0.001). The predicted PA,1 mM His at pH 8.0 is 0.13 (rather than the observed 0.24) assuming a ΔG1 mM His of −0.57 kcal/mol, and the predicted PA,1 mM His at pH 6.5 is 0.72 (rather than the observed 0.52) assuming a ΔG1 mM His of −1.03 kcal/mol.

Overall, we have observed that histamine-mediated activation of the β3 GABAA receptor is weaker (i.e., PA,1 mM His lower and ΔG1 mM His higher, closer to 0) when the pH is reduced and background activity increased due to receptor protonation. We infer that receptor protonation and histamine-activation do not act independently in the wild-type β3 receptor. Further, the results do not indicate that protonation of the histamine molecule modifies activation of the β3 receptor.

Activation by protonation and histamine in the β3(H267A) and β3 (H107G) receptors

We have shown that protonation of the H267 residue increases the constitutive activity of the homomeric β3 receptor, whereas protonation of the H107 residue decreases it [16]. To examine the interactions of protonation of individual residues with activation by histamine, we compared the effects of 1 mM histamine on the β3(H267A) receptor at pH 6.0 and pH 7.0. The constitutive PA were 0.021 ± 0.005 (n = 6) at pH 6.0 and 0.08 ± 0.02 at pH 7.0. Exposure to 1 mM histamine increased the PA to 0.06 ± 0.02 at pH 6.0 and to 0.31 ± 0.09 at pH 7.0. A sample conductance trace is given in Fig. 3A. The calculated ΔG1 mM His are −0.62 ± 0.06 kcal/mol and −0.96 ± 0.10 kcal/mol at pH 6.0 and 7.0, respectively (P < 0.001). Assuming unchanged ΔG1 mM His, the calculated PA,1 mM His are 0.10 at pH 6.0 and 0.21 at pH 7.0. So, unlike in the β3 receptor, in β3(H267A) histamine produces a larger than expected response at high pH where background activity is elevated. In terms of receptor protonation, however, the effects are similar in both receptors: reduced pH, i.e. higher [H+], is associated with reduced energetic contribution of histamine.

We next considered the possibility that H+-mediated inhibition at the H107 residue contributes to apparent interactions between histamine-activation and receptor protonation. The β3(H107G) receptor was activated by 1 mM histamine at pH 6.5 and 8.0. Exposure to histamine increased the PA from 0.03 ± 0.003 (n = 5) to 0.05 ± 0.01 at pH 6.5, and from 0.006 ± 0.002 to 0.01 ± 0.002 at pH 8.0. A sample conductance trace is shown in Fig. 3B. The calculated ΔG1 mM His were −0.25 ± 0.07 at pH 6.5 and −0.26 ± 0.08 at pH 8.0 (P > 0.85), indicating that H+-mediated increase in background activity in β3(H107G) is energetically additive with receptor activation by histamine. By extension, we propose that protonation of the β3(H107) residue in β3 and β3(H267A) receptors interferes with the ability of histamine to activate the receptor thereby increasing the calculated ΔG1 mM His at low pH.

We additionally tested sensitivity to histamine in the β3(H107G + H267A) receptor, reasoning that this mutant will exhibit unchanged holding conductance at various pH and indistinguishable responses to histamine. No responses to 1 mM histamine were, however, observed while the receptor was responsive to propofol and pentobarbital (not shown). The reason for the lack of histamine-activation is unclear and has not been followed up by us.

Energetic additivity between activation by protonation and the allosteric agonist pregnanolone in the β3 receptor

Next, we examined activation of the β3 receptor by the neurosteroid pregnanolone at different pH. Pregnanolone is a low-efficacy allosteric agonist of the GABAA receptor with binding sites in the transmembrane region [27]. Due to high lipophilicity of the compound (log P ~ 5) and poor washout from oocytes (not shown), the experiments at different pH were done on separate cells so that each cell was exposed to the steroid only once.

At pH 6.5, the application of 10 μM pregnanolone increased the PA from 0.33 ± 0.12 to 0.43 ± 0.13 (n = 5). At pH 8.5, exposure to 10 μM pregnanolone increased the PA from 0.06 ± 0.04 to 0.11 ± 0.05 (n = 5). Sample traces are shown in Fig. 4. The calculated ΔG10 μM Preg are −0.26 ± 0.05 kcal/mol at pH 6.5, and −0.38 ± 0.10 kcal/mol at pH 8.5 (P > 0.10). We conclude that activation of the β3 receptor by protonation and the neurosteroid pregnanolone are independent and energetically additive.

Lack of energetic additivity between activation by protonation and the allosteric agonist propofol in β3 wild-type and mutant receptors

Propofol is a high-efficacy allosteric agonist of the β3 receptor [28,29]. We compared activation of the β3 receptor by propofol at pH 7.4 and 6.5. At pH 7.4, the application of 10 μM propofol increased the PA from 0.06 ± 0.03 (n = 5) to 0.26 ± 0.14 (ΔG10 μM Prop = −1.01 ± 0.12 kcal/mol). In the same cells bathed at pH 6.5, 10 μM propofol increased the PA from 0.23 ± 0.11 to 0.31 ± 0.15 (ΔG10 μM Prop = −0.26 ± 0.04 kcal/mol). The ΔG10 μM Prop estimates are statistically significantly different (P < 0.001). Thus, propofol is a less efficacious agonist of the β3 receptor at low pH, where the background activity is elevated. Assuming a ΔG of −1.01 kcal/mol, the PA,10 μM Prop at pH 6.5 is 0.62 rather than the observed 0.31. A sample trace is given in Fig. 5A.

We considered various explanations to the difference in ΔG10 μM Prop at the two pH. The first is that ionized and nonionized species of propofol differently activate and/or inhibit the β3 receptor. This is, however, unlikely, given that the pKa of propofol (~11) is considerably outside the range of the pH of tested bath solutions and the calculated fraction of ionized propofol molecules is minuscule at both pH 6.5 and 7.4.

Another possibility is that protonation of the β3(H267) residue, located near a propofol binding site [30,31] modifies the response to propofol. Accordingly, we measured pH-dependence of the responses to propofol in the β3(H267A) receptor where the activating effect of H+ is not present [15,16]. At pH 7.4, the application of 10 μM propofol at pH 7.4 increased the PA from 0.09 ± 0.03 (n = 6) to 0.43 ± 0.10 (ΔG10 μM Prop = −1.23 ± 0.24 kcal/mol). At pH 5.5, the PA increased from 0.01 ± 0.008 to 0.22 ± 0.03 upon exposure to 10 μM propofol (ΔG10 μM Prop = −2.15 ± 0.29 kcal/mol). The ΔG10 μM Prop in β3(H267A) are statistically significantly different (P < 0.001). A sample trace is shown in Fig. 5B. So, in the β3 and β3(H267A) receptors, reduced efficacy of propofol is associated with increased PA,const rather than polarity of the effect of pH.

We then measured propofol-activation in the β3(H107G) receptor that retains activation but not inhibition by H+. At pH 8.0, 100 μM propofol increased the PA from 0.01 ± 0.01 (n = 5) to 0.12 ± 0.03. In the same oocytes bathed at pH 6.5, 100 μM propofol increased the PA from 0.05 ± 0.01 to 0.10 ± 0.02. The ΔG100 μM Prop are −1.68 ± 0.50 kcal/mol and −0.40 ± 0.14 kcal/mol at pH 8.0 and pH 6.5, respectively (P < 0.001). With the ΔG100 μM Prop of −1.68 kcal/mol, the calculated PA,100 μM Prop is 0.50 at pH 6.5. A sample trace is shown in Fig. 5C.

Lastly, we tested propofol-activation of the β3(H107G + H267A) receptor where the holding conductance is not affected by extracellular pH (Fig. 5D). In five cells, the responses to 100 μM propofol were 28 ± 8% and 27 ± 6% of the response to 1 mM pentobarbital at pH 8.0 and 6.5, respectively.

Thus, in β3, β3(H267A), and β3(H107G), a more negative ΔGProp (greater energetic contribution and increased gating efficacy) is associated with smaller constitutive PA rather than the pH of the bath solution while in β3(H107G + H267A) where pH does not modulate constitutive PA the responses to propofol were not sensitive to pH. We infer, first, that pH-mediated changes in receptor activity and activation by propofol are not independent and energetically additive. Second, the gating efficacy of propofol scales inversely with the magnitude of background activity rather than protonation of a specific residue. This is analogous to a more negative ΔGProp at lower levels of GABA-elicited activity reported previously [3]. Lastly, we note that the inverse relationship between pH and constitutive PA in β3 and β3(H107G) vs. β3(H267A) receptors supports lack of effect of ionization of the propofol molecule on receptor function.

Energetic additivity between receptor protonation and increased function in gain-of-function mutants

Independently-acting gain-of-function mutations act analogously to an allosteric agonist by increasing the response to the transmitter and left-shifting its concentration–response curve [9,10]. In the next set of experiments, we tested H+-mediated activation of β3 receptors containing a gain-of-function mutation. We reasoned that if the mutations act independently of protonation-generated activity then this would manifest as energetic additivity. We employed the F221W, F301S, and Y284F mutations shown in prior publications [28] or in unpublished research to increase background activity. The mutated residues are located in the first (F221) and third membrane-spanning domains (F301 and Y284). The locations of residues are given in Fig. 6. To eliminate H+-induced inhibition and simplify data interpretation, the receptors additionally contained the β3(H107G) mutation in the extracellular domain. The single mutant β3(H107G) receptor was employed as control in these experiments.

The secondary mutations increased activity at low [H+] to a different degree. The PA,pH 8.0 was 0.002 ± 0.002 in the β3(H107G) receptor, and 0.21 ± 0.06, 0.02 ± 0.01 and 0.003 ± 0.003 in β3(H107G + F221W), β3 (H107G + F301S) and β3(H107G + Y284F), respectively (n = 5 in each case). Sensitivity to pH was determined by measuring receptor function upon exposure to pH 7.4, 6.5, and 5.5 bath solutions.

The H+ concentration-PA relationships for the mutant receptors are shown in Fig. 7A. Using Eq. (5), we have calculated the free energy changes contributed by H+ at pH 7.4, 6.5, and 5.5 relative to pH 8.0. The main finding is that the ΔG values at pH 5.5 for the H107G + F221W and H107G + F301S mutants deviate significantly from the control (P < 0.001, two sample t-test with Dunnett’s correction). This indicates that the increase in constitutive activity in these two mutants is not energetically additive with activation induced by protonation of the H267 residue. In contrast, protonation of H267 and the gain-of-function effect of Y284F are energetically additive although we note that this mutation has minimal effect on constitutive activity. The observations are illustrated in Fig. 7B.

Discussion

The actions of combinations of independently-acting agonists manifest as energetic additivity in the GABAA receptor [23,24]. For example, a response to an orthosteric agonist is increased in the presence of an allosteric agonist [9,32]. Under most conditions, the increase observed is not additive in absolute terms of current amplitudes. Instead, it appears synergistic where the agonist combination generates a response with an amplitude that can be many times larger than the sum of amplitudes of individually applied orthosteric and allosteric agonists.

Protonation of the heteromeric GABAA receptor has been shown to modify its activation by a classic chemical agonist. In the α1β1 receptor, increased constitutive activity at low pH correlates with larger responses to GABA and a left-shifted concentration–response relationship [12]. Conversely, in α1β2γ2, where low pH is associated with reduced responses to GABA, protonation leads to right-shifted GABA concentration–response relationship [33,11]. The shifts in GABA concentration–response curves due to H+-induced changes in background activity at least qualitatively resemble those observed when background activity is elicited by an allosteric agonist or a gain-of function mutation [9].

One goal of the present study was to determine whether increased function due to protonation of the homomeric β3 GABAA receptor is energetically additive with its activation by classic chemical agonists. The β3 receptor is a convenient model system with known functional protonation sites [16]. We investigated pH-sensitivity of activation of the β3 receptor by the orthosteric agonist histamine and the allosteric agonists pregnanolone and propofol.

In the β3 and β3(H267A) receptors, the value of ΔG1 mM His became less negative at low pH, indicating reduced energetic contribution and lower gating efficacy by histamine. Block of the inhibitory effect of H+ with the β3(H107G) mutation revealed that activation by the orthosteric agonist histamine is in fact energetically additive with protonation of the β3(H267) residue. We propose that protonation of the β3(H107) residue, located near the orthosteric binding site (Fig. 6), interferes with receptor activation by histamine. The ΔG1 mM His in the β3(H107G) receptor also were small suggesting that the mutation too impairs histamine-elicited activation. The extent of impairment resulting from the H-to-G substitution was energetically similar to exposure of the β3 or β3(H267A) receptor to 3.2 μM H+ (pH 5.5).

Activation of the β3 receptor by the neurosteroid pregnanolone was independent of activation by protonation, i.e., steroid-induced activation was energetically additive with H+-mediated increase in background PA.

Activation by the sedative-anesthetic propofol did not directly correlate with extracellular H+ concentration, instead it followed pH-induced changes in control PA. In the β3 and β3(H107G) receptors, where protonation of the H267 residue increases constitutive activity, low pH was associated with reduced energetic contribution of propofol. In the β3(H267A) receptor, where protonation of the H107 residue leads to reduction of constitutive activity, low pH was associated with increased energetic contribution of propofol. Finally, propofol-activation of the β3(H107G + H267A) receptor that is insensitive to changes in pH was unaltered by changes in pH. Overall, these findings are analogous to those in a previous study that measured potentiation of the GABA-activated α1β2γ2L receptor by propofol and found reduced energetic contribution as PA,GABA was increased [3].

We also tested energetic additivity between protonation-induced function and three gain-of-function mutations in the transmembrane domain of the β3 receptor. To exclude contributions from pH-dependent inhibition, these experiments were conducted on the background of the β3(H107G) mutation. Indistinguishable sensitivities to H+, i.e., energetic additivity between mutation-based increase in PA and H+-activation, were observed in β3(H107G) and β3(H107G + Y284F). The introduction of the β3(Y284F) mutation had, however, only a small effect on constitutive PA (0.003 vs. 0.002). The other tested mutations (F221W and F301S) that increased constitutive PA to 0.21 and 0.02, respectively, showed reduced activation by H+ (Fig. 7B).

We have expressed the activating effects of protonation and chemical agonists as free energy changes. While response amplitudes or PA may be more familiar measures of function, comparison of agonist-induced changes in either is not necessarily intuitive or straightforward as they depend non-linearly on the differences between control and agonist-generated responses [25].

Full protonation of the wild-type β3 receptor contributes −2.73 kcal/mol towards stabilization of the active state whereas the binding of histamine at pH 8.5 contributes just −1.37 kcal/mol. For comparison, the energetic contributions made by various orthosteric and allosteric agonists in the αβγ receptor range between −1.5 and −7.2 kcal/mol [34].

In sum, the results indicate a complex relationship between protonation of the β3 receptor and activation by orthosteric and allosteric agonists. Activation by the steroid pregnanolone is energetically additive with changes in background PA due to protonation of H267 and H107 residues. Activation by histamine is energetically additive with changes in background PA caused by protonation of H267 whereas protonation of H107 reduces the ability of histamine to activate the receptor independently and in addition to its effect on background PA. For propofol, we observed reduced energetic contribution at higher background PA but this did not correlate with [H+] per se. As a result, the β3 GABAA receptor at low extracellular pH may be expected to be more sensitive to neurosteroids whereas activation by histamine or the anesthetic propofol tapers off as the background activity increases due to protonation of the receptor. We note that the observations in this study were made within the range of pH values reached in the brain during ischemia and other disease states.

While likely not physiologically relevant, the β3 homomeric receptor is a useful model system with only two functionally important protonatable residues [16]. Furthermore, protonation of the β3 subunit may be expected to modify the function of heteromeric β3-containing GABAA receptors. In future work, it will be interesting to follow up on the effect of protonation on activation by chemical ligands in more physiological subtypes of the receptor, particularly on the α1β3γ2 receptor, where even nominally small changes in background PA are expected to lead to large effects on overall sensitivity to the transmitter and allosteric agonists.
