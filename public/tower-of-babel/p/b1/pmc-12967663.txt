Anterior Ethmoid Artery Ligation for Epistaxis: A Systematic Review
Authors: Wynne Zheng, Christina Zhu, Jiling Chou, Jason Crossley, Timothy R. DeKlotz, Daniel B. Spielman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFor intractable epistaxis unresponsive to conservative measures, surgical intervention, such as anterior ethmoid artery ligation (AEAL), can control bleeding in the superior nasal cavity. Yet, the literature is limited around AEAL for epistaxis control. This systematic review aims to identify the outcomes and complications of the various AEAL approaches.MethodsMEDLINE and PubMed were searched for English‐language articles published in peer‐reviewed journals using “Epistaxis” AND “anterior ethmoid artery” along with associated terms. Inclusion criteria captured full‐text studies that examined AEAL in human subjects as the main treatment, or as part of a combination of treatments. Of the 160 articles reviewed, 21 (13%) met selection criteria. Data were extracted and descriptive statistics were calculated following PRISMA guidelines.ResultsA total of 122 patients underwent various AEAL approaches: external approach via a Lynch incision (76.2%), transnasal endoscopic approach (9.8%), and transcaruncular approach (13.9%). 7/108 patients had postoperative bleeding despite AEAL. 28/99 (28.3%) of patients were reported to have postoperative complications of varying severity. The following complications occurred after an external approach via Lynch incision: eyelid/orbital edema, ptosis, intracerebral abscess, diplopia, dilated pupil, ophthalmoplegia, trigeminal nerve irritation, keratoconjunctivitis, and orbital apex syndrome.ConclusionAEAL effectively reduces epistaxis in suitable patients, with most complications from the Lynch incision being temporary. Further research is required to optimize AEAL's approach and utility for intractable epistaxis.

Introduction

Epistaxis constitutes one of the most frequently encountered otolaryngologic emergencies, encompassing a broad spectrum of clinical presentations from benign, self‐limiting episodes to severe, life‐threatening hemorrhagic events [1]. The potential variability in the source of epistaxis necessitates a repertoire of management strategies [2, 3]. In instances where conservative management such as topical vasoconstrictors, cauterization, or anterior nasal packing fail to achieve hemostasis, surgical intervention may become appropriate. Anterior Ethmoidal Artery Ligation (AEAL) is a procedure targeting the anterior ethmoid artery, a principal vascular conduit supplying the superior nasal cavity, that is not amenable to endovascular embolization.

AEAL can be executed via three distinct approaches. The external approach, which is performed through a Lynch incision, provides direct access to the anterior ethmoid artery immediately proximal to its entry into the ethmoid bone [4]. Conversely, the transnasal endoscopic approach is minimally invasive, using an endoscope to access the artery through a transnasal transethmoidal approach to ligate the vessel within the ethmoid cavity or skull base. Lastly, the transcaruncular approach is a less frequently used technique which employs the use of an orbitotomy to access the medial orbital wall [5]. This approach is an alternative route that avoids nasal mucosal disruption and external scarring [6].

The literature describing the efficacy, safety, and associated complications of AEAL remains limited. This scarcity underscores a need for a comprehensive synthesis of data pertaining to AEAL.

The objective of this systematic review is to consolidate and evaluate the available literature concerning AEAL, with the intention of elucidating clinical applicability, procedural complications, and postoperative outcomes of the various AEAL approaches. We aim to supplement the current body of clinical knowledge around epistaxis management via AEAL and enhance the decision‐making framework for epistaxis management.

Materials and Methods

Study Selection

This systematic review was conducted based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A thorough search was carried out in the MEDLINE and PubMed databases, focusing on English articles from peer‐reviewed journals. The search used the terms “Epistaxis” AND “Anterior ethmoid artery” alongside associated terms to ensure a comprehensive search of relevant literature on AEAL as a treatment for intractable epistaxis. The aim was to include studies that presented AEAL as the main treatment modality or as part of a combination of treatments in human subjects. Conversely, studies that involved cadavers or were not available in full‐text form were excluded to maintain a focus on clinical relevance and to ensure the accessibility and completeness of the data for analysis [7].

The selection process was carried out by two independent reviewers. Initially, titles and abstracts were screened adhering closely to the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This step was followed by a detailed examination of the full texts of selected articles to ascertain their eligibility for inclusion in the review. Discrepancies between reviewers at any stage of the selection process were reviewed through discussion, ensuring a consensus‐based approach to study selection. A risk of bias for each study was assessed using a methodological index for nonrandomized studies (MINORS) score.

Data Extraction

From each eligible full‐text study, we extracted the following information: author, year of publication, study design, intervention sample size, surgical method, total rebleeding rate, and any other reported complications.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R program 4.3.1. The descriptive statistics were subsequently used to analyze study characteristics with adherence to PRISMA guidelines for the aggregated data. A random‐effects Bayesian meta‐analysis was conducted to assess variability across the small sample of extracted studies.

Surgical Method

Through our comprehensive review of the literature, we identified a total cohort of 122 patients who underwent AEAL. 81 (61%) patients underwent AEAL in combination with other procedures (e.g., posterior ethmoid artery ligation, sphenopalatine artery ligation (SPAL), external carotid artery ligation, internal maxillary artery ligation/embolization). The procedure was performed utilizing one of three distinct surgical approaches: an external approach via a Lynch incision, a transnasal endoscopic approach, or a transcaruncular approach. Analysis of the surgical approach distribution revealed that the Lynch incision was utilized in 76.2% of cases (n = 93). In contrast, the transnasal endoscopic and transcaruncular approaches were employed less frequently, in 9.8% (n = 12) and 13.9% (n = 17) of cases respectively, as detailed in Table 2.

Rebleeding Rate

Data on postoperative bleeding was available for 108 patients. A total of 6.5% (n = 7) of patients experienced re‐bleeding despite undergoing AEAL. Notably, these instances of re‐bleeding were classified as minor complications, with all cases being conservatively managed without operative intervention. Postoperative Complication Rate

Postoperative complications data was available for 99 of the 122 patients as detailed in Table 3. 28.3% of these patients (n = 28) encountered postoperative complications of varying severity. All patients who reported complications had undergone AEAL via the external approach utilizing a Lynch incision. The spectrum of reported complications included periorbital edema, ophthalmoplegia, diplopia, ptosis, orbital tenderness, intracerebral abscess, dilated pupils, trigeminal nerve irritation, and keratoconjunctivitis.

Discussion

The results of this systematic review demonstrate high efficacy of AEAL in decreasing refractory epistaxis in the appropriate patient, with a rebleed rate of 6.5%, none of which required repeat surgical intervention. Nevertheless, our data also highlight the relatively elevated complication rate of the AEAL procedure of 28.3%. The complication rate of 28.3% is likely inflated secondary to the modest sample size (n = 122) and the disparate studies spanning several decades of various surgical techniques. The surgical and perioperative practices for anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA) control have advanced substantially, with increasing surgeon comfort in the era of endoscopic skull‐base surgery. Earlier reports frequently included external or nonimage‐guided techniques, whereas contemporary AEAL may now be performed endoscopically via a transcarcuncular or transnasal approach with routine access to image guidance technology when necessary. These era‐related differences likely influence both effectiveness and the spectrum of complications, limiting the direct generalizability of older studies to present‐day practice.

Notably, the majority of the complications reported in studies were temporary in nature and also were all associated with a Lynch incision. It is important to recognize that AEAL is typically a last line therapy and reserved for patients who have failed, packing, SPAL, and embolization. In lieu of the traditional external approach, a transnasal endoscopic approach might have additional advantages regarding enhanced visualization and decreased scarring, lending itself to decreased risk of certain postoperative complications and expedited recovery in the appropriate patient [17]. When explicitly described, postoperative complications without recovery were rare. There was only one case of irreversible optic neuropathy/orbital apex syndrome that resulted from direct nerve injury via external Lynch incision and surgical clips within the apex described in Yeh et al.

A limitation of the endoscopic transnasal approach is that the anterior ethmoid artery may not be amenable to ligation if it runs within or above the skull base, rather than in a mesentery beneath it where it may be amenable to clip or cautery ligation techniques. Floreani et al. noted that 66% of patients in their cadaveric study were not amenable to an endoscopic transnasal approach [7, 17]. This is in line with larger studies that report the anterior ethmoid artery positioning below the level of the skull base in approximately 25%–35% of patients [19].

This systematic review has several important limitations. The included studies exhibited considerably different characteristics, granularity of data, and publication years. Ultimately, the lack of standardized outcome measures and reporting criteria across selected studies poses a challenge in analyzing the available evidence along with drawing definitive conclusions about the efficacy and appropriateness of AEAL for control of recurrent epistaxis. Such heterogeneity within the data precluded the performance of a meta‐analysis. Many of the included patients underwent concomitant AEAL with sphenopalatine artery ligation for intractable epistaxis. The concomitant use of both approaches, however, blurs the individual efficacy of each approach. Additionally, the heterogeneity in surgical techniques and patient populations across studies further complicates the determination of AEAL's efficacy. Along those lines, considering patient selection is a critical factor in achieving favorable outcomes with AEAL. Factors such as underlying comorbidities, anatomical variations, and the etiology of epistaxis can influence the success of the procedure and the likelihood of postoperative complications [28, 29].

Conclusion

AEAL is effective at curtailing epistaxis in the appropriate patient and can be performed via an external Lynch incision, transnasal endoscopic approach, or a transcaruncular approach. The majority of associated complications reported were temporary and associated with a Lynch incision approach. Further research distinguishing the respective complication rates of various AEAL approaches is required to determine the best approach and to better understand AEAL's clinical applicability in the management of intractable epistaxis.
