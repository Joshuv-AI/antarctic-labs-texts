Personality traits, technology affinity, and artificial intelligence readiness in medical students: a multinational cross-sectional study
Authors: Helmar Bornemann-Cimenti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence is increasingly embedded in clinical practice and medical education, yet the psychological determinants of students’ readiness remain poorly understood. We are aware of no study that has simultaneously examined personality traits, technology affinity and AI readiness in a single cohort.MethodsIn a cross-sectional online survey using convenience and snowball sampling, medical students from six continents completed three self-report instruments: the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale (MAIRS-MS), the Big Five Inventory–10 and the Affinity for Technology Interaction scale. Pearson correlations, multiple linear regression and ANOVA were applied. The protocol was prospectively registered (OSF: osf.io/7s89a).ResultsOf 1,920 respondents, 1,278 (66.5%) completed all instruments and constituted the analytic sample, whilst 642 (33.5%) with incomplete data were excluded by listwise deletion (54.8% female; mean age 19.9 ± 1.56 years; 65.9% European). Openness correlated with the Vision subscale (r = 0.669) and agreeableness with the Ethics subscale (r = 0.602); technology affinity was associated with overall readiness (r = 0.231; all p < 0.001). Male gender (B = 6.698), openness (B = 1.772) and agreeableness (B = 1.518) were independent predictors of overall readiness, whereas technology affinity did not retain an independent effect once personality and gender were accounted for. Men reported higher overall readiness (η² = 0.143); women scored marginally higher on ethical awareness.ConclusionsPersonality traits were independently associated with AI readiness, whereas technology affinity was associated with overall readiness only at the bivariate level. Given the cross-sectional design, these relationships denote associations rather than causal effects. Medical AI curricula should adopt differentiated instructional approaches informed by students’ psychological profiles.

Background

The integration of artificial intelligence into clinical medicine has accelerated markedly over the past decade, with applications now spanning diagnostic imaging, clinical decision support, drug discovery, and patient monitoring [1, 2]. As these technologies reshape the landscape of healthcare delivery, the question of how to prepare the next generation of physicians for an AI-augmented clinical environment has become a matter of considerable urgency. Medical education institutions worldwide face the challenge of equipping students not merely with technical knowledge of AI systems, but with the cognitive dispositions, critical appraisal skills, and ethical awareness necessary to employ such tools responsibly and effectively [3, 4].

In response to this challenge, the concept of ‘medical AI readiness’ has gained traction as a framework for understanding the multidimensional preparedness of healthcare learners. Karaca and colleagues developed the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale for Medical Students (MAIRS-MS), a validated psychometric instrument assessing perceived readiness across four domains: cognition (knowledge of AI terminology and logic), ability (practical competence with AI tools), vision (perception of AI’s transformative potential), and ethics (awareness of moral and regulatory dimensions) [5]. Since its publication, the MAIRS-MS has been employed in cross-sectional surveys across Saudi Arabia [6, 7], Iran [8], Jordan [9], Oman [10], and Malaysia [11], collectively demonstrating moderate levels of AI readiness among medical students worldwide, with notable variation across cultural and institutional contexts.

Despite this growing body of evidence, a significant gap persists in our understanding of what drives individual differences in AI readiness. The existing literature has largely focused on describing readiness levels and examining associations with demographic variables such as gender, year of study, and prior AI exposure. Conspicuously absent from this discourse is a systematic examination of the psychological antecedents of AI readiness, particularly the role of stable personality traits that are known to influence technology adoption behaviour more broadly. Personality psychology, particularly the Five-Factor Model (the Big Five), offers a well-established theoretical lens through which individual differences in technology engagement may be understood [12, 13]. The five dimensions—openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism—have been shown to predict attitudes toward novel technologies, willingness to adopt digital tools, and trust in automated systems across diverse populations and settings [14, 15].

A related but distinct construct is affinity for technology interaction, defined as a person’s tendency to actively engage with unfamiliar technical systems. Franke and colleagues developed the Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI) scale as a validated measure of this disposition, arguing that it captures a dimension of individual variation that is not fully reducible to personality traits alone but reflects accumulated experience with and confidence in digital environments [16]. In the context of medical AI readiness, technology affinity may serve as a proximal correlate, translating broader personality dispositions into concrete engagement with AI-specific tools and concepts.

To date, no published study has simultaneously examined the associations between validated measures of personality traits (BFI-10), technology affinity (ATI), and medical AI readiness (MAIRS-MS) within a single cohort of medical students. This study addresses that gap by investigating these relationships in a large, multinational sample. Specifically, we aimed to (a) determine which personality traits are associated with different dimensions of AI readiness, (b) assess the relationship between technology affinity and AI readiness, and (c) identify which psychological and demographic factors independently predict overall AI readiness in a multivariate model. These relationships can be situated within established accounts of technology acceptance. Models such as the Technology Acceptance Model and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology propose that engagement with a novel technology is shaped by dispositions and perceptions that precede its actual use [17, 18]. Read through this lens, stable personality traits function as distal dispositions, technology affinity as a more proximal orientation akin to perceived behavioural control and facilitating conditions, and AI readiness as a precursor of subsequent acceptance (Fig. 1). The framework is invoked here as an interpretive scaffold rather than as a path model to be estimated, since a cross-sectional design cannot adjudicate mediated pathways. Guided by this reasoning, we specified three directional expectations: that openness would be positively associated with the Vision dimension of readiness (H1); that agreeableness would be positively associated with the Ethics dimension (H2); and that technology affinity would be associated with overall readiness beyond the contribution of personality traits (H3). Gender was included as a demographic covariate in the regression model and examined in group comparisons, but without an a priori directional hypothesis; the gender differences reported below are therefore exploratory.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study employed a structured online survey distributed to medical students enrolled in accredited medical degree programmes worldwide. Data collection took place over a three-month period from 23 November 2024 to 20 February 2025. The study protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to data collection (osf.io/7s89a) and adhered to the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) [19]. The survey was administered via SoSci Survey (soscisurvey.de), a platform compliant with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), without recording IP addresses or employing tracking cookies.

Participants and recruitment

Medical students from all years of study were eligible for inclusion, provided they were actively enrolled in an accredited medical degree programme at the time of participation. Recruitment was conducted through social media platforms, university mailing lists, and professional networks. The survey was presented in English. Participation was voluntary, and all respondents provided electronic informed consent prior to accessing the questionnaire. In accordance with Austrian governmental regulations, formal ethics committee approval was not required for anonymous, non-patient research of this nature. Nevertheless, the study was conducted in strict adherence to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Recruitment therefore followed a convenience and snowball logic rather than probability sampling. A degree of self-selection cannot be excluded, in that students with a prior interest in artificial intelligence may have been more inclined to take part, a possibility that is weighed when interpreting the readiness estimates below. Because the questionnaire was offered in English alone, participation was additionally conditioned on a working command of the language, which may have shaped the linguistic and regional composition of the sample.

Instruments

The survey questionnaire was based on a combination of previously published tools.

Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale for Medical Students (MAIRS-MS)

The MAIRS-MS is a 22-item self-report instrument developed by Karaca, Çalışkan, and Demir, assessing perceived AI readiness across four subscales: Cognition (8 items), Ability (7 items), Vision (3 items), and Ethics (3 items) 5. Responses are scored on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The original validation study reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 and confirmed a four-factor structure through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses.

Big Five Inventory–10 (BFI-10)

Personality traits were assessed using the BFI-10, an ultra-short version of the Big Five Inventory comprising two items per dimension: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and openness to experience [20]. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale. The BFI-10 demonstrates acceptable test-retest reliability and convergent validity with the full BFI-44 across multiple samples and languages [20, 21].

Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI) scale

Technology affinity was measured using the ATI scale, a nine-item instrument assessing an individual’s tendency to actively engage with unfamiliar technical systems [16]. Responses are recorded on a six-point Likert scale. The ATI scale demonstrates good psychometric properties across multiple validation studies, with Cronbach’s alpha values typically exceeding 0.85 [16, 22].

Supplementary items

The survey additionally included a single item assessing willingness to use AI in medical education (five-point scale) and selected items from the Attitudes and Perceptions of Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare questionnaire addressing beliefs about AI’s benefits for patients, concerns about physician AI competence, and perceived need for AI oversight [23].

Statistical analysis

Only respondents who completed all items across all three core instruments were included in the final analysis. Internal consistency for each scale and subscale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Bivariate associations between personality traits, technology affinity, AI readiness subscales, and attitudinal items were examined using Pearson product-moment correlations with 95% confidence intervals. To identify independent predictors of overall AI readiness, a multiple linear regression model was constructed with the MAIRS-MS total score as the dependent variable and BFI-10 subscale scores, ATI score, and gender as independent variables. Gender differences across all study variables were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with eta squared (η²) reported as a measure of effect size, interpreted as small (η² ≈ 0.01), medium (η² ≈ 0.06), and large (η² ≥ 0.14) [24]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. As no a priori sample size calculation had been specified, a sensitivity analysis was performed to establish the smallest effect the regression model could reliably detect; with the achieved sample and seven predictors, the model afforded 80% power at α = 0.05 to detect an increment in explained variance corresponding to f² ≈ 0.011. Before interpretation, the assumptions underlying linear regression were examined. Multicollinearity was assessed through variance inflation factors and tolerance values; the normality of residuals was inspected through their skewness and kurtosis together with the Shapiro-Wilk statistic; homoscedasticity was tested using the Breusch-Pagan test; the independence of residuals was evaluated through the Durbin-Watson statistic; and the influence of individual observations was screened through Cook’s distance. Because the Breusch-Pagan test indicated a departure from homoscedasticity, heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors were computed alongside the conventional estimates. Model fit was summarised through R², the adjusted R², and the omnibus F-statistic. Standardised regression coefficients (β) were additionally computed to permit comparison of predictor strength across variables measured on different scales.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 1,920 individuals accessed the survey, of whom 1,278 (66.5%) completed all required items and constituted the analytic sample. The sample comprised 700 women (54.8%), 562 men (44.0%), and 16 individuals identifying as non-binary or diverse (1.3%). The mean age was 19.9 years (SD = 1.56), and participants were predominantly in the preclinical phase of their training (mean year of study = 1.52, SD = 1.33). The geographical distribution was weighted toward Europe (n = 842, 65.9%), followed by Asia (n = 196, 15.3%), North America (n = 99, 7.8%), South America (n = 72, 5.6%), Africa (n = 49, 3.8%), and Oceania (n = 19, 1.5%). Within Europe, the largest national contributions came from Germany (n = 167), Austria, and the Netherlands.

Reliability of measurement instruments

The MAIRS-MS demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.89). The ATI scale similarly showed high reliability (α = 0.87). Cronbach’s alpha values for the BFI-10 subscales ranged from 0.52 (conscientiousness) to 0.61 (openness). Whilst modest in absolute terms, these values are characteristic of ultra-short personality instruments and are consistent with published psychometric evaluations of the BFI-10 in other populations [20, 21], reflecting the well-documented trade-off between measurement brevity and internal consistency inherent in instruments designed for large-scale survey deployment.

Associations between personality traits and AI readiness

The correlational analyses revealed a differentiated pattern of associations between the Big Five personality traits and the four MAIRS-MS subscales (Table 1). Two correlations of large effect size emerged as the most prominent findings. Openness to experience was strongly and positively correlated with the Vision subscale (r = 0.669, 95% CI [0.636, 0.698], p < 0.001), indicating that students who score highly on intellectual curiosity and receptiveness to novel ideas also tend to hold favourable perceptions of AI’s transformative potential in medicine. Agreeableness showed a similarly strong positive correlation with the Ethics subscale (r = 0.602, 95% CI [0.566, 0.636], p < 0.001), suggesting that students characterised by warmth, cooperativeness, and concern for social harmony are more attuned to the ethical dimensions of AI implementation.

Several smaller but statistically significant associations were also observed. Extraversion was weakly negatively correlated with Cognition (r = − 0.093, p = 0.001), as was agreeableness (r = − 0.067, p = 0.017). Agreeableness showed a weak negative correlation with Ability (r = − 0.058, p = 0.038). Neuroticism was weakly negatively correlated with Vision (r = − 0.059, p = 0.035) and Ethics (r = − 0.068, p = 0.015). Conscientiousness showed no significant associations with any MAIRS-MS subscale.

Personality traits and willingness to use AI

Openness to experience showed a weak-to-moderate positive correlation with willingness to use AI in medical education (r = 0.227, 95% CI [0.175, 0.279], p < 0.001). Extraversion was negatively correlated with willingness (r = − 0.156, 95% CI [− 0.209, − 0.102], p < 0.001). Neuroticism (r = − 0.019, p = 0.492), conscientiousness (r = − 0.022, p = 0.433), and agreeableness (r = − 0.024, p = 0.382) showed no significant associations with willingness to use AI.

Technology affinity and its correlates

Affinity for technology interaction demonstrated a strong positive correlation with openness (r = 0.685, 95% CI [0.655, 0.713], p < 0.001), representing the largest bivariate association observed in the study. No other personality trait was significantly correlated with ATI scores. Technology affinity was positively associated with the overall MAIRS-MS score (r = 0.231, 95% CI [0.178, 0.282], p < 0.001) and with willingness to use AI in medical education (r = 0.168, 95% CI [0.114, 0.221], p < 0.001). Additionally, ATI showed a moderate correlation with the belief that AI will have a positive impact on medical education (r = 0.499, 95% CI [0.456, 0.539], p < 0.001).

Multivariate predictors of AI readiness

When all personality traits, technology affinity, and gender were entered simultaneously into a multiple regression model predicting the MAIRS-MS total score, three variables emerged as statistically significant independent predictors (Table 2). In standardised terms, male gender showed the strongest association (β = 0.390; B = 6.698, p < 0.001), followed by openness to experience (β = 0.196; B = 1.772, p < 0.001) and agreeableness (β = 0.140; B = 1.518, p < 0.001). Affinity for technology interaction, despite its moderate bivariate association with overall readiness (r = 0.231), did not retain a significant independent effect once personality and gender were taken into account (β = 0.051; B = 0.306, p = 0.156), a pattern consistent with its substantial shared variance with openness (r = 0.685). Extraversion, conscientiousness, and neuroticism did not contribute unique variance to the prediction of AI readiness once the other variables were accounted for. Because the contrast of interest was male versus female, the 16 participants who identified as diverse were excluded from this model (n = 1,262). The model accounted for a significant proportion of the variance in AI readiness (R² = 0.219, adjusted R² = 0.214, F(7, 1254) = 50.15, p < 0.001). Multicollinearity was not a concern: variance inflation factors ranged from 1.00 to 1.94 (openness 1.92, technology affinity 1.94), with tolerance values at or above 0.51, comfortably within accepted limits despite the substantial bivariate correlation between openness and technology affinity. The residuals were approximately normally distributed (skewness − 0.10, excess kurtosis − 0.22) and independent (Durbin-Watson 1.93), and no observation exerted undue influence (maximum Cook’s distance 0.02). The Breusch-Pagan test indicated heteroscedasticity (p < 0.001); the set of significant predictors and the direction of their associations were unchanged when heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors were applied.

Gender differences

Statistically significant gender differences were observed across the majority of study variables (Table 3). The largest effect among the MAIRS-MS readiness measures was found for the total score, where male students (M = 79.9, SD = 7.1) scored substantially higher than female students (M = 73.5, SD = 8.5) and diverse-identifying participants (M = 71.1, SD = 11.8), with a large effect size (η² = 0.143, p < 0.001). This pattern was consistent across all four MAIRS-MS subscales, with particularly pronounced differences in Cognition and Ability. Male students also reported higher ATI scores (M = 3.7, SD = 1.38) compared to female students (M = 3.4, SD = 1.4), with a small effect (η² = 0.014).

A noteworthy exception emerged in the Ethics subscale, where female students (M = 7.01, SD = 2.50) scored marginally higher than male students (M = 6.68, SD = 2.50), a small but statistically significant difference (p = 0.013) consistent with somewhat greater ethical sensitivity among women. Discussion

Principal findings

To our knowledge, this is the first study to simultaneously examine the associations between validated measures of personality traits, technology affinity, and medical AI readiness in a single multinational cohort of medical students. The findings reveal that personality traits, particularly openness to experience and agreeableness, are significant and independent predictors of AI readiness, even when demographic factors are taken into account, whereas technology affinity, although robustly correlated with readiness at the bivariate level, did not retain independent predictive value once personality and gender were considered. Crucially, the pattern of associations differed markedly across the four dimensions of AI readiness assessed by the MAIRS-MS, suggesting that the psychological determinants of cognitive preparedness, practical competence, visionary orientation, and ethical awareness represent distinct facets shaped by different constellations of individual characteristics. Gender was nonetheless the strongest predictor in the multivariate model in standardised terms (β = 0.386), an observation that merits attention in its own right and is taken up below.

The role of openness to experience

The strong positive correlation between openness and the Vision subscale (r = 0.669) stands out as one of the most robust findings in this study and is among the largest reported associations between a personality trait and an AI readiness dimension in the medical education literature to date. This result is theoretically coherent: openness to experience encompasses intellectual curiosity, aesthetic sensitivity, and a preference for novelty and variety [13], dispositions that would naturally incline students toward perceiving AI as an opportunity for clinical innovation rather than a threat to established practice. The equally strong correlation between openness and technology affinity (r = 0.685) suggests that these two constructs share substantial common ground, with open individuals gravitating toward technological engagement as an expression of their broader orientation toward exploration and discovery.

These findings are consistent with recent evidence from medical education research. Qi and colleagues reported that medical students with greater openness viewed AI more favourably and were more tolerant of its limitations [25]. Salem and colleagues similarly observed positive associations between openness and attitudes toward AI among nursing students [26]. In the broader technology adoption literature, Charness and Boot demonstrated that openness predicts willingness to engage with novel technologies across age groups [14], reinforcing the notion that this trait reflects a deep-seated orientation toward exploration that transcends specific technological contexts.

Agreeableness and ethical awareness

The strong positive correlation between agreeableness and the Ethics subscale (r = 0.602) represents a novel contribution to the literature on AI readiness. This association is plausible in light of the personality psychology literature, which consistently links agreeableness with prosocial concern, empathy, and sensitivity to fairness and justice [12, 14]. Students who are characteristically warm, trusting, and cooperative may be more attuned to questions of patient welfare, equitable access, transparency, and accountability—all central themes within the ethical discourse surrounding AI in healthcare. This finding has practical relevance for medical educators: approaches that emphasise collaborative deliberation and patient-centred values may resonate particularly well with agreeable students, whilst more structured ethical frameworks may be needed to engage those who score lower on this dimension.

Neuroticism as a barrier

Neuroticism showed small but consistent negative associations with the Vision and Ethics subscales. Students who experience greater emotional instability and anxiety may find the ambiguity inherent in AI systems particularly unsettling [27]. From a learning theory perspective, anxiety can increase cognitive load and reduce the mental resources available for critical engagement with complex, unfamiliar content [28]. The implication for educational practice is that high-neuroticism students may benefit from structured, low-stakes introductions to AI that minimise evaluative pressure and provide clear procedural guidance, thereby reducing the emotional barriers to engagement [29]. That neuroticism did not survive as a significant predictor in the multivariate model suggests that its bivariate associations may be partially confounded by its inverse relationship with openness.

Technology affinity and its overlap with openness

Contrary to the directional expectation specified at the outset (H3), technology affinity was not associated with overall AI readiness beyond the contribution of personality traits and gender. Technology affinity was robustly correlated with overall AI readiness at the bivariate level (r = 0.231), yet it did not retain independent predictive value once personality traits and gender were entered into the regression model (β = 0.051, p = 0.156). Its strong correlation with openness (r = 0.685) raises the question of whether technology affinity partially mediates the relationship between openness and AI readiness. Whilst the present cross-sectional design does not permit formal mediation testing, this hypothesis warrants investigation in future longitudinal research. From an educational standpoint, technology affinity is potentially more amenable to modification than personality: early, structured exposure to digital tools and AI applications may build the technological comfort that enhances readiness, even among students whose personality profiles are less naturally conducive to technology engagement. Because technology affinity did not retain an independent contribution once personality and gender were taken into account, and its association with readiness is largely shared with openness, this educational inference is necessarily tentative and would require confirmation before it could inform curriculum design.

Gender differences in context

The finding that male students reported significantly higher AI readiness across all MAIRS-MS subscales is consistent with a body of evidence indicating that men tend to report greater technology self-efficacy and more positive attitudes toward AI in educational and professional settings [30, 31]. Choudhary and Kandel observed similar patterns among management students [30], and Venkatesh and colleagues identified gender as a moderator of technology acceptance across multiple populations [18]. However, these findings must be interpreted with caution. The gender disparity was not uniform across all dimensions: female students scored higher on the Ethics subscale, suggesting that women may bring a distinctive strength in ethical awareness to their engagement with AI. Moreover, Al Shahrani and colleagues reported higher MAIRS-MS scores among female medical students in Saudi Arabia [7], indicating that cultural and institutional factors substantially modulate the direction and magnitude of gender differences in AI readiness.

The strikingly large effect size for the item ‘AI needs to be controlled’ (η² = 0.241) deserves particular attention. Male students expressed substantially stronger agreement with the need for AI oversight, a finding that warrants deeper exploration. One possible interpretation is that male students, who report both higher readiness and higher technology affinity, may differ in how they appraise the risks of AI. This reading is necessarily tentative, and several alternative explanations merit equal weight, among them differences in prior exposure to AI, digital literacy, response style, and the institutional and cultural contexts in which students are trained; the present data cannot distinguish between these accounts, and the finding should be treated as a question for further study rather than as evidence of superior risk awareness.

Strengths and limitations

Several methodological strengths distinguish this study from prior research on medical AI readiness. The sample of 1,278 respondents from six continents represents, to our knowledge, the largest multinational MAIRS-MS study conducted to date. The simultaneous deployment of three validated psychometric instruments enables a level of multifactorial analysis not previously reported. The prospective registration of the study protocol on OSF and adherence to CHERRIES reporting guidelines further enhance methodological transparency. The contribution of the study extends beyond its scale. It brings personality, technology affinity, and domain-specific readiness together within a single medical-student cohort, and it shows that the correlates of readiness are not uniform but differ systematically across its cognitive, practical, visionary, and ethical facets. The multinational composition allows these patterns to be observed beyond the confines of a single educational system, which bears on the relevance of the findings for medical curricula developed in different settings.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant acknowledgement. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The online, self-selected recruitment strategy introduces potential self-selection bias, whereby students with a pre-existing interest in AI may have been disproportionately attracted to the survey, possibly inflating readiness scores relative to the broader medical student population. The geographic composition of the sample, with nearly two-thirds from European countries, limits the generalisability of the findings to other cultural and educational contexts. The reliance on the BFI-10, whilst pragmatically justified for a large-scale survey, entails lower measurement precision than longer personality instruments; the modest internal consistency coefficients observed (0.52–0.61) are consistent with published norms [20, 21] but nonetheless attenuate observed correlations through measurement error. Finally, all outcome variables were self-reported, and objective measures of AI competence were not assessed; social desirability bias may therefore have influenced responses. The proportion of respondents who did not complete every instrument was substantial: of 1,920 who accessed the survey, 642 (33.5%) were removed by listwise deletion, and the analytic sample may differ systematically from non-completers in ways that cannot be observed here. Administration in English alone further restricts generalisability to students able to complete a survey in that language, which may under-represent certain regions and educational systems. The low internal consistency of the BFI-10 warrants emphasis in its own right: measurement error of this magnitude attenuates the observed associations, so the personality coefficients reported here are likely to be conservative, and a longer, more reliable personality instrument might have yielded more stable estimates. A further consideration concerns the magnitude of several associations: the strong correlations between openness and the Vision subscale, between agreeableness and the Ethics subscale, and between openness and technology affinity may in part reflect conceptual overlap between the instruments and shared method variance, since all constructs were captured through self-report Likert items within a single questionnaire, so the corresponding coefficients are better read as upper bounds on the substantive associations than as estimates untouched by common-method influence. The sample was also young and largely preclinical (mean age 19.9 years, mean year of study 1.52), so readiness was assessed for the most part before substantial clinical exposure to artificial intelligence, which limits extrapolation to students in the clinical phase of training.

Conclusions

This study provides comprehensive multinational evidence that personality traits are significant and independent predictors of medical AI readiness. Openness to experience emerged as the strongest personality correlate across multiple readiness dimensions, whilst agreeableness showed a distinctive association with ethical awareness. Technology affinity, although robustly correlated with readiness at the bivariate level, did not contribute predictive power beyond personality and gender; experiential comfort with digital technologies may nonetheless constitute a meaningful correlate of AI readiness that, unlike personality, may be amenable to modification. The differential patterns of association observed across the cognitive, practical, visionary, and ethical dimensions of the MAIRS-MS underscore that AI preparedness is not a monolithic construct but comprises distinct facets shaped by different psychological determinants. Medical education programmes aiming to enhance AI readiness should consider adopting differentiated instructional approaches that take into account the diverse personality profiles of their students, rather than relying on uniform curricula that may inadvertently advantage some learner types whilst leaving others behind. Because the design was cross-sectional, these associations cannot be interpreted causally, and the instructional implications drawn from them are best regarded as hypotheses for future evaluation rather than as established prescriptions.

Future research should address the limitations of this study through longitudinal designs tracking students from matriculation through clinical training, formal mediation analyses examining whether technology affinity mediates the openness–AI readiness relationship, and intervention studies testing personality-informed pedagogical approaches. Cross-cultural comparative research with adequately powered, non-European samples is essential for determining the generalisability of these associations across different healthcare systems and educational traditions.
