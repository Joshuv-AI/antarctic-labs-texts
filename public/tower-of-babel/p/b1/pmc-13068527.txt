T-tubular remodeling occurs in a cardiomyocyte-autonomous manner following pathological mechanical stiffness exposure
Authors: Michael Ibrahim, Vasiliki Courelli, Lan Cheng, Leo Song, Blanca Pamias-Lopez, Alexia Vite, Omar Toubat, Fotios Pitoulis, Benjamin Prosser, Kenneth Margulies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractT-tubules are invaginations of the cardiomyocyte sarcolemma, essential for excitation–contraction coupling and calcium-induced calcium release. Disruption of T-tubule structure contributes to contractile dysfunction in heart failure, yet the mechanisms driving this remodeling remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated whether mechanical load alone, independent of neurohormonal signals and extracellular matrix cues, is sufficient to induce pathological remodeling of T-tubules in isolated cardiomyocytes. Using a magnetorheological elastomer (MRE) culture system with tunable stiffness, we subjected adult rat cardiomyocytes to mechanical microenvironments spanning that of healthy (10 kPa) and diseased (50 kPa) myocardium. We assessed Ca2+-handling, T-tubule architecture, and the localization of key structural and functional proteins, as well as evaluated the role of the microtubule cytoskeleton using nocodazole-mediated depolymerization.We found that pathological substrate stiffness impaired Ca2+-transients, increased Ca2+-release dyssynchrony, and disrupted T-tubule organization, causing a shift from transverse to longitudinal orientation. We found no differences in key T-tubule total protein levels between experimental groups but observed spatial reorganization of TCAP in response to substrate stiffening. Microtubule depolymerization with nocodazole prevented pathological t-tubule remodeling, suggesting that microtubules act as mechanotransducers that orchestrate subcellular reorganization in response to mechanical cues. Our findings highlight the intrinsic ability of cardiomyocytes to remodel in response to mechanical load in the form of extracellular mechanical stiffness, in the absence of external hormonal influences, other cell types, the extracellular matrix, and other compensatory systemic responses, and that this is largely mediated by the microtubular network.Keywords: Mechanical load, Extracellular stiffness, T-tubules, Remodeling, Heart failure

Introduction

Excitation-contraction coupling (ECC) is the process by which depolarization of cardiomyocytes is followed by contraction. T-tubules are invaginations of the cardiomyocyte sarcolemma that enable proximal apposition between L-type Ca2+ channels and the ryanodine receptors (RyR2), and facilitate efficient calcium-induced calcium-release (CICR) and ECC [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. The T-tubule apparatus consists of an interconnected network of predominantly vertically oriented elements (transverse (T) tubules), that follow the striation pattern of the cardiomyocyte Z-lines, and lesser quantities of longitudinal and axial tubules that run along the long and short axis of the cardiomyocyte, respectively [2], [3], [4], [7], [8], [9].

We [4], [10], [11] and others [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18] have shown that in heart failure, T-tubular density is diminished and the T-tubular network becomes disorganized and irregular resulting in abnormal Ca2+-handling and contributing to systolic and diastolic dysfunction as well as arrhythmogenesis. As such, T-tubular associated proteins, which control the organization and structure of the T-tubular network, including caveolin 3 (Cav-3) [19], [20], bridging integrator 1 (BIN1) [21], [22], junctophilin-2 (JPH2) [23], [24], and telethonin (TCAP) [25] have received particular attention and therapeutic focus. A principal driver of T-tubular remodeling is mechanical load, which has been shown to dynamically modulate T-tubule density and regularity, as well as the abundance of T-tubule associated proteins in preclinical models [10], [11], [26]. Though in vivo studies are indispensable to understand the effects of mechanical load on heart function, the presence of multiple overlapping systemic responses to load, such as compensatory hormonal and fluid states changes, hinder our exact understanding of how and if mechanical signaling alone can drive T-tubular remodeling. Whether mechanical load alone is sufficient, in the absence of tissue and system-level compensatory responses, to drive cardiomyocyte T-tubular remodeling remains unknown.

Here, we investigated the effect of mechanical load in the form of extracellular mechanical stiffness on T-tubule structure and function using a magnetorheological elastomer (MRE) culture model [27], [28]. In this system, adult cardiomyocytes are cultured on an elastomeric polymer substrate impregnated with iron particles, which enables immediate and reversible tuning of the cardiomyocyte's mechanical microenvironment, via application of a magnetic field, across a stiffness range spanning healthy (10 kPa) to diseased (50 kPa) myocardium [28], [29], [30]. This reductionist model enables the study of cardiomyocyte mechanosensitive responses in the absence of external hormonal influences, other cell types, the extracellular matrix, and other compensatory systemic responses. Importantly, this platform models cell-level mechanical “load” through stiffness-dependent resistance imposed by the extracellular microenvironment and sensed through integrin-cytoskeletal coupling, rather than by chamber-level tissue stretch [29], [31]. Consistent with this, resting sarcomere length has previously been shown not to differ between soft (10 kPa) and stiff (50 kPa) MRE conditions [27], confirming that the observed stiffness-dependent effects occur independently of sarcomeric overstretch.

Using this model, we show that exposure to increased extracellular substrate stiffness (50 kPa) is sufficient to induce Ca2+-handling impairment, T-tubule network remodeling, and mislocalization of T-tubule–associated proteins in a cardiomyocyte-autonomous manner, providing new insight into how the T-tubular network is organized and regulated. Furthermore, we identify the microtubule network as a key mediator of these stiffness-dependent T-tubular changes, supporting the role of microtubules as active mechanosensors.

Methods

Magnetorheological elastomer (MRE) preparation

The MRE model was prepared as previously described [27], [28] to enable dynamic control of extracellular substrate stiffness within a range relevant to healthy and diseased adult myocardium. Briefly, the MRE is composed of equal amounts of Sylgard 527 silicone dielectric gel part A and part B (Dow Silicones Corporation, GMID: 02115387), as well as Carbonyl Iron Powder (CIP-CC, BASF, Ludwigshafen, Germany) at a 1:1 mass ratio. After the mixture was thoroughly mixed by using FlackTek SpeedMixer (DAC 330-100 Pro), 5 g of the mixture was poured into 35 mm culture dishes, degassed for 10 min, and baked for 24 h at 60 °C. A cylindrical neodymium rare-earth magnet (1–1/4″ × 1″; CMS Magnets, Garland, TX, USA) was used to modulate substrate stiffness by application of its magnetic field beneath the culture dish. MRE dishes were sterilized for 15 min with 70% ETOH, washed with sterile PBS, then coated with 10 μg/ml of laminin (Corning, 354232) and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C or overnight at 4 °C before use.

Isolated adult rat cardiomyocyte suspension was transferred and attached to laminin-coated MREs. In the absence of a magnetic field, the adherent cultures on the MRE dish experienced a substrate Young's modulus of approximately 10 kPa, corresponding to a compliant (“soft”) stiffness to represent healthy myocardium. For increased-stiffness conditions, an external magnet was applied immediately following cell attachment, resulting in exposure to a Young's modulus of approximately 50 kPa. These stiffness values have previously been shown to span the mechanical range from normal to diseased myocardium [28], [29], [30], [32], [33], [34], and to elicit differential cardiomyocyte functional responses [27], [35]. Both stiffness conditions involve active cell-matrix engagement through laminin-coated substrates under otherwise identical culture conditions. Therefore, any generalized culture-induced remodeling would be expected to affect both groups equally and cannot account for the stiffness-dependent differences observed. For the microtubule intervention study, either vehicle control (DMSO) or 1 μM nocodazole were applied at the same time as the stiffness exposure. Cells were maintained under these stiffness conditions for a total of 48 h.

Animal care and use procedures were performed in accordance with the standards set forth by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health. Protocols were approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All animals were housed in a facility with 12-h light/dark cycles and provided ad libitum access to water and chow. Temperature and humidity were checked daily to ensure that these parameters stay within appropriate ranges (20–26 °C, 30–70%, respectively).

Isolation & culture of adult rat cardiomyocytes

Primary ventricular cardiomyocytes were isolated as described previously [55]. Briefly, the heart was removed from 8- to 12-week-old Sprague Dawley rat anesthetized under isoflurane and retrograde - perfused on a Langendorff apparatus with a collagenase type II solution at 37 °C. The digested heart was then minced and triturated using a glass pipette. Minced tissues were transferred into a Tyrode's solution (130 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 0.5 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 0.33 mM NaH2PO4, 25 mM HEPES, 22 mM dextrose, 1 mM sodium lactate, supplemented with 2.5 mg/ml BSA) until the supernatant turned visibly cloudy. Dense undigested fragments, debris, and cardiomyocytes settled during sedimentation, while the supernatant contained interstitial cells and cardiac fibroblasts. The resulting supernatant was removed, the pellet was resuspended and passed through a 200-μm cell strainer to harvest cardiomyocytes, and significant amounts of rod-shaped cardiomyocytes were observed under a light microscope. Isolated cardiomyocytes were plated onto 10 μg/ml laminin-coated surfaces and maintained in M199 (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 1× insulin-transferrinselenium-X (Sigma), 1 μg/μl primocin (InvivoGen), and 20 mM HEPES (ThermoFisher Scientific), pH = 7.4, cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with the addition of 25 μmol/L of cytochalasin D (Cayman) in the media for 48 h. 10 μg/ml Hoechst 33258 (Invitrogen, H3569), a DNA-binding dye was utilized to stain cardiomyocytes nuclei. After 48-hr culture, cardiomyocytes on MRE dishes displayed the characteristic rod-shaped striated morphology.

For line scan Ca2+ transient and cell membrane staining, cells were detached from laminin-coated MREs by using 0.33 mg/ml Liberase (Roche Diagnostics GmbH) for 15–20 min at 37 °C. Cells were resuspended onto MatTek dishes with fresh M199 media without cytochalasin D.

Line scan Ca2+ transient

Ca2+ transients were examined in myocytes loaded with 20 μm fluo-4 AM (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA) for 30 min. Ca2+ transients were elicited by field stimulating myocytes at room temperature through a pair of platinum electrodes, with a 3 ms biphasic pulse 50% above threshold voltage and 1 Hz frequency by a cell stimulator (MyoPacer, IonOptix). A Zeiss LSM 880 scanning system (Oberkochen, Germany) was used for line scan Ca2+ transient imaging. A 512-pixel scan line was positioned randomly along the longitudinal axis of the cell, although care was taken to avoid crossing nuclei. After 10 to 30 s of pacing to achieve steady state, 10 traces were recorded. Images were then analyzed using Origin 10.1 (OriginLab Corporation, MA) to characterize the rising and decaying phase of the fluorescence recording. Standardized imaging conditions between experiments permitted reliable relative measurements of transient kinetics and amplitude using non-ratiometric Fluo-4 AM dye, but precluded absolute quantification of Ca2+ concentrations in nanomolar terms.

Labeling T-tubule structure on live cells

To observe the distribution of T-tubules, cell membranes were labeled by incubating living cardiomyocytes for 10 min with 10 μM di-8-ANEPPS (Molecular Probes), a hydrophobic voltage-sensitive fluorescent dye that has been extensively used to stain the surface membrane of excitable cells, followed by a 5 min washout at room temperature. Confocal images of the T-tubular network were sequentially scanned to obtain 1024 × 1024-pixel XY images along the Z-axis.

Protein isolation and gel electrophoresis

Cardiomyocytes were washed directly in the MRE dish by adding, then aspirating PBS, three times. Cell culture dishes were kept on ice throughout. Then, ice cold lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 3% SDS, 50 mM tris (pH 6.8), a small amount of phenol red, and 75 mM freshly added dithiothreitol to the dish. The lysis buffer was then diluted 1:1 with 50:50 glycerol with 2× Roche cOmplete Mini protease inhibitor tablets. Cardiomyocytes were scraped and cell lysate was collected in fresh tubes and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. For use, samples were thawed, briefly vortexed, and electrophoresed.

The proteins were separated on 4–20% Criterion™ TGX™ Precast Protein Gels (BIO-RAD) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were stained with Ponceau S to verify even transfer and equal loading, then pre-treated with SuperSignal Western Blot Enhancer (ThermoFisher) before applying blocking buffer. The membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies in Primary Antibody Diluent (ThermoFisher). The membranes were probed with anti-junctophilin-2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 40–5300); anti-BIN1 antibody, clone 99D (Millipore Sigma, 05-449); anti-telethonin antibody (G-11) (Santa Cruz, sc-25327) for overnight at 4 °C. Followed by incubation with secondary antibody conjugates, the proteins were visualized with an Odyssey DLx imager (kindly provided by the Dept. of Physiology, U Penn). Quantitative densitometric analysis was performed using Image Studio version 5.5 software (LI-COR).

Immunofluorescence labeling

To observe T-tubules, T-tubule associated proteins as well as the SR marker - RyR2 in cardiomyocytes, cells on 35-mm MRE dish were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with fish serum blocking buffer (Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C for RyR2 (Invitrogen, MA3-916), JPH2 (Invitrogen, 40-5300), TCAP (Invitrogen, PA5-93208), Amphiphysin II Antibody (BIN1) (2F11, Santa Cruz, sc-23918), α-actinin antibody sarcomeric (Sigma, A7732). The next day, the cells were washed once with 0.1% PBST followed by wash with PBS twice each at room temperature. Then, the appropriate Alexa Fluor 488 and 568 secondary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer at a 1:500 dilution ratio and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, the cells were washed three times to remove any unbound secondary antibodies. Cells on MRE dishes were immersed in PBS until being imaged.

Confocal microscopy imaging

Fluorescent images of live cardiomyocytes on MatTek dishes were viewed with a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope in Airyscan “super-resolution” mode with a 63× oil 1.4 numerical aperture objective. Three-dimensional images stacks were acquired. Raw Zeiss images were processed with a joint deconvolution plugin (ZEN3.5 blue).

Fluorescent images of stained cells on MRE dish were recorded with a Leica SP8 upright confocal microscope (kindly provided by the Penn Vet Imaging Core). Stacks of 20–30 images taken at 0.34 μm intervals were acquired with a 20 × 1.0 NA water immerse lens (Leica) with a 4×–10× zoom. Raw Leica image processing was performed using Huygens Essential (The Netherland).

Image analysis

Pre-analysis processing

Images were analyzed using ImageJ. A 235 μm × 645 μm region of interest from 3 confocal slices per cell was chosen in the middle of Z-slices or at least 2 μm distant from the top slice, the bottom slice and the cell nuclei. To minimize the influence of imaging artifacts, inconsistent labelling, staining located at the image edge and staining with low contrast were excluded. Only cells that were strictly rod-shaped by visual inspection via light microscopy were selected for confocal imaging. After exclusion of the surface sarcolemma, the whole Z-series was stacked to construct a 2D image with maximum intensity projection. Images were denoised with 3 × 3-pixel median filtering followed by a histogram normalization to correct any difference in the labeling levels between images. Fluorescent signals were binarized using the auto threshold function of ImageJ for further processing.

T-tubule density analysis

A measure of the abundance is the fractional area occupied by fluorescent staining. The percentage area in the binary images, representing the proportion of positive pixels (area fraction), was measured and taken to represent the signal density.

T-tubule directionality analysis

Fourier components method in ImageJ was used to profile orientation changes in these binary images. Structures at 0° angle were parallel to the cell longitudinal axis while those at 90° angle were orthogonal (or transverse) to the cell longitudinal axis.

T-tubule regularity analysis

To quantitatively assess the periodic pattern of striations of T-tubules labeled with either di-8-ANEPPS or WGA, JPH2, BIN1, TCAP and RyR2, spatial frequency analysis was used. Binary images were converted from the spatial to the frequency domain through a Fast-Fourier transformation (FFT) using TTorg plugin in ImageJ. The regularity of striations within the cardiomyocytes results in the emergence of a peak in the FFT spectrum of the image. This incidence of the peak is used to determine the striation periodicity, and its amplitude is considered as indication of an “organization index”. The extent of disorganization was estimated by the degree of departure from this regular striated pattern.

T-tubule spatial organization and morphology analysis

A region of interest (ROI) 10.8 μm × 21.6 μm × 6.4 μm was imaged in between the two nuclei of the cardiomyocyte labeled with Di-8-ANEPPS. The 3D T-tubule network was reconstructed by integrating sequential 2D confocal slices acquired at 0.34 μm intervals. Then, Zeiss Arivis788V4D 4.0-4.1 software was used to generate a centerline skeleton of the tubule network within the defined 3D ROI. The generated 3D ROI image underwent background correction, denoising, signal enhancement, and modification. Then, the T-tubules were identified and classified as axial, transverse, or longitudinal in orientation. Parameters such as the abundance (e.g. total tubule counts), spatial organization (e.g. tubule counts at different orientations) and morphology of T-tubules (e.g. tubule volume, length, cross-sectional area) were exported for further processing. Measurements were confined strictly to the imaged ROI, with identical dimensions and analysis parameters applied consistently across all datasets, ensuring consistent, unbiased quantification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. Quantitative datasets were tested for equal variances and normality using F-tests and Shapiro-Wilk tests, respectively, and then analyzed with parametric (two-tailed Student's t-test (with Welch correction where variances were unequal), one-way ANOVA) or non-parametric (Mann-Whitney U tests, Kruskal-Wallis) statistical tests as appropriate. All tests used are reported in the figure legends. N refers to the number of independent biological isolations (individual rats/hearts) and n refers to the number of cells analyzed per isolation (technical replicates). Data are presented as median ± interquartile range (IQR) throughout for consistency, given the greater proportion of non-parametric comparisons. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Pathological extracellular substrate stiffness disrupts cardiomyocyte [Ca2+]i transients

Cardiomyocytes cultured for 48 h under pathological (stiff) or physiological (soft) substrate stiffnesses using the MRE model were loaded with fluo-4 AM and assessed for Ca2+ handling using line-scanning confocal microscopy. We found that pathological substrate stiffness results in reduced Ca2+-transient amplitude (0.65 ± 1.60 vs 0.36 ± 1.45; p = 0.013; Fig. 1B, E), and decay times (0.43 ± 0.57 vs 0.48 ± 0.56; p = 0.045; Fig. 1F) compared to physiological conditions (Fig. 1F). Additionally, the pathological group had higher variability in the time to peak (TTP) Ca2+ amplitude, suggesting increased dyssynchrony compared to the physiological group (0.45 ± 1.09 vs 0.66 ± 1.50; p = 0.004; Fig. 1D).

Pathological extracellular substrate stiffness disrupts the T-tubular network in isolated cardiomyocytes

To assess the effect of pathological extracellular substrate stiffness on the cardiomyocyte T-tubular network, we stained live cardiomyocytes with di-8-ANEPPS. Pathological stiffness resulted in a disrupted tubular network (Fig. 2A) characterized by decreased tubular density (15.9 ± 21.2% vs 14.4 ± 10.6%; p = 0.026, Fig. 2B), as well as a shift in the orientation of the tubular network with increased % of longitudinal tubules (6.3 ± 21.4 vs 20.3 ± 38.8; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2D) and decreased % of transverse tubules (93.9 ± 21.7 vs 79.8 ± 39.0; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2C). Additionally, pathological stiffness resulted in significantly decreased T-tubular peak power of FFT, a measure of the regularity of the T-tubular network (71.5 ± 25.1 vs 58.0 ± 22.2; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2E), and significantly increased the luminal diameter of T-tubules (0.50 ± 0.36 μm vs 0.72 ± 0.36 μm; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2E).

We reconstructed the T-tubular network in physiological and pathological stiffness groups using AI-driven multi-dimensional image analysis pipeline (Zeiss Arivis Software) (Fig. 3A). When cultured under physiological substrate stiffness, the T-tubular network was comprised primarily of transversely oriented tubules (Fig. 3A, upper panel). In contrast, cardiomyocytes cultured under pathological conditions had decreased total tubular counts (168 ± 108 vs 145 ± 109; p = 0.018; Fig. 3B), with reduced transversely oriented tubules (158 ± 102 vs 114 ± 70.0; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3C) and increased longitudinal and axially oriented tubules (longitudinal: 3.0 ± 9.0 vs 17.0 ± 49.0; p < 0.0001; and axial: 2.0 ± 14.0 and 10.5 ± 27.0; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3C). Interestingly, although no changes were observed in total tubular network volume between the groups, the transverse tubule volume was significantly reduced in pathological conditions (61.1 ± 1.9 vs 77.9 ± 3.1 μm3; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3E), while the axial and longitudinal tubules were significantly increased (axial: 0.35 ± 0.54 vs 0.51 ± 0.96; p < 0.0001; and longitudinal: 0.37 ± 0.89 vs 0.51 ± 0.79; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3E). Cumulatively, the tubular volume lost from the transverse network was gained by the longitudinal and axial tubules. Moreover, pathological substrate stiffness led to elongated length of axial (1.52 ± 2.00 vs 2.04 ± 1.81 μm; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3G) and longitudinal tubules (1.47 ± 1.15 vs 1.80 ± 1.20 μm; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3G) while transverse tubules did not elongate and instead dilated, increasing in their cross-sectional area (0.43 ± 0.18 vs 0.48 ± 0.28 μm2; p < 0.0001; Fig. 3F).

Remodeling of the tubular network is not associated with changes in T-tubular protein expression

To probe the molecular determinants of the T-tubule remodeling caused by pathological stiffness conditions, we investigated the total protein expression levels of JPH2, BIN1, and TCAP all of which have been implicated in T-tubule formation and maintenance [24], [25], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]. Western blot analysis of whole cardiomyocyte lysates demonstrated no detectable differences between pathological (stiff) and physiological (soft) substrate conditions in JPH2 (n = 8, Fig. 4A, B), BIN1 (n = 10, Fig. 4A, C), and TCAP (n = 8, Fig. 4A, D).

TCAP localization is sensitive to extracellular substrate stiffness

TCAP, a Z-disc protein, has been suggested to act as a mechanosensor. Accordingly, and given the absence in TCAP protein abundance, we examined localization of TCAP in MRE-cultured isolated cardiomyocytes under x63 confocal microscopy. Both physiological and pathological stiffness groups showed striated TCAP staining (Fig. 5A, B), consistent with TCAP's Z-disc localization. However, pathological (stiff) conditions led to a higher % area stained by TCAP compared to physiological (soft) conditions (20.1 ± 8.5% vs 23.5 ± 6.9%; p = 0.0037; Fig. 5C), and greater regularity of TCAP staining (84.0 ± 17.0 vs 95.0 ± 15.0; p < 0.0001; Fig. 5D).

Directionality analysis revealed significantly greater TCAP oriented transversely (at 90o) in stiff conditions compared to soft conditions (4.23 ± 0.4% vs 2.92 ± 0.2%, Fig. 5E), representing a ∼ 45% relative increase in transverse TCAP orientation under pathological stiffness. Additionally, the regularity of TCAP was higher in pathological than physiological groups (84.0 ± 17.0 vs 95.0 ± 15.0; p < 0.0001; Fig. 5D).

Given the stark differences in T-tubular structure and the absence of abundance differences in TCAP and other T-tubular regulatory proteins between the two groups, it is possible that changes in distribution and localization of T-tubular associated proteins, such as TCAP, mediate T-tubular remodeling. Similar analyses were performed for the other T-tubular associated proteins: JPH2, BIN1 and α-actinin; produced a small, non-significant increase in JPH2 fluorescence density, and did not significantly alter the distributions of BIN1 or α-actinin (data not shown).

Nocodazole prevents pathological remodeling of the T-tubular system

Given the differences in localization of TCAP, we investigated whether inhibition of the microtubular system, which are known to serve as tracks for protein trafficking, could underlie the stiffness-dependent t-tubular differences. Isolated cardiomyocytes cultured on physiological or pathological conditions were treated with either vehicle control (DMSO) or 1 μM nocodazole at the beginning of culture and labeled for T-tubules using di-8-ANEPPS. Nocodazole is a microtubule depolymerizing agent, which inhibits the dynamic assembly of the microtubular network. As shown previously in Fig. 2, pathological (stiff) conditions led to a decrease in T-tubular density, regularity, and transverse elements, together with an increase in longitudinal elements and diameter of T-tubules. Addition of nocodazole prevented the observed decrease in T-tubule regularity (Fig. 6C), and significantly ameliorated the decrease in transverse and increase in longitudinal elements seen following culture in pathological conditions (Fig. 6D, E). Nocodazole also prevented T-tubular dilatation (Fig. 6F) though density was not affected (Fig. 6B). In their totality, our results suggest that the T-tubular remodeling is cardiomyocyte autonomous and mediated by the microtubular network.

Discussion

In this work, we show that the isolated cardiomyocytes, cultured under pathological substrate stiffness levels, develop T-tubular network disorganization, abnormal Ca2+-handling, and mislocalization of T-tubular associated proteins. Importantly, we also show that T-tubular remodeling in response to pathological stiffness is mediated by the microtubular network and prevented by nocodazole. By using the MRE platform to mechanically stress isolated cardiomyocytes in vitro, we demonstrate that this is a cardiomyocyte-intrinsic process and that stiffness-dependent mechanisms are sufficient to drive structural and functional remodeling.

In agreement with other studies studying the effects of pathological load [10], [11], [26], [41], [42], [43], [44], our work demonstrates that T-tubular remodeling occurs in response to pathological stiffness exposure, including tubule loss, dilation and increased irregularity, as well as a change in the composition of the t-tubular network with increased contribution of longitudinal and axial tubules. Though the effect of mechanical load in driving T-tubular remodeling has been shown before in vivo [10], our data provide novel and direct evidence that T-tubules remodel in a cardiomyocyte-autonomous manner in response to mechanical load in the form of disease-relevant extracellular stiffness, and that this remodeling has functional consequences seen as Ca2+-handling abnormalities.

To determine the molecular mechanisms underlying T-tubular remodeling, we assessed the change in abundance of several candidate T-tubule regulatory proteins such as JPH2, BIN1 and TCAP. Among those, JPH2 is a structural protein that bridges the sarcolemma to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and interacts with LTCC, Cav-3, and RyR2 to regulate Ca2+ induced Ca2+ release [36], [45], [46]. BIN1 regulates tubulogenesis and microdomain organization of T-tubule membrane, as well as forward trafficking of LTCCs to T-tubules [28], [29]. TCAP maintains T-tubule density, regularity [25] and adapts and responds to biomechanical stress [47]. No significant changes in the protein abundance of any T-tubular associated protein were observed between experimental groups, despite the overt differences in T-tubular structure. Interestingly, we observed different localization and distribution of TCAP between physiological and pathological conditions, highlighting its role as a mechanosensory [40], [47], [48], [49] and regulator of T-tubular organization [25], [50]. This supports previous studies showing altered localization of TCAP in disease, including transverse aortic banding (TAC) models of pressure-overload, where TCAP translocates to the nuclear compartment [40], and in samples from patients with dilated cardiomyopathy, where TCAP distribution is irregular or significantly decreased [47]. The exact mechanisms by which alterations in TCAP distribution are implicated in T-tubular remodeling require further investigation and will be the focus of future studies.

Importantly, while our group has shown that extracellular stiffness induces contractile dysfunction via microtubular-dependent mechanisms [27], we show that the observed T-tubular phenotype is also microtubule-mediated. Microtubules form part of the cardiomyocyte cytoskeleton and are crucial for protein transport, structural integrity, as well as regulation of cardiac mechanics [51], [52], [53]. In our MRE model, nocodazole, which reversibly inhibits microtubule polymerization and assembly, prevented pathological T-tubular remodeling of cells cultured in stiff (50 kPa) conditions. Similar results with nocodazole have been previously reported following pressure-overload hypertrophy in mice [23]. These results suggest that microtubules are not merely structural scaffolds but active mechanotransducers that sense and mediate stiffness-induced remodeling of the sarcolemma. Additionally, the fact that these effects were observed in isolated cardiomyocytes confirms a cell-autonomous mechanism, implicating microtubules as central players in translating mechanical cues into subcellular architectural changes. Given the dynamic nature of T-tubular remodeling, the recognized disruption of the T-tubular network in heart failure [4], [12], [54], and the ability of nocodazole to prevent this remodeling, such findings could merit therapeutic consideration. In the context of myocardial infarction, cardiomyocyte death and scar formation increase extracellular stiffness within and surrounding the infarct [32], [55]. This mechanically adverse environment could promote the structural deformations observed in the present study and thus may be partially mitigated through targeted microtubule inhibition of surrounding and border zone cardiomyocytes to achieve cytoprotection.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations which will be the focus of future studies. First, although we show no differences in abundance of canonical T-tubular associated proteins, we did not investigate post-translational modifications of key proteins such as JPH2, or TCAP, which may modulate their function under mechanical stress. Second, although the use of isolated adult rat cardiomyocytes is specifically valuable for highlighting the intrinsic cardiomyocyte response to extracellular stiffness in a model devoid of neurohormonal stimulation, multicellular interactions, and systemic compensatory mechanisms, it remains inherently reductionist. Accordingly, results should be interpreted as identifying cell-autonomous responses to changes in the mechanical microenvironment, rather than modelling long-term or organ-level pathological cardiac remodeling. Third, our study was limited to a single time point of structural and functional interrogation, and the absence of a time-zero or uncultured control means we cannot fully exclude a contribution of culture-induced remodeling to the observed phenotype, though we note that any such effect would be expected to affect both stiffness conditions equally. Lastly, while microtubule disruption with nocodazole prevented T-tubular structural deterioration, we did not assess the functional consequences of this intervention in terms of Ca2+-handling or contractility.

Conclusion

In summary, our work demonstrates that cardiomyocyte exposure to increased extracellular substrate stiffness is sufficient to drive T-tubule remodeling and Ca2+-handling impairment, in the absence of multicellular interactions, extracellular matrix interference, or neuroendocrine influences. We show that these effects are mediated largely by the microtubule network, highlighting its role as an active mechanosensory component rather than a purely structural scaffold. Together, these findings establish that cardiomyocyte-intrinsic mechanisms are capable of sensing and responding to disease-relevant mechanical stiffness, and implicate the microtubule cytoskeleton as a potential therapeutic target in the early stages of stiffness-driven cardiac remodeling.
