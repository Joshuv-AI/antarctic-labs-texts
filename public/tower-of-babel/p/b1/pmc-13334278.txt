Pharmacophore-based virtual screening, molecular docking, MD simulation, MM-GBSA energy calculation and DFT analysis for the in silico discovery of a SaFtsZ inhibitor
Authors: Sundarrajan T., Neerugatti Dora Babu, Anitha K. N., Sadik Shaik, Shankar M., Shankar Thapa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance demands the discovery of new antibacterial targets and inhibitors. Staphylococcus aureus filamenting temperature-sensitive protein (SaFtsZ), an essential cytoskeletal protein involved in bacterial cytokinesis and Z-ring formation, has gained attention as a promising target for antibacterial drug discovery. In the present study, an integrated computational strategy involving pharmacophore mapping, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and density functional theory (DFT) analysis was employed to identify potential SaFtsZ inhibitors. Initially, large compound library was screened from the Pharmit database using a structure-based pharmacophore model to identify molecules with key interaction features required for SaFtsZ inhibition. The selected 200 candidates were further evaluated through molecular docking to determine their binding affinity and interaction pattern within the active site of SaFtsZ. Among the screened molecules, compound 15 (CID 135468497) exhibited the highest binding affinity with a docking score of −10.8 kcal mol−1. Subsequent MD simulation confirmed the stability of the protein–ligand complex, while DFT analysis provided insights into the electronic characteristics and reactivity of compound 15. These findings highlight compound 15 as a computationally predicted scaffold for the development of SaFtsZ-targeted antibacterial agents. However, experimental validation is required to confirm the computational results.

Introduction

The rapid escalation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a major global health concern, significantly limiting the effectiveness of existing antibiotics and necessitating the identification of novel therapeutic targets.1 Among pathogenic bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus remains a clinically significant organism responsible for a wide range of infections, including skin infections, pneumonia, and bloodstream disorders.2 The increasing prevalence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) further underscores the urgent need for alternative antibacterial strategies targeting essential and less-explored bacterial proteins.3

Filamenting temperature-sensitive protein Z (FtsZ) is a highly conserved cytoskeletal protein that plays a central role in bacterial cell division. It polymerizes in a GTP-dependent manner to form the Z-ring at the future site of cytokinesis, serving as a scaffold for the recruitment of additional division proteins.4,5 Given its essential function, FtsZ has attracted considerable attention as a promising antibacterial drug target.6 Inhibition of FtsZ assembly or its GTPase activity can disrupt cell division, ultimately leading to bacterial cell death, making it a viable target for the development of novel antibacterial agents.7,8

Despite growing interest, the discovery of potent and selective FtsZ inhibitors remains limited, partly due to the dynamic nature of its binding pockets and the complexity of its polymerization mechanism. Computational approaches offer an efficient and cost-effective strategy to identify and optimize potential inhibitors by exploring molecular interactions at an atomic level.9 Structure-based pharmacophore modeling enables the identification of key interaction features required for ligand binding,10 while molecular docking provides insights into binding affinity and interaction patterns within the active site.11,12 Furthermore, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation allows evaluation of the stability and conformational behavior of protein–ligand complexes under physiological conditions,13 and density functional theory (DFT) analysis offers an understanding of the electronic properties and reactivity of candidate molecules.14,15

In the present study, an integrated computational workflow combining pharmacophore mapping, molecular docking, MD simulation, and DFT analysis was employed to identify potential inhibitors targeting Staphylococcus aureus FtsZ (SaFtsZ). By systematically screening compounds from the Pharmit database and evaluating their structural, dynamic, and electronic characteristics, this study aims to propose promising scaffolds for SaFtsZ inhibition. Importantly, the findings are intended to provide a hypothesis-generating foundation for further experimental validation and rational drug design.

Materials and methods

Protein selection and preparation

The crystal structure of SaFtsZ (PDB ID: 7OMJ, resolution: 1.57 Å, https://www.rcsb.org/structure/7OMJ) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank.16 FtsZ is a key bacterial cytoskeletal cell division protein. It is an essential bacterial cell division protein and a structural homolog to eukaryotic tubulin. Its structural and functional properties directly impact how antimicrobial resistance develops.17,18 Therefore, this protein was selected for this study. Protein preparation was carried out using AutoDock Tools v1.5.7,19 which involved removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens, assignment of Kollman charges, and conversion of the structure into the required PDBQT format.20

Protein validation

The protein was validated using Ramachandran plot generated from PDBSum server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/).21 The PDBSum offers PROCHECK services for a comprehensive evaluation of the stereochemistry of a protein structure. This evaluates the overall quality of the structure in comparison to well-refined structures of equivalent resolution and identifies areas that may require more examination.22

Pharmacophore modeling and virtual screening

A structure-based pharmacophore model was generated using the Pharmit server (https://pharmit.csb.pitt.edu). The Pharmit server is an open-source, web-based platform designed for interactive, structure-based virtual screening of massive chemical libraries. The platform search through compound databases containing millions of chemical molecules to find potential new drugs. It works by matching candidate molecules to a target receptor using 3D pharmacophores (spatial arrangements of chemical features, and volumetric aligned molecular shapes) and shape constraints.23 The SaFtsZ protein along with co-crystal ligand was (GCP) uploaded to the Pharmit server and MolPort was selected as search database (https://www.molport.com/). The binding site water molecules were ignored. The GTP-binding/nucleotide-binding site (NBS) was used to generate the pharmacophore model. Approximately 5.2 million compounds (around 79 million conformers) available in MolProt. Approximately 7000 compounds were initially screened, and candidate molecules were filtered based on their geometric alignment with the pharmacophore model. Only compounds exhibiting RMSD and mRMSD values below 2.0 Å were retained. To improve the drug-like characteristics of the screened compounds, molecules with molecular weights greater than 500 Da were excluded. A single representative conformer was selected for each compound to avoid redundancy. The retained hits were subsequently minimized and preliminary score of −5.0 kcal mol−1 was selected as threshold (within the Pharmit server). Finally, a set of 200 compounds that were advanced for detailed molecular docking studies.

Ligand preparation

The selected 200 compounds were downloaded from the Pharmit server and prepared using Open Babel tool.24 All the ligands were optimized and minimized using the MMFF94 force field25 to obtain stable conformations suitable for docking studies. Finally, all the ligands were converted into PDBQT format and saved in working directory for docking study. As of our best knowledge, currently no FDA approved FtsZ inhibitors are available on the market. Notably, TAXIS Pharmaceuticals developed an FtsZ inhibitor, TXA709 (CID: 75203933), which demonstrated favorable in vivo pharmacodynamic properties. TXA709 was chosen as the positive control for the in silico investigations (Fig. 1A). It is an orally active anti-MRSA agent that successfully completed a Phase I clinical trial without any serious adverse events.26

(A) 2D structure of FtsZ inhibitor Tax709. (B) Ramachandran plot of 7OMJ protein showing the stability and stereochemistry pattern.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.0. AutoDock Vina is a widely used, open-source molecular docking and virtual screening engine. It is designed to predict how ligands bind to a target-a critical step in structure-based drug discovery to predict binding modes and affinities.27 The grid box was defined around the active site based on the co-crystallized ligand coordinates (Table 1). Docking performed at 8 exhaustiveness and 10 ligand–protein interaction poses predicted. Default docking parameters were applied, and binding affinities were evaluated in terms of docking scores (kcal mol−1). The best binding pose for each ligand was selected based on minimum binding energy and favorable interaction profiles.

Visualization and interaction analysis

Protein–ligand interactions were analyzed and visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer 2021, PyMOL 3.1 (https://www.pymol.org/) and ProteinPlus web server (https://proteins.plus/).28 The 2D interaction diagrams were generated computationally to identify hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking interactions within the binding pocket.

Docking protocol validation

The docking protocol was validated by redocking the co-crystallized ligand into the FtsZ active site. The native ligand was extracted and superimposed with the redocked pose using PyMOL v3.1 software (https://www.pymol.org/), and the RMSD value was calculated to assess docking accuracy.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The dynamic stability of the top-ranked protein–ligand complexes was evaluated using the Desmond module of Schrödinger 2022 release. The system was solvated using the TIP3P water model in an orthorhombic box, and Na+/Cl− ions were added to neutralize the system. Energy minimization was carried out using OPLS3s force field.29 The equilibration was performed under NPT conditions (300 K and 1 atm). Finally, 100 ns production simulation was performed. Trajectory analyses, such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), polar surface area (PSA), solvent accessible surface area (SASA) and interaction stability, were carried out.30

Molecular mechanics generalized born surface area (MM-GBSA) binding energy calculation

MM-GBSA is a computational method used to estimate the binding free energy (ΔGbind) between a protein and a ligand. It provides a balance between computational speed and accuracy, making it highly valuable in drug discovery and molecular dynamics studies (eqn (1)). Binding free energy calculations were performed using the Prime MM-GBSA module of Schrödinger.31 The final 10 ns representative frames from MD trajectories were used to estimate binding free energies, considering van der Waals, electrostatic, and solvation contributions.321ΔGbind = Δcomplex − (ΔGreceptor + ΔGligand)

Density functional theory (DFT) analysis

Quantum chemical calculations were conducted using Gaussian 09 software (https://gaussian.com/). It is a widely-used, general-purpose quantum chemistry software package.33 Geometry optimization and electronic structure calculations were performed using Gaussian software at the B3LYP/6-31G level of theory.34,35 The calculations were conducted in the gas phase, and solvent effects were not explicitly considered. Frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) and related quantum chemical descriptors were calculated to obtain qualitative insights into the electronic characteristics of the selected compound.36

Drug likeness prediction

The drug-likeness properties of compound 15 were predicted using the ADMETlab 3.0 server (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/).37 The molecular structure of compound 15 was submitted in SMILES format to evaluate its physicochemical and drug-likeness parameters.

Results

Ramachandran plot analysis

The Ramachandran plot statistics demonstrated good stereochemical quality of the protein structure. A total of 251 residues (95.4%) were located in the most favored regions, while 12 residues (4.6%) were observed in the additionally allowed regions (Fig. 1B). Notably, no residues were found in generously allowed or disallowed regions, indicating the absence of major conformational strain or steric clashes.

Pharmacophore features

Application of the pharmacophore matching criteria (RMSD and mRMSD <2.0 Å), molecular weight filtering (<500 Da), conformer selection, and preliminary score threshold (≤−5.0 kcal mol−1) reduced compounds to 200 candidates for subsequent docking analysis. The pharmacophore model generated from the co-crystallized ligand (GTP-binding/NBS) of SaFtsZ revealed a total of eight key features, comprising two aromatic rings, three hydrogen bond donors, and three hydrogen bond acceptors (Table 2 and Fig. 2). The aromatic features are positioned at coordinates (53.6, −40.5, 18.3) and (54.4, −40.6, 20.3), indicating closely aligned hydrophobic interaction regions that may facilitate π–π stacking within the binding pocket. Hydrogen bonding features are densely clustered, particularly around coordinates (52.8, −42.9, 25.1) and (52.5, −44.1, 22.6), where both donor and acceptor characteristics overlap, suggesting possible dual interaction capability. An additional acceptor site at (52.5, −41.3, 18.7) further supports the presence of a multi-point hydrogen bonding environment. All pharmacophore features were defined with a uniform radius of 1.0 Å, indicating a spatially constrained interaction region that may favor ligands with precise geometric alignment.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking of the pharmacophore-filtered compounds against SaFtsZ revealed docking scores ranging from −10.0 to −10.8 kcal mol−1 (Table 3), indicating a generally favorable interaction profile within the active site. Among the screened 200 molecules, compound 15 (CID: 135468497) predicted the highest docking score (−10.8 kcal mol−1), followed by compound 140 (CID: 16219405, and docking score = −10.7 kcal mol−1) and several candidates with comparable scores (−10.6 kcal mol−1), suggesting a narrow distribution of high-affinity binders. However, the FtsZ inhibitor Txa709 and co-crystal ligand showed docking score of −9.1 kcal mol−1 each. Compound 15 exhibited the most favorable docking score among the screened compounds; however, the differences among the top-ranked candidates were relatively small. Therefore, compound 15 was selected as a representative lead candidate for further dynamic and electronic characterization.

Docking interaction

The SaFtsZ active site is composed of key residues, including Gly22A, Gly72A, Gly108A, Gly110A, Ala71A, Ala73A, Arg143A, Glu139A, Asn166A, and Arg29A. The co-crystal ligand exhibited multiple stabilizing interactions within the active binding pocket, indicating strong ligand accommodation. The native ligand occupied the catalytic pocket deeply and established an extensive hydrogen-bonding network with several key residues (Fig. 3A). Multiple phosphate oxygen atoms of the native ligand formed strong polar interactions and hydrogen bonds with the glycine-rich phosphate-binding region, contributing to tight stabilization of the ligand within the active site. Additionally, π–π stacking interactions with Phe183A further stabilized the ligand–protein complex, indicating favorable occupancy of the binding cavity.

Molecular interaction of native co-crystal ligand (A) and compound 15 (B) in the active site. (C) Molecular interaction of reference drug (Txa709) against FtsZ protein active site. (D) Superimposition of native (green) and redocked co-crystal (purple) ligand within the FtsZ active site for docking validation.

In comparison, compound 15 demonstrated a well-defined binding orientation within the active site and occupied a similar binding pocket as the co-crystal ligand, suggesting a comparable binding mode. Compound 15 formed several hydrogen-bond interactions with important amino acid residues, including Gly110A, Thr109A, Gly72A, Asn166A, Thr133A, and Arg143A (Fig. 3B). The oxygen atom of the phthalimide moiety of S15 was engaged in an H-bond with the OH group of Thr133 and NH of Asn166 chain A. Moreover, the benzene ring of the phthalimide moiety was optimally oriented for a favorable π–π aromatic interaction with the Phe183, and the nitrogen and oxygen of pyrimidine formed fruitful hydrogen interactions with Thr109 and Gly110 respectively. The presence of both hydrogen bonding and aromatic interactions suggests that compound 15 effectively anchors within the catalytic cavity and may exhibit favorable inhibitory potential comparable to the native ligand.

The molecular interaction pattern of the reference drug TXA709 within the active site of the FtsZ protein is given in Fig. 3C. TXA709 exhibited stable binding through multiple hydrogen-bond interactions with key amino acid residues, including Thr106, Gly105, Arg140, and Asn22, which may contribute to ligand stabilization within the binding pocket. Additionally, hydrophobic and π–π interactions with Phe180 and surrounding residues enhanced binding affinity and proper ligand orientation. The surface representation further confirms that TXA709 fits well within the active site cavity of FtsZ, supporting its suitability as a reference ligand for comparative docking analysis.

Docking protocol validation

The docking protocol was validated through redocking of the co-crystallized ligand into the FtsZ binding pocket (Fig. 3D). The resulting RMSD value of 2.79 Å demonstrated acceptable agreement between the predicted and experimental binding conformations, indicating the decent reliability and predictive capability of the docking protocol for subsequent molecular docking analyses.

MD simulation

RMSD

The RMSD profile of the SaFtsZ–compound 15 complex over the 100 ns simulation is presented in Fig. 4. The protein backbone RMSD initially increases from approximately 1.6 Å to ∼3.0 Å within the first 20 ns, followed by a gradual stabilization around 3.4–3.8 Å for the remainder of the simulation. Although these values are slightly higher than the range commonly reported for highly rigid globular proteins, the trajectory did not exhibit continuous drift during the latter stages of the simulation. The typical RMSD range of 1–3 Å was observed after approximately 30–40 ns, indicating a relatively stable conformational behavior of the complex. The ligand RMSD (Lig fit Prot) shows moderate fluctuations, ranging between ∼2.5 Å and 4.0 Å, with occasional transient spikes approaching ∼5.0 Å, particularly between 50–75 ns. However, the ligand RMSD does not exhibit a sustained increase relative to the protein RMSD, indicating that the ligand largely remains associated with the binding pocket despite conformational adjustments.

RMSF

The RMSF analysis of compound 15 (Fig. 5A) indicates relatively moderate atomic fluctuations, with values ranging from approximately 0.3 Å to 1.6 Å. Most ligand atoms exhibit fluctuations below 1.2 Å, suggesting limited internal flexibility, while a few regions show slightly higher mobility (∼1.5–1.6 Å), possibly corresponding to solvent-exposed or less constrained functional groups.

RMSF analysis of compound 15 and SaFtsZ protein. (A) Ligand RMSF showing atomic fluctuations within 0.3–1.6 Å, indicating moderate flexibility. (B) Protein RMSF showing residue-wise fluctuations. Secondary structure elements (α-helices and β-strands) are highlighted, and ligand-contacting residues are indicated.

The protein RMSF profile (Fig. 5B) demonstrates that the majority of residues fluctuate within 0.6–1.5 Å, indicating overall structural stability during the simulation. However, distinct peaks are observed at specific regions, notably around residues ∼60–70 (∼3.5 Å) and ∼160–170 (∼5.5 Å), suggesting localized flexibility. These fluctuations likely correspond to loop regions or terminal segments, which are typically more dynamic compared to structured α-helices and β-strands.

Importantly, residues involved in ligand interactions (indicated by green vertical bars) predominantly exhibit lower RMSF values (∼0.6–1.2 Å), suggesting relatively stable contacts between the ligand and binding pocket. The alignment of lower fluctuations with secondary structural elements (helical and strand regions) further supports the structural integrity of the protein during the simulation.

Ligand properties

The ligand property analysis over the 100 ns simulation (Fig. 6) provides insights into the conformational behavior and stability of compound 15 within the SaFtsZ binding pocket. The ligand RMSD remains relatively stable within the range of approximately 2.0–3.0 Å, with minor fluctuations, suggesting that the ligand maintains its binding orientation without significant deviation from the initial conformation. The rGyr fluctuates narrowly between ∼5.0 and 5.4 Å, indicating a relatively consistent compactness of the ligand structure throughout the simulation. The number of intramolecular hydrogen bonds varies, suggesting intermittent internal stabilization that may contribute to conformational adaptability. The MolSA remains within ∼450–480 Å2, while the SASA shows moderate variation between ∼160 and 300 Å2, indicating dynamic solvent exposure during the simulation. Additionally, the PSA fluctuates in the range of ∼300–340 Å2, reflecting consistent contributions from polar functional groups.

Ligand torsion analysis

The analysis reveals that several torsional angles are confined to preferred regions, with probability density peaks predominantly observed around −90°, 0°, and +90°, indicating energetically favorable conformations (Fig. 7). Certain rotatable bonds exhibit narrow distributions, suggesting restricted rotational freedom, likely due to stabilizing interactions within the binding site. In contrast, a few torsions display broader distributions spanning −180° to +180°, reflecting localized flexibility that may facilitate adaptive fitting of the ligand. The radial plots further indicate that most torsions stabilize after the initial phase of the simulation, with no continuous drift toward high-energy conformations. Additionally, the alignment of torsional populations with low-energy regions of the potential energy curves suggests that the ligand does not experience significant conformational strain while bound to the protein. This balance between rigidity and flexibility may support stable binding while allowing minor adjustments within the active site.

The protein–compound 15 interaction profile of the SaFtsZ–compound 15 complex over the 100 ns simulation reveals a consistent network of stabilizing contacts. The interaction schematic (Fig. 8A) indicates that key residues such as GLU A:139 (∼55%), PHE A:183 (∼52%), and GLY A:106 (∼30%) maintain interactions for a substantial portion of the simulation, suggesting their potential relevance in ligand stabilization. Notably, hydrophobic interactions, particularly involving PHE A:183, appear prominent, while hydrogen bonding interactions are distributed across glycine-rich and polar residues within the binding pocket. The interaction timeline (Fig. 8C) shows that the total number of contacts fluctuates approximately 5 interactions throughout the simulation, with a relatively higher interaction density observed during the initial ∼30 ns, followed by a moderately stable interaction pattern. Residue-wise mapping indicates that several contacts are maintained consistently across the trajectory, although transient interactions are also evident.

The PCA of the SaFtsZ–compound 15 complex (Fig. 9A) reveals the conformational space sampled during the 100 ns simulation. The projection along the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) shows a clustered distribution with gradual transitions, indicating the presence of distinct yet connected conformational states. Initial simulation frames (lower time scale) are primarily localized in one region, while later frames shift toward a separate cluster, suggesting conformational adaptation over time rather than abrupt structural changes. The spread along PC1 (approximately −80 to +40) and PC2 (−40 to +60) indicates moderate structural variability, whereas clustering within defined regions suggests that the system does not explore highly dispersed conformations. This behavior may reflect a balance between structural flexibility and overall stability of the protein–ligand complex.

MM-GBSA calculation

The MM-GBSA analysis predict binding affinity of the compound 15 toward the target protein with a total binding free energy (ΔGbind) of −75.03 ± 4.98 kcal mol−1 (Table 4). The binding was mainly driven by van der Waals interactions (−50.22 ± 4.78 kcal mol−1) and lipophilic interactions (−30.01 ± 5.09 kcal mol−1), indicating dominant hydrophobic stabilization within the binding pocket. Electrostatic interactions also contributed favorably with a coulombic energy of −19.87 ± 4.99 kcal mol−1. This energy calculation was based in relative contribution of individual energy component (even ΔGbind covalent) instead of absolute ΔG. We reported individual energy component to show the contribution. However, the compound 15 is not covalent inhibitor.

Discussion

The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance has intensified the search for alternative antibacterial targets, particularly those involved in essential cellular processes.38,39 In this context, FtsZ has emerged as a promising target because of its critical role in bacterial cytokinesis and the absence of a direct human homolog. The present study employed an integrated computational workflow combining pharmacophore modeling, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and DFT analysis to identify potential SaFtsZ inhibitors from the Pharmit database. Our findings collectively provide a preliminary and hypothesis-generating framework for understanding the possible interaction behavior of the identified compounds, particularly compound 15.

The pharmacophore model generated from the co-crystallized ligand of SaFtsZ identified key aromatic and hydrogen-bonding features within the active site. The presence of two aromatic centers along with multiple hydrogen bond donor and acceptor regions suggests that both hydrophobic and polar interactions may contribute to ligand stabilization. The clustering of donor and acceptor features within a confined spatial radius further indicates a structurally constrained binding environment. Such interaction patterns are generally consistent with previously reported FtsZ inhibitors,40,41 where aromatic stacking and hydrogen bonding have been recognized as important contributors to binding affinity.

The drug-likeness analysis revealed that compound 15 deviates from the recommended ranges for ring count (nRing), TPSA, and hydrogen bond acceptors (nHA). These deviations can be rationalized by the structural characteristics of the molecule, which contains phthalimide ring system, phenyl ring, and heterocyclic ring systems, numerous oxygen-containing functionalities, and several nitrogen atoms within the purine-like scaffold (Fig. 12). While an elevated ring count may increase molecular rigidity, the aromatic framework may contribute to favorable hydrophobic and π-mediated interactions within the SaFtsZ binding pocket. Similarly, the high TPSA and nHA values reflect the abundance of polar functional groups that can participate in hydrogen bonding but may also reduce passive membrane permeability. Therefore, although compound 15 demonstrated favorable computational interactions with SaFtsZ, its physicochemical profile suggests that further structural optimization may be necessary to improve developability while preserving target-binding characteristics.

2D structure of compound 15.

The docking analysis identified several compounds with comparable predicted docking scores toward SaFtsZ. Notably, the docking scores of the top-ranked compounds were distributed within a relatively narrow range, suggesting that the ranking should be interpreted cautiously. Small differences in predicted docking score may not necessarily translate into meaningful differences in biological activity. Consequently, compound 15 was selected for further investigation not solely because it exhibited the lowest docking score, but because it represented one of the top-ranked candidates with a favorable interaction profile and binding orientation within the active site.

The interaction patterns observed for compound 15 suggest that aromatic stacking and hydrogen bonding may collectively contribute to binding stabilization. Recent computational investigations of FtsZ inhibitors have likewise identified hydrogen-bond networks and aromatic interactions as important contributors to ligand stabilization within the binding pocket.42,43 Recurring interactions involving residues such as PHE A:183, GLU A:139, and glycine-rich regions may indicate their possible functional relevance in ligand recognition. Similar residues have been implicated in ligand stabilization in previously reported FtsZ inhibitor studies, particularly in compounds containing fused aromatic or heterocyclic moieties.44 In contrast, lysine rich region of FtsZ protein may indicate possible active region for the interaction of natural product especially Andrographolide and Andrograpanin.45 However, P. Sciò et al. explored the hydrophobic interaction as the contributing factor in binding mode in FtsZ protein and excellent IC50 value of 2 µg mL−1 against S. aureus.46 Recent studies have shown that active FtsZ inhibitors commonly occupy the interdomain cleft or allosteric binding region of FtsZ and are stabilized through a combination of hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions with residues located around the ligand-binding pocket.47 In particular, virtual screening and experimental validation studies have demonstrated that successful FtsZ inhibitors frequently engage residues involved in maintaining ligand recognition within this region.48–50

The MD simulation analyses provided additional insight into the dynamic behavior of the complex under simulated physiological conditions.51 The observed decent equilibration behavior and absence of continuous large structural drift suggest that the complex retained an overall stable conformational arrangement during the simulation period. At the same time, moderate fluctuations observed in both protein and ligand trajectories may reflect the intrinsic flexibility of the FtsZ binding region rather than instability of the complex itself. Such behavior is not uncommon in flexible binding sites and does not necessarily imply ligand dissociation. Since FtsZ is known to undergo conformational rearrangements during polymerization and GTP-dependent assembly,16,52,53 a certain degree of structural flexibility may be expected and potentially important for ligand accommodation.

The ligand property and torsional analyses suggest that compound 15 maintained an energetically acceptable conformational state during the simulation. The relatively stable compactness and moderate solvent accessibility indicate that the ligand did not undergo major conformational disruption after binding.54 In addition, the preferred torsional distributions observed for several rotatable bonds imply that the ligand was able to adopt energetically favorable conformations without experiencing excessive strain. This balance between rigidity and flexibility may be relevant for sustaining interactions within a dynamic protein environment.

DFT is a powerful quantum mechanical modeling method. DFT analysis calculates an electronic structure, energetic stability, and reactivity to predict how it will interact with biological targets before running costly physical experiments.55 The HOMO distribution appears predominantly localized over the heterocyclic and aromatic regions of the molecule, indicating potential electron-donating regions, whereas the LUMO density is distributed over adjacent electron-deficient moieties, suggesting probable electron-accepting sites.56 A moderate HOMO–LUMO gap is generally associated with a balance between molecular stability and electronic reactivity, suggesting that the compound may participate in intermolecular interactions without excessive electronic instability.57 Furthermore, the ESP map revealed distinct electron-rich and electron-deficient regions distributed around oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups, which may contribute to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with amino acid residues in the SaFtsZ binding pocket. The calculated softness and electrophilicity parameters further suggest that the molecule possesses moderate electronic adaptability, which could support interaction with amino acid residues within the binding cavity. Similar interpretations of HOMO–LUMO gap, chemical softness, and electrophilicity as descriptors of molecular reactivity and interaction propensity have been reported in recent DFT-based studies.58,59 However, these descriptors remain theoretical approximations and should not be directly interpreted as predictors of antibacterial efficacy.

Limitation

Although the integrated computational findings suggest that compound 15 may represent a structurally relevant scaffold for SaFtsZ targeting, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study is entirely computational and therefore cannot confirm inhibitory activity, selectivity, or antibacterial efficacy. Docking and simulation results are dependent on force-field assumptions, simulation length, and structural constraints of the selected protein conformation. Therefore, the MD simulation results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The DFT calculations were conducted at the B3LYP/6-31G level in the gas phase. While this approach is commonly employed for exploratory electronic structure analysis, the use of larger basis sets and implicit solvent models could improve the quantitative accuracy of the calculated electronic descriptors and should be considered in future studies. Furthermore, DFT descriptors provide useful information regarding molecular electronic properties, but they do not establish biological activity in our study.

Furthermore, the absence of experimental validation limits biological interpretation of the predicted interactions. Consequently, the present findings should be considered exploratory and may serve as a basis for future experimental studies, including enzymatic inhibition assays, microbial susceptibility testing, and structure–activity relationship investigations aimed at validating the proposed SaFtsZ inhibitory potential.

Conclusion

The present study employed an integrated computational approach involving pharmacophore mapping, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, MM-GBSA, and DFT analysis to identify potential inhibitors targeting Staphylococcus aureus FtsZ. Among the screened compounds, compound 15 demonstrated favorable interaction characteristics within the SaFtsZ binding pocket and maintained relatively stable interactions during molecular dynamics simulation. The combined docking, dynamic, and electronic analyses suggest that aromatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and moderate conformational adaptability may contribute to its binding behavior. The DFT findings further indicated that compound 15 possesses electronic properties compatible with intermolecular interactions within the active site. However, the current findings are based entirely on computational predictions. Overall, the study provides a preliminary and hypothesis-generating framework for the future development of SaFtsZ-targeted antibacterial agents.
