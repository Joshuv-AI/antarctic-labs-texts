Reply to: “Unaddressed concerns of potential follow-up bias in the International PSC Registry”
Authors: Bregje Mol, Johannes A Bogaards, Cyriel Y Ponsioen
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We have read with interest the letter from colleagues Shanahan and Slattery, in which they praise our efforts to analyze International PSC Registry data, but also express their concern about potentials for bias in our analysis.1 We welcome the concerns as it provides an opportunity to clarify the design and methodological strategy that we have applied in our observational International PSC Registry study.

It is important to realize that the exposure of interest – colectomy – is not determined at baseline but may occur at any point during follow-up. An analysis treating colectomy as a fixed exposure from the start of follow-up would therefore raise methodological questions and be particularly prone to a bias known as immortal-time bias.2

This bias refers to a period of follow-up during which, by definition, the outcome cannot occur because a patient must first survive long enough to receive a given intervention or exposure. This bias arises when such “immortal time” is attributed to the exposed group, artificially lowering the estimated hazard. For this reason, we have specifically modelled colectomy as a time-varying covariate. With this type of modelling, patients contribute person-time to the non-colectomy group up to the moment of surgery and only thereafter to the colectomy group. In this analysis, the risk accumulated prior to colectomy is accounted for in the estimation of the hazard after colectomy, because time is still counted in years since PSC diagnosis after patients enter the colectomy group.

The concerns raised regarding selection bias and follow-up bias seem to assume a naïve survival comparison with fixed baseline groups. However, our analytical framework was explicitly designed to avoid this problem. The differences in follow-up duration as shown in Table 2, arise because grouping was defined at end of follow-up: patients diagnosed earlier with PSC simply had more time in which a colectomy could occur.3 This reflects disease chronology rather than selective inclusion or biased baseline definitions.

Regarding propensity score matching, such methods are appropriate for comparing groups defined at baseline when all relevant prognostic variables are available. This was not the case in our collaborative study. Matching is not straightforwardly applicable when exposure varies over time, unless one moves to more advanced causal multi-state modelling strategies. Moreover, propensity-based adjustment cannot compensate for unmeasured confounding and does not account for bias due to censoring.4 In fact, matching on propensity scores may induce additional bias and increase model dependency when covariate information is incomplete.5

No observational study can be deemed fully free of residual confounding. However, the specific biases raised, selection-bias from baseline grouping and follow-up bias from longer observation, were explicitly addressed in our time-dependent modelling strategy. Hence, we believe that these concerns do not invalidate our findings.
