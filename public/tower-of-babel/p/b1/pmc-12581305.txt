Background

Foreign bodies are a common cause of eye pain and tears, with most being relatively small in size, generally including small pieces of sand, iron, silk thread, eyelashes, insect, or plant materials [1–6]. Foreign bodies may attach to the cornea or become sequestered in the conjunctiva of the upper or lower eyelids. Although the majority of foreign bodies are small and superficial, large subconjunctival foreign bodies, particularly those penetrating under the plica semilunaris, are extremely rare. Only a few cases of large foreign bodies trapped in the conjunctival fornix have been reported previously, including a coleoptera cincindelidae beetle (8 mm), a plastic toy (8 × 6 × 6 mm), and a bean seed (1 cm) [7–9]. In this report, we present a rare case of a large plant-derived foreign body covered with thorns that penetrated the conjunctiva into the subconjunctival plica semilunaris following vigorous eye rubbing.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old woman from rural Leshan presented with persistent redness and swelling at the nasal side of her left eye lasting 10 days. She had initially consulted a local hospital, where she was diagnosed with conjunctivitis and prescribed levofloxacin eye drops. However, her symptoms persisted, prompting referral to our institution. Ophthalmic examination revealed an uncorrected visual acuity of 20/20 in both eyes and an intraocular pressure of 14 mmHg in the right eye and 15 mmHg in the left eye. The conjunctiva in the plica semilunaris of the left eye appeared elevated, congested, and edematous. The sharp tip of a foreign body, approximately 1-mm in length, was visible on the nasal aspect of the elevated area. The temporal bulbar conjunctiva appeared pale and semicircular with little discharge (Fig. 1). The cornea was transparent, anterior chamber depth was normal, pupil was round with normal light reflexes, the lens was clear, fundus examination revealed no abnormalities.

The conjunctiva in the plica semilunaris appeared elevated, congested, and edematous. The sharp tip of a plant-derived foreign body, approximately 1-mm in length, was visible on the nasal side of the raised area (white arrow). The temporal bulbar conjunctiva was pale and semicircular (black arrow)

Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with a subconjunctival foreign body in the plica semilunaris of the left eye and secondary conjunctivitis. Under topical anesthesia with procaine hydrochloride, the exposed tip was grasped using ophthalmic microforceps, and a plant-derived foreign body measuring 8 mm in length and 4 mm in diameter was pulled out of the conjunctiva under a slit lamp. The foreign body was oblong with thorns covering the body, resembling a plant seed (Fig. 2). Upon further history-taking, the patient recalled that the ocular pain initially began while farming. She experienced a foreign body sensation and rubbed her eye vigorously in response. The patient was prescribed topical levofloxacin eye drops four times a day and tobramycin dexamethasone eye ointment once before bed for 7 days. At 1-month follow-up, the conjunctival swelling had completely resolved, without any signs of granuloma or infection.

Discussion and conclusions

Small ocular surface foreign bodies are commonly encountered in ophthalmology practice. They may adhere to the cornea or conjunctiva, or become lodged in the fornices, causing discomfort, lacrimation, and pain, and can be expelled by blinking or tearing [1–6]. Unexpected large foreign bodies trapped in the conjunctival fornix or subconjunctiva must be examined carefully and extracted surgically [7–9]. In the present case, the foreign body at the plica semilunaris had completely penetrated into the subconjunctival space. Once embedded, the patient’s acute symptoms subsided, likely contributing to the initial misdiagnosis as simple conjunctivitis. However, ongoing mechanical stimulation from the foreign body induced localized inflammation, increasing the risk of granuloma formation.

It is interesting to speculate how a large plant-derived seed with thorns on its surface could penetrate into the conjunctiva. In a case of lacrimal stone initiated by an cilium trapped in the lacrimal sac, an electron microscopy image of a cilium led the authors to speculate that the imbricated surface of the cilium caused preferential movement in the direction of the root of the cilium, while preventing its movement in the opposite direction [10]. In the present case, the elliptical shape and unidirectional orientation of thorns on the surface of the foreign body, similar to the surface of a cilium, might have facilitated its conjunctival penetration.

The clinical manifestations of ocular foreign bodies vary according to their location. Superficial conjunctival foreign bodies typically cause ocular pain, lacrimation, and photophobia. In contrast, large, deeply embedded foreign bodies may cause severe inflammation, posing substantial risks to both visual function and ocular integrity [7–9]. Patients with suspected ocular foreign bodies should avoid eye rubbing to prevent further damage and should seek immediate medical attention [11].

When ophthalmologists evaluate patients presenting with chief complaints of ocular irritation, detailed inquiry and thorough ocular examinations should be performed, particularly considering the possibility of occult foreign bodies in the conjunctival sac or subconjunctival. Concurrently, this case reinforces the importance of examining the plica semilunaris and conjunctival fornices in cases where symptoms persist despite treatment.
