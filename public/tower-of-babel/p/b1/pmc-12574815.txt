Solitary Fibrous Tumor of the Mesentery of the Small Intestine: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Go Ito, Katsuya Hattori, Yasunori Ota, Junko Mukohyama, Naoki Sakuyama, Dai Shida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare spindle cell neoplasms of mesenchymal origin, most commonly originating from the pleura. The discovery of the NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion has enabled more accurate diagnosis, particularly in extrapleural locations. We report a rare case of a 50-year-old woman presenting with localized abdominal pain, who showed a 46-mm heterogeneous mass in the left abdomen on contrast-enhanced CT; laparoscopic exploration identified a pedunculated tumor arising from the jejunal mesentery, located 50 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz. Segmental resection of 15 cm of the jejunum with the associated mesentery was performed. The tumor measured 55 × 45 × 30 mm and was confined to the mesentery, with no invasion of the bowel wall. Histopathological examination revealed proliferation of spindle cells within a collagenous stroma, and immunohistochemistry confirmed strong nuclear STAT6 and cytoplasmic CD34 positivity, while c-Kit, DOG1, Desmin, and S-100 were negative, establishing the diagnosis of SFT. The patient's abdominal pain resolved completely following resection. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of mesenteric SFTs and underscores the importance of complete surgical resection with intact borders and immunohistochemical analysis in securing an accurate diagnosis and favorable outcome. Keywords: solitary fibrous tumor (SFT), mesenteric tumor, STAT6, spindle cell tumor

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs), formerly known as hemangiopericytomas, are uncommon mesenchymal neoplasms characterized by spindle cell proliferation within a collagenous or myxoid stroma[1]. Although SFTs were originally recognized as pleural tumors, they have subsequently been identified in a wide range of extrapleural sites, including the mesentery of the small intestine, albeit extremely rarely[2,3]. Histologically, SFTs must be differentiated from other spindle cell neoplasms with overlapping morphological features. In the context of gastrointestinal submucosal spindle cell tumors, the differential diagnosis includes gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), smooth muscle tumor (such as leiomyoma and leiomyosarcoma), desmoid-type fibromatosis, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, and perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa)[4]. Immunohistochemical analysis is critical to making these distinctions.

Recent advances in molecular genetics have significantly improved diagnostic accuracy for SFTs. The NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion―resulting from the juxtaposition of the transcriptional repressor NAB2 and the transcriptional activator STAT6―has emerged as a defining molecular hallmark of both benign and malignant SFTs[5,6]. Accordingly, STAT6 immunohistochemistry, which reveals strong nuclear positivity, has become a highly sensitive and specific surrogate marker. Despite these histopathological advancements, preoperative and even intraoperative diagnosis of SFT remains challenging due to its nonspecific radiological and clinical features. Tumors arising in unusual locations such as the small intestinal mesentery can pose further diagnostic difficulty, often mimicking other intra-abdominal neoplasms.

Here we present a rare case of an SFT originating from the jejunal mesentery, which was initially indeterminate on imaging and even after intraoperative evaluation, but was ultimately diagnosed based on characteristic histological and immunohistochemical features. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of SFTs, as well as the importance of surgical resection and definitive pathological evaluation in the management of such rare intra-abdominal tumors.

Case Report

A 50-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with intermittent, dull, non-radiating pain localized to the left lumbar and iliac regions. The pain was not related to bowel movements and was not accompanied by diarrhea, constipation, nausea, vomiting, hematemesis, or other gastrointestinal symptoms. Initial gynecological evaluation was unremarkable, but abdominal CT revealed a well-defined, 46-mm heterogeneous mass in the left abdomen adjacent to the small intestine, prompting referral to our surgical department. Laboratory investigations, including tumor markers CEA (1.7 ng/mL) and CA 19-9 (4.1 U/mL), were within normal limits. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a 46-mm heterogeneous mass in the left abdomen in close proximity to the small intestine (Figure 1A, 1B). On contrast-enhanced CT, the lesion demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement, indicating uneven vascularity (Figure 1C, 1D). The origin of the mass―whether from the bowel wall or the mesentery―remained uncertain. Given its uncertain origin and the possibility of malignancy, surgical resection was deemed appropriate.

Laparoscopic exploration revealed a pedunculated mass arising from the mesentery of the jejunum, approximately 50 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz (Figure 2A). The umbilical port site was extended slightly, and the affected small bowel segment and associated mesentery were exteriorized. The mass appeared to arise from the mesentery and was not in continuity with the intestinal wall (Figure 2B). To achieve complete resection with clear margins, a wedge resection of the tumor, including 15 cm of the adjacent jejunum and mesentery, was performed. Systematic lymphadenectomy was not conducted. Macroscopically, the resected specimen revealed a pedunculated, well-encapsulated tumor measuring 55 × 45 × 30 mm arising from the mesentery (Figure 2C). Inspection of the mucosal surface of the resected bowel showed no evidence of tumor invasion into the lumen (Figure 2D).

Histopathological examination revealed proliferation of spindle cells within the fibrous stroma (Figure 3A, 3B). A spindle cell-type gastrointestinal submucosal tumor was suspected, and immunohistochemical studies were performed. The tumor was negative for c-Kit (CD117) and DOG1, ruling out GIST, but demonstrated strong nuclear positivity for STAT6 and cytoplasmic positivity for CD34 (Figure 3C, 3D). Additional markers, including Desmin and S-100, were negative, excluding smooth muscle tumors and schwannomas. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of SFT. Given the complete resection with negative margins, no adjuvant therapy was administered.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 8. At her first follow-up, abdominal discomfort had markedly improved, with only mild residual pain. This resolved completely by six months postoperatively. At one year postoperatively, she remained symptom-free and without recurrence.

Discussion

In the present case, an abdominal mass initially suspected to be a gastrointestinal submucosal tumor (SMT) based on CT findings was ultimately diagnosed as an SFT of the jejunal mesentery through histological and immunohistochemical evaluations following surgical resection. This case illustrates the diagnostic and management challenges associated with mesenteric tumors, particularly when preoperative assessments are inconclusive, and underscores the importance of immunohistochemical markers―especially STAT6, which has recently become more accessible and is increasingly valued for its diagnostic specificity―in the definitive diagnosis of SFT.

SFTs are rare mesenchymal neoplasms that most commonly originate in the pleura. While extrapleural SFTs have been reported in various anatomical sites including the liver, kidney, orbit, and meninges, those arising from the mesentery are exceedingly rare[2,3,7-11]. A literature review using the keywords “solitary fibrous tumor” and “mesentery (of the small intestine)” in PubMed and Japan Medical Abstracts Society databases identified only 22 reported cases since 2000, including 10 published in Japanese (Table 1) (cases with tumors of the mesentery of the colon or rectum were excluded). In all reported cases, immunohistochemical analysis was performed for definitive diagnosis. Prior to 2017, CD34 expression was predominantly relied upon as a diagnostic marker for SFTs[12]. However, this approach poses diagnostic challenges, as CD34 is a nonspecific marker that is also expressed in a wide range of other soft tissue tumors included in the differential diagnosis. Furthermore, although CD34 positivity is frequently observed in SFTs, CD34-negative variants―albeit uncommon―have been documented, with reported frequencies ranging from 5% to 10%[13]. In contrast, all five cases reported since 2020, including the present case, confirmed the diagnosis of SFT based on nuclear STAT6 positivity, an immunohistochemical marker that has recently emerged as a highly specific and reliable tool for SFT diagnosis[2,3,10,11]. This shift represents a significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy for SFTs, particularly in cases with atypical histological features or equivocal CD34 expression. Notably, CD34-negative SFTs have been associated with more aggressive behavior, including higher rates of metastasis, pronounced nuclear atypia, and the absence of the characteristic hemangiopericytoma-like vasculature―features that further complicate histological assessment and clinical decision-making[13].

An important insight from our review (Table 1) is that abdominal pain as a chief complaint was clearly described in 11 cases (50% of all cases), indicating that mesenteric SFTs can present with abdominal pain, despite their typical lack of bowel obstruction or luminal compression. Our case adds to this evidence: the patient experienced left-sided abdominal pain that resolved completely after tumor resection. Although the tumor originated from the mesentery without directly involving the intestine, the pain was localized to the tumor's anatomical location. This clinical course suggests that the mass may have exerted mechanical tension on the mesentery due to its size or weight, thereby eliciting pain. While this hypothesis remains speculative, it offers a plausible explanation for the symptomatology seen in anatomically isolated mesenteric SFTs and is consistent with previously reported cases. This unique presentation, without endoscopic abnormalities, highlights the importance of considering mesenteric tumors in the differential diagnosis of unexplained abdominal pain. The pedunculated nature of the tumor and/or associated localized peritonitis may have contributed to peritoneal or vascular traction, leading to visceral pain.

Among the cases reviewed in Table 1, only one (case 9) reported local recurrence within 9 months after surgical resection[14]. Although most SFTs follow a relatively indolent clinical course, there is a subset that exhibits malignant behavior, with reported recurrence or metastasis rates ranging from 12% to 22%[1,15]. While long-term outcomes in this case are not yet available, complete surgical resection with negative margins remains the standard of care for localized SFT, given its association with reduced risk of recurrence or metastasis in previous reports.

In the present case, surgical resection was performed because the tumor was symptomatic, and preoperative imaging raised suspicion for GIST, i.e., the primary differential diagnosis. According to clinical practice guidelines issued by the Japan Society of Clinical Oncology (JSCO) for the management of GISTs[4], surgical intervention is recommended for tumors measuring between 2 cm and 5 cm that exhibit “high-risk features”, such as heterogeneity, irregular margins, necrosis, or hemorrhage. In our case, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated intratumoral heterogeneity, meeting the criteria for surgical resection under these guidelines. Regarding the role of preoperative needle biopsy, this clinical practice guidelines recommend but do not mandate endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) for submucosal tumors ≥2 cm but <5 cm that are resectable and localized[4]. In our case, we did not perform a preoperative biopsy due to the risk of tumor rupture and dissemination, especially given the presence of localized peritonitis and tumor adherence. However, considering recent advancements in diagnostic modalities, a careful evaluation of the risk-benefit balance of EUS-FNA is warranted, and early surgical resection may be justified in similar cases. Although the final diagnosis was SFT, this could not have been confidently established without complete excision and histopathological evaluation. The present case underscores the diagnostic challenge posed by mesenchymal tumors with overlapping radiologic features and reinforces the role of surgical resection in both therapeutic and diagnostic decision-making.

In summary, we report a rare case of mesenteric SFT that was successfully managed by complete surgical resection. The clinical and radiological characteristics of the tumor mimicked those of more common gastrointestinal spindle cell tumors such as GIST, making preoperative diagnosis challenging. Immunohistochemical analysis, particularly STAT6 nuclear staining, played a pivotal role in arriving at an accurate diagnosis. Given the potential for malignancy in SFTs, even when asymptomatic or indeterminate preoperatively, surgical resection with negative margins is a prudent management strategy.
