Survival after resection of pancreatic cancer is similar with neoadjuvant gemcitabine-nab-paclitaxel or mFOLFIRINOX but one size will not fit all in the future
Authors: Sophia Hernandez, Ajay V Maker
Published: 2026

Current NCCN guidelines recommend neoadjuvant modified FOLFIRINOX (mFOLFIRINOX) or gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel (GnP) as preferred chemotherapy regimens for borderline resectable and locally advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Neoadjuvant therapy has become the standard of care in this patient population, with multiple studies demonstrating superior overall survival (OS) among patients treated with preoperative chemotherapy compared with those treated with upfront surgery followed by adjuvant therapy (1). This approach ensures early delivery of systemic therapy, increases the likelihood of R0 resection, and allows clinicians to identify patients with rapid metastatic progression who are unlikely to benefit from major surgery. Increasingly, these regimens have also been adopted for patients with primary resectable disease, reflecting a substantial shift in treatment philosophy over the past decade.

Historically, mFOLFIRINOX has been favored as first-line neoadjuvant therapy for patients with good performance status, supported by robust response rates and phase III clinical trial data in advanced and metastatic PDAC (2). In contrast, GnP has been reserved for frailer patients and those with significant comorbidities given its favorable toxicity profile (3). In several Asian countries, GnP is widely used as a first‑line systemic therapy, reflecting its established efficacy, potential superiority in this patient population, tolerability, and high completion rates in both metastatic and neoadjuvant settings. Although neoadjuvant therapy is a well-accepted strategy, whether survival differs by the regimen chosen remains uncertain. The study “Survival after neoadjuvant and induction (m)FOLFIRINOX versus gemcitabine nab-paclitaxel in patients with resected localized pancreatic adenocarcinoma: an international multicentre study” sought to address this fundamental question (4).

This retrospective multicenter cohort study analyzed 935 consecutive adult patients who received 2–6 months of neoadjuvant mFOLFIRINOX or GnP followed by pancreatectomy for localized PDAC from 2010–2018. Patients were drawn from 16 high-volume centers across 8 countries and 3 continents (Asia, Europe, and North America), making it one of the most geographically and clinically diverse studies to date, which is important given potential differences in response based on country of origin. Inclusion required localized disease at diagnosis and one line of preoperative chemotherapy. Patients were excluded if they presented with metastatic disease, had multiple lines of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or lacked complete chemotherapy-related data.

Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank testing evaluated OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) as primary endpoints. Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for prespecified preoperative variables including age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) performance status, tumor size, resectability category, serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), year of surgery, and geographic region. Among 935 patients, 65% received mFOLFIRINOX and 35% received GnP. Patients treated with mFOLFIRINOX were younger, more frequently male, and more likely to have borderline resectable or locally advanced tumors. In contrast, patients that received GnP had more primary resectable disease and shorter chemotherapy to surgery intervals. Baseline carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) and CEA levels at diagnosis were not significantly different between groups, nor was the proportion of patients demonstrating radiologic response according to RECIST criteria. These baseline imbalances reflect real-world practice patterns, where regimen selection is influenced by performance status, comorbidities, and tumor anatomy.

The study found no statistically significant difference in OS between the regimens. Unadjusted median OS was 43 months for mFOLFIRINOX and 36 months for GnP (HR 0.83, 95% CI: 0.68–1.02). After adjustment, the choice of preoperative regimen was not significantly associated with OS (HR 0.83, 95% CI: 0.64–1.08), with adjusted median OS (40 vs. 35 months), and 5-year OS (35% vs. 28%) for mFOLFIRINOX and GnP, respectively. Recurrence-free survival also did not differ significantly by regimen.

Despite similar survival outcomes, mFOLFIRINOX was associated with more favorable pathological features, including lower rates of lymphovascular and perineural invasion, and lymph node involvement (54% vs. 38%). Pathologic complete response was also significantly higher (6% vs. 2%) in patients that received mFOLFIRINOX. These findings suggest more profound tumor response to mFOLFIRINOX. However, this did not translate into improved OS, emphasizing that residual biology and micrometastatic disease may outweigh local pathologic response as determinants of prognosis. Additionally, multivariable analysis identified age and pathologic tumor size as the only independent predictors of OS. Adjusting for preoperative radiation therapy, which was more commonly administered in mFOLFIRINOX-treated patients, did not associate with survival, potentially suggesting that regimen equivalence is not confounded by radiation practices.

Methodologically, the study leverages a large, real‑world dataset with granular clinicopathologic variables and uses prespecified confounders known at the time of diagnosis with flexible parametric modelling to enhance clinical interpretability at the time of regimen selection. Furthermore, the consistency of survival results across geographically distinct populations strengthens the conclusion that both regimens are viable options for appropriately selected patients.

A key exploratory finding was the interaction between baseline CA19‑9 and regimen effect, finding that patients with low or normal CA19‑9, including non‑secretors and those with only marginal elevations, derived a greater survival advantage from mFOLFIRINOX relative to GnP. This pattern raises the hypothesis that CA19‑9 may function not only as a global prognostic marker, but also as a predictive biomarker for regimen‑specific chemosensitivity. Although this requires prospective validation, this represents a potentially meaningful step toward biomarker-guided neoadjuvant therapy selection, a critical need as PDAC treatment evolves toward precision oncology. Interestingly, GnP was associated with a larger relative decline in CA19‑9 despite the stronger survival signal for mFOLFIRINOX in patients with low baseline CA19‑9. This suggests that baseline level and on-treatment kinetics may capture different biological processes rather than a simple linear measure of treatment efficacy. Taken together, these observations support further study of CA19‑9 defined subgroups and additional molecular markers to refine regimen selection, rather than relying solely on histopathologic response or serologic dynamics.

Identifying biomarkers to guide selection of preoperative chemotherapy is a very important and rapidly advancing area in pancreatic cancer research. Ultimately, progress in this space will shape the future of treatment personalization by improving our understanding of tumor biology and tumor-specific chemosensitivity. However, despite growing interest and emerging data, robust biomarkers that can reliably direct regimen selection in routine clinical practice remain to be established. In light of the findings by Stoop et al., rational biomarker strategies should focus on tumor biology rather than anatomy or tolerability alone. Candidate biomarkers include genomic alterations associated with chemosensitivity, such as DNA damage repair deficiency signatures (e.g., BRCA1/2, PALB2, or broader homologous recombination deficiency scores), which may predict benefit from platinum-containing regimens like FOLFIRINOX. In addition, SMAD4 alterations have also been associated with higher rates of metastasis and lower resection rates in patients treated with mFOLFIRINOX compared with GnP (5). Furthermore, transcriptomic or proteomic markers related to stromal biology, drug metabolism, or nucleoside transport may help identify tumors more likely to benefit from gemcitabine-based therapy. Additionally, a 10-gene radiosensitivity panel has also shown predictive value for response to gemcitabine-based neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (6). Integration of circulating biomarkers, including baseline and on-treatment CA19-9 kinetics or circulating tumor DNA dynamics, offers an additional, noninvasive means to refine regimen selection and potentially pivot between regimens by providing early readouts of treatment sensitivity and occult systemic disease. Emerging immune profiling approaches further suggest a role for tumor-immune evaluation for predicting treatment response. Together, these findings support the potential utility of biomarker-guided neoadjuvant therapy, although further research and prospective validation will be needed.

Beyond their prognostic utility, biomarker-defined targets are increasingly being investigated, with KRAS-targeted agents and personalized mRNA vaccines emerging as promising strategies to augment standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy in selected patient populations (7,8).

This study also has several limitations. As a retrospective study, it is subject to selection bias, reflected in the baseline differences between the treatment groups. Though efforts are made to statistically control for differences in tumor characteristics, patient demographics, and receipt of radiation, the groups were inherently unequal, with the mFOLFIRINOX group biased toward more advanced disease, greater radiation use, and variation in the number of chemotherapy cycles administered. In addition, the analysis was restricted to patients who ultimately underwent surgical resection, excluding those who progressed on therapy, became unresectable, or were deemed unfit for surgery. Time to resection is a critical consideration in the neoadjuvant setting, as prolonged treatment may delay surgery and allow disease progression that precludes resection, indicating that upfront surgery may be preferable for select patients. Further, the absence of regimen-specific resection data, coupled with enrichment of the GnP cohort for upfront resectable disease, does impact interpretation of the observed interaction between baseline CA19-9 and regimen efficacy. Accordingly, while the association between low baseline CA19-9 and improved outcomes with mFOLFIRINOX is hypothesis generating, prospective studies that account for conversion to resectability and resection rates are needed. Notably, two phase II randomized controlled trials have reported similar resection rates between these regimens, suggesting that differences in surgical outcomes may be less pronounced in controlled settings (9,10).

Although the authors attempted to adjust for frailty using ASA status, they acknowledge that ASA scoring was unreliable in this context. More objective measures, such as frailty indices or comorbidity scores, may strengthen the analysis. Another limitation lies in the absence of genomic and molecular tumor data, which are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of chemotherapy response in PDAC. The lack of these variables may obscure treatment effect differences driven by biological heterogeneity. Despite these limitations, this study offers clinically relevant observations that warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, this international multicenter cohort study demonstrated no significant survival advantage associated with mFOLFIRINOX over GnP among patients who underwent resection for localized PDAC, despite more favorable pathologic responses with mFOLFIRINOX. These findings are consistent with SWOG S1505, in which outcomes were similar between treatment arms, although direct comparison was limited by trial design (10). More broadly, comparisons between gemcitabine backbone regimens and FOLFIRINOX in the metastatic setting have yielded heterogeneous results, including no OS difference in the PURPLE registry, improved survival with FOLFIRINOX versus gemcitabine alone, and improved survival with GnP versus mFOLFIRINOX in PASS-01 (11-13). Some recent trials have even taken to using both regimens sequentially in the neoadjuvant setting (14). Taken together, the available data support both mFOLFIRINOX and GnP as reasonable neoadjuvant options for appropriately selected patients with localized PDAC.

Importantly, if surgical extirpation is achieved, any survival benefit is likely driven by effective treatment of micrometastatic disease that has escaped immune surveillance. Although neoadjuvant therapy may enhance immune recognition by increasing antigen exposure and promoting effector responses, it can also deplete highly proliferative, tumor-reactive T-cells critical for durable control. Emerging data suggest regimen-specific immunologic effects, with mFOLFIRINOX associated with greater lymphocyte and regulatory T-cell depletion but relatively preserved or enhanced CD8+ effector infiltration and proinflammatory signaling compared with GnP (15). These emerging studies, combined with data in other malignancies where preservation of lymphoid structures may improve anti-tumor immune responses, highlight the complexities of not only chemotherapy regimen selection, but also of how timing and extent of surgery may impact the tumor immune microenvironment. While differential chemotherapy susceptibility exists, it remains largely unaccounted for in current treatment selection and was not stratified for in this analysis. Emerging data suggest that molecular subtypes, such as basal-like versus classical phenotypes, may differentially respond to GnP, underscoring that we are only at the earliest stages of defining biologically informed treatment selection (13). In this context, the CA19-9 findings are particularly interesting and highlight the need for further investigation into baseline biomarkers to guide regimen choice. Future prospective randomized trials incorporating molecular profiling, biomarker-driven treatment strategies, and intent-to-treat surgical conversion rates will be essential to refining neoadjuvant therapy for PDAC.
