A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with trial sequence analysis of remdesivir for COVID-19 treatment
Authors: Rupali Patnaik, Tatikonda Chandramouli, Shakti Bedanta Mishra
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Remdesivir is one of the proposed therapies for the corona virus disease 2019 (COVID-19). To assess the effect of remdesivir on mortality, need for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), time to clinical improvement, and significant adverse effects. The study protocol was prospectively registered with The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration #CRD42021283221). Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English detailing use of remdesivir in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 were included. Primary outcome was in hospital mortality among patients receiving remdesivir. Secondary outcomes were need for IMV and ECMO, time to clinical recovery, and significant adverse effects associated with remdesivir. Odds ratios (ORs) of worse outcome with 95% confidence interval (CI) in a forest plot were used to show the results of random effects meta-analysis. Remdesivir and placebo had similar in hospital mortality in the pooled analysis of five RCTs (OR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.82–1.06). The remdesivir group needed less IMV/ECMO (OR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.46–0.76) and recovered 1.06 days faster than placebo. Remdesivir did not affect transaminitis or renal damage. Trial sequence analysis showed that death has not reached the number of instances needed to predict futility. This meta-analysis shows that remdesivir therapy for COVID-19 is not associated with a mortality benefit. However, there is significant reduction in the need for IMV/ECMO.

INTRODUCTION

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) has shown its lethal face with repeated pandemics in almost every part of the world.[12] The disease caused by the novel coronavirus, severe acute respiratory distress syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS CoV2), has had almost more than 1.7 million documented deaths so far.[23] Remdesivir (GS-5734) is a prodrug of an adenosine analogue with a broad antiviral spectrum. Remdesivir, an inhibitor of the viral RNA-dependent, R. N. A. polymerase with in vitro inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-1 and the Middle East respiratory syndrome corona virus (MERS-CoV), was identified early as a promising therapeutic candidate for COVID-19 because of its ability to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 in vitro.[45] Adaptive COVID treatment trial 1 (ACTT-1) has shown positive benefits with a shorter time to recovery (10 days vs. 15 days with placebo) but no proven benefit in mortality.[6] Most of the clinicians used remdesivir after getting emergency approval from United States Food and Drug Administration in May 2020 due to a lack of specific drug therapy in the initial days of the pandemic. The recovery trial, which proved the mortality benefit of dexamethasone, an integral part of treatment in ACTT-1, led us to the dilemma of confounding effect.[7] Of late, a solidarity trial by the World Health Organization (WHO) has concluded the equivocal benefits of remdesivir.[8]

Sooner there was a pragmatic shift into immunomodulator-based treatment with positive results in mortality benefit where remdesivir was combined with JAK-STAT inhibitors like baricitinib.[9] The efficacy and beneficial effects of remdesivir and jak stat inhibitors were questioned after the premature termination of the ACTT-4 trial due to increased mortality. In a recently published trial by Ader et al. (DisCoVeRy), remdesivir treatment was neither associated with clinical improvement at day 15 or 29 nor with a reduction in mortality.[10] Moreover, concerns have been raised about the adverse drug effects of remdesivir. With the disparity in the literature and paucity of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in the same, we tried to evaluate the effect of remdesivir on mortality, time to recovery, need for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and adverse drug effects through systemic review and meta-analysis comparison of available RCTs.

The primary objective of our systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the effect of remdesivir therapy versus placebo on in-hospital mortality in hospitalized adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) being treated for laboratory confirmed COVID-19. The secondary objectives were to evaluate the effect of remdesivir on the need for IMV and ECMO, time to clinical recovery and the major adverse events associated with remdesivir.

METHODS

This systemic review was conducted and reported per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines.[11] The study protocol was prospectively registered with The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO).

Eligibility criteria

We included prospective human RCTs of hospitalized adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) being treated for laboratory confirmed COVID-19 and detailing the use of remdesivir. English language RCTs published from April 2020 to March 2021 were eligible for inclusion. Other than English RCTs, unpublished manuscripts, conference proceedings and abstracts and studies on pediatric patients were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

To identify the studies, we searched MEDLINE via PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, SCOPUS, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. The original database search used bibliographies of the relevant articles, published systematic reviews, and meta-analyses to identify not listed trials. Clinical trial registries were not searched for any ongoing trials.

The entire search strategy as explained in detail in Supplementary Table 1 was developed by the authors (RP and SBM). Two authors (RP, SBM) independently identified potentially eligible studies by screening titles and abstracts, then assessed through full-text review and retrieved. Any disagreement between screeners was resolved by mutual consensus, so a unanimous decision was made.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was mortality benefit among hospitalized patients admitted with COVID-19 and who received remdesivir. The secondary outcome measures evaluated the need for IMV and ECMO, time to clinical recovery in days (at least 2-point improvement in ordinal scale), and significant adverse effects associated with remdesivir treatment. A 7-point ordinal scale assessed clinical status improvement: (1) death, (2) hospitalized, receiving IMV or ECMO; (3) hospitalized, receiving noninvasive ventilation or high-flow oxygen; (4) hospitalized, requiring low-flow supplemental oxygen, (5) hospitalized, not receiving supplemental oxygen but requiring ongoing medical care, (6) hospitalized, not requiring supplemental oxygen or ongoing medical care, and (7) not hospitalized.[1]

Data extraction and quality assessment

After searching the titles and abstracts, two authors (RP, SBM) performed data extraction. Data were extracted to explain the baseline characteristics of patients and outcome measures in all of the included studies. All discrepancies during data extraction were settled by mutual consensus so that a unanimous decision was made. PRISMA Checklist

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment of the included studies was done independently by the two authors (RP, SBM). Any disagreement was resolved by consensus. The assessment was done in RevMan 5.0.[12]

Effect measures

We planned to analyze dichotomous outcomes by calculating the odds ratio (OR) of a worse outcome (i.e. mortality, need of IMV/ECMO, delay in time to clinical recovery, adverse effects of transaminitis, and acute kidney injury for each trial. Mantel-Haenszel OR was used to analyze the data. The confidence interval (CI) for each study and the complete analysis was kept at 95%. The studies that reported the exact numbers for the dichotomous outcomes analyzed were included for analysis.

Data synthesis and analysis

The two authors independently did the process and then compared the data. Any disagreement was resolved by consensus. We developed “Summary of findings” tables using GRADEpro G. D. T. These tables summarize the estimated intervention effect and the number of participants and studies for each primary outcome and include justifications underpinning. Results of random-effects meta-analyses are presented as OR with 95% CI in a forest plot. The extent and impact of between-study heterogeneity were measured by inspecting the forest plots and calculating the Tau-squared and the I2 statistics, respectively. The 95% C. I. s (uncertainty intervals) around the Tau-squared and the I2 were calculated to judge our confidence about these metrics. We randomly adopted the I2 thresholds of > 75% to be considered a feature of considerable heterogeneity, but we also judged the evidence for this heterogeneity (through the uncertainty intervals) and the localization on the forest plot. In order to include trials with zero events in both intervention and control arms (which are automatically dropped from analyses of pooled relative risks [RRs]), we also did sensitivity analyses for dichotomous outcomes in which we added a continuity correction of 0.5 to zero cells. All analysis was done in RevMan.

We planned to generate funnel plots for meta-analyses to assess small-study effects, including all five trials of varying sizes. If asymmetry in the funnel plot was detected, we planned to review the characteristics of the trials to assess whether the asymmetry was likely due to publication bias or other factors such as methodological or clinical heterogeneity of the trials.

Reporting bias assessment

To assess outcome reporting bias, we compared the outcomes specified in trial protocols with the outcomes reported in the corresponding trial publications; if trial protocols were unavailable, we compared the outcomes reported in the methods and results sections of the trial publications.

Certainty assessment

Two people (RP, SBM) separately evaluated the certainty of the evidence. The four GRADE was used to evaluate the certainty evidence related to the available literature was done. We evaluated the certainty of evidence as strong, moderate, low, or very low. The evaluation and grading were done according to the meta-analysis guidelines. We used the GRADEpro (GDT: GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool [Software]. McMaster University and Evidence Prime, 2022) to prepare the “Summary of findings” tables (GRADEpro G. D. T. 2015).[13]

Trial sequential analysis

Trial sequencing analysis was done for all-cause mortality, taking type I error as 5 and 80% power using O’Brien-Fleming for alpha and beta spending function.[1415] The prevalence of mortality in the control group was taken as 60%.

Study characteristics

There was variation in the characteristics of individual studies [Table 1]. In the four trials, patients received 200 mg of remdesivir on day 1, followed by 100 mg per day for the rest of the 9 days or a placebo. In the trial by Spinner et al., patients received either a ten or a 5-day course of remdesivir in the same dose or the placebo.[17] For this meta-analysis, the population who received a 10-day course was considered.

Impact on mortality

Five RCTs compared remdesivir versus placebo for in-hospital mortality benefit.[68101617] Four thousand forty-one patients received remdesivir, and 3924 patients received a placebo. Four hundred eighteen patients had mortality in the remdesivir group and 431 in the placebo group. On pooled analysis, we found no difference in all-cause mortality rates in the remdesivir compared to the placebo group [OR; 0.93, 95% CI: 0.82–1.06, Figure 2].

Four RCTs compared remdesivir versus placebo for the need of I. M. V. and/or ECMO.[6101617] 92 of 1167 patients who received remdesivir needed IMV/ECMO support. 137 0f 1060 patients who received a placebo needed IMV/ECMO. On pooled analysis, it was observed that there was a significantly lesser need for IMV/ECMO in patients who received remdesivir than those who received a placebo [OR; 0.59, 95% CI: 0.46–0.76, Figure 3].

Time to clinical recovery

Two studies assessed the time to clinical recovery (2-point improvement in ordinal scale). In the trial by Beigel et al., the time to clinical recovery was 11 days in the patients who received remdesivir compared to 14 days in those who received a placebo.[6] in the study by Ader et al., time to clinical recovery was 12 days in the remdesivir and 11 days in the placebo group.[10] On pooled analysis, time to clinical recovery was 1.06 days earlier in the remdesivir group compared to the placebo group, with a mean (standard deviation [SD]) of − 1.06 (−4.98, 2.86) days [Figure 4].

Forest plot assessment of trials comparing time to clinical improvement. CI: Confidence interval, SD: Standard deviation

Adverse effects of remdesivir

Three trials assessed deranged liver function with transaminitis as an adverse effect of remdesivir compared to placebo.[101617] Of the total 738 patients who received remdesivir, 77 had transaminitis. Of the 678 patients who received a placebo, 83 had transaminitis. On pooled analysis, no difference was observed in transaminitis in the remdesivir group compared to placebo [OR; 1.00, 95% CI: 0.44–2.28, Supplementary Figure 2].

Three trials assessed acute kidney injury as an adverse effect of remdesivir.[101617] Of the 737 patients who received remdesivir, 58 had acute kidney injury. Of the 679 patients who received a placebo, 70 had acute kidney injury. On pooled analysis, no difference was observed in acute kidney injury in the remdesivir group compared to placebo [OR; 0.85, 95% CI: 0.63–1.15, Supplementary Figure 3].

Heterogeneity among study results

We observed no heterogeneity in trials comparing hospital mortality among the remdesivir versus the placebo group (I2 = 0%). Similarly, no heterogeneity was observed in trials comparing remdesivir versus placebo for the need for I. M. V. and ECMO (I2 = 0%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed in trials comparing time to clinical improvement in the remdesivir versus the placebo group (I2 = 94%). Similarly, substantial heterogeneity was observed in trials comparing remdesivir versus placebo for adverse effects of transaminitis (I2 = 71%). No heterogeneity was observed in studies comparing the adverse effect of acute kidney injury (I2 = 0%).

The certainty of the evidence

The confidence in the literature was evaluated using GRADE. The death rates had a low level of certainty due to significant imprecision concerns regarding the wide 95% CI for the summary OR. This would be compatible with substantial benefit or harm. For the secondary analysis that included all seven RCTs, the questions regarding imprecision were decreased, and the certainty of the evidence was evaluated as moderate.

DISCUSSION

Our systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effect of remdesivir as a treatment modality for COVID-19. They explained its impact on four outcomes: mortality, need for IMV and ECMO, time to clinical recovery, and the major adverse events associated with remdesivir.

On pooled analysis, no difference was observed in all-cause mortality in the remdesivir group compared to placebo (OR; 0.93, 95% CI: 0.82–1.06). A meta-analysis by Angamo et al., including 4 RCTs and three nonrandomized observational trials, found that remdesivir-treated patients showed a 39% reduction in mortality risk on day 14 compared to the control group but no significant difference on day 28.[18] In the Meta-analysis by Bansal et al., including three clinical trials, remdesivir significantly reduced mortality compared to placebo.[19] In the meta-analysis by Piscoya et al., including four RCTs and 2 case series, remdesivir did not decrease all-cause mortality.[20] Meta-analysis by Al-Abdouh et al., including four RCTs, also did not find any significant benefit of mortality at 28-day follow-up.[21] An individual patient data meta-analysis by Amstutz et al., including recently published RCTs as our study found that remdesivir reduced 28 day mortality in patients hospitalized with COVID-19 requiring no or low oxygen support (OR; 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.93), but was inconclusive in patients requiring more respiratory support.[22]

In contrary to the previous studies, we did trial sequential analysis of our meta-analysis which showed that the number to achieve the power to predict futility had not been reached for mortality. The analysis does not show any superiority of remdesivir in mortality.

Apart from mortality, we assessed various other parameters like the need for IMV and ECMO in patients treated with remdesivir compared to placebo and found that there was a lesser need for IMV/ECMO in patients who received remdesivir than those who received placebo [OR; 0.59, 95% (CI: 0.46-0.76)]. Similar results were obtained in the meta-analysis by Angamo et al., where patients treated with remdesivir had significantly less likelihood of requiring high flow oxygen support and IMV than in the placebo group 27% (RR 0.73; 95% CI 0.54–0.99) and 47% (RR 0.53; 95% CI 0.39–0.72) respectively.[18] Meta-analysis by Rezagholizadeh found that remdesivir treatment was associated with significant improvement in IMV/ECMO requirement (RR 5.34; 95% CI 2.37–12.05).[3] A recent meta-analysis of 5 RCTs, incorporating data from the large solidarity trial, remdesivir treatment results in a slight reduction in the proportion of patients receiving mechanical ventilation (RR, 0.71; 95% CI 0.56–0.90).[19] In a contrary meta-analysis by Piscoya et al., including 4 RCTs and 2 case series, found that remdesivir did not decrease the need for invasive ventilation compared to placebo (RR 0.57; 95% CI 0.23–1.42).[20]

We assessed the time to clinical recovery in patients treated with remdesivir compared to placebo and found that time to clinical recovery was 1.06 days earlier in the remdesivir group compared to the placebo group with a mean (SD) of − 1.06 (−4.98, 2.86) days. A meta-analysis by Singh et al. found that patients treated with remdesivir had a significantly faster time to clinical improvement than the control [Hazard ratio = 1.28 (95% CI 1.12, 1.46)].[23] Meta-analysis by Rezagholizadeh, including five RCTs and five nonrandomized trials, found that pooled median difference of time to clinical improvement was 2.99 (95% CI = 2.71–3.28) days compared to the control.[3] In contrast, a meta-analysis by Al-Abdouh et al. of four RCTs, including the large solidarity trial, found that the use of remdesivir compared to control was not associated with a significant decrease in time to clinical improvement (standardized mean difference − 0.80 days; [CI, −2.12, 0.53]).[21]

We assessed significant adverse effects such as transaminitis and acute kidney injury due to remdesivir therapy. On pooled analysis, we observed no difference in transaminitis in the remdesivir group compared to the placebo (OR; 1.00, 95% CI: 0.44–2.28). No difference was observed in the worsening of renal function, i.e. acute kidney injury, in the remdesivir group compared to placebo [OR; 0.85, 95% (CI: 0.63-1.15)]. A meta-analysis by Angamo et al., including 4 RCTs, found that there was a lower risk of A. L. T. elevation (RR 0.80; 95% CI 0.69–0.94; P = 0.006) and deterioration of renal function (RR 0.66; 95% CI 0.55–0.80; P < 0.001) in the remdesivir group compared to standard treatment or placebo.[18] Similar to our studies, a meta-analysis by Piscoya et al. found no difference in adverse effects between the remdesivir and placebo arm (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.81–1.10).[20] Meta-analysis by Rezagholizadeh et al., including five RCTs and five nonrandomized studies, found that remdesivir administration was associated with a significantly lower risk of serious adverse events (RR 0.75; 95% CI, 0.63–0.90) compared to the placebo group.[3]

Our meta-analysis showed that remdesivir therapy for COVID-19 is associated with no mortality benefit compared to a placebo. There is no significant benefit in the form of time to clinical improvement. However, there was a significant reduction in the need for IMV/ECMO with remdesivir therapy. Also, remdesivir therapy is not associated with severe adverse events in the form of transaminitis and acute kidney injury.

Our systematic review and meta-analysis have several strengths. This systematic review and meta-analysis were prospectively registered with PROSPERO. It included only RCTs and was associated with a broad search. Recently published RCTs, Discovery trials and SOLIDARITY trials were included. We used the GRADE document on certainty evidence and did trial sequential analysis for mortality.

We recognize that our systematic review has certain limitations too. We observed significant heterogeneity among studies assessing time to clinical improvement and serious adverse effects related to remdesivir. As most trials are multicentric, the standard of care could not be assured equally. We observed the benefit of remdesivir in reducing the need for I. M. V. and ECMO, though it was not our primary outcome.

CONCLUSIONS

This meta-analysis shows that remdesivir therapy for COVID-19 leads to a significant reduction in the need for IMV and ECMO but is not associated with survival benefits or time to clinical improvement. Moreover, the risk of experiencing severe adverse events is comparable between remdesivir and control. Trial sequential analysis suggests that future trials are required to confirm the mortality benefit of remdesivir. We suggest that further good-quality RCTs assess the benefit of remdesivir for the need for mechanical ventilation and ECMO, taking it as the primary outcome.
