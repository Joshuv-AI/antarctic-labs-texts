RETRACTED: Physical activity motivation is inversely associated with anxiety: a cross-sectional serial mediation analysis involving smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality in medical undergraduates
Authors: Teng Ma, Lili Wang, Bingwei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety is common among medical undergraduates, yet modifiable behavioral pathways underlying the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety remain unclear. Guided by self-determination theory, the Interaction of Person–Affect–Cognition–Execution (I-PACE) model, and the two-process/hyperarousal framework of sleep, we tested smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality as parallel and serial statistical mediators of this association.MethodsIn a cross-sectional survey of 1,276 Chinese medical undergraduates, we assessed physical activity motivation (Motives for Physical Activity Measure–Revised, 15 items), smartphone addiction symptoms (Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version), sleep quality (Sleep Quality Questionnaire−9; higher scores indicate poorer sleep), and anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder−7). All instruments were administered using a harmonized 5-point Likert response format. Indirect effects were estimated using Hayes' PROCESS macro (Model 6) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples, controlling for sex and grade (academic year).ResultsIn bivariate analyses, physical activity motivation was inversely correlated with anxiety (r = −0.261, p < 0.001). In the covariate-adjusted serial mediation models, the total effect on anxiety was c = −0.311 (95% CI [−0.385, −0.237]) and remained statistically significant after accounting for smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality (direct effect: c′ = −0.216, 95% CI [−0.293, −0.139]). The total indirect effect was B = −0.095 (95% CI [−0.126, −0.066]). Specific indirect effects were significant via smartphone addiction symptoms (B = −0.062, 95% CI [−0.086, −0.040]) and via sleep quality (B = −0.028, 95% CI [−0.046, −0.012]), as well as via the serial pathway (physical activity motivation → smartphone addiction symptoms → sleep quality → anxiety; B = −0.006, 95% CI [−0.010, −0.002]; 1.80% of the total effect).ConclusionHigher physical activity motivation was associated with lower anxiety, with this association partially statistically accounted for by lower smartphone addiction symptoms and better sleep quality (lower sleep-quality scores), including a modest serial indirect pathway. Given the cross-sectional design, these indirect effects should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal mechanisms; longitudinal and experimental studies using objective smartphone-use and sleep metrics are needed to test temporal ordering.

Introduction

Anxiety among university students is a growing concern for higher education and public health. In China, a national “blue book” survey of 8,446 undergraduates reported an 8.4% prevalence of elevated anxiety and widespread insufficient sleep in the prior week: 43.8% reported insufficient sleep on several days, 7.9% on more than half the days, and 4.4% nearly every day [Social Sciences Academic Press (China), 2021]. Recent national reports and policy initiatives in China have further highlighted student mental health as a priority in higher-education governance and public health (Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, 2023; Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China, 2023). International evidence is consistent with this burden: in the American College Health Association survey, 64.3% of undergraduates reported “overwhelming anxiety” in the past 12 months, and 22.3% reported a diagnosis or treatment for anxiety in the previous year (American College Health Association, 2018). These patterns underscore the need to clarify actionable behavioral pathways that may help explain individual differences in anxiety, particularly in medical undergraduates who face intensive academic and training demands. Within a self-determination theory perspective, higher physical activity motivation is expected to be inversely associated with anxiety (Ntoumanis et al., 2021).

Although the association between physical activity and mental health is well documented, evidence on pathways remains fragmented. Recent syntheses emphasize heterogeneous mediators and moderators and call for more integrated frameworks focusing on modifiable behaviors such as sleep and digital media use (White et al., 2024; Nguyen Ho et al., 2023). Emerging student studies suggest that smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) and sleep quality (SQ) may both be involved in the physical activity motivation–anxiety link, yet models that examine these factors simultaneously, especially as an ordered pathway, remain limited (Qin et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2024). Guided by self-determination theory, the I-PACE framework, and contemporary sleep models, we specify a theory-informed statistical model in which smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality are examined as parallel and serial indirect paths in the physical activity motivation–anxiety association. Accordingly, this study aimed to: (1) examine the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety among medical undergraduates; (2) test whether smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality statistically account for this association via parallel and serial indirect paths; and (3) provide empirical evidence to inform theory-informed and practice-oriented campus strategies integrating motivation support with digital and sleep hygiene.

Literature review and research hypotheses

Physical activity motivation and anxiety

Self-determination theory (SDT) proposes that satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness facilitates the internalization of physical activity motivation and supports adaptive emotion regulation and mental health (Ntoumanis et al., 2021). Consistent with this perspective, studies in undergraduate and medical-student samples generally show that higher levels of physical activity are associated with lower psychological distress and anxiety across cultural and situational contexts (Rodríguez-Romo et al., 2023; Grasdalsmoen et al., 2020; Rogowska et al., 2020; Vidović et al., 2025). Higher-order evidence further suggests that anxiety symptoms are malleable: meta-analyses and umbrella reviews in university populations report meaningful reductions in anxiety following exercise-based programs (Chen P. et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2023). Importantly, beyond activity quantity, motivation quality appears consequential-autonomous motives (e.g., enjoyment and identification) show more consistent links to favorable psychological outcomes, whereas controlled motives tend to relate to less favorable profiles (Li J. et al., 2024; Zhong, 2024). From a measurement standpoint, the MPAM-R shows a stable five-factor structure and satisfactory reliability across samples, offering a practical tool for examining how motivational dimensions relate to anxiety differences (Albuquerque et al., 2017). Potential explanatory correlates include psychosocial processes such as self-esteem/self-concept, self-control and resilience, and body image (Kayani et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025; Biçki et al., 2025). In medical-student contexts, constraints such as limited time and restricted access to facilities often hinder regular participation, making motivation-supportive coursework and campus supports particularly relevant for translating motivational advantages into emotional benefit (Deisz et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2023). Based on this, Hypothesis 1 is proposed: Higher physical activity motivation is inversely associated with anxiety among medical undergraduates.

Smartphone addiction as a mediator

The I-PACE framework and the compensatory digital-media use perspective suggest that heightened negative affect combined with weakened executive/inhibitory control is associated with a higher likelihood of smartphone addiction symptoms, whereas stronger goal-directed self-regulation may be associated with lower levels of these patterns (Brand et al., 2019). In university and young-adult samples, physical activity motivation or actual participation generally shows an inverse association with smartphone addiction symptoms (SA), with self-control frequently emerging as a key correlate. For example, among students in sport-related majors, higher motivation corresponds to lower SA, with women tending to report higher SA overall (Aligül and Tolukan, 2024). In broader undergraduate cohorts, physical activity is indirectly linked to lower SA through self-esteem or core self-evaluations, suggesting a plausible “self-evaluation → self-regulation” bridge (Ke et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2023). Objective and self-report indicators also converge: individuals with higher SA tend to accumulate fewer steps and spend more time sedentary, consistent with time-displacement and attentional-capture explanations (Kim et al., 2015). Evidence syntheses indicate a small-to-moderate negative association between physical activity and addictive smartphone use, with self-control frequently emerging as a key correlate; longitudinal work further suggests that physical activity and self-control may mutually reinforce over time and jointly relate to lower SA levels (Pirwani and Szabo, 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024).

Downstream, SA is positively related to anxiety across cultures and often co-occurs with poorer sleep and broader health burdens (Alotaibi et al., 2022; Elhai et al., 2016). At the same time, some studies suggest that anxiety may also promote compensatory use, implying potential bidirectionality that should temper causal claims (Matar Boumosleh and Jaalouk, 2017). Accordingly, the present mediation hypothesis reflects a theory-informed statistical specification in which physical activity motivation is treated as the focal predictor, smartphone addiction symptoms as the intervening variable, and anxiety as the outcome; alternative temporal orderings (e.g., anxiety → smartphone addiction symptoms) were not tested in this study. A further caveat is measurement heterogeneity: constructs and instruments vary across studies, and terms such as “addiction,” “problematic use,” and “dependence” are not fully interchangeable, which can influence effect-size estimates even when the direction of association is stable (Panova and Carbonell, 2018; Harris et al., 2020). Taken together, existing theory and evidence support SA as a plausible statistical mediator linking physical activity motivation to anxiety. Based on this, Hypothesis 2 is proposed: Smartphone addiction symptoms statistically account for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety through an indirect pathway.

Sleep quality as a mediator

The two-process model of sleep and hyperarousal accounts propose that regular physical activity and stronger goal focus may reduce pre-sleep physiological and cognitive arousal, strengthen homeostatic sleep pressure, align sleep timing with circadian rhythms, and potentially improve subjective sleep quality (Xie et al., 2021; Ben Simon et al., 2020). In university and young-adult samples, higher physical activity is associated with better sleep, whereas insufficient activity co-occurs with multidimensional sleep impairments (Štefan et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020). Campus-based interventions and reviews further indicate that exercise programs have been reported to shorten sleep latency, reduce nocturnal awakenings, and enhance perceived sleep quality (Hurdiel et al., 2017). Structural-equation studies also suggest that the association between regular exercise and better sleep may be partly conveyed by reduced anxiety and increased wellbeing, highlighting potential reciprocal influences among these constructs (Zhu et al., 2025; Zhang Z. et al., 2025).

Downstream evidence links sleep to anxiety. Longitudinal syntheses indicate that baseline insomnia or poor sleep quality is associated with higher subsequent anxiety, and experimental work suggests that sleep loss can increase next-day anxiety, whereas deep non-REM (slow-wave) sleep may exert a protective effect (Hertenstein et al., 2019; Ben Simon et al., 2020). In student cohorts, the combination of adequate physical activity and good sleep is associated with the most favorable mental-health profile (Cahuas et al., 2020). Given evidence that sleep and anxiety can influence each other in youth and young adults, mediation models should interpret indirect effects as statistical pathways rather than definitive causal chains (Bacaro et al., 2024). Accordingly, in the present study, sleep quality is specified as an intervening variable in a theory-informed statistical model; alternative temporal orderings (e.g., anxiety → sleep quality) were not tested. Nevertheless, existing theory and evidence support sleep quality as a plausible statistical mediator. Based on this, Hypothesis 3 is proposed: Sleep quality statistically accounts for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety through an indirect pathway.

Serial mediation of smartphone addiction and sleep quality

Anchored in I-PACE and the two-process/hyperarousal accounts of sleep, converging evidence suggests a plausible “device use → sleep disruption → emotional burden” sequence. Two-wave cross-lagged work suggests that baseline smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) may predict subsequent declines in sleep quality (SQ), with bedtime procrastination and affective factors highlighted as plausible mechanisms (Cui et al., 2021). A semi-longitudinal chain further suggests that lower body satisfaction may be associated with greater problematic smartphone use, which in turn may be associated with increased sleep deprivation and reduced wellbeing, consistent with a possible temporal ordering from use to sleep (Yildirim-Kurtuluş et al., 2025). At the evidence-synthesis level, reviews consistently report that access to and use of portable screens before bedtime are associated with shorter sleep, poorer sleep quality, and greater daytime sleepiness (Carter et al., 2016). Intervention evidence also suggests potential modifiability: restricting phone use before bedtime or implementing broader screen-time reductions have been reported to shorten sleep-onset latency, improve subjective sleep, and enhance next-morning mood or wellbeing (He et al., 2020; Pedersen et al., 2022).

In university samples, SA commonly co-occurs with poorer SQ and elevated anxiety or stress (Al Battashi et al., 2021). Negative affect-including anxiety-may partially carry the SA–SQ link and may vary by sex, suggesting potential complexity in the underlying processes (Elkin et al., 2025; Li S. et al., 2025). Self-regulatory deficits and bedtime procrastination are repeatedly implicated as behavioral-psychological correlates linking SA with impaired sleep in undergraduate and medical-student contexts (Zhang and Wu, 2020; Feng et al., 2022). Incorporating upstream activity factors further supports the plausibility of an ordered pathway: higher physical activity levels are inversely associated with SA risk (Kim and Lee, 2022), and in collegiate models, greater activity has been associated with weaker downstream cascades from addiction-related behaviors to anxiety-related sleep problems (Wang et al., 2024). However, direct tests that begin with physical activity motivation and model SA and SQ sequentially in a single framework with anxiety as the endpoint remain limited. Many studies also lack objective usage and sleep indicators or do not contextualize bedtime phone use by timing, light exposure, and content, which may contribute to heterogeneity in effect sizes. Taken together, this literature provides a theory-informed rationale for specifying smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) preceding sleep quality (SQ) in the serial model; however, reciprocal relations cannot be adjudicated with cross-sectional data; an alternative ordering (SQ preceding SA) was examined only as a supplementary sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S2). Based on this, Hypothesis 4 is proposed: Smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality statistically account for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety through a serial indirect pathway, with SA specified before SQ (physical activity motivation → smartphone addiction symptoms → sleep quality → anxiety).

In summary, the literature reviewed above indicates that physical activity motivation, smartphone addiction symptoms, sleep quality, and anxiety are conceptually and empirically connected, and that self-determination theory, the I-PACE framework, and contemporary sleep models offer complementary explanations for how these constructs may be linked. Based on this synthesis, we established the hypothesized conceptual framework (Figure 1) and the theory-informed conceptual mapping of the proposed associations (Figure 2), and derived four research hypotheses to be tested in this study among medical undergraduates: (1) higher physical activity motivation is inversely associated with anxiety; (2) smartphone addiction symptoms statistically account for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety through an indirect pathway; (3) sleep quality statistically accounts for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety through an indirect pathway; and (4) smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality statistically account for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety through a serial indirect pathway, with SA specified before SQ (physical activity motivation → smartphone addiction symptoms → sleep quality → anxiety).

Methods

Participants

Undergraduate medical students enrolled at Xinjiang Second Medical College constituted the target population. We adopted a stratified cluster sampling approach with on-site recruitment organized by academic building and class period; strata were defined by academic building and class period, and clusters were defined as intact class sessions. Because recruitment occurred in intact class sessions, responses may be correlated within classes; analyses were conducted at the individual level, and the potential impact of within-class non-independence is addressed in the Limitations. Students in selected sessions were invited after class to scan a QR code and complete the questionnaire. Data collection was conducted during regular teaching hours from December 1 to 20, 2025. The survey platform automatically recorded start and end times and assigned anonymous identifiers based on submission order. In total, 1,400 questionnaires were distributed; according to prespecified data-quality criteria (completion time < 5 min, used as a prespecified screen for potentially inattentive responding given the questionnaire length (41 items), or >20% missing responses), 124 cases were excluded, yielding a final analytic sample of N = 1,276 (valid response rate: 91.14%). The sample included 640 men (50.16%) and 636 women (49.84%), spanning students from the first through the fourth year.

The study protocol was approved by the Science and Technology Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Second Medical College (IRB No.: MEC-XSMC-KT-20251118-001) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided electronic informed consent before beginning the questionnaire. Data were collected anonymously without personally identifiable information and were used solely for academic research and instructional improvement; no data were disclosed to third parties without permission.

Measures

Motives for physical activity measure-revised

The simplified Chinese Motives for Physical Activity Measure–Revised (MPAM-R, 15-item version; Ryan et al., 1997; Chen et al., 2013; Zhang L. et al., 2025) was used to assess physical activity motivation. The simplified Chinese 15-item version used in the present study employs a 5-point response format and therefore did not require additional response-format adaptation. The instrument originates from the SDT-based 30-item MPAM-R; in Chinese undergraduate samples, Chen et al. (2013) retained the three highest-loading items for each motive, yielding five dimensions-health/fitness, competence/challenge (often translated as competence), enjoyment, appearance, and social-for a total of 15 items. Consistent with this validated short form, all items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree,” 5 = “strongly agree”). Item-mean scores (range: 1–5) were computed, with higher values indicating stronger motives. Prior studies show good reliability and structural validity for this short form: in 2,072 undergraduates, Chen et al. (2013) preserved the original five-factor structure with acceptable psychometrics; in 1,530 secondary-school students, Zhang L. et al. (2025) replicated a robust five-factor solution (CFI = 0.998, IFI = 0.998, NFI = 0.992) and reported a total Cronbach's α of 0.954 with all corrected item–total correlations >0.40, supporting reliability and construct validity across contexts. A sample item is “Because I want to be physically fit.” In the present sample (N = 1,276), confirmatory factor analysis supported a second-order model in which the five motive dimensions loaded onto an overarching physical activity motivation factor (χ2/df = 1.180, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.012). Subscale Cronbach's α values ranged from 0.773 to 0.791, indicating good internal consistency. Accordingly, the overall 15-item mean score was used as the indicator of physical activity motivation (PAM) in subsequent analyses.

Smartphone addiction scale-short version

The Chinese version of the Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV; Kwon et al., 2013; Luk et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2022) was used to assess smartphone addiction symptoms among undergraduates. The SAS-SV is a 10-item abridgment of the original 33-item scale and is commonly administered with a 6-point Likert-type response format. To harmonize response options across measures and reduce respondent burden—and drawing on evidence that 5–7 response options typically perform well with minimal impact on internal consistency (Preston and Colman, 2000)—the response format was adapted to a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree,” 5 = “strongly agree”). Scores derived from this adapted response format are not directly comparable to those obtained using the original 6-point response options; therefore, cutoff-based classifications were not applied and scores were interpreted as continuous symptom levels within this sample. Item-mean scores (range: 1–5) were computed, with higher values indicating higher levels of smartphone addiction symptoms. Prior studies document solid psychometrics for the SAS-SV: in Korean adolescents (n = 540), Kwon et al. (2013) reported Cronbach's α = 0.911; in Hong Kong adults (n = 3,211), Luk et al. (2018) reported α = 0.844, a 1-week test–retest ICC = 0.76, and confirmatory support for the scale's construct validity; in Chinese university samples (two independent cohorts; combined n = 557), Zhao et al. (2022) reported α = 0.829/0.881 and good CFA fit (χ2/df = 1.883, RMSEA = 0.056, CFI = 0.978, TLI = 0.969, NFI = 0.954). A sample item is “I miss planned work due to smartphone use.” In the present sample (N = 1,276), CFA supported a second-order factor model in which the content facets Negative effect, Withdrawal, and Tolerance loaded onto a higher-order smartphone addiction factor (χ2/df = 1.138, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.999, RMSEA = 0.010). Internal consistency for the facet item clusters was α = 0.780 (Negative effect), 0.786 (Withdrawal), and 0.830 (Tolerance), indicating good reliability. Accordingly, the SAS-SV item-mean score served as the indicator of smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) in subsequent analyses. Facet mean scores for Negative effect, Withdrawal, and Tolerance were also computed and used in sensitivity analyses of the serial mediation model (Supplementary Table S1).

Sleep quality questionnaire

The simplified Chinese Sleep Quality Questionnaire (SQQ-C; Kato, 2014; Meng et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024) was used to assess subjective sleep quality in undergraduates, adopting the 9-item form (SQQ-9). The SQQ originates from a 10-item instrument developed for non-clinical populations and comprises two dimensions—Sleep Difficulty and Daytime Sleepiness. The original response format ranges from 0 to 4 (higher scores = poorer sleep quality). To harmonize response options across measures and reduce respondent burden—and consistent with evidence that five to seven categories typically sustain reliability and validity (Preston and Colman, 2000)—a 5-point scale was employed (1 = “strongly disagree,” 5 = “strongly agree”). Scores derived from this adapted 5-point response format are not directly comparable to those obtained using the original 0–4 response options; therefore, the SQQ-9 was used as a continuous self-reported sleep-quality index within this sample. Because no parallel-form data using the original 0–4 response format were available, measurement equivalence across response formats could not be directly evaluated in the present study. Prior research supports the scale's psychometrics: Kato reported a stable two-factor structure across samples of approximately n ≈ 1,400 with 8-week retest stability; in multicenter Chinese samples, Meng et al. (2023) reported S-CVI/Ave = 0.922, α = 0.712–0.838, ω = 0.723–0.840, ICC = 0.738–0.764, and showed that SQQ-9 outperformed the 10-item version in structural fit and invariance across groups and time; in health-professional students, Huang et al. (2024) confirmed good CFA fit for SQQ-9 (e.g., T1: χ2/df = 2.44, CFI = 0.970, TLI = 0.959, RMSEA = 0.067; T2: χ2/df = 2.39, CFI = 0.977, TLI = 0.968, RMSEA = 0.059) and demonstrated both cross-sectional and longitudinal measurement invariance, with satisfactory internal consistency and test–retest reliability. A sample item is “I had trouble sleeping” (Sleep Difficulty dimension). In the present sample (N = 1,276), a first-order correlated two-factor model showed good fit (χ2/df = 2.365, CFI = 0.991, TLI = 0.988, RMSEA = 0.033); Cronbach's α ranged from 0.795 (Sleep Difficulty) to 0.853 (Daytime Sleepiness), indicating good internal consistency. Item-mean scores (range: 1–5; higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality) were used as the overall sleep-quality index (SQ) for subsequent analyses.

Generalized anxiety disorder−7

Anxiety symptom endorsement was assessed using the Chinese version of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) (Spitzer et al., 2006; Tong et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2021). The GAD-7 is derived from DSM-IV criteria for generalized anxiety disorder, comprises seven items, and is unidimensional. The original response format is a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”). To harmonize response options across measures and reduce respondent burden—and consistent with evidence that five to seven response options typically perform well with minimal impact on internal consistency (Preston and Colman, 2000)—a 5-point response format was used in this study (1 = “strongly disagree,” 5 = “strongly agree”). Accordingly, scores in the present study reflect anxiety symptom endorsement rather than the original symptom-frequency response format and were used for continuous, group-level analyses rather than clinical screening or diagnosis. Item-mean scores (range: 1–5; higher scores indicate greater endorsement of anxiety symptoms) were computed for analysis. Foundational validation of the original GAD-7 reported good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.92) and test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.83); because the response format and scoring were adapted in the present study, established cut points from prior studies were not applied and are not directly comparable. In Chinese samples, Tong et al. (2016) (n = 213 patients with epilepsy) reported α = 0.888; among Chinese medical postgraduates (n = 1,021), Zhang et al. (2021) observed α = 0.93 and acceptable CFA fit after allowing an error covariance between items 3 and 4 (χ2/df = 3.48, CFI = 0.97, NFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.05), supporting a single-factor structure and multi-group measurement invariance. A sample item is “Not being able to stop or control worrying.” In the present sample (N = 1,276), a first-order single-factor model indicated good fit (χ2/df = 1.122, TLI = 0.999, RMSEA = 0.010), with Cronbach's α = 0.894; convergent validity indices were CR = 0.894 and AVE = 0.547, and standardized loadings ranged from 0.724 to 0.754. The GAD-7 item-mean served as the anxiety symptom-endorsement index (ANX) in subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics, and measurement models were evaluated via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in AMOS 26. Model fit indices included χ2/df, RMSEA, CFI, and TLI, with CR, AVE, and standardized loadings reported where applicable. To balance measurement evaluation and model parsimony, we adopted a two-step approach by first validating measurement models via CFA and then estimating regression-based mediation models using scale mean scores. Latent-variable SEM was not used in the present study, as our primary focus was on regression-based estimation of bootstrap indirect effects using PROCESS. Common method bias was examined as a preliminary diagnostic using Harman's single-factor test (unrotated principal component analysis). For all constructs, item-mean scores (1–5) were entered into models: higher values indicate stronger physical activity motivation, greater smartphone addiction symptoms, poorer sleep quality, and greater endorsement of anxiety symptoms, respectively. Descriptive statistics and bivariate associations are presented as means (M), standard deviations (SD), and Pearson's r (two-tailed, α = 0.05), with M/SD reported to two decimals and r to three decimals. Group differences were tested with independent-samples t-tests for sex [reporting t(df), p, and Cohen's d with 95% Cis] and one-way ANOVAs for grade [reporting F(df1, df2), p, and partial η2]; Tukey or Games-Howell procedures were used for post hoc comparisons based on homogeneity of variance. Mediation and serial mediation were estimated with the Hayes PROCESS macro: Model 4 tested the single-mediator paths (physical activity motivation → smartphone addiction → anxiety; physical activity motivation → sleep quality → anxiety), and Model 6 tested the serial path (physical activity motivation → smartphone addiction → sleep quality → anxiety). Sex and grade (academic year) were included as covariates in all PROCESS models. As sensitivity analyses, the serial mediation model (PROCESS Model 6) was re-estimated three times by substituting each SAS-SV facet (Negative effect, Withdrawal, and Tolerance) for the overall smartphone addiction symptom score as the first mediator; results are reported in Supplementary Table S1. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we re-estimated the serial mediation model with the mediator order reversed (sleep quality as M1 and smartphone addiction symptoms as M2; PAM → SQ → SA → ANX); results are reported in Supplementary Table S2. Indirect effects were evaluated using percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on 5,000 resamples. Reports include the unstandardized coefficient (B), standard error (SE), total effect (c), direct effect (c′), the total indirect effect, and each specific indirect effect; bootstrap CIs that did not include zero were interpreted as statistically significant. For reporting, standardized regression coefficients (β) are presented for the regression equations (as shown in the path diagram), whereas unstandardized effects (B) with bootstrap confidence intervals are reported for the total, direct, and indirect effects. Given the cross-sectional design, indirect effects are interpreted as statistical indirect associations and do not establish causal pathways or temporal ordering.

Results

Assessment of common method bias

This study assessed potential common method bias (common method variance, CMV) using Harman's single-factor test because all variables were collected in a single-wave self-report survey. All measurement items were entered simultaneously into an unrotated principal component analysis. As a preliminary diagnostic, the results yielded 11 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor (eigenvalue = 7.467) explained 18.21% of the total variance, which is below the commonly used 40% criterion. These findings suggest that no single factor accounted for the majority of covariance among the measures; however, given the limited sensitivity of Harman's single-factor test, common method variance (CMV) cannot be fully ruled out in the present dataset. To reduce potential method effects, the survey was administered anonymously with electronic informed consent; future studies may apply more rigorous approaches (e.g., marker-variable techniques or a common latent factor/latent method factor) to further assess and control for CMV.

Descriptive statistics and correlations

Table 1 reports descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations for physical activity motivation (PAM), smartphone addiction (SA), sleep quality (SQ), and anxiety (ANX) (N = 1,276; two-tailed). The means (SDs) were: PAM 3.00 (0.50), SA 3.02 (0.54), SQ 2.97 (0.52), and ANX 3.03 (0.69), with higher SQ scores indicating poorer sleep quality. Correlations showed that PAM was negatively correlated with SA (r = −0.279), SQ (r = −0.277), and ANX (r = −0.261), whereas SA was positively correlated with SQ (r = 0.235) and ANX (r = 0.256), and SQ was positively correlated with ANX (r = 0.204) (all ps < 0.001). Overall, coefficients were small-to-moderate in magnitude and were consistent with Hypothesis 1, providing the basis for subsequent mediation analyses.

In AMOS 26, a combined CFA measurement model including the four focal constructs demonstrated good fit (χ2/df = 1.068, RMSEA = 0.007, CFI = 0.997, TLI = 0.997). We then estimated the hypothesized serial mediation model of the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety via smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality using Hayes' PROCESS macro (Model 6; N = 1,276; percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, 5,000 resamples), with sex and grade (academic year) entered as covariates in all equations (higher SQ scores indicate poorer sleep quality).

Discussion

In this sample of medical undergraduates, physical activity motivation was inversely associated with anxiety, and the covariate-adjusted results (controlling for sex and grade) were consistent with partial statistical mediation (Table 5). Specifically, the total indirect effect accounted for 30.47% of the total association, comprising indirect effects via smartphone addiction symptoms (19.80%) and sleep quality (8.88%), as well as a statistically significant but modest serial indirect path via smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality in sequence (1.80%). Given the cross-sectional, self-report design, these indirect effects are interpreted as statistical indirect associations rather than evidence of causal mechanisms or temporal ordering. Conceptually, by integrating self-determination theory, the I-PACE framework, and contemporary sleep models within a single analytical framework, this study extends prior work by jointly modeling smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality as parallel and serial correlates of the physical activity motivation–anxiety association in medical undergraduates. From an applied perspective, this pattern of findings may inform integrated campus approaches that combine motivation-supportive physical-activity contexts with digital and sleep hygiene elements.

Physical activity motivation and anxiety

In this study, physical activity motivation was significantly and inversely correlated with anxiety (r = −0.261, p < 0.001). In the covariate-adjusted serial mediation model (controlling for sex and grade), the total effect on anxiety was c = −0.311 (95% CI [−0.385, −0.237]), and the direct effect remained significant after simultaneously accounting for smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) and sleep quality (SQ; higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality; c′ = −0.216, 95% CI [−0.293, −0.139]; Table 5), indicating partial statistical mediation. This pattern accords with prior evidence: across student and sport populations, autonomous/intrinsic or identified motivation tends to relate to lower anxiety or psychological distress, whereas controlled or amotivated regulation is often associated with higher anxiety (Ponseti et al., 2019; Vella et al., 2021; Granero-Jiménez et al., 2022; Macila et al., 2024; Melguizo-Ibáñez et al., 2023). Broader epidemiologic and longitudinal work likewise suggests small-to-moderate inverse links between physical activity and anxiety, and in some cases reduced risk of incident anxiety, lending convergent support for a motivation–affect connection (Herbert et al., 2020; Biddle et al., 2019; Schuch et al., 2019; Liu and Shi, 2023). From a Self-Determination Theory perspective, satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness may foster motivational internalization and may be associated with better emotion regulation and stress coping, which are in turn associated with lower anxiety (Ponseti et al., 2019; Granero-Jiménez et al., 2022; Macila et al., 2024). In educational and training settings, a task- or caring-oriented motivational climate may be associated with lower fear of failure, greater competence-relevant feedback, and stronger social support, whereas ego-/performance-oriented climates tend to be associated with higher state anxiety (Castro-Sánchez et al., 2018; Gómez-López et al., 2020; Hogue et al., 2017). Exercise self-efficacy is closely coupled with intrinsic motivation; higher efficacy corresponds to attenuated anxiety responses in evaluative or competitive contexts, consistent with the exercise-anxiolysis hypothesis (Lodewyk and Sullivan, 2016; Herbert et al., 2020). It is also noteworthy that reverse tendencies have been reported: elevated social or appearance anxiety may dampen exercise motivation and elicit avoidance, suggesting potential bidirectionality (Horenstein et al., 2021; Dimas et al., 2021). Even so, under the high academic and clinical demands typical of medical training, stronger activity motivation generally co-occurs with better mental and physical status and lower anxiety (de Souza et al., 2021; Liu and Shi, 2023), consistent with the association observed here. These patterns imply that need-supportive instructional strategies (e.g., offering meaningful choice, process-focused competence feedback, and peer connectedness) and attainable goal setting may facilitate motivational internalization and self-efficacy, with downstream benefits for affective outcomes. In sum, among medical undergraduates, physical activity motivation was inversely associated with anxiety, consistent with H1.

Independent mediation effects of smartphone addiction and sleep quality

Beyond the direct association, both indirect pathways were statistically significant in the covariate-adjusted model (controlling for sex and grade; Table 5). The indirect effect via smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) was B = −0.062 (95% CI [−0.086, −0.040]), accounting for 19.80% of the total effect, whereas the indirect effect via sleep quality (SQ; higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality) was B = −0.028 (95% CI [−0.046, −0.012]), accounting for 8.88%. The comparatively larger contribution of the SA pathway is consistent with student research testing the “physical activity (or motivation) → SA → anxiety” chain, and poorer sleep can co-occur with and potentially exacerbate the SA–anxiety association (Qin et al., 2025). Relatedly, adolescent evidence suggests that physical exercise is linked to lower psychological distress through reduced problematic mobile phone use and learning burnout (Li et al., 2021). From the perspective of the I-PACE and compensatory digital media use frameworks, stronger physical-activity motivation co-occurs with better executive control and goal-directed engagement, which is associated with lower non-purposeful, avoidance-driven screen time—an association repeatedly observed in student samples (Wang, 2025; Cheng et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2022); the protective effect can vary by activity type and participation mode (Yang et al., 2021). Conversely, the association between SA and anxiety may reflect proximal processes such as depletion of attentional and affective resources, social comparison, and bedtime procrastination (Geng et al., 2021; Pereira et al., 2020). Exercise-based interventions have been reported to coincide with reductions in SA and improvements in anxiety/stress, suggesting potential modifiability of these associations (Liu et al., 2022). Given heavy coursework and common nighttime screen use among medical students, these results point to actionable levers: pairing motivation-supportive strategies with core “digital hygiene” components (e.g., reducing pre-sleep scrolling and managing notifications) may help reduce the co-occurrence and linkage of smartphone addiction symptoms with anxiety.

By comparison, the sleep pathway was smaller in magnitude yet stable, consistent with multi-level evidence. Direct tests indicate that SQ significantly mediates the association between physical activity (or motivation) and anxiety (Chen X. et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Mu et al., 2024). Longitudinal and interventional work further suggests that exercise programs are often accompanied by improvements in sleep and reductions in anxiety (Ji et al., 2022; Wunsch et al., 2017). Syntheses show that regular physical activity improves sleep quality (Li L. et al., 2024), and better sleep is linked to lower anxiety levels (Li Z. et al., 2025). At the psychological and physiological levels, the two-process and hyperarousal models converge: regular activity and orderly routines promote alignment between homeostatic sleep pressure and circadian timing, dampen pre-sleep physiological and cognitive arousal, and thereby relate to better subjective sleep and less anxiety (Ye et al., 2022; Plesea-Condratovici et al., 2025). In medical student populations, these mechanisms support coupling need-supportive approaches with “sleep hygiene” practices (e.g., more stable bed/wake schedules and reduced evening stimulation/light exposure) to strengthen sleep-related functioning and downstream affective outcomes. Taken together, the statistically significant SA- and SQ-mediated pathways are consistent with H2 and H3. Given the cross-sectional design, these indirect effects are interpreted as statistical indirect associations rather than causal mechanisms or temporal ordering.

Serial mediation of smartphone addiction and sleep quality

Beyond the parallel paths, a statistically significant but modest sequential indirect effect was observed in which the association was statistically accounted for through SA and then SQ (higher SQ scores indicate poorer sleep): B = −0.006 (95% CI [−0.010, −0.002]), accounting for approximately 1.80% of the total effect (Table 5). This serial pattern is consistent with converging evidence supporting a “device use → sleep disruption → emotional burden” conceptual sequence proposed in prior work, in which higher SA is linked to poorer sleep and greater anxiety vulnerability (Cui et al., 2021; Carter et al., 2016; He et al., 2020; Pedersen et al., 2022; Al Battashi et al., 2021). At the aggregate level, physical activity and SA show a moderate negative association (Yu et al., 2025), and student studies repeatedly document the co-occurrence of SA, poorer SQ, and elevated distress (e.g., Song et al., 2024; Nikolić et al., 2023). For the SA → SQ segment, micro-behavioral mechanisms implicate bedtime procrastination and fear of missing out (FoMO) as plausible candidate mechanisms (Correa-Iriarte et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023). Time-series findings indicate that SA may precede subsequent sleep deterioration, although bidirectionality is also reported (Wu et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2020). Taken together, the literature delineates a coherent “motivation → device use → sleep → emotion” chain that is consistent with the small but stable serial estimate observed here. However, because these data are cross-sectional, temporal ordering cannot be established. Although an alternative mediator ordering specifying sleep quality as the first mediator and smartphone addiction symptoms as the second mediator yielded a similar pattern of indirect effects in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S2), this evidence does not adjudicate temporal sequencing, and the serial indirect association should be interpreted cautiously as a statistical pattern.

Conceptually, a dual-route account that is compatible with prior research suggests that “pre-sleep device use → sleep disruption → emotional dysregulation” is plausible: high-intensity social input and blue-light exposure near bedtime may elevate cognitive-emotional arousal and disrupt sleep homeostasis and circadian timing, and may be associated with poorer subjective sleep and greater anxiety vulnerability (Elhai et al., 2019; Exelmans and Van den Bulck, 2016; Hjetland et al., 2025). The chain appears malleable: randomized trials restricting screen time improve sleep and reduce negative affect (Pieh et al., 2025), and meta-analytic evidence links sleep-improvement interventions to reductions in anxiety (Scott et al., 2021). Moreover, regular physical activity and stronger activity motivation are associated with less pre-sleep phone use, better sleep, and fewer emotional symptoms (Su et al., 2024), and may buffer the adverse association between SA and sleep (Zhu et al., 2024). It is also important to note possible bidirectionality between SA and SQ and the intermediary role of affect, which may partly account for the modest magnitude of the serial effect (Wu et al., 2024; Li S. et al., 2025). Overall, the observed serial indirect effect is consistent with H4 at the level of statistical indirect associations, while remaining modest in magnitude.

Limitations and future directions

This study used a cross-sectional design with all variables collected via self-report at a single time point; accordingly, the indirect effects from the mediation and serial models are best interpreted as statistical indirect associations and do not establish causal pathways or temporal ordering. Although procedural remedies (e.g., anonymous participation) were implemented and Harman's single-factor test was used as a preliminary diagnostic, this approach has limited sensitivity and common method variance cannot be fully ruled out. Because recruitment was organized by intact class sessions, potential within-class non-independence was not explicitly modeled; future studies may apply multilevel modeling or cluster-robust inference. To harmonize scaling, smartphone addiction, sleep quality, and generalized anxiety were administered using a 5-point agreement response format and analyzed as item-mean scores; although CFA supported the intended factor structures and internal consistencies were adequate in this sample, these scores are not directly comparable to those obtained using the original response options or published cut points, and measurement equivalence across response formats could not be directly evaluated. Moreover, mediation was estimated using observed scale mean scores, which do not explicitly model measurement error; future studies may complement regression-based PROCESS analyses with latent-variable SEM. Objective smartphone logs and wearable-derived activity and sleep parameters were not included, and although sex and grade (academic year) were adjusted in the mediation models, other potential confounders (e.g., academic load, chronotype, caffeine intake, social support, and self-control) were not systematically measured or controlled, which may affect the precision and robustness of estimates. Bidirectionality between smartphone addiction and sleep quality may exist; the modest size of the serial effect also suggests that unmodeled parallel or alternative pathways remain. At the measurement level, invariance by sex and academic year was not tested, and competing temporal orders (e.g., sleep-first or affect-first) were not formally contrasted; although an alternative mediator ordering specifying sleep quality as the first mediator and smartphone addiction symptoms as the second mediator was examined as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S2), temporal direction cannot be established with these cross-sectional data.

Future work should employ multi-wave follow-ups and cross-lagged panel models, or preregistered randomized controlled trials, to examine temporal ordering and potential bidirectionality within the proposed associations among physical activity motivation, smartphone addiction symptoms, sleep quality, and anxiety. Integration of multi-source objective data is recommended, including time-of-day and app-type smartphone logs and notification exposure, together with wearable estimates of physical activity intensity, sleep onset latency, sleep duration, and sleep efficiency; diary methods and ecological momentary assessment can capture bedtime procrastination and pre-sleep arousal, thereby reducing common-method bias and refining mechanisms. On measurement, tests of invariance across sex and academic year are warranted, and further sensitivity analyses with parallel scoring versions should evaluate the impact of response scaling on conclusions. At the modeling level, alternative temporal orders and moderated-mediation structures should be compared; moderators such as academic burden, social support, self-control, fear of missing out, and physical-activity modality and intensity merit examination. Actual physical activity and subjective motivation should be modeled concurrently, with multilevel analyses to represent classroom and course contexts. For replication and transparency, multi-center, cross-discipline, and cross-region studies with a priori power analyses are encouraged, along with preregistration and open sharing of materials, data, and analysis code. At the implementation level, pilot combined programs in medical training and rotations-pairing need-supportive pedagogy with digital hygiene and sleep hygiene-could include device-free bedtime windows, evening low-to-moderate activity, and management of blue-light exposure and notifications; both subjective and objective indicators should track linked improvements in phone use, sleep, and anxiety while attending to feasibility and scalability.

Conclusions and recommendations

Drawing on a sample of medical undergraduates, this study estimated a serial multiple-mediator model to examine whether smartphone addiction symptoms (SA) and sleep quality (SQ) statistically accounted for the association between physical activity motivation and anxiety. Physical activity motivation was inversely associated with anxiety, and this association was partially statistically accounted for by indirect effects via smartphone addiction symptoms (19.80% of the total effect), via sleep quality (8.88%), and via a modest serial indirect pathway involving smartphone addiction symptoms and sleep quality in sequence (1.80%). Collectively, the total indirect effect accounted for 30.47% of the total effect, with the remainder retained as a direct association (all proportions refer to estimates in this sample under the present analytic framework). These estimates were obtained after adjusting for sex and grade (academic year); however, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. This integrated pattern is consistent with Self-Determination Theory (motivational internalization and emotion regulation), the I-PACE model (self-regulation and digital behavior), and the two-process/hyperarousal account of sleep, highlighting digital behavior and sleep as potentially relevant correlates in the motivation–affect association. Given the cross-sectional, self-report, single-institution design and the harmonized five-point response formats, these findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal mechanisms; multi-wave longitudinal studies and preregistered interventions incorporating objective smartphone-use and sleep metrics are needed to test temporal ordering, malleability, and generalizability.

For health promotion among medical undergraduates, a coordinated “motivation support–digital hygiene–sleep hygiene” approach may be considered as a potential, theory-informed direction. In curricula and training, institutions may cultivate a need-supportive instructional climate (meaningful choice, process-focused competence feedback, and peer connectedness) and facilitate feasible opportunities for regular activity that fit intensive schedules. Regarding smartphone-related behaviors, strategies may include a pre-bedtime “quiet window,” notification management, self-monitoring tools, and attractive offline alternatives (e.g., in-person social activities or light physical activity) to reduce fragmented bedtime scrolling. Regarding sleep, strategies may include consistent bed/wake timing, daytime light exposure and reduced evening light/stimulation, low-intensity pre-sleep routines or relaxation training, and moderation of evening caffeine and screen exposure to support sleep-related functioning. For implementation, resources can be stratified by need and academic year (e.g., prioritizing upper-year students or those reporting higher anxiety, higher smartphone addiction symptoms, or poorer sleep), and small-group delivery at the class- or dormitory-level may enhance feasibility. For process evaluation, low-burden periodic monitoring can be considered, and—under voluntary participation with clear privacy safeguards—objective indicators (e.g., anonymized smartphone-use logs and sleep metrics) may support iterative optimization. The effectiveness and feasibility of such integrated components should be evaluated in longitudinal and intervention studies.
