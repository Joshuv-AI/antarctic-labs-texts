Artificial intelligence-assisted quality control circles led by clinical pharmacists to improve the rational use of parenteral proton pump inhibitors among hospitalised patients
Authors: Ming Zhang, Wei-cheng Ma, Wen-yue He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are a class of drugs that inhibit gastric acid secretion and are commonly overused in clinical practice. We developed a quality control circle (QCC) assisted by artificial intelligence (AI) and led by clinical pharmacists to improve rational PPI use among patients in our hospital. A QCC team was established to follow QCC procedures. The root causes of inappropriate use were identified via AI-assisted brainstorming, and corresponding strategies were developed. The percentages of rational injectable PPI use before and after QCC implementation were calculated and compared. Radar charts were used to evaluate the capabilities of QCC members. Following QCC implementation, the percentage of rational injectable PPI use among hospitalised patients in our hospital increased from 66.51 to 93.43%, with a goal achievement percentage of 126.92% and a progression percentage of 40.47%. The increase in the percentage of rational PPI use was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Team cohesion, responsibility and proficiency in QCC techniques improved following QCC implementation. The standardised process for using injectable PPIs became more regulated following this process. AI-assisted, clinical pharmacist-led QCCs represent an efficient management tool for improving rational injectable PPI use among hospitalised patients, thereby promoting rational drug use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-16934-1.

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are a class of drugs that specifically block the H + /K + ATPase enzyme (proton pump) located on the apical membrane of gastric parietal cells, thereby inhibiting gastric acid secretion. Compared with histamine H2 receptor antagonists, PPIs provide a more complete and longer-lasting acid suppression effect, with an action duration of 16–18 h, effectively controlling both basal and meal-stimulated gastric acid secretion1. PPIs have come to replace histamine H2-receptor antagonists as the first-line treatment for conditions such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)2, acid-related diseases, erosive gastritis, gastrointestinal strictures and Barrett’s oesophagus as well as in efforts to prevent gastrointestinal bleeding caused by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids2,3. In the past decade, the use of PPIs has increased annually, with many countries even classifying PPIs as over-the-counter (OTC) drugs, leading to widespread long-term misuse without indications4–6. As a result of the global increase in PPI usage7, concerns regarding the inappropriate use of PPI formulations have also become more common. It has been reported that more than 50% of PPI use is inappropriate, meaning that the clinical indications for over half of PPI use do not align with guidelines or expert consensus recommendations4.

The long-term overuse of PPIs not only increases national healthcare costs but also significantly increases the incidence of adverse drug reactions, causing substantial harm to patients8,9. Studies have shown that long-term misuse of PPIs is associated with adverse reactions, which can be classified into two categories: acid-related reactions and nonacid-related reactions. Acid-related adverse effects include gastrointestinal infections; pneumonia; deficiencies in nutrients, such as calcium, iron, magnesium and vitamin B12; fractures; primary peritonitis; and small intestine bacterial overgrowth. Nonacid-related adverse effects include allergic reactions, acute interstitial nephritis, chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular events10. Therefore, long-term misuse of PPIs entails significant safety risks. Additionally, studies indicate a clear correlation between the irrational use of PPIs and increased treatment costs. Global misuse of PPIs is estimated to incur an annual cost of approximately £2 billion, representing a substantial financial burden on both patients and health care systems11. Moreover, PPI injections are considerably more expensive than are their oral equivalents, and medical justification for their routine use is limited12. Studies have shown that most PPI injection prescriptions in hospitals are inappropriate, leading to increased health care costs and potential adverse effects13. Therefore, improving the rational use of PPIs, especially for PPI injections, is an urgent problem.

Quality control circles (QCCs), which were originally used in Japan for corporate management and business operations, serve as tools for process management and problem solving14. QCCs leverage the strengths of all participants while fostering interdepartmental collaboration, simplifying complex issues, and ultimately resolving challenges related to institutional operations and management15. Since their introduction into Chinese health care institutions in 2001, QCCs have been used to identify and solve medical problems, thereby improving the quality of health care services in China16. QCCs are effective management tools in health care institutions that are used to address various issues encountered in clinical activities. Brainstorming is a crucial component of the QCC process, which includes key steps such as topic selection, cause analysis and countermeasure development. Traditionally, brainstorming relies heavily on the divergent thinking of QCC members, and the outcomes are often limited by the cognitive abilities of team members.

ChatGPT, an AI platform developed by OpenAI in November 2022, uses big data analysis to answer a wide range of user queries and can be employed to enhance brainstorming during QCCs by fostering divergent thinking. ChatGPT can analyse large datasets on the basis of user-defined prompts and answer related professional questions. This capability is particularly useful during the brainstorming phase of QCCs, as AI can complement the results of traditional brainstorming sessions by providing a comprehensive and efficient analysis of related factors and proposing solutions. In clinical pharmacy practice, clinical pharmacists play crucial roles in ensuring patient safety17, optimising medication therapy, facilitating medication reconciliation for hospitalised patients18, and promoting rational clinical drug use. This study pioneered the use of AI in QCC brainstorming sessions, implementing a clinical pharmacist-led, AI-supported QCC program to optimise inappropriate PPI injection use among hospitalised patients and enhance rational drug utilisation.

Methods

Context of QCCs

Our hospital is a large tertiary Grade B comprehensive medical centre in China. We have implemented an electronic prescription system and currently employ seven professionally trained clinical pharmacists who have completed standardised clinical pharmacy training. The hospital has established a Pharmacy Administration Committee, and our prescription management system has achieved HIMSS Electronic Medical Record Adoption Model (EMRAM) Stage 7 certification.

Prior to the implementation of the QCC initiative, PPI injections were widely used in our hospital; however, physicians demonstrated insufficient knowledge regarding the appropriate use of such injections. Furthermore, the current lack of clinical pharmacists specialising in gastroenterology at our institution has resulted in suboptimal medication management within this specialty. These factors have collectively contributed to the prevalence of inappropriate clinical applications of PPI injections.

Data collection

Before implementation of QCCs, PPI injections were widely used in the clinic at our hospital. Physicians had insufficient knowledge of their rational use.

Data were collected on all inpatient PPI injection prescriptions at our hospital from November to December 2023 (before QCC implementation) and from June to July 2024 (after QCC implementation).

Complete case records were available for all patients, and informed consent was obtained from all the subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). All methods were implemented in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All the experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the medicinal ethics committee of Ningbo Yinzhou No. 2 Hospital (Approval number: Yin Er Lun Review 2024 research approval No. 081). All patient-related data were anonymized for analysis.

Topic selection

A total of 1290 hospitalised patients in our hospital who were prescribed injectable PPIs (including omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole and ilaprazole) were selected for this research, which was conducted from November to December 2023. The initial assessment revealed compliance by 66.51% of patients based on case reviews conducted in accordance with the guidelines for clinical application of proton pump inhibitors (2020 edition) issued by the national health commission19. All subsequent medical record reviews concerning the rational use of PPI injections in this QCC activity were assessed for compliance on the basis of this standard. A QCC team that consisted of six members (including four clinical pharmacists, one member from the Medical Affairs Department and one from the Information Technology Department) was formed, and one AI participant (ChatGPT, version 3.0) was involved only in brainstorming and not in actual project implementation. On the basis of preliminary discussions and brainstorming results, combined with the L-type matrix method, the goal of this QCC was to improve the rational use of injectable PPIs among hospitalised patients.

Activity planning

Before the activities started, the tasks and responsibilities were assigned on the basis of each team member’s work capabilities and the content of each project phase. The activities were scheduled in a chronological sequence, and a Gantt chart was used to monitor and control progress. As shown in Fig. 1, the planning phase (P), implementation phase (D), checking and evaluation phase (C) and summary and assessment phase (A) accounted for approximately 30%, 40%, 20% and 10% of the total activity duration, respectively. The quality improvement project was conducted over a 10-month period on the basis of the QCC methodology.

Development of a process flowchart related to the topic

The QCC team held a meeting to thoroughly discuss and review the clinical use of injectable PPIs, resulting in the development of a flowchart for the use of injectable PPIs among hospitalised patients (Fig. 2). Because this activity was led by clinical pharmacists, the improvements focused on three key processes: complying with the indications for PPI use, issuance of a prescription for PPIs and a review of pharmacists’ prescriptions.

Collection of baseline data

Clinical pharmacists used rational drug use software to extract all PPI injection cases at our hospital from November to December 2023, resulting in a total of 1290 cases. A specialised review team evaluated these cases on the basis of drug labels, domestic and international guidelines and expert consensus. The specialised review team consisted of 10 clinical physicians and 5 pharmacists, all of whom held senior titles. The clinical pharmacists collected the review results, calculated the percentage of rational use of injectable PPIs, and performed a categorised analysis of irrational prescriptions on the basis of error types. Patient characteristics, inappropriate prescribing indications, and the characteristics of prescribers and pharmacists were included. The characteristics of the patients provided prescriptions, alongside information on the indications for irrational prescriptions, prescribing physicians, and pharmacists, were also collected.

Goal setting

According to the QCC "5-3-1" evaluation method, the QCC team’s circle capability was calculated to be 80%. The formula used to calculate the target value is as follows:

Current value + [(1 − Current value) * Improvement Focus * Circle Capability].

Root cause analysis

Numerous reasons can account for the low percentage associated with the rational use of injectable PPIs among hospitalised patients. The QCC team conducted a brainstorming session involving all members, and search results provided by ChatGPT (version 3.0, with the query set as follows: "Analyse all reasons for the low rational use percentage of injectable proton pump inhibitors among hospitalised patients from the perspectives of personnel, drugs, environment, methods and systems") were also considered. This analysis identified five major categories of causes: personnel, drugs, environment, methods and systems. Each major category was further analysed to identify all subcauses, which were then depicted in a fishbone diagram. All the causes were then scored on the basis of their importance using the "5-3-1" evaluation method20 to identify root causes. The "5-3-1" grading scale was as follows: 1 (not important), 3 (moderately important) and 5 (very important). According to the Pareto principle, factors that received scores of 24 or higher were considered to represent root causes.

Verification of root causes

The six key factors were used to design a survey questionnaire using the 5W1H principle and were distributed to various wards for root cause verification. A total of 60 people were surveyed, including 50 physicians and 10 pharmacists.

Development of countermeasures

The QCC team conducted a brainstorming session to develop corresponding countermeasures for the three identified root causes on the basis of the 5W1H principle. The QCC team then used the "5-3-1" evaluation method to score the proposed countermeasures in terms of feasibility, effectiveness and autonomy. Each item had a maximum score of 30 points, yielding a total possible score of 90 points. Following the Pareto principle, solutions scoring ≥ 72 points were selected for countermeasures. This process helps determine appropriate countermeasures for each root cause and establish the implementation timeline, location and responsible parties.

Implementation of countermeasures

The overall countermeasure implementation flowchart and timeline for this QCC activity are presented in Fig. 3. Three countermeasure teams were implemented to improve the rate of the appropriate use of PPI injection among inpatients: (1) insufficient medication review competency among pharmacists, (2) inadequate physician knowledge of PPI injection indications, and (3) the absence of an intelligent prescription control system for PPI injection management. The specific implementation timelines for each countermeasure team are also detailed in Fig. 3.

Countermeasure team 1: implementation of measures for the insufficient capacity of pharmacists to review prescriptions

Before the implementation of countermeasures, our hospital had not implemented a unified review standard for PPI use, which prevented preliminary reviews by pharmacists from accurately intercepting incorrect prescriptions according to a standard. Our hospital’s QCC team developed simplified PPI injection order review criteria by adapting the guidelines for clinical application of proton pump inhibitors (2020 edition) issued by the national health commission to our institution’s specific clinical context. The review standards for injectable PPIs are included in the supplementary material 1 and are solely intended for use by pharmacists who are responsible for these reviews, thus helping them assess the appropriateness of PPI injection orders. The Clinical Rational Medication Use Management Group under the Hospital Pharmacy Administration and Drug Therapy Committee convened to evaluate and validate these criteria for consistency. All preliminary review pharmacists were then trained in the new standards to enhance their preliminary review capabilities. After training is completed, preliminary review pharmacists conduct comprehensive reviews of PPI injection prescriptions and assess compliance with the new standards in terms of indications, dosing, administration route, treatment frequency, duration, contraindications, potential drug‒drug interactions and so on. Data concerning prescription interceptions were collected during two phases, preintervention (January 15 − 30, 2024) and postintervention (February 7 − 22, 2024), to evaluate the impact of countermeasure team 1 on the capacity of pharmacists to review prescriptions.

Countermeasure team 2: implementation of measures for physicians’ inadequate understanding of PPI indications

Before the countermeasures were implemented, our hospital faced issues due to the fact that internal medicine physicians lacked an understanding of the indications for injectable PPIs and surgical physicians lacking an understanding of the indications for PPI use in surgical prophylaxis. To address these issues, the QCC team created small tip cards to outline the newly established indications for injectable PPIs, which were distributed to all physicians to be placed on their white coats. This initiative aimed to increase physicians’ understanding of the indications for injectable PPIs. Additionally, the QCC members conducted training on the rational use of PPIs for key departments in our hospital, providing feedback on irrational use cases to improve the understanding of PPI indications among physicians in various departments. PPI injection prescription reviews were collected preintervention (February 23–March 8, 2024) and postintervention (March 29–April 12, 2024) to assess improvements in appropriate prescribing rates following the implementation of countermeasure team 2.

Countermeasure team 3: implementation of measures for the lack of intelligent management of prescription orders

Before the countermeasures were implemented, several limitations in the prescription of injectable PPIs by physicians were noted. (1) The prescription orders did not contain fasting reminders, thus leading to injectable PPIs being prescribed to patients who could take oral medication. (2) No reminders concerning the indications of injectable PPIs were, resulting in many injections being prescribed without appropriate indications. (3) The rational drug use database was incomplete, leading to a lack of intelligent control over dosage, administration, and duration, resulting in incorrect dosing and prolonged treatment with injectable PPIs.

To address these issues, the QCC team, in collaboration with our hospital’s Information Center, developed a template for prescribing injectable PPIs. The template focuses specifically on regulating the five PPI injections used in our hospital, while unnecessary formulary drug products are excluded. As shown in Fig. 4A, this template automatically checks for fasting orders when a PPI injection is prescribed, and if no fasting is indicated, the physician is prompted to consider oral PPIs instead. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 4B, when injectable PPIs are prescribed, the template requires physicians to select the corresponding indications on the basis of risk factors or treatment needs, thereby standardising the use of injectable PPIs. The template also enables automatic control of the duration of PPI injection, with the ability to automatically terminate medication orders upon reaching the 7-day threshold. Furthermore, the QCC team improved the rational drug use database, thus facilitating the employment of rational drug use software (Ipharmacare, developed by Hangzhou Yiyao Information Technology Co., Ltd.) to provide intelligent reminders and control over the dosage, route, frequency, potential drug interactions, contraindications, drug‒drug interactions, etc. Prescription review outcomes for PPI injections were collected during two phases, preintervention (April 13–27, 2024, before countermeasure team 3) and postintervention (May 13–27, 2024, after countermeasure team 3), to assess the improvement in the rates of appropriate PPI injection orders following the implementation of countermeasure team 3.

Effectiveness verification

After all countermeasures were implemented, a total of 731 cases of injectable PPIs in our hospital from June to July 2024 were collected via rational drug use software to facilitate rational drug use comments. These results were compared with the pre-QCC implementation results. The target achievement and improvement percentages were calculated, and Pareto charts, process flowcharts and radar charts were created to visualise the changes before and after QCC implementation21.

Target Achievement Percentage = (percentage of appropriate PPI injection use after improvement − percentage of appropriate PPI injection use before improvement)/(target percentage of appropriate PPI injection use − percentage of appropriate PPI injection use before improvement) *100%

Improvement Percentage = (percentage of appropriate PPI injection use after improvement − percentage of appropriate PPI injection use before improvement)/( percentage of appropriate PPI injection use before improvement) *100%

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the assistance of SPSS 19.0 software, and count data were analysed by conducting a chi-square analysis. A p value of < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Baseline data and target value

The results revealed that the percentage of rational injectable PPI use from November to December 2023 (before team implementation) was 66.51% (baseline value). The “Plato analysis” of error types in irrational prescriptions is shown in Fig. 5. According to the “Plato analysis” principle, “inappropriate indications” and "inappropriate dosage and administration" were identified as the primary areas of focus for improvement during this phase (improvement focus, 79.17%). Moreover, consistent with the goal-setting formula established in the methods section, the target value = 66.51% + [(100% − 66.51%) * 79.17% * 80%], resulting in a calculated target value of 87.72%.

Results of the root cause analysis

The fishbone diagram results from the root cause analysis are shown in Fig. 6. Among all causes, the lack of clarity in the indications for PPI use and the nonstandardisation of PPI use were identified by ChatGPT. After all causes were scored on the basis of their importance using the "5-3-1" evaluation method20 (Table 2), the QCC team identified six key factors that contributed to the low percentage of rational injectable PPI use: inadequate understanding of PPI indications among physicians, inadequate knowledge base of pharmacists, insufficient preprescription review capability of pharmacists, no standardised medication guidelines, the lack of intelligent management for PPI prescription orders and inadequate prescription review efforts.

The insufficient preprescription review capability of pharmacists refers to the lack of competency among the pharmacists responsible for prospective prescription review, leading to failures to promptly identify and intercept erroneous physician orders. Inadequate prescription review efforts, on the other hand, pertain to retrospective prescription review (postprescription auditing), where insufficient pharmacist expertise results in suboptimal evaluation outcomes. Although both roles involve pharmacists, their specific job distributions differ—preprescription reviews focus on real-time intervention, whereas postprescription reviews emphasise retrospective analysis and quality improvement. Therefore, these two factors should be classified and validated as ​distinct root causes in medication safety assessments.

Results of the root cause verification

The survey results were analysed and presented in the form of a Pareto chart (Fig. 7). On the basis of the Pareto principle, the survey confirmed that the insufficient understanding of the indications for PPI use, lack of intelligent management for prescription orders and inadequate preliminary review capabilities are the root causes of the low percentage of rational injectable PPI use among hospitalised patients.

Discussion

This project represents the first application of AI tools to facilitate the implementation of QCCs under the leadership of clinical pharmacists with the goal of improving the rational use of injectable PPIs among hospitalised patients in tertiary public hospitals. AI is employed predominantly during the brainstorming stage of QCCs. Lee and Chung reported that ChatGPT augmented human creativity in brainstorming22. Guided by the aforementioned literature, QCC members first leverage AI to formulate queries, after which AI produces a set of responses. Building upon these outputs, the members subsequently engage in brainstorming to generate supplementary recommendations, thereby significantly improving the creativity and efficiency of the brainstorming session.

The results revealed that after QCC implementation, the percentage of rational injectable PPI use among patients in our hospital increased from 66.51 to 93.4%, with a target achievement percentage of 126.92% and an improvement percentage of 40.47%. Research suggests that an achievement percentage exceeding 150% implies insufficient confidence in the predefined target, whereas a percentage below 80% may signal an overestimation of the QCC’s improvement capability23. Although the target achievement percentage in this study exceeded 100%, it remained within a reasonable range (80–150%), demonstrating that the QCC successfully attained its predefined objectives while maintaining appropriate target-setting validity. A comparison of the percentages of the rational use of injectable PPIs before and after QCC implementation revealed a significant difference (P < 0.01), indicating a marked improvement in the rational use of PPIs following QCC intervention. In addition to tangible improvements, QCC implementation has led to intangible benefits, such as the full engagement of the potential and capabilities of all team members and the enhancement of interdepartmental collaboration24. Accordingly, the radar chart in Fig. 8B shows that the QCC members’ competencies—such as QCC techniques, team responsibility, cohesion, confidence, problem-solving and communication skills—were significantly improved after QCC implementation. Moreover, according to the Pareto principle (80/20 rule), inappropriate indications and inappropriate dosages and administrations constituted the predominant prescription errors prior to QCC implementation. Consistent with prior studies25, our quality improvement cycle primarily evaluated these two error types. Postintervention data demonstrated a marked reduction in inappropriate PPI injections: the number of cases without proper indications decreased from 276 (63.89%) to 5 (10.42%), whereas the number of dosage and administration errors declined from 66 (15.29%) to 7 (14.58%). These findings confirm that the QCC intervention effectively addressed major issues pertaining to prescriptions, resulting in significantly improved rates of appropriate parenteral PPI orders. Furthermore, the standardisation of the PPI injection process was enhanced post-QCC, with the introduction of an intelligent management system for prescription orders, resulting in a more standardised and rational process for prescribing PPIs in our hospital.

Limitations

Despite the significant improvements achieved through QCC intervention, certain limitations persist. As clinical trials continue, the indications for PPI use are subject to ongoing updates. Therefore, continually optimising existing strategies, particularly rational drug use decision support systems and intelligent management systems for PPI injection prescriptions, is essential to increase their regulatory effectiveness. Moreover, while the QCC interventions could effectively improve the appropriateness of PPI injection order at this large public hospital, the single-centre study design used in this research may limit generalizability to other practice settings. Additionally, as this study focused on hospital-wide management of injections, inherent variations in patient demographics, disease types, and baseline characteristics across different periods made it challenging to assess the specific impacts of the QCC interventions through changes in medical costs or clinical outcomes. Thus, the analysis was restricted to process indicators, such as the percentage of appropriate PPI use in medical records. To address these limitations, subsequent research can focus on a specific department and target diagnosis with homogeneous baseline characteristics and compare medical expenditures and clinical outcomes before and after QCC implementation to elucidate its clinical impact more clearly.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that quality control circles (QCC teams) led by clinical pharmacists, who are assisted by AI technologies, can serve as efficient management tools to promote rational drug use in clinical settings. This approach effectively improves the percentage of rational injectable PPI use among hospitalised patients, thereby advancing rational medication practices. The integration of an intelligent PPI prescription management system further optimises workflow efficiency, underscoring the potential of AI-facilitated QCCs to serve as an innovative approach to antimicrobial stewardship and clinical decision support. However, this study has several limitations, as noted above. Future research should expand to encompass multicentre settings and incorporate patient-specific clinical and economic metrics (e.g., hospital costs) to validate the intervention’s scalability and cost-effectiveness. Targeted studies within homogeneous patient cohorts could further isolate the impact of QCCs while minimising confounding variables. In conclusion, AI-supported pharmacist-led QCCs represent a replicable and effective strategy for promoting rational drug use, which has implications for efforts to standardise high-risk medication management in hospital settings.
