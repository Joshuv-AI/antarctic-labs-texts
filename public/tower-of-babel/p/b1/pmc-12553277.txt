Indigenous youth mental health: an ecological approach to understanding risk and protective factors for Māori youth in Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Terryann C Clark, Esther S Yao, Nicola Harrison, Bradley Drayton, Logan Hamley, Terry Fleming, Sue Crengle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMāori (Indigenous) youth of Aotearoa New Zealand are experiencing widening inequities in mental health when compared to Pākehā (New Zealand European/other European/“White”; cultural majority) youth. This study used an ecological approach to investigate key risk and protective factors for Māori to help prioritise actions.MethodsA subsample of Māori participants (N = 1,528) from a large representative cross-sectional secondary dataset on secondary school students’ health and wellbeing (Youth’19) were used to examine the effects of explanatory variables selected from five domains (individual, and their wider social ecosystem, including whānau/family, school, and community) on four mental health outcomes (wellbeing, depressive symptoms, suicide thoughts, suicide attempts). Descriptive statistics (percentages and 95% CIs) were used to examine the equity of these variables between Māori and Pākehā/White youth.ResultsOverall, individual risk factors included female sex, functional disability, and sexual abuse. Wider adverse social ecosystems included housing precarity, household deprivation, bullying, and racial discrimination. Key protective factors included the Indigenous concept of whanaungatanga (relationships/connection) with whānau (family), and feelings of safety in social ecosystems including home, school, and neighbourhoods. These risk and protective factors were broadly similar across the four mental health outcomes. Compared to Pākehā/White youth, Māori youth were exposed to greater risk-conferring ecosystems, including socioeconomic deprivation, discrimination, and violence-related harms.ConclusionsAddressing inequitable mental health harm requires not only intervening at the individual level, but also addressing the wider social ecosystems that disproportionately harm Māori youth, including colonial systems that consistently impact Indigenous youth mental health, particularly housing precarity, poverty, violence, bullying, and racism. Culturally and developmentally appropriate approaches with a focus on creating safe and inclusive societies are urgently required to reduce Indigenous youth mental health inequity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24845-z.Keywords: Māori, Indigenous, Adolescent, Youth, Mental health, Suicide, Inequity

Over the past decade, there has been a concerning increase in mental health distress (e.g., anxiety and depression) among adolescents, particularly in high-income Western countries [1–3]. This trend is also observed in the colonial settler state of Aotearoa New Zealand (hereafter Aotearoa). The New Zealand Health Survey shows over a three-fold increase in 15- to 24-year-olds reporting high or very high psychological distress from 2013/14 to 2023/24 (7% to 23%) [4]. In addition, the large representative Youth2000 survey series shows that, between 2012 and 2019, the prevalence of secondary school students (approx. aged 13–18 years) reporting significant depressive symptoms increased from 13% to 23%, suicide thoughts in the past year from 15% to 21%, and suicide attempts in the past year from 4% to 6% [5].

Notably, mental health inequities are worsening for Māori (Indigenous) youth, who are more likely to experience distress than Pākehā (New Zealand European/other European/“White”; cultural majority) youth. For example, the proportion of Māori youth who reported significant depressive symptoms increased from 14% in 2012 to 28% in 2019 (compared to 12% in 2012 to 20% in 2019 for Pākehā), and the proportion of Māori youth who reported at least one suicide attempt in the past year increased from 6% in 2012 to 13% in 2019 (compared to 2% in 2012 to 3% in 2019 for Pākehā) [5, 6]. Compared to Pākehā youth, there is a pattern of persistent Māori disadvantage, particularly visible in experiences of socioeconomic deprivation, housing and food scarcity, and forgone healthcare [6]. There is a similar pattern of health, social, and educational inequities among colonised Indigenous populations globally [7–9].

The dominant response in Aotearoa, and other colonised nations such as Canada, Australia, and the United States, has been to attempt to increase mental health service delivery to individuals. However, services are unable to respond to the rapid increase in need, with funding in Aotearoa only available for the 3% to 5% of the population with the most severe need [10]. Given that 28% of Māori youth in secondary schools report significant depressive symptoms, there is no way that mental health services could respond to this magnitude of need, or with services that are appealing or safe for Māori youth and their whānau/families [6]. Evidence suggests that Māori are less likely to get the healthcare they require, less likely to get referral to services, and less likely to have successful outcomes [11–13]. There is also research that suggests that contact for Māori with mental health services is more likely to occur through coercive processes such as admissions to hospital, compulsory treatment orders, child protective services, or through justice pathways [14].

Looking upstream to determine the contemporary drivers, including key risk and protective factors, is required to understand, and respond to, the rapid increase in mental health distress and Māori youth mental health inequities. Youth mental health research has tended to over-emphasise an individual’s risk contributions to mental health burden and overlook the ecologies that affect individuals that might contribute to this rapid population level increase in distress [15–17]. Indigenous models of wellbeing are holistic and inclusive, highlighting the emotional, spiritual, social, and cultural interconnectedness of people to their environments, lands, and waters [18–20]. Given the vital connections between these broader factors, we sought to take a social ecological approach that examines the reciprocal influence between physical and social environments on wellbeing [21]. Indigenous and non-Indigenous researchers have long encouraged researchers to consider the reciprocal influence of macro-social (i.e., society at large) aspects of life when undertaking and interpreting research data [18, 22, 23]. This was affirmed by the World Health Organization in 1986, who determined that a stable social ecosystem (i.e., a complex system where human societies and their interactions are intertwined with the natural environment) is a prerequisite for health [24]. Physical, sociocultural, and commercial ecosystems are central determinants in shaping general population mental health and levels of distress [21].

For adolescents, their ecosystems are significantly influenced by the adults and peers within their homes, community, geographic neighbourhood, school environments, and wider social and political systems [25]. Further, living at the intersection of Indigeneity and physical, sociocultural, and commercial environments, many of the circumstances that influence mental wellbeing are compounded for Indigenous youth [9, 26–28]. Yet the Western, dominant mode of intervention remains individualised and therapy-focused, despite recent research findings that Indigenous and Māori youth mental health can be improved by adopting more holistic approaches [23, 29–34].

While there are efforts of health providers to increase cultural tailoring and cultural appropriateness [35], there remains limited recognition of the ecological contexts of wellbeing, and a paucity of literature addressing pathways to promote wellbeing for Indigenous youth [35]. Academic literature recognises the multifaceted nature of wellbeing, especially for Indigenous populations [36], yet policy analysis shows similar recognition is lacking in structural policy and interventions [6]. Much like other areas of public health, applying an ecological approach may well hold the most promise as a way forward in addressing youth mental wellbeing, especially for marginalised groups [35]. Thus, recognising the dynamic and multifaceted landscape of promoting youth mental wellbeing, this paper adopts an ecological approach to understanding and addressing youth mental health risk and protective factors. Specifically, this study aims to (1) investigate the key risk and protective factors for Māori youth mental health, and (2) examine the equity context of factors that support mental wellbeing for Māori youth when compared to Pākehā youth.

Methods

Sample

This study utilised secondary data from the Youth’19 survey—a cross-sectional health and wellbeing survey of secondary school students in Aotearoa (see Fleming et al. [37] for the full survey methodology). Briefly, Youth’19 employed a two-stage cluster sampling design: half of all eligible “mainstream” (i.e., English-medium) schools in Aotearoa were randomly selected to participate, and within each consenting school, 30% of Year 9–13 students (approximately aged 12–18 years) were randomly selected to participate (or >30 students if the school had < 150 students). “Mainstream” schools were eligible for selection if they had >50 students in Years 9–13, and were located in the Tāmaki Makaurau/Auckland, Tai Tokerau/Northland, and Waikato regions of Aotearoa. Together, these regions contained 46% of Aotearoa’s secondary school population, and had sufficient representation of diverse ethnic groups, socioeconomic circumstances, and urban/rural geography [37]. In addition, six out of eight wharekura (Māori immersion schools) in these regions—and all of their Year 9–13 students—were invited to participate. In total, 7,721 students across 49 schools participated (7,374 students in 45 mainstream schools; 347 students in 4 wharekura). This represents a student response rate of 60% in mainstream schools, and 71% in wharekura. Ethical approval for Youth’19 was granted by the University of Auckland Human Subjects Ethics Committee (#022244). In accordance with the Youth’19 data access policy [38], data access for the present analyses was granted following review and approval of a data access proposal submitted to the Adolescent Health Research Group.

For this study, we selected students from the Youth’19 sample who identified as Māori (defined by those who selected “Māori”, either alone or in combination with other ethnic groups, in response to the question “Which ethnic group do you belong to? (You may choose as many as you need)”). This resulted in a Māori sample of 1,528 students (19.8% of the Youth’19 sample; comparable to 19.1% Māori in the general school population), and represents 6.1% of all Māori students in the Aotearoa secondary school population [37].

For equity analyses, we utilised “sole Pākehā” as the reference group. This comprised of Youth’19 participants who only reported belonging to ethnic groups within the “European” category (e.g., New Zealand European, Australian, British, Dutch, etc.; n = 3,056). This method creates mutually exclusive groups (as some participants reported both Māori and Pākehā ethnicities), and is well-suited for examining the effects of privilege, as it excludes participants who also identified with other minoritised ethnic groups, such as Pacific and/or Asian ethnicities [39, 40].

Measures

The Youth’19 survey was self-administered in schools using a multi-media computer-administered survey instrument (M-CASI) on mobile tablets. Survey text and optional voice-over were available in both English and te reo Māori (the Māori language). The survey consisted of 285 questions across a range of domains, including home, school, community, activities, and health and wellbeing [37]. For this study, we selected four available mental health measures as outcome variables: (1) wellbeing score, measured using the WHO-5 Wellbeing Index [41]; (2) depression score, measured using the Reynolds Adolescent Depression Scale—short form (RADS-SF) [42, 43]; (3) suicide thoughts, measured by the question “During the last 12 months have you seriously thought about killing yourself [attempting suicide]?” (0 = no, 1 = yes); and (4) suicide attempts, measured by the question “During the last 12 months have you tried to kill yourself [attempted suicide]?”; 0 = no, 1 = yes). All measures have been normed for Aotearoa populations and appear appropriate for Māori youth [6]. As reported in the Supplementary Material, multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) between Māori and Pākehā showed that the scales used had acceptable to good model fit, and supported measurement invariance through to the residual level (i.e., equal factor structure, item loadings, item intercepts, and item residuals). A range of risk and protective factors informed by the literature were selected as explanatory variables, and were grouped into five blocks: individual (e.g., age), contextual (e.g., geography), whānau (family; e.g., safe home), school (e.g., safe school), and community (e.g., safe neighbourhood). Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 [44]. With the exception of preliminary multilevel modelling, the survey package [45] was used to account for the complex sampling design, and inverse probability of selection (IPS) weights were applied to adjust for unequal probability of selection.

Descriptive statistics for the explanatory and outcome variables were calculated for the Māori sample as follows. For categorical variables, percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used, with the number of participants who responded to the individual item specified as the denominator. For scale variables, means and 95% CIs were used (note scale scores were only calculated for participants who responded to at least 80% of the scale). We also calculated analogous descriptive statistics for Pākehā participants to contextualise these results (equity analysis). For each variable, a chi-square test of independence was conducted to test for significant differences between the Māori and Pākehā samples.

Descriptive statistics showed that 32.5% of the Māori sample (n = 490) had missing data for at least one explanatory variable. We used multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE), under the assumption that the data were missing at random (MAR), to impute missing explanatory values for subsequent regression analyses. All explanatory and outcome variables were included in the imputation model. Imputation was restricted to Māori participants who had less than 40% missing data (n = 1,454, 96.4%). For explanatory variables measured using scales, individual items were imputed, and scale scores calculated after imputation. Using the mice package [46], we generated thirty imputed datasets, with a maximum of 20 iterations per imputation.

Regression analyses were conducted with the imputed datasets to examine the risk and protective factors associated with mental health outcomes for the Māori sample. Preliminary analyses using multilevel modelling (Level 2 = schools; Level 1 = students) with the lme4 package [47] indicated minimal clustering at the school level (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] < 3% for each outcome). Therefore, we shifted to single-level regression for subsequent analyses (linear regression for continuous outcomes; binary logistic regression for binary outcomes). For each outcome, we entered the explanatory variables as five sequential blocks (individual, contextual, whānau, school, and community). Using the survey and mice packages, the analyses were performed on each imputed dataset, then pooled together using Rubin’s rules [48]. Results with p <.05 were considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses showed that the results were largely consistent between the imputed and non-imputed datasets.

Results

Sample characteristics

Weighted descriptive statistics (see Table 2) show that the Māori sample were significantly younger on average, with 70.2% of participants under 16 years of age compared to 62.5% of Pākehā participants. Just over half of the Māori sample reported female sex at birth (55.1%), 15.5% reported being gender-diverse and/or sexuality-diverse (hereafter collectively referred to as “takatāpui” [LGBTQI+])—these proportions were similar to the Pākehā sample. The percentage of participants who reported a functional disability (29.2%), and the percentage who responded “yes” or “not sure” to the sexual abuse or coercion question (23.0%),1 were both significantly higher in the Māori sample than the Pākehā sample (25.9% and 16.5%, respectively). The vast majority of Māori participants attended “mainstream” schools (96.4%), while a small percentage attended wharekura (3.6%). By contrast, fewer than 5 Pākehā participants attended wharekura (< 0.1%). Overall, Māori experienced significantly higher levels of socioeconomic deprivation compared to Pākehā, with around 1 in 4 reporting that their parents had financial worries “often” or “all the time” (25.6%; 2.4x higher than Pākehā), around 1 in 7 reporting housing deprivation (17.7%; 3.4x higher), and more than 1 in 3 attending low socioeconomic decile schools (36.9%; 8.0x higher). Almost three-quarters of Māori participants lived in urban areas (72.6%), with the others living in towns (23.5%) or rural areas (3.9%). Compared to Pākehā participants, Māori participants were significantly less likely to live in urban areas, and significantly more likely to live in towns.

In terms of the whānau (family), school, and community ecosystems, around 90% of Māori participants reported that their whānau usually or always want to know who they are with and where they are (hereafter referred to as “whānau know company”; 90.1%). In addition, over 4 in 5 Māori participants reported feeling safe at home (88.6%) and at school (82.1%). Just over half of Māori participants reported feeling safe in their neighbourhood (54.7%)—also a significantly lower rate than Pākehā (61.3%). Māori and Pākehā participants reported similar rates of being bullied often (“about once a week or more”; around 8%). Around one-quarter (24.4%) of Māori participants reported racial discrimination within the past 12 months—a proportion more than twice as high (2.3x) as Pākehā. Whanaungatanga (Indigenous concept of relationships/connection) subscale scores are only reported for Māori (see Table 3). Whanaungatanga with friends had the highest mean, followed by whanaungatanga with whānau, with both means indicating that Māori participants tended to “agree” or “strongly agree” to the subscale items. Whanaungatanga with other adults had a lower mean, with typical responses ranging between “neutral” and “agree”.

In terms of mental health outcomes (see Table 2), around two-thirds of Māori participants (66.3%) scored above the cut-off for good wellbeing—a rate similar to Pākehā participants (67.8%). However, the percentage of Māori students who reported significant depressive symptoms (31.4%), suicide thoughts in the past year (26.6%), and suicide attempts in the past year (13.0%), were all significantly higher than Pākehā students (22.7%, 19.9%, and 3.6%, respectively). Ethnic differences in suicide attempts were particularly stark, with the prevalence for Māori being 3.6 times higher than for Pākehā.

Mental health risk and protective factors

Table 4 shows the associations between the explanatory variables and each mental health outcome for the Māori sample, according to the full regression models (i.e., controlling for all other explanatory variables; see Supplementary Tables S2-S5 for results from each step of the hierarchical regression analysis). In terms of individual characteristics, being female, having a functional disability, and reporting “yes” or “not sure” to sexual abuse, were significantly associated with poorer outcomes for all four measures of mental health (wellbeing, depression, suicide thoughts, and suicide attempts). Age was only significantly related to wellbeing—those who were aged 16 or over tended to have poorer wellbeing.

In terms of social ecosystem characteristics, both household deprivation measures (parental financial worries and housing precarity) were significant risk factors for depressive symptoms. For wellbeing, both measures were initially significantly related to poorer wellbeing, but only housing precarity remained a significant risk factor after addition of the whānau variables (see Supplementary Table S2). For suicide thoughts, only housing precarity was significant, and for suicide attempts, only parental financial worries were significant. By contrast, school-level socioeconomic status (SES) was inversely related to wellbeing and depressive symptoms—these outcomes were significantly poorer for participants attending mid and high SES schools compared to those attending low SES schools. School SES was not significantly associated with suicide thoughts or attempts. School type (“mainstream” vs. wharekura) and geography (urban vs. town vs. rural) were not significantly associated with any of the mental health outcomes.

For the whānau ecosystem, higher scores on whanaungatanga with whānau scale were a significant protective factor for all four mental health outcomes. In addition, feeling safe at home was significant protective factor for wellbeing and depressive symptoms (as well as for suicide thoughts before school variables were added to the model; see Supplementary Table S4). Participant reports of whānau knowing their company (i.e., parents know where you are and who you are with) was a significant protective factor for suicide attempts only.

For the school ecosystem, feeling safe at school was a significant protective factor for wellbeing, depressive symptoms, and suicide attempts, and approaching significance for suicide thoughts. Being bullied often at school was a significant risk factor for wellbeing, depressive symptoms, and suicide thoughts (but not suicide attempts). Whanaungatanga (Māori concept of connection) with friends scale was a significant protective factor for wellbeing only.

For the community ecosystem, results were similar for wellbeing and depressive symptoms: whanaungatanga with other adults scale and feeling safe in the neighbourhood were significant protective factors, whereas racial discrimination was a significant risk factor. Whanaungatanga with other adults scale and feeling safe in neighbourhoods were higher among those reporting suicide attempts. None of the community ecosystem variables were significant associated with suicide thoughts.

In terms of magnitude of effects for the statistically significant variables, the pattern tended to be similar for wellbeing, depressive symptoms, and suicide thoughts. Specifically, being female, having a functional disability, reports of sexual abuse or coercion (or being “not sure”), and being bullied often at school, tended to be among the strongest risk factors; whereas whanaungatanga with whānau and/or feeling safe at home, as well as feeling safe at school, tended to be important protective factors. A different pattern was observed for suicide attempts: sexual abuse was a significant risk factor and feeling safe at school was the most important protective factor, followed by whānau knowing where and who they were with.

Discussion

Mental health distress among adolescents has been rapidly increasing, and mental health inequities between Māori youth and Pākehā youth have been widening in Aotearoa [5, 6]. This study used an ecological approach to investigate the key contemporary risk and protective factors for Māori youth mental health, as well as understand the equity context of these factors for Māori youth when compared to Pākehā youth. Although there were some nuanced differences in the contributing factors for the mental health outcomes examined among Māori youth (wellbeing, depressive symptoms, suicide thoughts, suicide attempts), overall these results showed a broadly consistent picture. Due to these similarities and the importance of all four outcomes for holistic wellbeing, our discussion will focus on the key risk and protective factors for the overall mental health of Māori youth. These primarily centred on the importance of safety in its various forms across all the ecosystems examined. While the specific factors are discussed separately below, note these have a cumulative effect, with those experiencing more risk factors and fewer protective factors more susceptible to mental distress.

Beginning at the individual-level, this study found that, consistent with previous research, being female [49, 50], having a disability [51, 52], and a history of sexual abuse or coercion [53, 54] were significantly associated with poorer mental health on all four outcomes examined. These three factors also tended to have the biggest effects, highlighting their importance to adolescent mental health. It is well recognised that females report higher levels of mental health symptoms, but males are more likely to die by suicide [50, 55, 56]. This pattern is called the gender paradox [57], and may reflect gender norms, individualism, the stigma associated with help-seeking, and the use of more lethal suicide methods by males [50, 55–57]. International literature has also highlighted takatāpui (LGBTQIA+) identity and whaikaha/disabled youth was also related to lower wellbeing and greater mental health distress [58–60]. While these factors were operationalised as individual characteristics, together their associations with mental distress highlight the negative impact of environments, both directly in the case of exposure to sexual abuse/coercion, and via stigma, discrimination, and structural disadvantage for individuals with marginalised identities [61].

For contextual characteristics, the results showed that household-level socioeconomic disadvantage, particularly housing precarity, was a significant risk factor for poor mental health outcomes for Māori youth. The literature indicates that socioeconomic disadvantage is linked to poorer mental health via multiple mechanisms, including limited access to resources and increased exposure to stressors [61, 62]. Concerningly, our equity analysis showed that Māori youth were around 2.5 times more likely than Pākehā youth to report parental financial worries, and around 3.5 times more likely to report housing precarity. This highlights the importance of addressing socioeconomic inequities and its underlying causes in tackling mental health inequities. Conversely, higher school-level SES (i.e., Māori students in wealthier schools) was related to lower wellbeing and higher depressive symptoms. This may be a counter-intuitive result, however school SES is highly associated with school ethnic composition in Aotearoa [37, 63]. Given that Māori youth were more likely to attend lower SES schools, those attending lower SES schools may experience a stronger cultural connection and affirmation, whereas those attending higher SES schools may experience less inclusive or discriminatory environments [63]. It is also possible that those attending lower SES schools benefited from the availability of government funding for school-based health services in ways that supported their mental health [64, 65].

Within the ecosystems of whānau, school, and neighbourhood, our findings highlight the strong protective effect of whanaungatanga with whānau for all four mental health outcomes. Whanaungatanga is a core practice and value for Māori [31, 66]. As a uniquely Indigenous form of relationality, research has demonstrated its role in supporting wellbeing for Māori youth [32, 66, 67]. Whanaungatanga with friends and other adults were important but less powerful, primarily contributing to wellbeing, but not as a buffer against mental distress. This aligns with existing literature [67–69] on the pivotal role that whānau/family can play in mitigating risk factors, but only if whanau/family have adequate financial resources and support. One counter-intuitive result was the finding that whanaungatanga with other adults (connections with adults outside of family) did not provide a similarly protective effect for suicide attempts. Whanaungatanga with other adults may be protective in some cases, but for suicidal Māori youth this relationship cannot replace the importance of strong and close family/whānau connections. Whānau/family knowing where and who Māori youth were with, was a protective factor against suicide attempts and speaks to the role of whānau balancing the need for developmental independence and maintaining relationships during adolescence [70].

Other notable protective factors for overall mental health were related to feelings of safety in each of the whānau, school, and neighbourhood ecosystems. One contrasting finding was that Māori reporting a suicide attempt in the previous 12 months reported living in safe neighbourhoods as a risk factor. This may be similar to findings that higher socioeconomic schools were a risk factor, with Māori youth living in safer neighbourhoods (i.e., wealthier neighbourhoods) more visible and scrutinised, or perhaps Māori youth with suicidality care less about their safety so do not perceive their neighbourhoods as unsafe. The well-recognised risk factors of bullying victimisation and racial discrimination [71–73] were also mental health risk factors for Māori youth. Māori youth experienced inequities compared to Pākehā youth in almost all the safety-related measures examined.

Limitations

As with all research, some limitations need to be noted. First, this study was based on cross-sectional data, meaning the results indicate associations rather than causation. For example, while it is possible that whanaungatanga leads to better mental health, it is also possible that better mental health leads to increased whanaungatanga, or that a bidirectional relationship exists. Second, although the study utilised a relatively large Māori sample, the results may not be generalisable to all Māori youth as the participants were all secondary school or wharekura students. Māori youth not attending school were not represented and may have a different profile of mental health risk and protective factors. Third, the analyses did not stratify mainstream and wharekura students due to the small wharekura sample size. Even though school type was not found to be significantly related to the mental health outcomes examined, it is possible that given the different experiences of these students, the contributing factors to mental health may differ. There are important gender/sex differences that are important to explore for future research (e.g., Māori females experience higher mental health distress, yet Māori males more frequently die by suicide). Fourth, this study was unable to explore some variables that have previously been shown to be associated with mental health, such as cultural identity and parental mental health, so there may be other factors associated with mental health that are not captured in these analyses. Finally, to complement this quantitative study, future qualitative research would be valuable for providing deeper and more nuanced insights into the experiences of Māori youth in relation to ecological factors influencing mental health.

Implications

These findings emphasise the importance of intervening in all levels of the ecosystem to maximise protective factors and minimise harmful risk factors for the holistic wellbeing of Māori youth. Addressing mental health inequities for Māori youth will require culturally-led solutions that privilege the preferences, holistic needs, and priorities of Māori youth [20, 32]. Māori youth are suffering and need urgent support now. Whānau/family want to nurture the needs of their children in ways that matter to them and that are strengths-based [74, 75]. School-based approaches that provide evidence-based clinical, educational, and cultural strategies to keep Māori youth well are required [76, 77]. This may include access to cultural activities such as performing arts like kapa haka, and traditional sports like waka ama, that foster wellbeing from a holistic perspective [78–81]. It is also vital to ensure that health and social services are available, culturally safe, and equipped to respond to immediate need and provide ongoing support.

Despite the immediate need for services, we assert the fundamental causes of mental health inequities among Indigenous and minoritised populations are related to ongoing colonial harms that systematically disrupt access to quality healthcare, equitable educational outcomes, economic stability, housing security, and safe neighbourhoods via social exclusion and discriminatory practices [15–17, 82]. These conditions are an ongoing legacy that constrain access to sufficient resources, land, language, culture, and rights, and then blame individuals for their poor wellbeing [8, 83, 84]. Addressing colonialism and racism in the broader ecosystems (e.g., social attitudes and norms, mass media, and systemic structures, services and policies) is essential, as these impact mental health both directly, and indirectly [8, 18, 23, 84].

A population mental health approach that seeks to address the social determinants of wellbeing, as well as promote safe and inclusive ecosystems to prevent and minimise mental distress, is required to stem the tide of increasing mental health concerns among young people. For example, Te Mauri Report [85] found that school exclusion/suspension was a significant risk factor for suicide death among Māori youth, with Māori disproportionately excluded from schooling. School exclusion processes must be scrutinised more carefully for bias and ensure that young people who are distressed have sufficient resources to stay engaged in schooling. He Ara Oranga: the Government Inquiry into Mental Health and Addiction [86] called for a whole-of-government approach to wellbeing to address the issues that influenced mental health, including housing precarity, poverty prevention strategies, violence, and discrimination, among a range of recommendations. Fleming et al.’s framework [87] also provides a population mental health approach that prioritises Indigeneity, equity, multi-sectoral action, effective leadership, and human rights approaches while leveraging global evidence. These actions will require strong cross-party political support for mental health and addiction reform to make sustainable and meaningful changes to prioritising and adequately resourcing mental wellbeing for Māori youth.

Conclusion

This study utilised a holistic ecological approach to examine the risk and protective factors for mental health among Māori youth. Overall, the results showed the cumulative importance of safe and inclusive ecosystems for supporting mental health, alongside the protective effect of whanaungatanga with whānau. Strategies to address Māori mental health inequity will require supporting young people to access culturally and developmentally safe care, while working upstream using a population mental health prevention framework to create safer and more inclusive environments.
