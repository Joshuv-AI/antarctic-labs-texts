7Li NMR Chemical Shifts in Battery‐Relevant Electrolytes: Ligand‐Induced Shifts Cancel in Both Experiment and Calculation
Authors: Daniel Sethio, Philippe Bopp, Kersti Hermansson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
7Li nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments in the literature have shown that lowering the concentration from 1 M to 0.1 M of LiPF6 salt dissolved in a 50:50 mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) has only a minor effect on the Li chemical shift (≈0.1 ppm). This is unexpected as the number of Li+−PF6 − contact ion pairs is expected to be large in the 1 M solution but small in the more dilute solution. The aim of this study is to explore how the chemical shifts for the two solutions can differ so little despite the fact that the ion pair concentration differs markedly between the two solutions. To resolve this apparent paradox, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results from the literature are combined with density functional theory‐based chemical shift calculations. The calculated chemical shifts are in good agreement with the experimental values for both the 1 M and 0.1 M solutions, and so is the small chemical shift difference between them, which can be rationalized in terms of a cancellation of many contributions of opposite signs to the 7Li chemical shifts. The workflow considers effects of both short‐time and long‐time dynamical averaging.

Introduction

Concentration effects can decisively modify the properties of electrochemical systems, e.g., batteries,[ 1 ] which adds opportunities for the active branch of electrochemistry devoted to finding efficient and sustainable electrolytes for liquid‐based energy storage devices.[ 2 ] A wealth of experimental spectroscopic, scattering, and diffraction techniques is being used to characterize such liquids. Experimental characterization is often complemented by physics‐based and data‐driven computational methods to assist in the interpretation of the experimental signals. Moreover, stand‐alone computer simulations can serve as virtual experiments to harvest static and dynamic knowledge that is difficult to gather from experiments, and thereby serve as predictive tools.

This study deals with the analysis of 7Li NMR chemical shifts in a battery‐related electrolyte at two different salt concentrations, and to what extent it is possible to interpret, or relate, the chemical shift to the local structures around the Li+ ions. Overall, NMR spectroscopy is a powerful tool for characterizing average local coordination structures in electrolytes.[ 3 , 4 ] The main objective of our study is to provide insight regarding the surprisingly small difference in the 7Li chemical shifts observed experimentally[ 5 ] between 0.1 and 1 M solutions of lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) salt dissolved in a solvent composed of 50:50 (vol%) of ethylene carbonate (EC, Figure 1 ) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC, Figure 1). The 1 M solution is very similar in constitution to the well‐known LP30 formulation, which is nowadays much used in battery chemistry and battery devices.

The two solvent molecules that, together with the Li+ and PF6 − ions, are in focus in this study.

In particular, we want to rationalize why the 7Li chemical shift appears to hardly be affected by the ion‐pairing which should be present in the more concentrated solution but not in the dilute solution.

The background is as follows. In a dilute solution, like in the 0.1 M solution studied here, contact ion pairs are expected to be scarce, but not so in the 1 M solution. Indeed, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations by Andersson et al.[ 6 ] performed with a well‐tested intermolecular force‐field for the LP30 system (i.e., 1 M LiPF6 in the 50:50 EC:DMC mixture) showed that, on average, circa 25% of the Li+ ions have at least one PF6 − anion in their first coordination shell. In the experimental study by Wahyudi et al.,[ 5 ] the 7Li NMR chemical shift was measured for the two solutions mentioned above. They reported a very small difference in the 7Li chemical shifts between the two concentrations, only 0.08 ppm (see the supplementary material of ref. [5]). We found it intriguing that the Li chemical shift should be so little affected by the dilution even though the ion:solvent molecular ratio differs significantly between the two concentrations, and as we will show in this study, changing an EC ligand for a PF6 − ion in the first solvation shell of Li will affect the 7Li chemical shift by about 1 ppm.

We investigate this apparent inconsistency by means of computational techniques; we will generate theoretical spectral signals to be compared with experiment and subsequently analyze the various structural contributions to the net spectral signal. It is important to bear in mind that the experimental NMR time scale is slow: it lies in the range between microseconds and seconds for different nuclei,[ 7 ] which is much slower than the reported average exchange times for EC and DMC molecules in the first‐coordination‐shell around Li+; both were reported in ref. [6] to be less than 200 ps. The NMR experiment will thus represent the (Boltzmann‐weighted) average over all Li+ ion coordination structures appearing within “the NMR timeframe” and will reflect a range of different coordination structures, including, in the 1 M solution, presumably both entirely EC and DMC solvated Li+ ions and cation‐anion contact pairs, if present.

One possible computational approach (which we have not used here) would be to perform molecular dynamics simulation for the targeted liquids, select many configurations (“snapshots”) from the MD trajectory, and perform quantum‐mechanical (QM) calculations of the 7Li chemical shift for those. A representative selection of snapshots would yield a probability distribution of chemical shifts for the modelled electrolyte. Reasonably converged distributions should be possible to obtain if a considerable number of snapshot calculations are performed; this would be required to sample also the less frequent first‐shell coordination environments sufficiently well.

An approach akin to this was recently published in ref. [8] for a battery‐related electrolyte, namely, the lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide salt (Li+FSI−) dissolved in 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME). In that publication, after very long MD simulations with an OLPS‐AA‐based force‐field, the last snapshot of the trajectory was selected for chemical shift calculations. For this snapshot, clusters consisting of each of the two hundred Li+ ions and their nearest surroundings were extracted from the MD box and subjected to 7Li chemical shift calculations. In those calculations, the distorted geometries from the MD snapshot were kept (i.e., no geometry optimization was made).

We have adopted an alternative approach. We make use of the fact that Andersson et al.[ 6 ] have already analyzed the occurrences of the different first‐shell compositions and topologies around Li+ during their ≈100 nanosecond long MD simulation for the 1 M solution. We do not perform any MD simulations ourselves in this article, but we make use of the results of Andersson et al. By accounting for all their different topologies and associated relative probabilities resulting from solvent exchange we take into account medium‐slow dynamical processes in our calculation. Furthermore, we averaged over the fast vibrational motions by optimizing the geometry for each topology, using polarizable continuum model (PCM‐)embedded clusters, as described in the Methods Section. Thus, we minimized the potential energy for each of the published topology categories in ref. [6] under the assumption that an average structure at 300 K should be reasonably close to the optimized structure (at 0 K). To construct the 7Li NMR signature, we performed QM chemical shift calculations for each of these clusters and the average signal was constructed based on the MD‐simulated probabilities. Details concerning the 0.1 M solution are given later in the article. Our calculated chemical shift values for the two solutions are compared with the experimental results. We find that the agreement between experiment and calculation is good, both for each solution and for the (small) difference between them.

In addition to the PCM‐embedded cluster calculations for the ionic solutions, we performed chemical shift calculations for tailored Li+(EC)i (DMC)j (PF6 −)k clusters, where i, j, and k are small integers. The purpose here was to examine trends as a function of systematic variations of the Li+ coordination shell to assist in our analysis of the different contributions to the chemical shift in the solutions. Our comment above about the sizable chemical shift increase occurring when an EC solvent molecule is exchanged by a PF6 − ion stems from our tailored systematic trend calculations (which are presented in Table 1 ). In the literature, there exist several QM cluster studies of the Li+ ion coordinated by small organic molecules (including EC and DMC), with and without an explicit anion present, and with or without an implicit solvent environment (see, e.g., ref. [9]).

Methods

While the QM calculations in this work are standard, it is the workflow itself for the solution studies (Figure 3) that may be somewhat novel. We combine statistically converged topological information from the MD study of Andersson et al.[ 6 ] with postprocessing involving QM calculations for structural relaxation and determination of the 7Li NMR chemical shifts, δ7Li. More specifically, we use the representative topologies generated in ref. [6] for the 1 M solution. These MD solvation structures were then energy‐minimized with respect to the total cluster energy (PCM‐embedded), and the NMR shifts were subsequently calculated for all optimized aggregates and averaged using the probabilities at 300 K given in the MD paper. The sample for 0.1 M was obtained by removing aggregates involving contact ion‐pairs.

We thus calculate the chemical shift as <shift (MD structures)> in two steps: first as averages over all the fast dynamics, approximated by the optimized clusters, and then by explicitly summing over slow processes, e.g., ligand exchange and anion exchange around Li+, by taking the occurrences of the different topologies from ref. [6] into account. The fast dynamics would include temperature‐induced geometrical distortions of the coordination configuration and intramolecular vibrations. We assume that these fast processes will occur around the potential energy minimum, so that their average can be replaced by the 0 K structure. The slower solvent and ion exchange dynamics are represented by the relative occurrences of different Li+ environments published by Andersson et al.

In addition to these clusters, tailored ion‐solvent clusters were constructed in a systematic way to examine trends chemical shift trends, as mentioned in the Introduction. In the Results and Discussion section, we present these clusters first (Figure 2 ).

The Systems

The systems subjected to QM calculations all consist of a Li+ ion embedded in different EC and DMC surroundings, with and without PF6 − counter‐ions and with and without the PCM embedding. Since our solution calculations make use of topologies identified by Andersson et al.[ 6 ] from their room‐temperature MD simulation, some information about their work is appropriate here. Their MD simulation was performed for a solution of 1 M LiPF6 in an EC:DMC solvent mixture of 50:50 by weight, i.e., they examined the well‐known LP30 battery electrolyte system. Simulations at constant number of particles, volume and temperature of a few hundred nanoseconds were performed with a modified version of the APPLE&P force‐field of Borodin et al.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ] Spectroscopic properties were not addressed in ref. [6].

For the 1 M solution, we made use of the MD trajectory analyses performed by Andersson et al. Coordination structures around the Li+ ions were extracted by them at selected intervals using the CHAMPION[ 13 ] method, which classifies the compositions and topologies of the dynamic coordination shells around ions in solution. Representative images of the 20 most abundant species for the 1 M solution are shown in Figure 3a. Our starting structures were built from the topologies presented in ref. [6] (and Figure 3a); for this, the GaussView software[ 14 ] was used. When subjected to subsequent QM optimization by us the coordination topologies were preserved and the structures remained similar to those shown in Figure 3a.

For the 0.1 M solution, we selected those topologies that do not contain a PF6 − ion in the first shell of Li+. We used the following reasoning. In a 0.1 solution of LiPF6 in EM:EMC, the presence of cation–anion pairs should be rare. Various estimates indicate that the percentage of contact ion pairs between Li+ and PF6 − in the 0.1M solution should be around 5%. Since no new MD simulations were performed, we cannot assess independently how frequently ion pairs occur at low concentration; our approximation is that they can be neglected. Furthermore, we make the approximation that the relative probabilities of occurrence of the selected configurations are the same for the 0.1 and 1 M solutions. We rationalize this approximation in the following way. For a given coordination of solutes around an ion, the interaction energy can be thought to be the sum of interactions within the first shell plus interactions between first shell particles and the rest of the solution. The second terms are much smaller and our approximation amounts to neglecting them.

Electronic Structure Calculations

We performed electronic structure calculations using the Gaussian 16 Rev. C.01 program[ 15 ] at the B3LYP[ 16 , 17 ]/aug‐cc‐pVTZ[ 18 , 19 ] level of theory and with the semiempirical Grimme's D3 dispersion correction[ 20 ] invoked. The implicit solvation model of Tomasi et al.,[ 21 ] called “PCM” for polarizable continuum model, was used to account for EC:DMC mixture solvation effects. A dielectric constant (ε) of 20.4 was used to represent the EC:DMC mixture solvation effects, as suggested by Borodin et al.[ 22 ]

All structures presented were fully geometry‐optimized, as well as PCM‐embedded, both the clusters constructed for the systematic exploration in Figure 2 and Table 1 and the clusters representing the MD structures in Figure 3 (Table S1, Supporting Information, presents nonembedded clusters for comparison).

Chemical Shift Calculations

The NMR chemical shift calculations were performed for our optimized PCM‐embedded clusters at the same level of theory as the energy minimizations, utilizing the Gauge‐Independent Atomic Orbital (GIAO)[ 23 , 24 , 25 ] method on optimized structures. The isotropic NMR chemical shift, δiso, is obtained from the trace of the isotropic chemical shielding tensor σ iso. The isotropic chemical shift δiso sample is subsequently obtained from ‘δiso sample = σiso ref − σiso sample', where σiso ref refers to the selected reference compound. We will skip the “iso” superscript from here on. Note that in agreement with common standards, moving along the chemical shift axis from right to left in our figures means moving toward larger (more positive) chemical shifts, which is equivalent to moving toward a lower shielding value σiso, i.e., toward a more de‐shielded Li species.

The reference system (σiso ref) in our calculations was the energy‐optimized [Li (H2O)4 ]+ + PCM(ε = 78.36) system. This represents a dilute aqueous solution of a Li salt, chosen because the experimental NMR study in ref. [5] used a 0.1 M LiCl(aq) solution for their δiso ref value. We used a 4‐coordinated aqueous Li+ cluster for the reference calculations since for dilute solutions there appears to be a consensus that the coordination number is 4 (cf. the reviews by Smirnov[ 26 ] and Persson[ 27 ]). For the tailored Li+(EC)i (DMC)j (PF6 −)k + PCM clusters (Table 1 and Figure 2) we used the same aqueous solution reference as above, but for the tailored clusters without PCM (Table S1, Supporting Information), we used the optimized [Li (H2O)4 ]+ cluster without the PCM.

Experimental Chemical Shift Data from the Literature

Comparisons are made with the NMR experiments of Wahyudi et al.[ 5 ] (see the Suppl. information in their article: Figure S16, Supporting Information) who measured 7Li chemical shifts at room temperature on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz liquid NMR spectrometer for a 1 M and a 0.1 M solution of the LiPF6 salt in a solvent mixture of EC and DMC. Their EC:DMC solvent mixture ratio was 50:50 by volume, which translates into an EC:DMC ratio of 45:55 by weight, i.e., their 1 M solution was close to the composition of the LP30 system (1 M LiPF6 in 50:50 % EC:DMC by volume) that Andersson et al. used. The experimental reference system for all the 7Li chemical shift measurement in ref. [5] was a 0.1 M aqueous solution of LiCl [Wandi Wahyudi, private communication; the published work mentions “a dilute solution”].

Results and Discussion

We will first discuss systematic trends found for the tailored clusters (Table 1 and Figure 2) and then proceed to the solutions (Table 2 and Figure 3).

The Li ion is believed to be predominantly 4‐coordinated in EC and DMC solutions in the concentration ranges discussed here;[ 26 , 28 ] this is also confirmed by the MD simulations of Andersson et al.[ 6 ] The effect on δ7Li of successively increasing the number of EC ligands around Li+ is illustrated in Figure 2a and listed on the first six rows in Table 1. This exercise provides insight regarding the magnitudes of the perturbation of the Li chemical shift. Li deshielding takes place as the number of EC ligands increases, which can be attributed to increased electron transfer away from the electropositive Li species. The deshielding effect becomes very substantial at full coordination. A similar trend, and also similar magnitudes, were observed by Alam et al.[ 29 ] for optimized PCM‐embedded hydrated Li+ clusters.

The results in Figure 2a–d and Table 1 pertain to PCM‐embedded clusters, however, the results for the naked gas‐phase clusters are found to be similar (Table S1 and Figure S1, Supporting Information). In most cases, the PCM‐correction increases the shielding and thus makes the δ7Li value more negative (or equivalently: less positive). The correction is particularly large for clusters with a not fully coordinated Li+ ion, presumably due to their higher electric dipole moments, while the correction is modest for the fully coordinated ion. We will only discuss the PCM‐embedded cluster systems calculations for the rest of this article.

We next generated a Li+ cluster with 5 first‐shell EC molecules, starting the optimization from a trigonal bipyramid. In another test, 6 EC ligands were placed on an octahedron. Both attempts led to a 4‐coordinated ion, i.e., the fifth and sixth EC molecules moved out to the second coordination shell, consistent with 4 being the preferred coordination number.

Next, starting from the fully coordinated and optimized [Li(EC)4]+ complex, the composition of the first solvation shell was varied with respect to the EC:DMC ratio and the presence of the PF6 − anion. While exchanging one or several EC molecule(s) for DMC was found to affect the chemical shift by only one or a few tenths of a ppm (Figure 2b, Table 1), replacing an EC molecule with a PF6 − ion yields a shift that is 5 to 10 times larger (Figure 2c, Table 1). The direction is toward more shielding of Li, as expected when a negative ion enters the coordination sphere. If the anion resides in the second coordination shell, its impact is small, as seen from a comparison of the rows for [Li (EC)4+1]+ and Li(EC)4 (PF6).

Next we make some comments on the effects of the cluster geometries on the chemical shift, using the [Li(EC)2(DMC)2]+ cluster as our probe system. Five conformations were constructed to represent a spread of coordination geometries, spanning (the considerable) energy range of about 4 kT300K (Figure 2d and Table S2, Supporting Information). The chemical shifts for these five were calculated in the same way as for all other cases in this study, and are seen to vary over a range of 0.7 ppm. However, such short‐term dynamical fluctuations would be averaged out in the experimental signal as would, in fact, also the slower effects of compositional exchanges exemplified in Figure 2b,c, which leads us to discussing fast and slow dynamical effects in more detail.

As mentioned, experimental NMR chemical shift measurements yield a signal corresponding to a long‐time average, and also the ligand exchange mechanism in LP30 is fast in comparison with the NMR time scale. Consequently, a QM prediction of the chemical shift would need to consider a range of coordination species and their relative probabilities (at a given temperature) to achieve a weighted average to compare with experiment. This is where statistical simulation results are needed, as described in the following, where we move to the solution systems.

We start by addressing the computed 7Li NMR shifts corresponding to the 1 M LiPF 6 solution, and follow the workflow reported in Figure 3. Representative snapshots for all appreciably abundant Li‐ligand arrangements (topologies) identified in ref. [6] are illustrated by the nearest‐neighbor clusters in Figure 3a, reproduced from Figure 5 in ref. [6]. From these, we constructed the ion‐solvent clusters and optimized them at our selected QM level, under the assumption that the optimized structure should be a reasonable approximation to the average structure around which the structures fluctuate (fast dynamics) at 300 K. The chemical shift was calculated for each of the 20 optimized PCM‐embedded clusters, and the result is shown in Figure 3b (and given in Table S3, Supporting Information). To account for the slower dynamics, each NMR contribution was then weighted according to the coordination cluster's occurrence in the MD simulation (Figure 3c), and the average signal was constructed (Figure 3d). The relative occurrences of the different topologies are given as a percentage for each image included (in small text) in Figure 3a. The (intuitive) formulas behind the calculations of the net chemical shift are provided in the Suppl. Material.

Already based on the results from the tailored clusters in Figure 2, one would expect the 20 representative structures in Figure 3a to span quite a broad ppm range, and this is indeed the case: the range is 1.79 ppm. Even so, the net δ7Li value comes out very close to the experimental value: −0.49 versus −0.42 ppm (Figure 3d,e, Table 2). It should be noted (again) that in both experiment and calculation, the reference state for the chemical shift is a dilute Li+ salt solution in water (see the Methods Section).

For the 0.1 M LiPF 6 solution, contact ion pairs will be scarce. Here, we proceeded in a way similar to that used for the 1 M solution except that we only considered coordination shells that do not contain cation‐anion contact pairs. 11 out of the 20 coordination arrangements from Figure 3a fulfill this criterion, and their NMR shifts were weighed with the same relative weights as they had in the concentrated solution (see motivation in the Methods Section). For the 0.1 M solution, the calculated value is again in agreement with the experimental chemical shifts: −0.35 versus −0.34 ppm.

In summary, as displayed in the data columns under the heading Δ(δ7Li)conc−dilute in Table 2, the net change in the chemical shift value when going from 0.1 to 1 M LiPF6 in the EC:DMC solvent ends up being very small in both experiment and calculation, and moreover has the same sign. These two aspects, plus the fact that the absolute chemical shift values themselves, given in the “δ7Li” columns, are also very close, underpin the conclusion that experiment and calculation are in good agreement in the present study. This is an interesting result as it suggests that our computational approach works well. It also provides insight into “how” a salt concentration increase from very dilute to 1 M can result in such a small change of the chemical shift, which was the question we set out to examine in the Introduction of this report. The answer is that the weak concentration dependence emerges from the partial cancellation between chemical shift contributions from many different Li‐ligand topologies, some involving upshifts, others downshifts.

Although the two most frequent clusters are the same in our calculations at the two concentrations, one should note that as many as 27% of the snapshots for the 1 M solution contain PF6 − in the 1st Li shell, and the anion presence has a considerable influence on the Li chemical shift (as can be seen, e.g., in Table 1). None of the PF6‐containing clusters are included in the 0.1 M solution. Overall it seems neither intuitive nor easy to conjecture, that the δ7Li values for the 1.0 and 0.1 M will be very similar. But the net result of our analysis shows exactly that.

Concluding Remarks

Using computational methods, we generated the 7Li NMR chemical shift values (δ7Li) for model solutions of LiPF6 salt in a 1:1 solvent mixture of EC:DMC at 1 M and 0.1 M salt concentration. This electrolyte is called LP30, and the electrolyte itself and variants of it have been dominant in commercial Li‐ion batteries since several decades. We studied the following question: “Why is the experimental Li chemical shift so insensitive (<0.10 ppm) to a considerable change of the salt concentration, while cation–anion contact ion pairs ought to have a large influence on this value?” This question is not only fundamental in nature but also of practical importance as NMR spectroscopy is one of the most widely used characterization method in the materials sciences, and a characterization method's merits rely on the possibility to give a correct interpretation of the measured signals. The expectation of a large anion effect emerges from the notion that a negative neighbor ought to increase the shielding of the Li ion and was also confirmed by the results from the tailored clusters in Figure 1c.

Our computational strategy and the calculated chemical shifts were validated by a successful comparison with the corresponding experimental chemical shifts from ref. [5]. Experiment and calculation both yield only a small chemical shift difference between the two concentrations. Furthermore, from the calculations we have to the contributions that make up the net signal, and the small concentration dependence can now be rationalized: the modest concentration dependence is the result of the partial cancellation of many sizable δ7Li contributions from the various Li+ clusters, where some contribute more shielding of the Li nucleus, others less shielding.

In the course of this study, we also performed systematic variations of the nearest‐neighbor environment around the Li+ ion and monitored the effect on the calculated chemical shift. It was found that the chemical shift of the Li+ ion is only slightly affected by changes in the EC‐DMC compositions, whereas the effect of explicit anion participation in the first coordination shell is more significant.

Figure 4 shows our attempt to relate the sign and size of the chemical shift of the Li‐ligand clusters to their compositions, an exercise which turns out to be quite successful. The figure collects the shifts for all 4‐coordinated clusters from Figure 3. The presence of counterions in the solvation shell is seen to lower δ7Li. Increasing the number of EC neighbors at the expense of DMC has, to a lesser extent, the same effect. However, this will hardly be experimentally detectable.

We also want to emphasize the merits of optimizing the local structures of the selected snapshots as the instantaneous distortions may otherwise dominate the picture. There are other techniques to filter out the effects of rapid motion from MD simulation trajectories (see, e.g., references by Oxtoby et al.[ 30 , 31 ] for the fast dynamics in the case of vibrational spectra) but the combination of cluster structure optimization and cluster population occurrences appears to be efficient in the current NMR study.
