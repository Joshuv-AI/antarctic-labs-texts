Skeletal and cardiac muscle longitudinal associations in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA)
Authors: Jie Jun Wong, Fei Gao, Edward G. Lakatta, Eleanor Simonsick, Luigi Ferrucci, Ru-San Tan, Majd AlGhatrif, Angela S. Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia, the age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, has been linked to adverse health outcomes. Cross-sectional associations have been observed between skeletal and cardiac muscle mass and function in healthy, community-dwelling older adults during normative aging. Further longitudinal studies on the skeletal muscle-cardiac axis during aging are needed to inform the temporal patterns and impact of these associations.MethodsWe analyzed data from participants from the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging. Individual longitudinal rates of change (random slopes) of echocardiography-derived cardiac function, appendicular lean mass (ALM), and maximal handgrip strength (HGS) were estimated with linear mixed-effects models. The associations between baseline measurements and rates of change (Change) were examined using Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).ResultsAmong 1025 participants (66.6 ± 13.1 years, 47.8% male), the baseline mean HGS was 33.1 ± 11.0 kg, and ALM was 20.8 ± 5.3 kg; the mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 67.5 ± 9.7%, and LV mass (LVM) was 145.6 ± 50.4 g. With aging, HGS decreased by 0.36 kg/year (95% CI: -0.41, -0.31), ALM decreased by 25.1 g/year (95% CI: -34.7, -15.5), and LVM decreased by 0.730 g/year (95% CI: -0.998, -0.460). Both ALMChange and HGSChange were inversely correlated with advancing age ([r = -0.159 p < 0.001] and [r = -0.172 p < 0.001], respectively), while LVMChange was not (p = 0.178). Adjusting for baseline, ALMChange was significantly associated with LVMChange independently of age, sex, and LVM (β = 0.287, p < 0.001, adj. R2 = 0.663); the association persisted after adjustment for pulse pressure, mean arterial pressure, and body mass index. The interaction term sex*baseline-adjusted ALMChange was statistically significant (p < 0.050). ALMChange was also correlated with LVEFChange (r = 0.102, p = 0.001), while HGSChange was not significantly correlated with LVMChange (p = 0.642).ConclusionsAmong community-dwelling normative aging adults, age-associated decline in skeletal muscle mass correlated with reductions in LVM and function, independently of age, sex, and hemodynamic loading conditions. Our findings suggest a skeletal muscle-cardiac axis characterized by parallel declines beginning in early aging, preceding cardiovascular disease. Further studies exploring cardiac and skeletal muscle aging-related declines may direct interventions to halt these adverse processes concurrently.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04829-5.

Background

Sarcopenia is the age-related syndrome characterized by progressive, widespread loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, leading to reduced physiological reserves and shortened life expectancy [1]. Sarcopenia is estimated to affect one in eight persons in the general population, but its prevalence is nearly tripled (estimated 35%) among individuals with cardiovascular disease (CVD) on pooled estimates [1]. The prevalence of CVD also increases sharply with age, from approximately 78% at ages 60–79 years to 90% at ages above 80 years. The global CVD burden continues to rise rapidly in tandem with population aging [2].

Skeletal muscle atrophy begins in the third to fourth decade of life and hastens in the seventh to eighth decade. In disease states, such as heart failure, muscle mass loss rapidly accelerates [3]. However, age-associated declines in skeletal muscle and cardiac remodeling may occur in parallel during normative aging [2, 4], often preceding the onset of overt clinical disease. While the simultaneous occurrence of sarcopenia and heart failure is a recognized late-stage syndrome linked to poor clinical outcomes [4–6], there is less data available regarding the early, preclinical interactions between these two muscle beds. Given the morbidity surrounding heart failure, deepening our understanding of the subclinical associations between cardiac and skeletal muscle changes would be clinically impactful and may guide future interventions to disrupt these processes before they progress into symptomatic cycles.

Recent studies have reported cross-sectional associations between skeletal and cardiac muscle masses in older adults [4, 7, 8]. First described as a ‘cardio-sarcopenia’ syndrome in community older adults without overt heart failure [4], smaller skeletal muscle mass and function (i.e., sarcopenia) were associated with smaller left ventricular mass (‘sarcopenia of cardiac muscle’). This finding was also reported in other cohorts, including community-dwelling and frail older adults with mild CVD [8, 9]. Although age is a common determinant of both processes, the relationships between skeletal and left ventricular mass were independent of chronological age. Furthermore, the associations between lean body mass and cardiac structure and function appear to be sex-specific [10]. These cross-sectional, observational findings advanced the hypothesis that concurrent age-associated processes of accelerated aging may drive parallel declines in both cardiac and skeletal muscle tissues, not explained by chronological age; such processes will manifest by an association in the longitudinal changes of these parameters, independently of the chronological age; i.e., in person with similar age, those who had greater loss in skeletal muscle mass and function compared to the average, also experienced a similar greater loss in cardiac muscle mass and function.

While the link between cardio-sarcopenia is recognized, existing evidence is largely derived from cross-sectional observations or clinical populations with late-stage disease [2, 11]. A knowledge gap remains concerning the temporal evolution of this relationship in healthy adults experiencing normative aging. It is not yet established whether individual rates of skeletal muscle decline parallel the trajectory of cardiac remodeling within the same person over time. To explore this, we leveraged the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA) [12], a decades-long investigation designed to capture the normative aging process, to move beyond static associations toward longitudinal assessment of concurrent aging trajectories.

We hypothesized that the longitudinal rates of decline in skeletal and cardiac muscle are correlated, such that accelerated loss of skeletal muscle mass and function predicts a steeper decline in cardiac muscle mass and function, independent of the effects of chronological age. We utilize individual longitudinal trajectories (rates of change) of muscle and cardiac mass over time to capture the dynamic nature of tissue decline across the lifespan. Using community-based longitudinal data from the BLSA cohort, we examine bidirectional relationships and adaptations in the skeletal and cardiac systems with advancing age.

Methods

Study population

We studied a subgroup of the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA), a longitudinal cohort study of normative human aging conducted in community-dwelling men and women since 1958 by the Intramural Research Program of the National Institutes of Health, National Institute on Aging [13]. The BLSA study has an ethnically diverse cohort comprising participants who self-identify as Black, White, Asian, or others. Community-dwelling participants, ranging in age from their 20 s to 90 s and without diabetes mellitus, stroke, kidney disease, liver disease, or heart disease at the beginning of the study, were recruited from the Baltimore-Washington, DC area [14]. In the present study, we utilized longitudinal data collected from January 2005 through December 2019. While the BLSA initially recruits healthy volunteers without major chronic diseases, participants are retained as they develop age-related comorbidities to ensure the cohort remains representative of the natural aging process. By including participants who remained in the study despite the onset of these conditions, we aim to capture the normative trajectory of aging in a community-dwelling population. However, to isolate aging-related changes from the confounding effects of severe cardiac pathology, we applied strict exclusion criteria for overt structural heart disease, including significant arrhythmias, significant valvular disease, or a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) below 50%.

For this analysis, we specifically selected participants who had undergone at least 2 repeated concurrent measurements of comprehensive echocardiography, a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan, and handgrip strength (HGS) during their clinic visits between January 2005 and December 2019 [15]. Variables used for the analysis presented in this manuscript were collected during a two-/three-day clinical visit at the National Institute on Aging Clinical Research Unit. Participants underwent extensive evaluations at regular, predefined intervals based on age to account for the faster functional decline in later life (every 4 years for those < 60 years; every 2 years for those 60–70 years; and annually for those 80 years and older) [15]. A final sample of 490 men and 535 women was used for this analysis. All participants provided informed consent, and the Internal Review Board of the Intramural Research Program of the National Institutes of Health approved the study protocol.

Data acquisition

Clinical variables, Anthropometry, and Hemodynamics

Blood pressure was measured in the morning following a light breakfast [16]. Participants were seated for a 5-min quiet resting period prior to measurement. Readings were obtained from both arms using a mercury sphygmomanometer with appropriately sized cuffs. The average values of the second and third measurements from both the right and left arms were used. Pulse pressure was calculated as the difference between systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference, were obtained for all participants. Body mass index (BMI) was derived using the formula kg/m2. Waist circumference was measured at the narrowest point of the abdomen, located between the lower rib cage and the iliac crests [17].

Smoking status was determined using a structured questionnaire, categorizing participants as current smokers, recent quitters (less than 10 years), or long-term quitters (more than 10 years) [16]. Cardiovascular comorbidities were identified through a rigorous combination of self-reported medical history, medication review, and objective physiological data. Diabetes mellitus was defined by a physician’s diagnosis, current use of glucose-lowering agents, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥ 7.0 mmol/L), or a 2-h glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥ 11.1 mmol/L) during an oral glucose tolerance test [18]. Hypertension was defined as the use of antihypertensive therapy or a mean resting blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg [17]. Hyperlipidemia was identified based on current lipid-lowering medication use or a total serum cholesterol level ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥ 5.18 mmol/L) [17].

Skeletal muscle variables

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-derived total body and lower extremities total lean mass, appendicular lean mass (ALM), and total fat mass were measured using the Lunar Prodigy scanner (General Electric, Madison, WI) and analyzed with Encore Software [15]. The scanner was calibrated daily before testing, and one investigator analyzed all scans. For scans conducted before the newer Lunar machine in 2003, an algorithm was used to standardize measures from older instruments to ensure comparability with newer instruments. DEXA measures are complemented with computerized tomography (CT) cross-sectional images (10 mm) at the mid-thigh area (10 mm, Somatom Sensation 10, Siemens, Malvern, PA), quantified using the Geanie 2.1 software (BonAlyse Oy, Jyvaskla, Finland) and the Tibia Estimation Tool (TibEsT v.1.4, Makrogiannis, NIH).

HGS was measured as the maximum force in kilograms using the Jamar Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer. Participants were seated and tested with their arms extended at 180° (shoulder height) for three trials with each hand, unless the participant had surgery on the affected hand within the past 3 months. Participants were instructed to squeeze as hard as possible for each measurement, and the best of 6 trials was used in analyses.

Cardiac variables

All transthoracic echocardiograms were performed at rest, following a standardized protocol, using the same echocardiographic instrument (HP Sonos-5500; Philips, Andover, Massachusetts), with a single diagnostic sonographer and interpreted by two experienced echocardiographers. LV linear dimensions were measured from the parasternal long-axis view according to guidelines, including LV internal diameters in systole (LVIDs) and diastole (LVIDd), and posterior wall diameter (LVPWd) [19]. These measurements were used to derive LV end-systolic volume (LVESV), LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), stroke volume (SV), LV mass (LVM), and LV ejection fraction (LVEF) using M-mode measurements as previously described [14, 19]. Diastolic function was evaluated through conventional Doppler tracings of the mitral valve obtained in the apical four-chamber view. Trans-mitral flows were recorded at the level of the leaflet tips. Isovolumetric relaxation time (IVRT) was measured using continuous wave Doppler as the interval between the aortic valve closure and the start of trans-mitral flow [20]. Peak early diastolic velocities (e’) were acquired at the septal and lateral mitral annulus via tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) and averaged according to clinical guidelines [14, 21].

Statistical analysis

Baseline clinical characteristics are presented as means (standard deviation) for continuous data and frequency (percentages) for categorical data. Bivariate linear associations between cardiac structure and function obtained from echocardiography, body composition, and HGS were assessed using Pearson product-moment correlation (r) and presented in scatter plots. Multilevel mixed-effects generalized linear models are used to estimate rates of change per year. Separate models were fitted for each index variable regressed against time (years since baseline visit) with subject as a random effect.

The primary analysis focused on the longitudinal relationship between skeletal (ALM) and cardiac (LVM) structural mass. Multiple linear regression was used to analyze the baseline and longitudinal associations of baseline-adjusted LVMChange, with standardized Beta coefficients (Models 1–9) (Table 3 and Additional File 1: Table S1). Models including baseline LVM were compared with models excluding baseline LVM, showing better statistical fit and hence all models were adjusted for baseline LVM. We first examined the associations between baseline-adjusted LVMChange and baseline-adjusted appendicular lean mass rate of change (ALMChange) with age as a covariate (Model 1), where baseline-adjusted ALMChange was calculated as ALMChange minus baseline ALM. Subsequently, we examined the association of baseline-adjusted LVMChange with the addition of other covariates, such as the interaction term age*ALMChange (Model 2), sex (Model 3) with the interaction term sex*ALMChange (Model 4), and sex with baseline LVM (Models 5). Model 5 was further adjusted for baseline ALM (Model 6), baseline hemodynamic variables, pulse pressure and mean arterial pressure (Model 7), and body mass index (Model 8). To examine the influence of baseline LVM on longitudinal ALMChange, we included the interaction term LVM*ALMChange (Model 9).

We further examined the influence of age and sex on LVMChange in predicting ALMChange (Models 10–13) (Additional File 2: Table S2). We first analyzed the associations between LVMChange and ALMChange, with age as a covariate (Model 10), and their interaction term age*LVMChange (Model 11). We then added sex to age as covariates (Model 12) and examined the interaction term sex*LVMChange (Model 13).

We also examined the relationships between functional parameters, specifically handgrip strength (HGS) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), to determine if declines in cardiac and skeletal muscle function are associated with structural decline (Additional File 2: Table S2). The multivariable model predicting LVEFChange was adjusted for age and baseline LVEF, as well as baseline and longitudinal structural mass parameters (LVM baseline, LVMChange, ALM baseline, ALMChange).

To investigate potential heterogeneity in the skeletal-cardiac axis, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age (< 65 years vs. ≥ 65 years), and hypertension (Additional File 4: Table S4; Additional File 5: Table S5; Additional File 6: Table S6). These analyses utilized the final adjusted Model (Model 8) to determine if the longitudinal association between baseline-adjusted ALMChange and LVMChange remained consistent across demographic strata. Standardized beta coefficients were compared across subgroups to evaluate differences in the magnitude. Continuous age was retained as a covariate in all stratified models to account for residual confounding within the specified age ranges. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 18 (College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

Among 1025 participants, the mean age was 66.6 ± 13.1 years, and 47.8% were males (Table 1). All participants underwent at least 2 concurrent repeated measurements. The median number of visits per participant was 3 (interquartile range 2 to 4), yielding a total of 4124 observations. The mean baseline BMI was 26.7 ± 4.4 kg/m2, and the waist circumference was 89.8 ± 12.2 cm. Of the data available, 298/1015 (29.4%) had hypertension, 90/1021 (8.8%) had diabetes mellitus, and 390/1016 (38.4%) had dyslipidemia. The mean HGS was 33.1 ± 11.0 kg, and the appendicular lean mass was 20.8 ± 5.3 kg). On echocardiography, the mean baseline LVEF was 67.5 ± 9.7%, and the mean LV mass was 145.6 ± 50.4 g (Table 1). The mean lateral e’ was 10.20 ± 2.75 cm/s, and septal e’ was 8.46 ± 2.21 cm/s. Associations of age with cardiac and skeletal muscle mass

The average rate of decline of ALM was −25.1 g/year (95% CI −34.7, −15.5; p < 0.001)), and HGS was −0.36 kg/year (95% CI −0.41, −0.31; p < 0.001) on multilevel mixed-effects generalized linear modeling (Table 1). For cardiac parameters, the average rate of decline in LVEF was -2.19%/decade (95% CI -2.97%, -1.4%; p < 0.001), and in LVM was −0.730 g/year (95% CI −0.998, −0.460; p < 0.001).

Correlation analysis showed that older age was associated with higher baseline LVM (r = 0.081, p = 0.009) and LVEF (r = 0.086, p = 0.006), but with lower baseline HGS (r = −0.348, p < 0.001) and ALM (r = −0.175, p < 0.001) (Table 2). At the longitudinal level, advancing baseline age was associated with steeper declines in HGSChange (r = −0.172, p < 0.0001) and ALMChange (r = −0.159, p < 0.0001) over time (Fig. 1a). We did not find a significant correlation between baseline age and LVMChange (r = −0.042, p = 0.178) (Fig. 1b). Longitudinal associations between left ventricular and skeletal muscle mass

On correlation analysis, higher baseline HGS and ALM were negatively correlated with LVMChange (HGS r = −0.386, p < 0.001; ALM r = −0.542, p < 0.001) (Table 2). ALMChange was positively associated with LVMChange (ALMChange r = 0.393, p < 0.001; HGSChange p = 0.642) (Fig. 1c), while HGSChange was not associated with LVMChange.

Multiple linear regression predicting baseline-adjusted LVMChange (Table 3 and Additional File 1: Table S1) showed a positive association with baseline-adjusted ALMChange (Model 1, β = 0.567, SE = 0.020, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.313), adjusting for age. Adjusting for age and sex, baseline-adjusted ALMChange remained positively associated with LVMChange (Model 3, β = 0.544, SE = 0.045, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.314). After further including baseline LVM, baseline-adjusted ALMChange remained positively associated with LVMChange (Model 5, β = 0.287, SE = 0.034, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.663). After further adjusting for hemodynamic parameters (i.e., pulse pressure and mean arterial pressure), baseline-adjusted ALMChange remained positively associated with LVMChange (Model 7, β = 0.269, SE = 0.035, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.665). Adjustment for body mass index retained statistical significance (Model 8, β = 0.155, SE = 0.037, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.684). We observed a decrease in the standardized beta coefficients across Models 1, 3, 5, 7, and 8, particularly between Model 3 and Model 5 with the addition of LVM (β = 0.544 vs β = 0.287). We then tested the interaction terms between baseline-adjusted ALMChange and age, sex, and LVM (Additional File 1: Table S1). The interaction term age*baseline-adjusted ALMChange was statistically non-significant (Model 2). The interaction term sex*baseline-adjusted ALMChange was statistically significant (p < 0.050) (Model 4), suggesting that biological sex modulates the relationship between ALMChange and LVMChange. However, the interaction term LVM*baseline-adjusted ALMChange was found to be statistically non-significant (Model 9).

We then examined the influence of age and sex on the associations between LVMChange and ALMChange using multiple linear regression (Additional File 2: Table S2). Adjusting for age, LVMChange was significantly associated with ALMChange (Model 10) (β = 0.387, p < 0.001), and the interaction term age*LVMChange was non-significant (Model 11) (p = 0.123). Including age and sex, LVMChange remained significantly associated with ALMChange (Model 12) (β = 0.145, p < 0.001), and the interaction term sex*LVMChange was non-significant (Model 13) (p = 0.375).

Stratified subgroup analysis by sex, age, and hypertension

To explore sex-specific differences in the skeletal-cardiac axis, we conducted a subgroup analysis of Model 8 (Additional File 4: Table S4). Among the 502 female participants, the longitudinal association between baseline-adjusted ALMChange and LVMChange was significant (β = 0.175, SE = 0.035, p < 0.001). While this association remained statistically significant in the 461 male participants, the effect size was notably smaller (β = 0.074, SE = 0.036, p = 0.043). Baseline LVM and BMI persisted as independent predictors of LVMChange across both subgroups (all p < 0.001). Notably, chronological age was not predictive in either the female (p = 0.672) or male (p = 0.133) groups.

Age-stratified analyses further indicated that the skeletal-cardiac association varies across the lifespan (Additional File 5: Table S5). Among participants younger than 65 years, the association between baseline-adjusted ALMChange and LVMChange was significant (β = 0.270, p < 0.001). In participants aged 65 and older, this association remained significant but demonstrated a reduced effect size (β = 0.101, p = 0.040). Body mass index was a significant negative predictor in both subgroups (p < 0.001), whereas continuous age was not (Age < 65 years: p = 0.346; Age ≥ 65 years: p = 0.332). Notably, sex did not predict the rate of LVMChange in those younger than 65 years (p = 0.730), whereas it was a significant predictor for individuals 65 and older (β = −0.111, p = 0.016), suggesting that cardiac remodeling trajectories differ between men and women in later life.

To further assess whether the longitudinal association between skeletal and cardiac muscle decline remains consistent across clinical profiles, we performed a sensitivity analysis stratified by hypertension status using Model 8 (Additional File 6: Table S6). In participants without hypertension (n = 655), skeletal muscle decline was an independent predictor of cardiac mass reduction (β = 0.225, p < 0.001). In contrast, the association between ALMChange and LVMChange was not statistically significant in participants with hypertension (β = 0.085, p = 0.183). In this subgroup, baseline LV mass (β = −0.696, p < 0.001) and BMI (β = −0.120, p = 0.002) were the main predictors of longitudinal change in LV mass.

Longitudinal associations of left ventricular systolic function with left ventricular and skeletal muscle mass

On Pearson correlations (Table 2), baseline LVEF was positively correlated with age (r = 0.086, p = 0.006) and LVM (r = 0.160 p < 0.001) and inversely correlated with baseline HGS (r = −0.080 p = 0.032) and ALM (r = −0.07 p = 0.002). LVEFChange was positively correlated with LVMChange (r = 0.102 p = 0.001) and ALMChange (r = 0.108 p = 0.001) and inversely correlated with baseline LVM (r = −0.083 p = 0.008), HGS (r = −0.149 p < 0.001), and ALM (r = −0.188 p < 0.001).

On multiple linear regression, LVMChange was inversely associated with LVEFChange (p < 0.001), whereas age (p = 0.599), ALMChange (p = 0.552), HGSChange (p = 0.650) were non-significant in the model adjusting for baseline LVEF, LVM, ALM, and HGS (Additional File 3: Table S3) (overall model R2 = 0.385, adjusted R2 = 0.378, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Among community-dwelling adults with preserved left ventricular function, we observed novel associations between longitudinal changes in left ventricular mass and function and those of skeletal muscle mass, independent of chronological age, sex, and baseline values. These associations remained significant after adjusting for hemodynamic loading parameters, pulse pressure, and mean arterial pressure, which are known to impact both muscle beds. These findings together demonstrate that individuals with steeper declines in cardiac muscle mass at any given age also experienced steeper declines in skeletal muscle mass, indicating possible shared processes of accelerated aging in these two muscular tissues.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to demonstrate such an association between skeletal muscle mass and LV muscle changes in normative aging adults across the lifespan. These findings build on our previous report showing associations between skeletal muscle mass and LV mass, using a cross-sectional analysis of healthy young-old Asian adults with sarcopenia, as defined by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) [4]. The present analysis affirms these findings in a US-based cohort and further demonstrates longitudinal associations between declines in left ventricular and skeletal muscle mass.

Longitudinal association between left ventricular mass and skeletal muscle mass

Biological aging is increasingly understood as a nonlinear process that varies across different organ systems. Shen et al. (2024) [22] characterized this via “waves” of accelerated aging, while Ding et al. (2025) [23] showed that organs typically age at different rates. Our findings highlight an important nuance within this context. We observed a concurrent longitudinal decline in both appendicular lean mass and left ventricular mass over time. This synchronous decline suggests that cardiac and skeletal muscle tissues may share upstream proteomic drivers or be similarly susceptible to accelerated aging, effectively coupling their trajectories in a cardiac-skeletal axis, which contrasts with the overall variability seen in organ aging.

This analysis found that ALMChange was positively associated with LVMChange among healthy community adults, independent of age, sex, baseline LVM, and hemodynamic parameters. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis of synchronous cardiac and skeletal muscle aging [2, 4], where declines in mass occur concurrently in a longitudinal fashion. The observed associations’ underpinnings are beyond the scope of this paper, though we may offer several possible explanations.

A potential driver for these observations in asymptomatic adults is a shared, upstream process affecting both cardiac and skeletal muscle at a preclinical stage [24]. In the European multi-center Sarcopenia and Physical Frailty in Older People: Multi-component Treatment Strategies (SPRINTT) cohort of older adults with sarcopenia and physical frailty, cross-sectional analyses also found that ALM was correlated with LVM, independent of blood pressure and physical activity [7]. In the Kangbuk Samsung Health Study, lower skeletal muscle mass index in middle-aged adults was significantly associated with echocardiographic markers of diastolic dysfunction, including E/E’, septal E’, and E/A ratios [25]. This finding is particularly important when considering the sex-specific trajectories of aging. Men tend to lose absolute muscle mass more rapidly during earlier stages of aging, while women show a sharper increase in muscle loss as they age and are more susceptible to developing sarcopenia, especially in the oldest age group, typically defined as 85 years and older [26]. This vulnerability aligns with women’s predisposition to diastolic dysfunction, which is characterized by concentric remodeling and increased myocardial stiffness. When coupled with sarcopenia in advanced age, these distinct remodeling patterns suggest that the skeletal-cardiac axis may manifest differently by sex, potentially placing women at a uniquely elevated risk for the combined burden of sarcopenia and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [27, 28]. Similar associations between cardiac and skeletal muscle wasting have also been observed in patients with established heart failure [29]. Our study results extend existing evidence to community-dwelling cohorts and support the presence of a cardio-sarcopenia syndrome during the preclinical phase of aging [2, 4, 6].

In heart failure, several systemic and humoral mechanisms mediate crosstalk between skeletal muscle and the diseased myocardium. These pathways involve inflammatory cytokines, hormonal changes, metabolism, endothelial dysfunction, physical inactivity, and malnutrition, which drive a vicious cycle of disease progression [4, 5, 30]. These maladaptive interactions lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, apoptosis, reduced effort tolerance, and muscle loss that manifests as symptomatic, worsening heart failure [31, 32]. Our observations in this cohort of community-dwelling adults suggest that detectable changes in both myocardial and skeletal muscle occur during normative aging, even among individuals without overt heart failure or severe cardiac pathology. Aberrant cardiac-skeletal muscle crosstalk signaling may occur on a spectrum that starts in preclinical, asymptomatic states and accumulates with aging, within the conventional limits of accepted aging norms.

Our stratified analyses indicate that the relationship within the cardiac-skeletal axis is both age-dependent and sex-dimorphic. The association between appendicular lean mass decline and left ventricular mass reduction was stronger among participants under age 65 compared to those aged 65 and older. Importantly, chronological age was a non-significant predictor within these stratified models. This further suggests that the rate of skeletal muscle loss is a more critical factor in cardiac decline than age itself. The stronger connection observed in the younger cohort likely reflects a period when common biological drivers, such as proteomic signatures or mitochondrial signaling pathways, exert a synchronized effect on both muscle tissues before organ-specific factors begin to influence their individual trajectories [33–35]. These results align with multi-omics research describing nonlinear “waves” of accelerated change, suggesting that cardiac and skeletal tissues may share early proteomic drivers that link their development [22]. Collectively, these findings provide preliminary evidence that preclinical cardiac-skeletal interactions begin in early normative aging and accumulate over time.

Furthermore, a significant interaction between sex and adjusted ALMChange suggests that biological sex modulates the relationship between age-related skeletal muscle loss and cardiac remodeling. Our sex-stratified models show a stronger correlation between skeletal and cardiac decline in women than in men. This interaction is consistent with documented sex-specific remodeling differences [36, 37], which may be rooted in mitochondrial signaling and variations in proteins such as myostatin and insulin-like growth factor 1 [38–40]. Communication within the skeletal-heart axis appears to be governed by hormonal or proteomic mechanisms that vary by sex [41], necessitating further study into these pathways.

The observation that sex emerged as a significant independent predictor of cardiac mass change only in the older cohort (aged 65 and above) supports the idea that sex-dimorphic patterns intensify with advanced age. Older women often exhibit higher rates of concentric remodeling and myocardial stiffness, increasing their vulnerability to diastolic dysfunction [36]. Previous literature describes distinct trajectories of myocardial mass loss with aging: men tend to experience more substantial reductions [42], while women may show stable or slightly decreased mass, maintaining systolic function through concentric hypertrophy [43]. Although this study focuses on a normative cohort, the findings mirror clinical observations of a high combined burden of sarcopenia and heart failure symptoms in older women [44, 45]. Given the significant associations between cardiac and skeletal muscle remodeling in women and with aging, preserving skeletal muscle mass may be critical for maintaining cardiac structural health, particularly in older women.

The simultaneous loss of cardiac and skeletal muscle may arise from shared upstream biological drivers that initiate parallel tissue declines [35]. Our stratified analysis indicates that this coupling within the cardiac-skeletal axis is most prominent in individuals without the confounding influence of hypertension. In participants without hypertension, the longitudinal association between baseline-adjusted ALMChange and LVMChange remained significant, suggesting that the interactions between muscle beds represent a feature of normative aging that potentially manifests before cardiovascular disease develops. Conversely, the association was not statistically significant in the hypertensive subgroup, where structural remodeling appeared more influenced by baseline cardiac load and body mass than by skeletal muscle decline. This divergence suggests a mechanistic hierarchy, where in the absence of major clinical stressors, the two muscle beds decline in unison; however, the presence of hypertension introduces pathological remodeling signals that likely overpower the more subtle aging signals driving the cardio-sarcopenia interaction [35]. While instantaneous hemodynamic load, measured by pulse pressure and mean arterial pressure, did not dictate the overall trajectory of muscle aging in our general models, chronic hypertension establishes a distinct physiological state where remodeling is governed by pressure-overload pathology rather than shared biological aging [46, 47]. In normotensive individuals, the heart experiences less external stress, allowing the preclinical aging signal to remain visible. Consequently, the cardio-sarcopenia axis may be described as a phenotype of normative aging, where upstream aging-related drivers such as inflammaging and cellular senescence may contribute to subclinical microvascular rarefaction and fibrosis that remain unapparent in older adults without overt disease [48, 49]. Ultimately, further research utilizing detailed hemodynamic assessments and cardiovascular inflammation markers is necessary to clarify how hypertension and other age-associated comorbidities modulate the progression of this cardiac-skeletal muscle axis.

Increasing age correlated with decreasing ALMChange and HGSChange but was not significantly associated with LVMChange (Table 2). Instead, we found that ALMChange was positively correlated with LVMChange and LVEFChange. These observations suggest that the predominant mechanism of cardiac dysfunction in sarcopenia was not mediated by chronological age but by the decline in muscle mass and strength that occurs with age. Similarly, in the Framingham Heart Study, healthy men and women showed minimal changes in LV mass with advancing age [50, 51]. Given that lean muscle mass naturally declines with age, our results emphasize the importance of maintaining adequate muscle mass early during aging to resist the progression of cardiac muscle sarcopenia [52, 53].

While the decline in structural mass was closely linked, our secondary analysis of functional parameters showed different results. In particular, the longitudinal changes in handgrip strength did not show a significant correlation with changes in LV mass. This suggests that structural changes may occur before or independently of measurable functional decline within this normative aging cohort. Our previous cross-sectional study reported linear associations between lower handgrip strength and lower LV mass in sarcopenic patients [4]. The present study also observed associations between lower baseline handgrip strength and lower baseline LVM in healthy adults; however, there were no significant associations between change in handgrip strength with either baseline LVM or LVMChange over time. This observation suggests that ALM is a more substantial determinant of LVM than handgrip strength. Similarly, the prospective UK Biobank cohort also reported a positive correlation between baseline handgrip strength and LVM in older individuals but did not observe any significant relationships between change in HGS over time with any cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR)-measures of cardiac structure or function, including LVM and LV dimensions; however, the authors note that interpretation was limited as CMR was only performed once at the end of study [54]. The cross-sectional community-based Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP) found that lower HGS was associated with lower LV wall thickness, smaller chamber size, and greater diastolic stiffness as determined through both echocardiography and CMR; the same study group also found associations between lower peak oxygen consumption (VO2peak) with lower LVM, dimensions, and stroke volume [7, 55]. It remains unclear why LVM was correlated with a change in ALM but not handgrip strength. Besides fat-free mass, other plausible biological explanations include a smaller amino acid reservoir, abnormal myokine secretion, and decreased glucose consumption, leading to higher insulin resistance [56, 57]. However, these postulations comparing ALM with HGS are hypothetical, and more research is needed to verify these associations.

Longitudinal associations of cardiac contractility with cardiac and skeletal muscle masses

On Pearson’s correlation, LVEFChange was positively associated with LVMChange and ALMChange. However, after the inclusion of age, ALMChange, HGSChange, and baseline cardiac and skeletal muscle parameters on multiple linear regression, LVEFChange was negatively associated with LVMChange. These exploratory findings suggest that skeletal muscle changes influence the relationship between LV hypertrophy and LV systolic function over time. It is possible that skeletal muscle partially mediates the effects of exercise on LV remodeling and contractility, although further research is needed.

Limitations

Several significant limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting our study findings. Firstly, we cannot exclude the possibility of confounding in this observational study, which may have influenced our conclusions even after adjusting for available clinical factors. For example, a competing risk of death may be incurred by participants who died during follow-up before the repeated measurements. It is possible that these participants were more likely to have sarcopenia. It is difficult to estimate how excluding these participants affected the results, although excluding more sarcopenia individuals may have attenuated the observed effects. Nevertheless, our findings provide novel supporting evidence of early cardio-sarcopenia occurring over time in a community-based adult population. Second, our multilevel mixed-effects models did not include detailed longitudinal data on physical activity or specific pharmacological treatments. Direct measures of functional capacity, such as VO2peak, were unavailable. Since regular exercise influences remodeling differently from sedentary behavior, this absence of data limits our ability to adjust for physical activity as a confounding factor [58]. Additionally, we could not account for specific medications that influence cardiac function, particularly beta-blockers and renin-angiotensin system inhibitors [59]. To address these limitations, we adjusted our models for hemodynamic loading conditions, specifically mean arterial pressure and pulse pressure. These adjustments reflect key pathways through which antihypertensive agents influence left ventricular mass. While some residual confounding from non-hemodynamic drug effects or physical activity may still exist, we believe that the observed parallel decline likely reflects a fundamental aging process in this community-dwelling cohort. Several design features were implemented to minimize potential bias from unmeasured factors. For example, participants with structural heart disease, such as reduced ejection fraction or significant valvular disease, were excluded. This approach reduces the inclusion of individuals requiring high doses of guideline-directed medical therapies, which could significantly distort natural cardiac remodeling. Regarding potential mechanisms, we proposed that shared factors, such as inflammaging and cellular senescence, are plausible drivers of the observed parallel declines; however, our analysis did not include concurrent inflammatory biomarkers, which limits our understanding of these connections. Since blood collection and imaging visits were not always synchronized, we could not incorporate a robust analysis of specific markers like interleukin-6 or C-reactive protein. Future research utilizing aligned multi-omics and imaging data is important to clarify these mechanistic pathways. Third, we are not able to rule out the influence of sarcopenic obesity, as the mean body mass index of our cohort falls into an overweight range. Although our participants were not obese on average, the influence of adiposity on skeletal muscle and cardiac remodeling cannot be excluded entirely [60]. Furthermore, we acknowledge the limitations of using DXA-derived ALM as a surrogate for skeletal muscle mass. Unlike other modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or CT, DXA measures lean soft tissue rather than directly measuring muscle cross-sectional area or volume [61]. Consequently, it cannot visualize myosteatosis (intramuscular fat infiltration) or separate muscle from other non-contractile components, such as connective tissue [62]. As aging is often accompanied by increased adiposity and fibrosis within the muscle compartment, DXA-derived estimates may fail to capture declines in muscle quality, potentially leading to an overestimation of functional muscle mass in older adults [63–65]. Fourth, although we excluded participants with reduced ejection fraction and significant valvular disease, our normative aging cohort includes participants with managed comorbidities, such as hypertension. While this improves the representativeness of our sample relative to the general aging population, the potential influence of these conditions on the skeletal-cardiac axis cannot be entirely dismissed, even after adjusting for hemodynamic loading. Fifth, the effect size of ALMChange on LVMChange was small, indicating that a significant change in ALM was necessary to manifest an observable difference in LVM. Furthermore, the moderate effects suggest that other confounders may remain unaccounted for; it is unlikely that ALM alone will be the sole determinant of LVM. While this study provides new insights into the longitudinal effects of sarcopenia on cardiac remodeling, the relevance and clinical implications of these findings need further investigation. Sixth, the clinical relevance of established diagnostic thresholds, such as sarcopenia, requires consideration alongside the continuous relationship between cardiac and skeletal muscle mass. In this community-dwelling cohort, the prevalence of sarcopenia, defined by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) criteria for low handgrip strength (men < 27 kg; women < 16 kg), was only 4.2% (n = 30) [66]. This low prevalence likely contributed to the lack of a statistical association between categorical sarcopenia and longitudinal changes in left ventricular mass on sensitivity analysis (R2 = 0.0053, p = 0.052). We focused on continuous measures of muscle mass to capture the wide range of physiological decline that begins in early aging, which binary clinical cut-points may not adequately represent. Future research in populations with a higher prevalence of clinical sarcopenia is necessary to determine if these longitudinal associations accelerate once diagnostic thresholds are crossed. Finally, while we quantified the rate of aging through longitudinal slopes of tissue decline, we did not utilize a validated composite frailty index. Future studies should investigate how these parallel tissue declines correlate with systemic measures of frailty.

Conclusions

Among community-dwelling normative aging adults across the life course, changes in skeletal muscle mass over time significantly correlated with changes in left ventricular muscle mass and function, independent of age. These findings offer preliminary evidence of preclinical upstream cardiac-skeletal muscle interactions that initiate in early normative aging and accumulate over time. Future studies are needed to examine whether interventions to counteract age-associated losses in lean muscle mass may be important therapeutic targets in preventing cardiac aging.
