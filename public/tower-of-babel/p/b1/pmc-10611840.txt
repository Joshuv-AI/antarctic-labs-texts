Expert Discussion on Challenges in C3G Diagnosis: A Podcast Article on Best Practices in Kidney Biopsies
Authors: Richard A Lafayette, Vivek Charu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplement 3 glomerulopathy (C3G) is an ultra-rare, progressive kidney disease resulting from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. Clinical presentation of C3G is heterogeneous and definitive diagnosis relies on kidney biopsy and immunofluorescence staining. The term C3G encompasses two subgroups, dense deposit disease and C3 glomerulonephritis, distinguished via electron microscopy. In this podcast article, the authors discuss the challenges associated with C3G diagnosis and the central role of kidney biopsy. Using an illustrative case study, key histological observations are described, and best practices are discussed from the perspectives of a nephrologist and a nephropathologist. Key Summary Points

Complement 3 glomerulopathy (C3G) is a rare and progressive complement-mediated kidney disease, which is challenging to diagnose because of clinical heterogeneity.Definitive diagnosis of C3G requires a kidney biopsy, which is associated with a low rate of major complications in most patients.C3G is diagnosed by dominant C3 staining in the glomeruli of immunofluorescence intensity ≥ 2 orders of magnitude higher than any other immune reactant; electron microscopy is used to distinguish between C3 glomerulonephritis and dense deposit disease.Antigen retrieval on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded kidney biopsy tissue can unmask monotypic immunoglobulin deposits due to monoclonal gammopathy.Ultimately, accurate diagnosis of C3 requires clinical-pathologic correlation and close communication between the nephrologist and pathologist to rule out differential diagnoses such as infection-related glomerulonephritis.

Vivek Charu

Thanks, Dr. Lafayette. So classic cases of C3G, or C3 glomerulonephritis, or dense deposit disease, by light microscopy show a membranoproliferative pattern glomerulonephritis (MPGN). A membranoproliferative pattern glomerulonephritis is characterized by diffuse mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity, lobular accentuation of the capillary tuft, and glomerular basement membrane double contouring [12]. So, this is the classic morphology that has been seen historically in cases of C3G. But, over time, we've learned that actually, the light microscopic findings in C3G can be quite diverse and can range from just plain mesangioproliferation to more of an endocapillary proliferative glomerulonephritis. Cases can have crescents, active crescents, cellular and fibrous crescents [1, 2]. So really, the glomerular changes and the glomerular morphology that's seen by light microscopy can be very heterogeneous. As has been mentioned, C3 glomerulopathy is really divided into two distinct diagnoses, C3 glomerulonephritis, or C3GN, and dense deposit disease, DDD, and to separate C3GN from DDD really requires both immunofluorescence (IF) but ultimately electron microscopy (EM) [1]. I'll just mention that in very well-developed cases of DDD, there can be some subtle findings on light microscopy with sort of thickened or glassy deposits all along the capillary walls [13]. For all of these cases of C3 glomerulopathy, immunofluorescence microscopy is essential to make the diagnosis [1].

So, as part of routine processing of kidney biopsies, we perform a set of immunofluorescence studies in which we stain a piece of frozen kidney biopsy tissue with a panel of immune reactants, including IgG, IgA, and IgM heavy chains, kappa and lambda light chains, and C3, specifically C3c, one of the breakdown products of C3, and C1q [1, 14]. By definition, cases of C3G must have dominant C3 staining in the glomeruli, and that dominant classification, that dominant staining of C3, must be greater than or equal to two orders of magnitude higher than the intensity by immunofluorescence for any of the other immune reactants in the panel [1].

So, most classic cases of C3G have only C3 staining in the glomeruli, but there are many cases actually that have dominant C3 staining, but some staining for some of the other immune reactants, including some immunoglobulin staining or even a little bit of C1q staining [14]. The presence of other immunoglobulins in cases of bona fide C3 glomerulopathy have been verified through mass spectrometry studies [15], and there are some findings in immunofluorescence that can, again, be clues to the diagnosis of dense deposit disease versus C3GN. But, ultimately, we really rely on electron microscopy to make a definitive diagnosis of dense deposit disease. That's because the ultra-structural findings in dense deposit disease are so characteristic, and those are characterized by deposition and really transformation of the laminate densa of the glomerular basement membrane by homogenous, very highly electron dense C3 deposits [13]. The density, the darkness, that we see in those deposits we often call hyperosmiophilic deposits. Those deposits have sort of a sausage-shaped characteristic, and they're sort of confluent, and elongated, and linear, and all along the glomerular basement membranes [14]. And when we see those, we can feel very confident about making a diagnosis of dense deposit disease.

In contrast, C3 glomerulonephritis does not have those very hyper-dense deposits along the glomerular basement membranes [7]. Classic cases of C3 glomerulonephritis demonstrate less dense but clear deposits in the mesangium, subendothelium, and subepithelial distributions [14, 16]. Many cases of C3 glomerulonephritis demonstrate subepithelial humps or bulky subepithelial deposits. In many other cases, distinguishing between C3 glomerulonephritis and dense deposit disease can be quite difficult because there can be a lot of overlapping findings on electron microscopy [16].

I think that's very helpful. We clinicians rely on the pathologists and their expertise also to dig a little deeper and tell us that it's really not post-infectious or infection-related glomerulonephritis. To see whether or not it might be caused by a monoclonal gammopathy.

This really brings up what are the main differential diagnoses that we think about when we're trying to make a diagnosis of C3 glomerulopathy. The first is infection-associated glomerulonephritis, formally called post-infectious glomerulonephritis. So, ultimately distinguishing between infection-associated glomerulonephritis and C3G is very, very challenging on the grounds of biopsy alone [1, 17]. That's because basically the findings can be completely overlapping across light, IF, and EM. Classic cases of infection-associated glomerulonephritis do demonstrate sort of neutrophilic inflammation in the glomeruli [18]. But I think ultimately, those cases of infection-associated glomerulonephritis, that's where the clinical-pathologic correlation and the clinical presentation and follow-up really help distinguish between the two.

The second big differential diagnosis that we think about is actually a membranoproliferative pattern glomerulonephritis with masked monotypic immunoglobulin deposits [19]. This gets, in part, to your question about the monoclonal gammopathies. So, actually it's been found in the past there was a subset of cases that were actually diagnosed as C3G, and that when we perform a particular technique called antigen retrieval on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue, so that's when we take the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue and we use a proteolytic digestion and then perform immunofluorescence studies on the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue, that, in a subset of these cases, that by routine immunofluorescence microscopy showed only C3 staining, on the antigen retrieval studies actually showed monotypic immunoglobulin deposits in the glomeruli [20]. This has now become a sort of a routine practice in renal pathology, and we've been able to identify now that there are certain immunoglobulin deposits that are "masked," meaning that we can’t identify them or pick them up on routine immunofluorescence studies, but we require this antigen retrieval technique in order to be able to detect them [21].

So, because of this discovery, that actually there were these subsets of cases with C3-dominant staining by routine immunofluorescence that turned out to be masked monotypic immunoglobulin deposits, it's become part of our routine processing of these cases. When we see dominant C3 in the glomeruli, we always perform antigen retrieval on the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue to rule out the possibility of masked monotypic immunoglobulin deposits. The reason is obviously that in those cases with masked monotypic immunoglobulin deposits, we think the real driver of kidney disease is immune complex-based as opposed to dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway [14]. So, that distinction is actually very important. And that's one distinction where actually we can make a lot of headway on histopathologic grounds alone.

Vivek Charu

Yeah. So, there has been development of a C3 glomerulopathy histopathology index, which is really a set of scores, both activity and chronicity scores, that are actually really meant to mimic the histopathologic scoring system we use in lupus nephritis [24]. So, the activity score captures glomerular changes such as mesangial or endocapillary hypercellularity, membranoproliferative pattern morphology, leukocyte infiltration of the glomeruli, whether there's crescents or fibrinoid necrosis, and interstitial inflammation. The chronicity score, again mimicking the chronicity score in lupus nephritis, captures the global glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and vascular changes. So, since this score was originally proposed in 2018 [24], there have been a couple of validation studies in large cohorts of patients with C3G [24, 25]. Those validation studies have suggested that, as you might expect, the most important prognostic information in the kidney biopsy is really the chronicity score [24, 25]. So, the interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and percentage of global glomerular sclerosis really are what are most predictive of kidney failure. One of those validation studies did also identify the presence of crescents as being prognostic [24]. So, I think they fit with what practicing nephrologists already know from the kidney biopsy. Concluding Remarks

Richard Lafayette

So, let me just conclude, the main points of the conversation are that understanding patients with clinical need for a kidney biopsy should result in a biopsy. Again, there's abundant evidence that for most patients, a kidney biopsy is a safe way forward without a significant complication rate, certainly a manageable complication rate [8, 9]. Again, communication between a nephrologist and the expert nephropathologist is really vital to getting the definitive diagnosis of C3 glomerulopathy and assuring that the differential diagnosis has been fully explored. And that while there's no single algorithm that can identify all cases of C3G, we do rely on our pathologist to give us a sense of how certain the diagnosis is and how far we should go looking for infections or other diseases. There's clearly a strong need to identify other biomarkers and maybe other pathologic-histologic characteristics, maybe to improve the separation of C3G from these other diagnostic considerations. But hopefully we'll get there.

While this is a rare disease, I think it's maybe a gateway diagnosis where it's the hallmark of alternate complement-mediated kidney disease. And we now suspect that numerous different glomerular diseases may have a substantial part of their pathophysiology in complement dysregulation. And perhaps the learnings we get from this disease will help other patients as well.
