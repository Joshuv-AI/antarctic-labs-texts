A biomimetic nanointegrator synergizing “dual-throttle” immune checkpoint blockade and enhanced DAMPs-mediated immune activation for potent anti-tumor T‑cell immunity
Authors: Han-Zhe Liu, Pan Liu, Zi-Yi Chen, Tong Wang, Lan Liu, Qiu-Jing Li, Zheng-Jun Shang, Guo-Feng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiving cell-derived bioactive nanotherapeutics have advanced the development of highly effective cancer treatment strategies. In this study, we elaborately develop a multifunctional nanointegrator (DOX@DPNVs) from bioengineered nanovesicles (NVs) that act as a potent immunoactivator to overcome immunosuppression for enhanced tumor therapy. By genetically modifying tumor cells with PD-L1 knockout as well as PD-1 overexpression, these cells were subsequently treated with immunogenic cell death (ICD)-inducer (i.e., doxorubicin (DOX)) to trigger the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in the extracellular environment. The resultant cell-involved mixture undergoes a one-step sonication and extrusion process, resulting in the formation of DOX@DPNVs, which are co-loaded with DAMPs and DOX and display PD-1 molecules on their surface. Consequently, the bioactive DOX@DPNVs enable the delivered DAMPs to combine with the DOX-induced ICD effect to accelerate DAMPs-mediated immune activation and effectively blockage the PD-1/PD-L1 axis for restoring cytotoxic function of T cells, eliciting an robust anti-tumor response in an aggressive squamous cell carcinoma mouse model. This naturally occurring NVs-based dual-throttle strategy provides an effective immunoactivation strategy to overcome immunosuppressive tumor environment, offering a promising platform for combating aggressive cancers.Graphical Abstract Nanovesicles derived from genetic engineered cancer cells (PD1-overexpressing and PD-L1 knockout) were modified with surface-enriched ICD-derived DAMPs and loaded with DOX. DOX@DPNVs efficiently induced ICD in cancer cells, whereas DAMPs and tumor-associated antigen presentation promoted dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation. Synergistic immune checkpoint blockade with DOX@DPNVs enhances cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, decreased T-cell exhaustion and establishes a robust and long-lasting anticancer immune memory. Introduction

Immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment for a wide range of malignancies by activating the host immune system to destroy tumor cells [1–3]. Among them, immune checkpoint inhibitors, especially those targeting the programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) pathway, have demonstrated particular clinical success [4–6]. Nevertheless, certain malignant cells evade immune surveillance by hijacking immune checkpoint pathways. These evasive cells express PD-L1 on their surface to bind with PD-1 on T cells, which transmits an inhibitory signal that effectively “applies the brakes” on the immune response, suppressing T cell function and enabling immune escape. Blocking this “braking” process with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors can rescue T cell function to recognize and eliminate cancer cells. However, single blockade often suffers from inadequate targeting and limited efficacy. This challenge is particularly pronounced in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), an aggressive malignancy characterized by rapid progression, high recurrence and poor survival [7, 8]. Therefore, dual-blockade strategies capable of targeting both PD-1 and PD-L1 represent a more promising avenue with considerable potential to overcome the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [9–11].

Meanwhile, as an alternative strategy to elicit anti-tumor immunity, immunogenic cell death (ICD) is emerging as a viable approach in the treatment of solid tumors [12]. It’s a unique mode of tumor cell death characterized by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT), high mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and heat-shock proteins (HSP70, HSP90) [13]. These signals collectively stimulate dendritic cell (DCs) maturation and enhanced antigen presentation, thereby promoting a robust antitumor immune response [14, 15]. Chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin (DOX) are effective ICD inducers [16, 17]. However, their systemic administration is often associated with severe adverse effects, off-target toxicity and limited bioavailability [18–20]. Moreover, the spontaneously released DAMPs during ICD are typically transient and inefficient to fully activate an effective anti-tumor immune response, particularly within the immunosuppressive TME [21]. Moreover, the combination of ICD with other therapies such as immune checkpoint blockade should be manipulated in spatiotemporal control, or it will be difficult to achieve optimal immune cell reinvigoration [22]. Therefore, to amply DAMPs-induced immunity, exogenously supplying of additional DAMPs molecules that can combine with the endogenously released DAMPs during ICD would be a feasible strategy to greatly boost the efficacy of immunotherapy.

The development of cell-derived nanovesicles (NVs) has advanced the design of multifunctional delivery systems for synergistic therapy [23–26]. They can be isolated from natural cells by gradient centrifugation, resulting in the formation of biofilm-enclosed nanostructures integrated with multiple bioactive substances (e.g., surface proteins and lipids), which are inherited from their parent cells [27–29]. Leveraging their inherent biocompatibility, immunomodulatory potential and tumor-specific targeting ability, exogenous agents, such as hydrophilic/hydrophobic drugs and bioactive molecules, can be additionally encapsulated into the nanoscale vesicular particles to provide an effective NV-based therapeutic platform for targeted therapy [30, 31]. Moreover, with the advances in genetic engineering, natural cells could be custom-made with specific properties, which reserve up to the derived NVs and equip them with intended functionalities [32, 33]. For example, Li et al. engineered cancer cells to overexpress PD-1 and prepared PD-1–displaying nanovesicles capable of enhanced tumor targeting and dual chemo-metabolic therapy [34]. Therefore, the development of NV-based therapeutic strategy has become a research hotspot in recent years and facilitates the multifunctional design of bioactive materials for cancer therapy.

Herein, we fabricated a NV-based nanointegrator (DOX@DPNVs), which integrates genetic PD-L1 knockout, PD-1 overexpression, exogenous DAMPs delivery, and DOX loading, to simultaneously achieve “dual-throttle” immune checkpoint modulation and enhance DAMP-mediated immune activation for boosting effective T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Specifically, mouse squamous cell carcinoma cells were engineered with concurrent PD-L1 knockout and PD-1 overexpression, which were then treated with chemotherapeutic drug DOX to induce a potent ICD effect with the release of abundant DAMPs in cell culture medium. By sonication and extrusion,

DOX could be packaged into the nanovesicles and the DAMPs generated during DOX-induced ICD were then conjugated onto the surface of the nanovesicles via SMCC-mediated coupling, resulting in the highly bioactive DOX@DPNVs (Fig. 1a). DOX@DPNVs enabled targeted delivery of a supplementary bolus of exogenous DAMPs at tumor tissues, which could synergize with the endogenous DAMPs released during DOX-initiated ICD, greatly enhancing the DAMPs-mediated anti-tumor immune response. Meanwhile, the display of PD-1 in the absence of PD-L1 on DOX@DPNVs effectively blocked the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in a dual-blockade way for restoring cytotoxic function of T cells. As a result, a robust anti-tumor immunity was elicited with promoted DCs maturation, facilitated antigen presentation and enhanced T cells activation, leading to significant tumor growth suppression in the murine HNSCC model (Fig. 1b). Compared with conventional ICD-based nanotherapies that rely on in situ tumor cell death to generate DAMPs, our strategy introduces a DAMP-preloading approach to enable immediate immunostimulatory signal transduction. In addition, the nanovesicle platform integrates immune checkpoint blockade and immunostimulatory signals within a single system, which is expected to achieve synchronized immune activation.

(b) DOX@DPNVs enhance immunotherapy against HNSCC through multiple immune activation pathways, including DOX-induced ICD, DAMPs-mediated DCs maturation, antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell activation, and ultimately synergistic anticancer effects in combination with immune checkpoint blockade

Results and discussion

Characterization of DOX@DPNVs

Firstly, mouse squamous cell carcinoma cells (SCC7 cells) were chosen for genetic engineering, and the successful establishment of the PD-1 overexpression (PD-1 OE) and PD-L1 knockout (PD-L1 KO) models was first confirmed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Compared with the significant PD-L1 expression but barely detectable PD-1 in wild-type (WT) group, cells in the PD-1 OE group exhibited intense green fluorescence, and PD-L1 KO cells showed a complete loss of red fluorescence (Fig. 2a and Fig. S1). In the dual-gene engineered model, only green fluorescence was clearly detected in cells, demonstrating the concurrently successful PD-L1 knockout as well as PD-1 overexpression by tumor cells. Flow cytometry (FC) analysis further confirmed the genetic modifications in SCC7 cells (Fig. 2b and Fig. S2). Specifically, a significant increase in PD-1 expression was observed in the PD-1 OE group, while PD-L1 expression was undetectable in the PD-L1 KO group, both of which were detected in the dual-edited group, further validating dual gene editing in SCC7 cells.

To generate a source of ICD antigens, the PD-1 OE/PD-L1 KO cells were pretreated with cytotoxic drug DOX to induce an in vitro ICD, leading to the release of abundant DAMPs from the dying cells to the culture medium. The resulting mixture, containing cells and cell debris, released DAMPs and free DOX, was then processed by sonication, extrusion, and ultracentrifugation in the presence of sulfosuccinimidyl-4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate (SMCC), leading to the formation of DOX@DPNVs, which were specialized with DOX loading and surfaced-decorated.

autologous DAMPs via covalent conjugation. Bare NVs obtained directly from engineered SCC7 cells with PD-1 displaying (i.e., PNVs) and DOX-loaded PNVs (i.e., DOX@PNVs) were prepared as well. These nanoformulations should be classified as artificial cell-derived vesicles, as they are generated through laboratory induced cell disruption processes, including sonication and extrusion. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that both PNVs and DOX@DPNVs were spherical with a uniform diameter of about 100 nm, showing typical exosome-like features (Fig. 2c). This finding was further supported by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements, which indicated a narrow size distribution with a diameter of 115.5 ± 7.5 nm and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.136 ± 0.029 for the PNVs, and a slight increase of diameter to 129.4 ± 21.4 nm and a PDI of 0.159 ± 0.023 for DOX@DPNVs (Fig. 2d). In addition, DOX@DPNVs maintained a consistent particle size and low PDI for seven days under storage in PBS at 4 °C or incubated at physiological conditions (37 °C, 10% FBS medium) (Fig. 2f and g), demonstrating the long-term stability of the synthesized nanosystem for biomedical application. Meanwhile, the drug loading efficiency of DOX in DOX@DPNVs was determined to be 17.50 ± 1.74% (Fig. S3). To confirm that the nanovesicles successfully inherited the distinct membrane protein profiles of their parent cells, typical proteins were detected using Western blotting. ATP1A1 was used as a membrane protein loading control to verify whether the protein amounts were comparable among different groups. As shown in Fig. 2e, S4 and S5, nanovesicles derived from WT cells (WT NVs) were detected with significant expression of PD-L1 with very low level of PD-1. And the gene editing for PD-1 overexpression did not significantly affect PD-L1 expression in cells. For nanovesicles derived from dual engineered SCC7 cells (concurrent with PD-1 OE and PD-L1 KO), both PNVs and DOX@DPNVs showed abundant PD-1 while lacking detectable PD-L1, demonstrating that the key surface characteristics of the source cells were faithfully preserved in the corresponding nanovesicles. To assess the immunogenic potential of DOX@DPNVs, the content of DAMPs, including HMGB1, HSP70 and HSP90, were quantitatively evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2h and Fig. S6, compared to PNVs, DOX@DPNVs showed significantly higher levels of these immunogenicity markers, with 8-fold (HMGB1), 2.8-fold (HSP70) and 3.5-fold (HSP90) increases, respectively. This significant enhancement suggests that DOX@DPNVs carried an adequate number of DAMPs to stimulate the maturation of DCs, which were expected to promote strong immune recognition and activation in the TME. Moreover, hemolysis assays showed that DOX@DPNVs were virtually unable to lyse erythrocytes even at the highest concentrations (Fig. 2i), revealing the feasibility of this nanovesicles-based therapeutic platform for clinical applications.

It was shown that the enclosed lipid bilayer of nanovesicles can destabilize with decreasing ambient pH, triggering the accelerated release of encapsulated drugs [35, 36]. To assess the on-command drug release for targeted tumor therapy, the in vitro drug release profiles of DOX@DPNVs at different pH conditions (pH 7.4, mimicking normal physiological conditions; pH 6.5, mimicking a slightly acidic TME; and pH 5.5, mimicking intracellular acidic environment in endo/lysosome) were investigated. As shown in Fig. 2j, DOX@DPNVs exhibited a clear pH-dependent release behavior, resulting in 85.8% and 66.1% DOX release at pH 5.5 and pH 6.5, respectively, which was much higher than that in physiological conditions (pH 7.4, 40.1% release at 48 h). This pH-dependent release profile highlights the potential of DOX@DPNVs to selectively deliver drugs to tumor tissues, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects.

In vitro cell specific uptake, cell killing and ICD induction

Owing to the presence of specific surface antigens responsible for homologous cellular adhesion, which are inherently present on nanovesicles derived from tumor cells, the as-prepared vesicle-based nanosystem would possess the intrinsic property for the specific targeting and delivering drugs into tumor cells. To confirm the homotypic targeting property, in vitro cellular endocytosis of the nanosystem was investigated in squamous cell carcinoma 7 (SCC7) tumor cells and national institutes of health 3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH-3T3) normal cells. As observed by CLSM, free DOX treatment resulted in moderate cytoplasmic and cytosolic red fluorescence in both types of cells with no discernible difference, because of passive diffusion of small molecular drugs into cells without selectivity (Fig. 3a). In contrast, a much stronger red fluorescence was detected in DOX@DPNVs-treated SCC7 cells, which was, however, faint in NIH-3T3 cells under the same condition, implying that the nanosystem was more readily internalized by tumor cells due to the active targeting ability of DOX@DPNVs. Given the engineered enrichment of PD-1 on the nanovesicles and the high expression of PD-L1 on SCC7 cells, the active targeting property would be also mediated by the specific interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1. Therefore, we further conducted cellular uptake experiments by pre-treating cells with excess of free αPD-L1. Since tumor cell-derived nanovesicles inherently possess homologous targeting capacity arising from multiple membrane components (e.g., adhesion molecules, integrin), PD-L1 blockade weakened rather than completely abolished cellular uptake. Also, statistical analysis confirmed that PD-L1 blockade resulted in a significant reduction in nanovesicle uptake (p < 0.01), providing clear quantitative support for our conclusion (Fig. S7).

Upon specific cellular internalization, the cytotoxicity of delivered DOX in killing tumor cells was evaluated using cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. SCC7 cells were incubated with PNVs, free DOX, DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs at different concentrations for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 3b, PNVs alone exhibited negligible cytotoxicity across all concentrations, demonstrating the good biocompatibility of the nanovesicle-based platform. For free DOX-treated cells, the cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner, with 48.3% of tumor cells remaining viable at the concentration of 16 µM. Comparatively, DOX@PNVs exhibited a greater cell killing effect, reducing cell viability to 31.2%, attributed to the enhanced drug delivery by nano delivery system to induce a more potent therapeutic effect. Notably, DOX@DPNVs achieved a comparable level of cytotoxicity (31.2% viability) to DOX@PNVs,

indicating that SMCC-mediated coupling of DAMPs in the nanosystem does not contribute additional toxicity. This specific cell death induction was primarily caused by the efficient intracellular delivery of DOX via the nanovesicle platform, rather than by any intrinsic vesicle components, since PNVs were shown to be safe even at high concentrations (Fig. S8). Furthermore, FC analysis of Annexin V/DAPI staining cells also revealed the outstanding ability of DOX@DPNVs in inducing cell damage, resulting in the maximum apoptotic/necrotic proportion of SCC7 cells in DOX@DPNVs group (over 40%), which was about 1.4-fold increase over DOX (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3c and d).

Furthermore, we explored whether DOX@DPNVs were efficient to induce robust ICD effect by detecting typical DAMPs signals, including CRT expression and the release of ATP, HMGB1, HSP70 and HSP90. Immunofluorescence (IF) images showed markedly elevated CRT exposure with strong red fluorescent signals after treatment with DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs, compared to the weak signal in PBS and PNV groups and the moderate induction by free DOX (Fig. 3e). Consistent with the IF observations, FC provided quantitative evaluation of CRT exposure by SCC7 cells after different treatments. As shown in Fig. 3f and g, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CRT increased by 1.3-fold in DOX@DPNVs groups relative to the DOX group. Compared to the PNVs group, the increase was approximately 5.1-fold. In addition, the extracellular release of ATP, HMGB1, HSP70 and HSP90 was detected by an enzyme-linked immuno sorbent assay (ELISA). (Fig. 3h-k). Extracellular levels of these markers were lowest in the PBS and PNVs groups. Treatment with DOX triggered an increase in the secretion of DAMPs, with levels rising by approximately 3.0-fold (ATP), 4.0-fold (HMGB1), 4.3-fold (HSP70) and 4.9-fold (HSP90) compared to the PBS group. Notably, DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs treatments further enhanced the release of these markers, resulting in the highest release in the DOX@DPNVs group, which reached 5.3-fold (ATP), 6.0-fold (HMGB1), 8.2-fold (HSP70) and 9.9-fold (HSP90) of those in the PBS group, respectively. Collectively, these findings highlight the effective role of DOX@DPNVs in triggering ICD effect, which is expected to elicit robust anti-tumor immune response.

Dendritic cells maturation and antigen uptake ability of DOX@DPNVs

DAMPs, no matter exogenously supplied by the nanosystem or generated by tumor cells during ICD, would bind to the pattern recognition receptors on dendritic cells (DCs), triggering their maturation and activating subsequent adaptive immune responses [37, 38]. To confirm the maturation of DCs through direct stimulation, bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were directly treated with PBS, PNVs, DOX, DOX@PNVs or DOX@DPNVs for 24 h, followed by detecting the co-stimulatory signals on the surface of BMDCs. FC analyses revealed a significantly upregulated expression of CD80 and CD86 in BMDCs in the DOX@DPNVs group compared to all other groups, with a double-positive ratio of 7.75%, indicating that the BMDCs had developed a mature phenotype (Fig. S9). This enhanced promotion of BMDCs phenotypic maturation can be attributed to the direct presentation of DAMPs to BMDC by DOX@DPNV without the need for prior tumor cell death. To further evaluate the ICD-induced enhancement of DC maturation, BMDCs were co-cultured with SCC7 cells that had been pretreated with PBS, PNVs, DOX, DOX@PNVs, or DOX@DPNVs for 24 h. CLSM imaging showed that DOX@DPNVs treatment resulted in the highest proportion of mature DCs, which were evidenced with the strongest expression of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 (green) and CD86 (pink) in CD11c⁺ (red) DCs (Fig. 4a). In contrast, BMDCs in the PBS, DOX and PNVs groups showed significantly weakened mature expression. FC analyses further confirmed a shift of DCs population toward the CD80⁺CD86⁺ quadrant in the DOX@DPNVs group, resulting in the highest proportion of mature CD11c⁺ DCs (66.5%) as compared with DOX@PNVs (46.4%), DOX (28.5%), PNVs (0%) and PBS (0%) (Fig. 4b and c). Such a mature-promoting effect was significantly higher than that in the DOX@PNVs group (p < 0.0001) and DOX group (p = 0.0005).

To assess the functional activation of DCs, we examined cytokines, including interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which were secreted by DCs in response to co-cultured tumor cells. These cytokines are critical for enhancing antigen presentation, amplifying immune response and inducing T cell activation [39–41]. ELISA results showed that the most pronounced increase in cytokine release from DCs was induced by the treatment of DOX@DPNVs. Specifically, IFN-γ levels in the DOX@DPNVs group reached 7.0 pg/100 µg protein, which was 1.8-fold higher than those in the DOX group (Fig. 4d). Similarly, IL-6 and TNF-α levels rose to 4.5 (Fig. 4e) and 5.5 pg/100 µg protein (Fig. 4f), respectively, which were significantly elevated compared to both DOX and DOX@PNVs groups.

Upon maturation, DCs would be equipped with the ability of presenting antigens from tumor cells. To confirm this, SCC7 cells labeled by carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) were pre-treated with different formulations, following by co-culturing with BMDCs. As observed by CLSM, DOX, DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs groups displayed green fluorescence (CFSE, tumor cell debris) within the cytoplasm of CD11c⁺ DCs (red fluorescence), indicating that DCs from these groups showed significant phagocytic internalization (Fig. 4g). Quantitative analyses of the percentage of CFSE⁺ CD11c⁺ cells revealed a similar trend in the phagocytosis rate (Fig. S10). In contrast, only negligible CFSE signals were detected in the PBS and PNVs groups, suggesting poor phagocytosis of DCs. 3D fluorescence intensity map provided a clearer distinction, showing the highest number of fluorescence intensity peaks in the DOX@DPNVs group, which confirmed its superior efficacy in promoting tumor-derived antigen uptake (Fig. 4h).

Given that mature DCs play a key role in priming T cell responses, we next evaluated the interaction between nanovesicles and primary T cells, which was assessed by FC analysis using fluorescent labeled anti PD-1 antibody as detection signal. Compared with nanoformulations with PD-L1 knockout (PD-L1 KO NVs, PNVs and DOX@DPNVs), WT NVs exhibited stronger interactions with primary T cells, which may be ascribed to the interaction between PD-L1 on WT NVs and PD-1 on the surface of T cells (Fig. S11). This interaction inhibited subsequent binding of anti PD-1 antibodies, indicating that vesicle-associated PD-L1 can directly engage PD-1 on T cells and interfere with checkpoint blockade. In contrast, T cells treated with PD-1 OE NVs displayed partial anti PD-1 antibody binding, which can be attributed to the binding of PD-1 OE NVs to T cells via vesicle-displayed PD-1, followed by antibody recognition of PD-1 presented on the nanovesicles. Functionally, PNVs and DOX@DPNVs induced significantly stronger T cell responses than WT NVs, PD-1 OE NVs or PD-L1 KO NVs alone, as evidenced by increased secretion of IL-2 and IFN-γ, as well as enhanced cytotoxic activity against tumor cells reflected by elevated LDH release (Fig. S12-S14). In contrast, nanovesicles lacking either PD-1 overexpression or PD-L1 knockout exhibited substantially weakened T cell activation, demonstrating that a single genetic modification is insufficient to fully restore T cell effector function.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy and immunogenic tumor remodeling of DOX@DPNVs

Prior to therapeutic efficacy assessment, the in vivo biodistribution of the nanovesicles was investigated using Cy5.5-labeled formulations on SCC7 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mouse model by a small animal imaging system. As shown in Fig. S15, whole-body fluorescence imaging revealed that both Cy5.5@PNVs and Cy5.5@DPNVs gradually accumulated at the tumor site over time, with the most specific fluorescence signals observed at 24 h post-injection. In contrast, free Cy5.5 exhibited weaker tumor retention and faster systemic clearance. Ex vivo imaging of major organs further confirmed that Cy5.5@PNVs and Cy5.5@DPNVs displayed strong tumor accumulation profiles. Meanwhile, moderate fluorescence signals were observed in the liver, likely due to uptake by the reticuloendothelial system. These results indicate that both PNVs and DPNVs possess favorable tumor-targeting ability and prolonged circulation in vivo.

To determine whether the immunomodulatory effects of DOX@DPNVs observed in vitro could also be recapitulated in vivo, we next evaluated their therapeutic performance in an immunosuppressive murine SCC7 tumor model established in C3H mice, which is widely regarded as an immune-cold tumor model owing to its low lymphocyte infiltration and highly suppressive tumor microenvironment. Since immune-cold tumors are typically refractory to immune checkpoint blockade, the SCC7 model provides a suitable setting to test whether DOX@DPNVs can convert to an immune-inflamed state and achieve effective tumor control. When subcutaneous SCC7 tumors reached about 50 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to receive PBS, αPD-L1,

PNVs, DOX, DOX@PNVs or DOX@DPNVs. After 21 days of treatment, all mice were sacrificed, and main tissues (i.e., tumors, hearts, lungs, spleen, kidneys, and livers) were collected for histological analysis (Fig. 5a). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs (e.g., lungs, kidneys, livers, spleens and hearts) showed no obvious pathological abnormalities or physiological morphological changes in any of the treatment groups (Fig. S16). Furthermore, to evaluate the long term safety profile (including the potential risk of tumorigenicity upon administration) of DOX@DPNVs, we additionally administered the SCC7 cancer cells and different nanoformulations (i.e., PNVs, DOX@PNVs, DOX@DPNVs) into C3H mice by intravenous injection, and the mice were euthanized on day 54 post-administration for H&E staining of main organs and serum biochemical analysis. Of note, mice in the SCC7 cell-injected group were euthanized on day 14 due to the appearance of significant tumor nodes in lungs (Fig. S17a), indicating severe tumor metastasis that precluded further observation. In marked contrast, no apparent tissue damage (Fig. S17a) or abnormalities in liver, kidney or cardiac injury-related indicators (ALT, AST, ALB, ALP, BUN, CR, GLU, CK, LDH) (Fig. S17b) were detected in NVs-based nanoformulation-treated groups. In addition, to observe the tumorigenicity directly, we also injected SCC7 cancer cells and different nanoformulations subcutaneously into the right back of immunodeficient BALB/c-nu/nu mice, which were monitored for a total of 6 weeks. As shown in Fig. S18, mice injected with SCC7 cells developed significant tumors (indicated by red circle), and had to be sacrificed after 4 weeks due to excessive tumor burden. Comparatively, none of the NVs-based nanoformulations were able to induce discernible tumors, demonstrating a low tumorigenic risk associated our employed nanovesicles. These findings conclusively demonstrate the long-term safety of DOX@DPNVs as well as its extremely weak tumorigenicity for biological use.

For in vivo therapeutic efficacy, tumor growth curves showed that both DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to other groups (Fig. 5b). These two groups exhibited the smallest tumors during the entire observation period, and the inhibition rate of tumor progression by DOX@DPNVs exceeded that of αPD-L1 or DOX alone, suggesting that the combination strategy of DOX@DPNVs provided a stronger therapeutic effect than either immune checkpoint blockade or ICD therapy alone. The superior anti-tumor efficacy of DOX@DPNVs was further corroborated by tumor images and weights at the endpoint, with a mean tumor weight of 241.6 mg, representing approximately one-third of that in the PBS group (Fig. 5c and d). Consistently, DOX@DPNVs achieved the highest tumor control rate (63.7%), followed by DOX@PNVs (58.3%), while αPD-L1 showed the weakest effect (Fig. 5e). It should be noted that although DOX@DPNVs exhibited the highest tumor control rate, the difference in tumor growth inhibition between DOX@DPNVs and DOX@PNVs was slight in some tumor inhibition metrics. This may be ascribed to that both DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs deliver the similar DOX payload and share the same tumor-derived membrane, which enables similar homologous targeting for eliciting similar early cytotoxic effects on tumor cells.

To investigate whether the tumor growth inhibition observed in the DOX@DPNVs group was associated with enhanced tumor cell death, histological analyses were performed. H&E staining of tumor sections showed extensive tissue necrosis, nuclear condensation, and fragmentation in tumors treated with DOX@DPNVs, in contrast to the dense and viable tumor cell morphology observed in the PBS and αPD-L1 groups (Fig. 5f). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining further confirmed abundant cell apoptosis, which displayed substantial green fluorescence indicative of DNA fragmentation, especially in the DOX@DPNVs group (Fig. 5g). Corresponding quantitative analysis showed that the apoptosis rate in this group reached 44.6%, which was significantly higher than that in the DOX (16.6%) and DOX@PNVs (35.5%) groups, thus verifying that DOX@DPNVs had a strong cell death induction capacity in vivo (Fig. 5h).

Since ICD is known to enhance antitumor immunity through DAMPs exposure and release, we next examined whether DOX@DPNVs induced immunogenic remodeling within tumor tissues. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of CRT (a hallmark of ICD) showed that the PBS, αPD-L1 and PNVs groups exhibited a small amount of brown staining, indicating that the tumor cells themselves expressed some level of CRT. The DOX group showed a modest increase, whereas the DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs groups exhibited extensive and intense brown staining, indicating strong CRT expression (Fig. 5i). Corresponding quantitation confirmed a significantly higher CRT density in both the DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs groups relative to the PBS group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5j). Furthermore, another four key DAMPs, including ATP, HMGB1, HSP70 and HSP90, in tumor tissues were also analyzed by ELISA (Fig. 5k-n). DOX@DPNVs significantly increased the levels of all four DAMPs compared to the PBS, PNVs or DOX groups, strongly suggesting that DOX@DPNVs effectively trigger ICD, thereby remodeling the immunogenicity of the tumor microenvironment.

DOX@DPNVs enhance systemic antitumor immune responses and long-term immune memory

Because ICD-mediated immune activation, together with immune checkpoint blockade, can generate sustained antitumor immunity rather than short-term tumor control, we next investigated whether DOX@DPNVs contribute to long-lasting antitumor immune responses. Over a 60-day period, SCC7 tumor-bearing mice were monitored for overall survival and immune response following different treatments (Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. 6b, the DOX@PNVs and DOX@DPNVs groups achieved median survival times of 47 and 57 days, respectively, both significantly outperforming the PBS group (23 days), with the latter extending survival more than two-fold. Notably, the DOX@DPNVs group, which received DOX@PNVs combined with self-coupled DAMPs, exhibited an improved survival benefit over the DOX@PNVs group (p = 0.0204), confirming a synergistic enhancement of the therapeutic effect rather than a simple additive effect. Individual tumor growth curves supported these findings, with DOX@DPNVs-treated mice showing much slower tumor progression than the other groups (Fig. 6c-h). Notably, all tumors in the DOX@DPNVs group remained under control following the dosing period, indicating not only potent tumor inhibition but also the induction of effective immune surveillance capable of restraining tumor progression.

To explore the underlying immune mechanisms, FC analysis was used to assess DCs maturation and T cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. As shown in Fig. 6i and j, DOX@DPNVs significantly increased the proportion of total CD11c⁺ DCs and mature CD80⁺CD86⁺ DCs subpopulations by 24.2% and 4.91%, respectively,

compared with PBS-treated mice, indicating an enhanced antigen presentation capability. Meanwhile, a strong T-cell recruitment was observed, with intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration enhanced by 3.2-fold (DOX@DPNVs group vs. PBS group), resulting in a markedly higher CD8⁺/CD4⁺ T-cell ratio (Fig. 6k and l). To further determine whether the activated CD8⁺ T cells maintained functionally competent rather than becoming exhausted, the expression of PD-1, a well-established exhaustion marker, on CD8⁺ T cells was assessed (Fig. 6m and n). As a result, DOX@DPNVs treatment reduced PD-1 expression to 49.6% compared with 76.2% in the PBS group, suggesting that cytotoxic T cells maintained their immunocompetent phenotype. These results suggest that DOX@DPNVs effectively promoted DC maturation, enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and mitigated T-cell exhaustion in vivo, thereby remodeling the tumor microenvironment toward an immune-activated state.

To determine whether the intratumoral immune activation extended to the systemic level, cytokines in peripheral blood were examined. Consistent with the intratumoral findings, systemic cytokine analysis showed that blood IFN-γ (Fig. 6o) and TNF-α (Fig. 6p) levels were elevated in the DOX@DPNVs group, both of which were more than 2-fold of those in the PBS group, confirming that the immunomodulatory effects of DOX@DPNVs were not confined to the tumor site but also triggered a strong activation of systemic immune responses. This systemic activation was further evidenced by analysis of spleen-derived lymphocytes. The DOX@DPNVs group showed an increased percentage of CD8⁺ T cells, which was 1.9-fold of that in the PBS group (Fig. 6q), along with an elevated CD8⁺/CD4⁺ ratio by 1.0 increase compared with the PBS group (Fig. 6r). This shift reflects a systemic cytotoxic T-cell-dominant immune profile, in line with enhanced tumor-killing capacity. More importantly, DOX@DPNVs remarkably enhanced the generation of memory T cells (CD44⁺CD62L−) (Fig. 6s and t), a cell population critical for long-term tumor control and prevention of recurrence. Specifically, the proportion of memory T cells in the DOX@DPNVs group reached 35.5%, representing a 17.3% increase relative to the PBS group. To further clarify whether the therapeutic benefit of DOX@DPNVs depended on spatiotemporal coordination manner, we compared the proportion of PD-1⁺CD38high CD8⁺ T cells in peripheral blood based on time and sequence difference in administrating different formulations (Fig. S19a). In the temporal dimension, simultaneous PD-1 blockade and DOX treatment may cause PD-1 intervention to occur before sufficient ICD-mediated immune priming, because DAMPs release requires 3–6 days. Consistently, PNVs administered 3 days before DOX@DNVs (DAMPs-incorporated and DOX-loaded NVs without PD-1 overexpressing) showed limited effect, with the proportion of PD-1⁺CD38high CD8⁺ T cells remaining close to the PBS group (37.80%, Fig. S19b and S19c), which was comparable with the reported results [42, 43]. In contrast, PNVs administered 3 days after DOX@DNVs reduced this population to 23.43%, suggesting that PD-1 blockade is more effective after DAMP-mediated immune stimulation has been initiated. In the spatial dimension, DOX@DPNVs outperformed all separately administered regimens, including the simultaneous PNVs + DOX@DNVs group (p = 0.0221), confirming that co-delivery by a single nanoplatform ensures both signals act on the same target cells within the same immune microenvironment with optimal efficiency.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DOX@DPNVs elicit not only direct tumor suppression, but also establish a robust and durable systemic anti-immune response characterized by robust DCs maturation, enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, reduced T-cell exhaustion, and induction of persistent immune memory. These features make DOX@DPNVs a powerful, integrated nanotherapeutic platform with high potential for achieving long-term tumor control and recurrence prevention.

Despite these encouraging findings, the therapeutic efficacy of DOX@DPNVs may still be limited by the aggressive nature of the SCC7 tumor model and its highly immunosuppressive microenvironment. Future studies may further improve the therapeutic performance in several ways. For example, combining current approach with additional immunomodulatory treatments, such as cytokine therapy and other immune agonists/adjuvants, may further amplify antitumor responses. Moreover, investigating combination strategies with conventional therapies like radiotherapy and photothermal therapy may yield synergistic effects and further improve overall treatment outcomes. The vesicle design itself could also be optimized by adjusting parameters including drug loading levels, vesicle dose, and treatment scheduling to improve therapeutic efficiency. In addition, evaluating the nanoplatform in tumor models with different immune profiles, including less aggressive subcutaneous tumors and highly immunosuppressive orthotopic tumors, would provide deeper insight into its therapeutic potential under diverse biological conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a bioengineered nanointegrator (DOX@DPNVs) that co-delivers DOX and immune-modulating signals (i.e., DAMPs) via NVs derived from PD-1-overexpressing/PD-L1-knockout tumor cells. This platform synergizes targeted chemotherapy, potent ICD and “dual-throttle” immune checkpoint blockade to enhance cancer immunotherapy. As a result, DOX@DPNVs significantly inhibited tumor progression and prolonged survival by enhancing dendritic cell maturation, cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and long-term immune memory, demonstrating considerable therapeutic potential. While the preparation of DOX@DPNVs involves multiple techniques, its core assembly is streamlined. Following genetic engineering and ICD induction, the multicomponent mixture, including DAMPs, cellular membranes, DOX, as well as SMCC, was co-processed via sonication/extrusion in a single, one-pot operation. This integrated approach eliminates the multiple purification steps typical for conventional nanomedicines. Built on well-established technologies like genetic engineering, membrane extrusion and bioconjugation, the process is reproducible and controllable at the research scale. Although industrial scalability and regulatory challenges remain, the DOX@DPNVs serves as a proof-of-concept platform demonstrating the power of combining multiple immunomodulatory functions (homologous targeting, ICD-derived DAMPs, checkpoint blockade) into a single nanocarrier. From a translational perspective, future studies could leverage master cell banks and simpler engineering approaches, such as mRNA transfection and transient protein engineering, which offer a safer alternative to viral transduction for clinical applications.
