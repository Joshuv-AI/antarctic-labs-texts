Pediatric Floor of Mouth Lipoma: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Javeria Awan, Sundas Masood, Amna Javaid, Fatima Shahid, Rahmat Gul Omarzai, Abdur Rehman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights the rare occurrence of a floor‐of‐mouth lipoma in a pediatric patient, emphasizing the importance of including lipoma in the differential diagnosis of oral masses. Magnetic resonance imaging facilitates accurate preoperative characterization, and complete surgical excision ensures excellent outcomes with minimal recurrence risk in anatomically complex regions.

Introduction

Lipomas, composed of mature adipocytes, are the most common benign soft tissue tumors. Although lipomas can arise in any location containing fat tissue, their occurrence in the oral cavity is relatively infrequent, accounting for approximately 1%–4% of all benign oral tumors [1, 2]. Within the oral cavity, the buccal mucosa is the most frequently affected site, followed by the tongue and the floor of the mouth [3, 4]. Lipomas of the floor of the mouth account for approximately 10.2% of all oral lipomas [5]. While this proportion is not uncommon among oral lipomas overall, their occurrence in pediatric patients is exceedingly rare, with only isolated cases reported in the literature [4].

Oral lipomas typically present as asymptomatic, slow‐growing, soft masses that may interfere with mastication, speech, or swallowing when large [6]. The etiology of oral lipomas remains unclear, although associations with mechanical factors, inflammation, obesity, radiation, and mucosal infections have been suggested [7]. Histologically, lipomas are classified into several subtypes, including conventional lipoma, fibrolipoma, spindle cell lipoma, and others, based on their cellular composition [8].

The diagnosis of oral lipoma is usually made through clinical examination and confirmed by histopathological analysis following surgical excision, which is the treatment of choice due to the low recurrence rate of 1%–4% [9, 10]. Imaging studies such as ultrasonography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be employed to delineate the extent of the lesion, particularly for larger or deeper‐seated tumors [11, 12].

In pediatric patients, oral lipomas are exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature [13]. The case presented herein describes a rare instance of a floor‐of‐mouth lipoma in a 7‐year‐old child, underscoring the necessity of including this entity in the differential diagnosis of pediatric oral masses, and ensuring appropriate surgical management to prevent recurrence and functional impairment.

Case Presentation

A 7‐year‐old male presented to the outpatient Department of Otolaryngology, Rawalpindi Teaching Hospital, Rawalpindi with a painless mass on the right side of the floor of the mouth, which had been progressively enlarging over the past 3 years. The mass resulted in tongue displacement with associated speech impairment and altered phonation. No dysphagia, respiratory distress, snoring, or nocturnal apnea was reported.

Physical examination revealed an elongated facial contour with visible bulging in the bilateral cheeks, submandibular, and sublingual regions. Intraorally, a pinkish, soft, mobile, non‐tender mass without transillumination, measuring 3.0 × 3.0 × 4.0 cm, was observed in the right floor of the mouth, displacing the tongue to the left (Figure 1). Tongue mobility remained intact, and the overlying mucosa showed no signs of inflammation or ulceration. The remainder of the otolaryngological and systemic examination was unremarkable.

Methods

MRI of the face revealed a well‐defined, hyperintense lesion on T1‐ and T2‐weighted imaging, located in the ventral floor of the mouth and extending into the right submandibular space (Figure 2). The lesion demonstrated preserved interfaces with adjacent mylohyoid and genioglossus muscles, without evidence of diffusion restriction or post‐contrast enhancement, consistent with a lipoma.

Axial (left) and coronal (right) MRI images of the face showing a well‐defined, hyperintense lesion (arrow) in the ventral floor of the mouth, extending into the right submandibular space, consistent with a lipoma.

Surgical excision was performed under general anesthesia. A midline horizontal mucosal incision between the sublingual caruncles was made, and the overlying mucosa was carefully dissected laterally to expose the posterior margin of the mass. Blunt dissection revealed a soft, yellowish, lobulated mass with a surrounding capsule, characteristic of adipose tissue and consistent with a classical lipoma (Figure 3).

Intraoperative view of the yellowish, lobulated mass with its capsule, characteristic of a classical lipoma.

As the mass was located in the submucosal plane, critical structures, including Wharton's duct, the lingual nerve, and the sublingual gland, were preserved. After achieving hemostasis, an absorbable sponge was placed to fill the defect, and the mucosal layers were closed with absorbable sutures (Figure 4).

Postoperative view of the floor of the mouth after mass excision, showing the negative space (left) and the closure with absorbable sutures (right).

Results and Conclusion

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the second day. At the one‐week follow‐up, significant improvement in speech was noted. Histology showed mature, uniform adipocytes organized into lobular architecture within a thin fibrous capsule, confirming a classical lipoma. The patient remained symptom‐free and without recurrence at the two‐year follow‐up.

This case underscores the importance of considering lipoma in the differential diagnosis of pediatric oral masses and highlights the role of MRI in surgical planning. In our scenario, complete excision provided excellent long‐term outcomes and should always be considered first in similar cases.

Discussion

Floor‐of‐mouth lipomas in pediatric patients are exceedingly uncommon. While lipomas are common soft tissue tumors in adults, their incidence in the oral cavity is low, and pediatric cases are even rarer still [4]. A review of 58 oral lipomas found only one instance in a patient under 10 years, highlighting the unusual nature of this presentation [8]. The floor of the mouth's rarity as a site, compared to more frequent locations like the buccal mucosa or tongue, further amplifies the uniqueness [5]. This anatomical location poses potential risks in a pediatric patient, where even a small mass could impact speech, swallowing, or airway function if growth progresses unchecked [6]. In our case, early diagnosis likely mitigated symptom progression. Prompt recognition is essential to avoid functional complications in pediatric patients.

Diagnosing a floor‐of‐mouth mass in a child is fraught with challenges due to its nonspecific presentation and broad differential diagnosis. Oral lipomas typically manifest as slow‐growing, painless, soft, and mobile masses with intact mucosa, as observed here (Figure 1) [4]. Yet, these features overlap with numerous other conditions, necessitating a systematic approach to rule out alternatives [7]. The differential diagnosis includes ranula, dermoid cyst, venolymphatic malformation, thyroglossal duct cyst, foregut duplication cyst, teratoma, and malignancies like rhabdomyosarcoma [14, 15]. Each entity requires distinct diagnostic and therapeutic considerations, making accurate identification critical.

Imaging, particularly MRI, was instrumental in this case. It revealed a well‐defined, homogenous, hyperintense mass on T1‐ and T2‐weighted sequences, consistent with adipose tissue, and no invasion into adjacent structures (Figure 2) [8, 9]. This profile distinguished the lipoma from fluid‐filled cysts (e.g., ranula), vascular lesions, or heterogeneous tumors (e.g., teratoma) [14]. The MRI's role in confirming the diagnosis and guiding surgical planning is crucial, particularly in a pediatric context where minimizing invasive procedures is essential [16].

Complete surgical excision is the gold standard for oral lipomas, offering a cure with a recurrence rate of 1%–2% [10, 13]. However, the floor of the mouth presents unique surgical challenges due to its dense anatomy, including the sublingual gland, Wharton's duct, lingual nerve, and hypoglossal nerve [16]. In a child, these structures are smaller and more delicate, heightening the need for precision to preserve function during growth [17]. Here, a midline horizontal incision and blunt dissection successfully removed the encapsulated lipoma (Figure 3), avoiding damage to adjacent structures. The uneventful recovery and excellent healing at the 2‐year follow‐up reflect the efficacy of this approach, aligning with pediatric surgical principles prioritizing functional preservation [16].

Histopathology confirmed a classical lipoma, revealing mature adipocytes in lobules within a thin fibrous capsule, devoid of atypia. This aligns with most oral lipomas, which are classical rather than variants like spindle cell or angiolipoma [17]. Although histological confirmation was obtained, we were unable to provide photomicrographs because the examination was conducted at an external laboratory without access to imaging facilities. This is a limitation of the present report, though the diagnostic certainty remains unaffected.

The benign nature excludes rare malignancies like liposarcoma, a critical consideration despite its rarity in children [14]. The prognosis is excellent post‐excision, with no recurrence at 2 years, consistent with the literature [10]. Long‐term follow‐up remains advisable in pediatric cases to monitor for developmental impacts, though recurrence is unlikely [13].

This case contributes to the limited literature on pediatric oral lipomas by emphasizing diagnostic challenges and surgical considerations. It supports the role of imaging and histopathology in confirming diagnosis and ensuring successful outcomes [18, 19].
