Cocaine-Provoked Pulmonary Vein Thrombosis
Authors: Hardik Patel, Greg Flaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery embolism (CAE) is an underrecognized cause of myocardial infarction, particularly in patients with limited coronary atherosclerosis. Pulmonary vein thrombosis (PVT) as an embolic source of CAE has rarely been described.Case SummaryA middle-aged female with known active cocaine use and Factor V Leiden mutation presented following out-of-hospital ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest. Coronary angiography revealed thrombotic left circumflex artery occlusion without atherosclerosis. Computed tomography identified a nonocclusive left superior pulmonary vein thrombus as a possible embolic source. Drug-eluting stent implantation and apixaban were initiated; ticagrelor was de-escalated to clopidogrel given anticoagulation. Thrombus resolved at 12 weeks.DiscussionCocaine-provoked PVT causing CAE in inherited thrombophilia is a rare mechanism requiring embolic source evaluation and tailored management.Take-Home MessagesIn patients with cryptogenic CAE, cocaine use with inherited thrombophilia should prompt PVT evaluation. When post–percutaneous coronary intervention antiplatelet therapy requires anticoagulation, P2Y12 de-escalation to clopidogrel reduces bleeding risk.

History of Presentation

A middle-aged female presented following a witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest after developing left-sided chest and arm pain. Bystander CPR was initiated immediately following her collapse. EMS found the patient in VF and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved after continued cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillation. The patient was intubated in the field.Take-Home Messages•Cocaine use combined with inherited thrombophilia exerts synergistic prothrombotic effects that can provoke pulmonary vein thrombosis and coronary artery embolism—a rare but life-threatening mechanism that warrants systematic embolic source evaluation including pulmonary vein imaging in patients with cryptogenic CAE and minimal atherosclerosis.•When post-PCI antiplatelet therapy requires concurrent anticoagulation, de-escalation of P2Y12 inhibitor potency to clopidogrel reduces bleeding risk without compromising ischemic protection.

On arrival, the patient was in cardiogenic shock and unresponsive but moving all extremities spontaneously. Vital signs included blood pressure 82/44 mm Hg, heart rate 105 beats/min, and SpO2 100% on mechanical ventilation. Pupils were equal and reactive. Cardiovascular examination revealed regular rate and rhythm without murmurs, with intact peripheral pulses. Post-ROSC ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with possible left atrial enlargement and nonspecific ST depression. Urine toxicology was positive for cocaine and cannabinoids; benzodiazepines were detected consistent with EMS administration. Serum high-sensitivity troponin on arrival was 622 ng/L and peaked at 3,515 ng/L. Serum lactate was 5.7 mmol/L, which normalized following fluid resuscitation.

Past Medical History

Past medical history was notable for known cocaine and tobacco use. She had no prior cardiovascular history and was not taking antithrombotic or anticoagulant medications at presentation. She did not take oral contraceptives. Family history was significant for coronary artery disease and Factor V Leiden mutation.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for VF cardiac arrest in this patient included 1) acute myocardial infarction secondary to atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion, 2) cocaine-induced coronary vasospasm with superimposed thrombosis, 3) coronary artery embolism (CAE) from a cardiac or vascular embolic source, and 4) primary arrhythmia.

Investigations

The post-ROSC ECG demonstrated nonspecific ST depression without ST elevation, precluding a definitive diagnosis of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction at the time of initial evaluation. In a patient without known cardiac history presenting with VF arrest and a nondiagnostic ECG, pulmonary embolism was considered a plausible precipitant. Therefore, to evaluate for pulmonary embolism as a precipitant of VF arrest, (CT) pulmonary angiography (Figure 1A) was performed in the emergency department and identified a nonocclusive filling defect within the left superior pulmonary vein (LSPV) consistent with thrombus, without pulmonary embolism. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the head and neck showed a filling defect raising concern for nonocclusive dural venous sinus thrombosis. Transthoracic echocardiography showed an ejection fraction of 47% with basal septal akinesia and mild-to-moderate mitral regurgitation; no intracardiac thrombus or vegetations were identified. Blood cultures showed no growth at 5 days. Continuous telemetry revealed no atrial fibrillation. CT of the abdomen and pelvis and bilateral lower extremity venous duplex ultrasound demonstrated no acute deep venous thrombosis. Given concern for dural venous sinus thrombosis, dedicated brain magnetic resonance venography performed on day 5 demonstrated resolution of the previously described defect with patent dural venous sinuses and no acute intracranial findings. Hypercoagulable workup confirmed heterozygous Factor V Leiden mutation; anticardiolipin immunoglobulin G and beta-2 glycoprotein antibodies were negative.

Management

The culprit mid-LCx lesion crossed readily with a Runthrough NS workhorse guidewire, and balloon predilatation with a 2.0 × 15-mm Emerge balloon promptly restored TIMI 3 flow. A 2.5 × 16 mm Synergy everolimus-eluting stent was deployed under intravascular ultrasound guidance, confirming excellent stent expansion and apposition. Final angiography demonstrated TIMI 3 flow, 0% residual stenosis, and no edge dissection or perforation (Figure 2). Left ventricular end-diastolic pressure was elevated at 25 mm Hg.

Cangrelor infusion and systemic heparin were administered periprocedurally. Ticagrelor 180 mg and aspirin 81 mg were initiated postprocedure. Given the concurrent LSPV thrombus requiring anticoagulation, ticagrelor was de-escalated to clopidogrel, and apixaban was initiated. Aspirin was discontinued at 2 weeks; dual therapy with clopidogrel and apixaban was continued.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged in stable neurological and hemodynamic condition on apixaban and clopidogrel, along with guideline-directed medical therapy for mild cardiomyopathy, with cardiac rehabilitation referral. She received counseling on complete cessation of cocaine and tobacco use. Repeat CTA of the chest at 12 weeks (Figure 1B) confirmed complete resolution of the LSPV thrombus with no new venous thrombosis. Apixaban was discontinued after 6 months given the provoked nature of the thrombotic event with a modifiable precipitant. At that point, aspirin 81 mg was reintroduced alongside clopidogrel to complete a total of 12 months of dual-antiplatelet therapy from the index percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), consistent with guideline-directed management following drug-eluting stent implantation.

Discussion

CAE accounts for approximately 2.9% of acute myocardial infarctions and remains frequently underdiagnosed, particularly in young patients with limited coronary atherosclerosis.1,2 A diagnosis of CAE was established using the Shibata criteria, with 1 major criterion—angiographic evidence of coronary thrombosis without atherosclerotic components—and 3 minor criteria: coronary stenosis <25% outside the culprit lesion, an identifiable embolic source on CT, and a hypercoagulable state.1 Consistent with these criteria, the mid-LCx lesion crossed readily with a workhorse guidewire, responded promptly to balloon dilation, and demonstrated no significant atherosclerosis in the remaining coronary vessels.

Pulmonary vein thrombosis is a rare thromboembolic disease with significant potential for arterial embolization, most commonly reported in the setting of malignancy, pulmonary resection, lung transplantation, or radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation.3 Cocaine exerts multiple prothrombotic effects including platelet activation, endothelial injury, and coronary vasospasm, predisposing susceptible individuals to venous and arterial thrombotic events.4 Factor V Leiden heterozygosity confers a 5- to 8-fold increased risk of venous thromboembolism and may potentiate cocaine-related coagulopathy.5 The convergence of an inherited thrombophilia and an acquired procoagulant stimulus represents a clinically important mechanism in young patients presenting with cryptogenic myocardial infarction.

The proposed sequence is as follows: cocaine use in the setting of Factor V Leiden heterozygosity provoked LSPV thrombus formation, with subsequent embolization to the mid-LCx producing total occlusion and VF arrest. Competing etiologies were systematically evaluated. Continuous telemetry demonstrated no atrial fibrillation. Echocardiography and CT examinations identified no intracardiac thrombus. Blood cultures were negative with no murmurs or vegetations on echocardiography, making infective endocarditis unlikely. A bubble study to exclude patent foramen ovale was not performed, and paradoxical embolism cannot be fully excluded. However, CT of the abdomen and pelvis and bilateral lower extremity venous duplex ultrasound demonstrated no deep venous thrombosis, and the identified LSPV thrombus represents a more direct embolic pathway, as pulmonary venous thrombi drain directly into the left atrium without requiring an intracardiac shunt. The initial imaging concern for dural venous sinus thrombosis, although not confirmed on magnetic resonance venography, further supported a systemic hypercoagulable state at presentation. Hypercoagulable workup was directed by a family history of heterozygous Factor V Leiden mutation with multiple thrombotic events. Factor V Leiden mutation was confirmed on polymerase chain reaction–based genetic testing. Anticardiolipin immunoglobulin G, beta-2 glycoprotein, and lupus anticoagulant were negative. Antithrombin testing was deferred given concurrent therapeutic heparin anticoagulation. Protein C, protein S, and prothrombin gene mutation testing were not performed; we acknowledge this as a limitation of the thrombophilia evaluation.

Antithrombotic management required reconciling dual-antiplatelet therapy following drug-eluting stent implantation with anticoagulation for the concurrent LSPV thrombus. Consistent with AUGUSTUS (Open-Label, 2 × 2 Factorial, Randomized, Controlled Clinical Trial to Evaluate the Safety of Apixaban vs Vitamin K Antagonist and Aspirin vs Aspirin Placebo in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Acute Coronary Syndrome and/or Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) data demonstrating reduced major bleeding without increase in ischemic events when less potent P2Y12 inhibition is combined with anticoagulation post percutaneous coronary intervention, ticagrelor was de-escalated to clopidogrel.6 Triple antithrombotic therapy was maintained for 2 weeks, then simplified to clopidogrel and apixaban, consistent with the 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Acute Coronary Syndrome Guideline recommending aspirin discontinuation 1 to 4 weeks after PCI in patients requiring long-term anticoagulation.7 Following apixaban discontinuation at 6 months, aspirin was reintroduced alongside clopidogrel to complete a total of 12 months of guideline-directed dual-antiplatelet therapy. Complete LSPV thrombus resolution at 12 weeks confirmed the efficacy of this approach.

Cocaine substantially increases the risk of death or recurrent myocardial infarction following PCI, with heightened 30-day and 1-year major cardiovascular event risk in cocaine-associated myocardial infarction compared with cocaine-negative controls.8 Complete cocaine cessation is therefore essential in this patient. Pulmonary vein imaging with CTA should be considered in patients with cryptogenic CAE and minimal atherosclerosis, particularly when cocaine use or inherited thrombophilia is present.

Conclusions

Cocaine-provoked pulmonary vein thrombosis causing CAE by established diagnostic criteria is a rare but life-threatening mechanism of myocardial infarction and cardiac arrest. This case highlights the additive thrombotic risk of cocaine use in the setting of inherited thrombophilia and underscores the importance of systematic embolic source evaluation—including pulmonary vein imaging—in patients with cryptogenic CAE and minimal atherosclerosis. Tailored antithrombotic management, including early P2Y12 de-escalation and sequential simplification of therapy, can reconcile competing ischemic and hemorrhagic risks in this complex clinical scenario.
