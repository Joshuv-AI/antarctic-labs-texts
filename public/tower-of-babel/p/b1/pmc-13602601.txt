Untreated dental caries complications, oral health-related quality of life, and dental service utilization among inmates in Southern Thailand
Authors: Manop Phongayukull, Narumanas Korwanich, Kanyarat Korwanich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInmates are recognized as a vulnerable population with limited access to healthcare and generally poorer oral health than the wider community, warranting focused investigation. This study, conducted at Takuapa District Prison, Thailand, aimed to assess oral health status and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) among inmates and to examine factors associated with dental service utilization.MethodsA total of 535 inmates underwent oral examinations and completed questionnaires, and descriptive statistics were used for this entire sample. Subsequently, logistic regression analysis was performed only on the 418 inmates who also completed the Oral Impacts on Daily Performance (OIDP) index.ResultsDental caries was present in 94.6% of inmates, with a mean DMFT score of 10.05 ± 7.23. Untreated caries affected 90.5% of participants (mean DT = 6.22 ± 5.50), while 58.9% presented with complications of untreated caries, with a mean PUFA score of 2.41 ± 3.61. Five inmates were edentulous, and among dentate inmates, 63.6% had periodontitis (CPI scores 3–4). Overall, 50.7% of inmates reported impaired OHRQoL, as assessed using the OIDP index, which was significantly associated with complications of untreated caries (PUFA). In addition, 48.6% of inmates reported access to dental services, which was also significantly associated with impaired OHRQoL.ConclusionInmates exhibit a high burden of untreated dental caries and periodontitis, which, along with limited healthcare access, significantly impairs their oral health-related quality of life. Implementing targeted oral health services and preventive programs within prisons is urgently needed to improve their well-being.Keywords: Inmates, Oral health status, Oral health-related quality of life

Introduction/objective

Oral health is an integral component of general health and quality of life, yet it is often overlooked in marginalized populations, particularly those who are incarcerated. Prisoners experience a disproportionately high burden of oral diseases, including dental caries, periodontal disease, tooth loss, and oral lesions, compared to the general population [1–3]. These conditions not only affect their masticatory function and speech but also impact self-esteem, mental health, and reintegration into society following incarceration [4–5].

Incarcerated populations experience distinct and complex oral health needs shaped by structural and social determinants, including socioeconomic disadvantage, limited health literacy, and a high prevalence of substance use and mental health conditions [6]. Studies across various countries including Finland [7], India [8], United Kingdom [9], and Brazil [10], consistently demonstrate high DMFT scores and poor periodontal status among prisoners. This substantial disease burden reflects both pre-existing behavioral risk exposures such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption and illicit drug use that often precede imprisonment [5], and systemic constraints within prison settings including limited access to preventive and curative oral health services overcrowding and under-resourced prison healthcare systems [11, 12].

The World Health Organization has identified oral health in prison settings as a pressing public health issue requiring integration into broader prison healthcare strategies. However, many correctional facilities worldwide continue to lack dedicated dental personnel, routine dental care, and oral health promotion programmes [12]. Since 2019, Thailand has implemented the nationwide “Good Health Good Heart” project, identifying oral health as a public health priority and improving prisoners’ access to dental care [13]. Within this framework, all prisons across Thailand was provided more intensive health care including dental services aiming better quality of life of prisoner population [14].

There is a growing recognition of oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) as an important outcome measure in correctional health research. Poor oral health has been shown to substantially reduce OHRQoL, particularly in domains such as physical pain, psychological discomfort, and social disability [3–5]. Despite this, few studies adopt a multidimensional approach when evaluating oral health programs in prisons, with most research remaining focused solely on clinical indices. Understanding both the clinical status of oral health and its impact on quality of life is therefore essential for identifying the needs of this population and for guiding targeted, sustainable improvements in care provision [15].

This study aimed to assess the oral health status, oral health behaviors, OHRQoL, and to analyze the factors associated with OHRQoL and dental service utilization among inmates. The findings are expected to enhance the evidence for prisoners’ oral healthcare planning and support public health strategies targeting underserved populations.

Materials and methods

Study design and Setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Takuapa District Prison, Thailand, a small correctional facility housing male and female inmates serving sentences of up to 10 years. The study was approved by the prison authorities, and all data collection procedures were carried out under the supervision and security regulations of prison staff.

Population, sample, and sampling method

All 621 inmates residing in Takuapa District Prison in April 2023 constituted the source population for this study, which spanned from April to October 2023. Using a total enumeration approach, all individuals who met the inclusion criteria (being a convicted inmate, present during data collection, and willing to participate) and were available during the study period, resulting in an initial sample of 535 inmates (498 males and 37 females). In the first stage (April–June 2023), oral examinations were conducted to collect clinical data. In the second stage (October 2023), 418 inmates (391 males and 27 females) were interviewed to assess OHRQoL. A total of 418 inmates completed both stages of data collection, as shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted between April and October 2023. In the first stage (April–June 2023), demographic characteristics, criminal offenses, oral health-related behaviors, and retrospective self-reported dental service utilization within the prison were collected using the Thai structured questionnaires adapted from the 8th Thailand National Oral Health Survey [16], administered by a trained dental nurse. Concurrently, all clinical oral examinations in this study were performed by one calibrated dentist (MP) using standard forms under dental light with a mobile dental unit, mouth mirror, and WHO probe. Clinical data recorded included dental caries (DMFT index) [17], periodontal status (Community Periodontal Index; CPI) [17], the presence of dentures, and other oral conditions (e.g., tooth gems). Furthermore, untreated caries with complications were evaluated using the PUFA index [18], which records the number of teeth with visible pulpal involvement (P/p), ulceration caused by dislocated tooth fragments (U/u), fistula (F/f), and abscess (A/a). The intra-examiner reliability for the clinical examinations was acceptable, with agreement rates of 86.05% for dental caries and 83.72% for periodontal status, yielding kappa coefficients of 0.85 and 0.71, respectively.

Subsequently, in the second stage (October 2023), oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) was assessed using the Thai version of the standardized eight-item Oral Impacts on Daily Performance (OIDP) questionnaire, which had been validated for use in the Thai population.15 This index specifically focused on oral impacts over the preceding six months. These face-to-face interviews were administered and recorded by the same dentist. Formal permission was obtained from prison authorities, and all data collection processes were facilitated by prison health volunteers.

Continuous variables, including age and incarceration duration, were dichotomized using a median split approach to ensure balanced group sizes and maximize statistical power within the multivariable models. This approach yielded specific sample cut-offs, such as 35 years for age and 18 months for incarceration length. For oral health impacts and clinical indicators, variables were categorized based on the presence or absence of the respective conditions. Specifically, Oral Impacts on Daily Performance (OIDP) was dichotomized into ‘no oral impact’ (OIDP = 0) and ‘having oral impacts’ (OIDP > 0). Similarly, dental caries (DT), missing teeth (MT), filled teeth (FT), and untreated caries complications (PUFA) were divided into absence (0) or presence (≥ 1). Lastly, periodontal status was dichotomized at a Community Periodontal Index (CPI) cut-off of CPI > 2 to distinguish between periodontitis and gingivitis or healthy status.

Data analysis

Data were double-entered for accuracy prior to analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 31. Descriptive statistics summarized the study findings. For inferential analysis, only participants with complete data from both stages were included, resulting in a final sample size of 418 participants. Initially, bivariate logistic regression was performed to estimate crude odds ratios (Crude OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables demonstrating statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) were subsequently entered into multivariable logistic regression models to determine adjusted odds ratios (aOR). The goodness-of-fit for both multivariable models was verified and confirmed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test (p > 0.05). Statistical significance for all analyses was set at p < 0.05.

Discussions

The present study demonstrated a substantial burden of dental diseases and distinct patterns of dental service utilization among inmates in Takuapa District Prison. Specifically, the inmates exhibited a mean of 6.22 ± 5.50 untreated teeth per person, with a PUFA prevalence of 59.8% in males and 45.9% in females, and periodontitis observed in 63.6% of the population. Regarding oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), only untreated caries complication (PUFA) was significantly associated with impaired OHRQoL. Furthermore, inmates who reported impaired OHRQoL and those with missing teeth were significantly more likely to utilize dental services during incarceration, whereas a negative association was found between periodontal disease or advanced untreated caries complications and service utilization.

Regarding oral health status, the mean of 6.22 ± 5.50 untreated teeth per person found in this study is higher than those reported in Finland (5 ± 5.1) [7], India (2.6 ± 2.9) [8], and the United Kingdom (2.47 ± 2.52) [9], but lower than in Brazil (11.06 ± 5.37) [10]. Additionally, the prevalence of PUFA among these inmates exceeded the 40% reported among female inmates in the United Kingdom [9], while the prevalence of periodontitis (63.6%) was drastically higher than that of the general working-age population in Thailand (32.6%) [19]. These findings indicate a substantial burden of dental diseases among inmates, consistent with international reports. As highlighted by the CareQuest Institute for Oral Health, incarcerated individuals generally exhibit higher rates of dental diseases compared with the general population, often worsened by restricted access to care due to limited resources and strict treatment policies within correctional facilities [2].

In terms of OHRQoL, which was conceptualized within a biopsychosocial framework using the OIDP interview [20], this study found that only untreated caries complication was significantly associated with impaired OHRQoL. This finding contrasts with studies in South Korea and India, which reported significant associations between general oral health status (such as dental caries and periodontal disease) and OHRQoL [21, 22]. However, it aligns with evidence from pediatric populations demonstrating that untreated advanced caries complications, measured by the PUFA/pufa index, are heavily associated with poorer OHRQoL [23, 24]. This suggests that in this population, advanced clinical consequences and pain drive subjective oral impacts more than the earlier stages of dental disease.

Concerning dental service utilization, inmates with impaired OHRQoL were significantly more likely to utilize dental services. This aligns with Andersen’s behavioral model where health service utilization depends on predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and need [14]. In structurally constrained prison contexts where enabling resources are limited, perceived need, particularly when oral conditions interfere with daily functioning, plays a decisive role in shaping care-seeking behavior on a voluntary basis [25, 26]. Conversely, the positive association between missing teeth and service utilization, alongside the negative association with periodontal disease, likely reflects service delivery practices driven by voluntary self-referral, where tooth extraction is the most accessible treatment. This is consistent with previous literature showing that high DMFT scores in prisoners are largely driven by missing teeth due to a systemic reliance on extractions rather than comprehensive, preventive, or restorative care [27].

The clinical and behavioral realities within this prison setting are further substantiated by our statistical modeling. Specifically, the discrepancies between Crude OR and adjusted OR (aOR) in Tables 5 and 6 reflect the impact of controlling for multivariate confounding factors. For instance, in Table 6, the effect of longer incarceration duration (> 18 months) attenuated from a significant crude OR of 2.22 (p < 0.001) to a non-significant aOR of 1.53 (p = 0.066). This indicates that the initial association was confounded by oral health status. Inmates with longer sentences accumulate more oral pathologies, which are the actual drivers for seeking care. Conversely, missing teeth (MT ≥ 1) exhibited a suppression effect, where its odds ratio increased from 5.02 to an aOR of 6.56 (p < 0.001) after adjustment. This demonstrates that once the distorting influences of other covariates were removed, missing teeth emerged as a powerful, independent predictor for prison dental service utilization.

Systemic and study design factors also shaped these utilization patterns. Because of the limitations of the cross-sectional design, the negative association between untreated caries complications (PUFA) and lower dental service utilization can be interpreted in two ways. First, it may indicate that advanced disease alone is insufficient to prompt care-seeking due to symptom-driven habits and systemic barriers [28–31]. Alternatively, the PUFA index captures the cumulative consequences of prolonged non-utilization rather than acting as an immediate trigger [17].

These utilization barriers persist despite the introduction of Thailand’s “Good Health Good Heart” program in 2019, which established a scheduled twice-monthly dental clinic by Takuapa Hospital. While this policy improved basic care access (extractions, restorations, scaling), complex procedures like third molar surgeries still require external referrals. Delivery remains constrained by workforce limitations and unclear budgetary allocation, leaving preventive and comprehensive restorative care insufficient.

This population-based study included all inmates during the study period, enhancing the comprehensiveness and representativeness of the findings. However, some health behaviors were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias, and its cross-sectional design limits causal inferences. Additionally, due to time constraints, the reliance on a single examiner, and strict prison protocols, data collection could not be completed in a single round as originally planned. Conducting the data collection at two distinct times created a gap between sessions, which consequently led to unavoidable participant dropout.

In the context of Takuapa District Prison, characterized by short-term incarceration and high inmate turnover, oral health services should prioritize interventions feasible within limited detention periods. Routine OIDP screening is recommended to identify inmates whose oral conditions substantially impair daily functioning and warrant timely care. Preventive efforts should focus on supporting self-care that can be sustained following release. Furthermore, the higher utilization among inmates with missing teeth indicates an unmet need for functional oral rehabilitation; hence, service planning should consider expanding access to basic prosthodontic care, such as removable dentures. Strengthening post-release referral pathways within the district health system is also essential to ensure continuity of care. Ultimately, an integrated approach combining clinical care, behavioral interventions, and policy-level strategies is required, and further research is warranted to examine the broader physical, social, and cultural contexts influencing oral health behaviors in prison settings.

Conclusion

These findings highlight that clinical disease status alone may not fully reflect the lived impact of oral health problems in this vulnerable population. Integrating OHRQoL measures alongside clinical assessment can enhance understanding of inmates’ daily functioning and support more responsive and equitable prison oral health service planning. Further research should explore environmental, social, and cultural contexts to inform the development of appropriate and sustainable prison oral health systems.
