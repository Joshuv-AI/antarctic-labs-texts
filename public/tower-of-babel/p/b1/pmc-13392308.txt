The long road to a rare diagnosis: A Malaysian case of childhood-onset progressive myoclonus, ataxia, and retinal dystrophy with biopsy features suggestive of mitochondrial dysfunction
Authors: Kishwen Kanna Yoga Ratnam, Jun Fai Yap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMyoclonus is a hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by sudden, involuntary jerks due to muscle contraction or brief lapses of muscular activity and may arise from diverse conditions. When accompanied by cerebellar dysfunction and retinal involvement, it suggests a heterogeneous spectrum of disorders, including mitochondrial, repeat-expansion, and other inherited neurogenetic disorders. In Malaysia, the diagnosis of rare diseases remains challenging because of phenotypic overlap and resource limitations. We illustrate the diagnostic dilemma of a possible mitochondrial disorder when definitive testing is not readily accessible.MethodsWe describe a woman in her early 40s with childhood-onset myoclonic jerks, progressive gait instability, cerebellar signs, and cognitive decline.ResultsOphthalmic assessment showed reduced visual acuity, optic disc pallor, and electroretinographic findings consistent with cone-rod dystrophy. Audiometry demonstrated bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. Brain computed tomography showed cerebral and cerebellar atrophy. Muscle biopsy revealed preserved architecture without ragged-red fibers, but oxidative enzyme histochemistry showed subsarcolemmal mitochondrial aggregates suggestive of mitochondrial dysfunction. Whole-exome sequencing did not identify a causative variant. Given the combination of cerebellar features and retinal dystrophy, a repeat-expansion disorder, particularly spinocerebellar ataxia type 7, remained a key differential diagnosis. Targeted repeat-expansion testing and mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid analysis were not performed because of patient-centered considerations. Despite disease progression, multidisciplinary follow-up was maintained, including structured physiotherapy and annual ophthalmologic surveillance.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of pragmatic care planning in resource-limited settings and maintaining comprehensive care for patients without a definitive molecular diagnosis. It also emphasizes the need to strengthen rare disease diagnostic pathways to support shared decision making.Keywords: Case report, cerebellar ataxia, cone-rod dystrophy, mitochondrial dysfunction, rare disease

Introduction

Myoclonus refers to a hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by sudden, involuntary jerks resulting from either muscle contractions or brief lapses of muscular activity. 1 This clinical sign may arise from a wide array of underlying neurologic or systemic conditions. 2 Myoclonic jerks accompanied by cerebellar dysfunction and retinal involvement represent an uncommon, heterogeneous neurodegenerative syndrome. The differential diagnosis is broad and may include mitochondrial encephalomyopathies (such as myoclonic epilepsy with ragged-red fibers (MERRF)–spectrum disorders), repeat-expansion disorders (notably spinocerebellar ataxia type 7 (SCA7)), and other inherited neurogenetic conditions that can produce overlapping neuro-ophthalmic signs. 3

Establishing a molecular diagnosis of mitochondrial disease requires careful alignment between phenotype and the testing modality. 4 Muscle biopsy may demonstrate features supportive of mitochondrial dysfunction but can be nonspecific, and normal routine markers (including serum lactate) do not exclude mitochondrial disease. 5 Importantly, whole-exome sequencing does not reliably exclude mitochondrial disease or repeat-expansion disorders, as it may not adequately capture mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) heteroplasmy or detect pathogenic trinucleotide repeat expansions. 6

Although individually uncommon, rare diseases such as mitochondrial disease collectively affect many individuals and families in Malaysia. Their complex clinical presentations pose challenges for healthcare practitioners in diagnosis and management.7–9 Local patients’ experiences highlight the ongoing gaps in accessibility to therapies such as enzyme replacement treatment. 10 In Malaysia and similar settings, diagnostic pathways for rare diseases are further shaped by the restricted availability of specialized tests, referral processes, and cost. 11 We present a rare case of myoclonus with ataxia and cone-rod dystrophy in which biopsy findings suggested mitochondrial dysfunction but whole-exome sequencing was nondiagnostic. We aimed to illustrate the diagnostic dilemma as well as the practical and counseling challenges encountered when definitive testing is unavailable. To this end, we collated longitudinal clinical data and archived reports from a multidisciplinary team to present this detailed case report.

Methods

The reporting of this study conforms to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 12 The completed CARE checklist is included with this submission. Written informed consent for treatment was obtained by the respective multidisciplinary teams as part of routine clinical care. Written informed consent for publication of this case report and any accompanying images was obtained from the patient's legal guardian. We have deidentified the patient's details. This case report was reviewed and approved by the National Medical Research Register of the Ministry of Health Malaysia (Approval Number: NMRR ID-26-00123-T4 K) on 6 February 2026.

Case report

The patient is a woman in her early 40 s, born to a nonconsanguineous family, who first presented to the pediatric clinic 30 years earlier with a long-standing history of progressive neurological dysfunction characterized by myoclonic jerks, cognitive regression, and cerebellar features. She was brought by her parents to multiple general practitioner clinics for evaluation; however, no conclusive treatment was provided until she was referred to a pediatrician at a tertiary hospital. She had an unremarkable antenatal, perinatal, and early developmental history. There was no significant family history of neurological disease. She was reportedly well until the age of 4 years, when she developed exercise-related leg cramps. At 7 years of age, she developed hand tremors that interfered with fine motor activities, such as piano playing. Subsequently, she experienced a gradual decline in academic performance over the following decade, accompanied by increasing clumsiness, impaired balance, frequent falls, and difficulty performing activities of daily living. Visual symptoms included difficulty reading small print and reduced vision in low-light conditions (nyctalopia). During early adolescence, her speech became mildly slurred, and learning difficulties with impaired long-term memory were noted.

Notably, the patient received a renal allograft when she was admitted to a tertiary hospital in India, in late September 2007. She had end-stage renal disease (ESRD) since her early 20s and was on continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis from 2006 following a short period of hemodialysis, which was discontinued because of vascular access difficulties. Her underlying renal diagnosis was presumed to be chronic interstitial nephritis, a nonspecific clinicopathologic term describing tubulointerstitial injury when the exact etiology is not clearly identifiable at an advanced stage. 13

On examination, she was of normal build but appeared short in stature. Neurological assessment revealed poor tandem gait, dysmetria (bilateral past-pointing), intention tremor, and mild dysarthria. Muscle strength, tone, and deep tendon reflexes were normal, with flexor plantar responses. There was no ptosis. Fundoscopic examination showed bilateral optic disc pallor, but no obvious pigmentary retinopathy was observed. Cognitive evaluation demonstrated preserved immediate recall with impaired long-term memory.

Routine laboratory investigations, including full blood count, electrolytes, liver function tests, serum lactate, ammonia, and bicarbonate, were within normal limits. Electrocardiography revealed no cardiac arrhythmia. Neurophysiological testing revealed prolonged P100 latency on visual evoked potentials, and electroretinography demonstrated features consistent with cone-rod dystrophy. Pure-tone audiometry revealed bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. Computed tomography of the brain showed cerebral and cerebellar atrophy. Electroencephalography demonstrated intermittent generalized spike discharges and spike-and-wave complexes, accompanied by diffuse background slowing. Muscle biopsy showed preserved fascicular architecture without classic ragged-red fibers on hematoxylin and eosin (Figure 1) and modified Gomori trichrome staining (Figure 2). However, succinate dehydrogenase (Figure 3) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide tetrazolium reductase (Figure 4) staining demonstrated subsarcolemmal mitochondrial aggregates.

Whole-exome sequencing did not identify any clinically significant variants. Although the muscle histochemistry findings support mitochondrial dysfunction, they are not diagnostic of a specific mitochondrial syndrome in isolation. Given the presence of cerebellar signs and electrophysiological evidence of cone-rod dystrophy, a repeat-expansion disorder (particularly SCA7) remained an important alternative diagnosis alongside mitochondrial encephalomyopathies (including MERRF-spectrum disorders).

Further testing (mitochondrial DNA analysis and trinucleotide repeat-expansion testing) was recommended to the patient and her family, with counseling that a molecular diagnosis can inform prognosis, surveillance strategies, family risk assessment, and medication safety, even in the absence of curative therapy. At the time of writing, definitive testing had not been completed because of limited access, cost considerations, and patient-centered prioritization of symptomatic management. She was treated with antiseizure medications (500 mg oral sodium valproate 4 times per week, 25/50 mg lamotrigine twice daily, and 500 mg levetiracetam once daily), achieving good seizure control. Nevertheless, her neurological disability progressed, requiring long-term physiotherapy and rehabilitation. Her renal transplantation history and long-term immunosuppression were managed concurrently, with ongoing annual ophthalmic care for visual complications.

Beyond diagnostic classification, this case highlights how the diagnostic journey of rare neurogenetic disorders is influenced by continuity of follow-up and access to rehabilitation services. These contextual factors were observed clinically in this patient and appeared to shape the overall care pathway. However, their impact on functional outcomes was not formally assessed in this case report. Whether and to what extent limitations in healthcare accessibility affect long-term functional trajectories remain to be elucidated using validated outcome measures in future studies.

Discussion

The coexistence of cerebellar ataxia with cone-rod retinal dystrophy is strongly suggestive of a repeat-expansion ataxia, particularly SCA7, and should be foregrounded as a leading alternative diagnosis rather than treated as a minor possibility. Nonetheless, this presentation of childhood-onset progressive myoclonus with cerebellar ataxia, cognitive involvement, sensorineural hearing loss, and cone-rod dystrophy is compatible with several rare neurogenetic conditions. A MERRF-spectrum disorder is considered less likely given the absence of ragged-red fibers on muscle biopsy and the lack of classic systemic features such as generalized epilepsy, exercise intolerance, or cardiomyopathy. Although muscle histochemistry demonstrated features suggestive of mitochondrial dysfunction, the absence of ragged-red fibers and otherwise normal routine markers neither confirms nor excludes a mitochondrial etiology. In this context of diagnostic uncertainty, together with cone-rod dystrophy, a repeat-expansion disorder such as SCA7 remains a particularly important consideration. Although definitive molecular confirmation was not achieved, maintaining a focused differential diagnosis is clinically valuable, as it may still guide supportive management rather than relying solely on generic symptom-based treatment.

SCA7 presents a diagnostic dilemma because its clinical phenotype (cerebellar ataxia, cone-rod dystrophy, and sometimes muscle weakness) strongly overlaps with mitochondrial disorders. Although SCA7 is technically a polyglutamine repeat-expansion disease caused by mutations in the ataxin-7 gene, recent animal research indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from decreased nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide could be a defining feature of its pathogenesis. 14 Therefore, accurate diagnosis can be challenging, and genetic testing is essential to distinguish conditions such as SCA7 from other causes of optic atrophy. 15 Nonetheless, there is often a low index of suspicion for genetic disorders at the general practitioner level before referral to a pediatrician, which may contribute to the initial diagnostic dilemma. In addition, the possibility of systemic involvement further broadens the differential diagnosis. For instance, the kidney involvement may be part of an underlying genetic disorder. Mitochondrial dysfunction is known to play a critical role in the pathogenesis of ESRD because of the kidney's high energy demand and rich mitochondrial content. 16 As a highly metabolically active organ, the kidney is particularly vulnerable to defects in cellular energy production. Accordingly, the renal manifestation in this patient was interpreted within the broader context of a possible mitochondrial disorder. However, much of the patient's prior renal history from treatment in India could not be retrieved because of limitations in historical recordkeeping.

Given the indolent early course and nonspecific initial features, the initial evaluation was symptom-focused. A comprehensive diagnostic assessment was initiated in early adolescence after scholastic slowing and functional decline suggested an underlying progressive disorder. A central learning point is that whole-exome sequencing does not exclude the most plausible genetic mechanisms in this phenotype. Whole-exome sequencing is not designed to detect pathogenic repeat expansions and may not adequately evaluate mitochondrial DNA heteroplasmy. 17 Thus, targeted repeat-expansion testing and mitochondrial DNA analysis remain essential to complete the diagnostic evaluation.18,19 In settings where these investigations are limited by availability, referral pathways, and cost, iterative counseling and shared decision making become part of the diagnostic pathway itself. Even when definitive genetic confirmation is pending or deferred, management should remain pragmatic and safety-oriented, including multidisciplinary rehabilitation, ophthalmic surveillance, and careful review of antiseizure therapy, given that some metabolic diagnoses carry medication-specific risks. For example, although valproate should be avoided in patients with high-risk mitochondrial subgroups, particularly polymerase gamma-related conditions, its universal exclusion in all mitochondrial diseases may not be evidence-based and could deny patients access to an otherwise relatively inexpensive and cost-effective therapy. 20

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, there was incomplete availability of detailed historical clinical information because the patient's initial presentation occurred nearly 3 decades ago. Consequently, several aspects of the early disease course, including the characteristics of the myoclonus, progression of ataxia, and severity of cognitive, visual, and hearing impairment, could not be reliably reconstructed from the available medical records or recalled by the patient's family. Second, as a single case report, the findings are not generalizable to broader populations, although they provide useful illustrative value for similar diagnostic scenarios. Finally, the absence of a confirmed genetic diagnosis (including the lack of mitochondrial DNA analysis and ataxin-7 repeat-expansion testing to support a definitive diagnosis of SCA7) limits definitive genotype–phenotype correlation, reinforcing the broader challenge of diagnostic uncertainty highlighted in this report.

Conclusion

This case describes myoclonic jerks with cerebellar signs and cone-rod dystrophy in which muscle histochemistry supported mitochondrial dysfunction, but a definitive molecular diagnosis was not established. Although the histopathological findings were suggestive of mitochondrial abnormalities, they are not sufficient on their own to confirm a primary mitochondrial disorder without supportive molecular evidence. The absence of confirmatory genetic testing, such as mitochondrial DNA analysis or repeat-expansion testing, emphasizes the inherent limitations of interpreting tissue-based findings in isolation. This report highlights the importance of transparently framing diagnostic uncertainty and aligning investigations with suspected genetic mechanisms. It also emphasizes the need to strengthen counseling and rare disease diagnostic pathways to enable shared decision making regarding the value of a genetic diagnosis beyond curative treatment.
