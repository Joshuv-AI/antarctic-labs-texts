Sustainable–resilient and climate-responsive e-waste supply chain network design under uncertainty using four-valued refined neutrosophic optimization
Authors: Ayesha Saeed, Ming Jian, Muhammad Imran, Zhijia Sasha Dong, Gul Freen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of electronic waste (e-waste) poses significant environmental and sustainability challenges and contributes to climate change. This paper presents a multi-objective, multi-period, mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model of sustainable e-waste management, which incorporates economic, environmental, social, and risk-related objectives. The model focuses on maximizing four objectives, namely, (i) profit maximization, (ii) CO2 emission reduction, (iii) maximization of job creation taking into account learning effects, and (iv) reduction of risk throughout the supply chain network. Moreover, the model addresses uncertainties in demand, recovery rates, and environmental conditions. The proposed model is solved using a four-valued refined neutrosophic optimization (FVRNO) approach, having a capability of dealing with truth, falsity, contradiction, and uncertainty in the decision-making process. A real-world e-waste case study validates the model. Computational results show that the proposed FVRNO approach is approximately 6.82 times and 11.82 times better than the epsilon-constraint and goal programming methods, respectively. The proposed framework supports circular economy implementation and climate-resilient decision-making for a sustainable e-waste management system aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

Introduction

The rapid obsolescence of electronic devices has intensified the global e-waste crisis, with 53.6 million metric tons generated in 2019 and projected to reach 74.7 million metric tons by 20301. E-waste contains hazardous substances such as lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg), which contaminate soil, water, and air and pose serious environmental and health risks2,3. In addition, improper e-waste management contributes to global climate change through greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. The rapid growth of mobile phone usage has further intensified e-waste generation, making mobile phones a critical focus for sustainable end-of-life (EOL) management4. Mobile phone usage has grown from 2.21 billion in 2005 to 7.74 billion in 2017, generating substantial e-waste, and frequent upgrades result in a short lifespan of about 2.2 years per phone5. Each device contains over 40 elements, including valuable metals such as copper (Cu), gold (Au), and silver (Ag)6.

To summarize these challenges, circular economy (CE) principles have attracted significant attention as a sustainable solution for e-waste management. Circular economy promotes recycling, reusing, and refurbishing to extend the life cycles of products and reduce the generation of waste, hence, reducing the impact on the environment and enhancing the efficiency of7,8. This change replaces the traditional take–make–dispose model with a regenerative system that integrates environmental, economic, and social sustainability9–11. In practice, CE is enacted in the form of closed-loop supply chains (CLSC) and reverse logistics networks (RLN) that allow for systematic collection, processing, and redistribution of EOL products12,13.

However, designing an efficient e-waste supply chain network design (SCND) is a complex task due to the presence of multiple conflicting objectives such as economic profitability, environmental sustainability, social welfare, and operational risk14,15. Previous studies have proposed optimization-based models to solve sustainable supply chain design problems, especially multi-objective mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) models6,16,17. Moreover, uncertainty in SC systems has been modeled by fuzzy, stochastic, and neutrosophic approaches18,19. However, there are several important limitations. Most of the studies focus mainly on economic and environmental goals and do not sufficiently integrate the social dimension, especially employment and human capital development20,21.

In addition, e-waste SC are intrinsically uncertain because of the variation of return rates, heterogeneous product conditions, and fluctuating market demand. These uncertainties are not only vague but can also be incomplete and contradictory. Although fuzzy models can capture vagueness and neutrosophic approaches can handle indeterminacy, most existing methods do not explicitly model the contradiction resulting from conflicting decision scenarios22,23. This limitation hinders the potential of accurate modeling of real-world e-waste SCND, where decisions have to be made under multiple dimensions of uncertainty.

To find solutions to these challenges, this study presents a multi-objective, multi-period MILP model for sustainable and resilient e-waste SCND. In this paper, the proposed model takes into account four key objectives simultaneously, namely, profit maximization, CO2 emission minimization, job creation maximization, and transportation risk minimization. The model includes CE principles and a learning effect in the job creation function to capture improvements in workforce productivity over time. Also. The network flow variables that determine the movement of e-waste between the SC nodes implicitly capture the transportation routing. To the best of our knowledge, integrating the learning effect in job creation and risk, along with cost in the e-waste context for the circular supply chain, has been scarcely reported in the literature. This combination of objectives not only achieves the sustainability objectives but also incorporates the SDGs in the modeling.

Moreover, to deal with uncertainty due to quality, quantity, and time, the developed model is solved by using a Four-Valued Refined Neutrosophic Optimization (FVRNO) framework. This approach extends the classical neutrosophic theory by introducing four dimensions of uncertainty: truth, falsity, indeterminacy, and contradiction. FVRNO provides a more comprehensive representation of uncertain and conflicting information compared to the conventional fuzzy and neutrosophic approaches, which helps to make more robust decisions in complex SC systems24,25.

The applicability of the proposed model is demonstrated through a case study of a mobile phone e-waste SC with realistic data and network structures. The results show the effectiveness of the model for optimizing material flows, enhancing sustainability performance, and supporting decision-making under uncertainty. Sensitivity analysis is also conducted to assess the effect of key parameters. Managerial and policy implications are derived to facilitate practical implementation. Literature review

This section reviews recent advances in e-waste supply chain network design (SCND), with a focus on sustainability, resilience, and uncertainty modeling. The increasing generation of electronic waste, along with growing climate change concerns, has intensified the need for low-carbon, adaptive, and efficient reverse logistics network designs. An in-depth examination of existing studies is performed to highlight the methodological trends, limitations, and research gaps, especially regarding the integration of CE principles, multi-objective MILP optimization, and advanced uncertainty modeling approaches into e-waste management systems.

The section is structured into six subsections: (1) e-waste supply chain; (2) Sustainable supply chain; (3) Sustainable e-waste supply chain; (4) Sustainable-resilient e-waste supply chain network design; (5) Solution techniques and uncertainty modeling; and (6) Comparative analysis of the literature and research gaps.

E-waste supply chain

The high turnover of electronic appliances and high usage frequency have instigated a dramatic increase in e-waste, with its swift generation posing significant environmental and population health risks26. Another aspect that leads to climate change is improper management that causes energy-consuming disposal, incineration, and carbon emissions CO227. E-waste is a resource that has both recoverable and hazardous resources and needs a structured and sustainable SC28. The LCA of printed circuit boards (PCBs) has identified potential resources recovery under the CE approach, particularly during disruptions29. Sustainable CLSC combines economic and social performance in the face of uncertainty, whereas open-loop CE strategies maximize recovery performance of mixed glass waste streams30.

Sustainable supply chain

A sustainable supply chain aims at striking a balance between environmental, economic, and social goals. The reduction of CO2 emissions, the adoption of renewable energy, and the increase in resilience of the long-term system became the components of the sustainability concept, which inspired the strategies. Gholizadeh et al.31 emphasized the importance of waste reduction, recyclability, and resource efficiency as key drivers of sustainability, especially in e-waste SCs. Sustainability has now become a source of competitive advantage due to globalization and the uncertainty of increasing demand. Nourmohamadi Shalke et al.32 demonstrated that sustainability performance is highly affected by the energy consumption, environmental pressure, and external disturbance that is in the form of consumer behavior changes and climate-related disasters. Besides, Esfahbodi et al.33 explored sustainability risks in low-tier SC nodes, and Salçuk & Şahin34 conducted a systematic review of sustainable SCND models and found key research gaps. Together, these studies consolidate the theoretical and practical grounds of sustainable SC management, especially in developing economies.

Sustainable e-waste supply chain

A sustainable e-waste SC integrates climate change mitigation principles through recycling and remanufacturing of e-waste, as well as recovering and reusing e-waste material responsibly. Doan et al.35 demonstrated that sustainable e-waste systems have the potential to not only mitigate environmental impacts but also generate economic value. The recent studies have focused on sustainable SCND in the electronics industry. A sustainable SC framework of the Saudi Arabian air conditioner industry, proposed by Oise & Konyeha6, emphasized recycling and remanufacturing. A CLSC model of durable goods was developed by Hejazi & Khorshidvand12 and greatly reduced C02 emissions and energy consumption. In the case of recycling of mobile phones, Budak36 used disassembly line balancing and applied it to TBL planning.

In terms of a global perspective, the introduction of Incoterms and advertising effects into a sustainable CLSC model was implemented by Edalatpour et al.37, and it captured the role of trade regulations and stimulation of demand in network design. Conversely, the current study focuses on the incorporation of both sustainability and resilience in a mobile phone e-waste SC under the framework of both CE and LCA. Sustainable e-waste management has also been supported by multi-objective optimization methods,such as, Saffari et al.38 proposed a stochastic reverse SC model of WEEE in the presence of uncertainty, and Safdar et al.19 presented a neutrosophic-based TBL optimization model of sustainable e-waste management. All these studies reveal the efficiency of sustainable SCND in managing the problems of e-waste, besides supporting the objectives of reducing CO2 emissions and recovering resources.

Sustainable-resilient e-waste SCND

The effective implementation of e-waste SCND should be based on the incorporation of sustainability and resilience, as the sector undergoes exposure to environmental pressures, market uncertainty, and disruptions caused by the climate. There is limited research that has looked at sustainability and resilience in e-waste SCND jointly. The compound model suggested by Uddin39 consists of a combination of sustainable and resilient approaches to supply chains. Yu et al.40 have developed a strong and sustainable SCND during epidemic interruptions using a viable SC framework of agility, resilience, and sustainability.

Saffari et al.38 also proposed a multi-objective model of resilient, sustainable, and responsive forward and reverse SCND. Although this has occurred, the adoption of CE principles, policy considerations, and LCA in sustainable-resilient SCND is inadequate. To CE-aware SCND, Govindan et al.41 used an augmented epsilon-constraint approach, and Lotfi et al.42 studied the agility and resilience dimensions. However, the explicit inclusion of the mitigation and adaptation of climate change in the resilient e-waste SCND models remains an under-researched topic, a critical research gap that has been addressed in the present study. Solution techniques & uncertainty modeling

Optimization techniques play a central role in sustainable and resilient supply chain network design (SCND), particularly under uncertainty and disruptions. Traditional exact approaches, such as weighted sum methods, goal programming, constraint programming, and branch-and-bound algorithms, have been widely applied to linear and mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) problems51. However, these techniques often face limitations in handling real-world multi-objective decision environments characterized by conflicting goals and uncertainty. Consequently, metaheuristic methods, including genetic algorithms, NSGA-II, particle swarm optimization, firefly algorithm, and ant colony optimization, have gained increasing attention due to their ability to generate Pareto-optimal solutions and support trade-off analysis52. Despite their flexibility, these approaches may perform inadequately when problem data are ambiguous, incomplete, or conflicting, particularly in reverse logistics networks (RLN) for e-waste management systems.

E-waste SCND is inherently characterized by high levels of uncertainty due to the unpredictable nature of product returns, material recovery, and market dynamics. The quantity and timing of returned electronic products depend on consumer behavior, product life cycles, and informal collection channels, making them highly variable. Furthermore, the condition of returned devices is uncertain, as products may be suitable for refurbishment, recycling, or disposal depending on their physical and functional state. Recovery rates of valuable materials are also uncertain due to processing efficiency and technological constraints. In addition, market-related factors such as demand for refurbished products and price volatility of secondary materials introduce further complexity. Such uncertainties are not merely imprecise but may also consist of uncompleted and conflicting data, which cannot be adequately represented by conventional deterministic methods.

To handle uncertainty, there are different modeling frameworks that were created. Fuzzy-based models, with fuzzy-random robust programming and fuzzy robust models being the most popular models used to represent vagueness and imprecision in the supply chain systems. The more advanced fuzzy extensions, like the Pythagorean fuzzy sets and type-2 intuitionistic fuzzy models, offer better efficiency in dealing with uncertainty in complex decision-making contexts. Neutrosophic methods also build on these paradigms by adding a category of indeterminacy to that of truth and falsity. Nonetheless, the majority of the current methods pay attention to the narrow dimensions of uncertainty and fail to explicitly represent contradiction which occurs due to the conflicting information. To circumvent these limitations, four-valued refined neutrosophic optimization (FVRNO) has become an emerging, promising solution to complex decision-making under uncertainty. FVRNO allows a more detailed representation of multi-dimensional uncertainty in e-waste SCND through the incorporation of truth, falsity, indeterminacy, and contradiction.

Previous research has demonstrated that it is efficient in managing conflicting sustainability objectives24,53, and Saeed et al.15 demonstrated that combining MILP and FVRNO is more effective in handling conflicting sustainability objectives than traditional goal programming methods. However, little attention has been given to the use of FVRNO in the design of e-waste management RLN, which is why the study is critically needed to address the research gap. As shown in Table 2, different uncertainty modeling frameworks capture specific aspects of uncertainty. Neutrosophic models extend fuzzy logic by incorporating additional dimensions of uncertainty, while the proposed FVRNO framework further refines this representation by explicitly modeling contradiction.

Comparison and research gap

Despite the extensive body of literature on e-waste supply chain network design (SCND), several important limitations remain. As summarized in Table 1, existing literature mainly focuses on economic and environmental goals35,44,46, and the overall integration of triple-bottom-line (TBL) dimensions, especially social aspects, such as job creation, remains limited26,36,42,54. Though circular economy (CE) practices such as recycling, reuse, etc. have been implemented in some studies18,19, their integration with end-of-life (EOL) policies, life cycle assessment (LCA), and resilience considerations remains incomplete, particularly in the context of developing countries. Moreover, the majority of the models target generic e-waste streams or developed areas with little emphasis on mobile phone-specific supply chains (SCs) and emerging economies. From a methodological point of view, the majority of the existing studies use deterministic, stochastic, or fuzzy-based methods to reflect the uncertainty. The fuzzy-based methods are effective in capturing vagueness and imprecision, as shown in Table 2. Neutrosophic approaches extend this to include indeterminacy.

Nevertheless, such approaches tend to only provide partial facets of uncertainty and do not explicitly model contradiction as a result of conflicting information in large-scale SCND problems. Moreover, the literature tends to separate SC design and uncertainty modeling, without an integrated framework that would integrate advanced uncertainty representation with multi-objective SCND.

The limitations indicate a significant research gap to develop an integrated framework that integrates principles of sustainability, resilience, and the principles of the CE, and a multi-dimensional uncertainty modelling within the network of e-waste SC. Specifically, higher neutrosophic techniques like FVRNO are not properly explored in the design of RLN, but they can be used to represent uncertainty via the truth, falsity, indeterminacy, and contradiction. To bridge this gap, this paper presents a multi-objective MILP model combined with an FVRNO framework, which will facilitate the comprehensive representation of uncertainty and support balanced decision-making in the context of economic, environmental, social, and risk-related goals.

Based on the research gaps identified, this study constructs an integrated modeling framework, which is the multi-objective MILP coupled with FVRNO, to address the limitations of current models. Specifically, the suggested framework allows to represent simultaneously truth, falsity, indeterminacy, and contradiction in a single decision-making framework, simultaneously incorporating the principles of the circular economy and risk-conscious system design. The proposed model has a detailed formulation and solution methodology that are discussed in the section below.

Development of a mathematical model

This part formulates a multi-objective MILP model of sustainable and resilient e-waste SCND. The proposed model combines four main performance dimensions, namely, economic profitability, the impact on the environment, social contribution through job creation, and the risk of transportation. The model is designed to support planning-level decision-making in an e-waste SCND, where end-of-life products are collected, processed, and redistributed through a structured RLN. The formulation represents the flow of material between collection centers, segregation centers, recycling centers, refurbishing centers, disposal centers, and market nodes. This model is developed under uncertain conditions by integrating circular economy principles and sustainability goals, with four key objectives: maximization of total profit, minimization of CO2 emissions, maximization of job creation, and minimization of system risk.

Problem statement

Consider the case of a multi-period e-waste SCND where the EOL products are produced in several cities and collected in several collection centers. The E-waste collected is transported to segregation facilities where it is sorted into recyclable, refurbishable, and non-recoverable fractions depending on the condition of the product. Recyclable resources are sent to recycling facilities to recover their resources and then sold to the material markets, whereas refurbishable products are channeled into secondary markets where they are processed and then sold to the material markets. The rest of the non-recoverable waste is sent to the disposal facilities to be safely disposed of. The decision problem is to find the optimal allocation and flow of e-waste across all the facilities in the network whilst considering the capacity and operational constraints. The model aims to simultaneously maximize economic and social benefit while minimizing environmental impacts in terms of CO2 emissions and reducing transportation-related risks under uncertain conditions.

The assumptions above are taken to ensure a balance is achieved between model realism and computational tractability. The constant learning rate is reflective of the overall average enhancement of workforce efficiency over time, which is consistent with the learning curve theory in labor-intensive processes like e-waste collection, sorting, and recycling. In the same manner, the predetermined location of facilities will enable the model to concentrate on how to optimize the operational flows instead of strategizing on where to locate facilities, which is out of the scope of this study. The formula of constant cost parameters simplifies the multi-period formulation and allows a more straightforward analysis of the operational dynamics of a demand-driven system. Though the risk of transportation is formulated as a parameter, it represents the risk of routes at the level of distance, infrastructure, and probability of disruption, and it is possible to explicitly consider the risk in the optimization model. These are the assumptions that are usually made in the literature on SCND and that make sure that the proposed model is transparent analytically and computationally efficient enough to reflect the key dynamics of e-waste SCND.

Development of the objective functions

The proposed model includes four objective functions representing the economic, environmental, social, and risk dimensions of the e-waste SCND. The sustainability economic objective is to maximize profit by factoring in the earnings of recovered materials and refurbished goods, and taking into consideration the transportation and operational costs. The environmental sustainability goal is to reduce the CO2 emissions associated with transportation, which is based on the quantity of material flow, the distance of transportation, and emission factors. The social objective of sustainability is the maximization of job creation in various facilities with a learning effect to capture the enhancement in the productivity of the workforce over the years. The four objectives are to minimize transportation risks in the e-waste movement between facilities within the SC network. Together, the objectives offer a balanced handling of economic efficiency, environmental sustainability, social responsibility, and risk.

Profit maximization

c. Job maximization

c. Job maximization

Job maximization

d. Risk factor minimization

Risk factor minimization

The risk objective is transportation-related vulnerability related to the transportation of e-waste between facilities in the network. In this model, the risk assigned to each route captures the degree of exposure to potential disruptions, which may be due to factors like transportation distance, road conditions, probability of accidents, and reliability of infrastructure. These risk values are determined based on expert judgment and the available literature, and are considered deterministic parameters in the optimization model. To make sure that the risk factors are consistent and comparable to those of different transportation routes, the risk factors are normalized. The overall risk is obtained by multiplying the route-specific risk factor by its associated flow of e-waste transported by that route. Thus, the routes with a larger amount of e-waste or routes with a greater value of risk contribute more to the overall risk of the system. This formulation enables the model to minimize risky transportation decisions while maintaining a balance with economic, environmental, and social objectives, as shown in Eq. Research methodology

This section presents the solution methodology used to solve the proposed multi-objective e-waste SCND model under uncertainty. Due to the presence of multiple conflicting objectives, including profit maximization, emission minimization, job creation, and risk reduction, a multi-objective optimization framework is required to obtain balanced and reliable decisions. Conventional optimization methods usually have limitations due to uncertainty, incomplete information, and conflicting decision criteria. Hence, this research follows a Four-Valued Refined Neutrosophic Optimization (FVRNO) approach to the problem to overcome these issues and improve decision-making in complex uncertainty conditions like e-waste SCND management systems.

Problem formulation and uncertainty modeling

The research forms the multi-objective MILP model of sustainable and resilient e-waste SCND under uncertainty. This model brings together economic, environmental, social, and risk-related objectives in a single framework. The SC network comprises several echelons, which include collection centers, segregation facilities, recycling centers, refurbishing units, disposal facilities, and market nodes, which capture both the forward and reverse logistics flows in line with the principles of the circular economy. Decision variables are used to make material flow allocation and transportation decisions, which are subject to capacity, demand, and operational constraints.

In real-world e-waste systems, uncertainty arises from multiple sources, including variability in return quantities, fluctuations in demand, differences in product conditions, and transportation-related risks. These uncertainties are often incomplete, imprecise, and sometimes conflicting, making them difficult to represent using conventional deterministic or fuzzy-based models. While fuzzy approaches address vagueness, they are limited in handling contradictory information and do not fully capture the complexity of uncertain decision environments in supply chain systems. Uncertainty can be caused by various sources in the real-life e-waste SCND systems, and these sources include variability of quantities returned, fluctuations in demand, variation in the condition of products, and risks associated with transportation. These uncertainties are not always complete, precise, and even sometimes conflicting, and therefore, they are hard to model in conventional deterministic or fuzzy-based models. Although the fuzzy approaches can be used to deal with the vagueness, it has limitation in the ability to deal with contradictory information and do not fully capture the complexity of the uncertainty decision environment in the SC systems.

To surmount these shortcomings, this work uses a neutrosophic logic-based system, which represents uncertainty in terms of four independent variables: truth (T), falsity (F), indeterminacy (I), and contradiction (C). Multi-objective optimization problems can frequently lead to conflicting results, resulting in varying levels of satisfaction, dissatisfaction, ambiguity, and conflict22,23. By incorporating these four dimensions, the proposed framework enables a more comprehensive evaluation of decision alternatives under uncertainty69. This approach extends classical neutrosophic and intuitionistic fuzzy methods by explicitly modeling contradiction alongside indeterminacy, thereby providing a more robust and realistic representation of uncertainty in e-waste SCND53,70.

Using the normalized values, four neutrosophic components are defined:

Truth (T): degree of satisfaction (closeness to ideal solution).

Contradiction (C): conflict among multiple objectives.

Subject to the set of neutrosophic constraints and original constraints in model Eq. (6–22). The neutrosophic constraints will be discussed after the membership functions are defined. Based on this transformation, the proposed FVRNO framework converts the original multi-objective e-waste supply chain optimization problem into a single, tractable optimization model that can be efficiently solved using standard optimization techniques. Neutrosophic elements allow one to assess the decision options comprehensively by considering the satisfaction, dissatisfaction, uncertainty, and conflict simultaneously. This is a balanced solution, reflecting the complexities in sustainable and resilient SC systems in the real world. The computational procedure, along with the solution steps, of the FVRNO method, is detailed in the next section.

Model properties

The proposed FVRNO-based framework has strong convergence and optimality characteristics to solve the multi-objective e-waste SCND problems. The finite feasible region that is determined by the MILP formulation and by the deterministic evaluation of neutrosophic components ensures convergence. The underlying MILP model can be used to ensure that the transformed single-objective problem is globally optimal, and the FVRNO framework can be used to ensure the robustness of solutions to the transformed single-objective problem. This makes the decision-making process under complex and uncertain conditions more reliable and stable.

From a computational perspective, the general complexity is dictated mostly by the MILP structure, NP-hard. Nonetheless, the neutrosophic transformation presents only a minor additional overall computation cost since it constitutes normalizations and basic algebraic computations. The model is efficiently solved with MATLAB in the case study, where the decision variables are 441, and the constraints are 321, indicating that the model is practically computationally feasible. The scalability of the framework is determined by the size of the network and the model dimensions; however, it can effectively be extended to larger-scale problems by decomposition methods, heuristic initialization, and parallel computation methods, thus maintaining computational efficiency.

Considering the practical applicability, the proposed framework brings noteworthy value to industry players as well as policymakers. The model allows a balanced consideration of the trade-offs in the face of uncertainty by incorporating economic, environmental, social, and risk objectives within a single decision-making framework. This helps in the development of sustainable and resilient e-waste SCND in line with the principles of the CE. Moreover, the framework provides actionable information for strategic planning, resource distribution, and policy formulation, which makes it a practical and implementable tool for real-world SC optimization issues.

Case study and model application

A case study of the mobile phone e-waste supply chain network in Pakistan is carried out to validate the proposed model.

Case study overview of the network design

This research uses the proposed optimization model on a case study of the e-waste SC of mobile phones in Pakistan to show how practical the proposed optimization model can be. The network is structured as a CLSC that is coupled with the concept of a CE and includes economic, environmental, social, and risk-related objectives. It comprises several interrelated facilities, such as collection centers, segregation centers, recycling centers, refurbishing centers, disposal facilities, material markets, and second-hand markets, which have allowed efficient processing and redistribution of e-waste. The selection of five major cities, Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Peshawar, and Quetta, as primary collection centers is based on the key areas of e-waste production.

The e-waste collected is transported to segregation centers in Gujranwala and Hyderabad, where it is sorted, dismantled, and separated. The waste stream, once separated, takes three main routes: the valuable metals included in the waste, such as copper, gold, and silver, are sent to recycling plants in Lahore and Karachi; reusable parts are directed to refurbishing centers and made available in second-hand markets; and non-recoverable waste is disposed of through designated facilities. The recovered materials are sold to eight material markets, and refurbished products are sold to eight second-hand markets, supporting longer product life cycles and minimizing environmental impact. Model implementation

The given multi-objective mixed-integer linear programming model is solved in MATLAB (R2021a) and is run on a system with 8 GB RAM and a 2.69 GHz processor, where all the calculations and the process of solving the problem are conducted within the MATLAB environment. It is also a model that considers profit maximization, minimization of carbon emissions CO2, maximization of job creation, and the minimization of risk, and is combined with the FVRNO framework to deal with uncertainty and ambiguity in decision-making. The implementation steps include the construction of a payoff matrix through optimization of each of the objective functions separately to find the best and the worst values of each of the objective functions, and then a normalization of the payoff matrix to ensure that the values of each of the objective functions are comparable across different objective scales. The neutrosophic elements, i.e., truth, falsity, indeterminacy, and contradiction, are computed using predefined membership functions based on these normalized values. This multi-objective model is then transformed into a one-objective neutrosophic model and solved in MATLAB to get the optimal decision variables, such as flows of materials, use of facilities, and patterns of distribution in the network. The resulting outputs are used to evaluate system performance and form the basis for the analysis presented in the results and discussion section.

Results and discussion

The proposed model, which was implemented in MATLAB (R2021a), was run to test the performance of the sustainable and resilient e-waste SCND under uncertainty. The section presents the results of the neutrosophic multi-objective optimization model and will discuss the model results in detail. First, the results of the neutrosophic optimization and optimal network configuration, including the payoff matrix, membership functions, and ultimate optimal solution, are analyzed. This is followed by a sensitive analysis that looks at how the important parameters, including demand and fuel price, affect the system performance. The effectiveness of the suggested method is also compared to baseline techniques, and the effectiveness of the proposed approach is then evaluated in terms of scalability and computational efficiency. Lastly, the managerial implications, policy implications, and research limitations are discussed to explain the practicality of the proposed framework.

The most optimal route of e-waste through the SCND is indicated in Figs. 4, 5 and 6, and it gives valuable insights into the patterns of facility allocation and the patterns of material distribution.Fig. 4Optimal flow between collection centers and segregation centers.Fig. 5Optimal flow among segregation centers, recycling centers, and disposal centers.Fig. 6Flow among recycling, refurbishing, second market, and material market.

The flow between collection centers and the segregation centers, as shown in Fig. 4, is majorly affected by the geographical distance, and also the capacity constraints of the facilities. Lahore and Islamabad are mostly assigned to the Gujranwala segregation center since the transportation distances and costs involved are lower. But once the capacity of the Gujranwala center is met, a part of the flow is redirected to the Hyderabad segregation center. Moreover, Quetta harbors a comparatively greater amount of e-waste since it covers a relatively large geographical area, consequently augmenting the overall volume of e-waste inflow into the network.

As shown in Fig. 5, the distribution of flows between the segregation centers and recycling, refurbishing, and disposal facilities is based on the transportation cost, proximity to the facility, and efficiency of operations. The recycling center in Lahore and the refurbishing center in Islamabad are the main beneficiaries of the Gujranwala segregation center because of the shorter distance and lower cost of transportation. Conversely, the Hyderabad segregation center is largely related to the recycling and disposal plants in Karachi, due to its proximity to Karachi. This distribution system saves transportation costs, emissions, and enhances the efficiency of the overall system, as well as helping to create jobs. Additionally, the model will only activate a single refurbishing center in Islamabad, whereas the second refurbishing center in Faisalabad will not be operational because of its high operational costs. This illustrates how the model can prevent unnecessary use of facilities and provide a cost-effective network design.

Lastly, Fig. 6 shows how the recovered materials and refurbished products are distributed to market nodes. The refurbishing center in Islamabad supplies second-hand markets, and the recycling centers serve markets of raw materials. The general network is a demand-driven (pull-based) approach where material flows are generated based on market demands as opposed to the availability of supplies. This will help in the efficient use of resources, less wastage in processing and production, in line with real demand.

In general, the findings indicate that the proposed model is effective in terms of integrating the economic, environmental, and social objectives to create an optimal and practical e-waste SC structure. The network structure that emerges as a result of the network structure indicates realistic operational decisions, such as the choice of facilities, capacity utilization, and flow allocation, and hence justifies the applicability of the proposed framework under uncertain conditions.

Scalability and computational performance

Scalability and computational performance are evaluated to evaluate the applicability of the proposed framework in practice. The optimization problem underlying this is an MILP problem, which is implemented in MATLAB, wherein all solution procedures are carried out. The size of the SCND, which is the number of facilities, transportation links, time periods, and decision variables, is the main determinant of the computational complexity. The larger the scale of the problem, the more the amount of computation is needed to get optimal solutions. The further computational load will be minimal because the FVRNO framework only introduces a limited additional computational load, since it only involves the normalization and algebraic transformations of the objective functions.

The key extra effort is associated with the construction of the payoff matrix, which involves the multiple solutions of the base model based on the number of objectives. To achieve efficiency and improve performance, the proposed framework can be further refined with the use of strategies like problem decomposition, scenario reduction, heuristic initialization, and parallel computation. In general, the model is balanced in terms of the quality of the solution and the energy required to operate it, which means that the model is appropriate to address the solution to the large-scale e-waste SCND issues.

Managerial and policy implications

This section outlines some of the managerial implications, policy implications, and limitations of the study of the proposed framework with reference to its practical applicability regarding the performance and sustainability of e-waste SCND.

Managerial implications

From a managerial implication, the findings can give clear indications on how to enhance the efficiency and resiliency of e-waste SC processes. To boost reliability within the system, decision-makers ought to consider risk-sensitive transportation policies by prioritizing those routes that are less likely to be disrupted, even though they may be associated with a slightly higher cost. The results also indicate that the maximization of the use of the existing collection and recycling facilities is more effective than the expansion of the infrastructure indiscriminately. In addition, the integration of a learning effect underscores the need to develop the workforce; hence, the managers should invest in the training programs to enhance labor productivity in sorting, dismantling, and recycling activities. Lastly, managers are advised to put greater emphasis on the cost minimization approach, since this is the most prevalent approach in managing SC, but instead emphasize a more sustainable and stable approach to SC management by focusing on balancing the economic, environmental, and risk considerations simultaneously.

Policy implication & recommendation

The findings of this research indicate that there are some actionable policy recommendations to enhance the sustainability and resilience of e-waste SCs. The policymakers should focus on developing formal collection and recycling systems by offering financial incentives, like subsidies or tax breaks, to participate in formal collection and recycling procedures. The investment in transportation and logistics infrastructure is also essential since the increase in the reliability of the infrastructure can greatly decrease the risk of the operation and increase the efficiency of the system. Moreover, specific policies that encourage the organization of the workforce and development of the workforce skills in dealing with e-waste could enhance productivity and enhance the social impact of recycling systems. Lastly, policymakers should also implement integrated decision-making frameworks that can simultaneously address economic, environmental, and social objectives, allowing planning CE principles initiatives to be more balanced and effective.

Limitations and future research directions

Even though the proposed framework has its contributions,l,k several limitations are to be considered in order to ensure transparency and lead to further research. The model presupposes predetermined locations of facilities and is rather focused on operational decision-making instead of on strategic location planning. Although this assumption is useful in enhancing computational tractability, it could be limiting in situations where facility siting decisions are crucial. Also, no explicit consideration is made regarding the fixed installation and infrastructure costs, as the model focuses on the efficiency of transportation, processing, and operations. Since this simplification enables easier examination of network flows, it can be used in real-world situations to affect the long-term planning of investments and the cost evaluation of the completed work. Moreover, the risk modeling methodology is based on aggregated parameters found in the available data and literature and might not be capable of fully capturing dynamic disruption and complex interdependencies found in the real SC environment.

The incorporation of climate-related factors is considered on a planning scale by carbon-aware routing optimization of transportation and facility placement decisions. Nevertheless, the model does not directly include dynamic climate-responsive factors like time-dependent CO2 rate, weather-dependent disturbances, or climate-resilient routing. Additionally, the application of triangular membership functions and normalized parameters brings about a simplified representation of the uncertainty. Despite the stability and robustness of the results, as confirmed by the sensitivity analysis, alternative uncertainty modeling techniques can also offer more information on the behavior of the systems in highly volatile conditions.

Future studies can build on this research by adding endogenous decisions of facility location and fixed cost structures to enhance strategic planning abilities. The inclusion of dynamic and time-dependent risk models and the inclusion of real-time or stochastic data would make the representation of uncertainty more realistic. Furthermore, future research can focus on superior methods of solutions, such as decomposition methods, metaheuristics, or hybrid optimization methods, in order to effectively deal with large-scale problem instances. The addition of dynamic climate-sensitive parameters, including weather-induced disruptions and weather-driven adaptive routing methods, is also a promising area of research in enhancing the end-to-end SCND to be more climate-aware. Nevertheless, the suggested model offers a useful decision-support model to analyze sustainable and resilient e-waste SCs in the face of uncertainty.

Conclusion

In this paper, multi-objective optimization model, a multi-objective optimization model has been formulated for sustainable and resilient e-waste SCND under uncertainty. The model proposed integrated economic, environmental, social, and risk-related objectives into one MILP framework and was optimized through an FVRNO model. The framework offers a more integrated and structured account of the uncertainty in the real world than traditional methods by integrating truth, falsity, indeterminacy, and contradiction simultaneously. Theoretically, this study contributes to the literature by extending neutrosophic optimization into a sophisticated four-valued framework, which expressly captures contradiction as an independent element, and thus can be used to model the ambiguous and conflicting information in a complex SC system more realistically. Practically, the findings indicate that the combination of sustainability and risk-related factors and economic goals results in more balanced and resilient network designs. It is demonstrated that transportation risk, emission, and workforce development play a significant role in making decisions related to facility allocation and material flow. The findings also have valuable managerial and policy implications, including facilitating risk-conscious logistics planning, efficient resource use, and the development of practitioners, as well as providing policymakers with a structured basis for ensuring that more people practice CE, enhance their environmental performance, and create employment opportunities, especially in developing economies. Despite these contributions, several limitations still exist in the current research. Moreover, there are chances that computational complexity can rise in large-scale applications. The limitations can be tackled in future studies by using dynamical and real-time data, integrating facility location and routing decisions, and exploring hybrid approaches that combine neutrosophic optimization with machine learning or metaheuristic methods. On the whole, the proposed framework is a solid decision-support tool to facilitate sustainable and resilient e-waste management and help develop more efficient, flexible, and sustainable SCND systems.
