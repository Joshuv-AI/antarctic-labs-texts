Establishment of a feasible surgical strategy for aortoesophageal fistula
Authors: Katsuhiro Yamanaka, Kenji Okada, Kazunori Sakaguchi, Tasuku Okada, Hironaga Shiraki, Ryo Kawabata, Jun Karaki, Yasufumi Koterazawa, Noriko Oyama, Soichiro Henmi, Hironobu Goto, Hiroaki Takahashi, Yutaka Okita

ABSTRACT

Central Message

Perspective

Prosthetic graft infection and infected aneurysm remain challenging. AEF is an even more catastrophic scenario due to additional esophageal complications. AEF has increasingly occurred as a complication of contemporary aortic interventions. The current strategy, including the ALPS approach, may provide a practical and effective solution for improving long-term outcomes.

Aortoesophageal fistula (AEF) is a rare but life-threatening condition characterized by abnormal communication between the aorta and esophagus. Primary AEF occurs as the result of a thoracic aortic aneurysm, foreign body ingestion, or esophageal malignancy without previous aortic surgery. Secondary AEF develops after aortic surgery. With the widespread adoption of thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR), the incidence of secondary AEF has increased.1 In addition, AEF after catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation has been increasingly recognized as a rare but catastrophic complication.2

Prosthetic graft infection and infected aneurysm represent devastating diseases that frequently challenge the optimal treatment strategy.3, 4, 5 AEF presents an even more catastrophic scenario as the result of additional esophageal complications. Critical issues regarding esophageal management include whether resection is indicated, the optimal timing and technique for removal, and the timing and approach for reconstruction. Furthermore, several important considerations remain, including optimal TEVAR use, the aortic-reconstruction method, and appropriate reconstruction materials. Although alternative strategies have been reported,6,7 consensus regarding the optimal surgical strategy for AEF remains lacking. Furthermore, we have recently chosen the anterolateral thoracotomy with partial sternotomy (ALPS) approach for AEF because the ALPS approach not only enables the protection of multiple organs but also provides satisfactory exposure from the aortic root to the descending aorta.8 This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, technical considerations, and clinical outcomes of our standardized multidisciplinary open surgical strategy for AEF, which has been consistently applied since 2008.

Methods

Ethics

This single-center, retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of Kobe Graduate University School of Medicine (institutional review board number: no. B220178; March 13, 2025), with a waiver of informed consent.

Diagnosis

A specialized multidisciplinary team, including infectious disease physicians, cardiovascular surgeons, plastic surgeons, radiologists, gastroenterologists, and esophageal surgeons, was established to determine treatment strategies. Diagnosis was established comprehensively. Careful evaluation of the clinical course (Chiari triad)9 is essential, particularly for symptoms including chest pain, fever, hematemesis, or melena in patients with thoracic aortic diseases. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated periaortic gas or contrast enhancement within low-density regions. The final diagnosis was confirmed using gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Medical Treatment

Following the Sanford Guide to Antimicrobial Therapy (1999-present) and the American Heart Association guidelines,10 antibiotics were selected on the basis of culture results and administered for 6 weeks. Lifelong oral antibiotics were prescribed for patients with residual prosthetic grafts or stent grafts, or at the surgeon’s discretion.

Surgical Treatment

Surgical management of AEF follows the principles of surgery for thoracic aortic infection (TAI). TAI management comprises 4 key components: (1) resection of infected tissues, (2) irrigation, (3) aortic reconstruction, and (4) graft coverage. Previously, infected tissue resection in TAI surgery was limited to abscess debridement. However, since 2008, radical resection of all infected tissues, including abscesses, necrotic tissue, and aortic wall, has been performed. In patients with AEF, esophageal resection has also been performed. Our current surgical strategy for AEF includes radical resection of all infected tissues, followed by saline irrigation and in situ replacement using a rifampicin-soaked, gelatin-impregnated Dacron graft, with final graft coverage using omentopexy or a pedicled latissimus dorsi musculocutaneous flap.11 In patients with secondary AEF who had multiple prosthetic grafts implanted or had undergone extensive aortic replacement, 18-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography was performed to determine the optimal resection area.5 TEVAR was selectively performed in cases with immunocompromised status (eg, advanced cancer) or as a bridge to open repair (bridge TEVAR). In situ replacement was completed within 7 days after bridge TEVAR. Since 2008, this therapeutic approach has been consistently implemented, although various treatment strategies were attempted previously (Figure 1). Surgical approach varied by replacement area: (1) midline incisions for replacement from the aortic root to arch; (2) ALPS for extensive arch involvement; and (3) left thoracotomy via the fourth or fifth intercostal space with the rib-cross technique for the descending or thoracoabdominal aorta.12 Our current standard procedure follows the ALPS approach8 (Figure 2). Patients are positioned in the right semirecumbent position. The thorax is entered through the third intercostal space with partial lower sternotomy. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is established with bicaval drainage and ascending aortic perfusion, followed by additional femoral artery cannulation. The left ventricle is vented through the right upper pulmonary vein. After the tympanic temperature reaches 23 °C with a rectal temperature <30 °C, cardiac arrest is achieved by antegrade cardioplegia and maintained by antegrade and retrograde cardioplegia. Before the aortic arch is opened, the descending aorta is clamped to perfuse the lower body from the femoral arterial cannula. After the aortic arch is opened and selective antegrade cerebral perfusion is established, the abscess, necrotic tissue, and aortic wall are resected as extensively as possible. Subsequently, while maintaining lower body perfusion, the esophageal surgeon performs an esophagectomy through the same surgical field. After aggressive debridement and thorough irrigation, an in situ graft replacement with a rifampicin-soaked gelatin-impregnated quadrifurcated Dacron graft is performed (Gelweave; Terumo Corporation). After CPB termination, an omental flap installation through a small diaphragmatic opening is completed. After complete graft wrapping, the chest is closed in a standard fashion. Finally, an esophagostomy is created in the left cervical region, and a gastrostomy is performed simultaneously (Video 1, Figure 3, A-D). When the omental flap is unavailable, a pedicled latissimus dorsi musculocutaneous flap is performed several days after the initial operation. Outcomes

The primary end point was freedom from infection-related death. Secondary end points included hospital mortality, overall survival, and the esophageal reconstruction rate.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP for Macintosh, version 18.2.2 (SAS Institute). Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard error. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to evaluate overall survival and freedom from infection-related death.

Operative Details

All 21 patients underwent an in situ aortic replacement with a rifampicin-soaked Dacron graft. Omentopexy was performed in 19 patients and latissimus dorsi musculocutaneous flap in 2 patients. Among the 21 patients, 7 underwent bridge TEVAR. In situ replacement procedures included total arch replacement through a midline incision in 4 patients, descending aortic replacement through a left thoracotomy in 10, and extensive aortic arch replacement through the ALPS approach in 7. For patients with total arch replacement through a midline incision, an aortic occlusion balloon in the descending aorta maintained lower body perfusion during radical resection of all infected tissues. The operation time was 860 ± 217 (755-965) minutes, and the CPB time was 291 ± 144 (214-368) minutes. Among the 21 patients, 20 underwent simultaneous aortic and esophageal resection under CPB. Only 1 patient received staged management because of the clinical condition. This patient underwent esophagectomy with video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery through a right thoracic approach 5 days after bridge TEVAR. Subsequently, 7 days after esophagectomy, radical resection of all infected tissues and aortic reconstruction were performed through the ALPS approach, followed by omentopexy.

Early Outcomes

Hospital mortality was 19.0% (4/21). The causes of death included pneumonia (n = 1), multiple organ failure (n = 1), mediastinitis (n = 1), and empyema (n = 1).

Long-Term Outcomes

At 10 years, overall survival was 38.4 ± 13.2%, and freedom from infection-related death was 89.7 ± 6.8% (Figure 4, A and B). No recurrent infection occurred, and 10 patients successfully completed esophageal reconstruction. In our current strategy, esophageal reconstruction was performed using a pedicled jejunum with internal thoracic artery and vein anastomosis to jejunal vessels (supercharge anastomosis) in 9 patients, and an ileocecum graft with supercharging anastomosis in 1 patient. The mean duration from resection to reconstruction was 221.4 ± 283.0 days (62-1012). Discussion

In the present study, hospital mortality remained substantial. However, late outcomes, especially freedom from infection-related death, were favorable, with no recurrent infections observed. These findings suggest that our current treatment strategy was crucial in enhancing the long-term prognosis in patients with AEF.

AEF is uncommon but devastating. Using a Japanese nationwide database, Iwahashi and colleagues1 reported a 0.27% incidence among all aortic surgical repairs, whereas Cutler and Mendeloff13 reported 0.15% among patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding, the incidence of secondary AEF increases to approximately 2%.14,15 With increasing aortic surgical interventions, the prevalence of secondary AEF has surpassed primary AEF.1 The true incidence of AEF may be underestimated because patients who were critically ill and died without treatment were excluded from databases.1 Although various etiologic factors and risk contributors have been described,16 exact mechanisms underlying secondary AEF formation remain unclear, complicating clinical decision-making and highlighting the need for standardized treatment strategies.

Before 2008, several nonstandardized strategies were attempted in a small number of patients, with unsatisfactory infection control. These early experiences informed the development of our current standardized strategy, which has been consistently applied since 2008. Changes after 2008 contributed synergistically to improved outcomes. First, radical resection, including complete infected aortic wall removal and mandatory esophagectomy, significantly reduced residual infection, a major cause of recurrent sepsis. Second, standardized in situ reconstruction using rifampicin-soaked Dacron grafts with routine omental or muscle flap coverage provided superior infection resistance compared with extra-anatomical bypass or limited debridement. Third, selective bridge TEVAR enabled rapid hemodynamic stabilization and patient optimization, facilitating planned definitive surgery under favorable conditions, although TEVAR alone cannot provide definitive treatment of AEF.1,17,18 Approximately 58 to 81% of patients with secondary AEF develop fever,18 and most patients with aortic infection are debilitated. Finally, the multidisciplinary team establishment enabled accurate diagnosis, appropriate surgical timing, and improved esophageal management coordination. These refinements collectively enhanced infection control, eliminated recurrent infection, and improved long-term survival.

Recent studies increasingly support aggressive approaches combining aortic reconstruction with esophagectomy.11,19,20 One-year survival rates of 42 to 46% align with our outcomes.19 Conversely, Mills and colleagues21 advocated primary esophageal repair for small defects (<3 cm) due to lower invasiveness; however, midterm outcomes appear inferior to aggressive strategies. These data support radical debridement and esophagectomy for infection control. Esophagectomy approaches include (1) simultaneous esophageal-aortic resection under CPB from the same surgical field or (2) separate resection via right thoracic approach. We favor simultaneous resection because the esophagus lies immediately posterior to the resected aorta, enabling straightforward removal without additional morbidity. Our esophageal surgeons complete this procedure in approximately 15 minutes. With secure multiorgan protection, slight CPB prolongation is inconsequential. For critically ill patients, staged approaches remain appropriate. Video-assisted transthoracic esophagectomy, which is a minimally invasive approach, is an established approach to reduce morbidity compared with open surgery.22,23 Regardless of approach, aortic grafting requires a completely clean surgical field.

Recently, highly aggressive one-stage approaches (simultaneous esophageal reconstruction, aortic reconstruction, and esophagectomy) have been reported.9 Although enabling omental coverage using a gastric tube, these approaches are excessively invasive for malnourished, debilitated patients with AEF and carry a high risk of anastomotic complication.24,25 Given that preoperative nutritional status strongly influences outcomes, staged esophageal reconstruction, using the antesternal route and a pedicled jejunal graft with supercharging, following nutritional optimization represents a safer approach.

With widespread TEVAR and frozen elephant trunk use for aortic arch repair, stent-related secondary AEF has become more prevalent, often involving complex arch pathology. In such cases, the ALPS approach is particularly advantageous because it provides excellent exposure from the aortic root to the descending thoracic aorta.8 The patient is placed in a right semirecumbent position, and rotation of the operating table is used to obtain both a median sternotomy-like view and a left thoracotomy-like view. This is the major advantage of this approach, allowing secure protection of multiple organs. CPB can be established in a conventional manner through median sternotomy, and myocardial protection can be reliably achieved using combined antegrade and retrograde cardioplegia. Left ventricular venting is performed through the right upper pulmonary vein. In addition, lower-body perfusion can be maintained by clamping the descending aorta and perfusing from the femoral artery, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of deep hypothermia. Furthermore, esophagectomy should be performed after resection of the involved aorta and radical debridement. At this stage, the esophagus is directly exposed posterior to the aortic bed, and no major structure remains between the operative field and the esophagus. This makes esophageal mobilization and resection straightforward through the same operative field. The wide operative field afforded by the ALPS approach facilitates not only radical debridement of infected tissues but also concomitant esophagectomy when required.

From our experience, successful management of AEF requires a multidisciplinary strategy organized around preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative principles. Preoperatively, prompt hemorrhage control and hemodynamic stabilization are essential, and bridge TEVAR is considered when anatomically feasible. Intraoperatively, definitive treatment requires radical resection of infected tissues, including esophagus, followed by extensive irrigation, in situ aortic reconstruction with a rifampicin-soaked Dacron graft, and graft coverage. Postoperatively, prolonged antimicrobial therapy, nutritional support, and rehabilitation are important to optimize the patient's general condition before staged esophageal reconstruction.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. It is a retrospective, nonrandomized, single-center study with a small sample size. Further prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate our findings and clarify the optimal management strategy.

Conclusions

The standardized strategy, incorporating bridge TEVAR, radical resection with mandatory esophagectomy, in situ reconstruction with rifampicin-soaked grafts, routine graft coverage, and multidisciplinary management, has substantially contributed to improved infection-related outcomes. Complete radical infection control under hemodynamically optimized conditions is essential for successful AEF treatment. Staged esophageal reconstruction after nutritional recovery ensures safety. As AEF continues to emerge as a complication of contemporary aortic interventions in the era of evolving endovascular technologies, the ALPS approach represents a beneficial and feasible treatment option. Our strategy offers a practical and effective approach to improving long-term outcomes.
