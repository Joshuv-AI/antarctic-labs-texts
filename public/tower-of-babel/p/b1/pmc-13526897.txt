Lecanemab in symptomatic PSEN1 p.Met233Val early‐onset Alzheimer's disease: Neuroimaging safety, regional amyloid‐PET dynamics, and longitudinal plasma biomarkers
Authors: Min‐Young Noh, Wonjae Sung, Hyunkee Kim, Bugyeong Son, Young‐Eun Kim, Yun Young Choi, David M. Holtzman, Seung Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe presenilin 1 (PSEN1) p.Met233Val variant is a Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network–Trials Unit (DIAN‐TU)–eligible autosomal‐dominant Alzheimer's disease (ADAD) variant for which lecanemab response data are unavailable.MethodsA 34‐year‐old symptomatic PSEN1 p.Met233Val carrier (APOE ε3/ε3) was prospectively followed through 26 lecanemab infusions over 12 months, with serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), and plasma biomarkers.ResultsNo amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities occurred. Composite Centiloid increased modestly (+7.2), masking regional divergence: basal ganglia (−24.5) and medial temporal cortex (−22.1) declined, whereas posterior cortical regions accrued amyloid. Plasma amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40 rose to 122% by T22, with concurrent decreases in phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p‐tau217), p‐tau181, neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score remained 0.5; caregiver reports noted improvements in spasticity, dystonia, and affective features.DiscussionLecanemab demonstrated neuroimaging safety and evidence of biological target engagement in this symptomatic PSEN1 p.Met233Val ADAD carrier. These longitudinal neuroimaging and plasma biomarker findings may help inform interpretation of treatment responses in genetically defined ADAD.

BACKGROUND

Autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease (ADAD) caused by pathogenic variants in presenilin 1 (PSEN1) is characterized by near‐complete penetrance, very early onset, and a causal relationship between amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation and neurodegeneration. 1 Among PSEN1 variants, the p.Met233Val variant (exon 7; ATG→GTG) is associated with very early onset and a pleiotropic phenotype, including spasticity, cerebellar signs, and psychiatric features that precede overt dementia. 2 , 3 Residue 233 lies at the substrate‐binding pore of γ‐secretase, 4 consistent with gain‐of‐function amyloid beta (Aβ) 42 overproduction that can elevate plasma Aβ42/40 ratios above thresholds typically observed in sporadic Alzheimer's disease (AD). 5

Lecanemab, a humanized antibody targeting soluble Aβ protofibrils, 6 has demonstrated clinical benefit in early sporadic AD, 7 and the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network–Trials Unit (DIAN‐TU) has opened anti‐amyloid treatment arms to ADAD carriers. 8 However, beyond a single brief case report involving the related p.Met233Thr variant, 9 no published lecanemab response data exist for the PSEN1 p.Met233Val variant despite its formal DIAN‐TU eligibility. Two specific uncertainties remain: whether the microvascular vulnerability associated with aggressive PSEN1 variants 10 translates into elevated amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) risk and whether the regional pattern of amyloid response in a high‐production ADAD variant mirrors the whole‐brain pattern seen in sporadic AD or instead shows region‐specific divergence.

We present a prospective 12‐month single‐case characterization of a 34‐year‐old PSEN1 p.Met233Val variant carrier treated with 26 lecanemab infusions at 2‐week intervals, focusing on three observational anchors: (i) neuroimaging safety, (ii) regional amyloid‐PET dynamics, and (iii) concurrent plasma biomarker profiling. We present this as a descriptive case report; no causal inference is made.

METHODS

This prospective single case was approved by the Hanyang University Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB) (HYUH IRB 2024‐02‐003‐003) and conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki; the patient gave written informed consent for publication. A 34‐year‐old symptomatic man carrying heterozygous pathogenic PSEN1 c.697A > G, p.Met233Val (pathogenic in ClinVar and Alzforum; APOE ε3/ε3) was enrolled on September 25, 2024. He received 26 lecanemab infusions at 2‐week intervals under stepwise dose escalation (200 mg × 2, 500 mg × 2, then 10 mg/kg), following the Korean lecanemab practical guide. 11 The first infusion (T1) was given on March 13, 2025; at the March 3, 2026 data cut‐off, 26 infusions were complete and treatment ongoing (timeline, Figure 1A). Baseline assessment included Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score and sum of boxes (CDR‐SB), and neurological examination. Baseline amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) (18F‐florbetaben) was performed 154 days before treatment; follow‐up PET occurred 343 days after initiation. Composite and seven‐region Centiloid (CL) values (frontal, posterior cingulate cortex [PCC]–precuneus, lateral temporal, lateral parietal, medial temporal, occipital, and basal ganglia, with hemispheric partitioning) were referenced to the standard Centiloid scale. 12 ARIA‐monitoring magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (FLAIR and hemorrhage‐sensitive sequences, dual neuroradiologist review) was performed at the sixth, 13th, and 26th infusions. Plasma at eight time points (T‐enroll, T0, T1, T3, T5, T7, T13, T22) was analyzed in duplicate on a Simoa HD‐X analyzer (Quanterix) for Aβ40, Aβ42, phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (p‐tau181), p‐tau217, neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). 13 , 14 , 15 Trajectories are presented descriptively as percentage of T0 (absolute values, Table S1). Analyses used Python 3.11 and GraphPad Prism 10.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We searched PubMed and Google Scholar through April 2026 for lecanemab treatment response in PSEN1 carriers. Apart from a brief report involving the related p.Met233Thr variant, no longitudinal imaging or plasma biomarker data have been published for the PSEN1 p.Met233Val variant, a DIAN‐TU–eligible mutation, before forthcoming DIAN‐TU NexGen read‐outs. Interpretation: This study provides the first 12‐month longitudinal characterization of lecanemab response in symptomatic PSEN1 p.Met233Val early‐onset AD. Although composite Centiloid increased modestly, region‐level amyloid‐PET revealed divergent responses, including reductions in the basal ganglia and medial temporal cortex. Plasma Aβ42/40 elevation was accompanied by concurrent decreases in downstream tau, neurodegeneration, and glial biomarkers. Across 26 infusions, lecanemab was neuroimaging‐safe, with no ARIA, and global CDR remained stable at 0.5, despite the minimal change in CDR‐SB. Future directions: These observations may serve as useful reference data for future DIAN‐TU and related ADAD studies, particularly for symptomatic carriers with substantial baseline amyloid burden. They support the incorporation of region‐level amyloid‐PET dynamics and longitudinal plasma biomarker profiling alongside composite measures, as these approaches may capture treatment‐related biological and clinically relevant signals – including changes in motor tone and affective features – that are not fully reflected by global CDR or composite Centiloid outcomes alone.

RESULTS

Baseline phenotype, 12‐month safety, and clinical stability

The patient presented with a decade‐long history of dysarthria followed by progressive gait disturbance. Examination showed predominant upper motor neuron features, including lower‐limb spasticity (Modified Ashworth Grade 2), brisk reflexes, sustained ankle clonus, and focal upper‐limb dystonic posturing, with reduced facial expressivity and blunted affect that the caregiver reported as preceding cognitive decline. Baseline cognition was MMSE 21, global CDR 0.5, and CDR‐SB 2, consistent with very mild impairment at the dementia threshold and characteristic of p.Met233Val. 2 , 3 All 26 infusions were completed without interruption. Protocolized MRI after the sixth, 13th, and 26th infusions showed no ARIA‐edema (ARIA‐E) or ARIA‐hemorrhage (ARIA‐H), with modest progression of cortical atrophy (grade 1 to 2; Figure S1). Across T1 to T22, global CDR stayed at 0.5, CDR‐SB 2.0 to 2.5, and MMSE declined modestly (21 to 19) (Figure 1C). From around the second to fourth infusion, serial clinical and concordant caregiver observations noted reduced lower‐limb spasticity and tone, improved dystonic posturing, and greater facial expressivity, spontaneous speech, and social engagement, sustained throughout but reported descriptively, without causal inference.

Regional amyloid‐PET divergence masked by composite

Baseline PET showed disproportionately prominent striatal deposition relative to cortex (Figure 1B); follow‐up showed visually reduced striatal uptake. Composite Centiloid rose from 86.26 to 93.49 (Δ+7.23 CL over 497 days), obscuring marked regional divergence (Table 1, Figure S2). The basal ganglia, with the highest baseline burden (193.2 CL), declined by −24.5 CL (−12.7%); the medial temporal cortex fell from 15.6 to −6.5 CL (Δ−22.1 CL; standardized uptake value ratio 1.11 to 0.97). Conversely, PCC–precuneus rose +13.9 CL (+16.9%) and lateral parietal +11.3 CL (+34.0%); frontal, lateral temporal, and occipital regions were stable. Medial temporal asymmetry reversed from right‐predominant to near‐symmetric (index −0.93 to +0.22).

Sustained plasma target engagement and downstream reductions

Plasma Aβ42/40 rose after the first infusion (T1, 104% of T0) to 122% by T22 (Figure 1D; raw values, Table S1), consistent with sustained engagement of the soluble compartment. The absolute ratio (∼0.23 to 0.28) is expected for PSEN1 carriers from γ‐secretase gain of function 5 and reflects pharmacodynamic engagement, not threshold crossing. Downstream markers decreased relative to T0 (Figure 1E,F): p‐tau217 to ∼80% by T22 (−20%), p‐tau181 to ∼94% (nadir ∼78% at T7), NfL to ∼79%, and GFAP to ∼88% (after an early T1 to T3 rise). Thus, by T22, all four downstream markers were reduced, coinciding temporally with sustained Aβ42/40 elevation and basal ganglia and medial temporal PET reduction, though the single‐case design precludes mechanistic inference.

DISCUSSION

This case offers three observations from ∼12 months of ongoing lecanemab in aggressive PSEN1 ADAD. First, lecanemab was neuroimaging‐safe across 26 infusions in a symptomatic p.Met233Val carrier with severe baseline amyloid burden, with no ARIA. Post‐codon‐200 PSEN1 variants carry severe leptomeningeal Aβ and microbleed susceptibility; 10 although APOE ε3/ε3 confers a lower‐risk stratum, 7 PSEN1‐associated cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) risk is APOE‐independent. Recently reported 6‐month DIAN‐TU NexGen data (N = 97 symptomatic ADAD; ARIA‐E 20.6% overall, 13.9% in ε4 non‐carriers) confirm elevated ARIA risk in ADAD without variant stratification 16 ; the absence of ARIA here across 26 infusions is therefore noteworthy. The clinical phenotype, dominated by spasticity, dystonic posturing, and pyramidal and pseudobulbar features out of proportion to cognition, aligns with the post‐codon‐200 transmembrane‐domain spectrum linked to spastic paraplegia and severe CAA, and variant location influences progression. 16 The striatal‐predominant baseline burden is a recognized PSEN1 hallmark. 2 , 3 , 17 , 18

Second, regional amyloid‐PET was heterogeneous. Striatal‐predominant retention is a characteristic PSEN1 imaging feature, distinct from the cortical‐predominant pattern of sporadic AD. 19 Despite a + 7.2 CL composite increase, the basal ganglia (193 CL baseline) declined −24.5 CL and the medial temporal cortex −22.1 CL, while PCC–precuneus and lateral parietal continued to accrue. This may reflect γ‐secretase gain‐of‐function sustaining Aβ42 overproduction 5 that locally outpaces clearance, lecanemab's preferential engagement of soluble protofibrils over fibrillar plaque, 6 and regional antibody‐penetration variation. The modest composite response is consistent with PK/PD modeling predicting slower reduction at younger ages, 20 relevant for this 34‐year‐old. Critically, basal ganglia clearance is invisible at the composite level, so region‐level quantification may capture treatment effects in aggressive ADAD that the composite reads as progression. The basal ganglia and medial temporal reductions also align temporally with the observed motor and affective improvements, which is hypothesis‐generating but not attributable to treatment.

Third, sustained Aβ42/40 elevation with concurrent decreases in p‐tau217 (−20%), p‐tau181, NfL (−21%), and GFAP (−12%) provides a within‐subject pattern in which target engagement and downstream tau, neurodegeneration, and glial markers move consistently with an amyloid‐directed response. Notably, plasma p‐tau217 exhibited a transient increase immediately after treatment initiation before subsequently declining. One possible explanation is transient mobilization of soluble tau species or an acute biological response accompanying rapid amyloid remodeling rather than worsening neurodegeneration. Overall, the subsequent trajectories of plasma p‐tau217 and p‐tau181 were consistent with amyloid‐driven biological change. 14 The downstream reductions oppose the rising NfL and GFAP expected from ADAD natural history. 15 Continued posterior cortical accrual despite plasma engagement suggests soluble and fibrillar compartments may respond divergently within one individual. Future studies or clinical trials in PSEN1 variants with prominent pyramidal, extrapyramidal, cerebellar, and neuropsychiatric features should include longitudinal standardized scales to objectively capture these non‐cognitive clinical domains, alongside cognitive, imaging, and fluid biomarker outcomes. 18

This study has several limitations. As a single open‐label case, all observations are descriptive and cannot establish treatment efficacy or causality. Tau PET and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers were unavailable, and baseline amyloid‐PET was performed 154 days before treatment initiation, limiting direct assessment of treatment‐related amyloid changes. Although serial plasma biomarkers provided dense longitudinal monitoring, the findings require validation in larger ADAD cohorts.

In conclusion, in this young symptomatic PSEN1 p.Met233Val carrier, 12 months of lecanemab was neuroimaging‐safe with sustained plasma target engagement. Regional amyloid‐PET diverged, with basal ganglia and medial temporal reductions alongside posterior accrual, together with downstream biomarker decreases and preserved CDR. Although no causal or efficacy inference can be drawn from a single case, this dense longitudinal profile suggests that lecanemab can engage amyloid biology in PSEN1 p.Met233Val ADAD and may generate regional and biomarker signals not captured by composite amyloid or global cognitive measures alone. This case highlights the importance of region‐level amyloid quantification, longitudinal plasma biomarker profiling, and standardized assessment of non‐cognitive motor and neuropsychiatric features in future DIAN‐TU and related ADAD studies.
