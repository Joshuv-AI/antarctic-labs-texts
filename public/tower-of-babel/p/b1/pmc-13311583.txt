Beyond health: a systematic review of cross-sector economic implications of school-based mental health programmes in Europe
Authors: Flora Wendel, Magdalena Walbaum, Celina Finger, Christian Brettschneider, Caroline Jung-Sievers, Martin Knapp, Eva A Rehfuess, Miqdad Asaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the economic evidence on school-based prevention and mental health promotion interventions, focusing on costs and outcomes across key societal sectors, including healthcare, education, social care, criminal justice, as well as in productivity and individual expenses.DesignA systematic review with narrative synthesis was conducted following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis approach, including an assessment of monetised outcomes across all sectors and an additional analysis of non-monetised outcomes in the education sector. Study quality was appraised using Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) and Consensus Health Economic Criteria (CHEC).Data sourcesMedline, Embase, PsycINFO, Econlit, ERIC and Scopus.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesEconomic evaluations of school-based prevention or mental health promotion interventions for children or adolescents in Europe that report outcomes across healthcare and other societal sectors.Results20 studies were included, comprising different types of economic evaluations as well as trial-based and model-based evaluations. Based on the primary analyses reported in the studies, which consider costs and benefits across multiple societal sectors, most evaluations suggested favourable economic outcomes. Benefits exceeded costs in four out of five (80%) cost–benefit analyses (average cost–benefit ratio of 5.14). Half of the cost-effectiveness analyses found that interventions were more effective but also more costly than the comparator while two interventions were both more effective and less costly. In cost–utility analyses, 9 out of 10 (90%) fell below commonly used thresholds for good value for money. Reported outcomes covered healthcare, education, social care, criminal justice sectors, as well as productivity and individual expenses. Pronounced cost savings from included interventions were observed in productivity and healthcare, with improvements also observed in non-monetised education outcomes. Study quality was mixed.ConclusionsSchool-based mental health interventions yield cross-sectoral economic benefits and, in many cases, societal returns on investment, in both the short and longer term, by influencing key determinants of health and well-being across multiple sectors.

Introduction

Mental health problems profoundly affect the lives and future prospects of children and adolescents, posing major public health challenges, with wide-ranging consequences for individuals, families and societies. In Europe, 10%–19% of school-aged children are affected by a mental health condition, with prevalence varying by age and gender.1 Ongoing socioeconomic, cultural, geopolitical and environmental megatrends are contributing to rising mental health problems among the young, while awareness, access to, availability and affordability of adequate preventive services and mental healthcare remain limited.2

Many mental health conditions have their onset in early adolescence and persist across the life course. These conditions have immediate impacts and often also have long-lasting repercussions in adulthood.14 They impose a substantial burden on individuals, including distress caused by the illness, as well as stigma, lower educational attainment, decreased employment opportunities, social and economic exclusion, which in turn reduce quality of life and increase the risk of premature mortality.2 The associated economic impacts arise from productivity losses, reduced lifetime earnings, welfare dependency, criminal justice involvement and increased use of health and social care services.257

To address this lifetime trajectory, preventive and promotive interventions are crucial. Implementing such interventions in schools—institutions attended by almost all children and adolescents over extended periods—represents a setting-based, near-universal approach that offers inclusive access to the target population, including disadvantaged groups. These interventions can also benefit from systemic and peer effects, amplifying their impact. Furthermore, established school infrastructures may support efficient implementation and cost-containment of programmes.2811

As interventions are often delivered through the education sector (eg, by teachers), focusing on educational outcomes is particularly relevant. In addition to impacts across multiple sectors, interventions can produce spillover benefits for other population groups (teachers, parents, siblings).1214 Limited consideration of these systemic or spillover effects, alongside potential ceiling effects in healthy populations, short follow-up periods and methodological challenges, may partly explain why research has so far found mixed effects or small effect sizes for intended health outcomes from school-based mental health programmes.27 1517 Nevertheless, even modest improvements at individual and population levels can yield substantial returns when long-term and cross-sector benefits are considered.2 6 7

Economists often consider health, education and productivity jointly, as they collectively determine overall welfare.1820 At the school level, good health and well-being support better learning, which in turn shapes future health behaviours, employment opportunities, income and wealth. Recent evidence also highlights the reinforcing role of learning in promoting broader mental well-being for children and adolescents across key domains—health, learning, connectedness, safety and resilience.2124

School-based mental health programmes may additionally influence other social determinants of health (SDH), including social position (eg, through social-emotional learning), exposure to violence or discrimination (eg, bullying), access to services (eg, counselling) and health behaviours (eg, substance use). These determinants contribute to health equity but are also shaped by structural power dynamics and resource distributions, highlighting the role of multisectoral and intersectoral policies, governance and social measures.25 26 By targeting multiple determinants simultaneously, school-based mental health interventions may improve student well-being, generate cross-sectoral benefits and enhance social equity.

Previous economic reviews in this field vary in scope and objectives. Some on school-based prevention and mental health promotion interventions target specific groups only, such as primary school children27 or those with pre-existing psychological problems.6 Other reviews focus on school-based interventions for physical health28 29 or include mental health prevention outside the school setting,30 are not limited to preventive interventions31 and rarely report outcomes beyond health in a disaggregated manner.627 28 3032 One review emphasising cross-sectoral returns in the education sector identified only physical health interventions, mostly from low- and middle-income countries.29 Additionally, a scoping review examined funding of intersectoral health programmes but did not assess intervention impacts.33 Economic implications extending beyond the health sector thus remain underexplored in both systematic reviews and primary research. Findings from cross-sectoral impact assessments could inform policy by exploring shared or flexible funding opportunities, enabling co-financing arrangements and guiding sector-specific priorities and value considerations.27 3436

Europe, defined here as the WHO European Region, comprises broadly comparable high-income countries that nonetheless vary substantially in school systems, welfare regimes and SDH. This combination makes it particularly well suited to examining the transferability and robustness of cross-sectoral economic evidence.

This systematic review aims to assess the economic evidence on school-based prevention and mental health promotion interventions, focusing on costs and outcomes extending beyond health, such as those associated with education, social care, criminal justice, productivity and individual expenses.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251002160). The reporting follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement37 (online supplemental appendix J) and the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis reporting guidelines.38

Search strategy and study selection

Searches were conducted in Medline, Embase, PsycINFO (via Ovid), Econlit, ERIC (via Ebsco) and Scopus on 23 March 2025. The strategy, developed with guidance from an information specialist, included terms and synonyms related to children and adolescents, schools, mental health interventions and economic analyses, and was adapted for each database (online supplemental appendix A). Database searches were complemented by hand-searching, screening reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and tracking research protocols to capture recently published results.

Studies were eligible if they targeted children and adolescents (<=19 years4) in European schools, aiming to ensure a degree of contextual comparability given broadly similar educational and healthcare infrastructures. Interventions had to be universal, aiming to promote mental well-being or prevent mental health problems. Those targeting or screening for diagnosed psychiatric disorders were excluded, while interventions for vulnerable groups (eg, children and adolescents presenting with subclinical symptoms or neurodevelopmental disorders) were included. Interventions were defined as school-based if delivered to students during school hours, on school premises, to entire classes or initiated or endorsed by schools or teachers. Programmes primarily targeting parents or teachers were excluded, although components targeting these groups were allowed, if they supplemented components targeting students. Studies with other primary aims (eg, obesity prevention or physical activity) reporting mental health outcomes only as secondary endpoints were excluded. No restrictions were applied regarding control conditions or publication dates. Eligible designs included full and partial economic evaluations,39 whether model-based or trial-based. Consistent with the review’s objective, studies were included if they extended beyond a healthcare perspective by reporting outcomes in at least one additional sector. All types of economic analyses were eligible, including cost–benefit analyses (CBA; monetising outcomes), cost–utility analyses (CUA; reporting costs per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY)) and cost-effectiveness analyses (CEA; reporting costs per effect in natural units).39 Studies that reported intervention costs alongside non-monetised outcomes beyond health, such as educational indicators, were also included and treated as cost–consequence analyses (CCA). Publications in English, Spanish and German were considered.

Selection process

Records retrieved through database searches were imported into Endnote40 for automatic de-duplication and complemented by manual de-duplication in Rayyan,41 a web-based screening platform, where title and abstract screening was carried out. Studies were screened by two independent reviewers (CF and FW), with discrepancies resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MW). Studies were excluded at this stage if the abstract did not mention any economic outcome or economic evaluation methods (eg, studies reporting only effectiveness data from trials). For all other eligibility criteria, an inclusive approach was adopted, forwarding studies to full-text screening whenever uncertainty existed. Full-text screening was conducted by two reviewers (CF and FW) with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (MW). Decisions were recorded in a purpose-built Excel sheet,42 documenting primary reasons for excluded studies and also separately listing studies that met all inclusion criteria but did not report outcomes beyond the health sector (online supplemental appendix B+C). Studies meeting all inclusion criteria were included in the final data extraction and synthesis.

Data collection and synthesis

Data were extracted from original publications and recorded in a purpose-built Excel42 sheet, collecting the following information: Basic study information (title, publication year, first author’s name, country), methodological aspects (economic study type, study design, perspective, time horizon, discounting: devaluation of future costs/effects, details on statistical analysis including adjustments and model specifications, methods for uncertainty and sensitivity analyses), intervention (type and characteristics, control condition, study population including age and sample size, costs of intervention(s), cost components), outcomes (sector of monetised outcome, measure and valuation of monetised outcome, year of cost data, currency, sector of non-monetised outcome, type and measure of non-monetised outcome), findings (effect estimate reported in base case analysis, subgroup or equity data, effects on monetised outcomes per sector, effects on non-monetised non-health outcomes). Data were extracted by one reviewer (FW) and checked for completeness and accuracy by a second reviewer (CF).

Effect sizes for monetised outcomes (cost consequences, such as costs of public sector service use) were presented as mean differences (MD) in costs per student, with adjusted MDs (for clustering and/or baseline variables) prioritised where reported in primary studies. In several cases, however, MDs had to be calculated manually based on unadjusted means. Statistical uncertainty, such as CIs or SD, was extracted whenever provided by primary publications. To enhance comparability, all monetary values were converted to 2025 Euros (Germany) using the EPPI-Centre Cost converter (up to 2022), which accounts for purchasing power parity,43 44 with subsequent inflation adjustments to 2025 based on country-specific indices.45 46 A single reference country (Germany) was used for pragmatic standardisation purposes, which does not affect comparability of results. Health outcomes were reported as QALYs in CUAs or as condition-specific natural units where appropriate, while non-monetised outcomes, particularly those related to education, were presented in their original study-specific measures.

Data were synthesised narratively and summarised in tables and figures, including bubble charts, with studies grouped by outcome sector and stratified by study design (trial-based vs model-based evaluations) to explore differences in evidence structure and time horizons. Due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs, interventions and outcomes, meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate. The analytical focus of this paper is on the cross-sectoral assessment. Results from the base case analyses (main analyses conducted in the respective publications) are presented for completeness but should not be interpreted as central to the review’s objectives. The Impact Inventory developed by the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine (USA)47 was used as a theoretical framework for classifying outcomes across sectors (online supplemental appendix D). This framework recognises that the relevant sectors under a societal perspective depend on the specific research question and context.47 Based on the included studies, a core set of sectors was derived including healthcare, education, social care, criminal justice, productivity and individual expenses (online supplemental appendix D). Monetised and non-monetised outcomes were categorised by the sector affected or budget impacted. Health professionals working within schools, such as educational psychologists or school nurses, were assigned to the healthcare sector, due to primarily medical roles and reimbursement structures.48 49 Costs associated with multisector services or with unclear financing sources were reported separately.

Heterogeneity was explored descriptively by grouping studies by outcome sector, study design and time horizon; no statistical methods were applied due to substantial heterogeneity.

The synthesis prioritised monetised outcomes, particularly those informing cross-sectoral cost differences. Non-monetised outcomes in education and other non-health sectors were reported, whereas detailed assessment of non-monetised health effects beyond those covered in base case analyses was outside the review’s scope and objective. Of all non-monetised outcomes, those in the education sector received particular emphasis, reflecting the fact that interventions are delivered within educational settings, often by school staff.

Subgroup analyses were not feasible due to substantial sector and outcome heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses of the cross-sectoral assessment were conducted for trial-based versus model-based studies.

Included studies were assessed against the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) checklist50 to evaluate the reporting quality of economic evaluations. Methodological quality, including aspects of internal validity and generalisability, was additionally appraised using the Consesnus Health Economic Criteria (CHEC) list.51 Reporting bias was assessed narratively. The confidence in our findings was assessed based on methodological quality (CHEERS/CHEC), consistency of effects and reported uncertainty across studies. No formal evidence grading system was applied, given the lack of a suitable approach and the heterogeneity of included study designs.

Patients and the public were not involved in this review; however, they were involved in some of the primary studies included in this review.

Quality appraisal

Assessment results for individual studies are presented in online supplemental appendix F. For reporting standards (CHEERS),50 notable gaps were identified regarding a health economic analysis plan, the characterisation of heterogeneity and distributional aspects, and engagement with children/adolescents or other affected persons. Uncertainty was not characterised in one quarter of studies. Appraising study quality (CHEC),51 a frequent limitation concerns the handling of uncertainty. Sensitivity analyses were absent or insufficient to jointly capture sources of uncertainty in many studies. Additional concerns in some studies included the absence of incremental analyses or a limited consideration of distributional aspects. To assess the item ‘all relevant costs for each alternative’ from the internally derived core set of sectors, at least five had to be reported, including education for trials and productivity for models. Only 5 of 20 studies adopted such a comprehensive societal perspective.606266 71

Further, concerns in outcome measurement and valuation were noted in almost all studies but, given the lack of robust or standardised alternatives, were not rated inappropriate according to the CHEC. These include reliance on self-reported outcomes and costs, which are prone to recall bias, social desirability bias and stigma, particularly in the context of mental health. The absence of standard tools for deriving QALYs in paediatric CUAs73 led to the use of various instruments, including adult EuroQol 5-Dimension scale (EQ-5D),53 58 61 68 70 youth-adapted EQ-5D-Y72 or Child Health Utility 9D Index (CHU9D).55 59 65 67

Many studies lacked prespecified economic evaluation plans, potentially affecting the completeness and transparency of reporting. Protocols identified in the searches were checked, although ongoing trials or analyses may partly explain missing publications. Lastly, studies reported negative effects alongside benefits, suggesting limited risk of reporting bias.

Intervention costs

Intervention costs ranged from €21.55 to €862.27 per student (2025 price level, Germany, table 2). Only two studies reported the cost of the alternative programme,56 57 and three explicitly adopted an opportunity cost approach, assuming zero cost for the control condition52 53 68; the remaining studies did not explicitly report control costs. There was considerable variation in the cost elements reported (table 3), with few studies accounting for expenditures beyond direct intervention costs, which are needed to reflect the full value of resources potentially employed elsewhere. For example, commonly reported elements included facilitator salaries, programme materials and training costs, but only three studies estimated the value of students’ time.52 61 62

Intervention outcomes

Intervention outcomes primarily comprised resource use related to public sector services, individual expenses, and monetary valuations assigned of selected conditions or behaviours (eg, productivity losses). Non-monetised outcomes included a range of health outcomes, education-related outcomes, and, in some cases, service utilisation (table 3).

Studies reported outcomes from two,53 54 57 64 65 70 three,5658 59 63 6769 four,52 55 or five sectors.606266 71 Productivity effects—such as changes in work absenteeism, presenteeism or income—were assessed in all model-based studies, whereas only one trial-based study included productivity outcomes, specifically parental productivity. Educational outcomes were assessed in 12 trial-based studies but only in one of the six model-based studies.

Findings of the base case analyses

Base case analyses

To contextualise the evidence on cross-sectoral outcomes, the base case characteristics are first described by type of economic evaluation, distinguishing between cost-effectiveness, cost–utility and cost–benefit approaches and between trial-based and model-based analyses. This overview provides the foundation for interpreting how different evaluation frameworks capture cross-sectoral and societal effects, before detailing the reported outcomes across sectors.

Base cases represent the main analyses conducted in the primary studies relating costs (intervention costs and cost differences in resource use) to effects (expressed in unit per health outcome (CEA) or in QALYs (CUA)) or relating intervention costs with the monetary value of outcomes (CBA). As all base cases adopt a societal perspective, reported costs represent aggregated cost differences and monetary values of outcomes across sectors covered by the respective study. Data and visualisations of the base case results by type of economic evaluation as well as an assessment of uncertainty are provided in online supplemental appendix G.

CEA, based on eight included studies: Half of the studies indicate that interventions were both more effective and more costly than controls.55 59 60 62 Two studies reported interventions being less costly and more effective,52 70 and in two studies the intervention was dominated by the control (more costly and less effective) for one outcome analysed.53 67

CUA, based on ten included studies: Four interventions were found to be more effective and less costly than controls (dominance).55 68 70 71 Three interventions would be cost-effective at a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of ≤€20 000 per QALY gained,58 59 67 one at a WTP ≤€30 000 per QALY65 and one at a WTP ≤€50 000 per QALY.61 These values lie within the range of thresholds typically applied by European health technology assessment agencies to guide funding decisions in public healthcare systems.74 75 Only one intervention was less effective and more costly than its control.53

CBA, based on five five included studies: All but one intervention56 showed benefits exceeding costs, with an average benefit–cost ratio of 5.14.

Distributional aspects

No study applied a distributional CEA (DCEA),76 77 but four studies reported distributional effects disaggregated by subgroup.58 61 62 68 Cost-effectiveness was higher among students with a low socioeconomic status,68 elevated depression scores,58 boys from deprived backgrounds,61 as well as adolescents with lower educational levels62 and those from older age groups (≥17 years).62

Monetised outcomes

Cost differences between intervention and control groups reported for both healthcare and selected non-health sectors related to service utilisation and monetary values assigned to specific conditions or behaviours. Studies varied in the number and types of services, conditions or behaviours considered, as well as in their applied time horizons. Total societal effects (excluding intervention costs) suggested an overall trend toward cost savings or economic benefits exceeding costs. Sector-specific results were mixed; education, social care, criminal justice and individual expenses showed both savings and increases among students receiving the intervention, whereas the productivity and healthcare sectors more consistently showed cost savings or economic benefits exceeding costs. Non-monetised outcomes

Across studies, non-monetised outcomes in non-health sectors predominantly captured effects on service utilisation without associated cost estimates. As such, in the education and social care sectors, some studies reported higher use of targeted support58 60 in the intervention group, while others found lower use of general support.71 In the criminal justice sector, differences in service contacts were negligible.60 The use of privately financed or services with unclear financing varied, with minor increases in helpline use58 60 and reductions in youth club attendance60 and digital tool use.58

Outcomes not directly related to service utilisation were rarely reported with the exception of education-related outcomes. These outcomes are presented in figure 3, with full details provided in online supplemental appendices H & I. Additionally, some studies reported non-monetised outcomes for which sectoral attribution beyond health could be debated, for instance vandalism, family relationships or non-consensual sex.

Educational outcomes (non-monetised).

Over 60 non-monetised educational outcomes were reported, often showing mixed effects.57 60 65 67 Significant effects favouring the intervention included student social behaviour, relationships, engagement and school climate,55 69 academic achievement and learning,69 truancy and student exclusions,56 69 as well as teacher-related outcomes such as behaviour, well-being or working conditions.54 69 Effects favouring the control were consistently minor and non-significant.

Overall, the certainty of evidence varied by analytical focus, with both assessments affected by heterogeneity in study designs, interventions, outcomes and time horizons. For the base case analyses (overall societal costs and effects, certainty was assessed as moderate, as the consistency of findings across economic evaluation types—with most interventions showing favourable outcomes—supports confidence in the overall direction of effects. For the cross-sectoral assessment, certainty was assessed as low, further driven by considerable uncertainty in effect estimates and incomplete capture of societal effects.

Discussion

This systematic review of economic evaluations aimed to assess the costs and outcomes of school-based prevention and mental health promotion interventions across multiple sectors.

Our main findings indicate that such interventions have cross-sectoral impacts in education, healthcare, social care, criminal justice, as well as on productivity and individual expenses. Within individual sectors, results were subject to substantial uncertainty, although cost savings and economic benefits were particularly pronounced in productivity and in healthcare. While monetised outcomes in the education sector did not provide unequivocal evidence of economic benefit, the non-monetised outcomes indicated a positive trend, the societal value of which remains to be quantified. Across sectors, model-based studies tended to show a trend towards cost savings and employed longer time horizons compared with trial-based studies. The results of the base cases (based on the aggregated effects across sectors) mostly indicated cost-effectiveness ratios within common WTP thresholds (CUA), higher effectiveness and higher costs of interventions (CEA) and benefits often exceeding costs (CBA). Overall, the quality of evidence was mixed. Certainty was rated moderate for the base case analyses and low for the cross-sectoral assessment.

Previous research has emphasised the wide-ranging impacts of poor mental health, including multi-sectoral costs and potential benefits of school-based interventions.2 7 Yet, findings from this review are marked by substantial uncertainty regarding the extent to which such interventions consistently generate economic gains across sectors.

Some studies even reported higher costs in certain sectors for intervention groups. Beyond statistical uncertainty, possible explanations include modest incremental effects due to active control conditions, increased awareness and help-seeking resulting from the intervention or societal trade-offs where gains in one sector may be offset by costs in another. Rather than diminishing their value, this underscores the complexity of cross-sectoral impacts and the need for both comprehensive evaluation and intersectoral coordination. Moreover, the positive trend in non-monetised educational outcomes suggests that some benefits may not yet be fully captured by current economic assessments.

While educational outcomes are commonly reported alongside health outcomes in clinical studies of school-based health interventions,78 79 the broader set of sectors that may be affected is rarely captured in clinical and public health research. For economic evaluations, the consideration of multiple sectors is conceptually embedded in the societal perspective. However, the studies included in this review demonstrate the practical challenges of applying a comprehensive societal perspective, with sectoral impacts variably operationalised, quantified or reported. The patterns observed in the included studies suggest that healthcare and productivity outcomes are most commonly reported, while social care, criminal justice, individual expenses and educational (monetised) outcomes are less frequently included. This may reflect established conventions in economic evaluation as well as differences in data availability and ease of monetisation and attribution. This imbalance may be particularly relevant for school-based interventions, where educational impacts are central, suggesting a need for more systematic inclusion of cross-sectoral outcomes in future evaluations.

Supporting evidence therefore remains limited and largely methodological, underscoring the need for standardisation and highlighting current knowledge gaps.29 36 80 81 Verguet et al29 found a small positive effect of school-based health programmes on the education sector (measured in Learning-Adjusted Years of Schooling (LAYS)) and suggested assessing impacts in social protection, agriculture and gender, based on included studies of physical health programmes in low resource settings.29 In a European context, Drost et al80 proposed components for multisectoral benefit assessment, including education and criminal justice. A costing study81 identified education, household and leisure, labour and social security, and healthcare as key sectors, with opportunity costs largely borne by education and offsets accruing primarily to households.81

The findings of this systematic review must be interpreted in light of two key methodological challenges in economic evaluations, particularly pronounced in mental health, prevention and interventions for children and adolescents.

First, choosing time horizons that adequately capture effects emerging or intensifying later in life is challenging, while discounting may reduce the apparent value of long-term preventive effects. The trial-based studies included in this review may have underestimated the benefits by excluding longer-term impacts, and often reported small or mixed economic gains. In contrast, the model-based studies often reported larger cost differences, likely reflecting the inclusion of long-term effects - particularly healthcare cost savings and productivity gains—even though these were discounted. This difference is likely driven by the structural ability of modelling studies to extrapolate beyond observed trial data, capturing downstream effects that occur later in life and across sectors, whereas trial-based studies are constrained by shorter follow-up periods. However, model-based estimates depend on assumptions regarding effect persistence, transition probabilities and cost trajectories, which introduces additional uncertainty and may result in both upward or downward bias depending on model structure.

Second, while CUAs using QALYs enable comparison across interventions and are often favoured by policymakers, their validity for mental health outcomes remains debated.82 Established instruments such as EQ-5D83 84 focus narrowly on anxiety and depression and show ceiling effects,85 86 whereas CHU9D,87 developed for children and adolescents, seems to better capture mental health, well-being, daily activities and school work.85 86 Despite these shortcomings, QALYs remain useful against WTP thresholds and sector-specific instruments, such as LAYS88 in education and the Adult Social Care Outcomes Toolkit (ASCOT89) in social care could provide valuable complements.

Limitations of the evidence include heterogeneity, uncertainty, incomplete capture of societal effects and inconsistent reporting, which reduce the strength and generalisability of conclusions. In the absence of established standards for a societal perspective, many studies focused on one or two non-health sectors, specific services or reported aggregated results. Consideration of full opportunity costs was not observed, the inclusion of which could have enabled a more thorough assessment of trade-offs across sectors.36 Inconsistent reporting of cost-related uncertainty estimates or necessary data resulted in unquantifiable uncertainty of the cross-sectoral analysis. Distributional aspects were underexplored, with little consideration of how benefits and costs were shared across population groups.90 Geographical bias was present, likely reflecting cross-country variation in the use of economic evidence for decision-making and funding. In addition, differences in national health, education and social care systems as well as variation in costing and reporting practices across countries may limit the comparability of economic outcomes. Given this heterogeneity in institutional and policy contexts, the findings should be interpreted as indicating overall patterns of economic value rather than precise, transferable effect sizes, underscoring the importance of contextualised economic evidence.

Review-level limitations include possible omission of clinical studies reporting costs only in full text and context-dependent variation in sectoral attribution of outcomes (eg, school nurses). The synthesis is further limited by substantial heterogeneity across study designs, interventions, outcomes, and time horizons, which required reliance on narrative synthesis and grouping by sector and study design. While this approach facilitated a structured comparison, it may obscure within-group variation and context-specific effects, meaning that findings should be interpreted as indicative patterns rather than precise effect estimates. Strengths include broad database coverage across health, education, and economics, combined with an inclusive search strategy and a structured cross-sectoral framework for extracting and synthesising economic outcomes. The review procedure follows current methodological standards, including protocol registration, systematic methodology and reporting,91 while the dual appraisal of quality goes beyond common practice. Importantly, this review introduces a novel and systematic approach to capturing wider societal impacts by drawing on economic literature and applying a structured assessment of monetised and non-monetised outcomes across sectors. This contributes to advancing methodological development in prevention research and provides a foundation for more holistic economic evaluations in the future.

Implications for research and policy

Future studies should prioritise robust trial-based and model-based economic evaluations adopting a comprehensive societal perspective, with disaggregated reporting of outcomes and costs across healthcare, education, social care, criminal justice, productivity and individual expenditures, reflecting the core set of sectors identified in this review. Well-conducted trials would provide the empirical foundation for modelling studies assessing longer-term impacts. Spillover effects on families, teachers and communities warrant systematic examination, while new approaches are needed to value non-monetised outcomes, for instance, school climate or student engagement. To assess the distribution of outcomes across the population, beyond subgroup or scenario analyses, DCEA could capture equity impacts and societal value judgements. Finally, robust methods to estimate opportunity costs are essential to assess trade-offs across sectors. Establishing sector-specific and outcome-specific WTP thresholds (eg, using generic measures), and eliciting stakeholder or public preferences, could further enhance transparency and guide multisectoral decision-making. Recognising that contextualised evidence is needed to inform decision-making, research should align with national, regional or local policy objectives and use methods linking economic evidence to real-world financing and implementation processes to support evidence-based policy.

This review highlights the potential of school-based mental health interventions to generate benefits across multiple sectors and societal returns, underscoring the need for increased investment in prevention and mental health promotion, and a shift from single-sector funding toward coordinated, multisectoral approaches. In light of rising mental health needs among children and adolescents and broader demographic changes, including an ageing population and a declining working-age population, strengthening mental health in younger generations is urgent, as current investments are likely to translate into future gains in productivity, healthcare demand, and social care needs. Strengthening the evidence base through contextualised, policy-oriented analyses can support co-financing arrangements and optimise resource allocation, thereby enhancing well-being, health, equity and societal returns.
