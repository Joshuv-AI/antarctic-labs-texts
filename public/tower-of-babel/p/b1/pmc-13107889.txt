Silicon mitigates combined cadmium and microplastics toxicity in rice by regulating glyoxalase system, and phytochelatin-mediated cadmium detoxification
Authors: Mehmood Ali Noor, Huang Guoqin, Muhammad Bilal Chattha, Baoyuan Zhou, Muhammad Umair Hassan, Jameel M. Al-Khayri, Othman Al-Dossary, Bader Alsubaie, Wael Fathi Shehata, Mustafa I. Almaghasla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCadmium (Cd) and microplastics (MPs) are common contaminants in paddy fields, posing a serious challenge to rice productivity. Silicon (Si) is widely used globally to counteract abiotic stresses; however, its role in mitigating combined toxicity of Cd and MPs remain unknown. Therefore, this study examined the different mechanisms mediated by silicon to counteract the combined Cd and MPs toxicity in rice.MethodologyThe study performed in CRD design with eight treatments: control (Cd- and MPs free soil), Cd -contaminated soil (20 mg kg−1), MPs contaminated soil (1%), Cd + MPs, control (Cd and MPs free soil) + Si, Cd + Si, MPs + Si, and Cd + MPs + Si. Silicon (SiO2) was sprayed (4.5 mmol L−1) at the tillering and jointing stages.ResultsCadmium and MPs decreased rice yield (~ 40%) by increasing oxidative damage and Cd accumulation, while decreasing chlorophyll production (~ 55%), hormonal synthesis (~ 70%), phytochelatin production, and soil nutrient availability (~ 46%). Silicon mitigated combined Cd and MPs stress by significantly improving chlorophyll content (84.44%) and enhancing antioxidant activities, along with increasing the proline (36.15%) and key phytohormones, including gibberellic acid (97.58%), indole-3-acetic acid (85.93%), jasmonic acid (90.20%), and salicylic acid (69.95%) synthesis. The improved growth was also associated with reduced methylglyoxal (MG) production (23.91%) due to enhanced activity of leaf glyoxalase-I (Gly-I) (31.11%) and glyoxalase-II (Gly-II) (45.45%). Silicon decreased Cd accumulation via increasing soil nutrient availability and phytochelatin (139.20%) production, while decreasing the expression of OsNRAMP1 (21.85%) and OsHMA3 (7.36%) genes involved in Cd uptake.ConclusionIn conclusion, Si alleviates the combined Cd and MPs toxicity by improving redox balance, restricting Cd uptake through phytochelatin induction and gene regulation, and restoring photosynthesis, osmolyte status, and hormone signaling, thereby reducing oxidative stress in rice. These results support Si-based approaches to enhance rice productivity in Cd- and MP-contaminated agroecosystems.

Introduction

The rice cropping system is crucial for global food security; however, the environmental sustainability of this system is increasingly threatened by the accumulation of pollutants [1]. Cadmium (Cd) is a toxic and bioavailable heavy metal, readily accumulating in paddy soils through agricultural inputs and industrial activities [2]. Cadmium compromises soil health and rice productivity and raises serious food safety concerns [3]. It is a persistent and toxic heavy metal that accumulates in soils and plants, and poses health issues [3, 4]. Its high mobility, with a substantial fraction in a readily exchangeable form, enables rapid plant uptake from both soils and atmospheric deposition, causing toxic impacts in plants [5]. Once absorbed, Cd moves through root apoplastic pathways into vascular tissues, disrupting chlorophyll synthesis, altering leaf anatomy, and impairing photosynthetic performance [6–9]. Cadmium accumulation also triggers oxidative stress [10], damage cellular membranes [11, 12], and interferes with nutrient uptake, thereby causing yield losses [13, 14]. Recent studies have reported a considerable decrease in rice growth and grain yield due to Cd stress [3, 15]. The concentration of Cd in paddy fields is reaching critical levels due to industrial and agricultural activities, necessitating the development of effective measures to remediate Cd -polluted soils [16, 17]. Biochar, phyto-hormones, nutrients, and microbial treatments have emerged as an effective tool to mitigate the Cd toxicity in rice [15, 18–21].

Adding to this longstanding challenge is the emerging issue of microplastics (MPs) pollution, which has recently been shown to infiltrate plants directly through airborne deposition onto leaves [22]. In rice fields, airborne microplastics can be transferred to the edible parts consumed by humans and livestock, providing a direct route into the terrestrial food web [23]. They induce oxidative stress, accumulate in plant tissues, and block root pores, impairing nutrient uptake and triggering oxidative stress [24–26]. MPs increase oxidative stress, affecting growth and yield, and thereby cause economic losses [27]. Moreover, MPs also block root pores, reducing nutrient and water uptake and contributing to excessive ROS production [26]. They also cause lipid peroxidation, decrease chlorophyll synthesis, and damage cellular membranes and photosynthesis [28]. In soils, MPs disrupt microbial communities, alter physicochemical properties, and influence heavy metal dynamics [29–33]. Microplastics also reduce rice yield; for instance, polyethylene MPs caused a reduction of 8.2% and 6.9% in grain yields of the common rice Nangeng 5055 and a hybrid rice, Jiafengyou 6 [21]. A recent study also reported a reduction of > 62% in rice yield due to MPs' toxicity [34]. They interact with metals such as Cd, modifying their bioavailability and accumulation in plants [35–40]. They act as carriers of heavy metals, and this interaction facilitates availability of toxicity metals, leading to significant damage [41, 42]. The presence of MPs favors Cd translocation in roots, triggers oxidative responses, and modifies soil–plant systems in ways that may exacerbate ecological and food safety risks [43, 44]. Therefore, it is necessary to develop cost-effective measures to mitigate the effects of Cd and MPs on soil–plant systems.

Different amendments such as biochar, silicon, and melatonin are used globally to alleviate Cd toxicity and mobility [45]. Among them, silicon application can modify soil chemical conditions, and increase the availability of sorption sites that facilitate cadmium retention. These changes, together with shifts in microbial activity, stabilize organic matter and encourage the development of Cd-binding phases, creating multiple pathways that collectively limit Cd bioavailability in paddy soils [46]. Silicon lowers heavy metal availability in the rhizosphere of different plant species (Poaceae family) by decreasing free-ion activity and promoting chelation and co-precipitation at the root–soil interface—processes aided by Si-induced root exudation and pH shifts that reduce uptake [47–51]. Within roots, Si accumulates in the cell wall (e.g., lignin matrix), where it complexes with metal ions and promotes their precipitation or immobilization, thereby limiting apoplastic movement toward the vascular tissues [50, 52–55]. As a structural defense, Si deposition as amorphous silica and phytoliths reinforces physical barriers and, together with Si-mediated regulation of metaltransport and chelation genes, restricts root-to-shoot translocation [47, 52, 56–59]. Functionally, Si enhances antioxidant enzyme systems and ROS scavenging, decreases Cd uptake and translocation, and improves Cd -mediated oxidative stress tolerance [47, 52, 59–61]. Silicon lowers cadmium uptake in plants and restricts its movement from roots to shoots [13, 62]. For the mitigation of MPs impacts, Si improves physiological functions and activates protective antioxidant pathways. Specifically, Si mitigates the detrimental impacts of MPs by enhancing photosynthetic performance, membrane stability, and antioxidant defenses [63–67]. Furthermore, it also reduces MP-induced oxidative stress, growth inhibition, and disruption of phytochemical synthesis by regulating photosynthetic efficiency and ascorbate–glutathione cycle [68].

This indicates that the co-exposure of Cd and MPs induces more toxic impacts on plant growth. Recently, the role of Si has been reported in mitigating MPs toxicity, however, its role in mitigating the combined effects of Cd and MPs is not yet understood. We hypothesized that exogenous Si application would mitigate Cd + MPs toxicity by limiting Cd uptake and translocation and alleviating MP-induced stress through coordinated regulation of physiological functions, hormonal balance, stress-defense systems, and Cd-related gene expression. Consequently, this research was aimed; i) to investigate the impacts of Si on physiological functioning and rice production under Cd and MPs toxicity; ii) to assess the impacts on antioxidant activities, the glyoxalase system, and hormone synthesis under Cd and MPs toxicity; and iii) to evaluate the impact of Si in regulating Cd uptake, translocation, and gene expression involved in Cd uptake under Cd and MPs toxicity.

Materials and methods

Experiment details

A pot experiment was conducted using soil (0–30 cm depth) taken from research area of Jiangxi Agricultural University. The study was carried out from 29 July to 29 October 2024. The experimental site is characterized as a humid sub-tropical with mean temperature of 18 °C and the soil is classified as silt loam. The collected soil was sieved to remove debris, and each pot was filled with 10 kg of soil (dry weight). The soil was acidic in nature (pH 5.51), with organic carbon (OC) of 11.78 g kg−1, total nitrogen (TN) of 1.65 mg kg⁻1, and available phosphorus (AP) and available potassium (AK) of 34.11 and 115.98 mg kg⁻1, respectively. The desired Cd concentration according to treatments was achieved using CdCl₂ (sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China). For this, soil samples from the pots were taken, mixed thoroughly with the Cd salt, and equilibrate at 70% field capacity under dark conditions. This process was carried out for two months to stabilize Cd in the soil. After stabilization, the soil was taken out and microplastics were mixed according to the treatment plan. The polyvinyl chloride with a 50 µm size was used in the study. The plastic pots were then filled, water was applied, and 25-day-old rice seedlings of cultivar Zhongjiazao 17 (five per pot) were transplanted into each pot (30 × 24 × 15 cm). A handheld shower was used to maintain water (2–3 cm) in each pot during the growth period to ensure good stand establishment. All plants were raised under ambient temperature and natural light in a greenhouse. The study followed CRD design with following treatments, each having four replicates: control (Cd- and MPs -free soil), Cd -contaminated soil (20 mg kg⁻1), MPs -contaminated soil (1%), Cd + MPs, control (Cd- and MPs -free soil) + Si, Cd + Si, MPs + Si, and Cd + MPs + Si. The desired concentration of Si was achieved using sodium metasilicate. Silicon was sprayed at a concentration of 4.5 mmol L−1 (SiO2) at the tillering and jointing stages for two consecutive days. Sodium metasilicate was as Si source which was provided by Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., LTD. MPs were acquired from BaseLine Co., Ltd. (Tianjin), and the information about their surface morphology is presented in Fig. 1. Silicon applied after 20 and 30 days of transplanting as foliar spray. The rate of silicon was selected based on previous studies showing that Si from 4.5 mM mitigates MPs toxicity [68]. Plant samples for growth and biochemical analysis were taken after 30 days of foliar spray, and final yield parameters were determined 100 days after transplanting. The sterilized instruments, along with analytical grade chemical were used to get the reliable results. Measurement of photosynthetic pigments and leaf water status

Fresh top leaves were collected and homogenized using 80% acetone at 0–4 °C. Leaf samples weighing 0.25 g were taken for biochemical analyses. After grinding, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected, and absorbance was measured at 663, 645, and 470 nm to estimate the concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids [69]. All absorption measurements were conducted through TU-1810 UV–Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Purkinje General Instrument. Beijing, China). For leaf relative water content (RWC), freshly collected leaves were weighed to determine the fresh weight (FW). The same leaf samples were water soaked for 24 h, after which they were removed and weighed again to obtain the turgid weight (TW). Finally, the leaves were oven -dried at 65 °C to determine the dry weight (DW) and RWC calculated as: (FW − DW)/(TW − DW) × 100.

Measurement of oxidative stress markers, and antioxidants activities and osmolytes synthesis

For measuring electrolyte leakage (EL), leaves were boiled (40 °C) for 30 min, and the first electrical conductivity (EC₁) was recorded. The same leaves were then boiled again at 90 °C for 10 min, and the second EC value (EC₂) was measured. EL was calculated as: EL (%) = (EC₁/EC₂) × 100. For measuring MDA content, freshly collected leaves (0.5 g) were ground using phosphate saline buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8). The mixture was homogenized for 15 min at 6000 × g. Then, 0.5 ml supernatant and 1 mL of each trichloroacetic acid (TCA: 20%) and 0.5 TBA solution were mixed. Then it was heated at 95 °C for 15 min and then cooled. After allowed to cool down. Later its centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min, absorbance was recorded at 532 nm and 600 nm to determine MDA content [70]. For measuring H₂O₂ concentration, fresh leaves (0.5 g) were ground in 1 mL of TCA (0.1%), and the supernatant was collected. It was then mixed with 1 mL of potassium iodide (1 M) and 10 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (PPB), and later absorbance was taken at 600 nm [71]. To measure ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity, a 3 mL reaction mixture was prepared containing 100 µL enzyme extract, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.3 mM ascorbic acid, 0.06 mM H2O2 and 100 mM PPB and reading done at 290 nm [72]. For catalase (CAT) mixture comprising 2 mL enzyme, 10 mM H₂O₂, and 50 mM PPB was made and reading measured at 240 nm [72]. For peroxidase (POD) activity, the reaction mixture contained 0.05 mL enzyme extract, 0.995 mL PPB (100 mM), 0.05 mL H₂O₂ (0.75%), and 0.25 mL guaiacol (0.25%). Absorbance was measured at 460 nm to determine POD activity [73].

Moreover, the standard methods of Dhindsa et al. [74] was used for measuring SOD activity by taking absorbance at 560 nm, and results were expressed as U mg−1 protein. For quantifying methylglyoxal (MG), fresh leaves (0.5 g) were ground in HClO4 (5%), then their centrifugation (115,000 rpm) was done for 12 min. The supernatant was obtained, treated with charcoal, and neutralized with sodium carbonate. Later the mixture consisting of enzyme extract, N-acetyl-L-cysteine, and PPB was made and reading measured at 288 nm to estimate MG concentration. In the case of glyoxalase I (Gly-1), the mixture comprising 700 µL enzyme extract along with 100 mM PPB, 3.5 mM MG, 15 mM magnesium sulfate, and 1.7 mM GSH was made, and the reading was measured at 240 nm to determine Gly-I activity [75]. Moreover, for Gyl-II the mixture containing 0.2 mM DTNB, 100 mM Tris–HCl buffer, and 500 µL enzyme extract was prepared. This mixture was then added to SLG, and absorbance was measured at 412 nm to estimate Gly-II activity [76].

For measuring leaf proline content, freshly collected leaves were ground in 3% sulfosalicylic acid and homogenized (10,000 rpm) for 10 min. A mixture containing 2 mL of acid ninhydrin, glacial acetic acid, and enzyme extract was heated for 60 min. After cooling, 2 mL toluene was added, and absorbance was recorded at 520 nm [77]. For total soluble protein (TSP), leaves (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5 mL PPB, then solution subjected to centrifugation (14,000 rpm) for 15 min. Then, supernatant and 2 mL Bradford reagent was mixed and reading measured at 595 nm [78]. For measuring phyto-hormones; 0.1 g of leaf samples was taken and subjected to homogenization following centrifugation (5000 rpm) for 15 min at 4 °C. The collected supernatant was used for ELISA assays, and the concentrations of gibberellin, auxin, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Meimian).

Measurement of plant Cd concentration, growth and yield traits

Plant samples were collected and dried at 65 °C until constant weight. They were then digested using hydrochloric acid (HCl) and nitric acid (HNO3) in a 1:2 ratio. The digested samples were filtered, diluted by adding water and Cd concentration was determined using atomic absorption spectrometry. The rice crop was hand -harvested after 90 days. Roots and shoots were collected to measure root length (RL), root fresh weight (RFW), and root dry weight (RDW). Spike length was recorded from five randomly selected spikes, and the harvested plants were weighed for measuring biomass yield (BY). Grains were separated to estimate grain yield (GY), and the harvest index (HI) was calculated as the ratio of GY to BY.

Measurement of soil properties

The soil samples were carefully brought to the laboratory and debris was removed to prepare the samples for determining different properties. The soil and water were mixed in a 1:5 ratio to make a paste, and later pH was measured with a pH meter. Soil TN was determined withKjeldahl method [79], and AP and AK were estimated with spectrophotometer and flame photometer methods, respectively [80, 81]. The soil samples were digested using HNO3 and HCl, then filtered and further diluted by adding water, and Cd was measured with atomic absorption spectrometry. Moreover, different Cd fractions were determined with the protocols of Tessier [82].

Determination of phytochelatin synthesis and gene expression

Fresh leaves were collected and homogenized using liquid nitrogen, and then these leaf samples were mixed with 1 mL extraction solution consisting of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid and 0.3 mM diethylene triamine pentaacetic acid (DTPA). The supernatant was then taken and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The samples were filtered (45 µm nylon syringe), and the concentration of phytochelatin (PC) was estimated using HPLC [83, 84]. To measure gene expression, rice samples were collected, and RNA was extracted using an RNA kit (Takara, China). For synthesizing cDNA, 2.5 µL of RNA was used, and reverse transcription was performed using the HiScript® III RT SuperMix kit. Thereafter, real-time PCR was performed using 1 µg of RNA, which was reverse transcribed into cDNA (FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix Kit). Subsequently, the SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) Kit (Tiangen; FP205–2) was used to perform qRT-PCR. Gene expression was analyzed following the procedure of Livak and Schmittgen [85].

Statistical analysis

For the analysis of the different traits, a one-way ANOVA was performed using Statistix 8.1 software. Furthermore, Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s test were employed to text the normality and homogeneity of data. The significance among treatments was determined using the honestly significant difference (HSD) test at p < 0.05. Figures were prepared using SigmaPlot, while PCA and correlation analyses were performed using R-Studio (1.4.1564).

Results

Effect of silicon on growth traits of rice under Cd and MPs toxicity

The results show that Cd and MPs significantly inhibited root growth, biomass, and plant height, with combined Cd–MPs exposure causing more severe growth suppression than either stress alone (Table 1). Co-exposure to Cd and MPs significantly decreased RL, RFW, RDW, and plant height (PH) by 138.18%, 70.43%, 66.54%, and 44.49%, respectively, compared with the control. Cadmium toxicity decreased RL, RFW, RDW, and PB by 129.67%, 55.98%, 45.09%, and 33.97%, respectively, while MPs decreased RL, RFW, RDW, and PB by 116.4%, 33.01%, 39.11%, and 28.97%, respectively, compared with the control. Silicon enhanced RL, RFW, RDW, and PB by 45.53%, 27.65%, 28.73%, and 13.08%, respectively, under Cd + MPs, and it enhanced RL, RFW, RDW, and PB by 66.33%, 26.55%, 28.22%, and 9.12%, respectively, under Cd stress alone. Moreover, exogenous Si supply enhanced RL, RFW, RDW, and PB by 80.54%, 28.63%, 34.11%, and 11.69%, respectively, under MPs toxicity alone (Table 1). Cadmium and MPs, particularly their co-exposure, significantly hindered the 1000 -grain weight, GY, and BY (Table 1). However, under Cd, MPs, and Cd + MPs conditions, Si application alleviated growth inhibition and significantly improved 1000-GW, BY, and GY of rice (Table 1). The results showed that Cd + MPs toxicity decreased 1000-GW, BY, and GY by 95.80%, 40.60%, and 47%, respectively, compared with the control. Effect of silicon on oxidative markers and antioxidant activity under Cd and MPs toxicity

The synthesis of EL, MDA, and H₂O₂ increased by 279.14%, 104.05%, and 142.55%, respectively, under Cd + MPs stress. Silicon offset the accumulation of these oxidative stress markers (Table 2). Under Cd + MPs conditions, Si supply significantly decreased EL, MDA, and H2O2 were increased by 34.69%, 42.67%, and 43.72%, respectively (Table 2). The results depicted that Cd and MPs stress reduced antioxidant enzyme activities (APX, CAT, POD, and SOD), with combined Cd + MPs causing greater inhibition than individual stresses. Si application significantly enhanced antioxidant activity under both single and combined Cd and MPs conditions (Fig. 2). Silicon enhanced APX, CAT, POD, and SOD activity by 34.75%, 22.58%, 40.21%, and 46.42%, respectively, under Cd + MPs pollution (Fig. 2). Silicon caused a marked reduction of 20.77%, 15.20%, and 23.91%, respectively, in MG synthesis under Cd, MPs, and Cd + MPs-polluted soil. The concentrations of Gly-I and Gly-II increased under stress conditions, with the maximum increase observed under Cd and MPs stress alone compared with their combined exposure (Fig. 3). However, Si supply augmented the concentration of both Gly-I and Gly-II under Cd, MPs, and Cd + MPs stress. Discussion

The effects of multiple stresses are more complex and severe compared to single stress. This study explored impacts combined Cd and MPs. We observed that Cd + MPs considerably decreased rice yield by reducing water and nutrient absorption and increasing oxidative stress [86]. Plant roots are the primary organs for nutrient and water absorption, and in this study, MPs facilitated the entry of Cd into roots, reducing root length and surface area. This alteration decreased water and nutrient uptake, resulting in substantial growth losses [87–89]. Microplastics hinders ability of roots to access nutrients and thus decrease plant growth [90]. Cd entry into plant cells disrupts chlorophyll by replacing Mg, damages membranes, suppresses antioxidant and microbial activity, reduces nutrient availability, and ultimately inhibits plant growth [91–94]. Co-exposure to Cd and MPs also increased H₂O₂ and MDA levels, which damage cellular membranes and lead to the loss of essential solutes. This could also explain the significant reduction in plant growth. We observed that Si significantly enhanced rice growth and yield, indicating that Si may have promoted cell development and counteracted negative impacts, thus improving growth. Silicon application reduced Cd uptake and accumulation by lowering its availability, likely through enhanced phytochelatin synthesis (Table 3), thereby reducing Cd-induced oxidative damage and improving growth. Moreover, Si also decreased the expression of genes linked with Cd uptake, which consequently reduced Cd accumulation in rice plants. The findings clearly indicate that Si enhanced antioxidant activity, proline synthesis, hormone production, and nutrient uptake, all of which contributed to maintaining better growth under Cd + MPs toxicity [95]. Furthermore, Si application maintained better water balance, as indicated by increased RWC, resulting in improved plant growth [96, 97].

Chlorophyll contents directly impact the rate and intensity of photosynthesis, thus affecting plant growth [98]. This study demonstrates that MPs combined with Cd seriously inhibited chlorophyll synthesis, aligning with previous studies reporting that pollutants cause chloroplast damage and hinder chlorophyll synthesis [99]. The results also indicated that Cd and MPs increased ROS production and decreased nutrient availability [89, 100, 101]. Notably, combined Cd and MPs also decreased carotenoid synthesis, which led to a reduction in ROS removal owing to the fact that carotenoids act as ROS scavengers [102]. The increase in oxidative stress damaged the cellular membranes as indicated by enhanced MDA production align with previous studies reporting the same findings [103]. Silicon increased chlorophyll synthesis by promoting carotenoid synthesis, and protecting chlorophyll from oxidative damage [104, 105]. We also found that Si decreased the Cd– and MPs–induced oxidative damages by increasing antioxidant activity, hormonal balance, and osmolyte synthesis, thereby improving chlorophyll synthesis [106]. Furthermore, Si also improves stomata conductance and photosynthetic rates, which increases the stability of chlorophyll thus increases its synthesis under stress conditions [107].

Plants possess an excellent defense system to counter ROS, and improved antioxidant activity plays a crucial role in ROS elimination [108]. Hydrogen peroxide and MDA are key indicators representing lipid peroxidation and stress intensity [109]. Cadmium and MPs enhanced ROS production, which affected DNA transcription, leading to suppression of antioxidant activity [110]. Exposure to a combination of Cd and MPs led to downregulation of antioxidant genes, resulting in a weakened antioxidant defense mechanism and diminished plant performance. This aligns with earlier studies reporting that a decrease in antioxidant gene expression reduces antioxidant activity and subsequent plant growth [111, 112]. This decreased gene expression may result from increased ROS production mediated by combined Cd and MPs stress. The elevated ROS, along with decreased antioxidant activity, indicates that membrane integrity was compromised. Damaged cell membranes facilitate Cd and MPs absorption by roots, leading to increased Cd and MPs accumulation and subsequent oxidative damage. The results showed that Si application significantly offset Cd- and MPs -mediated increases in MDA, EL, and H2O2 production by enhancing the expression of antioxidant genes, aligning with earlier findings [113, 114]. We also observed that the activities of all antioxidants were less elevated under combined Cd + MPs stress compared with individual Cd or MPs stress. This may be because combined Cd and MPs exposure enhanced ROS production, which compromised the scavenging capacity of antioxidants, thus resulting in a smaller increase in antioxidant activities [115].

Plants growing under stress regulate osmotic balance by adjusting the synthesis of osmolytes and hormones [116]. Cadmium and MPs augmented proline synthesis while decreasing FAA and TSP synthesis. The reduction in TSP and FAA synthesis under Cd and MPs stress was associated with impaired N uptake, whereas Si application significantly enhanced proline, TSP, and FAA synthesis, consistent with its role in promoting osmoprotective compounds under abiotic stress [65, 117]. Cadmium and MPs also disturbed hormonal balance, as indicated by substantial decreases in hormone synthesis. However, Si supply significantly enhanced hormone synthesis under Cd and MPs stress. This restoration might be linked to enhanced antioxidant activity and carotenoid synthesis, which serve as ROS scavengers [118]. We observed that Si supply enhanced IAA synthesis under Cd and MPs toxicity. It could be speculated that Si supply might have decreased phenol content, thereby leading to an increase in IAA synthesis under Cd and MPs toxicity. It is well acknowledged that GA and JA play crucial roles in stress tolerance by interacting with other phytohormones [119, 120]. Our findings showed that Si enhanced GA, IAA, JA, and SA contents under combined Cd and MPs toxicity. This might be because under Cd and MPs toxicity, Si alleviated photo-inhibition and upregulated the synthesis of all phytohormones [68, 121].

The size and surface charge of MPs play a crucial role in metal absorption. Microplastics enhanced Cd accumulation in plant tissues. In the growth medium, plants absorb toxic metals through roots; nevertheless, MPs reduce metal sorption capacity, thereby increasing Cd availability and accumulation in plant tissues [89, 122]. However, the impact of MPs on metal uptake and accumulation is highly complex. For instance, MPs promoted the growth of Brassica and tomato plants by decreasing Cd availability [123, 124]. Conversely, other researchers observed that MPs increase metal uptake [125], aligning with our results. Microplastics accumulate metals on their surface owing to a larger surface area and stronger hydrophobicity [90], thereby there increase metal availability [112]. Soil properties strongly affect toxic metal levels in Cd- and MPs -polluted soils, as MPs alter Cd adsorption–desorption processes and increase its availability [126]. According to Abbasi et al. [127], Cd and Pb are adsorbed onto MPs and later desorbed within the rhizosphere soil. Microplastics also influence the activity of microbes involved in Cd cycling by altering soil nutrient availability and enzyme activity [44, 111]. Microplastics promote the expression of the cadA/czcA genes in soil microbes, thus enhancing Cd toxicity and bioavailability [44]. Silicon application significantly decreased the Cd uptake and accumulation (Table 3) by decreasing expression of Cd uptake genes (OsNRAMP1 and OsHMA3). Previously, Zhou et al. [128] also found that Si reduced Cd uptake by downregulating the expression of the Cd transporter (TaNramp5). It is also plausible that Si facilitates Cd chelation, thereby limiting Cd binding to microplastics and ultimately reducing Cd availability and plant uptake [95]. Silicon supply also increased the soil which might also reason of decrease in Cd availability and its accumulation. Moreover, decrease in Cd accumulation by Si also results from its reducing impacts on Cd uptake.

Although this study demonstrates that Si effectively mitigates the combined Cd and MPs toxicity in rice, there exist several limitations. The present study conducted in pots which may not fully reflect field environments where soil and climatic variability could alter silicon responses. In addition, only one rice cultivar and narrow range of Cd and MPs concentrations were examined, which restricts the broader applicability of results. Future studies should therefore validate these findings under field conditions and across multiple rice cultivars and soil types. Integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches would further explain the regulatory mechanisms involved. Finally, cost–benefit analyses of silicon application in contaminated soils should be evaluated before recommending widespread agricultural use.

Conclusions

Cadmium and micro-plastics reduced rice yield by inducing oxidative stress, increasing Cd accumulation, and reducing photosynthetic pigments, osmolyte and hormone production, phytochelatin synthesis, and soil nutrient availability. Silicon up-regulated multiple protective pathways to counteract Cd and MPs toxicity. Under Cd + MPs stress, Si enhanced stress tolerance by regulating photosynthesis, antioxidant activity, osmolyte and hormone synthesis, while decreasing cadmium accumulation through lower availability and suppressed expression of cadmium uptake transporters. The improved growth with silicon was also associated with reduced methylglyoxal production due to enhanced activity of glyoxalase-I and II, increased phytochelatin synthesis, and improved soil nutrients availability. Overall, silicon represents a promising strategy to mitigate Cd + MPs toxicity in rice, although field validation, cost assessment, and advanced omics studies are needed before broad application.
