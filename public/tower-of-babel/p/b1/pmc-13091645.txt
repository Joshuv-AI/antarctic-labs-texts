The Burden of Laryngeal Cleft in Children With Refractory Asthma: A 10‐Year Multicenter Experience
Authors: Aledie Navas Nazario, Andre Rebaza, Emily Craver, Paula Martínez de Pinillos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionUncontrolled asthma and chronic cough are prevalent pediatric respiratory conditions frequently associated with delayed diagnosis of structural anomalies such as laryngeal cleft (LC). LC can exacerbate respiratory symptoms via chronic aspiration and recurrent infections, complicating asthma management. The study objective was to compare demographic and clinical characteristics of children with LC and asthma or chronic cough and evaluate the impact of LC diagnosis and treatment on health care utilization.MethodsRetrospective review of children with LC and uncontrolled asthma or chronic cough across five Nemours Children's Health sites from 2014 to 2024. Patients underwent diagnostic laryngoscopy/bronchoscopy and swallow studies. Health outcomes, including antibiotic and systemic steroid use and respiratory‐related hospitalizations, were compared pre‐ and post‐LC treatment. Subgroup analyses were performed for children under and over 2 years old.ResultsAmong 53 patients, children with asthma were significantly older than those without asthma (median age 28.5 months vs. 13 months, p = 0.001). Type 1 LC predominated in both groups. Children with asthma demonstrated greater respiratory health care utilization, more frequent antibiotic use (52% vs. 16%, p = 0.01), and greater hospital admissions. LC treatment reduced antibiotic use but did not significantly reduce overall respiratory health care utilization in children with asthma. Subgroup analyses mirrored full cohort trends, with persistent post‐treatment respiratory burden in children with asthma.ConclusionsChildren with asthma and LC demonstrate greater respiratory morbidity and health care utilization, both before and after LC repair. Multidisciplinary care and targeted asthma management remain essential even after anatomical correction.Keywords: aerodigestive disorders, aspiration, asthma, chronic cough, pediatric otolaryngology, pulmonary comorbidities

Introduction

Uncontrolled asthma and chronic cough are among the most prevalent pediatric respiratory disorders, leading to significant morbidity, increased health care utilization, and substantial economic burden [1, 2, 3]. Approximately 44% of children with current asthma experience uncontrolled symptoms, with a higher prevalence in boys (49.9%) compared with girls (36.2%) [4]. These conditions often require a thorough diagnostic evaluation to identify underlying structural or functional abnormalities contributing to persistent symptoms.

Laryngeal cleft (LC) is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by an abnormal connection between the larynx and esophagus, which can lead to chronic aspiration, recurrent respiratory infections, and worsening pulmonary symptoms [5, 6]. Aspiration occurs when food, liquid, or saliva enters the trachea and lungs, potentially causing lung inflammation and chronic cough [7] and increasing the risk of lower respiratory tract infections [8]. Early recognition and intervention are crucial, particularly in pediatric patients with persistent cough or poorly controlled asthma that is unresponsive to standard therapies.

Aerodigestive centers [9, 10], which employ a multidisciplinary approach integrating pulmonology, gastroenterology, otolaryngology, and speech pathology, have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing health care utilization by decreasing emergency department (ED) visits and primary care encounters [11]. These centers play a critical role in diagnosing complex conditions such as LC, particularly in children presenting with recurrent croup, chronic cough, or unexplained respiratory symptoms. By facilitating early diagnosis and coordinated management, multidisciplinary care models improve clinical outcomes and reduce the burden of respiratory morbidity in affected children [12, 13].

This study aims to compare demographic and clinical characteristics of children with LC with and without asthma and to evaluate the impact of LC diagnosis and treatment on respiratory health care utilization.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nemours Children's Health (IRB# 2125767‐3). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Study Design

A retrospective chart review was conducted on pediatric patients with LC and a history of uncontrolled asthma or chronic cough during a 10‐year period between April 2014 and April 2024. Patients aged 0–17 years who were evaluated at Nemours Children's Health locations in Orlando, Pensacola, Delaware, Jacksonville, and Lakeland were included if they underwent instrumental swallow evaluation and laryngoscopy/bronchoscopy that confirmed the presence of LC as part of their diagnostic evaluation. Patients with controlled asthma or comorbidities known to cause chronic cough, including tracheostomy dependency, cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency, cardiac disease, or bronchopulmonary dysplasia, were excluded from the analysis.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and included demographics, clinical characteristics, diagnostic methods, treatment modalities, health care utilization, comorbidities, and treatment outcomes. Demographic variables included age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), and hospital location. Clinical characteristic variables included age at LC diagnosis, which we reported in months, as over half of the cohort were less than 2 years of age, and presence and severity of asthma when applicable. Asthma diagnoses were established by board‐certified pediatric pulmonologists across participating sites, with one diagnosis made by a pediatric allergist with expertise in asthma management. Diagnoses were based on specialist clinical evaluation consistent with contemporary pediatric asthma guidelines, incorporating recurrent respiratory symptoms, response to therapy when applicable, and longitudinal follow‐up. Severity classification was determined according to documented specialist assessment aligned with guideline‐based criteria. Children younger than 12 months were analyzed separately due to diagnostic challenges in infancy. The “without asthma” group included patients who underwent subspecialty evaluation and were not given a diagnosis of asthma following clinical assessment.

Aspiration diagnoses were confirmed through structured chart review of instrumental swallow evaluations. Confirmation required documented evidence of aspiration on videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES).

Additional variables included LC classification, chronic cough diagnosis if present, and comorbidities, specifically gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), dysphagia, and congenital anomalies. Diagnostic variables included laryngoscopy/bronchoscopy and instrumental swallow evaluation results. The treatment variable focused on management of LC, particularly whether patients received no intervention, injection of Prolaryn (Merz Pharmaceuticals, Frankfurt am Main, Germany), or surgical repair. In addition, medication utilization was assessed, including antibiotic usage and systemic steroid usage, each over a 1‐year period pre‐ and post‐cleft treatment. Lastly, health care utilization variables included ED visits and hospital admissions for respiratory indications during the same pre‐ and post‐treatment periods.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline patient characteristics were summarized with the sample median (range) for continuous variables. For categorical and ordinal variables, the number and percentage were reported. Patient characteristics were compared according to the presence of an asthma diagnosis. Unadjusted comparisons were made between the two groups using a Wilcoxon rank sum test (continuous and ordinal variables) or a Fisher's exact test (categorical variables). Subgroup analyses were also performed by stratifying the cohort according to age at diagnosis (less than 2 years, and at least 2 years and older at LC diagnosis). Additional subgroup analysis was performed for children less than 12 months of age.

p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and all statistical tests were two‐sided. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Full Cohort Analysis

Baseline Characteristics

Children with asthma and LC were significantly older than those with LC without asthma (median 28.5 months vs. 13 months, p = 0.001, Table 1). The most common LC classification in both groups was type 1 cleft: 83% in those without asthma and 95.5% in those with asthma. Among patients with asthma, most had an asthma classification of mild persistent asthma. There was no difference between groups with regard to BMI, ethnicity, hospital site, or comorbidities, including GERD, dysphagia, and congenital anomalies. Overall, 71% of the population had a chronic cough.

Subgroup Analysis in Children Less Than 2 Years

Baseline Characteristics

When analyzing patients less than 2 years of age, the full cohort decreased to 32 patients: 24 without asthma and 8 with asthma (see Table S1). Patients with asthma were older than those without asthma. There was no difference based on sex, BMI, ethnicity, hospital site, or chronic cough. A higher proportion of patients without asthma had congenital anomalies compared with those with asthma (63% vs. 25%). As in the full cohort, the most common asthma classification was mild persistent asthma. Aspiration was confirmed on instrumental swallow evaluation in all patients with asthma and in all but four patients without asthma.

Health Care Utilization and Outcomes

Antibiotic prescriptions were significantly higher among children with asthma prior to cleft treatment (63% vs. 13%, p = 0.01, Table S2). Although not significant, there was a larger decrease in antibiotic use post‐cleft treatment among patients with asthma (43% vs. 8%, Table S3). There was no significant difference between groups in systemic steroids prescription, ED visits, or hospital admissions (see Tables S2 and S3).

Subgroup Analysis in Children 2 Years and Older

Baseline Characteristics

Twenty‐one patients were at least 2 years old in the full cohort: 14 patients with asthma and 7 without asthma (see Table S4). There was no difference based on sex, BMI, ethnicity, hospital site, or comorbidities. All patients had type 1 LC, and, unlike in the younger cohort, the most common asthma classification was moderate persistent asthma. Most patients had aspiration detected on swallow evaluation.

Health Care Utilization Before and After Treatment of LC

There was no significant difference between groups in antibiotic prescription, systemic steroid use, ED visits, or hospital admissions pre‐ or post‐treatment (see Tables S5 and S6).

Patient Outcomes After Treatment of LC

Antibiotics were prescribed more frequently in patients with asthma post‐treatment (36% vs. 0%, Table S6). There was no difference based on systemic steroid prescription, although both groups saw a decrease in average prescriptions post‐treatment. There was no difference based on BMI, ED visits, or hospital admissions.

Subgroup Analysis in Children Less Than 12 Months

Baseline Characteristics

Seventeen patients less than 12 months were in the cohort, with only 2 patients with asthma (Table S7). There was no difference based on sex, BMI, ethnicity, hospital site, or comorbidities.

Health Care Utilization and Patient Outcomes

There was no significant difference between groups in antibiotic prescription, systemic steroid use, ED visits, or hospital admissions pre‐ or post‐treatment (Tables S8 and S9).

Discussion

Our study highlights key differences in baseline characteristics and health care utilization between children with LC with and without asthma. The findings provide insight into how asthma may influence the clinical course of LC and its management.

We observed that children with LC and asthma were significantly older than their counterparts without asthma. This suggests that children with asthma may experience a delayed diagnosis or presentation of LC, potentially due to overlapping symptoms such as chronic cough. Dysphagia and micro aspiration, which are seen with LC, can also contribute to wheezing episodes, leading to clinical suspicion for uncontrolled asthma [9] among patients who underwent LC repair at an outside institution; half of these patients had GERD or asthma, further providing possibility of a delayed diagnosis [10]. Interpretation of age at diagnosis must be considered within the developmental challenges of diagnosing asthma in early childhood. In children younger than 2 years, recurrent cough and wheeze may reflect a spectrum of airway pathology, including aspiration‐related inflammation, rather than classic asthma. Importantly, type 1 LC was the predominant classification in both groups, aligning with prior literature that emphasizes type 1 as the most frequently diagnosed form. Additionally, there were no significant differences between groups in terms of comorbidities, including GERD and dysphagia, suggesting that the primary differentiating factor between these cohorts is the presence of asthma itself rather than other underlying conditions.

In addition, antibiotic prescriptions were higher among patients with asthma. This difference may reflect a greater frequency of respiratory infections or presumed bacterial superinfections in patients with asthma. The higher rate of antibiotic use among patients with asthma has also been documented in other studies, which highlights the importance of asthma education among clinical providers to minimize overprescribing unnecessary antibiotics [14, 15, 16]. There were no significant differences in systemic steroid use or ED visits, although hospital admissions were higher in patients with asthma, suggesting that coexisting asthma contributes meaningfully to respiratory morbidity in children with LC.

Persistent asthma‐related health care utilization following LC repair should not be interpreted as failure of anatomic correction. Rather, these findings likely reflect the presence of underlying airway inflammatory disease in a subset of patients whose respiratory symptoms are not solely attributable to aspiration. Surgical correction addresses the structural defect but does not modify intrinsic asthma biology. Recognition of this distinction is essential when interpreting post‐treatment utilization patterns.

The use of inhaled corticosteroids in patients without a formal asthma diagnosis may reflect attempts to reduce airway inflammation in children with aspiration‐related respiratory symptoms. However, evidence supporting this practice is limited, and unnecessary corticosteroid exposure carries potential risks. Careful differentiation between aspiration‐driven symptoms and asthma is critical to guide appropriate therapy. Higher antimicrobial and systemic steroid use in certain groups may reflect greater symptom burden or perceived exacerbations rather than confirmed asthma severity. Distinguishing aspiration‐related chronic cough from asthma exacerbations is essential, as antibiotics and systemic corticosteroids carry significant adverse effect profiles.

In the subgroup of children younger than 2 years, patients with asthma were older than those without asthma. Unlike in the full cohort, congenital anomalies were more prevalent among patients without asthma (62.5% vs. 25%). Aspiration was nearly universal in both groups, reinforcing its role in symptomatology and clinical presentation at this young age.

Antibiotic use post‐treatment in children older than 2 years remained exclusive to the group with asthma (36% vs. 0%), suggesting that while LC treatment may reduce aspiration‐related complications, underlying asthma may continue to contribute to respiratory vulnerability.

When analyzing between infants (less than 12 months), 88% of the cohort did not have asthma. Among this group, two infants had type 2 LC and 3 children had type 3 LC classification at diagnosis. As noted earlier, type 1 LC was seen in all children older than 2 years of age, which illustrates the point that type 1 LC may be frequently missed and not detected until later in life.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

Our findings suggest that children with asthma and LC experience higher health care utilization both before and after LC treatment, emphasizing the need for ongoing respiratory management beyond surgical intervention. The persistent burden of respiratory symptoms in children with asthma, even after treatment, underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving pulmonologists, otolaryngologists, and speech‐language pathologists to optimize outcomes. Recognizing key characteristics more consistent with LC without asthma including feeding‐related symptoms, documented aspiration on instrumental swallow evaluation, and limited bronchodilator responsiveness, may help reduce unnecessary escalation of asthma‐directed therapies and prompt earlier diagnostic evaluation.

Future research should explore whether specific asthma management strategies, such as enhanced inhaled corticosteroid regimens or early pulmonary interventions, could mitigate post‐treatment health care utilization. Additionally, longer follow‐up may help determine whether respiratory health care utilization declines over time following LC repair in children with coexisting asthma.

Conclusion

Children with LC and asthma exhibit greater health care utilization compared with their counterparts without asthma, both before and after treatment. While LC repair is essential for improving swallowing function and reducing aspiration, it does not fully address the underlying inflammatory airway disease present in children with comorbid asthma. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive respiratory management and close multidisciplinary follow‐up in this population.
