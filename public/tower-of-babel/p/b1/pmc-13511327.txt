Does Long‐Term Lower Extremity Strength Training in Adults With Knee Osteoarthritis and Varus Alignment Reduce Knee Joint Loading During Gait?
Authors: Stephen P Messier, Matthew N Vigliotti, Paige E Rice, Brian Pietrosimone, Shannon L Mihalko, Edward H Ip, Richard F Loeser, David J Hunter, Ali Guermazi, Ryan Hill, Santiago Saldana, Kim L Bennell, Paul DeVita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe examined whether 18 months of strength training in individuals with knee varus alignment and medial tibiofemoral osteoarthritis (OA) reduced knee joint loads during walking compared to an attention control group.MethodsThis study was a secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial that compared the effects of strength training to a control group in adults with knee OA. For this analysis, control participants had knee varus malalignment (≥2° varus; N = 49); participants in the strength training group met the varus malalignment criterion and increased their hip abductor strength by ≥20% from baseline to 18‐month follow‐up (N = 39). Linear regressions were used to compare means between groups at 18 months.ResultsThe strength training group had greater increases in strength in the quadriceps (45%), hamstrings (68%), and hip abductors (42%) than the control group (16%, 11%, 4%, respectively; P < 0.05). There were no significant differences in the mean peak internal knee abduction moment or mean peak knee compressive force between groups at 18‐month follow‐up. The adjusted means at 18‐month follow‐up for the internal knee extension moment were significantly less (27%) in the strength training group (P adjusted = 0.03).ConclusionAmong older adults with knee OA and varus alignment, long‐term lower extremity strength training results in significant increases in strength but does not significantly alter most measures of knee joint loading during walking relative to an attention control group. The results cast doubt on whether clinically meaningful improvement in lower extremity muscle strength translates to clinically important attenuation in knee joint loading.

INTRODUCTION

Arthritis is the leading cause of disability among adults, accounting for $304 billion in annual lost earnings and medical care costs. 1 Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common type of arthritis, 2 and the knee joint accounts for 85% of the total burden of the disease. 3 OA is a progressive disease that affects the entire joint and surrounding tissues including cartilage, bone, synovial membrane, ligaments, muscles, and tendons, resulting in pain, stiffness, inflammation, and limited mobility. Symptoms related to OA have a negative effect on an individual's daily function, overall health, and quality of life. 4 , 5 , 6 Identifying strategies that can reduce disease incidence and limit disease progression are important public health objectives. 7

Among older adults with knee osteoarthritis and varus alignment, long‐term lower extremity strength training results in significant increases in strength compared to an attention control group but does not significantly alter most measures of knee joint loading during walking. The results cast doubt on whether clinically meaningful improvement in lower extremity muscle strength translates to clinically important attenuation in knee joint loading.

The relationship between OA and muscle strength may be bidirectional; OA has a negative impact on muscle strength, but persistent muscle weakness may also hasten OA progression. 8 Compared to healthy controls, hip and knee isometric and concentric strength are significantly attenuated in patients with knee OA, generally between 16% to 27% less. 9 , 10 Eccentric strength deficits compared to controls can range as high as 76%. 10 Meta‐analysis data also indicate that low knee extensor and flexor strength worsen structural knee OA, although the authors noted that the included studies had low certainty of evidence. 8 Possible mechanisms that may explain the relationship between muscle weakness and knee OA include altered joint loading and the lack of muscle strength necessary to absorb forces that are transferred across the joint, resulting in increased coactivation and a disruption to cartilage/bone homeostasis. 11 Conversely, low strength may result in joint underloading or load redistribution, which can also be detrimental for cartilage/bone health. 12 , 13

Frontal plane knee alignment plays a role in knee joint load distribution, with knee varus malalignment resulting in an increase in medial compartment joint loading. 14 , 15 People with knee OA and varus‐aligned knees have greater knee joint loads approximated by the internal knee abduction moment. 16 Varus‐aligned knees result in a 33% increase in the loads on the medial compartment compared to individuals with neutral‐aligned knees. These larger loads on the medial portion of the knee joint may cause cartilage, bone, and other synovial joint alterations. 17 Varus malalignment is also related to knee OA progression and degradative changes to the joint. 18 , 19 Sharma et al 20 found that varus alignment measured at baseline was correlated with a four‐fold increased risk in knee OA progression at 18‐month follow‐up.

Chang et al 21 discovered a greater internal hip abduction moment at baseline was associated with decreased risk of medial tibiofemoral OA progression. For every one unit increase in the internal hip abduction moment, there was a 50% decrease in the risk of OA progression. Consequently, hip muscle strengthening to increase the hip abduction moment, reduce contralateral pelvic drop, and thus shorten the distance from the knee joint center to the projection of the ground reaction force became a popular therapeutic recommendation, although empirical evidence supporting this mechanism is unreported. 22 A 9% reduction in the internal knee abduction moment was attributed to an exercise regimen that strengthened the hip and knee musculature. Not all hip abductor strengthening interventions, however, found supporting results. Studies that specifically targeted hip abductor strengthening for individuals with knee OA and varus alignment found that those in the strengthening group increased hip muscle strength, improved physical function, and reduced knee pain but did not reduce medial knee joint loads. 23 , 24 Similarly, improved clinical outcomes without a change in joint mechanics was seen in a short‐term quadriceps strengthening clinical trial. 25 Taken together, these studies suggest that improved joint mechanics may not be essential for muscle strengthening to have a positive effect on clinical outcomes in people with knee OA. The short duration of these interventions (two to three months), however, was an important limiting factor.

We hypothesized that individuals with knee varus malalignment and medial tibiofemoral OA with at least a 20% improvement in hip abductor muscle strength consequent to 18 months of strength training will have attenuated knee joint loading during walking, including lower internal knee abduction and knee extension moments, and reduced peak bone‐on‐bone knee joint compressive forces, compared to an attention control group.

METHODS

Study design

START was an assessor‐blinded, single‐center (Wake Forest University, Winston‐Salem, North Carolina), randomized clinical trial with three parallel groups followed for 18 months. The trial protocol is published elsewhere. 26 The Human Subjects Committee of Wake Forest Health Sciences approved this protocol. Participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Participants in this randomized clinical trial (N = 377) were ambulatory, community‐dwelling adults, age ≥50 years with self‐reported disability due to knee OA. Enrollment occurred between July 2012 and February 2016. Inclusion criteria for the parent trial were as follows: (1) mild to moderate radiographic medial tibiofemoral OA (Kellgren–Lawrence score = 2, 3) in at least one knee and no to moderate patellofemoral OA noted on skyline view radiographs, (2) neutral (−2° valgus to 2° varus) or varus (≤10° varus) mechanically aligned knee, 27 (3) body mass index (BMI) 20 to 45 kg/m 2 , and (4) no participation in formal strength training >30 minutes/wk in the past six months. All participants continued their medications, including nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs. If pain decreased, they could reduce medications with their physician's approval. Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) severe patellofemoral OA (joint space width (JSW) = 3 using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International atlas from skyline x‐ray views 28 ), (2) lateral > medial JSW, and (3) <20 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. 29

This secondary analysis was limited to START participants who (1) were randomized to the control group with ≥2° varus alignment, (2) were randomized to either strength training group with ≥2° varus alignment and increased their hip abductor strength by at least 20% over the 18‐month training period, and (3) had all available strength and biomechanical gait data at baseline and 18‐month follow‐up (N = 88). The 20% strength improvement criterion was to ensure a clear and clinically relevant distinction in strength improvement between the intervention and control groups.

Interventions

Strength training

Group strength training sessions were conducted three times weekly over 18 months. Sessions consisted of a 5‐minute warm‐up, 40‐minute training, and 15‐minute cool‐down supervised by staff interventionists. The program included six lower body exercises, with each leg exercised separately to prevent unequal load distribution between the limbs, and four upper body and core exercises.

The high‐intensity group performed three sets of each exercise beginning at 75% of one repetition maximum (1RM) with eight repetitions/set for two weeks, progressing to 80% of the 1RM with eight repetitions/set for weeks 3 to 4, 85% of the 1RM with six repetitions/set for weeks 5 to 6, and 90% of the 1RM with four repetitions/set for weeks 7 to 8. Week 9 was a taper week with alternate exercises and establishing new 1RMs for each exercise. This nine‐week block was repeated using the new 1RM values. The low‐intensity group used the same nine‐week block pattern but performed three sets of 15 repetitions at 30% to 40% 1RM of the exercises described above. The target workload/total volume performed during these nine‐week cycles was the same regardless of whether the participant was assigned to the high‐intensity or low‐intensity group. 26

Attention control group

Participants attended 60‐minute group workshops biweekly for the first six months and monthly thereafter (total of 24 sessions over 18 months). Details of the control intervention are included in the study design publication. 26

Measurements and procedures

Bilateral posteroanterior (semiflexed view) weight‐bearing knee x‐rays were acquired to determine the diagnosis of OA. The Kellgren–Lawrence grading scale was used to determine OA diagnosis/severity (0 to 4 scale, with 0 = no OA and 4 = severe OA). 30 A full‐length lower extremity coronal view x‐ray was used to determine frontal plane knee alignment. 27 The measurement for knee alignment was determined by lines drawn from the femoral head to the knee and from the center of the ankle surface to the knee. Alignment was characterized as neutral (angle = 0°), varus (angle >0° in the direction of a bow‐legged appearance), or valgus (angle <0° in the direction of a knock‐knee appearance).

A six‐camera motion capture system was configured to collect three‐dimensional kinematic data at 60 Hz. Marker trajectories were captured, tracked, and processed in Cortex 3.0, in which the raw data were smoothed using a fourth‐order low‐pass Butterworth filter with a 6‐Hz cutoff to reduce high‐frequency noise while preserving physiologically meaningful motion. Ground reaction forces were recorded using two six‐channel force platforms integrated with the motion capture system, allowing synchronized kinetic data acquisition at 1,000 Hz. These analog force signals were low‐pass filtered with a fourth‐order Butterworth filter (cutoff 20 Hz).

The filtered motion capture and force‐plate data were then imported into Visual3D (C‐Motion) for biomechanical analysis. A rigid‐body, segmental model was constructed based on a modified Cleveland Clinic marker set, with each segment defined by its own local coordinate system and inertial parameters calculated using Dempster‐based regression equations and geometric assumptions. Pelvic and hip joint centers were estimated using the Bell and Brand regression approach, and marker placement on medial and lateral anatomic landmarks facilitated the definition of anatomic reference frames. 31 , 32 Joint angles were computed using a Cardan rotation sequence (X–Y–Z), corresponding to flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and internal/external rotation. Using the synchronized three‐dimensional marker trajectories and ground reaction forces, net internal joint forces and moments were calculated using a routine Newton–Euler inverse‐dynamics approach in Virtual3D. Participants were instructed to walk at their normal pace, as if walking purposefully around town. Six practice trials were conducted at this self‐selected walking speed, and the average time was calculated. Speed was recorded and monitored using a wireless infrared timing gate (Dual Beam System TF100, TracTronix). 26

Kinetic outcomes included comparison of hip and knee joint loading, including peak internal hip abduction moment, peak internal knee abduction moment, and peak internal knee extension moment (note: the internal moments are presented in this analysis). Kinematic outcomes of interest included maximum frontal plane contralateral (side with the less affected knee) pelvic drop during the first 20% of the stance phase and sagittal plane knee angle.

Tibiofemoral knee compressive force was calculated using a reduction approach with a statically determinant musculoskeletal model. 33 The input parameters included the linear position of the lower extremity and the internal joint moments and reactions forces. The original sagittal plane model was improved by including a frontal plane component, which was fully described previously. 34 The model's muscle and knee joint force predictions compared favorably in terms of maximum forces and the shape of the force by time curves with those from an electromyography‐based model and an optimization‐based model. 34 Further, the predicted first and second peak tibiofemoral forces during walking in a person with a prosthetic knee were within 7% and 3% of those measured within a knee prosthesis. 26 Finally, Dumas et al 35 independently applied our model to the gait data from five people with knee prostheses and compared the muscle and knee joint force predictions from our model with those measured in the prostheses during walking. They concluded that our model's predictions matched the measured data in both curve shape and force magnitude, that errors from our predictions matched those from optimization‐based models, and that our model is appropriate for osteoarthritic populations.

Several measures were used to assess baseline clinical characteristics of the two groups. The Likert version of the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) 36 was used to assess knee pain. It assesses knee pain over the last 48 hours. 37 The total score ranges from 0 to 20 (higher scores indicate greater pain). Pain categories on a 0 to 20 scale are 2 to 8 for mild, >8 to 14 for moderate, and >14 to 20 for severe (transformed from the 0–10 scale reported by Kapstad et al 38 ). WOMAC physical function assessed the degree of difficulty with activities of daily living in the last 48 hours with the total score range 0 to 68; higher scores indicate poorer function, with a score ≥21 indicating physical work limitations. 39 Six‐minute walk distance, a clinical measure of mobility, assessed the distance a participant could walk along a standardized walkway in six minutes. 40

Bilateral isokinetic strength of the knee extensors and flexors was assessed using a HUMAC NORM dynamometer (Computer Sports Medicine, Inc) with participants seated and the knee positioned at 90° flexion. Participants performed maximal concentric contractions from 90° to 30° and back at an angular velocity of 30°/s. Bilateral hip abductor strength was evaluated in a side‐lying position with a hip abduction attachment secured to the femur. Participants performed maximal abductions from 0° to 30° at the same angular velocity (30°/s). For both assessments, five trials were performed with 60 seconds of rest between each trial. The top three trials were used for analysis. Additional trials were collected if the variability among the top three trials exceeded 10%.

Statistical analyses

Primary analyses were conducted using complete‐case data. Of the 377 START participants, 107 were eligible based on the study's inclusion criteria—of these, 88 (49 in the control group, 39 in the strength training group) had all available baseline and 18‐month strength and biomechanical data. Participants randomized to the high‐ and low‐intensity strength training protocols that met the varus alignment, and 18‐month hip abductor strength improvement inclusion criteria were combined into one strength training group for this secondary analysis.

Descriptive measures were used to summarize the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. These characteristics included age, BMI, weight, height, sex, ethnicity, race, baseline Kellgren–Lawrence score, patellofemoral OA, WOMAC pain, WOMAC function, usual gait speed, and the six‐minute walk distance. Linear regressions with adjustments for baseline outcome, age, sex, gait speed, and 18‐month body weight were used to compare quadriceps, hamstrings, and hip abductor strength; internal knee abduction, hip abduction, and knee extension moments; and knee compressive force between the control and strength training groups at 18‐month follow‐up. The P value was set at ≤0.05 for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Strength

Unadjusted hip abductor strength increased by 2.9 ± 2.0 Nm (4% ± 3%) and by 24.6 ± 2.2 Nm (42% ± 8%) in the control and strength training groups, respectively. Knee extensor strength increased by 11.8 ± 2.8 Nm (16% ± 5%) in the control group and 30.8 ± 4.7 Nm (45% ± 8%) in the strength training group; knee flexor strength increased by 4.2 ± 1.9 Nm (11% ± 4%) in the control group and 22.9 ± 3.4 Nm (68% ± 7%) in the strength training group.

The adjusted mean value for hip abductor strength at 18 months was 70.3 ± 1.7 Nm for the control group and 85.8 ± 1.8 Nm for the strength training group, a difference of 22% that was statistically significant (mean difference 15.6 Nm, 95% confidence interval [CI] 10.6–20.5, P ≤ 0.0001). There was a 13% difference in knee extensor strength between groups at 18 months (mean difference 11.3 Nm, 95% CI 3.2–19.4, P = 0.007) in favor of the strength training group. The adjusted difference in knee flexor strength between groups at 18 months differed by 26% and favored the strength training group (mean difference 11.7 Nm, 95% CI 6.5–17.0 Nm, P ≤ 0.0001; Table 2).

Gait biomechanics

The sample means for the unadjusted internal hip abduction moments increased in both the control (1.5 ± 3.6 Nm, 2% ± 2%) and strength training (11.3 ± 3.8 Nm, 15% ± 6%) groups with no significant difference between the groups in adjusted 18‐month means (mean difference 7.0 Nm, 95% CI −2.2 to 16.2, P = 0.13). The mean increase in the unadjusted peak internal knee abduction moment across the 18‐month intervention period was 0.7 ± 1.6 Nm (1.7% ± 2%) for the control group and 2.2 ± 1.9 Nm (5% ± 4%) for the strength training group. Adjusted peak internal knee abduction moment values at follow‐up were not significantly different between the groups (mean difference 0.1 Nm, 95% CI −4.2 to 4.3, P = 0.98; Table 3; Figure 1).

DISCUSSION

Among older adults with knee OA and varus alignment, 18 months of strength training that leads to a significant improvement in hip and knee strength does not have a significant effect on the magnitude of the peak hip and knee abduction moments, the latter being a surrogate measure of medial compartment joint loading. The hip musculature has become a popular addition to strengthening interventions for patients with OA to counterbalance external loads, reduce contralateral pelvic drop, and in turn cause a subsequent decrease in the internal knee abduction moment. 21 , 41 Bennell et al 23 showed that a short‐term, three‐month home‐based hip abductor strength training intervention (13% increase in strength) reduced pain and improved function but had no effect on the internal abduction moment compared to control. Sled et al 24 found similar results from a two‐month home‐based hip abductor strength training intervention. The current study was longer in duration (18 months) than these studies and included only participants in the strength training group that improved hip abductor strength by at least 20% from baseline; mean improvement was 42% compared to a 4% increase in the control group.

The increased strength significantly reduced contralateral pelvic drop compared to the control group; however, the combination of reduced pelvic drop and increased hip strength did not translate to a reduction in the peak internal knee abduction moment. In contrast, Thorp et al conducted a four‐week lower extremity strength training intervention exercising the knee extensors, knee flexors, and hip abductors in six participants with radiographic knee OA and found that the knee abduction moment decreased by 9%. The small sample size, short intervention duration, and lack of a comparator group limited the generalizability and the strength of evidence of the results.

An internal knee extension moment refers to the torque produced by the extensor muscles relative to that produced by the flexor muscles when the extensors contract eccentrically to control knee flexion after heel strike, essentially acting as a measure of the force being applied to the joint during this time. 42 The strength training group increased knee extensor strength by 45% compared to 16% for the control group. This resulted in a significant decrease of 27% in the adjusted internal knee extension moment during the first 20% of the gait cycle. The knee flexion angle did not change from baseline to follow‐up, suggesting that the reduced knee extension moment may be reflective of a reduction in medial compartment contact force.

The benefits of load reduction on clinical outcomes are evident in long‐term dietary weight‐loss clinical trials 43 , 44 and in studies of OA disease progression. 15 In contrast, higher internal knee extension moments are characteristic of healthy control groups compared to people with knee OA. 45 These higher joint moments are likely affected by the faster walking speeds of people with healthy knees. Nevertheless, it is important to note that people with healthy knees tend to have greater joint loads compared to people with knee OA. 46

Clinical trials of diet and exercise have shown reductions in bone‐on‐bone knee joint compressive forces with weight loss. 47 In our weight‐stable (mean change = 1.4%) strength training group, however, there was no reduction in compressive forces. These data cast doubt as to whether strengthening the knee extensor muscles has any shock‐absorbing benefit in osteoarthritic gait.

This study has several limitations. Our study samples were quite specific and therefore our results may be limited to a similar population. Namely, these results may be generalizable to individuals who are White and male with knee OA, obesity, and middle to older age, have varus malalignment, and who have made substantial increases in their lower extremity strength. We used musculoskeletal modeling to estimate knee joint compressive forces. All such models have simplifying assumptions about the tissues they describe, including bones, ligaments, and muscles, and the joint properties. 48 Despite these limiting assumptions, our model predictions have been shown to be reasonable. Healthy older adults and older adults with knee OA typically use a greater percentage of their maximum strength during gait than healthy young adults; changes in submaximal functional muscle capacity were not assessed. 49 We hypothesized that greater muscle strength would lead to lower joint loads. However, lower joint loads may represent redistributed loads, either to other parts of the same joint or to other joints. Our analysis did not distinguish between load attenuation and load redistribution. Finally, this study was a secondary analysis from a randomized clinical trial and included a subset of participants, hence randomization was no longer preserved. Despite these limitations, we propose the data and results are reasonably robust and accurately reflect responses in a real‐world setting.

Among older adults with knee OA and varus alignment, long‐term lower extremity strength training results in significant increases in strength but does not significantly alter most measures of knee joint loading during walking relative to an attention control group. The results cast doubt on whether clinically meaningful improvement in lower extremity muscle strength translates to clinically important attenuation in knee joint loading.
