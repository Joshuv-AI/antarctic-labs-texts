Unleashing the ultrasonic frequency as a preparative parameter in tailoring the surface morphology and hence charge storage in Co3O4:MnO2@CoMnO3 composite flexible electrodes for supercapacitors
Authors: A. V. Thakur, S. G. Malpure, Manjunath Nookala Krishnamurthy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we investigated the influence of ultrasonic frequency during ultrasound-assisted chemical bath deposition (UCBD) on the surface morphology and electrochemical performance of Co3O4:MnO2@CoMnO3 composite flexible electrodes for supercapacitor applications. By systematically varying the ultrasonic frequency (1.0–2.5 MHz), a significant modulation in surface architecture from dispersed nanoflakes to densely packed marigold-like structures was achieved. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and contact angle analysis confirmed improved surface ordering and wettability with increasing frequency. Electrochemical analyses demonstrated that electrodes fabricated at 2.5 MHz (F4) exhibited the highest specific capacitance (SC) of 722.27 Fg−1 at 2 mVs−1, attributable to enhanced electroactive surface area and reduced ion diffusion resistance. The symmetric supercapacitor device (SSD) assembled using these electrodes achieved SC of 840.35 Fg−1, alongside excellent cycling stability, retaining 90.49% of its initial capacitance after 3000 cycles. These results highlight the efficacy of ultrasonic modulation in tailoring nanostructured electrode surfaces for next-generation energy storage devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-025-04349-w.

Introduction

In light of escalating global energy demands and the urgent imperative for decarbonization, there is an increasing drive toward the development of advanced energy storage technologies that exhibit superior performance, scalability, and environmental compatibility [1–3]. Among the spectrum of available storage modalities, electrochemical supercapacitors have garnered considerable attention owing to their ability to deliver high power density, rapid charge/discharge kinetics, and prolonged cyclability, thereby positioning themselves as indispensable components in next-generation power systems [4–6]. Supercapacitors are broadly classified into electrical double-layer capacitors (EDLCs) and pseudocapacitors based on their charge storage mechanisms [7, 8]. While EDLCs rely on electrostatic accumulation of ions at the electrode–electrolyte interface, pseudocapacitors utilize fast and reversible redox reactions at or near the electrode surface, thus offering substantially higher specific capacitance (SC) [9–11]. Among various pseudocapacitive materials, transition metal oxides (TMOs), particularly cobalt oxide (Co3O4) and manganese dioxide (MnO2), stand out due to their rich redox chemistry, chemical stability, and cost-effectiveness [12–15]. The combination of Co3O4 and MnO2 in hybrid architecture provides a promising platform for high-performance electrodes, leveraging the complementary pseudocapacitive behavior of the Co2+/Co3+ and Mn3+/Mn4+ redox couples [16–18]. However, practical deployment is often constrained by intrinsic limitations such as poor electronic conductivity, sluggish ion transport, and morphological instability under electrochemical stress [19, 20]. Addressing these challenges necessitates precise control over the nano-structural attributes of the electrode materials to maximize electroactive surface area, optimize porosity, and ensure mechanical robustness during electrochemical cycling [21–24].

Surface morphological tailoring has emerged as a critical strategy in enhancing electrochemical kinetics and interfacial charge transport [25–27]. In particular, ultrasound-assisted chemical bath deposition (UCBD) offers a facile, low-temperature, and substrate-friendly route for growing nanostructured thin films with controllable morphologies [28, 29]. The application of ultrasonic vibrations during synthesis plays a crucial role in governing the nucleation and growth dynamics of loosely bound species on the substrate. The induced acoustic cavitation leads to enhanced ordering, improved structural stability, and stronger film adherence. The process leverages acoustic cavitation, which generates transient high-energy micro-environments facilitating enhanced nucleation rates, reduced particle agglomeration, and improved film adhesion [30–32]. Notably, the ultrasonic frequency is a critical yet underexplored parameter in modulating cavitation intensity and, by extension, the morphological evolution of the deposited films [33–36].

Despite substantial progress, a systematic study delineating the influence of ultrasonic frequency on the morphological and electrochemical characteristics of the Co3O4:MnO2 hybrid electrodes has not been done yet. In this context, the present study investigates the effect of ultrasonic frequency variation (1.0–2.5 MHz) on the synthesis of Co3O4:MnO2@CoMnO3 composite flexible electrodes via UCBD. The work studied the correlation between acoustic frequency, surface nanostructure, and capacitive performance, thereby underscoring the potential of frequency-tuned UCBD as a design strategy for high-performance energy storage systems aiming high-rate capability and long-term operational reliability.

Materials and methods

Materials

Analytical grade cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O), potassium permanganate (KMnO4), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) and used without further purification. Ultrapure Milli-Q water was employed as the solvent throughout the experimental procedures. Flexible substrates were prepared using the stainless steel (SS 304) strips of dimensions 1 cm × 5 cm × 60 μm. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) molecular weight corresponding to 30,000 grade, hand-cleaning tissue paper, and red light-emitting diodes (LEDs) with electrical specifications of 1.7 V forward voltage and 20 mA forward current were utilized to fabricate demonstration displays.

The ultrasound chemical bath deposition (UCBD) system consisted of a variable-frequency probe sonicator integrated with a custom-designed electrode holder assembly. The setup also incorporated a magnetic stirrer, a dedicated reactant reservoir, and a magnetic stirring bead to ensure the homogeneous nature of the reacting solutions and efficient acoustic energy distribution during the deposition process.

Methods

Preparation of electrodes

Flexible SS 304 strips were subjected to mechanical polishing using 1200-grit emery paper to eliminate surface oxides and residual contaminants. Following mechanical treatment, the substrates were immersed in a 2 mM aqueous hydrochloric acid (HCl) solution for 30 min to activate the surface chemically, which was followed by ultrasonic cleaning in double-distilled water for an additional 30 min to ensure complete removal of any remaining particulates or residues. The overall surface pre-treatment protocol was designed to increase the number of reactive sites on the substrate, thereby promoting efficient nucleation and uniform growth of ions derived from precursor solutions during the subsequent deposition process.

The pre-treated substrates were mounted in the ultrasonic electrode holder and immersed in the reaction mixture for 30 min under constant stirring at 600 rpm. The reaction bath consisted of 40 mL of 1 M cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O) solution and 10 mL of 1 M potassium permanganate (KMnO4) solution, serving as cationic precursors, along with 50 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution, acting as the anionic precursor. This composition facilitated the deposition of desired electroactive species to form a uniform coating onto the substrate surface via ultrasound-assisted chemical bath deposition (UCBD).

The coated substrates were dried in ambient air at 300.15 K to obtain pre-electrodes after deposition. These were subsequently annealed at 573.15 K to achieve crystallization and phase formation, resulting in the final composite flexible electrodes (CFEs). The CFEs prepared at different frequencies of the probe sonicator, viz., 1 MHz, 1.5 MHz, 2 MHz, and 2.5 MHz, were successively labelled as F1, F2, F3, and F4.

Preparation of gel electrolyte

0.5 g of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was gradually added to 10 mL of Milli-Q water under continuous magnetic stirring at 1000 rpm and a temperature of 320.15 K until a clear, homogeneous solution was formed. Subsequently, 10 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was added dropwise to the PVA solution under constant stirring, creating a uniform, transparent gel. This gel was the solid-state electrolyte in the assembled symmetric supercapacitor devices (SSDs).

Fabrication of symmetric supercapacitor

A piece of tissue paper was immersed in the prepared PVA–NaOH gel to allow complete soaking. The gel-impregnated paper was then positioned between the two electrodes to function as an electrolyte reservoir and separator (Fig. 1a). The entire assembly was encapsulated using adhesive (sticky) tape, ensuring proper sealing to prevent electrolyte leakage and to avoid direct electrical contact between the electrodes. Characterizations

The mass of the active materials deposited on the substrates was determined using the mass difference method with a high-precision analytical microbalance (Tapson's 100 TS). Crystallographic analysis of the synthesized materials was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku D/max 2550 Vb + /PC, 18 kW) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15405 nm). The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 15° to 90° with a step size of 0.5°. Surface wettability has been evaluated using a contact angle meter (Halmark). The static contact angles have been measured to assess the surface for hydrophilic behavior.

The deposited films' surface morphology analyses and elemental composition study were performed via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using a JEOL JSM-7600F system. Film adhesion was qualitatively assessed using the standard Scotch tape peel test.

Electrochemical characterization was conducted using a Metrohm DropSens μStat-302N-i Multi-8 electrochemical workstation (USA). Measurements were performed in a standard three-electrode configuration, wherein the fabricated CFEs served as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference. All experiments used the aqueous NaOH solution with a molar concentration of 0.2 M as an aqueous electrolyte. A two-electrode configuration was employed to assess device-level electrochemical performance to evaluate symmetric supercapacitor devices (SSDs).

Synthesis of FE using Co(NO3)2·6 H2O as precursor

When cobalt nitrate hexahydrate and KMnO4 were precursors for Co2+ and Mn6+ cations, respectively, the cell reactions formed cobalt hydroxide and manganese hydroxide. KNO3 and NaNO3 were formed during the reaction, which again undergoes ionization, during which O2 gas molecules were evolved. The electrodes were dried in air at 300.15 K, leading to the formation of Co2O3 and MnO2. Later on, annealing at 673.15 K in excess oxygen, the Co2O3 is further oxidized to Co3O4. During this stage, NO2 and O2 gas molecules were evolved. Thus, in each case, the desired composite Co3O4:MnO2@CoMnO3 is formed, but depending on the reaction rate, the nucleation and growth occur differently, which may lead to variation in surface morphology.

Following the optimization of synthesis parameters, the CFE F4 exhibiting the highest SC was subjected to detailed electrochemical evaluation using cyclic voltammetry (CV). The CV study was conducted in 0.2 M aqueous NaOH using scan rates ranging from 2 to 100 mVs−1 (Fig. 3b). The SC values, calculated using Eq. 1, are provided in the inset of Fig. 3b. A broad reduction peak is seen at − 0.27 V, which can be attributed to the reduction of cations (Co3+). The oxidation peak at + 0.36 V is due to Co2+ to Co3+ transition. Despite the increasing scan rate, only a minimal shift was seen in redox peak potentials, indicating good electrochemical reversibility of the system. This shift can be attributed to the partially oxidized species during the prior sweep of potentials. While the current integral increased with the scan rate, the SC gradually decreased. This inverse trend is because, at higher scan rates, the electrolyte ions cannot efficiently access the inner active sites of the electrode, leading to reduced charge accumulation. At lower scan rates, ions have sufficient time to diffuse into the porous structure, enabling more complete redox reactions and higher capacitance. The redox transitions of Co2⁺/Co3+ and Mn2+ species are primarily responsible for the observed pseudocapacitive behavior. The diffusion of electrolyte ions contributes less significantly to overall charge storage.

The linear relationships between anodic and cathodic peak currents and the scan rate to further support this dual mechanism. The capacitive and diffusive contributions in the charge storage by F4 have been measured at various scan rates from 2 to 100 mVs−1 (Fig. 3f). At a low scan rate of 2 mVs−1, the capacitive contribution was estimated at 65%, while the diffusion-controlled processes accounted for 35%. As the scan rate increased, the capacitive contribution increased, reaching a maximum of 99% at 20 mVs−1, suggesting that redox processes are in synchrony with the changing potential at moderate scan rates. Beyond 20 mVs−1, the rapid potential variations could not be followed entirely by redox reactions. As a result, ionic accumulation at the surface increased, driving deeper ion penetration into the electrode matrix. Consequently, the diffusion-controlled contribution begins to rise again. At 100 mVs−1, the CV curve (Fig. 3e) shows that while capacitive mechanisms still dominate, the diffusion-controlled processes have a measurable impact. Thus, the capacitive processes exhibited a maximum contribution of 77.14%, whereas the contribution from diffusion-controlled mechanisms was 22.86%. This confirms that surface-controlled charge storage dominates the overall electrochemical behavior, though a significant portion also arises from ion diffusion into the electrode matrix.

Electrochemical analyses of symmetric supercapacitor

The symmetric supercapacitor device (SSD) was tested for electrochemical performance using CV, GCD, and EIS. The CV curves were recorded at various scan rates ranging from 2 to 100 mVs−1 in the potential window of 0.0–1.2 V vs Ag/AgCl electrode (Fig. 4a). At the lowest scan rate (2 mVs−1), the electrode exhibited a high SC of 840.35 Fg−1, indicating effective utilization of the electroactive surface and pronounced redox activity. As the scan rate increases, the SC decreases gradually to 128.75 Fg−1 at 100 mVs−1 due to the limited diffusion of electrolyte ions into the porous matrix at higher scan rates. The asymmetric nature and the broad redox peaks of the CV curves confirm the presence of pseudocapacitive behavior, highlighting a hybrid charge storage mechanism involving faradaic and capacitive processes.Fig. 4a CV curves at different scan rates; b Cycling stability study; c GCD curves; d Nyquist plots for the symmetric supercapacitor device prepared with optimized electrodes

To evaluate the long-term stability of the optimized CFE, cyclic voltammetry was performed at a scan rate of 100 mVs−1 for 3000 consecutive cycles (Fig. 4b). The SC showed an initial decrease to 97% of its original value by the second cycle and gradually declined to 92% after 10 cycles. The degradation rate slowed over the subsequent few cycles, with SC reaching 91.8% after 20 cycles. The rate of fall becomes very minute as SSD exhibits 90.61% capacitance retention after 200 cycles. Notably, beyond this point, the electrode exhibited excellent electrochemical stability with 90.49% capacitance retention after 200 cycles even after 3000 cycles. This impressive cycling durability highlights the structural integrity and robustness of the electrode material under repeated redox operations. The SC shows slight degradation during the initial cycles but stabilizes afterward, maintaining over 100 Fg−1 up to 3000 cycles, which demonstrates good electrochemical stability and reversibility of the redox processes, confirming the electrode's suitability for repeated charge–discharge operations in supercapacitor applications.

GCD analysis was done at various constant current densities ranging from 2 to 10 mA (Fig. 4c). The GCD curves show an atypical pattern from the ideal capacitor. The charging portion shows three segments. The first rapid increase in potential is due to the rapid diffusion of the electrolytic ions in the matrix of the FE. The second segment shows exponential charging with time due to the slight opposition of the initially adsorbed and diffused ions opposing the upcoming ions from the electrolyte (negative capacitance effect). The last portion shows a small, sudden hike in the potential at the peak that might be due to the fast-forward redox reactions at the surface of the FE. The discharging portion also shows three segments. The initial segment is a small ohmic drop, possibly due to the solution and interfacial resistance, which is followed by the exponential discharging portion, which may be due to the removal of the adsorbed ions from the FE matrix. The last segment showing prolonged discharging is attributed to the redox transitions in Co3O4 and MnO2, and some extent CoMnO3.

We observed that the SSD shows high specific energy; hence, it is more suitable for high-energy storage applications such as power stations, pacemakers, hybrid vehicle batteries, etc. The SSD material shows appreciably high specific power and hence can be used in high power applications, viz. defibrillators, power motor starters, etc.

The Nyquist plot of the SSD is in Fig. 4d. A small intercept on the real axis corresponds to the solution resistance (Rs) of 1.71 Ω. The diameter of the semicircle in the high-frequency region represents the charge transfer resistance (Rct), which is calculated to be 0.6 Ω, indicating efficient electron transport at the electrode–electrolyte interface. The low-frequency region forms a straight line with a slope of ~ 49°, typical of Warburg impedance (Rw = 1.4 Ω), confirming the diffusion-limited ion transport behaviour. The inset shows the equivalent circuit fitting, validating the impedance components. These results collectively confirm mixed capacitive behaviour consistent with the CV and GCD observations.

Conclusion

Our study establishes ultrasonic frequency as a key preparative parameter in tuning the surface morphology and electrochemical properties of Co3O4:MnO2@CoMnO3 composite flexible electrodes. The electrode synthesized at the highest frequency of 2.5 MHz (F4) demonstrated a well-organized marigold-like nanostructure, translating to a maximum specific capacitance of 722.27 F·g−1 at 2 mVs−1. A symmetric supercapacitor device (SSD) fabricated using these electrodes delivered a specific capacitance of 840.35 Fg−1 at 2 mVs−1 and retained 90.49% of its capacitance after 3000 cycles, confirming excellent stability and charge retention. These findings underscore the potential of frequency-tuned UCBD for engineering high-performance supercapacitor electrodes with practical viability.
