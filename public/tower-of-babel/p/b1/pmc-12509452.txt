Adding Anodal Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation to Mindfulness Meditation Abolishes Mindfulness-Induced Improvements in Emotion Regulation in Fibromyalgia
Authors: Perianen Ramasawmy, Andrea Antal, Olga L. Gamboa Arana, Frank Petzke, Anne Kästner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) appear to improve emotion regulation (ER) in disorders involving emotional dysregulation like fibromyalgia (FM). Despite the therapeutic potential of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) in FM, its concurrent application with mindfulness meditation (MM) has not yet been explored. Potential synergistic effects may arise from aligning neural mechanisms underlying both interventions. This study evaluated the impact of a 4-week MBI and a combined tDCS and MM intervention on ER, implementing a responder analysis approach for FM patients.Methods:Following the MBI, 40 participants with FM were randomly allocated to receive 10 days of 20 minutes MM combined with either anodal tDCS of the primary motor cortex (M1) or sham tDCS. ER was gauged using a composite score integrating 4 self-report questionnaires. We performed assessment at 6 time points (baseline, post-MBI, immediately postcombined intervention, 4 wk, 8 wk, and 12-wk follow-ups). Clinical responders were defined as patients exhibiting a clinically relevant reduction in FM symptom load.Results:Responders to the MBI showed better ER than nonresponders, lasting for 6 weeks. Interestingly, responders to the combined intervention in the sham group reported larger improvements in ER than nonresponders, which was not observed in the anodal tDCS group. No significant interaction between responder status and time was found for responders to either intervention.Discussion:Our findings evidence that mindfulness practice is able to improve ER in FM. However, the add-on combination of MM with M1-tDCS abolishes this mindfulness-induced effect. Therefore, based on our data, combining MM and M1-tDCS must be reconsidered as a therapeutic intervention targeting ER in FM.

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic pain disorder characterized by widespread and persistent musculoskeletal pain driven by nociplastic changes.1–3 It is usually associated with a conglomerate of physical, cognitive, and psychological symptoms. Depression and anxiety are common psychiatric comorbidities in FM, which have been associated with difficulties in emotion regulation (ER).4–6 Among the various ER processes that have been shown to mediate FM disability, pain intensity, and high rates of psychological distress, the imbalance of negative and positive affective states appears to play an important role in this disorder.7–11 Other common ER deficits in FM include alexithymia (difficulties in identifying, describing, and communicating feelings) and emotional avoidance.12–15 Furthermore, Balducci et al16 recently found abnormal functional connectivity between brain regions involved in emotion processing in FM patients compared with healthy controls, which was indexed through neuroimaging during an emotion-related task. Importantly, recent integrative accounts emphasize the mediating role of difficulties in ER (eg, self-soothing abilities) in FM pathophysiology.17

To date, FM treatment remains a clinical challenge. Evidence-based recommendations emphasize the use of nonpharmacological interventions, including psychotherapy and mind-body exercises, as first-line treatments of the disorder over medication.18–22 Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have been associated with stress reduction and a decrease in psychological symptom load, and improved mental well-being in FM patients.23–27 The experience of emotions from a mindful mental state, involving nonattached and nonjudgmental awareness and acceptance, termed as mindful ER,28–31 constitutes one of the mechanisms underlying mindfulness-based interventions in ER and employs both top-down and bottom-up ER processes.32 MBIs have been shown to increase functional connections among different brain regions, including anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and anterior insular cortex- brain areas involved in ER and affective pain processing.33 Moreover, electroencephalographic (EEG) and functional magnetic resonance imaging studies addressing neural mechanisms related to MBIs converge on the notion that mindfulness exercise induces changes in the brain “state,” including activation of the ACC.

Interestingly, although the role of the primary motor cortex (M1) in pain modulation has not been fully clarified, M1 is one of the cortical areas most commonly targeted by invasive and noninvasive neuromodulation technologies.34,35 Repeated application of anodal transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) of the left primary motor cortex (M1) has also demonstrated positive effects in pain relief and improvements in quality of life and psychological and cognitive symptoms in FM.36–44 As summarized by Brighina et al,45 DLPFC-targeted tDCS was used in fewer randomized clinical trials and resulted to be less effective with respect to pain relief, and other FM symptoms. Neuroimaging studies provide evidence for analgesic effects of M1-neuromodulation to derive from an activation of multiple areas beyond the cortical zone that have been implicated in pain processing: for example, orbitofrontal cortex or dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and their remote inner brain structures, such as the insula, ACC, middle, posterior cingulate cortex, the putamen, the thalamus, and proportions of the brain stem, such as the periaqueductal gray.46–49 Some of these regions, especially ACC, have been implicated in some aspects of ER (eg, attentional deployment, reappraisal, response modulation).50,51 Mechanistically, the increase in motor cortex excitability induced by repeated neurostimulation is assumed to lead to the activation of inhibitory interneurons in the thalamus. The decrease in thalamus hyperactivity observed in FM patients has been associated with increased activation of the periaqueductal gray. The periaqueductal gray has been implicated in the descending inhibition of pain. Furthermore, the thalamus is involved in the modulation of pain-associated brain regions, such as insula and cingulate cortex.

These functional imaging findings are corroborated by a recent animal study employing optogenetic and chemogenetic approaches to unravel the neurobiological underpinnings of M1 in pain relief.52 By reversibly manipulating different layers of M1 microcircuitry in mouse models of neuropathic pain, Gan et al52 discovered that M1 makes use of distinct, layer-specific pathways to attenuate sensory versus aversive-emotional dimensions of neuropathic pain. More precisely, the authors discovered a connection from M1 to the nucleus accumbens reward circuitry specifically implicated in the suppression of negative emotional valence. Consequently, accumulating evidence highlights that some of the neural effects of M1-tDCS and MM may converge in emotion-processing brain regions. On the basis of these findings, we set out to explore potential synergistic effects of mindfulness meditation (MM) and M1-tDCS with respect to ER in FM.

Recently, combining tDCS and MM has been studied to yield enhanced synergistic therapeutic effects.53 In our pilot study testing the preliminary efficacy of pairing anodal tDCS over the left M1 with MM in FM patients, participants in the active group reported a clinically relevant improvement in quality of life, without any changes in pain and associated symptoms compared with control groups.54 Addressing the methodological loopholes of the pilot study, we conducted a triple-blind, sham-controlled, and adequately powered randomized clinical trial to evaluate the effects of 10 daily 20-minute sessions of anodal M1-tDCS combined with MM in patients trained in mindfulness through a brief MBI tailored for FM. Both the active and sham groups showed reduced levels of depressiveness, anxiety, stress, and negative affect over time, alongside pain relief and improvements in quality of life and sleep quality. No superior benefit of combining anodal M1-tDCS and MM was found to placebo in FM patients.55

In the present manuscript, a responder analysis of the main clinical trial was conducted to compare clinical responders and nonresponders to the brief MBI or the combined intervention with respect to improvements in ER. The aforementioned research suggests that both M1-targeted tDCS and MM impact brain regions involved in the processing of emotions (eg, ACC, insula). Likewise, ER emerged as a transdiagnostic mechanism mediating beneficial effects of MBIs on clinically relevant symptom domains. Therefore, we hypothesized that a symptom reduction following the combined mindfulness and M1-tDCS intervention would be associated with improvements in ER. More specifically, we hypothesized that clinical responders to the combined intervention would exhibit better ER compared with nonresponders, with a larger effect size in the active group, compared with the sham group. Exploratory analyses were conducted to test the long-term effects of the combined intervention and responders to both the brief mindfulness and the combined intervention.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Göttingen (ID: 9/1/22) according to the Declaration of Helsinki and registered at the German Clinical Trials Registry (DRKS00029024). Data was collected from May 2022 to October 2023. All participants signed an informed consent form.

Study Design

This responder analysis is a secondary analysis of a triple-blind, sham-controlled, randomized clinical trial to evaluate the effects of 10 daily 20-minute sessions of anodal tDCS combined with MM over 2 weeks (Monday to Friday) in individuals with FM trained in mindfulness. “Triple-blind” refers to the fact that participants, data collectors, and data analysts did not have access to details of group assignment. The study had 5 phases—a 2-week baseline period, a 4-week MM, a postmindfulness intervention measurement week, a 2-week combined tDCS and MBI, and a 3-month follow-up period, as illustrated in Figure 1. Following successful completion of the BMIF, the participants were randomly allocated to receive either anodal tDCS paired with MM (MM+active tDCS) or sham tDCS with MM (MM+sham tDCS) following an age-matched and sex-matched 1:1 block randomization protocol. The combined intervention was delivered as a home-based treatment or at the clinic. A battery of questionnaires in German was administered at different measurement time points: at baseline (BA), immediately after completing the mindfulness training (PM), within 1 week postcombined intervention (PC0), 4 weeks postcombined intervention (PC4), at 8-week follow-up (PC8), and at 12-week follow-up (PC12). The battery of questionnaires assessed FM impact, mindfulness levels, psychological impairment, and ER in the patients. At BA, patients also reported the number of years they have been experiencing FM-associated pain.

Participants

Forty-four mindfulness-naive FM patients, aged from 30 to 75 years, with stable chronic pain for 6 months before the study met the inclusion criteria for participation in the study. Included participants met the American College of Rheumatology Diagnostic criteria for FM—widespread pain index ≥7, symptom severity ≥52, and no other symptom explaining illness. Participants were allowed to continue their established medication and nonpharmacological therapies, which had to be stable for at least 4 weeks before inclusion and maintained throughout the study. Exclusion criteria included major medical conditions, more than 3 FM-related medications, pregnancy or breastfeeding, metallic implants in upper body, repeated or unknown history of loss of consciousness, history of brain surgery, running or contested disability application, and drug or alcohol abuse. Not permitted medication included strong opioids, carbamazepine, phenytoin, benzodiazepines, calcium channel antagonists, N-Methyl-d-Aspartate receptor antagonists, anticholinergics, ad analgesic infiltration of the spinal cord. Psychotherapy, with a maximum of one session per week, and/or physiotherapy not more than thrice per week were permitted. Detailed selection criteria, recruitment plan and enrollment procedures are described elsewhere.55 This current responder analysis included all participants who received the combined intervention either at home or in the clinic following successful completion of the BMIF. The patients who received the home-based tDCS and MM intervention in this study were those included in Ramasawmy et al.55

Mindfulness and tDCS Paradigms

All participants completed an at-home 4-week mindfulness training tailored for FM, with the primary goal of training the patients in mindfulness, primarily MM before the combined intervention. The mindfulness intervention comprised 24 sessions—one guided and supervised online 2-hour group session per week through Zoom (maximum 10 participants) and 5 weekly unsupervised guided home-based practices. The weekly components of the brief mindfulness intervention are detailed in Table 1. The patients monitored their MM and mindfulness routine in a mindfulness diary. The development of the mindfulness intervention was spearheaded by OLGA, an expert mindfulness teacher with more than 10 years’ experience teaching mindfulness, together with PR and FP. The intervention was adapted from the classic mindfulness-based stress reduction program,56,57 with a similar structure to de Moreira et al.58 The online group sessions in German were carried out by a trained mindfulness facilitator with more than 5 years’ experience teaching meditation. The online group sessions included 3 main aspects—the practice of sitting MM, with the breathing as the object of meditation, mindful activities such as mindfulness eating and walking, and topical education. The goal of the education was to inform the participants about the practice of mindfulness and how it relates to FM (Table 1). Participants were encouraged to have an open discussion about their practice and experiences as well as to ask questions to the facilitator. At the end of each group meeting, the participants were instructed to carry out a daily audio-guided MM (20 min) and an informal mindfulness practice of their choice (∼5 min), for example, brushing teeth, walking, and eating. Handouts with the contents of the topic education and the MM recordings were also provided to the participants.

Home-based anodal tDCS comprised a constant 2 mA direct electrical current through a pair of saline-saturated 25 cm2 circular electrodes delivered through a portable battery-driven Sooma tDCS stimulator (Sooma Medical, Helsinki, Finland). Clinic-based anodal tDCS at 2 mA was delivered through a pair of rectangular rubber electrodes (5​​​​​​×7 cm; 35 cm2) by the NeuroConn DC-Stimulator Plus stimulator (NeuroConn, Illmenau, Germany). The anode was placed over the left M1 (C3 according to the 10 to 20 EEG system) and the cathode over the contralateral supraorbital prefrontal cortex (FP2) for both home-based and clinic-based stimulation. For the sham tDCS, the electrodes were placed in the same position as the active stimulation. The previously validated sham protocols59–61 produced an identical indistinguishable sensation to the anodal tDCS.61,62 For the home-based stimulation, patients received prior training in how to operate the device (Fig. 1). All combined interventions were supervised by trained investigators and conducted in groups comprising a maximum of 6 participants. At-home combined intervention sessions were supervised by trained personnel through Zoom. Concurrent to the tDCS, a 20-minute audio-guided MM from the last week of the mindfulness training was applied, which focused on the nonjudgmental, nonattached, and present-moment awareness of breathing.57,58 The same MM audio was played daily throughout the combined intervention. Following the 2 weeks of combined intervention, participants continued their daily mindfulness practice until PC4, and were requested to keep a minimum of 3 times of mindfulness practice per week from PC4 to PC12.

Regarding blinding of the type of stimulation received, the study leader (A.A.), who was not directly involved in the experiments, programmed the NeuroConn stimulators and allocated codes A to D to each stimulator. Two of these codes were anodal tDCS; the others were sham tDCS. For the home-based stimulation, the stimulators were preprogrammed by the Sooma tDCS company and the person in charge of the randomization process received the code of each stimulator and information regarding the nature of the protocol. The experimenters were only provided information about which stimulator was to be assigned to each participant. Since the codes for both clinic-based and home-based stimulators were preprogrammed in the stimulator, both the patient and the interventionist were blinded to the type of stimulation received.

Defining Clinical Responders

Participants rated the German Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ; Cronbach α=0.92), a multidimensional measure covering the most relevant symptom domains of FM, at all measurement time points as part of the battery of questionnaires (Fig. 1).63 The FIQ comprises 10 questions relating to the past week. Sum scores range from 0 (no impact of FM) to 100 (maximal impact of FM). The FIQ is widely used in fibromyalgia studies and has been shown to be more sensitive to subjective changes in symptomatology than ratings of pain intensity.64

We based our definition of responders on an analysis by Bennett et al65 suggesting a 14% reduction in overall FIQ score to be clinically relevant. To provide evidence for symptom reductions upon MM being associated with improved ER, we defined responders to MM a priori as participants reporting ≥14% reduction in FIQ scores at PM compared with BA (clinical responder at PM). To find out whether participants with clinically relevant symptom reduction have an additional benefit in terms of ER from the combination of MM and active tDCS, a second approach to responder classification was chosen: immediate “add-on” responders to the combination of MM and tDCS were defined as those reporting ≥14% decrease in FIQ at PC0 in comparison to PM (clinical responder at PC0).

Description of Questionnaires Used for the Emotion Regulation Composite Score

ER is a multidimensional construct referring to people’s deliberate or unconscious efforts to influence one’s own emotions.66 Although the connection between different strategies of ER and mental health has increasingly become the subject of scientific interest in recent years, a consistent conceptualization is still lacking.67 Therefore, initially, questionnaires either directly or indirectly operationalizing facets of ER were selected to cover a broad spectrum of possible construct definitions originating from varying theoretical backgrounds, as described below. Interestingly, we found ER target variables from the different assessment instruments to be highly intercorrelated (Fig. 2; Cronbach α=0.866). The high internal consistency suggests that all ER target variables selected for the responder analysis appear to measure a similar underlying construct. Consequently, for the main analysis, we integrated the individual ER target variables into an Emotion Regulation Composite Score (ERCS; see statistical analysis for details). In the following section, the theoretical background of the questionnaires used for the assessment of ER is briefly introduced together with their description.

High internal consistency among self-report questionnaires assessing ER. Pairwise intercorrelations of z-standardized emotion regulation (ER) target variables averaged across all measurement time points (BA, PM, PC0, PC4, PC8, PC12) per variable. The internal consistency (Cronbach alpha) averaged across all measurement time points of 0.866 reflects a high covariance between pairs of variables which indicates that all variables appear to measure a similar underlying construct. Emotion regulation composite scores (ERCS) were calculated by averaging z-standardized and equally polarized scores of ER target variables per measurement time point for further analyses.

Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ)

The FFMQ is widely used to assess the effectiveness of MBIS and was constructed based on factor analysis integrating items from 5 independently developed mindfulness questionnaires.68 The German version of the FFMQ consists of 5 factors: observing (α=0.90), describing (α=0.88), acting with awareness (α=0.74), nonjudgment (α=0.80), and nonreactivity (α=0.78), representing the different facets of mindfulness as it is currently conceptualized.69 Individual statements mostly address how people deal with their feelings, thoughts, and bodily sensations (eg, “I watch my feelings without getting lost in them”). In total, the FFMQ contains 39 items (8 per subscale, 7 for subscale nonreactivity) that are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1=“never or very rarely true” to 5=“very often or always true.” For the present analyses, the total summary score of the instrument was used with higher scores indicating a higher level of mindfulness. Psychometric properties (including change sensitivity) of the German version of the FFMQ were shown to be acceptable.69

Emotion Regulation Skills Questionnaire (ERSQ)

The ERSQ comprises 27 items indicative of 9 competencies relevant for successful emotion regulation according to the competence-oriented model of adaptive emotion regulation by Berking and colleagues: attention, clarity, body awareness, understanding, acceptance, resilience, self-support, confrontation, and regulation. Individual statements referring to the past week are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1=“not at all” to 5=“very often or always true” (eg, “During the past week, I paid attention to my feelings”). As the high internal consistency of the total scale of Cronbach α=0.90 suggests one underlying factor, the total sum score was used with higher values indicating better ER competencies. Psychometric qualities (including change sensitivity) of the German version of the ERSQ were found to be satisfactory.70

Volitional Components Inventory (VCI, 52 Items Short Form)

On the basis of previous studies highlighting the impact of mindfulness meditation on self-regulation,71–75 the factors self-regulation, self-access, and general life stress were selected from the German version of the 52 items short form of the VCI.76,77 The VCI was constructed against the theoretical framework of Personality Systems Interactions theory assigning a fundamental role to the (intuitive) self-regulation of positive and negative affect for behavioral flexibility during goal pursuit and thus “successful” adaptation to chronic pain.78 Self-regulation consists of the subscales self-determination, self-soothing, and self-motivation with statements such as “Even when in a state of strong inner tension, I manage to relax.” Self-access comprises the subscales coping with failure, self-awareness, and integrating contradictions with statements such as “When I am in a bad mood, I find it hard to get out of it.” The factor assessing general life stress contains the 2 subscales stress and load (eg, “I must come to terms with huge changes in my life”). Each subscale of the VCI (52 items short form) comprises 4 statements addressing the current situation. The level of agreement is rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (fully agree). Higher scores reflect better self-regulation and self-access and higher general life stress. Psychometric properties (including change sensitivity) of the German version of the 52 items short form of the VCI were shown to be acceptable.76,77

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scales-21 Items (DASS-21)

Convergent to the subscale general life stress of the Volitional Components Inventory, the subscale stress of the German version79 of the DASS-2180,81 (Cronbach α=0.82 to 0.87) was used as an indirect measure of the downregulation of negative affect (eg, “I found it difficult to calm down”). The stress subscale comprises 7 statements relating to the past week which are rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (was very true for me/most of the time true for me) and the sum of the scores for each statement was calculated. Higher scores reflect higher stress levels. Psychometric qualities of the German version of the DASS-21 have been shown to be acceptable.79

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Data analyses were blinded to the type of stimulation received (active or sham). Continuous variables are described by mean and SD, ordinal variables by median and interquartile range. Discrete variables are presented as absolute numbers and frequencies. Data are displayed as mean±SD in tables and mean±SEM in figures. All P-values are 2-tailed. Statistical significance was set at P≤0.05. The respective statistical procedures applied are additionally mentioned in table footnotes and figure legends. Bonferroni multiple testing corrections were performed as indicated there.

Before delving into the responder analysis, we first tested the comparability between the treatment groups (MM+active tDCS and MM+active tDCS) at baseline through an unpaired t test for age, clinical status (assessed by the Symptom Severity Index, Widespread Pain Index, and duration of pain), and FIQ scores. A χ2 test was used to test the comparability of the groups for biological sex.

For the intercorrelation pattern of the target variables integrated into the ERCS, pairwise correlations were assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficient averaged across all measurement time points (BA, PM, PC0, PC4, PC8, PC12). In accordance with the polarity of the majority of target variables, for stress (DASS-21) and general life stress (VCI) polarity was reversed such that higher values represent less stress, reflecting better self-regulatory capacity. The internal consistency averaged across measurement time points was determined by Cronbach alpha reflecting the extent to which the target variables measure aspects of the same latent construct (ie, ER). The ERCS was calculated by averaging z-standardized and equally polarized scores (mindfulness, emotion regulation, self-regulation, self-access, stress, and general life stress) for each measurement time point excluding BA (PM to PC12).

To ensure comparability between responders and nonresponders to the MBI at baseline, we conducted an unpaired t test for age, duration of pain, symptom severity index, widespread pain index, ERCS, and the individual variables within the ERCS. A Mann-Whitney U test was employ in case of violation of parametric assumptions. We conducted the same tests to compare the responders and nonresponders to the combined intervention within each intervention group (MM+active tDCS, MM+sham tDCS).

To assess differences between responders and nonresponders over time (PM to PC12) with respect to the ERCS and individual target variables, mixed-model ANOVA was selected as statistical procedure due to its popularity and “relative simplicity.”82 To adjust for baseline scores, linear regression analyses were performed with the ERCS and individual target variables at PM, PC0, PC4, PC8, and PC12 as dependent variables and respective baseline scores as independent variables. Standardized residuals (adjusted for the respective baseline values) were used as dependent variables for mixed-model ANOVA. Assumptions for mixed-model ANOVA were reviewed by explorative data analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normal distribution. Violations of the normality assumption were detected for the following variables: standardized residuals of ER (ERSQ; PC4) and standardized residuals of self-access (VCI; PC4 and PC8). To account for these, adjunct to the main analyses, analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) of the area under curve (AUC) were conducted, as described below. All remaining variables were normally distributed across measurement time points. Extreme outliers, defined as exceeding three times the interquartile range of a score, could not be identified. The Greenhouse-Geisser adjustment was used to correct for violations from sphericity.

Mixed-model ANOVAs were performed with responder status at PM and responder status at PC0 as fixed factors and standardized residuals of the ERCS and individual target scores as dependent variables over time (PM to PC12). For the responder status at PC0, analyses were carried out stratified by tDCS condition (sham vs. active tDCS). Following the recommendations of Leppink et al,83 main effects were only interpreted in case of insufficient evidence for a group-by-time interaction effect. For group comparisons at each measurement time point, Mann-Whitney U tests were performed (stratified by stimulation group when appropriate). A χ2 or the Fisher exact tests were used to evaluate stimulation group differences with respect to the frequency of responders and nonresponders.

As a complementary approach to mixed-model ANOVA, for analyzing group differences in trajectories of the ERCS, we used the AUC. This approach is widely used in pharmacokinetics and now increasingly being applied with repeated measures designs in psychological and behavioral research.82 Recent evidence supports that AUC performs comparably to Latent Growth Curve Modeling, especially when dealing with smaller sample sizes.82 AUC was determined for the ERCS by applying the composite trapezoidal rule for numerical integration. Each trapezoid was calculated by multiplication of length (sum of 2 consecutive ERCS divided by 2) and width (week difference between 2 consecutive measurement time points; eg, ERCS at PC0 minus score at PM) excluding the baseline. Differences between responders and nonresponders with respect to the AUC of the ERCS were determined by one-way ANCOVA with the baseline score as covariate. For both the ANOVAs and ANCOVAs, the effect size was computed as η2 and was interpreted based on Cohen benchmark categorization (small, η2=0.01; medium, η2=0.06 and large, η2=0.14).84

To explore the long-term response to the combined intervention with respect to differences in ERCS, clinical responders at PC4 were defined as participants reporting ≥14% improvement in FIQ at PC4 in comparison to PM. The proportion of responders and nonresponders stratified by type of stimulation received were compared by the Fisher exact test. For each intervention group, a mixed model ANOVA (responder status as fixed factor and standardized residuals of ERCS corrected to baseline as dependent variables over time) was conducted to investigate how standardized residuals of ERCS corrected to baseline (dependent variable) changes over time (within-subjects factor) depending on responder status (between-subjects factor).

As another exploratory approach to test the response to both the mindfulness training and the combined MM and tDCS intervention, responders to both interventions were defined as participants with responder statuses at both PM and PC0. No statistical analyses were conducted to assess differences in ER comparing responders to both treatment with nonresponders, responders to the brief mindfulness intervention (BMBI) only and responders to combined intervention only due to the low numbers within each group. However, the data was described mathematically.

RESULTS

Participants, Responder Rate, and Baseline Characteristics

Of the 45 participants who completed the 4-week mindfulness intervention and were randomized to the 2 treatment groups, 40 were included in the responder analysis (Fig. 3). The FM patients were 53 years old (SD±11.2) on average (39 women, 1 man). Patients reported to have experienced from widespread pain for an average of 7.4 years (SD±5.6). The mean Widespread Pain Index and Symptom Severity Index were 12.8 (SD±2.9) and 8.3 (SD±1.9), respectively. The MM+active tDCS and MM+sham tDCS groups were comparable at baseline in terms of demographics and FIQ score (Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1. http://links.lww.com/CJP/B221, which compares baseline characteristics between the 2 groups).

Immediately after the BMIF, 18 participants reported a symptom reduction of ≥14% in the FIQ as compared with baseline (ie, responders at PM; Fig. 4A). Of the 18 responders at PM, 10 patients demonstrated an additional benefit from the 2-week combination of MM and tDCS as compared with PM. Of the nonresponders at PM (n=22), 13 individuals remained nonresponders after the combined intervention (Fig. 4B). In total, immediately after the 2-week combination of MM and either active or sham tDCS, (ie, responder status at PC0), 19 patients had an immediate “add-on” response (ie, ≥14% reduction in FIQ in comparison to PM) while 21 patients were classified as nonresponders (ie, <14% reduction in FIQ in comparison to PM) (Fig. 4C). Responders and nonresponders at PC0 were equally distributed between tDCS conditions (active vs. sham tDCS, χ2=0.0; P=0.987).

Responders and nonresponders at PM did not differ with respect to age, indicators of symptom load (duration of widespread pain, Widespread Pain Index, and Symptom Severity Index), and ER target variables at baseline (Table 2). Results for the analysis of individual ER target variables uncovered similar and consistent data trajectories for clinical responders and nonresponders to the BMBI. DISCUSSION

This study was the first, to our knowledge, to explore the effects of a BMBI tailored for FM and the combination of MM with M1-targeted tDCS on ER in individuals with FM. We found that clinical responders to the 1-month MBI exhibited higher ER competency than nonresponders. This improvement in ER lasted for about 6 weeks. Interestingly, however, the beneficial effect of MM on ER was negated by combining MM and M1-tDCS. A consistent pattern was found for the emotion regulation composite score as well as for the individual ER variables. We observed superior ER competencies in responders who had received a combination of MM and sham M1-tDCS as compared with nonresponders of the same treatment condition. In FM patients who had been randomized to the MM+active M1-tDCS condition, no such group difference depending on responder status could be detected. Moreover, although improvements in ER faded over time, significant group differences between responders and nonresponders in the sham stimulation condition could still be detected at PC12.

The rationale for our study was based on the idea to “boost” the effects of MM on brain regions involved in ER by making use of M1 projections to the same circuitry.52 Unexpectedly, our findings point toward antagonistic neuronal mechanisms underlying M1-targeted tDCS and MM. Contrary to the extensive body of literature addressing mindfulness-based ER, fewer studies have looked into tDCS-induced ER. The main tDCS target was the DLPFC, which plays a central role in ER. While single sessions85–87 or multiple sessions88–90 of anodal DLPFC-tDCS were ineffective in ER in healthy individuals, repeated anodal tDCS of the DLPFC has repeatedly shown positive benefits in ER in patients with FM and depression by improving depressive symptoms.38,39,91,92 In a recent meta-analysis, stimulation of the prefrontal cortex by repeated anodal tDCS targeting the prefrontal cortex showed positive effects on explicit ER, which entails the voluntary modification of emotional responses, but not on implicit automatic ER compared with controls.93 No study has yet explored the impact of anodal M1-tDCS on ER strategies. Our current findings contradict the positive effects of combining M1-tDCS and MM in chronic pain conditions54,94,95 in prior investigations. While we previously demonstrated the lack of add-on effect of anodal M1-tDCS to mindfulness practice, which was noted by the lack of group effects on psychological well-being, quality of life, and resilience,55 our findings suggest the antagonistic effects of the 2 methods, suppressing ER when combined with active tDCS over the M1. We postulate that the top-down modulation by the increased excitability of the M1 as a result of repeated anodal tDCS96–99 might inhibit the mindfulness-based mechanistic processes involved in improving ER in FM patients. Future studies should investigate the combination of MM with anodal tDCS over prefrontal cortices involved in ER, rather than the M1 to better assess the add-on effect of tDCS on emotional processing in FM.

Our findings are consistent with the emerging literature supporting mindfulness-induced improvements in ER, which could partially mediate the effect of MBI on psychological distress.74,100,101 Mindfulness practice has been found to support ER28,102–105 and fosters positive mental health by mitigating negative emotions and anxiety in both nonclinical106–109 and clinical samples with chronic pain.110–112 In patients with FM, a 20-week mindfulness intervention demonstrated small to moderate improvements in positive affect, mental health, and resilience compared with treatment-as-usual controls, with benefits maintained up to 6 months postintervention.113 Higher mindfulness levels in FM patients have also been associated with lower pain interference and disease impact as well as improved quality of life and psychological well-being. Mindfulness also has been shown to moderate the impact of FM on anxiety in patients, suggesting that it can play a role in changing coping mechanisms in patients.114 Implementing shorter mindfulness interventions, Amutio and colleagues investigated the effects of a 7-week MBI in FM. Patients exhibited reduced state anxiety, depression levels, and internal expression of anger as well as a higher control of anger, with effects lasting up to 3 months postintervention compared with a waiting-list group.115 Moreover, they have recently shown that this MBI improves ER in women with FM, highlighted by long-lasting (up to 3 mo) increases in emotional intelligence and improved adaptive coping strategies in the intervention group compared with a waiting-list control group.116 A major loophole is the large variability in the content and duration of mindfulness protocols across studies, complicating the interpretation and limiting the comparability across investigations.

Addressing the potential mechanisms underlying mindfulness-based improvements in ER, 2 key processes are top-down cognitive reappraisal and bottom-up experiential alteration.103,117 The Mindfulness-to-Meaning Theory posits that the broadened attention developed during mindfulness enhances the effectiveness of ER by preventing habitual responses to emotion-eliciting stimuli and fostering cognitive reappraisal focused on self-relevant meaningful aspects.118,119 Meanwhile, the Monitor-and-Acceptance Theory proposes that rather than enhancing the effectiveness of ER, mindfulness attenuates the regulatory resources required to downregulate emotional arousal due to the awareness and acceptance of negative and positive emotions.120 Despite disagreements in the field that mindfulness employs an experiential rather than a cognitive mechanism to regulate emotions, the novel and integrative dual-mode model of mindful ER proposes that both mechanisms play important roles in ER.103 The model postulates that mindfulness can enhance the efficiency of ER strategies through cognitive reappraisal and attentional deployment. Furthermore, when mindfulness becomes a trait in regular practitioners, it alters both the generation and regulation of emotions, which becomes more automatic over time, by modulating affective and cognitive systems.32

The neurobiological changes induced by mindfulness meditation are manifold. A recent study addressed the neural mechanisms underlying mindfulness meditation by combining DLPFC-targeted TMS and EEG.121 The authors showed that meditators (on average 6.23 y of regular meditation practice) differ from controls with respect to connectivity between cortical and subcortical brain regions. Likewise, in regular meditators, a shift in the balance between excitation and inhibition (P60/N100 ratio) in the DLPFC was found. The downstream effects on brain systems functionally connected to the DLPFC and implicated in the cognitive top-down regulation of emotions have not been specified, however. Taren et al122 observed greater connectivity between the DLPFC and regions of the ventral and dorsal-corticolimbic networks (which are involved in top-down cognitive control processes) associated with meditation practice.

Central senitization has been proposed as the essential pathogenetic mechanism underlying the multitude of psychophysiological and functional symptoms characterizing patients with FM.123 It is facilitated by early life stress and current psychosocial stressors and has been associated with a dysregulation of the HPA-axis, which is prevalent among individuals with FM.124,125 The detrimental effect of elevated levels of stress and negative affect on various symptom domains in patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain can be mitigated by therapeutic interventions fostering the downregulation of negative affect and the upregulation of positive affect (for an overview, see78). Along these lines, the “Imbalance of Threat and Soothing Systems model” of FM integrates the predominant observations regarding the pathogenesis of FM by hypothesizing that FM results from an imbalance in ER, reflected by an overactive “threat” system and an underactive “soothing” system which maintains the midcingulo-insular brain network in a constant alert mode.17 Interpreted in the light of this model, our findings corroborate the mediating role of ER and may inspire future investigations addressing whether mindfulness meditation contributes to a gain of function of the “soothing systems” through the improvement of the ability to downregulate negative affect.

Our findings must be interpreted in the light of the study’s limitations. First, the lack of a monotherapy intervention group for anodal tDCS and treatment-as-usual controls makes it difficult to draw meaningful interpretations from the add-on effects of pairing MM and tDCS to the mindfulness-based ER effects. A variety of processes involved in the regulation of emotions have been proposed. Gross’126 process model of ER intends to organize these processes, considering the multidimensionality and temporal dynamics of emotions. In short, the model proposes 5 stages of emotion regulation starting with situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation. The questionnaires we selected to operationalize ER in our study most likely measure constructs which can be assigned to the last stage of response-focused ER strategies. Albeit developed on the basis of diverging theoretical considerations, we found the different facets of response-focused ER to be highly intercorrelated in patients with FM. Given the plethora of instruments used for ER assessment across studies, construct analyses including the ER questionnaires most widely used are urgently needed and may ultimately facilitate the interpretation of findings.

Following another line of reasoning, ER can be divided into explicit and implicit processes, which can be differentially impacted by different mindfulness103 or neuromodulation93 protocols. Explicit ER encompasses voluntary and conscious efforts to modify one’s emotional experience or response, while implicit ER involves automatic and unconscious ER processes.127 Our study captures the therapeutic effects of explicit ER through the administered self-report questionnaires, but not the implicit aspects, which cannot be studied using self-reports. Future investigations should include behavioral (eg, emotion N-back task) and psychophysiological measures (eg, heart rate variability) to study implicit ER in FM patients. Furthermore, a recent mechanistic study found abnormal functional connectivity underpinning emotional processing in FM patients, which was measured using functional magnetic resonance imaging during an ER task.16 Implementing such neuroimaging techniques in future studies will allow us to understand the mechanisms potentially involved in ER following treatment with mindfulness and tDCS interventions in FM.
