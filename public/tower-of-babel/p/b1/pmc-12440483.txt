Nonlinear association of non-HDL-C/HDL-C with total trabecular bone score in adults: NHANES 2005-to-2008 study
Authors: Lihua Jiang, Xixi Liu, Jingyun Zhang, Qian Liu, Mingming Yang, Qiumei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The trabecular bone score (TBS) has been demonstrated to be significantly correlated with the mechanical strength of bones, thereby assisting in the prediction of fracture risk. Recently, the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) has become a new way to evaluate abnormalities in lipid metabolism. While some studies have examined the relationship of NHHR to bone mineral density (BMD) and osteoporosis, the evidence regarding its association with trabecular bone score (TBS) is inconclusive. The primary objective of this study was to examine the relationship between NHHR and TBS in a large group of adults, employing a cross-sectional study design. The investigation was predicated on adult NHHR and total TBS statistics from the 2005-2008 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). We used covariate-adjusted multivariate linear regression models, smoothed curve-fitting analyses, and analyses of threshold and saturation effects to investigate the relationship between NHHR and total TBS. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were used to determine the robustness of the results. In addition, we characterized the study population by dividing it into 4 groups based on NHHR quartiles. The findings of the study indicated a substantial negative correlation between NHHR and total TBS, with adjustments made for pertinent covariates. Participants in the lowest quartile demonstrated a decrease in total TBS of 0.019 (95% CI −0.027 to −0.010, P = .0076), in contrast to those in the highest quartile. Furthermore, a nonlinear relationship and a saturation effect were identified between NHHR and total TBS. The study identified an inflection point with a calculated value of 2.467. Subgroup analyses yielded consistent results across age, gender, and BMI subgroups. However, utility values exhibited variability between ethnic groups. The study’s results indicate that adults exhibit a negative correlation between NHHR and total TBS and that this relationship is nonlinear with a saturation effect.

1. Introduction

In the domain of lipid metabolism research, the ratio of non-high-density lipoprotein (NHDL) cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (NHHR) has emerged as a reliable indicator of dyslipidemia. This ratio has demonstrated significant clinical value in assessing cardiovascular disease risk and metabolic syndrome.[1,2] Elevation of the NHHR index suggests the potential adverse impact of lipid metabolism on systemic health.[3] Substantial evidence base has emerged from studies conducted on NHHR and bone mineral density (BMD), as well as osteoporosis, demonstrating a notable relationship between the 2. This suggests a plausible role for NHHR in monitoring bone health status and evaluating risk for osteoporosis.[4]

Trabecular bone score (TBS), a gray-scale texture measurement obtained from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry images of the lumbar spine, reflects early bone microstructural changes.[5] In contrast to conventional BMD, which only reflects bone mass, TBS captures information about bone microstructural degradation. Therefore, TBS can be used as a complementary indicator of BMD for the prediction of fracture risk, especially in patients with normal BMD but increased bone fragility in clinical practice.[6,7] Some clinical studies have further confirmed that bone trabecular fraction can be used independently of BMD as an important predictor of osteoporosis risk.[8,9]

Previous studies have identified correlations between lipid profiles and BMD at both the femoral neck and lumbar spine, but no significant association with TBS.[10] NHHR is a comprehensive index currently used to assess the status of lipid metabolism, while TBS reflects early changes in bone microarchitecture. Therefore, studying the relationship between NHHR and TBS is important for early intervention and management of bone metabolism, as well as for investigating potential mechanisms of influence between lipid metabolism and bone metabolism. To this end, a cross-sectional study design was employed, utilizing data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected between 2005 and 2008.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

Our analysis included 20,497 participants from NHANES 2005-to-2008 cycles. Following established NHANES adult classification criteria and TBS measurement protocols, we excluded 9583 participants aged < 20 years.[11,12] Subsequent exclusions included 2526 participants with missing total TBS data and 343 with missing NHHR data. Following a comprehensive review of the data, the study’s final sample comprised 7855 participants. The process of screening the participants is illustrated in Figure 1.

2.2. Ethical approval

The NHANES database is overseen by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The research team did not have access to any personally identifiable information at any stage of data processing or analysis, and there were no additional interventions or direct interactions with participants during the course of this investigation. All procedures involving human participants were strictly conducted in compliance with the ethical principles delineated in the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as reviewed and endorsed by the NHANES Research Ethics Review Board. Prior to their enrollment in the NHANES program, all study participants were required to provide informed consent. The official NHANES portal provides access to the relevant documentation (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes).

In this study, the expected independent variable is NHHR; total cholesterol (TC) and HDL were measured using enzymatic assays on a Roche Cobas 6000 analyzer. 2.4. The dependent variable selection

The dependent variable in this study is total TBS. The TBS is a texture index derived from the gray-scale changes in the lumbar spine scanned with a Hologic QDR-4500 A fan-beam densitometer. The software used to calculate the TBS was MedIMAP SA (version 2.1.0.2), and the total TBS score for adults aged 20 years and older was used in this study.

2.5. Covariate selection

In the present study, we adjusted for several variables that have the potential to affect the association between NHHR and TBS, including age, gender, race, income (PIR), smoking status, drinking status, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, and cancers. We also adjusted for laboratory findings that may affect bone metabolism, including serum calcium, phosphorus, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, uric acid levels, and vitamin D (Vit D) levels.

2.6. Statistical analysis

NHANES is a population-based, nationally representative, cross-sectional survey. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) has established analytical guidelines for computing all estimates, and sample weights were used to comply with these guidelines.[13] The statistical analysis encompassed the utilization of MEC weights. In the course of conducting a statistical description of the population, the participants were divided into 4 groups according to the quartiles of NHHR. Continuous variables were described by the mean and standard error. For variables that exhibited a normal distribution, 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for the purpose of conducting comparisons between groups. For variables that did not follow a normal distribution (P < .05, Shapiro–Wilk), the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons between groups. Categorical variables were described by percentages, and the chi-square test was used for comparisons between groups. Missing variables are treated with dummy variables. Three distinct models of multiple linear regression analysis were employed to examine the association between NHHR and total TBS. In the context of trend analysis, the quartiles of NHHR were considered as continuous variables. Model 1 was the unadjusted model, while Model 2 adjusted for age and gender. The Model 3 was the fully adjusted model, which controlled for age, gender, race, and education. The nonlinear relationship between NHHR and total TBS was studied by means of smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis. These analyses were used to identify the inflection points. Log-likelihood ratio tests were utilized to analyze the differences between linear fitting and segmented model fitting. In order to evaluate the consistency and reliability of the results, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted based on gender, age, race, and BMI. The remaining variables and their interaction terms were adjusted for, with the exception of NHHR, TBS, and stratification variables. In this study, statistical significance was indicated when P < .05. We evaluated the data statistically using the R programming language (http://www.r-project.org) and EmpowerStats software (https://www.empowerstats.net/cn/).

3.2. Association between NHHR and total TBS

The findings indicated an inverse relationship between NHHR and total TBS in all models (Table 2). When stratified by the quartiles of NHHR, the β values of the higher quartile groups (Q2 to Q4) were significantly negative, in contrast to the Q1 group (P for trend < .0001). In model 3, which was utilized to adjust for confounding factors, the negative correlation between NHHR and total TBS was slightly weakened but remained statistically significant (β = −0.007, 95% CI: −0.009 to −0.005, P = .0003). At the same time, the highest quartile group (Q4) had a significant negative correlation with the reference group (β = −0.019, 95% CI: −0.027 to −0.010, P = .0076), and the trend test was also significant (P for trend = 0.0020).

3.4. Subgroup analysis of the relationship between NHHR and total TBS

Subgroup analyses confirmed a consistent inverse association between NHHR and total TBS in most populations, with significant effect modification by race/ethnicity (p for interaction = 0.038). While robust negative associations were observed in Non-Hispanic White (β = −0.008, 95% CI: −0.011 to −0.005) and Non-Hispanic Black (β = −0.005, 95% CI: −0.010 to −0.000) subgroups, no significant associations were detected in Mexican American, Other Hispanic, or Other Race subgroups. The inverse relationship was consistent across gender (both sexes P < .05) and all BMI categories (p for interaction = 0.099). However, the association became less pronounced in older adults (60–85 years: β = −0.003, 95% CI: −0.006 to 0.000). No significant interactions were identified for gender (P = .739) or age (P = .221) (Table 4).

4. Discussion

NHHR, as a composite lipid parameter, combines the atheroprotective HDL cholesterol and the atherosclerotic non-HDL cholesterol-related lipid components to provide a more complete picture of the overall lipid imbalance.[14] In the field of orthopedics, TBS represents a texture index derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), a sophisticated imaging technique that provides quantitative insight into the microstructure of bone trabeculae. Applied in conjunction with traditional BMD measurements, it provides a more comprehensive assessment of bone quality, thus helping to predict fracture risk more accurately.[7] The results of the present study demonstrated that elevated NHHR was associated with lower total TBS, even after controlling for potential confounding variables. At the same time, we discerned an inflection point at 2.467.

Research on NHHR has been conducted extensively in the cardiovascular field and metabolism-related diseases. The results of NHHR studies in recent years have shown an association with many cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, including coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, myocardial infarction, abdominal aortic aneurysm, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease.[15–18] Meanwhile, concurrent studies in the domains of lipid metabolism and bone metabolism have demonstrated that elevated levels of NHHR may result in decreased lumbar BMD,[4] and high levels of TC and triglycerides (TG) have been correlated with an increased risk of osteoporosis.[2] A study conducted in a Chinese population demonstrated a statistically significant negative correlation between LDL-C/HDL-C and bone mineral content as well as BMD.[19] Meanwhile, animal experiments have shown that mice that have been consuming a high-fat diet for a long time have experienced an augmentation in both TC and LDL cholesterol concentrations, and at the same time, their bone density has significantly decreased.[20] These observations suggest a potential multifaceted correlation between aberrant lipid metabolism and bone metabolic processes.

Osteoporosis is closely related to lipid markers and lipid metabolism. Previous studies have primarily focused on associations between traditional lipid parameters (e.g., LDL-C, triglycerides) and BMD, with limited attention to bone microstructural changes.[21,22] A study by Zolfaroli et al (2021) in menopausal women found a positive relationship between HDL-C levels and BMD in the study cohort.[23] Dysregulation of lipid content and ratios has been demonstrated to contribute to bone imbalance and increased bone resorption by modulating the activity of immune cells and inflammatory factors, thereby promoting osteoclast differentiation.[24,25] Abnormal lipid metabolism can affect the bone microenvironment, promote the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into adipocytes, and reduce the formation of osteoblasts.[26] Reduced HDL levels have been shown to inhibit osteoblast differentiation and affect osteoclast function by altering specific signaling cascades, affecting bone synthesis and ultimately leading to reduced bone mass.[27] LDL-C has been proven to have a deleterious effect on the differentiation and maturation of osteoblasts, as well as to augment the activity of osteoclasts. This results in an imbalance in the process of bone remodeling and a concomitant disruption of bone homeostasis.[28]

Abnormalities in lipid metabolism have been documented to exert a deleterious effect on bone metabolism by modulating a variety of cellular pathways.[29] Oxidatively modified LDL has been shown to increase peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPARγ) expression in monocytes/macrophages and increase C/EBP expression; this, in turn, has been demonstrated to inhibit osteogenesis of bone-derived bone progenitor cells and induce differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells to adipocytes.[30] Increased cholesterol has been shown to inhibit Runx2 expression and to promote PPARγ/C/EBPα expression. This effect is a consequence of the suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by bone morphogenetic proteins and transforming growth factor-β, which in turn promotes the differentiation of mesenchymal cells into adipocytes and the reduction of osteoblast formation.[31] It has been demonstrated that excessive levels of cholesterol can also lead to increased levels of estrogen, which has been found to act on X receptors in the liver to upregulate the expression of RANKL/OPG. Disrupting the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts is a potential consequence of this process, which can result in increased bone resorption.[32]

This investigation sought to ascertain the association between NHHR, a composite lipid index, and total TBS in adults. The findings further demonstrated that disorders of lipid metabolism can not only affect BMD but may also be associated with early alterations in bone microarchitecture. The study identified the threshold phenomenon, which complements previous studies on the correlation between NHHR and BMD. Nonetheless, the study is subject to certain limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design of the study, it was not possible to establish a definitive causal link between NHHR and total TBS, as it merely demonstrates the relationship between the 2 variables. Moreover, it should be noted that the population from which the sample was drawn consisted primarily of subjects originating from a single region. This particular aspect may limit the applicability of the results of this investigation to other demographics. Additionally, the study lacked data on the interaction mechanism between lipids and the bone microstructure.

5. Conclusion

Our research confirms a significant, negative, nonlinear association between NHHR and total TBS in American adults. Our study provides some reference for further research on the relationship between NHHR and bone metabolism.
