Bioinformatics analysis of the expression of TRIM31 mRNA and protein in pan-cancer tissues and their prognostic relevance
Authors: Yujie Mei, Yan Chen, Yuxin Zhou, Lei Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
TRIM31 is a member of the tripartite motif (TRIM) protein family. However, its precise expression pattern and functional role in diverse cancer types remain unclear. This study aimed to analyze the role and clinical significance of TRIM31 in pan-cancer and gastric cancer (GC). We investigated the relationships between TRIM31 expression and multiple aspects, including immune infiltration, stemness index, and responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, and examined the association between TRIM31 expression and the prognosis of patients with GC. TRIM31 expression was elevated in most tumors. Further scrutiny revealed an association between TRIM31 expression and multiple tumor characteristics. Increased TRIM31 expression reduced the benefits of immunotherapy, which were positively correlated with all six stemness indices. The expression level of TRIM31 affected sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents and was linked to prognosis in patients with GC. In both in vitro and in vivo models, TRIM31 promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GC cells. Given its frequent overexpression in various malignancies, TRIM31 could represent a valuable indicator of poor prognosis and altered immune infiltration in individuals with cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19932-5.

Introduction

With population aging and socioeconomic development, cancer has become either the leading or secondary cause of death among older adults worldwide, and there is a marked increase in the global burden attributed to both cancer incidence and mortality rates1. Pan-cancer analysis simultaneously analyzes the genomes of different types of tumors to identify the common characteristics and occurrence processes of tumors from different sources and presents a wide-ranging therapeutic and diagnostic target for various types of tumors in the clinical setting2.

Gastric cancer (GC) is a prevalent form of cancer characterized by subtle early symptoms and a low rate of early detection. For patients with advanced GC3, chemotherapy, instead of radical surgery, has become the main treatment; however, cancer still recurs despite long-term, effective chemotherapy, and the median overall survival is less than 1 year4. The pathogenesis of GC is not completely clear and involves an intricate interplay of environmental and genetic factors as well as the participation of multiple molecular pathways5. Personalized molecular therapy, immunotherapy, and molecular typing have emerged as potential treatment modalities for GC. Research has demonstrated that combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy can lead to an enhancement in the overall survival (OS) duration for patients with advanced stages of GC6. However, GC is a disease with high molecular and phenotypic heterogeneity, creating a pressing need for predictive biomarkers to refine the diagnostic processes and treatment strategies to help improve outcomes.

As part of the tripartite motif (TRIM) protein family, TRIM31 possesses a characteristic RING finger domain that confers its enzymatic function as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, together with the B-box domain, and is responsible for regulating ubiquitination to promote the degradation of specific target protein proteasomes7. Furthermore, as a signaling pathway effector and regulatory gene, it participates in tumor cell invasion, metastasis, and phenotypic regulation, and the crimped helix domain participates in the direct interaction between TRIM31 and p538. TRIM31 exerts function in multiple cellular processes, including cancer development8, immune response regulation9, inflammatory responses10, autophagy11, and cell death12. Upregulated in GC and overexpressed in the precancerous stage, it is a potential biomarker of GC13. However, several studies have indicated that TRIM31 may exhibit a dualistic function in the context of cancer14. Notably, TRIM31 interacts intimately with five other TRIM family proteins (TRIM10, TRIM15, TRIM26, TRIM39, and TRIM40) along with ring finger protein 39 (RNF39), all of which reside within the MHC class I (6p21.33-6p22.2) genomic region. Studies have established that the E3 ubiquitin ligase enzymes derived from these genes are critical regulators of the innate immune response. These enzymes facilitate the efficient elimination of invading pathogens and aberrant cells, thereby playing a decisive role in maintaining immune balance and homeostasis15, but the functional implications of TRIM31 across various cancers, specifically GC, remain unclear.

This study investigated the associations between TRIM31 expression levels and various aspects such as cancer prognosis, implicated biological pathways, the intricate immune milieu within tumors, responses to immunotherapy treatments, and sensitivity to pharmaceutical agents with the aim of evaluating the carcinogenicity of TRIM31 and its effect on pan-cancer immunity and treatment. We also used GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) to validate the expression of TRIM31 and its impact on GC prognosis, aiming to determine the potential prognostic significance of TRIM31 in GC. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that TRIM31 regulates key malignant behaviors of GC cells, including growth, migration, and invasiveness, thereby identifying prospective novel molecular biomarkers for predicting the course of the disease and guiding treatment strategies.

Methods

Downloading and processing of TRIM31 expression data

Standardized pan-cancer datasets were obtained from the Xena database (http://xena.ucsc.edu). The expression data of TRIM31 across individual samples were extracted and logarithmically transformed for data analysis, and the expression data of 33 tumor types were obtained. The R Program version 4.3.1 (https://www.r-project.org/) was used to compute the differential expression.

Prognostic significance of TRIM31 in pan-cancer

The corresponding prognostic datasets for each sample were extracted from the TCGA pan-cancer clinical data resource16. Using the R package survival, a Cox proportional hazards model was constructed, followed by statistical tests. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to generate survival curves. The prognostic disparity between the high and low TRIM31 expression cohorts was assessed.

Gene function enrichment analysis

Based on the weighted gene co-expression network analysis method, the linkET package was used to batch-compute the correlations and P values between TRIM31 and all other mRNA transcripts. Using the limma package, we conducted a comparative analysis of the gene expression profiles between groups with high and low TRIM31 expression. The identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) constituted a subsequent step. Then, the R package clusterProfiler was used to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses17,18. The outcomes of these enrichment analyses were visually rendered using the enrichplot, GOplot, and ClusterProfiler R libraries. The top 100 mRNAs were selected for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) based on their correlation coefficients.

Analysis of mutation characteristics, copy number variation, tumor mutation load, and microsatellite instability

From the Genomic Data Commons database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), we sourced simple nucleotide variation and copy number variation (CNV) datasets for all TCGA samples that had undergone preprocessing. Using the R package maftools, the tumor mutation load (TMB) was systematically calculated and incorporated into our analysis, and the correlation was evaluated using the Pearson test. The microsatellite instability (MSI) scores for each cancer type were obtained from existing studies19, and the MSI data and gene expression patterns for the samples were combined and subjected to Pearson’s correlation coefficient calculation.

Analysis of immunological characteristics

To determine the extent of tumor immune cell infiltration, eight separate algorithms within the R package IOBR, namely Estimate, CIBERSORT-ABS, MCP-counter, quanTIseq, TIMER, xCell, immunophenoscore (IPS), and EPIC, were used. We used the psych package in R to calculate the correlation between TRIM31 expression and immune infiltration scores across various tumor types. The data of 150 immunomodulation-related genes20 were extracted from the expression profiles of tumors. Tumor purity data from previous studies20 were integrated, and Pearson correlation analysis was carried out. Using the R package ESTIMATE, we computed the StromalScore, immuneScore, and ESTIMATE Scores for each GC patient’s tumor sample and further analyzed the linkages between TRIM31 expression and the immunological characteristics of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in GC in the aforementioned aspects.

Associations between TRIM31 expression levels and tumor stemness

From a previous study21, we acquired six tumor stemness indices. We then computed the Pearson correlation between the expression of TRIM31 and the stemness index.

Assessment of interrelationships between TRIM31 and drug sensitivity

According to the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; https://www.cancerrxgene.org), the natural logarithmic values of the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the respective drugs were retrieved. Clinical chemotherapeutic responses were predicted using the R package oncoPredict based on TRIM31 expression.

Analysis of TRIM31 expression and survival and prognosis among individuals diagnosed with GC

The GSE26942, GSE57302, GSE183136, and GSE38749 datasets were downloaded from the GEO. To identify independent variables affecting OS, we applied both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. The research variables included the pathological stage, TNM classification, as well as the expression level of TRIM31. The R package rms was used to construct the line diagram of the OS of patients with GC and construct a correction curve to verify the accuracy of the alignment diagram. Decision curve analysis was performed to compare this line chart with the AJCC version 8 TNM stages to assess its benefits.

Clinical samples

A total of 188 paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were obtained from GC patients who had not received any preoperative anti-tumor therapy at the First Hospital of Lanzhou University (Lanzhou, China) between 2019 and 2021. Clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. An additional cohort consisting of four fresh GC tissues and matched normal adjacent tissues was collected from the same institution for qRT-PCR analysis and assessment of TRIM31 protein expression. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research on Drugs and Medical Devices at the First Hospital of Lanzhou University.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed as previously described22,23. The IHC score for each tissue slide was determined by multiplying the staining intensity score by the percentage of positively stained cells22,23. Scoring was independently conducted by two pathologists in a blinded and standardized manner.

Cell culture and treatments

Human gastric cancer (GC) cell line MKN-45 and the immortalized gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 were acquired from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (HyClone, USA), enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixture (Beyotime, China) to inhibit bacterial growth. Cultures were kept at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and 95% air. All experiments were carried out with cells in the exponential growth phase to ensure consistent and comparable results.

Cellular transfection

Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) was employed for cell transfection in compliance with the supplier’s protocols. MKN-45 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and transfected with either a TRIM31-targeting shRNA or a non-targeting control construct (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). All experimental procedures were replicated three times to ensure data consistency.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from gastric cancer (GC) cells using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the isolated RNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). The mRNA expression levels of selected genes were then quantified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Endogenous β-actin was used as an internal control for normalization of gene expression data. The primer sequences are in Table S2.

Colony formation assays

Six-well plates were seeded with approximately 500 cells per well and cultured in complete medium until colony formation was observed, based on a previously published method22,23. Colony numbers were analyzed with the aid of Image J software. To ensure experimental reliability, each condition was tested in at least three independent replicates.

Migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion abilities were assessed using Transwell experiments, following previously established protocols22,23. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure data reliability.

Experiments with animals

Six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Lanzhou University (Lanzhou, China) and randomly allocated to different experimental groups. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University. Gastric cancer (GC) cells treated with various inhibitors were resuspended in PBS, adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL, and injected subcutaneously into the flank region of each mouse at a volume of 100 µL. Tumor growth was assessed regularly by caliper measurements. Mice were monitored twice weekly after tumors became detectable. At four weeks post-injection, the animals were euthanized humanely via isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Subsequently, tumors were carefully excised from the injection sites for further analysis.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0, SPSS 23.0, and R software (v4.3.1; R Core Team). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 signifies statistical significance. we used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to compare two independent groups and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare matched pairs. A paired-sample t-test was used to analyze discrepancies within paired samples. The correlation between TRIM31 expression and immune cells was determined using Spearman’s rank correlation test. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared via the Log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were utilized to estimate hazard ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research (Drugs and Devices) of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University (Approval No.: LDYYLL2023-168). The study conforms to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University (Approval No.: LDYYLL2023-167). The study was conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the U.S. National Institutes of Health and was reported following the ARRIVE guidelines for animal research.

Results

Differential expression of TRIM31 in pan-cancer

Analysis of the TCGA data revealed that TRIM31 exhibited significantly increased expression levels in eight different types of cancer tissues relative to their normal tissue counterparts: stomach cancer (STAD), bile duct cancer (CHOL), kidney clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), liver cancer (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma, pancreatic cancer (PAAD), and thyroid cancer. The expression of TRIM31 was significantly down-regulated in breast cancer (BRCA), colon cancer (COAD), kidney chromophobe cancer (KICH), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), and prostate cancer (PRAD) (Fig. 1A, B). Compared with matched paracancerous tissues, a similar increase was observed in tumor tissues (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, we integrated the data from both the TCGA and GTEx databases to comprehensively assess the expression profile of TRIM31 across 34 different cancer types. These findings indicate a statistically discernible disparity in TRIM31 expression levels across 22 distinct cancer types (Fig. 1D).

Relationship between the expression of TRIM31 and clinical stage in pan-cancer

The analysis demonstrated that in stages I, II, and III of lung adenocarcinoma (P = 0.01), stages II, III, and IV of esophageal cancer (ESCA, P = 0.02), stages II, III, and IV of stomach and esophageal carcinoma (STES, P = 0.03), and stages I, II, and III of KIRP (P = 0.04), pan-kidney cancer (KIPAN, P = 5.6e−3), and rectal cancer (READ, P = 0.04), the expression of TRIM31 was upregulated with an increase in stage (Fig. 2A–F). In bladder cancer (BLCA) (P = 1.3e−3), the expression of TRIM31 decreased with increasing stage (Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, the expression of TRIM31 decreased with increasing stage in adrenocortical cancer (ACC) (P = 4.9e−3; Fig. 2H).

Prognostic value of TRIM31 in pan-cancer

Forest plots were constructed using univariate Cox regression analysis. We plotted the Kaplan-Meier survival curves. The outcomes revealed that in KIRP, KIPAN, KIRC, PAAD, and CHOL, increased TRIM31 expression was significantly linked to inferior OS. Conversely, lower TRIM31 expression levels in skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), BLCA, and ACC were associated with poorer prognosis (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S1A). The expression of TRIM31 was significantly associated with disease-specific survival (DSS) in seven distinct tumor types: KIRP, KIPAN, PAAD, KICH, SKCM, BLCA, and ACC. In survival analysis, higher expression levels of TRIM31 in KIRP, KIPAN, PAAD, and KICH tumors were associated with a shorter disease-free interval, thereby suggesting a worse prognosis in terms of disease recurrence or progression (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. S1B). In KIRP, KIPAN, PCPG, low-grade glioma (LGG), and glioblastoma and low-grade glioma (GBMLGG), the expression of TRIM31 was correlated with disease-free interval. In KIRP, KIPAN, and PCPG, increased TRIM31 expression was indicative of an unfavorable prognosis (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. S1C). Simultaneously, increased expression of TRIM31 was found to correlate negatively with progression-free intervals in cases of KIRP, KIPAN, PAAD, and KICH, indicating poorer outcomes. The prognosis was poor for six tumor types: GBMLGG, LGG, PRAD, SKCM, BLCA, and ACC (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. S1D).

Functional analysis of TRIM31 in pan-cancer

Cancers were grouped according to prognosis, good and poor outcomes. Taken together, the above results showed that high TRIM31 expression in KIRP, KICH, KIRC, PAAD, and STAD was significantly correlated with poorer prognosis. Based on the Weighted Gene Correlation Network Analysis method, the correlation and P value of TRIM31 and other mRNAs in the above-mentioned cancers with poor prognosis were batch-calculated, and the top 100 genes that correlated with the expression of TRIM31 were derived and analyzed with GSEA. The results showed 22 common pathways that TRIM31 might be involved in, including glycolysis, apical junction, KRAS signaling DN, complement, p53, estrogen response late, epithelial, mesenchymal transition, DNA repair, xenobiotic metabolism, myogenesis, TNFA signaling via NF-κB, protein secretion, mitotic spindle, IL-6/JAK/STAT-3 signaling, inflammatory response, allograft rejection, mTORC1 signaling, α/γ interferon responses, E2F targets, unfolded protein response, G2M checkpoint, and MYC targets v1 (Fig. 4A). Differential analysis was performed on the samples with high and low expressions of TRIM31 to obtain the DEGs. GO (Fig. 4B) and KEGG (Fig. 4C) enrichment analyses were performed, and the numbers of common pathways were 9 and 4, respectively. In BLCA, ACC, LGG, and SKCM, low TRIM31 expression was significantly associated with poorer prognosis. GSEA revealed 18 common pathways, including epithelial mesenchymal transition, androgen response, bile acid metabolism, allograft rejection, fatty acid metabolism, peroxisome, estrogen response late, complement, xenobiotic metabolism, apical junction, inflammatory response, α interferon response, p53 pathway, myogenesis, angiogenesis, mitotic spindle, estrogen response late, mTORC1 signaling, unfolded protein, spermatogenesis response, MYC targets v1 and v2, E2F targets, and G2M checkpoint (Fig. 4D).

Varying expression levels of TRIM31 in GC have been linked to the clinicopathological characteristics of the affected patients

Based on the TCGA, we analyzed the expression of TRIM31 in tumor and normal tissues in GC and demonstrated that TRIM31 expression was upregulated in GC compared to that in normal tissues. A similar increase in TRIM31 expression was observed when compared with matched adjacent non-tumor tissues (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A, B). Moreover, the expression patterns of TRIM31 mirrored those in the TCGA dataset across the independent datasets GSE66229, GSE51575, and GSE29272 (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C-H).

Various clinicopathological characteristics in patients with GC

We examined the correlation between the expression of TRIM31 in the TCGA database and clinicopathological characteristics among individuals diagnosed with GC, which demonstrated that the expression of TRIM31 was not related to sex, pathological stage, or cancer progression stages (T, N, and M stages) in patients with GC (Fig. 6A, C–F). However, increased TRIM31 expression was observed in elderly patients (P = 0.03, Fig. 6B).

Functional analysis of TRIM31

Based on the weighted gene co-expression network analysis method, the correlation and P values between TRIM31 and all other mRNA were calculated in batches, and the first 10 genes related to TRIM31 expression were obtained (Fig. 7A). A heatmap was used to identify DEGs (Fig. 7B). GO analysis revealed that TRIM31 was significantly associated with several biological processes, including the modulation of chemical synaptic transmission, regulation of trans-synaptic signaling, and cellular responses to xenobiotic stimuli. Moreover, it implicated TRIM31 in various biological functions involving membrane transporter activities, such as metal ion transmembrane transport, quaternary ammonium group transmembrane transport, organic cation transmembrane transport, and sodium ion transmembrane transport, as well as participation in gated channel activity (Fig. 7C–G, Supplementary Fig. S2A-D). KEGG analysis showed that abnormally increased TRIM31 expression was enriched in neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, cell adhesion molecules, xenobiotic metabolism by cytochrome P450, retinol metabolism, chemical carcinogenesis DNA adducts, insulin secretion, maturity-onset diabetes of the young, and other signaling pathways (Fig. 7H–I, Supplementary Fig. S2E). In addition, the GSEA revealed that TRIM31 expression was significantly linked to several key pathways and processes, including α/γ interferon responses, E2F target gene activation, G2/M cell cycle checkpoint regulation, proliferation-promoting pathways such as Myc targets versions 1 and 2, the KRAS signaling cascade, NF-κB signaling pathway, early estrogen response, and DNA repair mechanisms, among others (Fig. 7J–L, Supplementary Fig. S2F–S).

Mutational features, copy number variation, and correlation with tumor mutational load of TRIM31

To explore the reasons for the increased TRIM31 expression, we scrutinized the genetic variations, CNV, TMB, and MSI pertaining to TRIM31 in the TCGA dataset. First, we used the R package maftools to extract the structural domain information of the TRIM31 protein (Fig. 8A). After integrating the copy number variation data and gene expression data of the samples, we further calculated the differential expression of the gene across various clinical stages within each tumor type and observed that COAD, COAD/READ, BRCA, STES, lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), LIHC, and ovarian cancer (OV), including STAD, were significantly different (Fig. 8B and C). Second, we examined the genetic alteration status, and the results of the test of variance for all genes are presented in Fig. 8D. Our analysis revealed that TRIM31 expression correlated with TMB in five tumor types, specifically ESCA, STES, KIPAN, LUSC, and uterine carcinosarcoma. Conversely, it displayed a negative correlation with TMB in COAD, COAD/READ, and PRAD (Fig. 8E) and was significantly positively correlated with MSI in four tumors, GBMLGG, STES, KIRP, and KIRC, and a significant negative correlation with MSI in six tumors, including COAD, COAD/READ, PRAD, HNSC, OV, and uterine carcinosarcoma (Fig. 8F).

Immunological characterization of TME

We hypothesized that there may be a specific link between TRIM31 and immunotherapy based on these results. Our findings indicated that TRIM31 expression was significantly associated with immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoint genes across most cancer types (Supplementary Fig. S3A-I). Understanding the intricate interactions between tumor cells and their TME is critical for elucidating cancer development. To determine how increased TRIM31 expression contributes to unfavorable outcomes in patients with GC, we performed an analysis correlating TRIM31 expression with TME-related scores. Our findings indicated that TRIM31 expression in GC was strongly and positively associated with StromalScore, immuneScore, and ESTIMATE Scores. In contrast, there was a marked inverse correlation with tumor purity (Fig. 9A, Supplementary Fig. S3J). Using multiple algorithms for immune infiltration analysis, we found a correlation between the expression of TRIM31 and the immunological traits inherent to the TME in GC (Fig. 9B–I). CIBERSORT analysis revealed a higher proportion of infiltrating follicular helper T (Thf) cells and M1 macrophages in tumors expressing high levels of TRIM31 than in those with low TRIM31 expression. Conversely, tumors with low TRIM31 expression exhibited significantly increased proportions of infiltrating M2 macrophages and monocytes (Figs. 8E-I and 9C, Supplementary Fig. S3K-L). Additionally, the results of the single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm indicated that in STAD, TRIM31 expression had a predominantly negative correlation with a range of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, including effector memory CD4 + T cells, mast cells, natural killer cells, natural killer T cells, and type 1 helper T cells; conversely, it had a positive correlation with type 17 helper T cells (Fig. 9D). In addition, we confirmed that in STAD, TRIM31 correlated with immune checkpoints, such as tumor necrosis factor-related immune genes (TNFRSF14, TNFRSF15), HHLA2, and LGALS9 (Fig. 9J). We examined the IPS in the context of different TRIM31 expression levels using the TCGA database. The analysis revealed that the low TRIM31 group exhibited higher IPS than the high TRIM31 group, implying that patients with lower TRIM31 expression might derive more benefits from immunotherapy (Fig. 9K).

TRIM31 is an independent predictor of prognosis in patients with GC

The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high TRIM31 expression displayed notably shorter OS times than those with lower TRIM31 expression (Fig. 13A–D). We assessed the predictive power of TRIM31 expression with the pathological characteristic parameters and TNM stage for patients with GC. Cox regression analysis confirmed that TRIM31 expression serves as a standalone predictor of OS in GC (Fig. 13E–L). These findings suggested that TRIM31 expression independently predicts the prognosis of patients with GC. We found that TRIM31 could predict prognosis by constructing a nomogram (Fig. 13M–O), and this was verified by calibration curves (Fig. 13P–R). Using decision curve analysis to assess whether there was a benefit of the model in clinical application, the nomogram model showed a greater net benefit in predicting 5- and 10-year OS rates compared to the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system (Fig. 13S–U).

TRIM31 is highly expressed in GC tissues and correlated with prognosis of GC patients

To validate the above findings, we further investigated the expression levels and clinical relevance of TRIM31 in gastric cancer (GC) tissues. A total of 188 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded GC samples were collected for immunohistochemical (IHC) staining (Fig. 14A). TRIM31 was overexpressed in GC patient samples (118/188, 62.77%), with a significantly higher frequency compared to adjacent normal tissues (68/188, 36.17%) (Fig. 14B). In four pairs of fresh GC specimens, TRIM31 expression at the mRNA level was elevated in tumor tissues compared to corresponding normal gastric tissues (Fig. 14C). Elevated TRIM31 expression was significantly correlated with advanced TNM stage (P = 0.006), deeper tumor invasion (P = 0.003), and the presence of lymph node metastasis (P = 0.041), (Table 1; Fig. 14D), Univariate and multivariate analyses confirmed that TRIM31 expression is an independent prognostic predictor for both OS and progression-free survival (PFS) in GC patients (Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that elevated TRIM31 expression is associated with poor prognosis of patient with GC (Fig. 14E).

TRIM31 promotes the proliferation and invasion of GC cells

To further elucidate the biological function of TRIM31 in GC cells, a colony formation assay was performed. As shown in Fig. 15A, silencing TRIM31 in MKN45 cells led to a notable decrease in colony numbers relative to the control group. Transwell invasion assay revealed that the knockdown of TRIM31 significantly reduced the capacity of invasion in MKN45 cells (Fig. 15B). The volume and weight of subcutaneous xenograft tumors derived from the TRIM31-knockdown group were significantly lower than those of mock cells (Fig. 15C–D), Hematoxylin and Eosin staining confirmed that the subcutaneous xenograft tumors were originated from human GC cells (Fig. 15E–F). Collectively, these findings indicate that TRIM31 promotes the proliferation and invasion of GC cells.

Discussion

Currently, the molecular mechanisms underlying GC invasion and metastasis remain incompletely understood. Emerging evidence indicates that tripartite motif-containing protein 31 (TRIM31) facilitates GC cell proliferation and invasion by modulating Axin1 protein stability, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway24. Our data suggested that TRIM31 expression is significantly linked to clinicalpathological parameters and prognostic outcomes in GC patients. In vitro and in vivo experiments further confirmed that TRIM31 promotes GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Given its widespread upregulation in malignancies and its associations with aggressive tumor phenotypes and immune modulation, TRIM31 may represent a novel biomarker for predicting poor clinical outcomes and immune landscape alterations in cancer patients.

The TRIM family, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, participates in a variety of cell signal transduction and biological processes in different cancer types, thus exhibiting the dual functions of carcinogenesis and cancer inhibition25. The TRIM31 gene is structurally and functionally similar to its family members. However, the differential expression of its three subtypes (TRIM31α, TRIM31β, and TRIM31γ) may lead to different roles of TRIM31 in cancer14. However, its expression profile and functional roles across different cancer types remain poorly characterized. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer and GC-specific analysis to explore the biological and clinical relevance of TRIM31. Our findings revealed that TRIM31 is frequently overexpressed in most tumor types and is significantly associated with multiple clinicopathological features, including TMB, MSI, tumor purity, and stemness index. Furthermore, TRIM31 expression was closely linked to immune infiltration, the expression of immune regulatory and checkpoint-related genes, and responses to immunotherapy or chemotherapy. Notably, elevated TRIM31 levels were correlated with reduced immunotherapeutic efficacy and positively associated with all six stemness-related indices.

GO enrichment analysis showed that the expression levels of TRIM31 protein in GC have emerged as intimately associated with diverse biological functions, such as the regulation of chemical synaptic transmission and heterobiotic metabolic processes, and are closely related to biological functions, such as metal ion transmembrane transport activity and gated channel activity. The KEGG analysis revealed that TRIM31 overexpression was associated with perturbations in several signaling pathways, prominently including those involving neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, drug metabolism, cell adhesion molecules, insulin secretion, cytochrome P450 metabolism of xenogeneic organisms, chemical carcinogen-DNA adduct, and retinol metabolism. In addition, the GSEA showed that TRIM31 expression is closely associated with α/γ interferon response, G2M checkpoint, E2F transcription factor family, processes associated with cellular proliferation, including Myc targets version 1, KRAS signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, early estrogen response, and DNA repair. Previous research has established that TRIM31 relates to tumor advancement through its regulatory effects on key signaling pathways such as P53, mTORC1, PI3K-Akt, NF-κB, and Wnt/β-catenin24,26–34. TRIM31 interacts with Axin1 protein by ubiquitination and degradation, which in turn activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby contributing to the development and progression of GC24. Inhibition of TRIM31 gene expression in bladder cancer cells can inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, thereby regulating the EMT of bladder cancer cells and inhibiting the movement and infiltration capabilities of bladder cancer cells28. Meanwhile, p53 is known to play a pivotal role in cellular senescence29. TRIM31 can directly promote the poly ubiquitination of the K48 connection and the proteasomal degradation of p53 and promote the resistance of liver cancer cells to lost-nest apoptosis30, while mediating the up-regulation of inflammatory cytokines through the NF-κB pathway, and promoting the invasion and metastasis of colorectal cancer31. TRIM31 stimulates the growth, invasiveness, and mobility of glioma cells via the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway32 and enhances the chemotherapy resistance of temozolomide in glioblastoma by activating PI3K/Akt33. Abnormal cell cycle progression is a significant contributor to cancer development34. An imbalance in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway drives excessive proliferation and abnormal differentiation of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which can ultimately contribute to the onset of various conditions, including cancer35. TRIM31 knockout after irradiation increases the production of ROS, aggravates radiation-induced DNA damage, and prolongs the G2/M stasis time, thus causing apoptosis and radiosensitization of colorectal cancer cells36. Furthermore, after transfection with TRIM31, the expression levels of cyclin D1 and cyclin E decreased in NSCLC cell lines37. Our findings align with these results, TRIM31 may promote the development of multiple cancers, but the specific molecular mechanisms need to be further explored.

CNV represents a type of genomic structural alteration that leads to an irregular number of gene copies, affecting the function of multiple signaling pathways and significantly contributing to the onset and progression of numerous types of cancer. Several recognized or potentially promising therapeutic targets for anticancer drugs, such as HER2, TUBB3, and TOP2A, frequently exhibit high levels of CNVs38. These CNVs could serve as valuable data for refining the molecular classification of GC and tailoring treatment strategies accordingly39. Emerging evidence indicates that co-expression of HER2 and HER3 may enhance mTOR phosphorylation, potentially activating downstream signaling pathways40. In addition, HER3 plays a critical role in the clinicopathological characteristics and prognostic outcomes of GC41. Our study found significant differences in the CNV in COAD, COAD/READ, BRCA, STES, LUSC, LIHC, and OV, particularly in STAD, which is consistent with previous research results42–44. TMB and MSI are significant biomarkers that serve as predictors of the immunotherapy response45–47. In 2014, TCGA proposed four subtypes of GC: Epstein-Barr virus positive, MSI, genomic stability, and chromosomal instability48. Recent research has revealed a strong correlation between TMB status and the prognosis of patients with GC49–51. Our findings revealed a positive correlation between the expression of TRIM31 and TMB in various types of tumors, including ESCA, STES, KIPAN, LUSC, and UCS. TRIM31 expression levels positively correlated with MSI in GBMLGG, STES, KIRP, KIRC, and other tumors.

Immune cells are the most abundant non-cancer cells in the TME52 and actively participate in intricate crosstalk with stromal cells, cancer cells, and cytokines, thereby shaping the immune landscape and significantly impacting the trajectory of tumor progression53. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key drivers of tumor-associated immune responses54–57. The induction of M2 polarization in TAMs within the TME and the concurrent suppression of M1 polarization are pivotal factors that contribute to the establishment of immune tolerance58,59. Research indicated that macrophages can stimulate mesenchymal stem cells to differentiate into fibroblasts in GC, which subsequently contribute to the creation of an immune-tolerant environment60. Within the TME, Thf cells promote B-cell activation mainly through the secretion of interleukin 21 (IL-21)61,62. Inhibition of IL-21 alone can significantly reduce B-cell activation induced when anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 treatments are administered concurrently, highlighting their significant role in enhancing the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy63. We used eight independent algorithms to comprehensively calculate the level of immune cell invasion and found that TRIM31 expression was significantly linked to most instances where cancer cells infiltrate and evade the immune system, immune regulatory, and immune checkpoint genes. CIBERSORT immune cell infiltration analysis of GC showed that the infiltration proportion of Tfh cells and M1 macrophages was markedly greater in the population characterized by increased TRIM31 expression than in those with lower TRIM31 expression levels. The infiltration percentages of M2 macrophages and monocytes in the low TRIM31 group were significantly higher. We also used the ssGSEA algorithm to estimate the penetration level of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the GC TME and found that TRIM31 expression inversely correlated with effector memory CD4 + T cells, mast cells, NK cells, and NK T cells in STAD. Conversely, it exhibited a positive correlation with T helper 17 cells, indicating that TRIM31 is associated with immunosuppression in GC. Several preclinical and clinical studies have shown the effectiveness of NK cell-based cancer immunotherapy64, which may play a key role in future multimodal cancer treatment strategies65. The prognosis of patients with GC tends to improve as the infiltration levels of NK cells increase both within the tumor tissue and in the peripheral blood66. Activated NK cells in animal experiments show antitumor activity against GC cells67. Diminished expression of human leukocyte antigen class I (HLA-I), located at the 6p21.32 genomic region, results in decreased NK cell infiltration within GC tissues and diminished responsiveness to NK cell cytotoxicity68, which is consistent with our findings. Tumor cells lead to immunotherapy resistance through immune escape mechanisms such as an impaired capability for antigen presentation and increased activation of immune checkpoint mechanisms69. Extensive research on immune checkpoint inhibitors offers hope for improving the pretreatment of patients with cancer70–73. This study found that the expression of TRIM31 in GC is associated with several immune checkpoints, such as TNF-related immune genes (TNFRSF14 and TNFRSF15), HHLA2, and LGALS9. Targeting TRIM31 may contribute to the OS of patients. Moreover, the findings revealed a negative correlation between TRIM31 expression and response to immunotherapy.

Meanwhile, some evidence suggests that heterogeneous interactions between tumor cells and CSCs exert a substantial influence within the TME53. Research has demonstrated that CSCs significantly contribute to the development of chemoresistance in cancers. It is also associated with tumor recurrence after conventional chemotherapy74. For example, lung CSC markers lead to EGFR-TKI drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma by regulating CSC stemness, proving the existence of a discernible association between lung CSCs and drug resistance observed in lung adenocarcinoma cases75. In the TME, GC stem cells engage with immune cells, inflammatory cells, cytokines, chemokines, and other elements, fostering tumor invasion. The mobilization of immune cells triggers angiogenesis and sustains self-renewal capabilities76. This investigation revealed that TRIM31 expression is notably associated with most CSC indicators. In particular, within GC types, the group with high TRIM31 expression displayed a statistically significant positive correlation with the dry index score. Cuproptosis, a recently identified form of cell death, is hypothesized to be strongly associated with changes in mitochondrial respiratory capacity, cellular metabolism, and protein lipoylation77. Research has shown that copper ion levels in the serum of patients with different types of tumors are markedly increased compared with those in healthy individuals, with a direct link to several signaling pathways active within tumor cells78. However, some tumors characterized by stem cell properties and drug resistance display increased mitochondrial metabolic activity, which may be associated with copper death79–81. Therefore, it is speculated that overexpression of TRIM31 may potentially enhance the proliferation and maturation of tumor stem cells by modulating copper-induced cell death, consequently contributing to a less favorable patient prognosis.

Drug sensitivity analysis showed that the higher the expression level of TRIM31, the greater the resistance to 12 commonly used antitumor drugs, including afatinib and gefitinib. Xiao et al. found that the knockdown of TRIM31 enhanced the sensitivity of acute myeloid leukemia to daunorubicin82. These results suggest that TRIM31 has great potential for predicting therapeutic benefits in GC. With the expression level of TRIM31, the outcome of drug treatment can be predicted more accurately.

Although our findings suggest that TRIM31 may play a role in modulating tumor–immune interactions, the specific mechanisms by which it participates in signaling pathways, protein ubiquitination processes, or downstream functional regulation remain to be fully elucidated. Further investigations using approaches such as gene knockout/knockdown, overexpression systems, protein interaction assays, and in vivo models are necessary to uncover the functional and mechanistic roles of TRIM31 in gastric cancer (GC) development, and combining multi-omics profiling with experimental validation could further enhance our understanding of its biological relevance. It should be noted that due to incomplete clinical treatment data, our survival analyses did not adjust for treatment modalities such as chemotherapy or targeted therapy; future studies based on prospectively collected and well-annotated treatment information are needed to validate our findings under more rigorously controlled conditions. Importantly, the implication that TRIM31 could serve as a biomarker for immunotherapy outcome remains speculative at this stage. Future research should aim to validate these findings in well-annotated clinical cohorts with immunotherapy treatment records and to mechanistically explore the role of TRIM31 in immune evasion and response to checkpoint blockade.

Conclusions

This study discovered that TRIM31 expression levels were upregulated in most tumors and were associated with clinicopathological features, poor prognosis, stemness of some tumors, and TMB, MSI, and TME changes. Its increased expression is notably linked to a poorer prognosis for patients with GC, multiple biological processes and signaling pathways, abnormal immunological characteristics of the TME, the efficacy of immunotherapy, and a higher tumor stemness index in GC. Taken together, these findings establish TRIM31 as a potential prognostic indicator and a candidate therapeutic target not only in GC but also across a range of malignancies. Further mechanistic investigations are necessary to delineate its molecular actions and assess its applicability in clinical and translational oncology research.
