Introduction to the special issue on ethics and equity in implementing novel biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias: current progress and new directions
Authors: Fred B. Ketchum, Annalise Rahman‐Filipiak
Published: 2026

For this Special Issue of Alzheimer's & Dementia: Diagnosis, Assessment, and Disease Monitoring authors were invited to submit manuscripts that speak to ethical, psychosocial, and pragmatic considerations for the implementation of blood and other novel biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease (AD). These include the delicate balance between promoting maximum benefit (beneficence) and reducing potential harms (non‐maleficence), supporting informed decision‐making and respecting the right to know or not know (autonomy), and ensuring novel biomarker testing and follow‐up clinical and psychological care are distributed equitably across diverse populations and settings (equity and justice). These manuscripts advance our understanding of biomarkers in novel populations and highlight the complex and highly nuanced ethical issues relevant to AD biomarker testing across a variety of settings, contexts, and geographic locations.

The development and validation of less invasive, cheaper, and more widely accessible biomarker tests represent an opportunity to address the well‐documented disparities in how early, how often, and how accurately patients from underrepresented and underserved backgrounds are diagnosed and treated. 1 , 2 Given the increasing use of biomarker screening for clinical trial eligibility, these novel markers may also catalyze much needed improvement in the diversity of populations enrolled in AD research. However, several papers in this Special Issue demonstrate that participants from certain racial‐ethnic and cultural backgrounds may be less likely to engage in cognitive, biomarker, or other diagnostic testing. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Despite the fact that almost half of older adult respondents to the National Poll on Healthy Aging endorsed concerns about memory loss and Alzheimer's dementia, awareness of blood biomarker tests remains limited. 3 Asian and Hispanic participants in particular endorsed lower rates of completing cognitive screening, an important piece of information to determine whether biomarker testing is appropriate. Several contributions highlight the significance of culturally adapted counseling to address the unique needs of different patient populations as a fundamental step toward elucidating and addressing these disparities. Among Puebloans residing in the Southwest United States, Ryman et al. recommend strategies for collective decision‐making regarding testing that incorporates perspectives of patients, their families, and important community figures. 4 The authors also promote pre‐test education that directly addresses a common belief that may hinder testing in this community: that indication of elevated dementia risk via a positive biomarker test will increase family burden. Similarly, multiple papers demonstrate that culturally informed education and counseling may enhance willingness to undergo biomarker and diagnostic testing for AD and improve post‐disclosure outcomes in Latin American populations, including the Mapuche community in Chile 5 and older adults in Colombia and Argentina. 6 Additional research from Stites and colleagues 7 highlights that participants from diverse backgrounds may differentially emphasize the potential risks of learning a biomarker result, such as psychological distress, stigma, and medicolegal discrimination. This preliminary evidence underlines the need for both individual/family counseling and community‐level education around the potential utility, benefits, risks, and limitations of testing to explain this discrepancy, implying that clinicians and researchers offering testing should engage in thorough screening of psychological health and supports, as well as advanced care and other planning prior to offering testing that is sensitive to patient or participant context, and provide pre‐test education.

Other papers in this issue extend the concept of personalized pre‐ and post‐disclosure counseling, education, and resources regarding genetic and biomarker testing to new patient populations such as those with autosomal‐dominant AD, 6 syndromic Lewy body disease (LBD), 8 , 9 or frontotemporal dementia (FTD). 10 We have seen great progress in the development and validation of AD biomarkers, as well as in understanding the implications of amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau positivity in non‐AD or mixed etiologies. The existing frameworks for disclosure, including the language used to describe results and their meaning, must be updated for these diseases: Because these results are both clinically actionable and personally meaningful, they warrant equal attention in the rapidly moving field of disclosure science. It will be critical to think about the unique needs of these populations. For instance, given the neuropsychiatric symptoms present in Lewy body dementia, additional psychological screening, monitoring, and support must be integrated into return‐of‐results protocols. 8 , 9 Similarly, Bagnati and colleagues lay out details of pre‐ and post‐test genetic counseling tailored to patients’ and families’ literacy and educational history, personal and familial risk, and mental health – ingredients that appear to assuage psychiatric distress in carriers and non‐carriers of markers for autosomal‐dominant AD. 6 Finally, Graafland and colleagues discuss considerations for onset‐predictive biomarker tests (OPBTs) for genetic FTD framed around four concepts that are broadly applicable to other conditions: actionability, respect for autonomy and informed consent, psychological impact, and social and societal impact. 10

Shifting from research to clinical implementation, blood biomarkers for AD may significantly improve diagnosis and referral if used in primary care, but ethical and practical challenges remain. Reporting data from the Netherlands and France, respectively, Claessen et al. 11 and Perrin et al. 12 add to growing evidence indicating that while primary care providers (PCPs) see value in AD blood biomarkers, they require evidence‐based, real‐world guidance to identify the appropriate clinical context for use. To support PCPs in providing high‐quality counseling and management around biomarker testing, guidelines and training are needed. 12 , 13 Further, novel assessment tools may be needed to empower patients to make decisions about therapies on the basis of biomarker testing, further contributing to patient‐centered care. 14 We will best realize the potential for such novel biomarkers to improve care only if they are integrated into approaches that support values such as patient autonomy and informed decision‐making in line with patient preferences. 13

Alongside the focus on individual care, Roth et al. point out that the wider context of resource allocation must be considered to maximize the benefits of novel biomarkers. 13 The rise of technologies such as telemedicine since the COVID‐19 pandemic may further goals such as equitable access to counseling for biomarker testing 13 ; similarly, positron emission tomography (PET) tau imaging may improve the accuracy of diagnosis and prognosis. 15 However, the potential utility of these technologies does not currently match their actual use in clinical practice. Finding a path to ethical and equitable implementation will require more evidence and broader discussions within the field of AD and related dementias to identify the most promising – and pragmatic – approaches to improve care.

Several contributions examine the validation of novel biomarkers across diverse populations. Multiple studies demonstrate growing evidence of concordance between novel blood assays (tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 [p‐tau217] 16 and p‐tau181 17 ) and Aβ PET imaging, supporting the use of blood biomarkers as a more accessible test for AD. However, performance characteristics vary by demographic factors. For instance, Hodsdon and colleagues 16 present preliminary evidence of different sensitivity and specificity in Hispanic/Latino adults, underlining the importance of not only heterogeneous validation samples but also care in clinical use with patients from backgrounds historically underrepresented in research. While multiple potential explanations are explored (e.g., disparate prevalences of Aβ pathology vs sample characteristics), there is a critical need for larger samples of sociodemographically diverse adults to contextualize findings. In one such example, Molina‐Henry and colleagues demonstrated that Hispanic Black, Hispanic White, as well as Non‐Hispanic Black and Asian participants had lower rates of clinical trial eligibility based on the plasma p‐tau217 ratio compared to non‐Hispanic White individuals; however, among plasma‐eligible participants, amyloid positivity rates did not differ by racial or ethnic identity, supporting the use of blood biomarkers as part of a two‐step screening process for trial enrollment. 18

Additional fluid biomarkers beyond those designed to measure amyloid and tau pathology show promise to detect AD and predict patient course. Data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative suggest that increases in a composite index of allostatic load that combines plasma neuroendocrine, immunological, and metabolic markers of chronic stress not only raises MCI‐to‐AD conversion odds by 15% but also correlates with other established markers of AD. 19

In parallel with the tremendous growth of fluid biomarker research in understudied populations, our understanding of the varied roles of genetic markers across diverse groups is expanding. The effect of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene, which is known to influence hippocampal volume, episodic memory, clinical syndrome, and risk for side effects of anti‐amyloid treatments, varies by self‐identified heritage. 20 Data from the Minority Aging Research Study 21 reveal evidence of different relationships between APOE translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 40 (APOE‐TOMM40)‐'523 haplotypes and white matter integrity among non‐Hispanic White and Black older adults. These studies indicate that further work is needed to understand the implications of biomarkers in different populations, and their nuanced and intentional approach of recruiting a diverse sample and incorporating racialized groups into analyses provides a roadmap for future researchers seeking to understand these complex relationships.

Authors also explore new geographic contexts of testing. Tsukie and colleagues present data demonstrating comparable performance of Aβ42/Aβ38 and Aβ42/Aβ40 in predicting Aβ PET positivity in a new population – Japanese adults. 22 Heikal and colleagues present some of the first proteomic data arising from the Egyptian Dementia Registry, demonstrating specific profiles in proteins implicated in inflammation and lipid metabolism in patients with dementia versus healthy controls. 23 These studies broaden our understanding of geographic populations rarely represented in research, illuminating areas in which further data are needed.

The range of contributions emphasizes that novel biomarker tests have great potential to mitigate the significant inequities in access to personalized diagnosis, treatment, and clinical trials, but they will require intentional efforts to expand the evidence base. These findings deepen our understanding of the responsibilities of researchers around providing counseling and support, particularly as research moves into biomarker testing of other neurodegenerative diseases, as well as testing for AD in underrepresented populations and novel contexts. Testing is not without risk, and the profile of potential benefits versus harms varies significantly based on patient characteristics, clinical stage, and environment. In addition to thoughtful counseling and shared decision‐making models, more coordinated advocacy efforts are needed to shift the legal landscape such that patients seeking testing are protected from discrimination and to address the stigma associated with AD at the community level. More work on implementation is also required, particularly focused on how biomarker‐based diagnostics and treatment can further patient‐centered, effective, and equitable care. These efforts must rest on a foundation of sound science that is inclusive of racially, ethnically, and geographically understudied populations.
