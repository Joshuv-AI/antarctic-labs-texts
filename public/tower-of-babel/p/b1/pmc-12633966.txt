Clinical course of myxedema coma in an Alzheimer’s disease patient and co-founding of J wave: A case report
Authors: Manar Shalak, Harsha Sai Sreemantula, Eze Amaechi, Seyedehferyal Mousavi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMyxedema coma is a rare condition and is associated with a high mortality rate. Management has been based on the medical experience due to its rarity. Here, we are presenting a case of a 78-year-old African American woman with Alzheimer’s disease and primary hypothyroidism, who presented to the emergency department with altered sensorium, hypotension, severe hypothermia, and bradycardia. She was diagnosed with myxedema coma and admitted to the medical intensive care unit (ICU). Despite appropriate management, including early administration of intravenous thyroxine (FT4), the patient’s hospital course was complicated. She went into multi-organ system failure, requiring multiple re-intubations due to the poor mental status. Although thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and T4 reached normal levels, there was no improvement in the patient’s mental status which could be due to multiple factors including dementia at baseline, development of delirium and functional deconditioning later in her course. Myxedema Coma seen in elderly patients with dementia poses significant morbidity and mortality. Hence, a primary care physician or a geriatrician’s role becomes crucial in maintaining proper follow-up of these patients to alleviate the above outcomes.

Introduction

Roughly around eight percent of cases of myxedema coma occur in females. Myxedema coma occurs almost exclusively in persons 60 years and older.[1] It represents the extreme clinical picture of severe hypothyroidism in that patients exhibit multiple organ abnormalities and progressive mental deterioration.[2] It may occur in patients with a background of long-standing undiagnosed hypothyroidism or may be precipitated by an underlying medical or surgical condition. Myxedema coma has a mortality rate of about 25%–60% percent.[3] It involves deterioration of mental status, in addition to hypothermia, hypotension, hyponatremia, and hypoventilation. Early disease diagnosis and treatment with thyroid hormones in the intensive care unit is vital and reduces the mortality rate.[4] Because early recognition of the disease is essential, clinicians ought to find initial tell-tale signs to identify medical emergencies. In our case, one of those signs was the presence of “J” or Osborn waves on the initial electrocardiogram.

The J wave (Osborn wave) is a positive deflection of more than one mm seen at the J point (where the QRS complex joins the ST segment) in precordial and limb leads. It is typically associated with hypothermia, but other etiologies include hypercalcemia, myocardial infarction, subarachnoid hemorrhage and severe myocarditis in addition to congenital J wave syndromes.[56] Few studies found a link between J wave and ventricular fibrillation in hypothermia and ST-elevation myocardial infarction.[67] However, no prognostic value for the J wave has been established until now.

Case presentation

Our patient is a 78-year-old African American woman with a past medical history of Alzheimer’s disease and primary hypothyroidism, who presented to the emergency department (ED) of our community hospital in the Bronx, New York with altered sensorium and decreased oral intake. Patient had been off her thyroid hormone replacement therapy for about a year. The patient moved to another city and missed her prescription refills. In the emergency department, the patient was noted to be hypothermic to 69.8 F, bradycardic to 30’s and hypotensive. Initial laboratory findings showed severe leukopenia, macrocytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and mild acute kidney injury (AKI). Urinalysis was suggestive of a urinary tract infection (UTI). An electrocardiogram showed severe bradycardia with J point elevation in V3–V6, also known as a J (Osborn) wave [Figure 1]. Patient was immediately resuscitated with intravenous fluids and started on low dose norepinephrine to maintain mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) above 65. Active external rewarming was implemented by using heating blankets. In view of worsening respiratory acidosis and persistent lethargy, the patient was intubated.

EKG with J wave clearly seen in leads V3-V6

The presumptive clinical diagnosis was acute on chronic encephalopathy secondary to severe decompensated hypothyroidism (i.e., myxedema coma) precipitated by UTI. Thyroid hormone replacement was administered with an initial dose of 300 mcg FT4 intravenously, followed by 100 mcg once daily. Considering the possibility of secondary adrenal insufficiency; Hydrocortisone 100 mg was administered every 8 hour. Thyroid function test was consistent with severe hypothyroidism. Free T4 was less than 0.1 ng/dl, T3 0.78 pg/ml and TSH 35.80 μIU/ml. On the fourth hospital day, her FT4 level improved to 0.5 ng/dl, and TSH decreased to 12.40 μIU/ml [Table 1].

Patient’s physical condition including alertness, respiratory state and circulation showed marginal improvement on the fourth day. Norepinephrine was tapered off; thyroid hormone was switched to oral form and the patient was extubated on day seven. However, despite initial recovery, the patient subsequently developed septic shock and went into multi-organ failure secondary to Clostridium difficile infection and aspiration pneumonia. Palliative care team was consulted and were following the patient throughout the stay. The patient’s hospital course was complicated by multiple re-intubations due to failed extubating trials and poor mental status. This in part was compounded by her Alzheimer’s disease, which made it difficult to assess her mental status. The patient eventually improved after a prolonged hospital stay, however she contracted the novel coronavirus (nCOV-2) during the pandemic and developed acute hypoxemic respiratory failure then eventually passed away, while in the hospital. Her code status was do not resuscitate.

Discussion

This is the case of a 78-year-old African American woman with a past medical history of primary hypothyroidism and Alzheimer’s disease who presented with severe hypothermia and altered sensorium. The patient had been off her levothyroxine medication for about a year prior to admission. Despite an early suspicion of myxedema coma and initiation of intravenous FT4, the patient had a complicated hospital course and ended up developing septic shock and multi-organ system failure.

In Myxedema coma poor prognostic factors in general include older age, requirement for mechanical ventilation, bradycardia of <44 bpm, persistent hypotension, hypothermia of <34°C, reduced level of consciousness, delayed treatment, APACHE II >20, and association with sepsis and myocardial infarction.[8] Our patient had most of these factors. Cardiovascular complications in myxedema coma may include heart failure, pericardial effusion, and bradycardia. In addition, electrocardiogram (EKG) may show AV-block, long QT-interval, T-wave changes, low voltage, and cardiac ischemia.[9] The presence of the J wave on the initial electrocardiogram was an incidental finding and a sign to reinforce the need to correct the underlying hypothermia. J wave, also referred to as an Osborn wave, is a deflection immediately following the QRS complex. When partially buried in the R wave, the J wave appears as a J point elevation or ST segment elevation. Brugada syndrome, arrhythmias associated with hypothermia and the acute phase of a ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), are possible causes.[10]

The pathophysiology of J wave, involves multiple mechanisms at the cellular level and the action potential of the myocyte, particularly phases one and two. One mechanism entails a heterogeneous potassium current moving outward across the ventricular wall that produces a voltage gradient more prominent in the epicardium reflected as J wave on the EKG. A second mechanism postulates increase in conduction delay and the presence of a prominent action potential notch caused by augmented phase one in the epicardium compared to the myocardium and endocardium. Another mechanism includes phase two action potential re-entry, leading to early repolarization abnormalities seen in ventricular arrhythmia and Brugada syndrome. Lastly, a delayed depolarization observed in inferolateral myocardial ischemia also adds to the etiology.[61112]

In our case, we think the finding of severe hypothermia correlates with the presence of the J wave. This correlation is well-known in the medical literature. Although there is no specific prognostic value of J wave in hypothermia, there is some evidence on the relation between sudden cardiac death from ventricular fibrillation and J wave especially in inferior and lateral leads.[12] Harhash et al. noted a worse prognosis in hypothermia cases with J waves. This was not the case in two other studies, which makes the evidence of association with worse prognosis mixed and inconsistent.[13141516] This observed correlation can be an early identification sign to increase alertness among treating providers. There are not many existing cases of myxedema coma and J wave in the medical literature.[17] Kaneko et al. presented a fatal case of severe myxedema coma and J wave in a 75-year-old male patient with Alzheimer’s and schizophrenia in Japan. The patient developed hypothermia, hyponatremia, hypoxemia, and hypotension and went into cardiac arrest. His EKG initially showed junctional bradycardia and J wave.[18]

To our knowledge, no case of myxedema has been reported with such severe hypothermia which was around 20°C in our patient. We believe severe hypothermia could be an independent predictor of worse outcomes in patients with myxedema coma. Prior case studies have documented higher levels of TSH (usually greater than 90 uIU/ml) associated with myxedema coma; however, our patient had a TSH of 35.80 IU/ml.[181920] Murthy et al. showed that TSH level is not a predictor of worse outcomes in patients with myxedema coma and this case supports that finding.[4]

In our case, serial assessments of the patient’s mental status were difficult due to the baseline dementia. Moreover, a high possibility that the patient’s underlying cognitive impairment may worsen during critical illness period, and we should rather expect a longer period of recovery than anticipated and or worse prognosis in dementia patients. Hence, Palliative care team was consulted and had been following the patient throughout her stay.

Conclusions

Several studies have been focused on management of patients with myxedema coma and the debate on what is the standard therapy. This case presentation highlights the challenges of management of myxedema coma in a patient with Alzheimer’s disease. While a high index of suspicion and early initiation of proper management is needed, a longer period of recovery should be anticipated in such patients and consequently a decline in their baseline cognitive status. Poor prognostic signs include older age, demand for respiratory support, hypothermia, bradycardia, sepsis, and myocardial ischemia. Classically J wave is associated with hypothermia which was found in our case. However, until now it still has no clear prognostic value, and further studies are required to establish its prognostic significance. Follow-up on timely refills of thyroid related medications is essential especially in elderly and dementia patients to avoid unnecessary adverse outcomes.
