Vascular cognitive impairment and dementia drug‐development pipeline 2025
Authors: Ying Xu, Perminder S Sachdev, Matthew J Lennon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThere are currently no approved medicines for vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID).METHODSWe reviewed clinical trials across nine registries and included, summarized, and evaluated trials that had not yet published peer‐reviewed results by August 22, 2025.RESULTSWe found 88 clinical trials using 62 drugs. Cardiovascular disease‐targeted therapies accounted for 37% (23 drugs). Synthetic and plant‐based cognitive function enhancers accounted for 21% (13 drugs) each. Drugs targeting neuropsychiatric symptoms comprised 10% of the pipeline (six agents), biological disease‐targeted therapies 5% (three agents), and other drugs 6% (four agents). There were 30 trials assessing 25 drugs in Phase 4, 24 trials assessing 21 drugs in Phase 3, 29 trials assessing 28 drugs in Phase 2, and five trials assessing four drugs in Phase 1.DISCUSSIONWhile small relative to Alzheimer's disease (138 drugs, 182 trials in 2025), the drug pipeline for VCID shows some promise and will require further investment.Keywords: vascular cognitive impairment, vascular dementia; medicines

INTRODUCTION

Vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) 1 is the second most common cause of dementia, with vascular pathology the primary cause in 20% of dementia cases and contributing in nearly 75% of cases. 2 Despite this, there has been a relative underinvestment in VCID research compared to Alzheimer's disease (AD). For instance, the United States National Institutes of Health allocated only around 10% of its annual investment in dementia research specifically to VCID. 3 , 4 Consequently, disparities in drug development between AD and VCID are evident. While a number of medicines for AD have been approved by regulatory bodies, 5 there are no approved medicines for VCID.

In AD research, the annual drug development pipeline, 6 first published in 2014, 7 has helped to map the direction, trends, gaps, and opportunities within AD treatment. The pipeline documented agents’ success rates and transition from year to year, compared aspects of drug development such as therapeutic purposes and mechanisms of action, and observed an overall growth in numbers of trials and drugs in the past decade. These annual observations have also spurred greater interest, evidenced by over 30 citations in pharmaceutical patents, 6 , 7 thousands of citations in academic articles, and extensive coverage by the digital media. 6 , 7 While published data of VCID treatment have been systematically reviewed multiple times, 8 , 9 , 10 similar work to monitor agent development has been lacking, with the exception of one publication, 11 which identified 17 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of VCID drugs that were ongoing or completed between 2012 and 2022. This work only searched the ClinicalTrials.gov website and has not been updated since. 11

Cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) is caused by vascular aging, hypertension, chronic mild ischemia, and reduced glymphatic function, with neuroimaging markers of white matter hyperintensities, dilated perivascular spaces, lacunes, and microbleeds. 12 Since the etiopathogenesis of VCID is varied, the molecules being investigated target diseases in large cerebral vessels or cSVD or mechanisms downstream such as hypoxic or hemorrhagic injury to the brain. In this paper, we present the current pipeline (i.e., ongoing and unpublished trials) of drugs for the treatment and/or prevention of VCID across the globe in nine14] national and international clinical trial registries.

METHODS

We followed the 2025 AD drug development pipeline work as a guide for our study design, data extraction and analyses, and reporting. 6 As drug regulation varies between countries and drug development for VCID has occurred across the globe, our search covered trials registered in several platforms, including international, North American, Canadian, Australian and New Zealand, European, British, African, Indian, and Chinese. Nine trial registries were searched: World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Clinical trials, ClinicalTrials.gov (USA), Health Canada Clinical Trials Data, Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, European Union Clinical Trials Register, International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) registry (UK), Pan African Clinical Trials Registry, Clinical Trial Registry of India, and Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. Search terms included cognit* OR vascular dementia (VaD) OR candidate medicines for VCID, e.g., metformin. We searched these terms under condition/disease or as other terms, as appropriate to each registry (see Table e‐1 for search terms). The index date for this review was August 22, 2025.

The key inclusion criteria were that the trials examined VCID as an outcome. Trial participants could be any of healthy volunteers, those with at‐risk VCID, vascular cognitive impairment (VCI), or any stages of VaD. At‐risk participants were those with risk factors for VCID (e.g., “at risk” from their history of hypertension, transient ischemic attack [TIA]) but with no objective cognitive impairment or imaging changes meeting VCID criteria. Healthy volunteers and individuals with at‐risk VCID were recruited for agents targeting vascular pathologies. Outcome of these trials included cognitive measures or cSVD. We also included trials of participants with mild cognitive impairment, minor neurocognitive disorder, dementia, or major neurocognitive disorder, of any cause, if VCID was one of the outcome measures.

Trials that focused solely on activity interventions (e.g., music therapy, exercise) were excluded, whereas trials involving consumables related to people's daily intake (e.g., specific dietary supplements, herbal interventions, or diet changes) were included. The prior AD drug pipelines did not include such non‐pharmaceuticals, 6 , 7 , 11 given their prominence in the prevention and management of cSVD and VCID, we include them in this report.

We contacted the study teams for trial results when trials were estimated to be completed in 2020 or earlier, when results were not yet published in peer‐reviewed journals. For trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, we included those labeled as not yet recruiting (registered but participants are not yet being enrolled), recruiting, active not recruiting (recruitment completed and continuing with intervention and/or follow‐up), and completed or unknown (if peer‐reviewed publications of trial results were unavailable). We excluded trials with published data in peer‐reviewed journals prior to the index date or registered after the index date. Trials labeled as suspended, terminated, or withdrawn were also excluded.

We included trials in Phase 1, Phase 1/2, Phase 2, Phase 2/3, Phase 3, Phase 3/4, and Phase 4. When a trial spanned two phases, we used the more advanced phase for our calculations. The phases were not recorded in registries for non‐pharmaceutical interventions or several other anomalous trials, and thus they are presented in this report but not counted in the pipeline.

Data extraction included therapeutic agents, number of participants, sex and minimum age of participants, trial registration number, region, year of trial start, prospective or retrospective registration, industry or non‐industry funded, single‐ or multicenter, trial phase, length of follow‐up, masking status, and outcome measures. Agents were grouped based on their therapeutic purpose and mechanism of action. We categorized agents under six therapeutic purposes: disease‐targeting therapies (biological and cardiovascular protection), cognitive function enhancers (synthetic and plant‐based), neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPSs), and other. Cognitive function enhancers are designed for cognitive symptom control and are mainly synthetic or plant‐based nootropics. Medicines in the NPS group are designed to reduce NPSs. In the “other” group are medicines that cannot be categorized into any of the other five groups.

Based on information presented in trial registries and the literature, we categorized the agents into 19 mechanisms of action: (1) antihyperglycemic, (2) antihypertensive, (3) antioxidation and anti‐inflammation, (4) antiplatelet, (5) choline or cholinergic, (6) glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists, (7) glutamatergic system, (8) hormone, (9) increasing arterial oxygen supply, (10) neurotransmission, (11) neurotrophic, (12) non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants, (13) phosphodiesterase (PDE) 4, (14) PDE 5, (15) glucose co‐transporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, (16) statin, (17) supplement, (18) vasodilator, and (19) unclear. When one medicine had multiple mechanisms of action, we selected the main one for this categorization.

RESULTS

Overview

After screening 3849 clinical trials, we included 88 trials assessing 62 drugs in the VCID pipeline on the index date. This included 30 trials assessing 25 drugs in Phase 4, 24 trials assessing 21 drugs in Phase 3, 29 trials assessing 28 drugs in Phase 2, and five trials assessing four drugs in Phase 1 (Figure 1, Tables e‐2 to e‐6). Therapeutic purpose and mechanisms of action

The VCID drug development pipeline comprised three agents (5% of the 62 unique agents) with biological disease‐targeted functions, 23 agents for cardiovascular disease‐targeted functions (37%), 13 (21%) that act as synthetic cognitive function enhancers, 13 (21%) that act as plant‐based cognitive function enhancers, six agents that target NPSs (10%), and four with other mechanisms (6%).

In the category of biological disease‐targeted therapies, all three agents had neurotrophic activity.

Agents for cardiovascular disease‐targeted functions included five vasodilators, four antihypertensives, three each of antihyperglycemics and antiplatelets, two each of GLP‐1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, statins, one hormone (estradiol), and one non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant.

There were four synthetic cognitive enhancers that had antioxidative and anti‐inflammatory mechanisms, four that had pro‐cholinergic functions, and one (memantine) that acted as an N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor partial antagonist. The mechanism of action was unclear for two agents, and two synthetic cognitive function enhancers were considered as nutritional supplements without specific mechanisms.

Given the diverse effects of plant‐based compounds, there is generally no strong scientific understanding of their mechanisms of action. Mostly, they are said to have a mixture of anti‐oxidative, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐thrombotic, vasodilatory, and neuroprotective activities. Thus, for the 11 plant‐based ingredient agents, the mechanism of action was classified as unclear.

In NPSs, five agents blocked neurotransmission for symptom control. The main mechanism of cannabidiol was unclear.

For the category of “other,” two agents were PDE‐4 inhibitors and one was a PDE‐5 inhibitor. One agent was thought to function by increasing arterial oxygen supply.

Median planned numbers of participants were 60 and 200, respectively, in trials of biological and cardiovascular disease‐targeted therapies; 240 and 200, respectively, in trials of plant‐based and synthetic cognitive function enhancers; 80 in trials of agents for NPSs; and 80 in agents for “other” therapeutic purpose (Table e‐6). Over two‐thirds of the trials of biological disease‐targeted therapies and NPS agents planned to recruit or recruited fewer than 100 participants (Figure 2). Planned follow‐up duration was not reported in four (19%) trials of plant‐based cognitive function enhancers. All trials were prospectively registered, except for three trials studying plant‐based cognitive function enhancers. There was no industry investment added to trials of biological or cardiovascular disease‐targeted therapies. In each of the plant‐based and synthetic cognitive enhancers, NPSs, and “other” groups, over two‐thirds of the trials began in or before 2020.

Participants

A total of 30,840 participants were recruited or planned to be recruited across all phases of trials: Phase 4, n = 9817, 31.8%; Phase 3, n = 15154, 49.1%; Phase 2, n = 5655, 18.3%; and Phase 1, n = 214, 0.7%. The numbers of participants were not reported in two Phase 3 trial registrations. All trials planned to recruit or recruited adult participants only. In six trials, the minimum participant age was set at 65 years. All but one trial (NCT03681691 female participants only) recruited participants of both sexes.

Based on six therapeutic categories, trials examining agents for cardiovascular disease‐targeted therapies had recruited or were aiming to recruit 14,495 participants (47.0%), those examining plant‐based cognitive function enhancers aimed to recruit 9214 participants (29.9%), those examining synthetic cognitive function enhancers aimed to recruit 5464 participants (17.7%), those examining other aimed for 655 participants (2.1%), those investigating NPSs planned to recruit 563 (1.8%), and those studying biological disease‐targeted therapies planned to recruit 449 participants (1.5%).

Geographic distribution

Phase 4 trials were conducted primarily in China (13 trials), Korea (four trials), and Australia (three trials). Each of the remaining 10 Phase 4 trials was conducted in the following regions: Brazil, France, Israel, Italy, Philippines, Romania, Russia, Singapore, UK, and USA (Figure 4).

Initiations of clinical trials for treatment of VCID. (1) Australia, Brazil, Georgia, Malaysia, Netherlands, Nigeria, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Switzerland, Taiwan, United Kingdom, Vietnam. (2) Denmark, Germany, Norway, Sweden, Switzerland.

Seven Phase 3 trials were conducted in China, two in Russia, and two in the USA. Two Phase 3 trials had recruitment sites located in multiple countries. Each of the remaining seven Phase 3 trials was conducted in following countries: Australia, Brazil, Canada, Ghana, Iran, Mexico, and the UK. The study locations were unclear in four Phase 3 trials.

There were nine Phase 2 trials in the United States, seven in China, two in each of Australia, Canada, Israel, and the UK. Each of the remaining Phase 2 trials was conducted in Hong Kong, Korea, Netherlands, Singapore, and Korea.

Four Phase 1 trials were conducted in the USA and one in China. Figure e‐2 shows the trial characteristics by region.

Year of trial initiation

The most recent year of trial initiation was 2025, whereas the earliest years of trial initiation were 2004 (Phase 4), 1995 (Phase 3), 2002 (Phase 2), and 2007 (Phase 1) (Figure 4). There are 20 Phase 4 trials (67% of all Phase 4 trials), 16 Phase 3 trials (67%), 12 Phase 2 trials (41%), and four Phase 1 trials (80%) starting in or before 2020. Yet, based on a literature search and communications with study teams, we found that none of these trials has published results in any peer‐reviewed academic journals.

DISCUSSION

We identified 62 agents in 88 trials and described regional and global trends in drug development. In the current pipeline, there are small numbers of Phase 1 trials and few novel agents studied or biological drugs that target disease mechanisms. Our inclusion criteria were broader than the previously published work on VCID, 11 as well as similar work for AD, 6 as we sought other clinical trials sources than the Clinicaltrials.gov. This international perspective was crucial, as much of the VCID treatment research is being performed in China (one‐third of all trials). Further, we included Phase 4 trials and all currently registered trials, whether recruiting or continuing with intervention or follow‐up, as well as completed trials if results are unavailable in peer‐reviewed publications. Nonetheless, the pipeline for VCID is smaller compared to 138 drugs being assessed in 182 clinical trials in the AD pipeline in 2025. 6

Compared to the limited number of Phase 1 trials in the current work, the AD pipeline featured far more and observed nearly a doubling of numbers of Phase 1 drugs and trials in 2025 (45 drugs in 48 trials) compared to 2024 (25 drugs in 26 trials). 6 While drug repurposing remains essential, 13 investment should also be directed to novel agents and preclinical models, like those put into AD and frontotemporal degeneration. 6 , 14 Over two‐thirds of the trials in the pipeline started in or before 2020, with no data published yet. While delays in the publication of trial results are not uncommon in other fields as well, the problem is particularly acute in the field of dementia, with only a small percentage moving through the phases and on to regulatory approval, in part because of negative trial results. For example, out of 244 agents for AD tested between 2002 and 2012, only one, memantine, was approved (success rate 0.4%). 7 Finally, many individuals with dementia have mixed AD and VCID pathologies. 2 While we followed the methodology of 2025 AD drug pipeline, with comparisons made in terms of number of agents and trials in different phases and overall, drug developments for these two are indeed interconnected. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy and amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities would affect the safety and efficacy of anti‐amyloid agents. 15 Further developments of these pipelines and agents are likely to facilitate dual or multiple strategies that simultaneously address mixed pathologies.

The identified agents had a diverse range of therapeutic purposes and mechanisms of action. In the following section we explore the extant evidence for some of the agents (based non‐systematic review of the literature) and the rationale for their therapeutic approaches.

Biological disease‐targeted therapies

Cerebrolysin, a pig‐derived combination of neurotrophic factors, had a small statistically meaningful but negligible clinically meaningful effects in improving cognition in VCID 8 and has been approved for post‐stroke recovery in a range of jurisdictions (e.g., Austria, Germany, China, South Korea, and Russia).

Cardiovascular disease‐targeted therapies

Lowering blood pressure may reduce the risk of dementia. 16 , 17 , 18 There are many systematic reviews on the antidementia properties of anticoagulants, but there is little evidence on non‐vitamin K antagonist anticoagulants alone. 19 A Cochrane review of antithrombotic therapy to prevent cognitive decline in those with cSVD without dementia included three studies (3384 participants) with antiplatelet agents but none with anticoagulants. 19 , 20 It reported no cognitive benefits and an increased bleeding risk with antiplatelets in people with cSVD. 20 Prior evidence on statins in VCID was not promising. 21 Two RCTs of simvastatin and pravastatin in people at risk of vascular disease suggested neither agent prevented cognitive decline or dementia. 22 , 23 Insulin had no significant effects on the risk of cardiovascular outcomes 24 and was associated with an increased risk of dementia in individuals with type 2 diabetes. 25

Pentoxifylline was effective in improving cognition in two VaD RCTs. 8 Nicergoline has shown cognitive protective effects in people with mild to moderate dementia, including VCID. 26 Metformin, GLP‐1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors are associated with reduced risk of dementia in individuals with type 2 diabetes. 25 Although their cognitive protective effects through non‐vascular pathways in VCID are uncertain, 27 in the EVOKE (AD patients) and EVOKE+ (AD patients with vascular pathology) trials, semaglutide reduced AD biomarkers. 28 Phase 2 and Phase 2 to 3 RCTs have trialed cilostazol with and without isosorbide mononitrate in participants with symptomatic lacunar ischemic stroke. 29 , 30 Participants on isosorbide mononitrate had greater improvements in points achieved on the Trails A test and reduced incidence of cognitive impairment from baseline to follow‐up. 29 , 30 Improvement in cognition was, however, not superior in the cilostazol arm, 30 which agrees with two early trials using cilostazol alone, 31 although greater reduction in white matter hyperintensities was observed. 30

For less commonly used cardiovascular medicines, one systematic review reported that two out of three animal studies using angiotensin‐(1–7), a hormone that acts as a counterbalance to angiotensin II, found improved memory performance. 32 Estrogen is known for its cardiovascular protective effects, and higher postmenopausal estrogen levels are related to reduced risk of dementia in observational studies, 33 but there are no trial results so far.

Synthetic cognitive function enhancers

Three trials involving 496 participants with acute ischemic stroke and 10 trials involving 768 participants with acute intracerebral hemorrhage did not recommend using Edaravone for the management of acute cerebrovascular events. 34 , 35 Ergoloid mesylates did not improve cognitive function in three earlier trials. 36 Donepezil did not change the clinician's and carer's interview‐based impression of change scales compared to placebo, 37 although individuals with VCID on donepezil, galantamine, or memantine showed greater improvement on Vascular–Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale cognitive subscales compared to those on placebo. 8 , 37 Choline alphoscerate alone or in combination with donepezil improved cognition among patients with cSVD, AD, or dementia. 38

Plant‐based cognitive function enhancers

Plant‐based ingredients, such as the Indian herb Bacopa monniera (brahni) and several Chinese herbal medicines, may modulate neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, neurogenesis, or neuroplasticity and have been trialed for various neurodegenerative diseases. 36 , 39 Yet the mechanism pathways and active compounds are not fully elucidated, and aside from a few retrospective registrations we identified, many published trials were also single arm or had small sample sizes or short durations. 36 , 39 Plant‐based trials appear to be common in certain regions, possibly due to health‐system traditions, accessibility, regulatory pathways, and local funding patterns.

Neuropsychiatric symptoms and other

Evidence is generally lacking for these two groups of medicines used in individuals with VCID. A trial of a PDE‐5 inhibitor, tadalafil 20 mg, reported no difference in changes of subcortical cerebral blood flow in 55 individuals with symptomatic cSVD in a crossover Phase 2 RCT. 40

This is the first study to review the current status of drug development for VCID on a global scale. A few limitations need to be acknowledged. First, there is a limitation in recording trial phases in registries. Phases of clinical trials depend on the trials’ purpose (safety or efficacy) and sample size and on whether medicines are already approved by regulatory bodies for other purposes. Investigators define the phase of their trial based on the study design, and thus, the decision may vary among investigators for the same study design. Sometimes the phase classification was missing in the registries (Table e‐7). Additionally, standard data fields vary between registries, with limited number of mandatory items. Mandatory specification of compound details will provide transparency and is especially important for plant‐based agents, as many of them are multi‐compound and not widely used. Second, we followed and made comparisons to the AD pipeline work; however, we note the limitations of cross‐disease pipeline comparisons. Third, although our search is thorough, our work did not include procedures to treat VCID (e.g., remote ischemic pre‐conditioning 41 ), and activities (e.g. various exercise programs) that may be critical in VCID prevention and management. Finally, only one person conducted the search, screening, and data extraction, although the categorizations of each agent by therapeutic purposes and mechanisms of action were checked for appropriateness by a second reviewer.

CONCLUSION

The global VCID drug pipeline, while modest compared to AD (62 drugs 88 trials vs 138 drugs 182 trials 6 ), shows diverse therapeutic approaches. Significant investment in novel agents and the timely publication of all trial results are critical factors in advancing treatments for this major cause of dementia.
