Childhood trauma is associated with mobile phone dependency through self esteem and depressive symptoms in adolescents
Authors: Qin Zhang, Xinbo Sun, Mingyue Gao, Li Wang, Chengqi Cao, Binbin Wang, Hejia Yan, Kunlin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the association between childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency among adolescents, as well as the mediating roles of self-esteem and depressive symptoms in this association. A questionnaire survey was conducted among 3,866 middle school students from Guizhou Province, using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF), the Self-Esteem Scale (SES), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and the Mobile Phone Addiction Index (MPAI). The results showed: (1) Childhood trauma positively predicted mobile phone dependency in adolescents (β = 0.55, p < 0.001); (2) Self-esteem (β = 0.05, p < 0.001) and depressive symptoms (β = 0.17, p < 0.001) each played a partial mediating role in the relationship between childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency, accounting for 9.10% and 30.91% of the total effect, respectively; (3) Childhood trauma also indirectly influenced mobile phone dependency through a chain-mediating effect composed of self-esteem and depressive symptoms (β = 0.15, p < 0.001), with an indirect impact accounting for 27.27% of the total. Childhood trauma is not only a significant external factor influencing mobile phone dependency among adolescents but also exerts an indirect effect on such dependency by impacting adolescents’ self-esteem and depressive symptoms levels.Keywords: Childhood trauma, Mobile phone dependency, Self-esteem, Depressive symptoms, Adolescents

Introduction

Adolescents have become a central user group in the development of the mobile Internet. According to the latest survey results released by the China Internet Network Information Center [9], young users account for a substantial proportion of new Internet users in China. With the widespread availability of smartphones, mobile devices have become deeply embedded in adolescents’ social communication, information seeking, entertainment, and emotion regulation. However, because adolescence is a developmental period marked by relatively immature self-regulation and heightened sensitivity to social rewards, adolescents may be especially vulnerable to excessive or dependent use of mobile phones [22, 69]. Mobile phone dependency has been associated with poor sleep quality, academic difficulties, emotional problems, and risk behaviors such as smoking, drinking, and suicidal behaviors [11, 18, 51]. Therefore, identifying the developmental risk factors and psychological mechanisms underlying adolescent mobile phone dependency has important theoretical and practical significance.

Beyond the prevalence and consequences of mobile phone dependency, it is important to identify why some adolescents are more vulnerable than others. Childhood trauma is a major developmental risk factor for later maladaptive emotional and behavioral outcomes [25, 34]. It generally refers to adverse experiences before the age of 18, such as emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect, that may threaten an individual’s psychological and physical development [3]. Prior studies have shown that childhood trauma is related to Internet addiction and other behavioral addictions among adolescents [23, 57]. More directly, childhood maltreatment has also been linked to mobile phone dependency or problematic mobile phone use among Chinese adolescents [36, 56, 57]. These findings suggest that early traumatic experiences may increase adolescents’ tendency to use mobile phones as a compensatory or avoidant coping tool.

The association between childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency should not be understood merely as an extension of Internet addiction research. Although mobile phone dependency and Internet addiction share features such as impaired control and excessive use, mobile phones have unique characteristics, including portability, constant availability, instant social feedback, and continuous access to entertainment and social media [12]. These characteristics may make mobile phones particularly attractive to adolescents who lack emotional security or supportive offline relationships. Thus, the key question is no longer simply whether childhood trauma is associated with mobile phone dependency, but how early traumatic experiences are translated into later dependency behaviors during adolescence.

One possible mechanism involves the self-system. From a developmental systems perspective, a coherent and positive self-system is an important protective factor for psychological adaptation [50]. Childhood trauma may disrupt the development of the self-system by undermining adolescents’ basic psychological needs for belongingness, safety, and competence [49]. Self-esteem, as a core component of the self-system, reflects an individual’s overall evaluation of self-worth and is shaped by repeated interpersonal feedback from family members, peers, and other significant others [28]. Adolescents who experience abuse or neglect may internalize negative interpersonal experiences, leading to lower self-esteem [32, 42] .

Low self-esteem may further increase the likelihood of mobile phone dependency. When adolescents feel inadequate or unsupported in offline contexts, they may seek immediate feedback, social reassurance, or emotional support through mobile phone use. Mobile phones provide rapid access to social media, games, and online interactions, which may temporarily compensate for unmet psychological needs [6, 31]. However, repeated reliance on such compensatory feedback may strengthen habitual and dependent use. Empirical studies have shown that low self-esteem is associated with mobile phone dependency [62], and self-esteem has been found to mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency in college students [67]. Whether this pathway also operates among adolescents remains important to examine, because adolescence is a critical period for identity formation, self-evaluation, and the development of self-regulatory capacities [14, 55].

A second pathway may operate through depressive symptoms. Childhood trauma is a well-established risk factor for depressive symptoms in adolescence [61, 63, 65]. Early traumatic experiences may impair secure attachment, threat processing, and emotion regulation systems, thereby increasing vulnerability to persistent negative affect and depressive symptomatology [15, 59]. Adolescents with depressive symptoms may experience low mood, anhedonia, avoidance, and a stronger tendency to seek immediate relief from distress. In this context, mobile phones may function as easily accessible tools for distraction, emotional escape, and social compensation [8, 26, 30]. Although such use may provide short-term emotional relief, it may also increase the risk of mobile phone dependency over time [37].

Self-esteem and depressive symptoms may also operate sequentially rather than independently. The vulnerability model proposes that low self-esteem is a risk factor for later depressive symptoms, whereas the scar model proposes that depressive symptoms may erode self-esteem [41, 52]. Although the relationship can be bidirectional, longitudinal studies and meta-analytic evidence suggest that low self-esteem more strongly predicts subsequent depressive symptoms than the reverse pathway [40, 53, 54]. Recent longitudinal evidence from Chinese samples also supports the predictive role of self-esteem in later depressive symptoms [35]. Therefore, in the present study, self-esteem was specified before depressive symptoms in the serial mediation model.

Despite growing evidence linking childhood trauma to mobile phone dependency, several gaps remain. First, previous studies have focused more on direct associations than on the psychological pathways underlying this association. Second, although self-esteem and depressive symptoms have each been linked to childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency, few studies have examined their independent and sequential mediating roles within the same model. Third, adolescence is characterized by heightened neuroplasticity, identity formation, immature self-regulation, and increased sensitivity to social feedback, making adolescents particularly vulnerable to using mobile phones as a compensatory coping tool [7, 38]. To address these gaps, the present study tested a serial mediation model in which childhood trauma was associated with adolescent mobile phone dependency through self-esteem and depressive symptoms (as shown in Fig. 1).

Theoretical hypotheses of the serial mediation model

Based on the above theoretical and empirical evidence, the present study proposed the following hypotheses:

H1: Childhood trauma is positively associated with adolescent mobile phone dependency.

H1: Childhood trauma is positively associated with adolescent mobile phone dependency.

H2: Self-esteem mediates the relationship between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency.

H2: Self-esteem mediates the relationship between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency.

H3: Depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency.

H3: Depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency.

H4: Self-esteem and depressive symptoms serially mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency.

Methods

Study participants

A school-based convenience sampling strategy was used. Three middle schools in Guizhou Province were selected based on accessibility and willingness to participate. A total of 4,000 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding invalid questionnaires with excessive missing responses or patterned responses, 3,866 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 96.65%. The final sample included 1,971 boys (50.98%) and 1,895 girls (49.02%). The participants ranged in age from 14 to 18 years (M = 15.62, SD = 0.68). Regarding only-child status, 1,696 participants (43.87%) were only children and 2,170 participants (56.13%) had siblings. Procedure

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Approval No. H24005), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before data collection, permission was obtained from school administrators and homeroom teachers, and informed consent was obtained from guardians and participating students. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Students were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty if they felt uncomfortable during the survey. Mental health education teachers assisted only with classroom organization and questionnaire distribution; they did not inspect individual responses. Completed questionnaires were collected on site. Students who completed the survey received a notebook as compensation.

Measures

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form

Childhood trauma was assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF) [5]. The Chinese version of the CTQ-SF has been validated in Chinese populations [21, 71] and has been widely used in large-scale studies of Chinese adolescents [66]. In the present study, the total CTQ-SF score was used to represent overall childhood trauma exposure because the study focused on cumulative trauma burden rather than subtype-specific effects. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total score was 0.67. Although this value is slightly below the conventional 0.70 threshold, alpha should be interpreted in relation to scale dimensionality and research purpose; for a multidimensional measure of heterogeneous trauma experiences, a marginal alpha may still be acceptable for group-level analyses when results are interpreted cautiously [58]. This issue is further addressed in the Limitations section.

Self-esteem scale

Self-esteem was assessed using the Self-Esteem Scale(SES) [48]. The scale contains 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 10 to 40. Higher scores indicate higher levels of self-esteem. The Chinese version of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale has been widely used in Chinese samples and has shown acceptable psychometric properties [43]. In the present study, Items 3, 5, 9, and 10 were reverse-scored before the total score was calculated. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in this sample was 0.90.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [33]. The PHQ-9 consists of nine scored items assessing depressive symptom severity during the past two weeks. Each item is rated from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 has shown good reliability and validity for school-based depression screening among Chinese adolescents [60]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.89. Because the PHQ-9 is a self-report symptom measure rather than a structured clinical interview, PHQ-9 scores were interpreted as depressive symptom severity rather than clinical diagnoses of depressive disorder.

Mobile phone addiction index

Mobile phone dependency was assessed using the Chinese version of the Mobile Phone Addiction Index (MPAI), which has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese student samples [24]. The MPAI contains 17 items across four dimensions: loss of control, withdrawal, escape, and inefficiency. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 17 to 85. Higher total scores indicate greater mobile phone dependency. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.92.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and the PROCESS macro version 3.3. Questionnaires with excessive missing responses or patterned responses were excluded, and scale scores were computed according to each instrument’s scoring instructions. Descriptive statistics, reliability analyses, and Harman’s single-factor test for common method bias were first conducted. The distribution of the main study variables was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values, and Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine bivariate associations. The serial mediation model was tested using PROCESS Model 6 with 5,000 bootstrap samples, with childhood trauma as the independent variable, mobile phone dependency as the dependent variable, and self-esteem and depressive symptoms as sequential mediators. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% bootstrap confidence intervals did not include zero [20, 45]. Age, gender, and only-child status were included as covariates because these were the demographic variables collected in the original survey. The order of mediators was specified based on the vulnerability model and longitudinal evidence suggesting that low self-esteem predicts later depressive symptoms. For correlation and regression analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Common method deviation control and inspection

Because all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. The results showed that 11 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 23.04% of the total variance, which was below the commonly used 40% threshold [44]. This indicates that no significant common method bias was present in the study data.

Discussion

Childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency

The present study found that childhood trauma was positively associated with adolescent mobile phone dependency, suggesting that adolescents with higher levels of early traumatic experiences were more likely to report dependency-related mobile phone use. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that childhood maltreatment and adverse family experiences are associated with problematic mobile phone use and other dependency-related behaviors among adolescents [57]. Rather than treating mobile phone dependency as a purely self-control problem, the present finding suggests that it may also reflect adolescents’ attempt to cope with unresolved emotional and relational difficulties originating from earlier adverse experiences.

From an ecological systems perspective, the family is a core microsystem in adolescent development [46]. Traumatic experiences within this microsystem may weaken adolescents’ perceived security, disrupt attachment-related expectations, and impair their ability to regulate distress in interpersonal contexts. Under such conditions, mobile phones may provide an easily accessible source of distraction, emotional relief, and social connection. For adolescents who experience insufficient support in offline environments, mobile phones may gradually function as a “digital shelter” that temporarily reduces distress or compensates for unmet relational needs. However, repeated reliance on this form of digital compensation may increase the likelihood of dependency, particularly during adolescence, a period marked by heightened sensitivity to social feedback and still-developing self-regulatory capacities.

This finding has practical implications for prevention and intervention. Interventions targeting adolescent mobile phone dependency should not focus only on limiting screen time or strengthening behavioral control. For adolescents with trauma-related experiences, assessment and intervention should also consider family functioning, emotional security, attachment quality, and adaptive emotion regulation. School-based mental health services may benefit from screening for early adverse experiences and providing trauma-informed support, so that mobile phone dependency can be addressed not merely as a behavioral symptom but as a possible manifestation of broader developmental and emotional difficulties.

The mediating role of self-esteem

The present study found that self-esteem partially mediated the association between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency. In other words, adolescents with higher levels of childhood trauma tended to report lower self-esteem, which was further associated with greater mobile phone dependency. This finding suggests that the impact of childhood trauma on mobile phone dependency may be partly explained by changes in adolescents’ self-evaluation. Self-esteem is an important psychological resource that shapes how individuals cope with stress and regulate their behavior [4, 39]. For adolescents who have experienced abuse, neglect, or other adverse family experiences, a weakened sense of self-worth may make them more likely to seek affirmation, emotional comfort, or interpersonal feedback through mobile phones. In this sense, mobile phone use may temporarily compensate for unmet psychological needs in offline life, but repeated reliance on such compensation may gradually increase dependency. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence that self-esteem is involved in the relationship between attachment-related vulnerability and problematic smartphone use [29].

This mechanism may be especially important during adolescence. Adolescence is a period of rapid psychological and neural development, during which behavioral habits are still being formed and may be more easily reinforced [10, 16]. Therefore, if adolescents repeatedly use mobile phones to obtain reassurance, social feedback, or emotional relief, this pattern may become increasingly stable over time. These findings suggest that interventions for adolescents with trauma-related experiences should not only focus on reducing mobile phone use itself, but should also help adolescents rebuild self-worth, develop healthier interpersonal connections, and obtain emotional support from offline environments.

The mediating role of depressive symptoms

The present study found that depressive symptoms mediated the association between childhood trauma and adolescent mobile phone dependency. Specifically, adolescents reporting higher levels of childhood trauma tended to report more severe depressive symptoms, which in turn were associated with higher levels of mobile phone dependency. This finding is consistent with evidence that childhood trauma is a robust risk factor for depressive symptoms in adolescence and later life [19, 64]. From a developmental psychopathology perspective, early traumatic experiences may disrupt adolescents’ attachment security, stress-response systems, and emotion-regulation capacities, thereby increasing vulnerability to persistent negative affect and depressive symptoms [17, 70]. In this sense, depressive symptoms may represent a psychological pathway through which early adverse experiences are translated into later maladaptive behavioral patterns.

The association between depressive symptoms and mobile phone dependency can be understood from the perspective of compensatory and avoidant coping. Adolescents with depressive symptoms often experience negative mood, anhedonia, social withdrawal, and reduced motivation [2]. Under these conditions, mobile phones may provide immediate access to distraction, entertainment, social contact, and temporary emotional relief. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies suggesting that adolescents exposed to neglect or psychological abuse may use mobile phones to regulate negative emotions, and that maladaptive coping styles are associated with greater risk of problematic mobile phone use [56]. Similarly, studies have shown that depressive and anxiety symptoms are closely related to more severe problematic smartphone use, partly because mobile phone use may serve as an easily accessible strategy for escaping distress or compensating for unmet emotional needs [1, 13]. However, when mobile phone use becomes the primary or habitual strategy for relieving negative emotions, it may reinforce avoidance rather than promote adaptive emotion regulation, thereby increasing the risk of dependency. Given the cross-sectional design of the present study, this pathway should be interpreted as a theoretically informed mediation model rather than evidence of causal temporal ordering.

The serial mediating role of self-esteem and depressive symptoms

The present study further identified a significant serial mediation pathway from childhood trauma to mobile phone dependency through self-esteem and depressive symptoms. This finding suggests that the association between childhood trauma and mobile phone dependency may involve a progressive psychological process rather than a single isolated mechanism. Childhood trauma may first weaken adolescents’ self-evaluation by undermining their sense of security, acceptance, and self-worth [42, 47]. Lower self-esteem may then increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms, which is consistent with the vulnerability model and longitudinal evidence showing that low self-esteem prospectively predicts later depressive symptoms [27, 53]. Depressive symptoms may subsequently become a more proximal psychological driver of mobile phone dependency, as adolescents experiencing persistent negative affect may rely on mobile phones for distraction, emotional relief, and social compensation [37, 68].

Notably, the indirect effect through depressive symptoms and the serial pathway were both larger than the indirect effect through self-esteem alone. This pattern suggests that self-esteem may function more as an upstream vulnerability factor, whereas depressive symptoms may represent a more proximal affective pathway linking early trauma-related vulnerability to dependency-related mobile phone use. Therefore, interventions for adolescents exposed to childhood trauma should not focus on a single psychological factor. Instead, prevention efforts may be more effective when they simultaneously address self-worth, depressive symptoms, emotion regulation, and healthier alternatives for obtaining social support and emotional relief.

In addition, the direct effect of childhood trauma on mobile phone dependency remained significant after including self-esteem and depressive symptoms, indicating partial mediation. This suggests that the serial pathway identified in this study is meaningful but not exhaustive. Other mechanisms, such as parenting style, peer relationships, loneliness, impulsivity, perceived social support, and maladaptive emotion-regulation strategies, may also help explain how childhood trauma is associated with mobile phone dependency.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of this study prevents causal inference. Although the proposed serial mediation model was grounded in theory and prior longitudinal evidence, the temporal ordering among childhood trauma, self-esteem, depressive symptoms, and mobile phone dependency should be further examined using longitudinal designs. Second, all variables were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias, social desirability bias, and common method bias. Although Harman’s single-factor test suggested that common method bias was not serious, future studies should incorporate multi-informant, clinical, or behavioral measures where possible.

Third, childhood trauma was assessed retrospectively, and the CTQ-SF showed marginal internal consistency in the present sample. Therefore, findings involving childhood trauma should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9 rather than structured clinical interviews, meaning that the present findings reflect symptom severity rather than clinical diagnoses. Finally, the sample was drawn from three middle schools in Guizhou Province using school-level convenience sampling, and only age, gender, and only-child status were available as demographic covariates. Other potentially relevant family and social factors, such as parental education, household income, living arrangement, parental mental health history, parenting style, and peer relationships, were not measured. Future studies should include more diverse samples and more comprehensive background variables to further test the robustness of the proposed model.

Conclusion

This study found that childhood trauma was positively associated with adolescent mobile phone dependency and that self-esteem and depressive symptoms played significant mediating roles in this association. Specifically, childhood trauma was related to mobile phone dependency through three indirect pathways: the independent mediating pathway of self-esteem, the independent mediating pathway of depressive symptoms, and the serial mediating pathway of self-esteem and depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that adolescent mobile phone dependency should be understood not only as a behavioral problem, but also as a developmental and psychological outcome associated with early adverse experiences, self-evaluation, and depressive symptom severity.
