Preptin, PPARγ, and NLRP3 as inflammatory biomarkers in pediatric obesity – a pilot study
Authors: Ezgi Kürkçü Kahraman, Orkide Donma, Mustafa Metin Donma, Ahsen Yılmaz, Savaş Güzel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood obesity leads to an increased inflammatory response, which in the long term can lead to serious health problems such as metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cancer and cardiovascular disease, and may also increase the risk of obesity in adulthood. Our aim was to evaluate inflammatory markers associated with pediatric obesity in 30 healthy children (BMI between the 15th–85th percentiles) and 30 obese children (BMI > 95th percentile) and to determine the possible associations of these markers.MethodsAnthropometric measurements of the children were obtained. The serum preptin, peroxisome proliferator activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), nod-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3), and interleukin-18 (IL-18) levels of thirty children with obesity and thirty healthy children were determined using ELISA.ResultsNLPR3, preptin, and PPARγ levels were greater obese children than in healthy children (p < 0.05). We have shown that high PPARγ levels may have an inhibitory effect on the inflammatory activation of NLRP3.ConclusionsIn our study, the changes observed in preptin, NLRP3 and PPARγ parameters were found to be closely associated with pediatric obesity. These molecules may play an important role in understanding the relationship between pediatric obesity and inflammation and may be used as biomarkers in determining obesity treatment and complications in future studies.Keywords: Child, Obesity, Preptin, NLRP3, PPARγ

Introduction

The increase in the prevalence of obesity has become a major health problem worldwide in children and adolescents as well as adults [1]. Childhood obesity creates the basis for adult obesity and is an important health problem, especially because of its association with morbid obesity, diabetes mellitus, cancer, and cardiovascular diseases [2]. Childhood obesity is no longer seen as just a matter of excess weight; it is now widely recognized as a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation. As fat accumulates in the body, adipose tissue transforms from a passive energy store into an active endocrine organ. It begins to secrete a range of pro-inflammatory molecules, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-18 (IL-18). These cytokines circulate throughout the body, fueling systemic inflammation and disrupting normal metabolic processes. Over time, this inflammatory environment contributes to insulin resistance and increases the risk of serious health conditions such as type 2 diabetes and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, even in children [3].

Preptin is a derivative of proinsulin growth factor II (pro-IGF-II) that is secreted by pancreatic β cells and is considered to increase insulin secretion and regulate glucose-mediated insulin secretion in addition to lipid, protein, and carbohydrate metabolism. Preptin is a hormone that regulates energy expenditure [4, 5]. Increased serum preptin levels have been observed in overweight individuals and adults with obesity [6]. Preptin may be effective in the mechanism of inflammation in obesity, and this may trigger oxidative stress by increasing inflammatory cytokines in obese patients [7]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is a transcription factor found in adipose tissue that plays a role in the differentiation and function of adipocytes [8, 9]. Increased free fatty acid levels and lipid accumulation in nonadipose tissues are effective in the development of insulin resistance. This is associated with PPARγ ligands that regulate the expression of adipocyte-derived hormones that affect fatty acid storage and glucose homeostasis [10]. PPARγ affects feeding behavior, energy balance and leptin sensitivity [11]. The inflammasome associated with type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, and obesity is Nod-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) [12]. This parameter triggers the release of IL-1β and IL-18, which are proinflammatory cytokines involved in the innate immune response. NLRP3 activation is associated with systemic chronic low-level inflammation and the regulation of energy homeostasis, which are the main features of obesity and insulin resistance [13–15]. Cytokine and NLRP3 levels are elevated in children with obesity and adults. It has been shown that adipose tissue affects the secretion of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in obese individuals, and high levels of IL-18, a proinflammatory cytokine, are effective in the development of type 2 diabetes [16].

The main aim of this study was to evaluate the parameters contributing to inflammation and the relationships between them in healthy children with normal body mass index (BMI) and children with obesity to determine the parameters that promote obesity.

Materials and methods

Laboratory studies

Children between the ages of 6 and 18 years without acute or chronic diseases and without major congenital anomalies were included in the study. Peripheral blood samples were collected from children admitted to Tekirdağ Namık Kemal University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Pediatrics after 8–12 h of fasting. Two groups were formed from 30 children with obesity BMI values above the 95th percentile according to the WHO criteria accepted by the Ministry of Health and 30 children with healthy BMI values between the 15th–85th percentiles who constituted the control group. A total of 60 children were enrolled in the study, comprising 35 girls and 25 boys. Among them, 21 girls and 9 boys were assigned to the control group, while the obese group included 14 girls and 16 boys. All participants were free from acute or chronic illnesses and major congenital anomalies.

Exclusion criteria included: age over 18 years, secondary obesity-related conditions (e.g., hypothyroidism, polycystic ovary syndrome), history of bariatric surgery or repeated dieting, and presence of serious acute or chronic diseases (e.g., infections, cardiovascular, renal, pulmonary diseases, or malignancy).

Before the study, Ethics Committee approval documents numbered 2020.01.01.01 were obtained from Tekirdağ Namık Kemal University where the study was conducted. The families of the children, especially their parents, were informed. After a detailed pediatric physical examination and informing them about their inclusion in the study, signed consent forms from the consenting parents were obtained, and the children were informed verbally. Peripheral blood samples obtained from the children were collected, centrifuged, and stored in the biochemistry laboratory in deep freezers at −80 °C.

The weights, heights, and waist, hip, head, and neck circumferences (C) of all the children were measured and recorded. BMI, waist/hip ratios, and other ratios and formulas related to obesity were calculated from anthropometric measurements, and statistical analyses of the anthropometric measurements of controls and children with obesity were performed.

NLRP3 (Cloud-Crone Corp.), preptin (Elabscience), PPARγ (Elabscience) and IL-18 (Elabscience) were analyzed by the sandwich ELISA method in the control and obese groups. The kits used in laboratory studies, CV (%) and measurement ranges are shown in Table 1.

Statistical evaluation

In the statistical evaluation of the data, parametric or nonparametric statistical tests appropriate for the distribution characteristics of the data obtained were applied. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to determine whether the values of the parameters conformed to a normal distribution. Independent samples t tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to evaluate the differences between the groups, and Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine whether the values of the parameters changed together. Additionally, logistic regression analysis was performed to identify predictors, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine optimal cut-off values. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) Version 26. The means, standard deviations, standard errors, and medians were compared between the control and obese groups. The statistically significant difference between them was evaluated according to whether the values showed a parametric or nonparametric distribution. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Body mass index values were 17.3 ± 2.8 kg/m2 (mean ± SD) and 26.5 ± 3.7 kg/m2 (mean ± SD) for the control and obese groups, respectively. We also evaluated the performance of the biomarkers NLPR3, Preptin, PPARγ, and IL-18 in distinguishing obese individuals using ROC analysis. The AUC values for NLPR3, Preptin, and PPARγ were 0.678, 0.662, and 0.690, respectively, and all were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). These results indicate that these biomarkers have a moderate discriminative power in identifying obesity. In contrast, the AUC value for IL-18 was 0.531, with a p-value of 0.679, suggesting that its discriminative ability is weak and not statistically significant.

For each biomarker, the optimal cut-off value was determined based on the Youden Index, which provides a balance between sensitivity and specificity. The index was calculated using the formula: Youden Index = Sensitivity + Specificity – 1.

According to this calculation, the optimal cut-off value for NLPR3 was 0.00363 ng/ml, yielding a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 46.7%, with the highest Youden Index of 0.367. For Preptin, the best threshold was 80.664 pg/ml (80% sensitivity, 40% specificity), and for PPARγ, it was 0.153 ng/ml (80% sensitivity, 56.7% specificity). The optimal cut-off for IL-18 was 41.82 pg/ml, with 83.3% sensitivity and 30% specificity, resulting in a relatively low Youden Index of 0.133.

These findings highlight the potential of these biomarkers in differentiating obese individuals and provide insight into which threshold values may be more effective in clinical applications. Discussion

The prevalence of pediatric obesity has increased significantly worldwide over the last five decades. Age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles can be used to assess obesity in children. The increase in pediatric obesity is mediated by factors such as changes in diet, sedentary lifestyle, high consumption of sugar-sweetened products, imbalances in energy intake, genetic predisposition and socioeconomic factors [17].

Preptin is a derivative of pro-IGF-II that is secreted by pancreatic β cells and increases glucose-mediated insulin secretion in a calcium-dependent manner by activating the insulin-like growth factor II receptor at high glucose concentrations [18]. In many studies, preptin levels were found to be greater in obese individuals than in healthy individuals [19–21]. Mahmood and Al-Helaly showed in their studies that there is a negative correlation between preptin hormone and antioxidants and a positive correlation between preptin hormone and oxidants, and this relationship may be due to the positive correlation of preptin hormone with BMI [7]. In our study, preptin levels were significantly greater in obese children than in healthy controls (P < 0.05). Various studies have shown that PPARγ is involved in adipose cell development and adipocyte differentiation, is expressed at high levels in adipose tissue, is closely associated with physiological conditions such as glucose-lipid metabolism, insulin resistance, inflammation, aging and pregnancy and is therefore highly active in the regulation of energy metabolism [22–24]. Martínez-Jiménez et al.. reported that PPAR γ expression is increased in subcutaneous adipose tissue in obese individuals compared with that in normal weight individuals [25]. In our study, PPARγ levels were significantly higher in obese children compared to healthy controls (P < 0.05) and there was a significant positive correlation between PPARγ and IL-18 (p < 0.05). Excess adipose tissue formation in obesity triggers chronic inflammation, resulting in dysfunctional adipose tissue characterized by impaired angiogenesis, local hypoxia, altered extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrosis. Currently, adipose tissue-derived inflammation in obesity and many other obesity-related diseases may be mediated by the NLRP3 and IL-1β pathways [26, 27]. In their study, Unamuno et al. showed that the expression levels of NLRP3, IL-1β and IL-18 increased in visceral adipose tissue and liver tissue in obese and obesity-related type 2 diabetes patients compared to those in the control group. They also suggested that there is evidence that NLRP3 inhibition reduces adipose tissue inflammation [26]. In our study, NLRP3 levels were found to be significantly greater in obese children than in normal weight children (P < 0.05). The ability of PPARγ to suppress NFκB signaling positions it as a potential regulatory factor in immune responses mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome. A pivotal study conducted on CGI-58 (Comparative Gene Identification-58) deficient mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) demonstrated that the NLRP3 inflammasome was activated in adipose tissue, liver, and adipose tissue-derived macrophages (ATMs) due to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). As a result of overnutrition, impaired PPARγ/PGC-1 signaling within the mitochondria leads to substantial mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) generation, which subsequently triggers NLRP3 inflammasome activation. These pathological mechanisms were completely reversed following treatment with the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone. This finding highlights the anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial regulatory role of PPARγ in obesity-associated immune responses [28, 29]. In obese individuals, chronic inflammation occurs with a high release of proinflammatory molecules from adipose tissue and IL-18 is produced and released by human adipose tissue [30, 31]. In a study by Wang et al.. IL-18 levels were found to be greater in obese children than in normal weight children and a correlation was found between IL-18 levels and BMI [32]. In our study, although IL-18 levels were slightly greater in obese children than in normal children, the difference was not significant. This suggests that IL-1β and IL-18 activation via NLRP3 is mostly mediated by IL-1β in the low-grade inflammation that occurs in obesity and that IL-18 has a minor effect.

The ROC analysis conducted in this study demonstrated that the biomarkers NLPR3, Preptin, and PPARγ possess moderate discriminative power in identifying obese individuals, with AUC values of 0.678, 0.662, and 0.690, respectively (p < 0.05). Although these biomarkers did not emerge as strong predictors in the multivariate model, their individual performance suggests potential clinical relevance. Among them, NLPR3 exhibited the highest Youden Index (0.367), with high sensitivity (90%) but relatively low specificity (46.7%), indicating that it may be more suitable for screening purposes rather than definitive diagnosis. Preptin and PPARγ also showed acceptable sensitivity levels; however, their specificity remained limited. In contrast, IL-18 had the lowest Youden Index (0.133), making it the least effective biomarker in this context. Overall, while the combined use of these biomarkers may not be sufficient for diagnosing obesity, individual markers such as NLPR3 and PPARγ appear promising. Future studies involving larger sample sizes and additional variables may help to better elucidate the roles of these biomarkers and enhance their predictive accuracy.

In conclusion, serum concentrations of NLRP3, preptin and PPARγ were significantly higher in obese children than in healthy controls. This suggests that NLRP3, preptin and PPARγ parameters are strongly associated with pediatric obesity inflammation. The potential inhibitory role of elevated PPARγ on NLRP3 and IL-18-mediated inflammatory activation may contribute to a better understanding of the inflammatory mechanisms underlying obesity. These findings suggest that the studied biomarkers could serve as early indicators and potential therapeutic targets in the management of childhood obesity and its complications. This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size (30 obese and 30 healthy children) may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Although the gender distribution was relatively balanced (35 females and 25 males), age- and sex-specific effects were not analyzed separately. Moreover, Tanner staging was not performed to assess the pubertal development of the participants, which is a notable limitation given that certain biological parameters may vary not only with age but also with pubertal status. Due to data availability, laboratory infrastructure limitations, and budget constraints, routine biochemical markers and classical inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, IL-6, and C-reactive protein could not be included in the analyses. Nevertheless, the association between these markers and obesity has been well-documented in the literature, underscoring their relevance in understanding the metabolic and systemic consequences of pediatric obesity [33–36]. Future studies with larger, more diverse populations and stratified analyses are needed to validate these findings and explore the influence of age and gender on the studied parameters.

Although this study has certain limitations, it lays important groundwork for future research. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in which preptin, PPARγ, NLRP3, and IL-18 have been examined together in the context of childhood obesity. The lack of previous studies combining these specific markers makes our findings particularly valuable, offering new insights into the complex relationship between inflammation and metabolism in obese children. We believe these results could help shape more effective diagnostic and treatment approaches in the fight against pediatric obesity.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant alterations in preptin, NLRP3, and PPARγ levels in children with obesity compared to their healthy peers, underscoring their potential roles in the inflammatory processes associated with pediatric obesity. The observed elevation in PPARγ levels and its possible modulatory effect on NLRP3-mediated inflammation suggest a complex interplay between metabolic and immune pathways. These biomarkers may serve as valuable tools in the early diagnosis, personalized treatment, and prevention of obesity-related complications in children, offering new insights into the pathophysiology of pediatric obesity.
