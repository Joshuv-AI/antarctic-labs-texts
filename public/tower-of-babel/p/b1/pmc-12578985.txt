Responsible AI in student management: preventing misdecision in career choice of university students under inaccurate guidance
Authors: Shi Yin, Raiha Imran, Kifayat Ullah, Zeeshan Ali, Izatmand Haleemzai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In today’s digital era, university students’ career-related decision-making has become increasingly complex, shaped not only by personal aspirations and societal expectations but also by the algorithmic recommendations embedded in AI-supported technologies. While such systems provide convenient references, their outputs are often vague, generalized, or contextually limited, which underscores the irreplaceable role of human judgment in situations of uncertainty. Against this backdrop, the present study develops a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) framework tailored to the context of student management and career guidance. Specifically, it integrates the compromise ranking strategy CRADIS (Compromise Ranking of Alternatives from Distance to Ideal Solution) with the expressive flexibility of Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy Sets (Cir-PyFS). Cir-PyFS was chosen to reflect the modalities of non-decision, inexactness, and subjective desire in human evaluation that people may have. The linguistic assessments of each alternative were redefined in CPFS representations within the proposed Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy-based CRADIS model (Cir-PyFBCM), which incorporates membership, non-membership, and radius values. The model outperformed traditional fuzzy MCDM approaches in terms of stability, interpretability, and sensitivity to the influence of decision-makers in an empirical exercise using a realistic career-planning example with several alternative courses of action, multiple expert decision-makers, and eight benefit-based evaluation criteria. These comparison and sensitivity analysis results support that the proposed methodology serves as an effective bridge, linking mathematical precision with the human approach to reasoning, and offers a more careful, flexible, and reliable tool for governing high-stakes decisions, especially university students, when making their career decisions in AI-supported realms. More broadly, the study underscores the potential of responsible AI-assisted decision-making in promoting reflective, evidence-based practices within higher education management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22127-7.

Introduction

Career decision-making among university students is a serious, emotionally laden, and inherently complex process, shaped by a wide range of predictors, including social, economic, and personal factors. In navigating this process, students are confronted with multiple options and must reconcile their internal desires, motivations, and aspirations with external expectations and constraints, while also attempting to anticipate and evaluate potential future outcomes1. This career decision-making process becomes even more critical when it extends to the evaluation of individual skills, adaptation to technological change, and responsiveness to the dynamics of the job market. In addressing these challenges, artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly emerging as a vital tool in career guidance, offering data-driven insights and personalized recommendations to support students in navigating complex choices2. Intelligent decision-support systems powered by algorithms and language models are increasingly applied in platforms such as curriculum vitae builders, job search engines, and virtual career counselors. With features including recommendation functions, efficiency, effectiveness, scalability, and personalized decision-making, these systems have become valuable tools for students as they prepare career materials, explore employment opportunities, and seek guidance. At the same time, they also provide higher education institutions with new possibilities to strengthen career services and improve student management practices3. Despite the growing adoption of AI-driven decision-support systems, important questions regarding their transparency, reliability, and potential for spreading misinformation remain unresolved. In practice, many students tend to place unquestioned trust in AI-generated suggestions, which may lead to misguided choices, missed opportunities, and eventual dissatisfaction with career outcomes. These concerns highlight the need for closer examination of how such systems function, how students perceive and interpret their outputs, and how these factors ultimately shape the decision-making process and the quality of career-related decisions.

Although AI assistance could exist, an ever-growing number of data points suggests a significant weakness in AI-driven career decision support systems: their outcomes are not always accurate, trustworthy, or contextually relevant. Despite their increasing complexity, algorithmic consultants and language models sometimes give incorrect or biased advice because their training data lacks algorithmic design or customized context. Such errors might have significant and lasting effects in professional settings4. Career decisions, unlike daily choices, have a profound impact on a university student’s sense of self, mental health, financial independence, and overall life satisfaction. A single mistake, such as accepting poorly designed AI advice, could lead a student down an unsuitable academic path, cause a job applicant to pursue an unattainable position, or prompt an employee to abandon a promising career. These choices are not easily reversed and have financial, social, and emotional consequences. This situation becomes more complicated when we consider that many people, especially teenagers and young adults, make these choices under significant external pressure, particularly from their relatives. The expectations of parents, societal pressure, and cultural norms are the limits within which a person feels free to make decisions. In such situations, AI system recommendations may either support established prejudices or generate contradictory stories, leaving people unsure about whether to follow familial or algorithmic direction. Misplacing trust is the risk here. Because AI tools frequently imitate confidence and neutrality, particularly when there is no human mentorship or critical examination, users tend to overrate their reliability. This increasing difference between trust and the actual accuracy of the system poses a particular risk to weak users who are inexperienced, lack domain expertise, or have limited access to expert counselors. It poses pressing concerns about how society prepares people, particularly young people and those entering the workforce, to make educated, thoughtful, and independent choices in a world increasingly influenced by algorithms5.

To make wise choices, especially in high-stakes situations like career planning, it’s essential to use tools that can handle both the natural ambiguity of human likes and judgments, as well as multiple competing criteria. Traditional crisp models6 are insufficient in dealing with such uncertainty, prompting scientists to use soft computing paradigms, particularly fuzzy set (FS) theory7, which bridges the gap between mathematical structure and linguistic ambiguity. The decision-making frameworks have undergone significant changes. The theory began with Zadeh’s traditional FS, then moved to Intuitionistic Fuzzy Sets (IFS)8, which takes into account both membership and non-membership degrees, and then to Pythagorean Fuzzy Sets (PyFS)9, which allows greater flexibility by employing the square sum limitations. By using q-rung orthopair fuzzy sets (q-ROFS)10, which allows for even more flexibility in expressing uncertainty, this was further generalized. By incorporating a radial dimension that quantifies the uncertainty radius, circular intuitionistic fuzzy sets (Cir-IFS)11 provide a geometric explanation for fuzzy logic. This is a significant addition that mirrors the degree of inaccuracy involved in human evaluations. Circular pythagorean fuzzy sets (Cir-PyFS)12, which represents expert judgments using a triplet, i.e., membership, non-membership, and radius, has emerged as a robust framework within this path. The radius r signifies the degree of ambiguity; the greater the radius, the more uncertainty there is in the judgment.

Although this theoretical development has significantly enhanced our ability to model uncertainty, its integration with MCDM techniques remains a topic of ongoing research and is utilized in various studies13–15. To process expert inputs, several aggregation techniques have been developed, including aggregation operators (AOs) that range from arithmetic operators to weighted geometric methods, as well as methods such as CoCoSo, Edas16, TOPSIS17,18, AHP, MABAC, and others. However, the majority of frameworks do not account for radius-based uncertainty or overlook the inclusion of complex cooperation ranking methods. Despite its established efficiency in MCDM for balancing ideal and anti-ideal distances, the CRADIS method has not yet been thoroughly investigated in the Cir-PyFS environment. This error is especially significant due to the method’s ability to perform exact linguistic assessments under high uncertainty. This research fills that gap by suggesting a unique integration of CRADIS into a Cir-PyFS framework, which enables a more comprehensive representation and stronger aggregation of expert opinions. In this way, it enhances both the theoretical representation of uncertainty and its practical application in complex decision-making scenarios, such as career counseling, where ambiguity, emotion, and irreversibility intersect. Moreover, this combination is especially appropriate for understanding the profession choices made by students at universities, which involve both quantifiable aspects (such as labor market trends and skill demands) and qualitative ones (including personal ambitions, emotional reservations, and perceived uncertainties), which are decisive in such highly valued and multidimensional situations.

Career planning and decision-making

When considering a career, it’s essential to create a plan for the future that aligns with a person’s values, talents, and goals, rather than simply choosing a profession. In a society, making informed career choices has become increasingly essential and challenging as it experiences rapid technological change and shifting job markets. According to Otago Polytechnic19, Selecting a career is a highly individual process that involves self-awareness, thorough opportunity investigation, and careful consideration of several options. Yale University’s five-step model highlights the self-assessment, exploration, decision-making, goal-setting, and action as a well-organized route to clarity and confidence, as in Fig. 120. Effective planning guarantees not only employment but also satisfaction, regardless of whether you’re a student entering the workforce or an adult making a career transition. It is one of the most essential life skills in the twenty-first century, as it empowers people to take control of their future in an unpredictable world.

Challenges in career guidance: AI and social influences

Selecting a career path is rarely a linear process, as a complex network of factors, stresses, and uncertainties often shapes it. Although helpful, traditional career counselling programs usually struggle to provide individualized, real-time advice that keeps pace with the rapid changes in industries and occupations. As a result, AI-driven solutions, such as resume analyzers, job matching platforms, and large language models, have gained popularity as potential career assistance systems21. These technologies also have risks, but they provide speed, convenience, and customized recommendations. AI systems may also produce biased information, offer general recommendations, or oversimplify individual objectives. Effective career counseling requires a high level of self-awareness, ongoing introspection, and well-informed judgment, all of which may be challenging for even the most sophisticated algorithms to replicate. The impact of social customs and family expectations remains significant in shaping the decisions people make, alongside technological factors. Often at the expense of individual satisfaction, parents’ expectations, peer comparisons, and societal definitions of “success” frequently influence which occupations are considered suitable. Young people and adults undergoing career transitions may feel confused or misled by this dual pressure coming from both algorithms and expectations. The five-step Yale University model20 emphasizes the importance of independence and self-awareness in career planning; however, achieving this clarity can be challenging without carefully considering both external influences and online advice. Giving students the ability to make wise, reflective, ideal choices requires more than simply providing access to resources. It demands an awareness, discrimination, and a fair combination of ethical AI, trusted human support, and individual insight.

Research motivation and objectives

Despite the increasing use of fuzzy models and AI-driven technology in decision-making, a significant research gap remains in accurately understanding complex uncertainty and human judgment, particularly in sensitive areas such as career planning and decision-making. Despite the evolution of fuzzy sets from classical to interval and then to more expressive forms, such as Pythagorean and q-rung orthopair fuzzy sets, these models often fail to capture the level of uncertainty expressed by decision-makers completely. The uncertainty is represented mathematically with a greater degree by the advent of Cir-PyFS, which includes a radius component in addition to membership and non-membership degrees. However, in many studies, this architecture has not been used in complicated ranking methods. As a result, the CRADIS strategy has been included in the Cir-PyFS framework in this study. Furthermore, by neglecting to consider the hierarchical uncertainty, social norms, and incomplete knowledge that are inherent in real-world career decisions, the majority of MCDM techniques either rely on standard grouping methods or oversimplify decision situations. Therefore, by the recognized research gap, this study aims to answer the following research questions:

i.In multi-criteria settings, how can decision-makers utilize Cir-PyFS to model uncertainty, hesitancy, and inaccuracy successfully?ii.For enhancing the framework of decision quality, is it possible to effectively integrate and extend the CRADIS approach within a Cir-PyFS?iii.When faced with complex decision-making challenges, such as career planning among university students or choosing between options in the face of social and cognitive uncertainty, how well does the proposed Cir-PyFBCM approach compare?

These questions are designed to help create and assess a robust, uncertainty-aware decision-making model, which will contribute theoretical insights and practical techniques to the MCDM area. By creating a new decision-making model, this research aims to address the issue that leverages the expressive capabilities of Cir-PyFS and the advantages of CRADIS. The suggested approach is specifically designed to address the complex, multi-criteria problems with significant uncertainty and conflict that characterize modern career decision-making situations. This model considers not only the degree of agreement or disagreement with a principle but also the level of hesitancy and certainty behind it by including the radius term in the evaluation process. The main contribution is to enhance the reliability and clarity of MCDM tools in uncertain settings, offering a more thoughtful, accurate, and human-centred approach to decision-makers, particularly students, job seekers, and counsellors. The goal of this study is to address a significant methodological gap and enhance the design of next-generation decision support systems, enabling students to make informed decisions in high-stakes, value-based environments.

Section 2 provides the preliminaries, fundamental concepts, and definitions applicable to the proposed approach. Section 3 explains the proposed methodology in detail, combining the CRADIS technique with Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy Sets (Cir-PyFS) and creating the Cir-PyFBCM framework. In Sect. 4, an evaluation of the model is presented through a numerical assessment, where a suggested method is applied to a relevant career-planning problem. Lastly, Sect. 5 summarizes the paper’s findings and significant implications, limitations as well as potential future research directions.

Development of a circular pythagorean Fuzzy-Based CRADIS model

This section presents the proposed CRADIS model within a Cir-PyFS environment, taking into account uncertainty, imprecise data, and subjective hesitation. In MCDM, uncertainty, imprecision, and decision-maker cognitive reluctance are the standard processes of real-world problems. Although the CRADIS method is well-known as a balanced method, where alternatives are ranked according to their relative proximity to both ideal and anti-ideal solutions, the classic version of the process does not adequately embrace the indeterminacy and fuzziness inherent in human ratings. To fill this gap, this paper introduces a new extension, the Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy-Based CRADIS model (Cir-PyFBCM), which combines the power of expressiveness of the Cir-PyF framework and the systematic compromise mechanism of CRADIS. A triplet \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(t,f,r)$$\end{document} defines each element of the Cir-PyFS environment, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r$$\end{document} indicates the degree of acceptance, the degree of rejection, and a critical term radius r, which represents the amount of hesitation or uncertainty. This radius enables the creation of a circular geometric representation of expert opinion, allowing the model to represent not only whether and how strongly an expert agrees or disagrees with a criterion, but also the level of confidence or imprecision in the expert opinion. The CRADIS integration into this enhanced structure enhances the quality of decision-making by considering both subjective ambiguity and objective distances during the ranking process. This section develops the proposed model, its calculation procedure, and theoretical foundation are described, offering a solid guide to understandable decision-making in the context of high uncertainty.

Assessment of AI-Influenced career decisions

Choosing a career path is one of the most crucial but challenging aspects of a university student’s life, as it involves both self-discovery and social interaction. A growing number of factors, including digital ecosystems, personal preferences and hobbies, parental expectations, peer pressure, cultural values, and more than just logic, are contributing to the development of a more emotionally driven process. Social media sites provide both a source of motivation and a low-impact method of pressure, as selected success stories and favourite jobs influence how people perceive happiness and success in our hyperconnected culture. Even while offering advice, family members and friends frequently project their hopes and experiences, which unintentionally creates a conflict between outside counsel and internal calling. This complex environment has given rise to new decision-making influencers, such as ChatGPT, resume evaluators, and career-matching platforms that AI powers. These resources are gaining popularity among young people, who utilize them for guidance, industry education, or advice on skill matching. These programs often provide personalized routes based on keyword inputs, labour market data, pattern recognition, or user preferences. Even if they are quick and convincing, their results are not free of bias, imperfection, or overconfidence. This highlights the dual nature of the relationship between humans and AI in career decision-making, presenting both potential and danger. Therefore, the conclusion is rarely straightforward. It entails considering several factors with uncertainty and hesitation that are irregularly ambiguous. Choosing a career becomes a decision-making challenge, where options must be weighed against a variety of criteria, some of which are ambiguous, such as job security, passion, social standing, financial rewards, and independence. A framework that can handle both realistic data and human vagueness and ambiguity is necessary to address this. The following case study shows how the suggested Cir-PyFBCM catches this complexity and helps one to make carefully, AI-informed career decisions.

Case study

Young professionals, particularly university students, are increasingly relying on AI-driven platforms to support their career planning as artificial intelligence becomes deeply embedded in everyday decision-making processes. To illustrate this complexity, consider a fictional scenario involving four graduate students from diverse academic and cultural backgrounds who are approaching the transition from university to the labor market after completing a multidisciplinary program. Faced with a wide range of career options, each student turns to an AI-based career advisory system, which generates potential pathways aligned with their academic qualifications, skill sets, personal interests, and prevailing labor market trends. Although these recommendations appear data-driven and rational, they also introduce new layers of ambiguity. The systems often fail to incorporate critical personal and cultural dimensions, such as lifestyle aspirations, emotional well-being, family expectations, or the symbolic prestige of particular professions. For students, these overlooked aspects can carry as much weight as measurable skills or job prospects. In parallel, the influence of social media narratives, peer comparisons, and parental expectations creates competing discourses about what constitutes a “successful” career. Rather than simplifying decision-making, the interplay of algorithmic advice and social pressures can amplify uncertainty and tension, leaving students with conflicting signals and heightened anxiety in navigating high-stakes choices.

This scenario reflects the real-world challenges faced by many university students today. It underscores the necessity of examining not only the technical capacity of AI-based decision aids but also their psychological, social, and cultural implications for students’ career development. By centering the student perspective, research can contribute to more responsible and holistic applications of AI in higher education management, ensuring that technological support complements rather than overshadows individual values and human judgment in career decision-making. Effective career decision-making for university students requires a structured approach that integrates both objective and subjective factors. Students must consider labor market demand, growth potential, and future opportunities, while also reflecting on personal values, social influences, lifestyle preferences, and individual aspirations. To support this process, the current study frames career selection as a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) problem, in which students’ career options are evaluated through expert guidance and AI-informed recommendations using eight benefit-oriented criteria.

A structured decision-making approach is necessary to overcome this challenge, one that incorporates subjective, fuzzy, and value-laden factors, such as social influence and personal alignment, alongside objective data, including employment demand and growth potential. Consequently, this case is presented as an MCDM issue, in which the inputs of four professional decision-makers are used to analyze six potential career pathways using eight benefit-type criteria. Verbal evaluations are used to evaluate each career option. The results are then translated into Cir-PyFN to reflect the degree to which decision-makers are cautious about joining or not joining, as well as their level of confidence or uncertainty. The evaluations are processed by using the CRADIS method, a compromise-based ranking approach that compares each alternative’s closeness to both ideal and anti-ideal solutions. Consequently, in complicated situations, this Cir-PyFBCM model facilitates a mathematically sound and human-centred evaluation of career choices. It enables the selection of a career route that best represents the decision-makers’ values and opinions, as well as the AI-informed environment that influences current career decisions. The novelty of this paper offers a unique perspective on AI-based, AI-informed career decision-making as a multi-criteria decision problem, with the explicit inclusion of subjective human values, social factors, and the modeling of uncertainties using Cir-PyFS. The flexibility of the CRADIS approach enables consideration of both ideal and anti-ideal solutions. Moreover, the Cir-PyFBCM framework in a career planning setting offers a human-friendly and mathematically rigorous framework for comparing a complex and high-stakes set of decisions used in an AI-assisted context.

Students’ assessments of each potential pathway are captured using verbal evaluations, which are then translated into Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy Numbers (Cir-PyFN) to reflect the degree of caution, confidence, and uncertainty they experience. These evaluations are processed through the CRADIS method, a compromise-based ranking approach, allowing students to compare alternatives in relation to both ideal and anti-ideal outcomes. This student-centered model ensures that the evaluation process is both mathematically rigorous and aligned with students’ individual values, priorities, and career goals.

Sensitivity analysis

To assure the strength and stability of the proposed Cir-PyFBCM decision-making model, a two-part sensitivity analysis was conducted. The aim was to determine how much of a difference changes in the weight assigned to decision-makers would make regarding the ultimate rankings of the alternatives in terms of utility. In the first case, we identified whether a uniform linguistic weighting of decision-makers would make a difference in the model. The initial stages of the proposed framework involved specifying the linguistic levels of importance (e.g., Extremely High, High, Moderate, Low) for decision-makers based on their relevance to the domain. Further, to ensure the consistency within the model, the same value of linguistics was given to each decision-maker at four tested conditions of EH, H, M, and L. Interestingly, the resulting utility function values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:g\left({\fancyscript{p}}_{\mathcalligra{i}}\right)$$\end{document} were similar to all of these conditions. The fact that the relative position of the alternatives stays the same is a confirmation that as long as all decision-makers are regarded of equal significance regardless of the level of importance, high or low, the position of the alternatives will not be altered. This indicates the internal equity of the model and its independence of absolute magnitudes of weights when their distribution is uniform. The second scenario used a sensitivity test based on perturbation. In this case, the initial significance (weight) of a Decision-Maker \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathfrak{O}}_{1}$$\end{document} was augmented by 3%, 7%, 10%, and 50%, while the weights of other decision-makers were proportionately reduced so that the overall weight sum equaled 1. This test was conducted under both the proposed weighting and the condition that all linguistic terms are uniform. The outcome indicated that there were slight to minor changes in the utility function values, particularly as the amount of weight perturbation increased. As Fig. 6 illustrates, IFS and Cir-IFS have failed to rank the alternatives. While the traditional models, including PyFWA and PyFWG, showed the basic aggregate scores of the alternatives, they did not capture the hesitation and uncertainty inherent in real-world expert judgment. The same applies to the Cir-PyFWA and Cir-PyFWG methods, which are slightly superior due to their circular nature, but fail to provide a compromise ranking strategy, a critical ingredient in resolving multidimensional disputes in group decision-making. The proposed Cir-PyFBCM model showed better utility values of best alternatives (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{4}=0.8077\:$$\end{document}and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{A}_{1}=0.7756$$\end{document}), indicating that the model better represents the expert consensus, hesitation (through the radius term), and overall performance trade-offs. Conclusion

In contemporary career landscapes, students’ interests are increasingly shaped by digital ecosystems, algorithm-based recommendations, and the complex interplay of personal values. To address the uncertainty inherent in such career decisions, this paper proposes a human-centered decision-making framework that integrates the compromise ranking method (CRADIS) within the expressive structure of Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy Sets (Cir-PyFS). The resulting Circular Pythagorean Fuzzy based CRADIS model (Cir-PyFBCM) is designed to manage both quantitative ambiguity and qualitative hesitation that students face when evaluating career options. The model supports thoughtful, confidence-weighted choices by accommodating the fuzziness of human judgment, considering social influences such as peer and family expectations, and acknowledging the limitations of AI-generated recommendations. Its utility is demonstrated through a case study of AI-informed career selection, which incorporates multiple expert perspectives and subtle benefit-oriented criteria. The inclusion of the radius term within CPFS enhances realism by capturing not only the degree of career fit but also the strength and depth of students’ evaluations. Comparisons and sensitivity analyses indicate that the Cir-PyFBCM model outperforms traditional fuzzy MCDM approaches in stability, interpretability, and responsiveness to expert influence. These findings underscore the importance of decision-support systems that combine computational rigor with attention to students’ emotional, contextual, and value-driven considerations. By facilitating critical engagement with AI recommendations, reflective assessment, and confident decision-making, such frameworks empower students to navigate complex career choices effectively. As AI increasingly enters educational and professional domains, models like Cir-PyFBCM provide essential tools for ensuring that career decisions are both practically viable and closely aligned with students’ individual values and aspirations.

Limitations and future direction

Although the Cir-PyFBCM provides a structured and practical framework for managing uncertainty and integrating subjective judgments in multi-criteria career decision-making, it is not without limitations. First, the model relies on expert input and students’ verbal evaluations, which, despite the flexibility of Cir-PyFS, remain inherently subjective and may vary across different cultural and psychosocial contexts. In addition, while the case study incorporates diverse and realistic input parameters, it is still hypothetical. In real-world settings, students face data noise, competing stakeholder influences, and rapidly changing information flows, making large-scale empirical validation desirable. In the real world, where data noise, competing stakeholder views, shifting information flows, and culturally constrained descriptions of linguistic terms are likely to be encountered, empirical confirmation is therefore desirable on a large scale.

Future development of the model could further enhance its relevance and applicability for students. Dynamic weight adjustment mechanisms, informed by machine learning or feedback-based approaches, could allow students’ priorities and expert influences to evolve over time. Integration with real-time labor market analytics, social sentiment tracking, or explainable AI outputs could also improve the responsiveness and transparency of career guidance. Moreover, applying the model to scenarios such as group decision-making with conflicting student interests, university program selection, or career counseling interventions would demonstrate its broader flexibility. Ultimately, transforming Cir-PyFBCM into a hybrid human-AI interactive platform could empower students not only to make more informed and optimal career decisions but also to develop a deeper understanding of the trade-offs, uncertainties, and value-based considerations involved in their choices. Applying the model to areas such as group decision-making in cases of conflicting interests, as well as healthcare, university admissions, and policy formulation, would further demonstrate its flexibility. Moreover, statistical analysis is included, such as the weighted Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, to understand the relationship between pairwise comparative techniques when applying the proposed approach to real-world data. Ultimately, transforming the model into a hybrid human-AI interactive platform has the potential not only to enable individuals to make optimal decisions but also to gain a deeper understanding of the trade-offs involved.
