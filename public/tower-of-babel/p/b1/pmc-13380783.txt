Mitochondria-targeted MXene-based nanozymes promote mitophagy and inhibit mtDNA-triggered cGAS/STING inflammation in osteoarthritis
Authors: Tiancheng Li, Ao Zheng, Cheng Zhu, Yixin Li, Zitong Yang, Xinyue Tang, Yu Jin, Ting Dong, Bin Li, Lunguo Xia, Lingyan Cao, Bing Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent and debilitating joint disease driven by progressive cartilage degradation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation. In this study, we introduced MS@PMXene-TK, an innovative, mitochondria-targeted nanozyme designed for cartilage repair by addressing these key pathological features. This nanozyme platform uniquely integrated a chondro-inductive peptide (SPPEPS)-loaded, polydopamine (PDA)-modified MXene core (S@PMXene) with a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive thioketal-linked polyethylene glycol (PEG-TK) shell and a mitochondria-targeting peptide (MTP-131), enabling precise and responsive therapeutic intervention at the subcellular level. In vitro and in vivo analyses demonstrated that MS@PMXene-TK effectively scavenged mitochondrial ROS within chondrocytes, acting as a “cruise missile,” which led to restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential and promotion of mitophagy. This cascade mitigated mtDNA leakage and subsequent activation of the cGAS/STING pathway, a critical inflammatory driver in OA. Concurrently, sustained release of SPPEPS enhanced chondrogenic marker expression and extracellular matrix synthesis, while alleviating macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses, further modulating the inflammatory microenvironment. In an anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT)-induced OA mouse model, intra-articular administration of MS@PMXene-TK significantly improved cartilage protection and subchondral bone integrity. These findings establish the potential of this targeted, multi-modal nanozyme strategy to disrupt intertwined pathologies of oxidative stress and inflammation in OA, offering a promising avenue for OA treatment.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a widespread degenerative joint disorder that imposes considerable disability and economic strain [1]. Recent epidemiological evidence indicates that the burden of OA continues to increase, with substantial growth in prevalent cases, incident cases, and disability-adjusted life years, particularly among aging populations and individuals with high body mass index [2]. Its characteristic pathology includes cartilage breakdown, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation, all of which culminate in pain and impaired joint function [3]. Owing to an incomplete understanding of its etiology and the poor regenerative capacity of cartilage, treatment options remain scarce. Apart from joint replacement, available therapies are largely palliative and focus on alleviating pain and inflammation but fail to halt or reverse disease progression [4]. Although recent studies have explored advanced therapeutic platforms for OA, including microenvironment-responsive hydrogels, spatiotemporal delivery systems, and stimulus-responsive nanotherapeutic strategies, effective disease-modifying treatments remain limited [[5], [6], [7]]. Clinical and experimental data increasingly show that mitochondrial dysfunction severely impairs tissue repair and provokes metabolic disturbances and cellular damage through elevated oxidative stress. In OA, these dysfunctional mitochondria overproduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which fuel inflammation and tissue degradation, ultimately establishing a pro-inflammatory microenvironment at cartilage lesion sites. This reduces downstream chondrocyte function, leading to compromised tissue healing. Therefore, in order to regenerate cartilage defects in OA microenvironment (characterized by high level of ROS, inflammatory cytokines, proteinases, etc.), a comprehensive coordination and orchestration of various biological events in the mitochondrial oxidative stress process and downstream inflammatory cascade is required, which, however, remains a challenge in the field of tissue engineering.

Although mitochondria are the main source of ROS, ROS in turn can damage mitochondrial components. Mitochondrial integrity is crucial for controlling inflammation, and when it is compromised, released mitochondrial constituents and metabolites act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that trigger immune responses. For instance, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released into the cytosol is detected by the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS/STING) pathway, leading to downstream signaling that can drive chondrocyte senescence, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix imbalance [[8], [9], [10]]. Mitophagy, the conserved process that selectively removes damaged or excess mitochondria, is essential for preserving mitochondrial homeostasis and cell viability [11]. Impairment of mitophagy exacerbates mitochondrial injury and can provoke pyroptosis [12], undermining cellular ROS clearance [13]. When these quality control mechanisms are overwhelmed or dysfunctional, mitochondrial-driven inflammation may become pathogenic and contribute to OA development. Hence, a mitochondria-targeted biomaterial capable of modulating mitophagy and suppressing cGAS/STING–mediated inflammation under oxidative stress could create a favorable intracellular environment for cartilage repair and represents a promising therapeutic strategy for OA.

Inflammation control via antioxidative nanozymes has recently drawn significant attention in osteoarthritis therapy [14]. Recent studies have shown that ROS-scavenging nanozymes and mitophagy-regulating strategies can reduce oxidative injury, suppress inflammatory activation, and alleviate OA progression [15,16]. Transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes), a thriving class of two-dimensional materials, have demonstrated promising antioxidative capabilities under pathological conditions, serving as a paradigm for guiding nanozymes to eliminate reactive oxygen species [17]. However, MXenes are inherently unstable nanosheets prone to oxidative degradation into carbon dioxide, which undermines their structural integrity [18]. Coating MXene with polydopamine (PDA) prevents degradation while imparting enhanced photothermal conversion and additional antioxidant activity; nevertheless, PDA forms only discrete clusters on the MXene surface, limiting full encapsulation and stability [19]. Meanwhile, MXene@PDA (PMXene) also exhibits reactive surface groups that can trigger immune clearance [20]. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) offers excellent biocompatibility, low protein adsorption, and facile functionalization, making it a preferred candidate for improving nanoparticle stability. In particular, thioketal-linked PEG (PEG-TK) remains intact under physiological ROS levels but cleaves when ROS exceed a threshold, enabling on-demand release to maximize payload utilization and therapeutic efficacy [21]. Compared with non-responsive surface modification, PEG-TK grafting provides PMXene with improved physiological stability and biocompatibility, while enabling ROS-responsive activation in the high-ROS OA microenvironment.

Bioactive peptides have attracted increasing attention in tissue repair because of their biocompatibility, functional diversity, and potential for targeted regulation, which may further enhance the biological specificity of nanozyme systems [22]. In this study, the chondro-inductive peptide SPPEPS was introduced to endow the nanozyme with cartilage repair-related potential, given its reported chondro-inductive activity and the emerging role of bioactive peptides in cartilage regeneration [23,24], while the mitochondria-targeting peptide MTP-131 was incorporated to direct the nanozyme toward mitochondria, the major source of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) and mtDNA release [25]. Together, SPPEPS and MTP-131 provide a rational basis for developing a cartilage repair-associated and mitochondria-targeted antioxidative nanozyme for OA therapy.

Herein, we hypothesize that this mitochondria-targeted, ROS-responsive antioxidative strategy may reduce excessive mitochondrial ROS in inflamed OA joint tissues, thereby preventing further inflammation. Similar to cruise missiles that accurately locate and hit targets, our nanozymes are designed to precisely locate mitochondria and eliminate excess ROS. This “mitochondrial ROS cruise missile” strategy ensures that therapeutic effects are limited to the site of mitochondrial dysfunction, avoiding additional damage. To achieve this goal, we developed a novel nanozyme, MS@PMXene-TK, for precise ROS-responsive therapy by incorporating the chondro-inductive peptide SPPEPS into the inner layer and grafting the mitochondria-targeting peptide MTP-131 onto the outer layer. The outer-layer MTP-131 modification enables precise mitochondrial targeting, limiting the therapeutic action to mitochondria and endowing the nanoparticles with the precision of a “cruise missile”. By utilizing PEG-TK grafting design, the ROS reactivity of nanoparticles was achieved. In the high-ROS environment of OA local cells, the degradation of PEG-TK triggered controlled release of therapeutic agents. The encapsulated PMXene nanoparticles further cleared excess mtROS, promoted mitophagy, limited mtDNA leakage, and prevented cGAS/STING activation. At the same time, the cartilage-inductive peptide SPPEPS incorporated into the inner layer enhances chondrogenic activity, achieving the integration of treatment and repair, and the nanoparticles are endowed with the effectiveness of a “cruise missile”. In the process of reducing mtROS and promoting cartilage repair, MS@PMXene-TK improved the local microenvironment of OA and alleviated macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses under OA-like inflammatory conditions. It is worth noting that in the anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT)-induced OA mouse model, MS@PMXene-TK significantly reduced oxidative stress and inhibited inflammation, thereby preventing the progression of osteoarthritis. Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MS@PMXene-TK

MS@PMXene-TK was designed as an integrated nanobiocatalytic platform that combines MXene-based antioxidative catalysis, mitochondrial targeting, ROS-responsive release, and chondroprotective peptide delivery within one coordinated system. It is reported that bio-nanochemistry inspired by mussels reveals that dopamine containing catechol structures can form strong coordination covalent bonds with exposed titanium (Ti) atoms on the surface of MXene [29]. The adhered PDA thin nano layer protects MXene from erosion by H2O and O2, serving as an effective physical barrier. The interaction between MXene and PDA was characterized by fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Fig. 1E). After the PDA grafting on MXene flakes, the characteristic absorption bands of PDA were observed in the FTIR spectra at 3400 cm−1 (N-H stretching) and 1500 cm−1 (benzene ring). The new peak at 1253 cm−1 indicates a binding between the catechol/quinone groups in PDA and the - OH/- F terminal groups in MXene, and these vibrational bands are also retained MS@PMXene-TK (Fig. S1C) [30]. Compared with the XRD spectrum of MXene (Fig. 1F), the decrease in the MXene characteristic diffraction peak in PDA-modified PMXene and MS@PMXene-TK reflected the increase in interlayer distance caused by the PDA coating (Fig. S1D). Shape and position of the other characteristic diffraction peaks did not show significant changes, indicating that PDA is an amorphous polymer with minimal impact on the crystal structure of MXene. MS@PMXene-TK also exhibited no significant new diffraction peaks [31]. By detecting the surface chemical composition of nanocomposites through XPS, as shown in Fig. 1G, the instrument detected characteristic peaks of C1s, O1s, Ti2p, and F1s in the MXene sample. In PMXene and MS@PMXene-TK (PDA-containing groups), a newly emerged N1s peak in the nanoparticles is attributed to nitrogen in DA, and the intensities of the Ti 2p and F 1s peaks are significantly reduced [32]. The S2s and S2p peaks present in MS@PMXene-TK are derived from the PEG-TK, confirming the successful grafting of PEG-TK (Fig. 1G). There are obvious Ti 2p3/2 states corresponding to 454.7 eV (Ti-C bond) and 458.6 eV (Ti-O bond) in MXene, while in MS@PMXene-TK they were almost completely obscured by the coatings (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 1I, the N 1s peaks at 396.1 eV and 401.3 eV in MS@PMXene-TK are primarily due to PDA binding (Fig. S1E), along with the presence of N in PEG-TK and MTP-131 on the surface. The peak of N-Ti in the N 1s curve is 396.1 eV, indicating a strong chemical bond between the amine groups in surface grafting and the Ti atoms suspended on MXene. PDA coated MXene provides abundant nitrogen-containing (N) functional groups, which not only enhance surface hydrophilicity but also provide more drug loading sites [33]. The loading content of SPPEPS in MS@PMXene-TK was approximately 8.31%, with an encapsulation efficiency of about 40%. In addition, MS@PMXene-TK showed ROS-responsive release behavior in PBS containing 1 mM H2O2 (Fig. S3). The deconvolved C1s spectra in Fig. 1J also demonstrate a strong affinity for the catechol titanium coordination bond (C-O-Ti) between the MXene skeleton and PDA layer at 286.9 eV. The increase in normalized intensity of the O 1s peak in the O1s spectrum in Fig. 1K is mainly related to the outer layer of PEG-TK and MTP-131. The XPS and FITC results mentioned above confirmed that MS@PMXene-TK was successfully prepared.

To further elucidate the ROS scavenging efficiency of nanoparticles in vitro, we explored the free radical scavenging ability of different nanoparticles [34]. As shown in Fig. 1L–N, MS@PMXene-TK exhibiting the most significant antioxidant capacity. For example, its ROS clearance rate for H2O2 reached approximately 86% (Fig. 1L), while the clearance rate for ·O2− reached about 75% (Fig. 1M). Similarly, MS@PMXene-TK demonstrated a more significant ·OH scavenging effect compared to other nanoparticles (Fig. 1N). Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 1L–N, PMXene exhibited a significantly higher ROS scavenging efficiency compared to MXene after 30 min of incubation. This result indicates that the PDA coating does not merely serve as a protective layer but actively contributes to the antioxidant capacity, resulting in a synergistic enhancement. Moreover, PDA modification is necessary for improving MXene stability and providing abundant catechol/amine groups for subsequent functionalization and drug loading. Notably, PDA itself has been reported to possess redox-active and radical-scavenging properties, which may also contribute to the overall antioxidant performance of the composite system [35]. The enhanced antioxidant performance of MS@PMXene-TK may be further attributed to the ROS-responsive TK linkage, which facilitates the release or exposure of functional components under high-ROS conditions, thereby improving the overall ROS clearance efficiency [36].

CCK-8 assays at 1, 2, and 3 days further showed that concentrations up to 50 μg/mL did not impair proliferation (Fig. 2C). The cytocompatibility of MXene and PMXene were also verified (Fig. S4 and S5). In addition, Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that M@PMXene-TK maintained high chondrocyte viability at the tested concentrations, supporting its favorable biocompatibility (Fig. S6). Considering the above results, this study selected an M@PMXene-TK concentration of 25 μg mL−1 for the subsequent experiments. Mitochondrial targeting is a prerequisite for an effective therapeutic agent for improving mitochondrial homeostasis in OA chondrocytes. The selectivity of MTP-131 modified PMXene (M@PMXene) or PMXene-TK (M@PMXene-TK) for mitochondria of chondrocytes was evaluated using co-localization analysis of fluorescence microscopy images. As shown in Fig. 2H, compared with the unmodified formulation (PMXene and PMXene-TK), the MTP-131-modified formulation (M@PMXene and M@PMXene-TK, red channel) was primarily colocalized with the mitochondrial network (green channel), with higher Pearson's correlation coefficients (Fig. 2F), indicating that MTP-131 modification enhanced mitochondrial accumulation of drugs in chondrocytes. This mitochondrial preference was further supported by bio-TEM observations, which showed intracellular nanozyme structures in close proximity to mitochondria, consistent with enhanced mitochondrial targeting after MTP-131 modification (Fig. S7). Together with the fluorescence colocalization data, these ultrastructural observations provide direct evidence of efficient chondrocyte internalization and subsequent mitochondrial accumulation of the nanozymes, supporting the proposed intracellular mode of action.

Since ROS is closely related to cellular activities such as mitochondrial homeostasis, this study assessed intracellular ROS levels following hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) exposure. Fluorescent staining demonstrated a marked increase in oxidative stress in untreated cells (Fig. 2I and J). Pretreatment with PMXene-TK or M@PMXene significantly reduced ROS accumulation, and the dual-modified M@PMXene-TK formulation, containing both MTP-131 and TK linkages, achieved the greatest reduction in oxidative burden (Fig. 2I and J). These trends were further validated by DCFH-DA flow cytometry, which quantitatively confirmed that M@PMXene-TK most effectively decreased the ROS-associated mean intensity under both H2O2 and IL-1β stimulation (Fig. S8). MXene-based nanocomposites are proposed to scavenge excessive reactive nitrogen species and ROS from oxidative damage, thus mitigating tissue damage, eradicating bacteria and avoiding infection [17,20]. Recently, mitochondrial targeting modifications have been demonstrated to enhance the ROS scavenging efficiency of nanomaterials, thereby improving OA inflammatory microenvironment [37]. These results indicated that combining the mitochondrial targeting ligand with PMXene enhances ROS clearance through a synergistic effect.

Chondro-inducive effect of MS@PMXene-TK under inflammatory conditions

After confirming the ideal cytocompatibility and mitochondrial targeting abilities of MS@PMXene-TK nanozymes, this study assessed their effects on chondrogenic activities under inflammatory conditions. Inflammation reduced the mRNA levels of Col2a1, Sox9, and Acan of the IL-1β group, leading to decreased biosynthesis of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and type II collagen (Fig. 3A–C). PMXene treatment alone showed similar expression of cartilage synthesis genes compared to the control group. The MS@PMXene group significantly increased the expression of these genes, indicating that polypeptide modification could effectively promote cartilage formation. Furthermore, the MS@PMXene-TK group exhibited the highest level of chondrogenic factors, as well as the highest content of GAG (Fig. 3A–C), indicating the synergistic effect of polypeptide modification and TK linkage treatment. Correspondingly, the increased Mmp3, Mmp13, and Adamts5 levels in IL-1β group were most significantly inhibited in the MS@PMXene-TK group (Fig. 3D and E). Western blot analysis further supported these findings, showing that MS@PMXene-TK restored cartilage anabolic markers (Collagen II, SOX9, and Aggrecan) while suppressing catabolic enzymes (MMP13, MMP3, and ADAMTS5) under IL-1β stimulation (Fig. 3F and G, and Fig. S9). The progressive degeneration of osteoarthritic cartilage reduces treatment effectiveness, as inflammation-driven alterations in chondrocyte function can impair their responsiveness to external stimuli, including chondrogenic agents [38]. Mitochondrial regulatory effects of MS@PMXene-TK under inflammatory conditions

Given that the vicious cycle of ROS accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction serves as a significant factor in intensifying the inflammatory response during OA progression [39,40], this study measured mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels to evaluate the changes in mitochondrial function. Specifically, TMRM staining was used as an indicator of ΔΨm, and MitoSOX staining was used to assess mitochondrial superoxide/mtROS levels. The results indicated that both PMXene and MS@PMXene alone could increase mitochondrial membrane potential, while the ROS-responsive property imparted by the TK bond maximized the restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 4A and B). As expected, MS@PMXene-TK markedly decreased mitochondrial ROS levels, lowering mean fluorescence intensity to 1.15-fold versus 3.05-, 2.17-, and 1.64-fold increases observed in the IL-1β, PMXene, and MS@PMXene groups, respectively (Fig. 4C and D). Consistently, γH2AX western blotting further showed that MS@PMXene-TK alleviated IL-1β- and H2O2-induced oxidative DNA damage in chondrocytes (Fig. S10). Mitophagy, as a protective mechanism, eliminates damaged mitochondria to prevent further cellular injury. However, mitophagy may be suppressed in OA, which suppresses timely removal of impaired mitochondria and further exacerbates chondrocyte damage and inflammatory response [41]. Therefore, mitophagy-related events were evaluated by quantifying mitoTracker/lysoTracker colocalization, where MitoTracker/LysoTracker colocalization, where LysoTracker labels acidic organelles (primarily lysosomes) and its overlap with MitoTracker reflects mitochondria delivery to lysosomal compartments. IL-1β exposure impaired mitophagosome formation relative to the control. In contrast, MS@PMXene-TK markedly enhanced mitophagosome biogenesis compared with both PMXene and MS@PMXene treatments in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes (Fig. 4E). Simultaneously, Western blot analysis showed that MS@PMXene-TK restored the expression of key mitophagy-related proteins BNIP3, PINK1, and Parkin and reduced the level of the autophagic substrate p62 under IL-1β stimulation, suggesting that MS@PMXene-TK most effectively improves mitophagy (Fig. 4F). In addition, the MS@PMXene-TK group displayed higher SOD1, GPx1, CAT, and NRF2 expression than the PMXene and MS@PMXene groups, indicating that the ROS-responsive property endowed by the TK linkage efficiently enhanced the cellular antioxidant defense system (Fig. 4G–J). Notably, Cat expression in the MS@PMXene group was lower than that in the PMXene group, which may reflect a distinct cellular feedback response after SPPEPS delivery. While PMXene mainly provides exogenous antioxidant protection, SPPEPS may preferentially contribute to chondrocyte anabolic regulation, and its incorporation may also partially affect the exposure of PMXene-associated catalytic sites, thereby affecting the endogenous expression of antioxidant enzymes such as Cat. In contrast, the PEG-TK linkage in MS@PMXene-TK improved nanoparticle stability and enabled ROS-responsive activation under oxidative stress, thereby enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant regulation and markedly increasing CAT expression and enzymatic activity. Consistently, SOD and CAT activity assays further confirmed enhanced antioxidant capacity in the MS@PMXene-TK group (Fig. S12). In addition, SA-β-Gal staining showed that MS@PMXene-TK reduced IL-1β-induced chondrocyte senescence, and qPCR analysis further confirmed the downregulation of SASP markers Il6 and Ccl2 (Fig. S13). These findings suggest that MS@PMXene-based treatment involves coordinated regulation of chondrocyte anabolism and oxidative stress responses, and inflammatory senescence, rather than a simple additive antioxidant effect. It is noteworthy that disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis (e.g. mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy) is the primary contributor to drug resistance, particularly evident in the study of cancer and neurodegenerative diseases [42,43]. These adaptive mitochondrial stress responses enhance plasticity but simultaneously disrupt cellular functions, diminishing drug responsiveness and regenerative potential. In fact, mitochondrial dysfunction-mediated excessive ROS generation is implicated in cartilage regeneration and compromises OA therapeutic effectiveness [44]. Strategies that preserve mitochondrial homeostasis and reduce local oxidative stress have been shown to improve cellular responsiveness, reverse drug resistance, and enhance therapeutic outcomes across various disease models [45,46]. Our results demonstrated that MS@PMXene-TK nanozymes resisted OA-mediated oxidative stress in chondrocytes by targeted mitochondrial ROS clearance, mitochondrial quality control, and enhancing intracellular antioxidant defenses. This explains the best chondrogenic effect demonstrated by SPPEPS in the MS@PMXene-TK group.

MS@PMXene-TK induced mitophagy contribute to improved chondrocyte function under inflammation

To determine whether the restoration of chondrogenic function by MS@PMXene-TK depends on mitophagy activation, Mdivi-1, a mitophagy inhibitor, was added in the NP + Mdivi-1 group. In contrast to NP group, immunofluorescence and Safranin O staining revealed an inverted trend in NP + Mdivi-1 group, with MS@PMXene-TK treatment augmenting the expression of chondrogenic-related markers, while addition of Mdivi-1 curtailed biosynthesis of GAG and type II collagen (Fig. 5A–D). Protein expression of COL2A1, SOX9 and MMP13 were quantified using Western blot. The NP + Mdivi-1 group contained more MMP13 and less COL2A1 and SOX9 than those in the NP group, indicating that the inhibition of mitophagy significantly limited chondrogenic activities induced by MS@PMXene-TK (Fig. 5E and F). In fact, evidence of impaired mitophagy in OA chondrocytes has been provided, which exacerbates chondrocyte degeneration [47]. Novel therapeutic approach with promoted mitophagy and reversed mitochondrial dysfunction effectively modulate the microenvironment of oxidative stress in OA [48,49]. To elucidate the mechanisms and associated biological events underlying chondrocyte responses to the mitochondria-targeting nanozymes, in-depth mRNA expression profiling was performed (Fig. S14). Heatmap analysis of mitochondrial-related genes showed that expression of mitophagy component (Pink1, Prkn, Fundc1, Optn) was suppressed in the IL-1β group and restored in the MS@PMXene-TK group, which are consistent with previous results in this study (Fig. 5G). The overlap between genes downregulated by IL-1β versus the Control group and genes upregulated by MS@PMXene-TK versus the IL-1β group was identified as a set of reversely regulated genes, from which the top 20 representative genes are shown in Fig. 5H. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were subsequently performed on the overlap genes. Eight pathways showed significant enrichment in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis, with particular relevance to mitochondrial homeostasis observed in the calcium signaling and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways (Fig. 5I). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed predominant associations with mitophagy and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-related processes, including DNA damage response, phagocytic vesicle, and oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 5J). This study proposes that alterations in endogenous cytosolic DNA may underlie the reduced inflammatory response observed with MS@PMXene-TK treatment. The cGAS/STING axis functions as a key sensor and downstream effector of inflammation initiated by mitochondrial DNA release [50]. The sequencing results showed that the MS@PMXene-TK group exhibited low levels of cGAS/STING mediator and downstream IRF3 target (Fig. 5K). Consistently, GSEA revealed that the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, RIG-I-like receptor signaling, and JAK–STAT signaling were significantly attenuated relative to the IL-1β group (Fig. 5L), while broader inflammatory programs including IL-17, TNF, and NOD-like receptor signaling were also reduced (Fig. S15). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the mitophagy induced by MS@PMXene-TK may inhibit inflammation by reducing mtDNA-dependent activation of the intracellular cGAS/STING pathway, thereby improving chondrocyte function.

MS@PMXene-TK induced mitophagy curtails cytosolic mtDNA dependent activation of cGAS/STING inflammation

Located on the inner membrane of mitochondria, mPTP participates in the release of mtDNA through its formation and opening, thereby triggering inflammatory responses [[51], [52], [53]]. We detected the mPTP of chondrocytes in each group and showed that MS@PMXene-TK treatment significantly improved the mPTP opening induced by IL-1β (Fig. 6A). However, after intervention with Mdivi-1, the closure effect of nanozymes on mPTP was reversed (Fig. 6A). Considering that mitophagy inhibition facilitates mPTP opening, this study assessed whether mtDNA escapes into the cytosol under such conditions. Cytosolic mtDNA levels were quantified by qPCR, revealing that mitophagy deficiency increases the abundance of mitochondrial DNA in the cytoplasm of chondrocytes (Fig. 6B). Given that NP-40-based cytosolic fractionation may not completely eliminate mitochondrial contamination, the possibility of mitochondrial carryover cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, fluorescent double staining of dsDNA and Tomm20 was further used to visualize intracellular mtDNA leakage, showing that MS@PMXene-TK treatment markedly reduced mtDNA leakage, but this effect was attenuated by Mdivi-1 treatment (Fig. 6C and D). Mitophagy impairment can result in the release of mitochondrial DNA into the cytoplasm. Once in the cytosol, mtDNA is recognized by intracellular sensors such as cGAS, leading to activation of cGAS and the downstream STING pathway [54]. Western blot analysis confirmed that Mdivi-1 treatment induces cGAS/STING pathway activation (Fig. 6E and F). Consistently, ISG15 and IFIT1 expression further supported the suppression of cGAS/STING-related downstream inflammatory signaling by MS@PMXene-TK (Fig. S16). In conditions like Alzheimer's disease, kidney disease, and alcoholic hepatitis, suppression of mitophagy leads to the accumulation of cytoplasmic mtDNA, which ultimately result in ultrastructural, geometric and functional anomalies in the affected organs [[55], [56], [57]]. With the aim of substantiating this hypothesis, Mdivi-1 was used to inhibit the MS@PMXene-TK activated mitophagy in inflammatory chondrocytes. Subsequently, siRNA was introduced to the IL-1β + NP + Mdivi-1 group to impair STING expression within chondrocytes to determine whether STING inhibition could rectify the decline in chondrogenic function (Fig. 6G and H). Notably, the treatment with Mdivi-1 evinced a decline in expression of chondrogenic protein, whereas the Mdivi-1 + siRNA cohort displayed partial restoration of chondrogenic protein expression (Fig. 6I and J). Furthermore, the results of COL2A1 and Safranin O staining also exhibited similar trends (Fig. 6K and L). These findings indicate that inhibiting the intracellular inflammation and immune response promoted by activated cGAS/STING pathway can partially improve chondrocyte function affected by mitophagy. In keeping with the transcript data, our findings indicate that during the progression of OA, the impaired mitophagy and increased mitochondrial permeability led to mtDNA leakage, which activates the cGAS/STING pathway and triggers inflammation. Increasing evidence has shown that the activation of cGAS/STING pathway plays a pivotal role in the progression of diseases such as OA and intervertebral disc degeneration, as it promotes intracellular inflammation and immune responses, thereby releasing inflammatory factors and exacerbating inflammatory reactions [10,58]. In the treatment of OA, however, limited therapeutic designs have been reported targeting the cGAS/STING pathway till now. In this study, MS@PMXene-TK was constructed aiming to improve mitochondrial homeostasis, enhance mitophagy, and subsequently curtail cytosolic mtDNA-dependent activation of cGAS/STING inflammation, which effectively mitigated the adverse effects of OA on chondrocytes. Therefore, cGAS/STING suppression is more likely a downstream effect of MS@PMXene-TK-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis improvement, rather than an independent primary target. The partial rescue of chondrogenic markers by STING knockdown under mitophagy inhibition further supports cGAS/STING signaling as an important downstream mediator linking impaired mitophagy to chondrocyte dysfunction.

The effects of MS@PMXene-TK treatment on macrophage inflammatory phenotype

Accumulating evidence suggests that macrophage-dominated innate immunity plays a vital role in the symptomatic and structural progression of OA [59,60]. Lowering the M1/M2 ratio has been shown to slow local inflammation and bone defect progression [37]. Because excessive ROS promotes macrophage inflammatory activation and M1-like phenotypic changes [61], this study examined the effect of MS@PMXene-TK on macrophage phenotype-associated markers under OA-like conditions. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that IL-1β treatment markedly increased IL-1β and IL-6 expression. PMXene and MS@PMXene treatments induced only modest elevations in anti-inflammatory markers IL-10 and TGF-β. In contrast, proinflammatory factors decreased, and anti-inflammatory factors increased significantly in the MS@PMXene-TK group (Fig. 7A and B). Immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis demonstrated elevated expression of M1-associated markers iNOS and CCR7 and decreased levels of M2-associated markers Arg1 and CD206 in the IL-1β group. Treatment with MS@PMXene-TK significantly downregulated M1 markers and upregulated M2 markers (Fig. 7C–H). Several studies have reported that by inhibiting the cGAS/STING pathway with designed nanomaterials, the polarization of macrophage was induced towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which ultimately improves disease progression through disrupting the positive feedback loop mediated by proinflammatory cytokines [[62], [63], [64]]. In line with these studies, our results showed that MS@PMXene-TK attenuated IL-1β-induced macrophage inflammatory activation and promoted an anti-inflammatory phenotype transition, which may be partly associated with the regulation of inflammation-related signaling pathways such as cGAS/STING. In vivo evaluation of MS@PMXene-TK nanozymes for OA cartilage regeneration

Intra-articular injection enables direct delivery of bioactive molecules to the joint. However, in the absence of controlled delivery systems, drug interventions suffer from poor bioavailability, low stability, and rapid clearance, severely limiting therapeutic efficacy [65]. Environment-responsive, controlled-release drug delivery systems represent a key focus in contemporary OA research. To determine the retention time of MS@PMXene-TK in the joint cavity, ICG-labeled formulations were administered to OA mice, and fluorescence signals in the knee joint were monitored using the IVIS imaging system. Quantitative analysis of the ROI signals showed strong fluorescence in the OA knee in all groups at 12 h post-injection, and the signals were maintained during the early time period. By 48 h post-injection, the fluorescence signals of PMXene and MS@PMXene had markedly decreased, whereas MS@PMXene-TK still exhibited a clear fluorescence signal at 72 h, indicating its prolonged retention in the OA joint (Fig. S17). Based on this intra-articular retention profile, we selected a twice-weekly dosing regimen for subsequent therapeutic studies to sustain local exposure while minimizing unnecessary injection frequency. Importantly, ex vivo imaging of major organs showed no obvious fluorescence distribution in the heart, liver, spleen, or kidney, suggesting limited systemic dissemination and favorable in vivo biosafety of the local administration strategy (Fig. S18).

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of MS@PMXene-TK nanozymes, an ACLT-induced OA mouse model was established. At 2 weeks post-surgery, mice in the PMXene, MS@PMXene, and MS@PMXene-TK groups received intra-articular injections of PMXene, MS@PMXene, and MS@PMXene-TK nanozymes (25 μg mL−1), respectively, whereas Control and OA mice underwent saline injections. At eight weeks after ACLT, mice were sacrificed (Fig. 8A). Three-dimensional reconstructions of the OA group revealed typical osteoarthritic alterations, such as osteophyte formation and joint deformities, whereas knees from the MS@PMXene-TK treated group showed minimal morphological changes (Fig. 8B, upper panels). Coronal views of the medial tibial plateau highlighted extensive subchondral bone loss and sparse trabeculae in OA controls, while MS@PMXene-TK treatment preserved trabecular structure (Fig. 8B, lower panels). Quantitative micro-CT analysis demonstrated modest improvements in bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular number (Tb.N) for the PMXene and MS@PMXene groups, with a significant restoration of BV/TV in the MS@PMXene-TK group (Fig. 8C and D). Furthermore, trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) was markedly reduced following MS@PMXene-TK administration, indicating effective subchondral bone repair and regeneration (Fig. 8E). Histological staining and OARSI scoring revealed similar trends (Fig. 8F–H), and synovitis scoring showed that OA induced pronounced synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory infiltration, which were markedly alleviated by MS@PMXene-TK treatment (Fig. S19A). qPCR analysis of synovial tissues revealed reduced IL-1β and IL-6 expression in the MS@PMXene-TK group compared with OA (Fig. S19B), further supporting its anti-inflammatory efficacy in vivo. In addition, the protective effects of MS@PMXene-TK nanozymes on OA were confirmed by increased COL2A1 expression (Fig. 8I and J) and decreased MMP3 activity (Fig. S20A and B) revealed by immunohistochemistry staining. Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed that MS@PMXene-TK downregulated iNOS expression and upregulated Arg1 expression in OA mice (Fig. S21). Downstream cGAS/STING signaling was evaluated by quantifying pTBK1 in chondrocytes. Both PMXene and MS@PMXene treatments attenuated pathway activation, whereas MS@PMXene-TK produced the most pronounced inhibition (Fig. S20C and D). Finally, gait analysis was performed to assess functional outcomes (Fig. 8K). OA mice exhibited marked gait impairment, characterized by reduced hind-foot stride length and print area, indicating shortened step length and decreased weight bearing. PMXene treatment led to only modest improvement, whereas MS@PMXene-TK substantially restored both parameters toward control levels, suggesting superior functional recovery (Fig. 8L and M). Moreover, beyond the absence of obvious histopathological abnormalities in major organs (Fig. S22), the repeated-dose intra-articular safety evaluation in healthy mice further supported its biosafety. Specifically, routine hematological parameters, including WBC, RBC, HGB, PLT, NEUT, LYMPH, and MONO, showed few differences among groups after treatment (Fig. S23A–G), suggesting that MS@PMXene-TK has little adverse influence on the circulatory system. Serum biochemical analysis further showed that the liver function biomarkers AST and ALT and the kidney function biomarker UREA in the MS@PMXene-TK-treated group were comparable to those in the control/saline groups (Fig. S23H–J), indicating no apparent hepatic or renal toxicity. Together with the reported biocompatibility of PEG-TK [[66], [67], [68]], these findings further support the favorable biosafety profile of MS@PMXene-TK and highlight its potential as a promising nanozyme for OA therapy. Nevertheless, the long-term intra-articular retention, degradation behavior, and clearance pathway of MS@PMXene-TK after joint injection still require further systematic investigation. Overall, this work proposes a coordinated therapeutic strategy that integrates oxidative stress regulation, immune microenvironment remodeling, and cartilage matrix repair for OA treatment.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations should be noted. First, although MS@PMXene-TK showed effective therapeutic activity in vitro and in vivo, the penetration depth of the nanozyme into degenerated cartilage tissue was not systematically characterized. Second, the size distribution of MXene nanosheets may influence cellular uptake, tissue diffusion, and catalytic performance, and more precise control of MXene size should be further optimized. Third, although PDA coating improved the stability and antioxidative performance of MXene, the coating thickness and reaction conditions were not systematically compared. Future studies will focus on optimizing these physicochemical parameters and evaluating their influence on cartilage penetration, mitochondrial targeting, and therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion

In summary, our multifunctional “mitochondrial ROS cruise missile” MS@PMXene-TK nanozyme offer a novel and advanced therapeutic approach for osteoarthritis by simultaneously targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, promoting cartilage regeneration, and modulating the inflammatory microenvironment. By scavenging excessive ROS and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis, the nanozyme mitigates mtDNA leakage and subsequent cGAS/STING activation, while the controlled release of the chondro-inductive SPPEPS peptide supports robust extracellular matrix synthesis. Moreover, the modulation of macrophage polarization further alleviates joint inflammation. These integrated actions not only enhance chondrocyte function in vitro but also preserve joint integrity in vivo, underscoring the promising potential of our platform for clinical translation in OA and other ROS-related inflammatory disorders.

Experimental section

Materials

Ti3AlC2 powder was purchased from 11 Technology Co. Ltd (Jilin, China). Lithium fluoride (LiF), hydrochloric acid (37%), and ethanol (AR, 99.7%) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China).

Synthesis of MXene Ti3C2Tx (MXene) nanosheets

As we previously reported [69], MXene Ti3C2Tx nanosheets were synthesized by selectively etching the Al layer of Ti3AlC2 powder with LiF + HCl. Briefly, 2 g LiF was dissolved in 40 mL HCl solution (9 M), then 2 g Ti3AlC2 powder added into the solution, and stirred continuously at 35 °C for 24 h. The slurry was washed repeatedly with deionized water to achieve a pH at 6-7. Subsequently, ethanol solution was added and sonicated for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 5 min to obtain single layer MXene Ti3C2Tx nanosheets.

MS@PMXene-TK synthesis of nanoparticles

Dopamine hydrochloride solution (2 mg/mL) was added dropwise into 10 mL of MXene aqueous dispersion (2 mg/mL) (MXene: dopamine hydrochloride wt = 10:1), and 2 mg of cartilage inducing peptide SPPEPS (synthesized by standard solid-phase peptide synthesis by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; purity >98%) was also added. After reacting for 12 h at 200 rpm and 30 °C, followed by centrifuging to remove any unreacted monomers the polydopamine modified composite nanoparticles MXene containing SPPEPS (S@PMXene) was obtained. S@PMXene (20 mg) was dispersed in 8 mL of EDC/NHS solution (5 mg/mL) under stirring. For S@PMXene-TK, 3.2 mg of PEG-TK was added and reacted for 1.5 h at room temperature. For S@PMXene-TK/MTP131, both 3.2 mg of PEG-TK and 1 mg of MTP131 were added concurrently. Both products were isolated by dialysis to remove unreacted reagents. Simultaneously, polydopamine modified composite MXene nanoparticleswithout the addition of SPPEPS (PMXene) was prepared, as well as composite MXene nanoparticles grafted with PEG-TK (PMXene-TK) was prepared, and directly adsorb MTP-131 onto PMXene without PEG-TK treatment (M@PMXene) was prepared. The abbreviations and corresponding composition details of all samples used in this study are summarized in Table S1.

OA models

ACLT was performed on the knee joints of 10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, while sham controls underwent joint exposure without ligament transection. Animals were randomized into five groups: Control, ACLT, PMXene, MS@PMXene, and MS@PMXene-TK. Control and ACLT mice received intra-articular saline (50 μL), whereas treatment groups received 50 μL of the respective nanozyme suspension (25 μg mL−1) twice weekly. After eight weeks, all animals were euthanized and knee joints collected for analysis. All procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital (SH9H-2025-A20-1).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism was used for all statistical analyses, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA compared more than two groups, and Student's t-test evaluated differences between two groups with equal variance. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance was indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
