Deep Learning–Derived Body Composition Analysis Predicts Long-Term Mortality After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Chia-Hao Liu, Agata Sularz, Ghasaq Saleh, Panos Korfiatis, Jeremy Collins, Mohamad Alkhouli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between body composition metrics derived from preprocedural computed tomography (CT) angiography and all-cause mortality after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).Patients and MethodsWe included patients who underwent TAVR between September 1, 2011 and November 30, 2023 at a single academic center. Skeletal muscle (SM), subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), visceral adipose tissue (VAT), and intermuscular adipose tissue areas (cm2), as well as SM index (SMI; cm2/m2), were quantified from CT angiography using a validated U-Net–based deep learning model. Associations between each parameter and 3-year all-cause mortality were assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for clinical covariates, with adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) expressed per 1-SD increase.ResultsAmong 2642 patients (median age, 80.0 years [interquartile range, 74.0-85.0 years]; 1572 were men [59.5%]), median follow-up was 2.8 years, and 74.8% survived to 3 years. Lower SM, SAT, VAT, and SMI (analyzed as continuous variables) were independently associated with higher 3-year all-cause mortality (SM: aHR, 0.831; 95% CI, 0.762-0.906; SAT: aHR, 0.847; 95% CI, 0.775-0.926; VAT: aHR, 0.826; 95% CI, 0.762-0.896; SMI: aHR, 0.832; 95% CI, 0.763-0.907; all P≤.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis showed increased mortality risk below threshold values of the following—SM<128 cm2, SAT<161 cm2, VAT<104 cm2, and SMI<41 cm2/m2; sex-specific thresholds were also derived.ConclusionReduced SM and adipose tissue reserves are independently associated with increased mortality after TAVR. Automated CT-derived body composition assessment may improve preoperative risk stratification and guide clinical decision making in TAVR candidates.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become the standard of care for patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, with more than 100,000 procedures performed annually in the United States.1, 2, 3 However, despite high procedural success rates, a substantial proportion of patients undergoing TAVR fail to achieve meaningful clinical benefit from the procedure.4, 5, 6 Frailty and multimorbidity, which are frequently underrepresented in traditional risk scores, have been implicated as key contributors to this variability in post-TAVR outcomes.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Consequently, multiple studies have examined frailty markers as predictors of adverse outcomes and as potential adjuncts to existing risk models to refine patient selection for TAVR. For instance, lower body mass index (BMI, calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by the height in meters squared) has been associated with worse outcomes after TAVR—a manifestation of the so-called obesity paradox.12,13 Similarly, specific body composition measures, including muscle and fat mass indices, have been shown to predict mortality, length of stay, and postprocedural health status after TAVR.14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 This pattern aligns with evidence from other surgical cohorts21, 22, 23 in which diminished visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and skeletal muscle (SM) mass were correlated with reduced functional capacity and adverse clinical outcomes.24, 25, 26, 27

Body composition can be assessed using various techniques, including anthropometry, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and, more recently, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging.28 Deep learning algorithms for automated body composition assessment from CT imaging have shown high accuracy and strong agreement with manual reference standards.29, 30, 31 Computed tomography angiography, routinely performed as part of preprocedural TAVR planning, offers a unique opportunity for comprehensive assessment of body composition.32,33 To date, most studies have examined the impact of individual body composition parameters on short-term mortality and quality of life after TAVR, whereas their influence on longer-term outcomes remains less well characterized. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of artificial intelligence (AI)-derived body composition metrics obtained from preoperative CT angiograms.

Patients and Methods

Patient Cohort

This was a retrospective study of patients who underwent TAVR at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, between September 1, 2011, and November 30, 2023. Baseline characteristics and mortality data were obtained from the electronic health records. Patients were included if a preprocedural CT angiogram was available. Those with missing or incomplete CT studies or intraoperative mortality were excluded. For patients who underwent multiple TAVR procedures, the CT angiography (CTA) preceding the initial (index) procedure was analyzed. At our institution, CTA is performed as part of routine pre-TAVR evaluation using multidetector CT systems. Examinations were acquired predominantly on Siemens scanners (2633/2642; 99.7%). All scans were obtained according to standardized institutional TAVR CTA protocols, reconstructed at thin section thickness, and performed with intravenous iodinated contrast. The primary end point was 3-year all-cause mortality, selected based on the median follow-up duration of the cohort. All-cause mortality was determined using a combination of institutional medical records, the Mayo Clinic registration system, and linkage with external death registries including the National Cardiovascular Data Registry registry. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic’s institutional review board, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Image Analysis

Body composition parameters were derived from preprocedural CTA using a deep learning algorithm. The algorithm was based on a convolutional neural network (U-Net) architecture, as previously described.29 Cross-sectional images at the third lumbar vertebral (L3) level were used to segment and quantify selected tissue compartments (Figure 1), including the SM index (SMI; SM area/height²; cm²/m²), SM area (cm²), subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) area (cm²), visceral adipose tissue (VAT) area (cm²), and intermuscular adipose tissue (IAT) area (cm²). The performance of the automated algorithm for compartmental area quantification has been previously validated and shown to be comparable with that of expert human observers.29,34

Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Pearson χ2 test was used for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Univariable Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed for the primary end point using selected baseline characteristics and CT-derived body composition parameters (SM area, SAT area, VAT area, IAT area, and SMI) to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% CIs. Variables with P<.05 in univariable analysis were included as covariates in multivariable Cox regression models. To minimize multicollinearity, each multivariable model included only 1 CT-derived parameter. Restricted cubic spline curves were generated to visualize the relationship between HRs (y-axis) and each CT-derived parameter (x-axis), with the x-intercept at HR=1 used to determine optimal thresholds. Splines were specified using 4 knots placed at prespecified quantiles of the predictor distribution, consistent with standard recommendations for survival analyses. This approach allowed flexible modeling of nonlinear relationships while minimizing overfitting. Patients were subsequently stratified into 2 groups based on derived thresholds. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0; IBM) and R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) software. Analysis was conducted for the whole cohort as well as stratified by sex.

Results

A total of 2642 patients were included. The median age was 80.0 years (IQR, 74.0-85.0 years), and 59.5% were men. The median follow-up duration was 1031 days. Thirty-day mortality was 1.4% (37/2642), and the overall 3-year survival was 74.8% (1976/2642). The median BMI was 26.5 (IQR, 23.9-34.0). Body composition analysis of the full cohort showed a median SM area of 123.0 cm2 (IQR, 103.0-148.0 cm2), SAT area of 206.0 cm2 (IQR, 149.0-283.0 cm2), VAT area of 202.0 cm2 (IQR, 112.0-307.0 cm2), IAT area of 33.0 cm2 (IQR, 25.0-44.0 cm2), and SMI of 43.5 cm2/m2 (IQR, 38.3-49.8 cm2/m2). All but IAT body composition parameters differed significantly between patients who were alive and those who deceased at the 3-year follow-up (Table 1). In univariable Cox regression analysis, SM area, SAT area, VAT area, IAT area, SMI, age, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke, cerebrovascular disease, chronic lung disease, conduction defects, previous myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, porcelain aorta, chronic dialysis, and previous percutaneous coronary intervention were statistically predictors of all-cause mortality. In contrast, BMI was not associated with mortality in the univariable analysis (Table 2). In the female cohort, univariable analysis identified SM area, SAT area, VAT area, and SMI as statistically predictors of mortality, whereas IAT area, BMI, and age were not significant. Statistically clinical predictors for females also included atrial fibrillation, cerebrovascular accident, chronic lung disease, conduction defects, peripheral arterial disease, porcelain aorta, dialysis, and previous aortic valve procedures (Supplemental Table 3, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Conversely, for the male cohort, all body composition metrics—including SM area, SAT area, VAT area, IAT area, and SMI—were statistically predictors of all-cause mortality. Clinical factors significantly associated with mortality in men also included atrial fibrillation, cerebrovascular disease, chronic lung disease, prior myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, and dialysis status (Supplemental Table 9, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Separate multivariable Cox regression models were constructed for SM area, SAT area, VAT area, IAT area, and SMI, each analyzed as a continuous variable. All parameters except IAT area remained statistically significant independent predictors of all-cause mortality in the multivariable models (Table 3, Table 4, Table 5 and Supplemental Tables 1 and 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). In addition, atrial fibrillation, chronic lung disease, peripheral arterial disease, and chronic dialysis were independently associated with mortality across all models (Table 3, Table 4, Table 5 and Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). In the female cohort, multivariable analysis showed that SM area, SAT area, and VAT area to be independent predictors of all-cause mortality; however, SMI and IAT area were not. Unique independent clinical predictors for females included conduction defects and porcelain aorta, alongside atrial fibrillation, chronic lung disease, and dialysis (Supplemental Tables 4-8, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Conversely, in the male cohort, SM area, SAT area, VAT area, and SMI were confirmed as independent predictors of mortality. Peripheral arterial disease was also a statistically independent predictor of mortality for men (Supplemental Tables 10-14, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Restricted cubic spline analyses were performed for SM area, SAT area, VAT area, and SMI to identify optimal binary thresholds (Figures 2A-D and 3A-D). The derived cutoffs were 128 cm2 for SM area, 161 cm2 for SAT area, 104 cm2 for VAT area, and 41 cm2/m2 for SMI. For an SM area of ≥128 cm2, SAT area of ≥161 cm2, and SMI of ≥41 cm2/m2, the HR for mortality decreased progressively with higher values. In contrast, for a VAT area of ≥104 cm2, the HR initially declined and then gradually increased at higher VAT values, crossing HR=1 at approximately 750 cm2. In sex-stratified analyses, optimal thresholds differed between men and women. In men, the identified cutoffs were 133 cm2 for SM area, 142 cm2 for SAT area, and 43 cm2/m2 for SMI. In women, the corresponding cutoffs were 97 cm2 for SM area and 194 cm2 for SAT area. Sex-specific VAT patterns also differed. In women, for a VAT area of ≥85 cm2, the HR initially declined and then increased at higher values, crossing HR=1 at approximately 297 cm2 (Supplemental Figure 1, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). In contrast, in men, the HR did not decline below baseline until substantially higher VAT levels; for a VAT area of ≥249 cm2, the HR initially declined and then gradually increased, crossing HR = 1 again at approximately 680 cm2 (Supplemental Figure 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Discussion

This study evaluated the long-term prognostic value of multiparametric AI-driven body composition analysis derived from routine pre-TAVR CT angiograms. Our findings highlight 2 key observations. First, SM area, SAT area, VAT area, and SMI obtained from automated CT analysis were independently associated with all-cause mortality after TAVR. In sex-stratified analyses, SMI predicted outcomes in men but not in women. Second, we identified optimal cutoff values for each body composition parameter, which may facilitate risk stratification among patients undergoing TAVR.

With the widespread adoption of TAVR, many patients with aortic stenosis who were previously considered high or prohibitive surgical risk are now able to undergo valve replacement. Nonetheless, concerns have been raised regarding the potential futility of TAVR in certain patients. Previous studies have reported that up to 40% of individuals undergoing TAVR are either deceased or experience no symptomatic improvement 1 year after the procedure.4, 5, 6 Conventional risk models have performed suboptimally in predicting health status after TAVR, motivating ongoing research to identify complementary markers that could enhance risk prediction and clinical decision making. For example, numerous studies have found a strong association between frailty and adverse outcomes after TAVR.35 These studies, however, underscored the challenges of assessing frailty in routine clinical practice. Subsequent efforts have focused on identifying quantitative surrogates of frailty that correlate with clinical outcomes.

Body mass index has been investigated as a predictor of post-TAVR mortality. A meta-analysis of 38 studies including 99,829 patients showed that overweight and obese individuals had lower long-term mortality than those of normal weight. This obesity paradox was observed despite the higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia in the overweight and obese cohorts.13 However, other studies have reported contradictory findings. In a cohort of 1613 Japanese patients, BMI was not statistically associated with mortality. In contrast, lean body mass—defined as body weight minus fat mass—was identified as an independent predictor of mortality in both men and women.36 In our study, BMI was not predictive of mortality (HR, 0.998; 95% CI, 0.995-1.002; P=.140), whereas most body composition parameters were. This finding suggests that BMI may not be an optimal surrogate for frailty or nutritional status because it represents only a gross approximation of body weight without differentiating between muscle, visceral fat, and subcutaneous fat. In contrast, body composition metrics provide more physiologically relevant markers of frailty and nutritional reserve. Nevertheless, their quantification remains technically challenging, and their prognostic relevance in the TAVR population has not been well established.

In patients undergoing TAVR, body composition analysis is highly feasible because CTA is routinely performed for preprocedural planning. Although several studies have shown this feasibility, previous investigations were generally small and heterogeneous and relied primarily on manual tissue quantification performed by the interpreting radiologist—a process that is inherently time consuming (Supplemental Tables 19 and 20, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Using a validated deep learning algorithm, we automatically derived body composition parameters from preprocedural CTA. The prognostic significance of the individual body composition metrics is described further.

Our body composition analysis included several parameters—SM, subcutaneous, visceral, and intermuscular fat areas—quantified at the L3 vertebral level on cross-sectional CT imaging. Although SM area and SMI cannot substitute for functional assessments such as grip strength, these measures are well established markers of muscle atrophy and sarcopenia and have been associated with reduced mobility and increased fall risk. In our study, both SM area and SMI were statistically independent predictors of mortality, with comparable effect sizes (adjusted HR [aHR], 0.831; 95% CI, 0.762-0.906; P<.001, and aHR, 0.832; 95% CI, 0.763-0.907; P<.001, respectively). Consistent with our findings, prior studies have shown that higher SMI is associated with shorter hospital stay37 and improved quality of life after TAVR.14 Similar associations have also been reported when using the psoas muscle area as a surrogate for SM mass (Supplemental Table 19).15,16

Visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissues are distinct compartments, with differing metabolic and cardiovascular implications.38,39 Unlike subcutaneous fat, visceral fat is metabolically active and proinflammatory; its excess is associated with insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and the metabolic syndrome.40,41 Conversely, subcutaneous fat may reflect overall body adiposity and serve as a marker of nutritional reserve, as well as means to assess the effectiveness of weight-loss interventions. Nonetheless, a minimal amount of both visceral and subcutaneous fat is essential for energy storage and organ protection, which may partly explain the higher post-TAVR mortality observed in patients with very low BMI.42 In our study, both subcutaneous and visceral fat areas were statistically independent predictors of the primary outcome, each associated with comparable risk (SAT: aHR, 0.847; 95% CI, 0.775-0.926; VAT: aHR, 0.826; 95% CI, 0.762-0.896; P<.001 for both). Interestingly, the spline curve for VAT showed 2 inflection points, consistent with increased mortality at both extremes of VAT area (<104 and >750 cm2). Consistent with our findings, Mancio et al19 also reported a nonlinear association between VAT and mortality, suggesting that maintaining VAT within a healthy range may be optimal. Sex-stratified analyses found distinct VAT–mortality patterns, with women reaching the VAT-related risk inflection point at approximately 297 cm2, compared with approximately 680 cm2 in men. These observations underscore the need for sex-specific considerations in future studies evaluating adiposity and outcomes after TAVR.

In this contemporary TAVR cohort, our analysis suggested that adipose tissue distribution, rather than total fat burden, was differentially associated with mortality after valve implantation. In univariable analyses, higher IAT was associated with increased mortality, whereas higher VAT and SAT were associated with improved survival. However, after multivariable adjustment accounting for the advanced age and comorbidity burden typical of the TAVR population, the association between IAT and mortality was attenuated and no longer statistically significant, whereas both VAT and SAT remained independently protective. These findings are consistent with previous literature demonstrating that IAT primarily reflects impaired muscle quality and myosteatosis, with its prognostic relevance varying across populations and often modified by age, comorbidity burden, and frailty-related factors, supporting its role as a marker of systemic vulnerability rather than an independent causal driver of risk.43, 44, 45, 46 In contrast, SAT has shown a relatively consistent protective association in TAVR-treated patients, with multiple observational studies and a large systematic review reporting improved survival among patients with greater subcutaneous fat area or volume.47 The relationship between VAT appears more heterogeneous because absolute VAT burden has shown inconsistent associations with mortality in other reports, whereas qualitative measures such as VAT density and relative adipose distribution metrics, including the VAT-to-SAT ratio, have shown more consistent associations with adverse outcomes.15,48,49 Collectively, these findings suggest that adipose tissue quality and relative distribution may provide more prognostic information than absolute fat burden alone.

These results emphasize the need for a multidimensional approach to risk assessment in patients undergoing TAVR, integrating nutritional and functional evaluation.50, 51, 52 The proposed AI-based model allows automated quantification of body composition from routine pre-TAVR imaging, offering an opportunity to enhance risk stratification and optimize procedural selection.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, only baseline BMI and body composition parameters were analyzed; postprocedural changes were not evaluated. Second, several comorbidities—such as atrial fibrillation, chronic lung disease, peripheral arterial disease, and end-stage renal disease—were also associated with adverse outcomes. The nature of their interaction with body composition remains uncertain and warrants further study. Third, we acknowledge that deriving diagnostic thresholds based on HRs may overestimate clinical utility. Accordingly, these thresholds should be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than prescriptive for clinical decision making. Fourth, the use of all-cause mortality likely reflects both cardiovascular and noncardiovascular vulnerability associated with sarcopenia and adipose depletion, particularly in an elderly TAVR population. Cause-specific mortality, however, was not uniformly available for this analysis. Finally, we recognize that formal frailty and nutritional assessments could provide important mechanistic insight into the observed associations with body composition. Unfortunately, standardized measures such as gait speed, grip strength, or validated frailty indices were not systematically collected across the full study period. Finally, this was a retrospective, single-center analysis, and application of the AI algorithm has so far been limited to our institution. External validation in larger, multicenter cohorts will be essential to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion

In this large cohort of consecutive patients undergoing TAVR at a tertiary center, AI-derived body composition metrics obtained from routine preoperative CT scans were independently associated with all-cause mortality. Future studies should further explore the relationship between body composition and frailty and assess whether nutritional or physical rehabilitation interventions can improve long-term outcomes.
