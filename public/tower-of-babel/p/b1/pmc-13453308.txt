From evidence to practice: factors influencing the implementation of cord blood treatment for cerebral palsy in the United States
Authors: Madison C B Paton, Joanne Kurtzberg, Jessica Sun, Sarah Reedman, Remy Blatch-Williams, Michael Fahey, Iona Novak, Megan Finch-Edmondson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsCord blood infusion is an emerging treatment for cerebral palsy (CP) currently offered in the United States through clinical trials and an expanded access pathway. This study aimed to understand the barriers and facilitators influencing implementation of cord blood treatment for CP, to help inform future implementation efforts.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with US professionals involved in the cord blood treatment program for CP at Duke University and a collaborator site. Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFrom 16 interviews, 5 themes were identified capturing patterned barriers and enablers. Key facilitators included boundary-spanning clinical leadership, interprofessional coordination, dedicated resourcing, responsiveness to community demand, an established safety profile, and a strong research culture. Barriers included the need for cross-disciplinary expertise, uncertainty regarding efficacy evidence, funding and regulatory constraints, and system-level challenges. Participants described that many barriers were overcome to establish the program, while acknowledging that others may persist and are context dependent.ConclusionsFindings highlight that implementation of cord blood treatment for CP is feasible within specialized settings but contingent on local infrastructure, governance, and workforce capacity. Identified facilitators align with established implementation strategies and offer transferable insights, while underscoring the need for regulatory approval, ethical oversight, and system-level supports to enable equitable and sustainable access to emerging regenerative therapies for CP.

This study provides real-world insights into how cord blood treatment for cerebral palsy (CP) can be implemented within a clinical and research environment. By identifying key enablers such as boundary-spanning leadership, dedicated resourcing, research culture, and an established safety profile, as well as barriers related to broader clinician unfamiliarity, resourcing and system-level constraints, findings illuminate the practical realities of translating emerging cell therapies into clinical care. The work highlights that while implementation is feasible, it is context-dependent, offering valuable guidance for future programs and informing pathways toward equitable, sustainable access to regenerative therapies for CP.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a physical disability that affects movement and posture.1 It is the most common lifelong physical disability, resulting from injury and/or genetic changes to the developing brain, with evidence of inflammatory underpinnings that persist into adulthood.2 Umbilical cord blood is a mixed-cell biological that has been under investigation as a treatment for CP for 2 decades. A recent individual participant data meta-analysis combining 7 randomized controlled trials provides moderate-certainty evidence that cord blood treatment is safe and improves gross motor skills for children with CP.3 Best responders are identified as younger than 5 years of age, with ambulant CP, receiving higher doses of cord blood.3 However, cord blood is not an approved treatment for CP in any country.

At Duke University in the United States, cord blood treatment for CP is available through a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-authorized expanded access program.4 Concurrently, the team conducts clinical trials on the pathway to regulatory approval. This unique scenario of providing treatment access while generating necessary data for treatment approval and clinical adoption of cord blood infusion for CP presents a valuable opportunity to examine real-world implementation of this treatment.

The aim of this study was to understand the barriers and facilitators of implementing cord blood treatment for CP via a US expanded access and research program, with the goal of informing other countries and sites seeking to potentially implement cell therapies for CP in the future.

Methods

Design

A descriptive qualitative study was conducted using semi‑structured interviews with professionals involved in delivering cord blood treatment for CP within a US program. A qualitative approach was selected to ensure that the dynamic, multi-level factors influencing implementation success could be explored thoroughly from the perspective of those directly involved, including practical, organizational, cultural, ethical and financial factors.5 Given the small, highly specialized population with direct implementation experience, qualitative interviews were considered better suited than survey methods for generating an in-depth understanding of implementation determinants. In this study, “program” refers to cord blood infusions for CP provided via clinical trials and expanded access at Duke (North Carolina) or their collaborator site (MD Anderson, Texas).

Ethics

Ethics approval was granted by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (project number: 2024/HE000702). Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to interviews. Study methods followed the Recommended Reporting for Qualitative Studies6 and chronicled using the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research Checklist (COREQ,7 Supplementary Material 1).

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed by M.C.B.P., S.R., and M.F.E. (Supplementary Material 2). The guide was informed by the updated consolidated framework for implementation research (CFIR),8 whereby questions were mapped to individual CFIR domains to ensure adequate coverage of known implementation determinants. The CFIR was selected as it can be applied across multiple stages of implementation and provides a structured approach to identifying barriers and facilitators across domains determined to be relevant to the program. Interviews were estimated to take 90 minutes. The guide was field-tested in the first interview with no amendments made as questions were well understood and elicited relevant data.

Participants and sample size

Purposive sampling was used to recruit US-based professionals with direct experience delivering the program at Duke or MD Anderson, the only sites currently offering both clinical trials and regulated access to cord blood infusion for CP at the time. The decision to limit recruitment to those with direct experience in cord blood infusion implementation was purposeful so that real barriers and enablers to implementation could be examined, rather than more speculative ones. Potential participants included site leaders, research coordinators, nurses, allied health professionals, and laboratory staff. Email invitations were sent by the chief investigator (M.C.B.P.), and potential participants completed a screening questionnaire to collect demographics, details about their experience in the program and confirm eligibility before proceeding to interview (Supplementary Material 3). Participation or nonparticipation had no impact on employment or professional relationships. Confidentiality was emphasized, data were de-identified, and findings are reported in aggregate. Given the small, bounded population, sample size was guided by information power rather than saturation. A target of 10-15 interviews was set a priori, with sampling continuing until adequate coverage of professional roles, sites, and implementation processes was achieved and no substantively new concepts relevant to the study aims emerged.

Interview procedure and data analysis

M.C.B.P. conducted all interviews with participants one-on-one either in-person or online, with audio and visual recording via Zoom. To reduce social desirability bias, participants were explicitly invited to discuss challenges, failures, and unintended consequences as well as successes, and neutral, non-leading prompts were used throughout. M.C.B.P.’s interviewer profile and relationships with interviewees are described below. Interviews were transcribed verbatim, de-identified, and imported into NVivo (Version 15, University of Sydney License). M.C.B.P. made notes during the interviews to summarize the main topics raised. Participants could amend or clarify any statements prior to finishing their interview.9

Data were analyzed using an inductive, codebook‑style reflexive, thematic analysis within a pragmatic, experiential orientation. An initial set of codes was generated from close reading of a subset of transcripts by M.C.B.P., which was validated independently by 2 authors (M.C.B.P. and M.F.E.) who met to discuss, refine, and define codes. The resulting codebook was reviewed by subject matter experts (J.K. and J.S.), with allowance for refinement through discussion if any new concepts emerged as additional transcripts were coded. Remaining transcripts were coded by M.C.B.P. and, once coding was complete, themes were developed through patterning across the data and clustering codes with shared meaning. In an exploratory step to support interpretive sense-making, codes were examined in relation to CFIR domains. The use of CFIR to structure data collection did not imply a priori categorization of findings; themes were still derived inductively from participant accounts and were not constrained by CFIR constructs.

Trustworthiness, reflexivity, and bias mitigation

M.C.B.P. is an Australian national, female and Senior Research Fellow at Cerebral Palsy Alliance Research Institute with >13 years’ research experience in cord blood and CP (PhD, BSc). M.C.B.P. had prior professional relationships with some interviewees, including the leaders of the program as research collaborators. During the study, M.C.B.P. was a visiting Fulbright Scholar at Duke University. This position created a degree of insider-outsider duality. Existing relationships may have facilitated rapport and openness during interviews through established trust but may also have influenced participants’ willingness to discuss challenges or may have led certain assumptions about the program to remain unexplored. Conversely, M.C.B.P.’s lack of a clinical or leadership role within the program, and her position as an external researcher, may have enabled questioning of practices that could otherwise be taken for granted. These positional influences are acknowledged as potentially shaping both data generation and interpretation. All interviewees reported and declared their relationship to the program, as well as their background and training. Two program leaders (J.K. and J.S.) were included as both interview participants and co‑authors. This dual role was considered necessary due to the small, specialized population, and that their experiential knowledge was considered essential for addressing the study aims. To minimize confirmation bias, explicit analytic firewalls were established: program leader participants were not involved in initial coding and did not have final authority over theme development. Throughout data collection and analysis, M.C.B.P. maintained reflexive awareness of these positional influences through notes, analytic discussions with coauthors, and ongoing consideration of how prior knowledge, relationships, and professional interests in cord blood and CP may have shaped interpretation of the data. Moreover, coding decisions and emergent themes were discussed with coauthors who had no affiliation with the program.

Transferability

This study was designed to characterize implementation determinants within a highly specific programmatic context rather than to establish effectiveness or general feasibility. Transferability is supported through description of context, roles, and processes, allowing readers to judge relevance to other settings.

Clinical leadership and collaboration to deliver on community priorities

Participants consistently described the presence of formal program leads—referred to here as Clinical Champions—as a central facilitator of the program. Leadership was described as enabling coordination across disciplines that do not traditionally intersect, including pediatric neurology and hematology/oncology. Clinical Champions were portrayed as enabling access to cord blood through advocacy, resource mobilization, and sustained institutional engagement in a clinical environment primarily oriented toward oncology care. Participants emphasized that such leadership was not readily replicable, noting skepticism within the broader hematology/oncology community regarding the efficacy of cord blood for CP. This tension between innovation and disciplinary conservatism emerged as a key barrier to broader implementation, with participants anticipating that sites lacking individuals with hybrid expertise would struggle to establish similar programs.

Coordination across diverse internal and external teams was also identified as a facilitator. Participants described how program success relied on clearly delineated roles, access to training, and dedicated coordination staff. Clinical Research Coordinators and Nurse Practitioners were positioned as pivotal to day-to-day functioning, acting as organizational anchors that sustained continuity across clinical, laboratory, and research processes. The availability of program-dedicated resources, rather than reliance on shared institutional infrastructure, was positioned as enabling operational independence and resilience.

A further driver described by participants was the shared recognition of unmet community demand for novel CP treatments. Commitment to families was framed as a core cultural value, with participants articulating motivations grounded in compassion, innovation, and a desire to “do good.” These moral commitments coexisted with awareness of uncertainty, reflecting a value-driven rather than evidence-settled rationale for program continuation.

Procedures, setting, and cost of cord blood infusion for CP

Participants described the technical components of cord blood infusion as familiar and routine for clinicians experienced in infusion medicine and cryopreserved products. However, this apparent procedural simplicity contrasted with accounts of substantial upstream and downstream complexity, encompassing eligibility screening, human leukocyte antigen matching, coordination of cord blood units, and accommodation of children with diverse medical needs. Participants emphasized that program complexity was managed through iterative process optimization, including adoption of electronic data capture and standardized billing. These accounts highlighted implementation as an evolving practice rather than a static protocol, with efficiencies developing over time through experiential learning. Some participants anticipated that regulatory approval could further streamline processes, although such expectations were speculative.

Access to outpatient beds within the hospital day unit was described as both a facilitator and a constraint. While existing infusion infrastructure enabled delivery, physical space limitations and staffing pressures periodically constrained capacity. Participants described mitigation strategies, including employing program-specific nursing staff, which helped to enhance continuity and efficiency but also emphasized the resource-intensive nature of the program.

The $15 000 USD out-of-pocket cost for expanded access was a recurring point of moral and practical concern. Participants recognized that while hospital costs were perceived as modest for example, when contrasted to the high cost of other CP interventions (eg, orthopedic surgery), the direct financial burden for families could be substantial, and beyond what many could afford. This juxtaposition of “affordability” was evidenced by the contradictory coding of cost-related sentiments as both barriers and facilitators in this analysis. Moreover, the treatment cost does not include additional “real” costs for families such as travel, lodging and time off work. While all CP interventions carry some cost and burden for families, existing limited access mechanisms for cord blood infusion exacerbate some of these additional costs (eg, by requiring long-distance travel). Accounts reflected moral tension rather than consensus, with some expressing discomfort about equity implications while simultaneously expressing hope that future insurance coverage might reduce financial barriers. Cord blood was frequently framed as “complementary” to standard CP therapies, with participants weighing potential benefits against time, effort, and cost, for example, the time investment for a cord blood infusion may be less than for other therapies.

Evidence of safety and efficacy of an unapproved treatment and looking to the future

Participants described perceived safety of cord blood infusion as facilitating program continuation, emphasizing tolerability and absence of serious adverse events within their experience. However, these safety narratives were juxtaposed with acknowledgment of evidentiary uncertainty, particularly the absence of definitive Phase 3 trial data establishing efficacy. Participants articulated strong support for rigorous efficacy trials, recognizing these as a prerequisite for adoption into standard care. At the same time, many endorsed continued access via expanded access pathways while such evidence is generated. Limited awareness of existing research and mechanisms of action of cord blood infusion for CP among external clinicians and the broader community was described as hindering program legitimacy. Participants identified communication of the evolving evidence base as central to addressing skepticism and supporting informed engagement with the program.

Trust and reputation established through research and experience

Participants described Duke’s longstanding involvement in cord blood research and treatment for CP as foundational to program credibility. This included references to preclinical research elucidating mechanisms of action and identification of active cellular components,10 as well as clinical trials and real-world treatment experience. Trust was described as accruing over time through demonstrated safety, experiential knowledge, and accumulated “know-how.” Participants linked this trust to practical outcomes, including fundraising success, process optimization, and the ability to navigate institutional and regulatory challenges.

Resourcing, infrastructure, and commercial appeal

Funding emerged as a pervasive constraint shaping program sustainability and future growth. Participants described how past philanthropic and research funding enabled program establishment, while ongoing funding uncertainty limited capacity to pursue large-scale trials. Phase 3 trials were described as particularly resource-intensive, requiring substantial financial investment, regulatory sponsorship, and workforce capacity.

Participants perceived limited commercial appeal as a structural barrier, citing CP’s relative rarity and the pediatric context as disincentives for industry investment. These accounts framed implementation as occurring within a misalignment between societal value and market logic. Beyond funding and industry involvement, infrastructure facilitators included access to an on-site public cord blood bank and proximity of the stem cell laboratory, which enabled streamlined product handling and preparation.

Reflections on implementation at other sites and opportunity for change

Participants offered speculative reflections on whether similar programs could be established elsewhere. While some expressed confidence that cord blood infusion could be delivered in other settings, these views were often accompanied by recognition of site-specific constraints and unknown barriers. Participants suggested that broader adoption could reduce travel burden for families and help meet community demand, as well as presenting an opportunity to build new ways of working, while cautioning that contextual challenges—organizational, regulatory, and cultural—may only become visible during implementation. These accounts underscored implementation as contingent and locally negotiated rather than readily transferable.

Discussion

This study examined barriers and facilitators to implementing cord blood treatment for individuals with CP through expanded access and research, drawing on interviews with professionals at Duke University and a collaborating site. Analysis highlighted the central role of multidisciplinary Clinical Champions in initiating and sustaining the program by bridging pediatric neurology and hematology/oncology. Participants described these leaders as fostering a treatment-positive culture grounded in translational research and responsiveness to family priorities. Additional enablers included interprofessional teaming, dedicated coordination roles, and the relative procedural familiarity of cord blood infusion, despite substantial upstream and downstream complexity required for program delivery. Participants also emphasized the importance of the program’s long-standing research history and reputation, built over 2 decades of safety and translational outputs. While some participants speculated that similar programs could be established elsewhere, they consistently emphasized that transferability would be highly contingent on local infrastructure, regulation, workforce capacity, and institutional context.

Interpreting the barriers

The barriers identified align closely with established implementation frameworks, including those summarized in a review by Cochrane et al.11 From an implementation science perspective, these barriers reflect predictable determinants across clinician knowledge and experience, professional identity, resource availability, and system-level constraints. Cognitive and attitudinal barriers—such as limited familiarity with cord blood outside transplantation contexts and uncertainty regarding efficacy in CP—were prominent, reflecting the novelty of this intervention relative to traditional neurological care paradigms.

Resource constraints were also salient, including challenges securing large-scale research funding, administrative and regulatory demands associated with expanded access programs, and limited commercial incentives. System-level barriers included lack of treatment awareness, inadequate insurance coverage, and competition for clinical space within busy tertiary pediatric hospitals. Importantly, participants framed these barriers as familiar and therefore amenable to established implementation strategies rather than unique to cord blood treatment for CP. Leadership emerged as a key strategy, enabling boundary-spanning across specialties, mobilizing institutional support, and supporting navigation of regulatory and organizational hurdles. At the same time, participants emphasized that many challenges—particularly those related to funding, equity, and system capacity—may persist even with regulatory approval of the intervention. Collectively, these findings illustrate the intersecting challenges of translating promising biologics into routine care, even within highly resourced academic settings.

Understanding the enablers

Several enablers were identified reflecting implementation strategies with established empirical support. Central among these was the presence of research-active clinicians (Clinical Champions) whose dual roles as investigators and treating clinicians aligned scientific uncertainty with clinical workflow and institutional priorities.12 Further, the established safety profile of cord blood treatment supported program delivery and access, despite acknowledged uncertainty regarding treatment efficacy. The program further benefited from a multifaceted organizational approach, including dedicated nursing and coordination staff who addressed practical constraints such as bed availability and infusion scheduling. These strategies align with prior research emphasizing the importance of operational leadership in overcoming site-specific barriers to intervention delivery.13

Community engagement was another critical enabler. Participants described how demand from families influenced program development and responsiveness, consistent with evidence that community involvement in health service design and research enhances relevance and outcomes.14 However, this demand also introduced ethical tensions related to the management of treatment expectations and equity of access, underscoring the need for careful governance during early implementation. This is particularly important for a therapeutic that has not yet been proven to work for children with CP, yet patients and their families are often driven by a sense of hope that this type of treatment will provide benefit/s.15 Clinicians caring for children with CP therefore play an important role in counseling families about treatment decision making, helping them to weigh up the research evidence supporting cord blood infusion for CP, existing access pathways, costs and expectations.

Progressing cord blood infusion for CP

The aim of this study was to better understand the barriers and facilitators of cord blood infusion for CP to help inform future implementation efforts. Clearly, the absence of Phase 3 confirmatory trial data is the most significant barrier to widespread clinical acceptability and adoption into standard care; a need which has been voiced in the literature.3,16 Indeed, participants raised that many of the barriers highlighted in this study would likely be resolved upon regulatory approval of cord blood for CP. However, opportunities for large-scale research funding in cell therapies, particularly cord blood, which lacks a traditional commercial Sponsor or pharmaceutical backing, are limited. Moreover, CP being a pediatric and relatively rare condition (eg, compared to heart disease or neurodegenerative conditions), additionally constrains investment and innovation, as echoed by participants in this study. This brings into question the role and implications of an expanded access program plus research structure in supporting future approval. Participants were clear that expanded access is not a long-term solution and that progressing cord blood infusion to US FDA-approval and insurance reimbursement remains a core goal. Specifically for cord blood infusion for CP, expanded access can enable maintenance of clinical know-how and system readiness for implementation, allowing optimization of processes, and continued accumulation of safety data which can support future regulatory submissions. Expanded Access is also only permissible when enrollment in a clinical trial is not possible, meaning that this pathway does not jeopardize the conduct of active clinical trials to generate further research evidence. Therefore, expanded access should not be viewed as a substitute, or impediment, for a Phase 3 trial, rather it can offer an alternate access pathway while the necessary evidence is generated.

While the cost-recovery model enabled for the Expanded Access Program has facilitated continued treatment access in the absence of treatment approval (ie, via US FDA clearance) and insurance reimbursement, the substantial out-of-pocket expense for families raises important equity considerations. In an era of emerging ultra-expensive treatments for various conditions (eg, gene and chimeric antigen receptor [CAR]-T cell therapies), healthcare systems are balancing complex economical and ethical realities. In their study, Bomhof et al. discuss several ethical dilemmas relating to whether patients should be allowed to pay out of pocket, using private funds, for medical treatments.17 The authors cite the medical-ethical principles of beneficence and autonomy in support of pay-for-treatment, however they acknowledge that this leads to unequal access, undermining the values of equality and solidarity as foundations of a universal healthcare system. A key difference in the case of cord blood infusion for CP is that this treatment is not yet approved for widespread access. Precisely how different healthcare systems may fund and support access to cell therapies (like cord blood infusion) in the future, remains to be determined.

Challenges, opportunities, and transferrable insights

Unlike many of the conventional therapies for CP, such as rehabilitation training and orthopedic surgery, the application of cellular therapies is substantially more complex and accompanied by several challenges. In their 2015 study, Dodson and Levine examined the development of several cell therapies, identifying various barriers for the development and oversight of this new generation of therapeutics.18 Their research, and that from others,19,20 demonstrates that existing drug development frameworks are not fit-for-purpose for regenerative medicine approaches. Moreover, these therapies will likely need different implementation models, with greater emphasis on clinical, regulatory, manufacturing and reimbursement requirements. Nevertheless, of the various cell therapies being investigated for clinical use, cord blood presents several advantages. Cord blood is already an approved product for a range of hematological conditions, with cord blood transplants occurring in specialized hospital-based transplant centers worldwide (>40 000 cord blood transplants performed), and it is being actively researched for diverse indications.21

Cord blood’s long standing clinical use means that there already exists substantial infrastructure, know-how and international standards to support the collection, storage, matching, thawing, handling and infusion of this product. Consistent with this, participants indicated that cord blood infusion for CP is “pretty standard,” being familiar for those with experience in handling blood products. This means that it may be technically feasible that cord blood infusion for CP could occur anywhere that cord blood and bone marrow transplants are currently done. Furthermore, many of the barriers and enablers identified in this study are consistent with known implementation determinants, especially for complex health interventions. However, it remains that clinical trials and expanded access to cord blood infusion for CP have only been implemented at a handful of sites internationally. Thus, it is likely the combination of key enablers present at Duke University and MD Anderson outlined in this study that has contributed to the success of the program.

When considering whether it is possible to replicate Duke’s model of cord blood infusion at external sites, some implementation enablers are easier to realize than others. From a practical standpoint, there exist innovative models of de-centralized manufacturing for complex and costly CAR-T products, demonstrating the potential for improved access and patient experience.22 Research has demonstrated that clinician attitudinal barriers can be overcome by offering resources and education to support, clinician confidence and willingness to refer to cell therapy trials for CP.23 This study also identified trust and institutional reputation as a key implementation facilitator. Trust and trustworthiness are essential in clinical research and healthcare, leading to improved research and patient outcomes, and acceptance.24 While the trust and reputation built by decades of research and intervention delivery at Duke University cannot simply be exported to other sites, experience and know-how built over time can still support implementation at other sites. For example, this could be achieved through provision of training and accreditation, transfer of know-how via sharing of protocols and standard operating procedures, or centralized oversight. In 2025, a child with CP became the first in Australia to receive an infusion with their own cord blood cells utilizing a somewhat comparable compassionate access pathway.25 This demonstrates feasibility of implementation outside of the US context, supported by the current level of evidence for cord blood infusion for CP.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Findings are most directly applicable to the US context and to regulatory and health systems that permit the combination of both research (clinical trials) and expanded access pathways. As discussed above, Duke and MD Anderson’s unique configuration as treatment site, cord blood bank, and research hub may limit transferability to settings with less developed cell therapy infrastructure or different funding and reimbursement models, although many insights, particularly regarding cross-disciplinary leadership and organizational coordination, are consistent with broader implementation literature.26,27. Importantly, all participants were current or former program personnel, meaning that findings reflect internal perspectives. While this was deemed appropriate to address the aim of this study—specifically to understand the real-world implementation barriers and enablers of cord blood infusion for CP—the decision to exclude other stakeholders (eg, families and patients, external clinicians, regulatory personnel and payers) means that our analysis may substantially underestimate the barriers to implementation. As it was, there was a substantial imbalance in the number of facilitator and barrier codes identified, with twice as many facilitators generated. During interviews, M.C.B.P. observed that participants often raised barriers that had been overcome to establish the program at Duke (or their collaborator site), reflecting on the various factors that contributed to successful implementation (ie, how these barriers were overcome, what was required). In contrast, true facilitators were often raised as continuing to support the current and ongoing program. It is likely that this asymmetry in facilitator/barrier codes was a direct consequence of the participant composition (including only those involved in sites where cord blood infusion had been successfully implemented), which artificially reduced the number of identified barriers. Finally, consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, our interpretation is acknowledged as analytic rather than neutral. While the lead interviewer did have preexisting relationships with some participants, which could have contributed to social desirability bias and analytic interpretation, credibility was supported through several firewalls and safeguards as described in the Methods section.

Future research directions

This study was a targeted examination of what helps and hinders the implementation of cord blood infusion for CP at 2 defined clinical sites in the United States. As acknowledged above, this study was purposefully narrow in its scope to answer a defined question, and thus much remains for further exploration. Key future research directions include studies to understand the perspectives of a range of other relevant external stakeholders to assist in understanding additional barriers to more widespread implementation, chiefly families and patients (in particular those unable to access treatment), hematologists/oncologists at other sites (both those supportive and unsupportive of cord blood infusion for CP), and rehabilitation medicine physicians who serve as the primary long-term clinicians for children with CP. Longer-term-focused studies should additionally seek to interrogate the apparent mismatch between the societal value and market incentive for a therapy like cord blood infusion for CP. This is critical as sustained implementation of cell therapies will not only hinge on safety and efficacy data, but also on reimbursement structures, legal frameworks, and long-term integration into standard care; themes which are emphasized in other policy-focused reviews on access to advanced therapy medicinal products.28

Further, although this study was restricted to cord blood infusion for CP, the findings have broader relevance for the translation of other emerging cell-based and regenerative therapies into clinical practice. Future studies could examine the distinct differences and commonalities across various medical indications and regenerative medicine products to increase the transferability and generalizability of findings to increase the impact of these findings. These could include dissection of regulatory complexity, cross-disciplinary expertise, infrastructure needs, lack of reimbursement, and limited commercial incentive. Finally, equity of access to advanced cell therapeutics is an important ethical consideration the field will need to address. Future work will need to understand how implementation of cord blood therapy can move beyond highly specialized centers, including the potential for reducing costs so that individuals with CP in low-and-middle-income countries, where the prevalence is substantially higher,29 can also benefit from these emerging technologies.

Conclusion

This study offers an implementation-focused examination of a mature cord blood treatment program for CP, illuminating barriers and enablers that are largely consistent with those observed for other complex health interventions. Rather than identifying unexpected challenges, the findings underscore the predictability of implementation determinants when introducing a novel therapy across disciplinary, regulatory, and institutional boundaries, in addition to known infrastructure requirements for delivery of cord blood cells. Importantly, participant accounts suggest that implementation of cord blood programs is contingent rather than universally replicable, likely to require local adaptation rather than direct transfer of a single model. Promisingly, cord blood treatment is already established clinically for hematological conditions worldwide, and a recent case report of compassionate access to cord blood infusion for CP outside of the US supports implementation feasibility beyond this specialized program. Future efforts should focus on garnering insights from additional key external stakeholder groups, evaluating implementation strategies across diverse contexts, clarifying post-approval pathways for cord blood therapies, and identifying models that balance innovation with equity and responsible stewardship as the evidence base continues to evolve.
