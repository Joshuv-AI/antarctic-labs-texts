Cranioplasty with a 3D-printed titanium implant for post-decompressive craniectomy syndrome: A case report
Authors: Vuong Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Decompressive craniectomy (DC) is a life-saving intervention for severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), but often results in large cranial defects leading to syndrome of the trephined (ST)- a neurological condition characterized by headaches, cognitive decline, and motor deficits caused by atmospheric pressure acting on the unshielded brain.Case Description: This report describes a 45-year-old male who developed debilitating post-craniectomy headaches and cognitive deficits. While preoperative imaging suggested cerebral atrophy, the diagnosis of ST was confirmed by distinguishing reversible parenchymal compression from permanent atrophy, paradoxical herniation, and hydrocephalus. A custom 3D-printed titanium implant featuring a novel in situ-generated selenium nanoparticle (SeNP) surface was used to repair the defect. Following the procedure, the patient experienced a complete resolution of his headaches (Visual Analog Scale score 8/10 to 0/10) and a significant functional recovery.Conclusion: Postoperative imaging demonstrated rapid cerebral re-expansion and normalization of ventricular caliber, validating the mechanical etiology of the deficits. This case is unique in its application of nanostructured SeNP technology to enhance biocompatibility and highlights the importance of distinguishing dynamic compression from static atrophy in TBI recovery.

INTRODUCTION

Cranioplasty is a neurosurgical reconstructive procedure performed to repair a skull defect, typically following a decompressive craniectomy (DC). DC is a critical, life-saving surgery used to manage refractory intracranial hypertension in patients with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) and life-threatening cerebral edema.[12] While effective, DC leaves the patient with a large cranial defect that can lead to a constellation of symptoms known as the syndrome of the trephined (ST), which includes headaches, cognitive impairment, and other neurological dysfunctions. Cranioplasty serves to restore normal cerebral dynamics and has been shown to improve patient outcomes.

TBI is a major global health issue, with an estimated 64–74 million new cases reported annually.[8] It is a leading cause of death and disability, particularly among young adults and the elderly.[16] In the United States alone, there were approximately 214,110 TBI-related hospitalizations in 2020 and 69,473 TBI-related deaths in 2021.[4] Over 50% of severe TBI cases require a DC to prevent fatal brain herniation. However, recovery is often complicated; studies indicate that up to 30% of these patients develop post-traumatic epilepsy.[7] Cranioplasty is ultimately performed in 60–80% of craniectomy patients to provide cranial protection and restore brain function.

Furthermore, the timing of cranioplasty requires balancing the benefits of rehabilitation with surgical risks. Early procedures (within 3 months) are often associated with better cognitive recovery. However, a recent large multicenter study found no significant difference in 12-month functional outcomes between early and delayed cranioplasty but did note a higher risk of hydrocephalus in the early group.[18]

The choice of material for cranioplasty is also critical. Autologous bone flaps have been used traditionally but are associated with high resorption rates, ranging from 44% to over 66%.[9] In recent years, custom 3D-printed titanium implants have become a preferred option for their durability, precise anatomical fit, and lower rates of infection. This case is unique in its application of a 3D-printed titanium mesh enhanced with in situ-generated selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs), a nanotechnology designed to optimize osseointegration and minimize biofilm formation in high-risk cranial defects.

This report presents the case of a 45-year-old male with significant cerebral atrophy and debilitating postcraniectomy headaches who was successfully treated with a custom 3D-printed titanium implant, emphasizing the importance of timely intervention and optimal material selection in TBI recovery.

CASE PRESENTATION

History

Three months before admission, the patient, a 45-year-old male with no significant past medical history, was involved in a high-impact motor vehicle collision and sustained a severe TBI. Emergency evaluation revealed an acute right subdural hematoma and a left temporal lobe contusion. Due to neurological deterioration and rising intracranial pressure, he underwent an emergency right hemispheric DC.

The patient’s initial postoperative recovery was uneventful. Over the following weeks, he developed persistent headaches and a noticeable sunken appearance over the craniectomy site. His symptoms had progressively worsened, significantly impacting his daily activities. These symptoms were indicative of ST, caused by the atmospheric pressure imbalances across the large cranial defect.

Diagnosis

Upon admission for cranioplasty, the patient was alert, oriented, and hemodynamically stable. The neurological examination was unremarkable, with no focal motor deficits, sensory loss, or cranial nerve dysfunction. A visible and palpable concave defect was present over the right hemisphere [Figure 1], confirming the extensive bone loss. His headaches, rated as 8/10 on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) (where 10 is the worst imaginable pain), worsened with postural changes, further supporting the diagnosis of ST. Clinically, it was vital to distinguish ST from paradoxical herniation; the patient remained alert with no signs of brainstem compression. His preoperative cognitive assessment through the mini-mental state examination (MMSE) was 25/30, suggesting mild cognitive impairment. His functional status on the Glasgow outcome scale-extended (GOS-E) was 6 (lower moderate disability), indicating he was independent at home but dependent outside. A preoperative non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the head confirmed the large cranial defect with evidence of underlying cerebral atrophy [Figure 2]. While the atrophy was likely multifactorial, stemming from the initial diffuse axonal injury, the cranial defect appeared to exacerbate the mass effect and midline shift. The scan showed no acute hemorrhage, and crucially, excluded hydrocephalus as the cause of his neurological decline.

Surgery and outcome

Given the size and complexity of the defect, a custom 3D-printed titanium implant was designed based on the patient’s preoperative CT data to ensure a precise anatomical fit. The implant, consisting of a titanium mesh with an in situ-generated nanostructured surface formed by the adhesion of SeNPs to enhance biocompatibility, was placed over the defect and securely fastened with titanium mini-plates and screws [Figure 3]. The temporalis muscle was re-approximated, and bio-cement was used to reinforce the reconstruction by filling small peripheral gaps to ensure a seamless contour, optimizing both structural integrity and cosmetic appearance.

Intraoperative photograph showing the custom 3D-printed titanium mesh with a nanostructured surface created by in situ adhesion of selenium nanoparticles. The implant is positioned over the cranial defect prior to being secured to the surrounding bone.

The patient’s postoperative course was remarkable. His debilitating headaches resolved completely within days of the surgery. At his 6-month follow-up, he reported a complete resolution of headaches, with a VAS score of 0/10. He remained neurologically intact and experienced no surgical complications, such as infection, hematoma, or implant rejection. Post-operative imaging confirmed the successful reconstruction [Figure 4]. Postoperative assessments showed marked functional improvement, with his MMSE score increasing to 29/30 and his GOS-E score improving to 8 (upper good recovery), indicating a near-complete return to normal life. Early physical and occupational therapy helped him regain confidence, and he made excellent progress in his physical and cognitive recovery.

DISCUSSION

DC is a crucial intervention for severe TBI, but the resulting cranial defect exposes the brain to atmospheric pressure, which alters cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics and cerebral blood flow. This can lead to the ST, a condition characterized by headaches, cognitive decline, and other neurological symptoms.[1] Our patient presented with classic ST symptoms, most notably disabling postural headaches that directly impacted his quality of life and made cranioplasty a clinical necessity.

Cranioplasty aims to correct these pathophysiological changes by restoring the skull’s rigid structure, which helps normalize intracranial pressure and improve cerebral perfusion.[10] The success of this intervention was evidenced by the dramatic reduction in our patients’ VAS score from 8/10 to 0/10. Postoperative imaging demonstrated significant re-expansion of the cerebral parenchyma. This suggests that the preoperative “shrunken” appearance was not solely due to permanent cortical atrophy, but was significantly exacerbated by reversible atmospheric compression ST.

Definition and diagnostic criteria

ST versus paradoxical herniation

The diagnosis of ST relies on distinguishing a spectrum of atmospheric pathology. While ST presents subacutely with postural headaches and cognitive slowing (as seen in this patient),[2] paradoxical herniation represents an acute neurosurgical emergency characterized by brainstem compression and autonomic instability.[17] Our patient’s preserved alertness and lack of brainstem signs allowed for the clinical exclusion of acute herniation despite the severe radiological mass effect.

Radiographic correlates and hydrocephalus

A critical diagnostic challenge is differentiating ST from post-traumatic hydrocephalus, as both present with cognitive decline. However, they are pathophysiologically distinct: ST results from CSF hypotension and atmospheric compression, while hydrocephalus involves CSF hypertension.[14]

Radiographically, our patient demonstrated a “sinking skin flap” with effaced sulci and slit-like ventricles, contrasting with the ventriculomegaly and transependymal flow typically seen in hydrocephalus. This distinction confirmed that his “atrophy” was actually reversible parenchymal compression, predicting the successful brain re-expansion observed post-cranioplasty.

Implant material and safety

The choice of implant material is paramount for a successful outcome. While materials such as autologous bone and polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) are available, 3D-printed titanium implants are increasingly favored for large defects due to their superior biocompatibility, durability, and demonstrably lower infection rates compared to other materials.[5,19] Regarding infection risk, the literature presents mixed findings. While some studies suggest titanium has a favorable profile, others indicate infection rates comparable to polyetheretherketone (PEEK), particularly in previously contaminated fields.[15] In this case, the custom implant provided excellent structural stability and a superior cosmetic result, while bio-cement reinforcement of the temporalis muscle helped prevent subsequent atrophy. Postoperative CT provided a distinct imaging correlation: the nanostructured SeNP-titanium interface demonstrated minimal streak artifact, allowing for precise visualization of the implant-bone integration that is often obscured in standard titanium plating.

Nanotechnology and infection prevention

While standard titanium implants are durable, they remain susceptible to bacterial colonization and biofilm formation. This case is unique in its utilization of a custom titanium mesh modified with SeNPs. Selenium is a trace element with potent antibacterial properties, capable of inhibiting bacterial growth through the generation of reactive oxygen species while promoting osteoblast proliferation.[3] The rapid wound healing and lack of inflammatory complications in this patient suggest that the SeNP nanostructure successfully enhanced the bio-interface, offering a novel advantage over standard PMMA or PEEK implants, which rely solely on bulk material properties for biocompatibility.

Timing and complications

The timing of the procedure is also a key factor.[6] Early cranioplasty (within 3 months) has been associated with improved neurological recovery and reduced complication rates.[13] However, this benefit must be weighed against risks such as hydrocephalus and hygroma formation, which are more prevalent in early interventions.[11] The procedure for this patient was performed 3 months post-craniectomy. This timing fell within an optimal window, balancing the resolution of acute brain swelling with the benefits of early rehabilitation. The notable improvement in his cognitive and functional scores (MMSE and GOS-E) aligns with studies linking timely cranioplasty to enhanced neurological recovery.

Although cranioplasty carries risks such as infection, hematoma, and implant failure, careful planning can mitigate them. Titanium implants have a demonstrably lower infection rate compared to other materials.[19] Meticulous preoperative planning, strict sterile technique, and diligent post-operative monitoring all contributed to this patient’s smooth, complication-free recovery.

CONCLUSION

This case demonstrates the profound impact of cranioplasty in the recovery of patients with large cranial defects following DC. The procedure does more than restore cosmetic appearance; it corrects underlying pathophysiological disturbances in intracranial pressure, leading to significant improvements in neurological function and quality of life. The use of a custom-designed, 3D-printed titanium implant provided a durable, esthetically optimal, and safe solution for this patient, minimizing the risk of common complications such as infection. This successful outcome underscores that the modern standard for TBI rehabilitation hinges on both the procedure and the material. Timely cranioplasty corrects the physiological deficits, while the use of a custom 3D-printed titanium implant ensures that the restoration is precise, safe, and durable.
