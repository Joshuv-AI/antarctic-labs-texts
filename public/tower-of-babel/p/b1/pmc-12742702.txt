A challenge for treating patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancers, particularly for those with liver metastases
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang
Published: 2025

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 10% to 15% of all lung cancers (LC), but it is associated with an extremely poor prognosis, particularly for extensive-stage SCLC patients (ES-SCLC) with a stagnant 5-year overall survival (OS) <10%.1 Compared to other non-SCLC, SCLC is a biologically highly aggressive LC characterized by rapid disease evolution, widespread metastases, and dissemination to other vital organs, and it is estimated that over 70% of SCLC patients are in the extensive stage.1–4 Based on its vulnerability to vital-organ metastases, such as liver and brain metastases, and once ES-SCLC is diagnosed, therapeutic challenges arise. Conventional dual-drug chemotherapy (CT) regimen (etoposide-platinum: EP regimen) alone has produced acceptable short-term responses (60%-80%, including 43% of complete response)1; unfortunately, almost all ES-SCLC patients progress and result in the historical OS benchmark hovering between 8 and 10 months,1 which is also confirmed by recent Taiwan domestic data with 8.5-month OS in their cohort study.5 Recently, due to an integration of immunotherapy (IO) into the CT, called immunochemotherapy (IO+CT),6 the historical dismal outcomes of these cancers have been reversed, and compared to CT alone, have shown significantly improved outcomes in patients (prolongation of progression-free survival [PFS] and OS), which include non-SCLC,4 SCLC,5 gastric cancer,7–9 neuroendocrine cancer,10 cervical cancer,11–13 ovarian cancer,14 endometrial cancer,15,16 and many others.13

According to the dismal outcomes of patients with ES-SCLC treated by CT alone, Dr. Liao’s group using IO-CT for treating ES-SCLC patients is worthy of. This study was published published in the August 2025 issue of the Journal of Chinese Medical Association (JCMA).5 Dr. Liao reported 33 women with ES-SCLC undergoing the first-line (1L) IO+CT (immune checkpoint inhibitors [ICIs] as a backbone of IO) to investigate the therapeutic effectiveness, and found that, although PFS in the IO+CT group did not reach the statistically significant difference compared to CT alone group (median PFS: 5.0 vs 4.8 months), OS in the IO+CT group was significantly increased by 5.4 months compared with the CT alone group (from historical 8.5-month OS to 13.9-month OS),5 suggesting that IO+CT therapy should be applied as standard of care (SOC) therapy for all ES-SCLC patients. However, unlike gynecologic cancers with clear biomarker-guided agonistic therapy (a benefit of integration of IO into CT based on programmed death protein ligand 1 [PD-L1] expression pattern of the cervical cancer [immunohistochemical staining score, combined positive score, CPS] and mismatch repair deficiency [MMRd] of the endometrial cancer), the biomarkers of ES-SCLC seem to be unclear. The aforementioned limitation is also found in the very similar histological types from other origins, such as prostate neuroendocrine cancer, which has been discussed in our previous editorial comment.10

Additionally, baseline liver metastases still play the most important independent risk factor associated with worse outcome in ES-SCLC patients, even though IO+CT has been applied as the first-line therapy with a hazard ratio (HR) of 11.23 (95% CI, 2.95-42.68) for PFS and HR 3.5 (95% CI, 1.18-10.50; p = 0.024) for OS.5 In fact, the aforementioned finding has also been shown in our previous editorial comment.17 Moreover, this ominous sign of liver metastases is not limited to ES-SCLC patients, and may be particularly important for nearly all cancer patients, since the identification of the liver metastases in metastatic/recurrent (m/c) cancers making cure nearly impossible if only systemic therapy is given, even though new combination therapy, such as IO plus CT, which has successfully improved the outcomes in the current study by Dr. Liao.5 Recent meta-analysis has shown the negative impact of liver metastases on immunotherapy (ICIs) efficacy in LC with a pooled OS HR of 0.83 vs 0.73), highlighting the organ-specific immunosuppressive role of the liver across different cancer types, of which liver metastases present a “cold” tumor immune microenvironment due to their unique immune composition and special distribution of the tumor microenvironment (TME), along with systemic immune suppression.18

To overcome the therapeutic resistance and combat the death signs of liver metastases, there is a long way to go. As shown by our previous editorial,18 may help shorten the pathway toward this goal, since a combination of local treatment and systemic therapy may offer a better chance in the management of patients with extensive-stage cancers. However, there are many uncertainties that need clarification, such as combination therapy, concurrent therapy, sequential therapy, and many others. For example, determining the optimal sequence of therapies is often debated when planning sequential treatment. Therapy-related toxicities and adverse events are well recognized. In fact, the expected synergistic effect (one plus one is exceeding two) without adding toxicities and adverse events is a long-term dream. However, this is not always achievable. To reach this goal, laboratory, pre-clinical, clinical, and postmarketing research and data are all essentially needed and the emergence of all these findings necessitates additional investigation to validate their role in guiding precision treatment for liver metastases. Most importantly, how to interpret and integrate these extensive data is another major challenge. These efforts require substantial resources and manpower, and cooperation among institutions, companies, industries, and governments must not be delayed. Of course, all stakeholders should continue their efforts to further advancing technology and provide a better understanding of tumor molecular pathophysiology and TME, which is a shortcut to overcoming the challenges in treating m/r cancer patients with liver metastases.
