Probabilistic-graph-based spatial context-aware framework for interpretable spatial omics denoising and augmentation
Authors: Xianhan Qin, Chang Liu, Fei Gu, Xun Lan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatially resolved omics technologies offer unprecedented insight into tissue organization, yet current analytical methods face challenges in effectively handling technical noise while preserving biological heterogeneity. We present CadaST, an interpretable and unified computational framework that integrates spatially aware feature selection and an adaptive imputation strategy to address this limitation. By inferring the spatial molecular pattern for each feature and conducting pattern-guided aggregation, CadaST effectively denoises and augments spatial omics data while preserving sharp biological boundaries. This gene-centric approach robustly denoises data without the oversmoothing common to other methods. CadaST is versatile and highly effective, outperforming existing methods across diverse spatial technologies. It accurately resolves delicate anatomical layers in the brain, characterizes complex tumor microenvironments, and scales efficiently to large-scale developmental atlases. By providing a more accurate, interpretable, and scalable solution, CadaST represents a significant methodological advance for elucidating the principles of tissue architecture in health and disease.

Introduction

The spatial organization of cells within tissues is fundamental to their biological function. Recent advances in spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT) have significantly advanced our ability to profile gene expression while preserving essential spatial information [1]. Broadly, spatial technologies can be classified into two categories [2]: the imaging-based methods such as seqFISH [3] and MERFISH [4], which directly quantify individual RNA molecules at single-cell resolution, and the sequencing-based approaches such as 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\times $\end{document} Visium [5], Stereo-seq [6], and Slide-seq [7], which capture genome-wide transcriptional information through positional barcodes, although typically at a lower cellular resolution. These technologies have facilitated key analytical tasks in SRT, such as spatial domain discovery, ligand–receptor interaction mapping, and trajectory inference.

A key challenge in spatial transcriptomics is the precise identification of spatial domains—regions with distinct gene expression profiles that are essential for understanding tissue architecture [8, 9]. While numerous computational methods exist, ranging from probabilistic models to graph neural networks [10–14], their effectiveness is constrained by two fundamental bottlenecks.

First, the inherent technical noise and sparsity of spatial omics data, caused by low capture efficiency, significantly obscure accurate biological signals [15, 16]. Critically, current denoising approaches often fall short. Methods adapted from scRNA-seq overlook crucial spatial information, while recent SRT-specific tools [16, 17] rely on static neighborhood relationships. This lack of adaptability prevents them from accommodating individual genes’ unique and complex spatial expression patterns, limiting their utility in heterogeneous tissues. Second, identifying the most informative spatially variable genes (SVGs) remains a significant hurdle for deciphering tissue organization [18]. Despite a variety of available methods [19–24], there is still a pressing need for tools that offer greater efficiency, interpretability, and flexibility in capturing intricate spatial patterns.

To address these limitations, we introduce CadaST, a unified computational framework that integrates spatially aware gene selection and a novel signal enhancement strategy guided by dynamic gene expression state inference. Our approach uniquely leverages an iterative process where the latent expression state of each gene is inferred and then used to guide an adaptive imputation kernel. This gene-specific, context-aware mechanism effectively denoises spatial omics data by smoothing expression values primarily among spots sharing the same inferred state, thereby preserving sharp biological boundaries. We demonstrate that CadaST is practical and versatile across diverse spatial transcriptomics technologies, and shows versatility on spatial multi-omics datasets. It accurately characterizes tissue heterogeneity, revealing distinct domains with gene-level interpretability, while maintaining remarkable computational scalability and efficiency.

Iterative spatial pattern identification and context-aware imputation

The core of CadaST is an iterative, two-step process applied to each SVG independently. First, it identifies the underlying spatial expression pattern for the gene. Second, it uses this pattern to guide a context-aware imputation.

For the spatial pattern identification step, we infer a discrete latent state (e.g. “high” or “low” expression) for each spot using a hidden Markov random field (HMRF) model. This model optimizes a hidden state configuration that balances the observed expression at a spot (modeled by a Gaussian Mixture Model) with the states of its spatial neighbors (enforced by a Potts prior).

Following state inference, we perform context-aware imputation. The denoised expression of a spot is computed as a weighted average of its spatial neighbors. This weight is critically modulated by a label consistency term, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta _{ij}$\end{document}, which heavily penalizes averaging between spots that have been assigned different latent states. This design ensures that smoothing primarily occurs between spots that are both spatially close and share the same inferred biological state, thus preserving sharp boundaries between distinct domains.

The method is primarily governed by two hyperparameters: the imputation weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha $\end{document}, which balances the contribution of neighboring expression, and the consistency penalty \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta $\end{document}, which modulates the degree of smoothing allowed across state boundaries. We set default parameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha =0.6$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta =0.2$\end{document}) that perform robustly across platforms. However, users can adjust these parameters for finer control; for instance, a lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta $\end{document} will result in sharper boundaries, while a higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha $\end{document} will yield spatially smoother domains. We provide detailed guidance on parameter tuning in our package documentation and tutorials. The complete mathematical formulation of the HMRF model, its energy function, and the imputation weight matrix are detailed in Supplementary Note.

Clustering and visualization

We employ dimensional reduction and clustering techniques to analyze the spatial patterns in the processed data. Principal Component Analysis is applied to embed the CadaST-processed expression matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$\end{document} into a lower-dimensional space. The top 15 principal components, capturing the majority of data variance, are retained for downstream analysis. The clustering step can be performed with various algorithms, including K-means, Gaussian Mixture Model-based clustering (mclust) [28], and the Leiden [29] algorithm implemented in SCANPY [25]. The resulting clusters are visualized by mapping spots back to their original 2D spatial coordinates for visualization.

Performance evaluation and methods comparison

CadaST’s performance was quantified by assessing both clustering accuracy and spatial coherence. We adopted a suite of well-established evaluation metrics as detailed by Yuan et al. [10], specifically NMI and ARI for clustering accuracy. If true labels were unavailable, Silhouette coefficient and NC were used as alternative metrics. For spatial coherence, we utilized the CHAOS score and PAS for spatial coherence assessment. We compared CadaST with several existing methods widely used in SRT clustering analysis. The mathematical details of evaluation metrics, comparison methods, and their brief introductions are provided in Supplementary Notes.

Results

Overview of CadaST

CadaST utilizes a spatial context-aware aggregation kernel that integrates molecular similarities and gene-specific niche variations (Fig. 1). Our approach leverages expression correlation in spatially proximal cells [30] to enable expression level estimation through neighborhood analysis. However, the robustness of this assumption diminishes in complex biological systems characterized by significant cellular heterogeneity and intricate intercellular interactions. As a result, traditional aggregation methods relying on naïve averaging of neighboring features may inadvertently amplify technical noise and introduce systematic biases, potentially compromising downstream analyses. CadaST employs an iterative, gene-specific pattern identification and state-constrained imputation strategy to address these limitations, offering a data denoising and augmentation framework.

The schematic illustration of CadaST workflow: For each of the top SVGs identified using GLS, CadaST infers its gene-specific hidden states; this state information modulates the smoothing kernel, which aggregates expression values from neighboring spots. The aggregation process prioritizes information from spots with identical inferred gene states (solid line), effectively reducing the influence of spots with different states (dashed line). HMRF, Hidden Markov Random Field; Cada, Context-aware Denoising and Aggregation.

For SVG identification, we introduce the graph Laplacian score (GLS), a feature selection metric derived from the Laplacian score (LS) [27] and adapted for spatial transcriptomics data. This metric is specifically designed to quantify the effectiveness with which gene expression patterns preserve spatial and transcriptomic locality. GLS enables the identification of genes that exhibit distinct spatial distribution patterns, which are termed SVGs. In its standard implementation, CadaST focuses on the top 2000 SVGs, ranked according to their GLS values (Methods).

To promote spatial coherence and continuity in gene expression patterns, CadaST first employs a probabilistic framework [31] to infer latent states for each gene individually. Operating under a default binary state model (high/low expression), the model infers hidden labels to approximate the underlying expression state for each gene basis. Subsequently, a self-adaptive smoothing kernel is utilized to compute denoised expression values by weighting the nearest-neighbor spots (Fig. 1). The weighting scheme is modulated based on cell–cell similarity, quantified using a normalized exponential cosine metric, and gene-specific state constraints, impeding aggregation weights between spots and divergent states.

CadaST achieves computational efficiency and scalability by incorporating parallel processing and vectorization techniques and optimized sparse matrix operations. These computational strategies are implemented to accelerate matrix calculations and reduce memory footprint, thereby enabling efficient processing of large-scale spatial transcriptomics datasets without requiring specialized hardware. Validation across various spatial transcriptomics platforms has demonstrated CadaST’s improved performance in data denoising and computational efficiency relative to existing methods. Imputed expression improved clustering accuracy and more significant differential expression analysis, underscoring its versatility in varied tissue architectures.

CadaST accurately segments domains in the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex dataset

To evaluate CadaST performance, we used the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) dataset [5]. This dataset comprises 12 tissue sections from three donors for benchmarking spatial domain identification methods due to its inherent complexity and the availability of expert-annotated laminar cortical layers and white matter regions (Fig. 2a). For comparative performance assessment, we conducted benchmarking against nine established spatial clustering algorithms: Seurat [32], SpatialPrompt [33], SpaGCN [34], DeepGFT [35], BayesSpace [11], BASS [12], SR-DGN [13], STAGATE [13], and GraphST [14] (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Using tissue section 151673 as a representative example, which comprises 33 538 genes across 3460 spatial spots, we illustrated the clustering performance of CadaST (Fig. 2a), revealing notable preservation of the DLPFC’s laminar architecture, as quantified by an adjusted rand index (ARI) of 0.64. Across the 12 DLPFC sections, the quantitative analysis further supported CadaST’s improved performance. CadaST achieved the highest median normalized mutual information (NMI) score of 0.72, indicating enhanced agreement with the expert-annotated tissue layer boundaries (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Figs S2 and S3). Furthermore, CadaST demonstrated greater spatial coherence, as evidenced by significantly lower median spatial CHAOS [36] of 0.0598 and percentage of abnormal spots (PAS) [36] of 0.0148 scores compared with other methods (paired t-test, Fig. 2b).

To quantify the contribution of each component, we performed ablation studies comparing several variants of our method against the full CadaST model (Fig. 2c). The results demonstrate that the context-aware aggregation and GLS feature selection are both crucial, as their removal leads to a notable decrease in performance compared with the complete CadaST framework.

To systematically benchmark the key components of CadaST, we performed comprehensive comparative analyses on the DLPFC dataset. First, we benchmarked CadaST’s denoising capabilities against other methods. We evaluated CadaST against several established spatial [13, 16] and non-spatial [37–39] methods. The performance was assessed both quantitatively using a suite of rigorous metrics (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Note) and qualitatively through marker gene visualization (Supplementary Fig. S4). CadaST achieved the strongest performance on metrics related to structural fidelity and error, including structural similarity index measure, root mean squared error, and Jensen–Shannon divergence. Notably, simpler methods like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k$\end{document}-nearest neighbors (KNN) and Gaussian kernel smoothing achieved higher Spearman correlation coefficient scores. This suggests that while these methods can achieve higher correlation, they do so at the cost of ineffective denoising (Supplementary Figs S4 and S5). Moreover, we benchmarked SVG selection. This analysis showed that GLS has state-of-the-art performance in recovering informative SVGs and that the genes it selects lead to more accurate downstream clustering results (Supplementary Figs S6 and S7).

Compared to other spatial methods, a key distinguishing feature of CadaST is the gene-specific imputation mechanism, which leverages inherent regional expression patterns. This approach aims to enhance the detection of characteristic genes associated with distinct spatial domains, potentially leading to improved biological interpretability. We ranked genes based on the degree of overlap between their expression patterns and the tissue’s spatial domains, quantified using Jaccard Similarity (Methods) to examine the patterns of three marker genes: PLP1, PCP4, and CAMK2N1 in tissue section 151673 (Fig. 2d). Although these genes’ domain-specific expression patterns were present in the raw data, they were obscured by considerable noise. Following CadaST processing, these patterns were enhanced, revealing a much clearer spatial organization.

We compared the marker genes identified by CadaST and nine baseline methods against those derived from ground-truth annotations to validate the biological accuracy of these findings. Using the mean Jaccard similarity to measure concordance, we found that CadaST’s marker genes showed the top-tier level of agreement with the ground-truth set (Supplementary Fig. S9), providing strong evidence that our method accurately captures domain-specific biological signatures. Furthermore, we extended this systematic comparison to the functional level. We compared the GO enrichment terms from each method’s marker set against the GO terms from the ground-truth markers. This analysis revealed that the functional pathways identified by CadaST are in the closest agreement with the ground-truth functional profile (Supplementary Fig. S10), underscoring that our method provides more accurate biological insights. Focusing on cortical layer two and white matter regions as representative examples (Supplementary Fig. S8), GO enrichment analysis indicated that layer two is primarily enriched regarding synapse formation and neuronal excitation. In contrast, the white matter region is enriched mainly in terms of oligodendrocyte function (Fig. 2e). These findings demonstrate strong concordance with established biological knowledge, validating CadaST’s capacity to identify spatial domain-specific genes relevant to DLPFC tissue organization [5].

Furthermore, differential gene expression analysis of the MOB dataset revealed that CadaST-derived clusters exhibited more distinct gene expression patterns (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. S12).

This superior performance was validated quantitatively, where CadaST achieved the highest SC score (0.226) and NC score (0.379) compared to other methods, indicating a more distinct and transcriptionally coherent clustering. Visually, CadaST’s delineation of key hippocampal regions also exhibited high spatial consistency and well-defined boundaries (Fig. 3e). For example, CadaST notably distinguished between the CA2sp and CA3sp regions, which are often challenging to separate. In particular, CA2sp, which is often less clearly defined by other methods, was more distinctly delineated by CadaST based on the expression of marker genes such as Strip2, a gene implicated in regulating cell morphology and cytoskeletal organization [41]. Binary gene states contain sufficient information for clustering on high-spatial-resolution data

A notable finding from our framework is that the inferred binary latent states, an intermediate product of CadaST, can themselves serve as effective features for spatial clustering. To investigate the significance and context of this finding, we quantitatively compared its performance against the full CadaST model across different data platforms. On the high-spatial-resolution, sparse datasets (e.g. Slide-seqV2 and Stereo-seq), we found the binary states contain sufficient information to achieve highly accurate clustering. For the Hippocampus data, this approach demonstrated competitive performance, achieving metrics (SC=0.211, NC=0.366) that were comparable to the full CadaST model (SC=0.226, NC=0.379), outperforming baseline methods on the SC while maintaining a comparable NC (Fig. 3e and g). This is consistent with recent findings by Lin et al. [42], which suggest that for sparse, high-spatial-resolution data, the “on/off” state is the dominant signal. Our finding demonstrates that CadaST’s inferred binary states effectively capture this core spatial heterogeneity. Conversely, on the denser, low-spatial-resolution DLPFC dataset (e.g. 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\times $\end{document} Visium), we found this binary information is insufficient. The binary-state approach leads to a significant loss of performance compared with the full CadaST model (Supplementary Figs S14 and S16). This demonstrates a clear trade-off: for denser data, the nuanced transcriptional gradients discarded by binarization are essential for resolving complex tissue architecture.

CadaST resolves spatial heterogeneity in tumor microenvironment

Building upon the demonstration of CadaST’s capability to reveal spatial heterogeneity in tissues under normal physiological conditions, we further evaluated its performance in characterizing tumor microenvironment (TME) heterogeneity. This evaluation focused on two distinct, yet clinically relevant applications: (i) identifying tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) [43] within primary liver cancer (PLC) using an SRT dataset [44], and (ii) characterizing human tumor heterogeneity in breast cancer (BRCA-A) tissue utilizing the Visium platform [45].

In the PLC dataset, CadaST-based clustering exhibited strong concordance with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining patterns, effectively distinguishing adjacent normal, tumor, and stromal regions (Fig. 4a). Notably, CadaST identifies TLS regions independently and validates them with expert pathologist annotations. TLSs [46], ectopic lymphoid formations that develop in non-lymphoid tissues, have been reported in multiple cancer types and are frequently associated with improved patient prognosis [43]. Quantitative assessment using “TLS-50” signature genes (the top 50 genes expressed explicitly in expert-annotated TLS regions, Supplementary Table S1) [44] revealed significantly higher TLS scores in CadaST identified TLS-associated clusters compared with the other areas (Fig. 4a). Comparative performance analysis using prediction metrics demonstrated that CadaST achieved enhanced accuracy in TLS region prediction compared with existing methods (Supplementary Fig. S15), as evaluated against expert pathologist annotations (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. S16).

To identify TLS-specific biomarkers, we employed Jaccard similarity score ranking analysis, which quantifies the degree of spatial overlap between gene high-expression regions identified by CadaST and pathologist-annotated TLS regions. This analysis revealed substantial overlap between CadaST-identified gene sets and established TLS-50 signature gene lists. Specifically, 21 genes were common between these sets, including well-established TLS-associated factors such as CCL19, CCL21, LTB, and CXCL13 (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Table S2). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of CadaST-identified gene sets demonstrated significant enrichment in functions relevant to TLS biology, including immune cell activation and neutrophil chemotaxis (Fig. 4d). These findings further demonstrate CadaST’s dual capability in TLS detection and characterization by effectively identifying region-specific gene expression patterns.

Subsequently, we extended our evaluation to dataset BRCA-A [45]. The tissue architecture in this dataset comprised 20 distinct spatial regions, categorized into 4 major morphological categories: ductal carcinoma in situ/lobular carcinoma in situ (DCIS/LCIS), healthy tissue, invasive ductal carcinoma, and tumor edge regions exhibiting low malignancy features [47]. Comparative analysis of spatial domain detection methods revealed that while all methods effectively captured broad morphological features, we quantitatively evaluated their performance against the pathologist annotations using ARI and NMI. As shown in Fig. 4e, CadaST achieved the highest NMI score (0.70) and top-tier ARI score (0.53), confirming its strong concordance with the ground-truth annotations and its ability to resolve fine-grained tissue organization. Further analysis of the DCIS/LCIS_3 region revealed a fine-grained concentric organizational pattern (Tumor core and edge), which both CadaST and BayesSpace were able to identify (Fig. 4f). To quantitatively compare these structures, we calculated the SC and CHAOS score for this specific region. While both methods performed well, CadaST achieved a higher SC and a lower CHAOS score, suggesting its partitioning of the TME is marginally more distinct and spatially coherent. Differential gene expression analysis between the tumor core (domain 16) and tumor edge (domain 1) regions revealed more pronounced transcriptional differences in CadaST-imputed data compared with raw data, suggesting an enhanced capacity for biological signal detection (Fig. 4g; Supplementary Fig. S17). Subsequent pathway analysis highlighted distinct functional profiles associated with these regions: tumor edge regions exhibited enrichment in neutrophil activation and antigen presentation pathway activity, whereas tumor core regions showed enrichment in Wnt signaling and interferon response pathways (Fig. 4h). These findings collectively demonstrate CadaST’s effectiveness in characterizing TME heterogeneity, providing potentially valuable insights for cancer research and future clinical applications [48, 49].

To validate the biological accuracy of our findings, we visualized the expression patterns of known marker genes in the E14.5 embryo. For general tissue types, CadaST clearly enhanced the specific patterns of markers for the epidermis (Krt5), muscle (Tpm2), cartilage primordium (Col1a1), and hepatic tissues (Slc25a37) [50–53], effectively capturing their detailed organization while improving clarity and reliability (Fig. 5h). This detailed partitioning was also evident within the complex architecture of the developing brain (Supplementary Fig. S21). For instance, Domain 5 was enriched for genes associated with neuronal migration and axon guidance (e.g. Tuba1a and Stmn2), suggesting it represents a region of active neuronal development. In contrast, Domains 12 and 16 were characterized by well-established markers for neural stem and progenitor cells, including key transcription factors defining the neural stem cell pool (Sox2 and Lhx2) and markers for radial glial cells (Fabp7 and Ttyh1) [54–56]. Together, these findings confirm that CadaST can accurately partition large-scale datasets into functionally and cytologically distinct regions. By effectively mitigating noise, CadaST provides a refined representation of gene expression dynamics that is robust to the prespecified number of clusters (Supplementary Figs S22 and S23), thereby facilitating a more nuanced understanding of spatial gene regulation during embryonic development.

This clear separation is crucial for studying neurodevelopment and highlights CadaST’s potential for dissecting complex developmental processes. The biological accuracy of these domains was validated by examining the expression of lineage-defining marker genes (Fig. 6b). The method successfully identified 11 distinct spatial domains with clear anatomical correspondence to the Allen Brain Atlas reference [40] (Fig. 6d and e). CadaST’s clustering restored the brain’s overall architecture and segmented functionally significant regions. For instance, it differentiated the islands of Calleja (ioc, cluster 10), part of the brain’s reward circuitry; the corpus callosum (cca, cluster 6), a major white matter tract; and the lateral septal nucleus (ls, cluster 7), a region involved in processing emotion.

This validated, multi-layered analysis from chromatin state to gene expression showcases how CadaST can provide deeper biological insights into the cellular organization of complex tissues.

Discussion

CadaST introduces a robust, comprehensive framework that advances spatial omics analysis by synergistically integrating spatial context, gene-specific state inference, and adaptive data denoising. A unique combination of technical innovations drives its superior performance. The framework first employs a spatially aware efficient gene selection using GLS, which prioritizes genes based on their ability to preserve local tissue structure, ensuring the analysis focuses on the most informative markers. In contrast to static methods, the core of CadaST is its adaptive imputation strategy. It flexibly models diverse spatial patterns by applying latent state inference to each gene individually. Finally, this detailed state information guides an adaptive denoising kernel, constraining smoothing to occur primarily between spots with similar inferred states, thereby maximizing intra-region similarity while faithfully preserving biological boundaries.

This unique, gene-specific approach enables CadaST to resolve complex spatial structures with high biological fidelity while providing a spatially aware approach for domain marker identification. Our comprehensive evaluations confirm its robust performance and versatility across different technologies and biological contexts. For instance, CadaST accurately delineated cortical layers in the human brain and identified functionally relevant TLS in liver cancer. Crucially, its utility extends beyond transcriptomics; when applied to a multi-modal spatial ATAC-RNA dataset of the developing mouse brain, CadaST successfully resolved fine neurodevelopmental domains by integrating epigenetic and transcriptomic signals. Furthermore, its application to large-scale embryonic datasets highlighted its remarkable scalability and computational efficiency, making it a broadly accessible tool for researchers.

A particularly novel finding is that the binary gene expression states inferred by CadaST are often sufficient for accurate spatial domain identification. This highlights an efficient, noise-resilient strategy rather than a hardcoded model limitation. This suggests that focusing on gene patterns’ fundamental “on/off” state is a highly effective strategy for analyzing sparse, high-resolution spatial data. We present this as a finding on the information content of spatial data: the sufficiency of binary states for accurate clustering is highly platform-dependent. While the full CadaST model remains the most robust solution across all data types, our finding highlights that its interpretable, binary intermediate states are surprisingly powerful on sparse datasets. By moving beyond static, one-size-fits-all assumptions and instead focusing on the distinct behavior of individual genes, CadaST offers a more interpretable and biologically faithful solution for understanding complex tissue organization.

However, several limitations should be considered. The method’s reliance on primarily spatial proximity assumptions may not fully capture long-range cellular interactions or complex 3D tissue architecture in all biological scenarios. Future developments may address these limitations by incorporating more sophisticated multi-state models and integrating additional biological prior knowledge, such as cell type information or known spatial gene regulatory networks. Furthermore, the method’s ability to identify SVGs and characterize domain-specific expression patterns opens compelling new avenues for studying tissue organization and cellular communication in healthy and disease states, particularly in developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In this work, we represented a methodological advance in spatial omics analysis, offering a robust, interpretable, and scalable framework named CadaST for understanding tissue architecture. Its ability to precisely identify key features and delineate functional domains in complex tissues has direct implications for biomedical research. It offers a powerful tool to elucidate the cellular basis of neurodevelopmental disorders and clarify the TME’s architecture to guide immunotherapy. Its unique combination of computational efficiency, biological accuracy, and scalability makes it a highly valuable tool for the expanding spatial omics community, with clear potential to accelerate the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
