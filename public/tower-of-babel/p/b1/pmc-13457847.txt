Long-term continuous monitoring of wearable ECG signals for atrial fibrillation detection
Authors: Estela Ribeiro, Quenaz B Soares, Douglas A Almeida, Jaqueline J Pereira, Renata G S Verardino, Tallita C Reis, Denival N Vieira-Junior, Nelson Samesima, Rosangela Monteiro, Fábio B Jatene, Marco A Gutierrez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtrial Fibrillation (AFib) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, including increased risk of stroke and heart failure. Accurate detection of AFib in long-term electrocardiographic (ECG) monitoring is essential for timely diagnosis and early clinical intervention, particularly in ambulatory settings using wearable devices. In this study, we evaluate the performance of a lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN) for automated AFib detection, initially trained on the CinC2021 dataset and subsequently fine-tuned with real-world wearable ECG data. The model was validated across three independent datasets: InCorDB, CODE15, and the TRAdA cohort, with the latter representing long-term ECG monitoring from wearable devices. The TRAdA cohort comprised 173 subjects (87 with AFib and 86 healthy subjects) with continuous ECG recordings obtained over 24 h in Phase 1 (56 subjects) and 7 days in Phase 2 (117 subjects). In total, the dataset included 165,175 annotated ECG segments, including 24,252 AFib episodes. The fine-tuned model demonstrated superior performance compared to the original model on the TRAdA cohort, indicating improved adaptation to wearable ECG signals. On the Phase 2 dataset, the fine-tuned model achieved higher specificity (0.971 vs. 0.914), F1-score (0.891 vs. 0.777), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC; 0.992 vs. 0.988), and accuracy (0.969 vs. 0.924), highlighting its enhanced ability to detect AFib in extended real-world monitoring scenarios. These findings demonstrate that the domain adaptive fine-tuning significantly enhances AFib detection in long-term wearable ECG monitoring. Our findings support the feasibility of lightweight deep learning models for reliable AFib screening in real-world ambulatory settings.Subject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Atrial Fibrillation (AFib) is a common arrhythmia characterized by an irregular and often rapid heart rhythm resulting from a disorganized electrical activity in the atria1. This disruption impairs effective blood flow and significantly increases the risk of stroke, heart failure, and other cardiovascular complications2. AFib can be classified into subtypes based on its duration and recurrence. Persistent AFib refers to continuous episodes lasting more than 7 days, often requiring medical intervention to restore normal sinus rhythm3,4. In contrast, paroxysmal AFib involves brief, self-terminating episodes that typically resolve within 48 h that can reoccur unpredictably2,5. Given the substantial health risks associated with both forms, continuous monitoring and timely intervention are critical for effective clinical management6.

The electrocardiogram (ECG) remains a fundamental diagnostic tool for AFib detection, whether performed using standard 12-lead acquisitions or continuous recordings via a Holter monitor7,8. A 12-lead ECG provides a detailed, multi-angle view of the heart’s electrical activity at a specific point in time, offering high spatial resolution. In contrast, a Holter monitor typically uses fewer leads and provides a more limited spatial view, but enables continuous recording over extended periods, making it valuable for capturing intermittent arrhythmia that may be missed during brief 12-lead ECG recordings.

In this context, advanced Deep Learning (DL) models have shown considerable promise in detecting AFib from ECG signals, achieving high levels of accuracy across various data sources. Other studies have used the CinC2021 dataset12, consisting of 10-s 12-lead ECGs. While most approaches rely on one-dimensional signal-based models, some studies have explored image-based representations of ECG data to leverage spatial features. In general, these methods focus on short, discrete ECG segments, offering accurate AFib detection.

The integration of state-of-the-art detection models into wearable technology holds great promise for continuous ECG monitoring. Such systems can enable early identification of arrhythmic events, allowing timely clinical intervention, improving patient outcomes, preventing complications, and potentially reducing healthcare costs6,22,23.

However, the adoption of these models in clinical practice, particularly in continuous monitoring scenarios, requires the incorporation of explainable analysis methods. As physicians retain responsibility for the final clinical decision, transparency and interpretability of model predictions are critical to ensure trust and facilitate clinical integration24,25. DL models, while powerful, often function as “black boxes”, making their decisions difficult to interpret. Therefore, it’s important that any predictions they generate are accompanied by clear, interpretable explanations. This fosters clinical trust, facilitates informed decision-making, and ensures alignment with established medical knowledge. Without explainability, even accurate models may be met with skepticism, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatment25.

In this study, we evaluated a previously developed lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model designed for AFib detection26, originally trained on a public ECG dataset12. The model is computationally efficient, and contains a reduced number of parameters, making it suitable for deployment in wearable devices. To assess its performance in a real-world setting, we conducted a clinical study involving 173 subjects, including both healthy subjects and patients previously diagnosed with persistent or paroxysmal AFib. Continuous ECG signals were recorded in two phases: 24 h (Phase 1) and 7 days (Phase 2). All recordings were independently reviewed and annotated by an experienced clinician, blinded to the model’s classifications to establish reference labels.

We applied a fine-tuning strategy using data from Phase 1 and subsequently evaluated the model’s performance on the Phase 2 dataset, as well as on two additional datasets comprising standard 12-lead ECG recordings19,27. Furthermore, two explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques were integrated into the fine-tuned model to facilitate the interpretation and validation of its classification. Among the datasets evaluated, the Phase 2 dataset, collected using the wearable ECG configuration, is particularly relevant for assessing model robustness in real-world settings. The fine-tuned model outperformed the original model in most key metrics on this dataset, achieving higher specificity (0.971 vs. 0.914), F1-score (0.891 vs. 0.777), AUC (0.992 vs. 0.988), and accuracy (0.969 vs. 0.924). While the original model achieved a higher sensitivity (0.982 vs. 0.952), the fine-tuned model’s superior specificity and F1-score, supported by non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals, demonstrate a more robust balance for continuous monitoring by reducing false positive detections without a critical loss in detection capability. These results suggest that fine-tuning on Phase 1 data improved the model’s adaptability to wearable ECG signals, enhancing its suitability for real-world AFib detection. To mitigate the high false-alarm burden, we evaluated a post-processing strategy that aggregates six consecutive non-overlapping 10-s windows into 1-min segments. Under this approach, a positive event is assigned only when the number of positive windows exceeds the number of negative windows within the corresponding 1-min interval. As shown in Table 3, this majority-voting scheme substantially reduced the FP/day from 343 to 27.0 in the full dataset, corresponding to 7.5 and 45.8 FP/day in the healthy and arrhythmia cohorts, respectively. Because the Phase 2 reference annotations were derived from a sampled subset totaling 210 min per subject, these daily rates represent statistical extrapolations based on the validated segments, assuming stable model performance throughout the complete 7-day monitoring period.

The fine-tuned model demonstrated strong agreement between predicted and reference AFib burden computed from 10-s segments in the Phase 2 dataset that contains a sampled subset of 210 min per subject. As shown in Fig. 2, the predicted values closely followed the reference measurements, yielding a high correlation (r = 0.944, R = 0.891). The Bland–Altman analysis further confirmed this agreement, showing a small positive bias of 0.019 with limits of agreement ranging from −0.199 to 0.237. These results indicate that the model provides reliable AFib burden estimates under the wearable ECG acquisition conditions used in Phase 2.

Discussion

In this study, we propose a DL-based framework for detecting AFib from wearable ECG recordings. The increasing adoption of wearable ECG devices has enabled continuous cardiac monitoring, creating new opportunities for the early detection of arrhythmia such as AFib. However, real-world ECG signals pose substantial challenges due to noise, motion artifacts, and inter-subject physiological variability, which can compromise reliable detection. To address these challenges, our approach combines pretraining on a large, diverse dataset with domain-specific fine-tuning using real-world clinical data, thereby improving the model’s adaptability to wearable ECG signals.

As shown in Table 2, the performance patterns of the original model, trained on the CinC2021 dataset, and the fine-tuned model, trained on Phase 1 data differ across evaluation datasets. Although both models demonstrated strong overall performance, their effectiveness varied depending on the data source. The original model, trained on a large and heterogeneous CinC2021 dataset, achieved higher sensitivity on InCorDB (0.960 vs. 0.869) and CODE15 (0.834 vs. 0.733). Furthermore, it achieved a higher sensitivity on Phase 2 (0.982 vs. 0.952), the dataset most representative of our wearable ECG monitoring scenario. In contrast, the fine-tuned model outperformed the original model on Phase 2 in terms of specificity (0.971 vs. 0.914) and F1-score (0.891 vs. 0.777), while also achieving higher AUC (0.992 vs. 0.988) and accuracy (0.969 vs. 0.924). These results suggest that the fine-tuned model, trained with more context-specific data from Phase 1, adapted better to the wearable ECG characteristics represented in Phase 2, but with a trade-off in sensitivity across external datasets. Despite this trade-off, the fine-tuned model consistently exhibited a more balanced relationship between sensitivity and specificity. Such balance is particularly desirable in real-world monitoring applications where both excessive false positives (high sensitivity with low specificity) and missed detection (high specificity with low sensitivity) can negatively impact clinical usability. The superior performance on Phase 2 in terms of specificity and overall classification metrics suggests that exposure to Phase 1 data improved the model’s ability to handle the modified dII-lead signals, making it more suitable for deployment in wearable ECG monitoring.

It is important to note that Phase 2 presents a more challenging scenario than Phase 1, given the longer monitoring period (7 days) and the increased presence of noise and artifacts. As shown in Table 5, Phase 2 includes 145,015 annotated segments, with 19,620 labeled as AFib, and 37,271 artifacts, indicating a high proportion of noisy signals. In comparison, Phase 1 had only 4632 AFib segments and 2391 artifacts. This difference underscores the need for robust models that can operate reliably in noisy, real-world conditions. Additionally, the mean age of Phase 2 subjects (50.7 years) suggests a population more susceptible to arrhythmias, which may have further influenced models’ performance.

The analysis of the fine-tuned model’s performance on Phase 2 revealed contrasting outcomes between the healthy and arrhythmia groups (Fig. 1). In the healthy group, the model achieved high specificity (99.0% for Non-AFib and 99.2% for noise instances) and accuracy (99.0%), demonstrating its reliability in correctly identifying Non-AFib cases. However, since no confirmed AFib events were present in this group, sensitivity could not be meaningfully assessed. In contrast, the arrhythmia group showed robust classification performance, with a sensitivity of 95.2%, specificity of 93.2% for Non-AFib and 97.2% for noise instances, and an F1-score of 90.6%, indicating the model’s effectiveness in detecting AFib episodes. These results suggest that the proposed model is well-suited for automated screening of AFib using wearable ECG devices. Moreover, stratifying the negative class into noisy and Non-AFib ECG segments confirms that the model maintains low false alarm rates related to noisy data.

When compared to previous studies (Table 1), our results remain competitive. Several CNN-based methods, including those proposed by Xia et al.13, Tutuko et al.15, and Phukan et al.18 reported accuracy values above 98%. Liu et al.16 achieved accuracy of 97% using an ensemble learning approach but reported a lower sensitivity (86%). In contrast, Dias et al.19,28 explored image-based CNNs, obtaining sensitivity values between 94.3% and 94.5%, which are comparable to our results. Ribeiro et al.21 also used image-based CNNs, achieving similar results. However, none of these studies accounted for the variability inherent to wearable ECG signals, highlighting the relevance of our approach. The work by Kim et al.20 is more closely aligned with our application, using a single-lead mobile ECG device for wearable monitoring and a ResNet50-based model. Despite its higher complexity and parameter count, their model underperformed compared to ours (Table 1), demonstrating the efficiency and effectiveness of our LiteVGG-11 architecture in wearable ECG analysis.

One of the key clinical advantages of long-term wearable ECG monitoring is its ability to detect paroxysmal AFib, an intermittent and often asymptomatic form of AFib that is difficult to identify using standard short-term ECG recordings or in-clinic examinations. Although we do not explicitly label or differentiate between paroxysmal and persistent AFib in the dataset, the design of our clinical protocol inherently supports the detection of paroxysmal episodes. The use of a wearable device enabling continuous, long-duration monitoring, increases the likelihood of capturing transient AFib events that might otherwise remain undetected in conventional short-term ECGs recordings.

In real-world settings, automated AFib screening must rely on patient-level conclusions, i.e., determining whether a patient exhibited AFib at any point during the entire recording. Our current work does not include an explicit patient-level classification or aggregation strategy, other than the burden across the samples with annotation. However, it is possible to use the trained model to perform continuous inference over the entire ECG recordings, using a sliding 10-s window approach. This would enable the system to detect AFib throughout the full duration of monitoring. Based on these continuous predictions, we can then compute patient-level summaries, such as AFib presence (binary decision), full-time AFib burden, and event timelines, indicating when AFib episodes occurred. It is important to note that the values presented in Table 3 correspond to raw window-level detections. In practical monitoring systems, predictions are typically aggregated over time to estimate AFib burden and identify sustained AFib episodes, which reduces the effective alert burden compared to isolated segment-level predictions.

Moreover, in our study, annotations were performed by a single, experienced cardiologist due to resource constraints. However, our AFib detection model was trained on a publicly available dataset and validated on two additional datasets. While this ensured consistency in labeling, it is well known that inter-expert variability is a challenge in ECG interpretation19. Future work should incorporate multi-expert annotations and assess inter-rater agreement to further strengthen the reliability of the reference labels used for model training and evaluation.

Another potential application of our method is in the detection of postoperative Atrial Fibrillation (POAF), the most common arrhythmic complication after cardiac surgery. POAF is associated with serious consequences, such as hemodynamic instability, heart failure, and stroke. Our findings suggest that continuous wearable ECG monitoring could provide a practical and accurate method for early POAF detection, potentially improving patient outcomes through timely intervention.

Other studies have explored POAF prediction using feature-based approaches29. He et al.30 used a support vector machine with handcrafted features extracted from a single-lead ECG combined with clinical data. Tohyama et al.31 developed a DL model using 12-lead ECGs to predict POAF onset within 7 days post-surgery. Tutuko et al.15 proposed a VGG-16-inspired DL model, achieving accuracy 98.94% and sensitivity/specificity 98.97%. Phukan et al.18 explored hyperparameter-optimized CNN achieving accuracy up to 99.84%. While promising, most of these approaches were not tested in continuous real-world settings, reinforcing the contribution of our work.

Through this evaluation, we gain valuable insights into the strengths and limitations of our models. The fine-tuning process significantly improved accuracy for wearable ECG monitoring, highlighting its real-world applicability. These findings underscore the clinical relevance of our approach and suggest avenues for further research and refinement.

Regarding the interpretable methods, the differences between LIME and SHAP in the context of ECG interpretability highlight the importance of using multiple explanation methods when analyzing DL models. LIME, which constructs a local linear approximation, emphasizes the features that are most influential in small perturbations of the signal. In contrast, SHAP, based on Shapley value principles, evaluates the contribution of each segment within the global distribution of model decisions. Overall, these results suggest that while both methods provide some insights, their interpretations differ based on the underlying assumptions of each approach. SHAP relies on Shapley values, tending to capture more global influences, while LIME provides a localized view of feature importance.

LIME and SHAP methods have inherent limitations that affect their reliability and interpretability, particularly in the context of ECG analysis. LIME builds a surrogate linear model around a specific prediction, but its explanations may vary depending on how the data is perturbed. The selection of the number of segments and perturbations influences the results. Moreover, since LIME is a local method, it does not capture the overall distribution of the model’s behavior, meaning that the importance of features may differ across different samples. SHAP requires more computational costs since it necessitates the evaluation of numerous perturbed versions of the input. Moreover, since it is a global method, it can distribute feature importance across all input segments. Moreover, while model predictions can be validated against labeled data, there is no ground truth for feature importance rankings. This makes it difficult to assess whether an explanation is truly accurate or an artifact of the XAI method.

Our findings demonstrate the potential of DL models for AFib detection in both clinical and wearable ECG settings. However, it is important to acknowledge that our operational metrics (FP/day) are based on statistical extrapolations from a 210-min annotated subset per subject. While this sampling strategy provides a robust estimate of model performance, future studies utilizing fully reviewed, continuous 7-day recordings would be beneficial to further validate these daily alarm rates in real-world clinical workflows. Furthermore, additional post-processing techniques may be incorporated in the future to further enhance these operational metrics. Future research should focus on further optimizing the model and expanding the dataset to include more diverse populations and arrhythmias. Additionally, while explainability methods such as LIME and SHAP offer valuable insights, they also present limitations in practical interpretability. Continued efforts are needed to refine these techniques and explore the integration of domain-specific clinical knowledge to improve the relevance and usability of model explanations.

Methods

The overall structure of our proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 5. The AFib detection model was initially trained on a publicly available 12-lead ECG dataset and subsequently fine-tuned using data from our clinical study, referred to as the TRAdA cohort. This cohort is divided into Phase 1 and Phase 2, in which subjects’ ECG signals were continuously recorded over 24 h and 7 days, respectively. The model processes 10-s ECG segments, outputting the probability of AFib and corresponding interpretability metrics. A detailed description of the datasets used is presented in the following section. All experiments were implemented using Keras API (version 2.15.1) with TensorFlow (version 2.15.1) backend, running Python (version 3.11.9) to build our model. For model interpretability, we employed the SHAP library (version 0.46.0) to generate explanations of the model’s predictions.

The model was trained using the publicly available PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2021 dataset (CinC2021), which contains 88,252 annotated 12-lead ECG recordings12. This dataset comprises 111 diagnostic classes, including 5,255 signals labeled as AFib and 8,374 as Atrial Flutter (AFlut). In this study, AFib and AFlut were grouped into a single positive class. This consolidation is justified by the clinical context of wearable-based screening, in which the detection of either rhythm triggers a similar clinical workflow, including expert review, diagnostic confirmation with a standard 12-lead ECG, and assessment of thromboembolic risk. Furthermore, this grouping reflects the known limitations of single-lead ECG in reliably distinguishing these rhythms solely on waveform morphology. It also mitigates the impact of labeling inconsistencies observed in large-scale public datasets, such as the Ningbo sub-dataset from CinC202133, thereby improving the robustness of the model. After this consolidation, the dataset included a total of 13,597 signals labeled as AFib for training purposes.

For external validation, we used a private dataset, referred to as InCor-DB, which comprises 115,581 annotated 12-lead ECGs, containing 52 distinctive cardiac diagnoses, including 8219 AFib and 1309 AFlut cases19,28,33. These data were collected between 2017 and 2020 at a specialized tertiary cardiology hospital in Brazil. Consistent with the strategy applied to the CinC2021 dataset, we merged the AFib and AFlut classes into a unified AFib category.

Additionally, we used the dataset from the Clinical Outcomes in Digital Electrocardiography (CODE15) study for further external validation. This dataset was obtained from the Telehealth Network of Minas Gerais (TNMG) in Brazil34. It comprises digital 12-lead ECG recordings collected between 2010 and 2016, covering 811 municipalities in the state of Minas Gerais. For this study, we used the CODE15 subset27, which includes 345,779 ECG exams, representing 15% of the total CODE study population. Among these, 7,033 exams were labeled as AFib. The dataset contains seven diagnostic classes.

Clinical experiment

For our continuous wearable ECG signal monitoring experiment, data were collected using a medical Device (MD) sensor, a certified class IIa device for ECG acquisition compliant with the EU Medical Device Regulation 2017/745 (Certificate CR-03-1229-813-23 02 / FI-MF-000024281). The sensor was configured with a sampling rate of 128 Hz. It was connected via a custom adapter configured in a modified lead II (dII) arrangement, with the reference electrode placed on the right shoulder and the positive electrode on the left abdomen. This setup approximates one of the standard ECG lead vectors in a wearable form, enabling continuous ECG monitoring in a real-world clinical setting. Our original AFib model6 was initially trained on a traditional dII-lead configuration. Our Clinical Experiments were conducted in two independent phases, as described below.

Phase 1: We collected continuous ECG recordings from 56 subjects, 28 subjects with a prior diagnosis of AFib, either persistent or paroxysmal. Data acquisition was performed over a 24-h period using wearable devices. After acquisition, each recording was segmented into 30-s windows, from which 120 windows per subject were randomly selected for annotation by an experienced cardiologist. This process yielded 60 min of annotated data per subject6.

Phase 2: This phase involved 117 subjects, including 59 subjects previously diagnosed with persistent or paroxysmal AFib, and 58 subjects with normal sinus rhythm. The recordings were conducted under conditions similar to Phase 1, but over a continuous 7-day period. Post- acquisition, ECG signals were segmented into randomly selected 10-s windows, resulting in a total of 210 min of annotated data per subject.

Annotation process: In phase 1, each selected window was labeled by an experienced cardiologist into four classes: (i) AFib, (ii) Normal, (iii) Other abnormalities, and (iv) Artifacts. Since the model’s objective is a binary classification of AFib vs. Non-AFib, the Normal, Other abnormality, and Artifacts categories (ii-iv) were consolidated into a single Non-AFib class. In phase 2, windows were annotated into three classes: (i) AFib, (ii) Non-AFib, and (iii) Artifacts. The same criterion was applied, treating categories (ii) and (iii) as Non-AFib for training and evaluation purposes.

In Phase 1, each annotated 30-s annotated window was further split into three non-overlapping 10-s segments, resulting in a total of 20,160 annotated segments, including 4,632 AFib instances. In Phase 2, the annotated dataset consisted of 145,015 10-s segments, containing 19,620 AFib instances, based on 210 min of data per subject across 117 subjects. Preprocessing

We adopted the same preprocessing steps proposed in Soares et al.6. Before model training, all ECG signals underwent a comprehensive preprocessing pipeline to ensure consistency across datasets. For the CinC2021, InCor-DB and CODE15 datasets, which consisted of 12-lead signals, only the dII lead was extracted. These signals were then resampled to 128 Hz to match the sampling frequency of the MD sensor.

To ensure uniform input dimensions, signals shorter than 10 s were zero-padded, while those longer than 10 s were truncated. This process ensured that all signals across the datasets had a uniform sampling rate, duration, and number of channels, resulting in vectors with a consistent shape of 1280x1 (samples per channel) for each ECG segment.

To reduce baseline drift and low-frequency noise, a second-order Butterworth high-pass filter with a 1 Hz cutoff frequency was applied. This was followed by a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 40 Hz cutoff to isolate the frequency range most relevant for diagnostic analysis. Finally, all signals were normalized to zero mean and unit variance, providing standardized inputs for model training and inference.

Deep learning model

We employed the LiteVGG architecture, a lightweight CNN model derived from the well-known VGGNet, originally proposed by Soares et al.26. To optimize computational efficiency while preserving classification performance on ECG signals, several key architectural modifications were introduced. Depthwise separable convolutions replaced traditional convolutional layers, substantially reducing the number of parameters while maintaining effective feature extraction capabilities. Additionally, the number of filters per convolutional block and number of hidden units in the dense layers were reduced. To further streamline the model, we employed global average pooling instead of conventional flattening techniques, minimizing overall complexity. The final classification layer used a sigmoid activation function for binary classification. The resulting model, referred to as LiteVGG-11, comprises 11 layers and contains a total of 20,365 parameters, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

The model was trained using the Adam optimizer, with a learning rate of for the original model training phase and for the fine-tuned model training. Binary cross-entropy was used as the loss function, and training was performed for up to 200 epochs, with early stopping applied (patience = 50 epochs based on validation loss). To address class imbalance, a weighted loss function was used, where the class weights were computed based on the inverse class frequencies. Training was conducted using a batch size of 128. A learning rate reduction strategy was implemented, reducing the learning rate by a factor of 0.2 if the validation loss plateaued for 30 epochs.

The training process consisted of two sequential phases. First, the model was trained on the CinC2021 dataset from scratch to learn general ECG features. Second, a fine-tuning phase was performed using data from Phase 1 of our clinical experiment. During fine-tuning, all layers were frozen except the dense (fully connected) layers, enabling adaptation to the Phase 1 ECG distribution while preserving the general representations learned from CinC2021. This fine-tuning step was essential for optimizing performance on wearable ECG recorded using a modified dII-lead configuration in our continuous monitoring study.

Model evaluation

To evaluate model performance, we compared the original model, trained solely on the CinC2021 dataset, with the fine-tuned model, which was further trained using data from Phase 1 of our clinical study. The objective of this evaluation was to assess the model’s effectiveness across multiple datasets and signal acquisition configurations, thereby examining its ability to generalize across diverse real-world scenarios. Figure 5 presents an overview of the evaluation framework used in this study.

For performance evaluation, we considered the following metrics: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. These metrics provided a comprehensive assessment of each model’s performance. To quantify the uncertainty of these estimates in the primary external validation scenario (Phase 2), we calculated 95% confidence intervals for each metric. The results enabled a detailed comparison between the original and fine-tuned models across the three datasets, highlighting differences and improvements, particularly in Phase 2, where data were acquired using the modified dII lead configuration.

To further investigate the impact of signal quality on model performance, we conducted a stratified analysis of the negative class in the Phase 2 dataset. In the original classification task, both Non-AFib rhythms with clear ECG and artifact segments were grouped into the Non-AFib class. For the stratified evaluation, the negative class was separated into two subcategories: (i) Non-AFib physiological rhythms and (ii) noisy segments, which include recordings affected by noise, motion artifacts, or other signal disturbances that impair human interpretability. Model predictions were then analyzed separately for these subgroups in order to assess whether signal artifacts contributed disproportionately to misclassifications, particularly false positive AFib detections. This analysis provides additional insight into the robustness of the model under varying signal quality conditions commonly encountered in wearable ECG monitoring.

Model training and internal validation

During model development, both models were evaluated using a 5-fold cross-validation strategy. To prevent potential data leakage caused by correlations between ECG segments from the same subject, all data splits were performed at the subject level. Specifically, we employed a Group K-Fold strategy in which the grouping variable corresponded to the subject identifier. This approach ensured that all 10-s ECG segments belonging to a given subject were assigned to the same fold and never appeared simultaneously in both the training and testing sets.

For each data split, approximately 80% of the subjects were used for training and 20% as testing for the cross-validation. Within the training subset, 20% of the data was further reserved as an internal validation subset during model training. This validation set was used exclusively to monitor the validation loss during training.

Following cross-validation, each model was retrained using its respective complete training dataset in order to maximize the amount of data available for the final model.

To better align the evaluation with the intended application of continuous monitoring, we performed a patient-level analysis using the data from Phase 2. Furthermore, to assess the operational impact of false positive detections in a continuous monitoring scenario, we computed the false positive rate in terms of detections per hour and per day. False positive segments were defined as 10-s windows predicted as AFib when the ground-truth annotation corresponded to either Non-AFib rhythms or artifact/noisy segments. The total duration of the analyzed recordings was obtained by converting the number of evaluated segments into hours, considering that each segment corresponds to a 10-s window (360 segments per hour). The false positive rate per hour was then calculated as the ratio between the number of false positive segments and the total recording duration in hours. This value was also expressed as an estimated number of false positive detections per day by multiplying the hourly rate by 24.

To mitigate the high frequency of false alarms inherent in window-level analysis, we implemented a hierarchical temporal aggregation strategy. This post-processing stage transforms raw 10-s window predictions into 1-min diagnostic segments by grouping six consecutive non-overlapping windows. Within each 1-min epoch, a majority voting scheme was employed as the decision logic; a positive AFib detection was registered only if a majority consensus was reached, requiring the number of AFib-positive windows to exceed the number of Non-AFib windows. This smoothing technique was designed to filter out transient misclassifications caused by motion artifacts or isolated signal disturbances. The false positive rates were subsequently recalculated under this aggregated framework to quantify the reduction in the daily false-alarm burden while maintaining clinical relevance.

Explainable AI methods

To analyze individual ECG signals and better understand model behavior, we employed two widely used local explainability methods: LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)36 and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)37. Both approaches provide post-hoc explanations by identifying regions of the input signal that most influence the model prediction.

LIME analysis

LIME36 is a model-agnostic approach that generates local surrogate models to approximate the behavior of the original model around a specific prediction. In this study, LIME was implemented using the “lime-for-time-series” library. Each 10-s ECG segment (1280 samples recorded at 128 Hz) was divided into 80 non-overlapping temporal segments of equal length (approximately 16 samples per segment) which were treated as interpretable features.

To generate the neighborhood of perturbed samples required by LIME, random perturbations were applied to the ECG signal. A binary perturbation matrix was used to determine whether a segment remained unchanged or was perturbed. When a segment was perturbed, its values were replaced by the mean value of that segment, effectively removing the waveform morphology within that interval while preserving the global amplitude scale.

For each ECG segment, 500 perturbed signals were generated. The trained classifier was then used to obtain prediction probabilities for each perturbed signal. The cosine distance between each perturbation vector and the original signal representation (in which all segments are active) was used to measure similarity between perturbations. These distances were converted into weights using an exponential kernel function with a kernel width of 0.25, assigning higher importance to perturbations that were more similar to the original signal.

A weighted linear regression model was then fitted using the perturbation vectors as inputs and the predicted probabilities as targets. The resulting regression coefficients represent the contribution of each temporal segment to the model prediction.

SHAP analysis

SHAP37 is based on cooperative game theory, using Shapley values to quantify the contribution of each feature to a model’s prediction. Unlike LIME, which weights perturbed instances based on similarity to the original input, SHAP estimates feature importance through Shapley value attibution. This difference provides an alternative perspective on the contribution of features to the prediction process. In this study, SHAP was conducted using the shap library.

SHAP explanations were computed using the GradientExplainer implementation from the shap library, which leverages gradient-based approximations to estimate Shapley values. As in the LIME analysis, the ECG signal was interpreted as a time series composed of temporal features.

A subset of 10,000 ECG signals from the dataset was randomly sampled to be used as the background reference distribution required for SHAP value estimation. For each selected ECG instance, SHAP values were computed for all time points of the signal, resulting in attribution scores that quantify how each portion of the ECG contributes to the final prediction.

To facilitate visualization, SHAP values were aggregated using a moving-window approach and displayed as heatmaps over the ECG waveform. This representation enables the identification of signal regions that strongly influence the model decision, highlighting temporal patterns potentially associated with AFib.

Explanation’s visualization

The SHAP values were aggregated into 80 non-overlapping temporal segments by averaging the values within each segment, enabling a direct comparison with the segment contributions obtained from the LIME analysis. Both sets of explanations were visualized as heatmaps overlaid on the ECG signal, using a diverging colormap centered at zero. In these visualizations, segments contributing to the positive AFib class are shown in red shades, whereas segments contributing to the negative Non-AFib class are shown in blue shades. For each explanation, the colormap was scaled according to the segment with the largest absolute contribution, ensuring consistent representation of the relative importance across the signal.
