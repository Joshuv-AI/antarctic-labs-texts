Live video versus audio-only emergency calls in simulated emergency medical dispatch: a randomized mixed-methods study
Authors: Mario Krammel, Benedikt Degischer, Florian Kahlfuss, Josef Michael Lintschinger, Lena Reischmann, Mojca Hriberšek, Andreas Gleiss, Daniel Grassmann, Thomas Hamp, Alexander Lagara, Patrick Glaninger, Sebastian Schnaubelt, Christina Hafner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDispatchers in emergency medical coordination centers face the challenge that bystanders can usually only provide verbal information during medical emergency calls. However, accurate information is crucial for dispatching appropriate resources. With the rise of smartphone use, video calls offer new possibilities. However, little is known about the broader impact of video calls on call duration, accuracy, and usability during emergency calls.MethodsTwenty dispatchers and 80 laypersons participated in a randomized simulation study. Each dispatcher managed four standardized emergency scenarios, two via audio-only and two via video call, using a web-based platform (FlagMii® EML). Outcomes investigated total call duration, accuracy of the dispatch decision compared to the simulated scenario, and occurrence of technical issues. A linear mixed model was used to analyze the primary outcome. Furthermore, a focus group was held with dispatchers (n = 9) and qualitative data analysis was performed.ResultsThe median duration of emergency calls was significantly longer in the video group (307 s, IQR 249–369) compared to the audio group (222 s, IQR 175–288), corresponding to a 40% increase (p < 0.001). The accuracy of the dispatch codes with the simulated emergency was higher in the video group (41.1% vs. 25.0%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (OR 2.71, p = 0.085). The focus group identified six topics of interest: medical, technical, legal/ethical, psychological, institutional, and human/environmental aspects. While video was considered valuable for verifying first aid and assessing urgency, concerns were raised about technical reliability, dispatcher workload, emotional burden, and cultural acceptability.ConclusionsReal-time video emergency calls can support medical dispatchers and may improve assessment accuracy, but at the cost of longer call duration. Implementation should be guided by careful consideration of various factors including technical, ethical, and psychological factors. Background

Emergency medical dispatchers of Emergency Medical Services (EMS) face the challenge that bystanders of a medical emergency are typically limited to providing verbal information only, which is often incomplete or inaccurate due to stress, limited medical knowledge, or language and cultural barriers. However, accurate situation assessment is essential to ensure appropriate resource allocation and to minimize both over-triage and crowding of EMS and other emergency services such as police or fire brigade [1, 2]. With the widespread use of smartphones, real-time video calls have become technically feasible. This development opens up new opportunities for communication between dispatchers and bystanders during emergency calls [3–5].

A limited number of studies have explored the use of video emergency calls, particularly in the context of dispatcher-assisted cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and have shown that video support can improve the quality of bystander CPR [6]. Linderoth et al. reported that video-emergency calls altered dispatchers’ perception of the patient in about half of cases [7].

Despite these promising results, little is known about the broader impact of real-time video emergency calls, particularly regarding call duration and the dispatchers’ ability to assess and interpret emergency situations. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the duration of real-time video emergency calls with audio-only calls and to assess the accuracy, usability, and the perception of both bystanders and emergency medical dispatchers in simulated emergencies. In addition, key aspects related to the practical implementation of video calls were explored in discussions with dispatchers.

Methods

Study design

This mixed-methods study containing a simulation study and a qualitative analysis of a focus group was conducted in collaboration between the Medical University of Vienna, the EMS Vienna, and the Ludwig Boltzmann Institute Digital Health and Patient Safety in October 2024 in Vienna, Austria. Ethical approval was obtained by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (Reference number: 1332/2024). The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects and was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06598592) prior to participant enrolment.

Simulation study

The simulation study was conducted over four days in October 2024 at two locations; at a shopping center in Vienna where 80 participants were recruited, and at the dispatch center of the EMS Vienna where dispatchers handled the emergency calls.

A total of 20 dispatchers, all of whom provided written informed consent and had at least six months of professional experience as dispatchers, participated in the study. Each participant attended a one-hour training session on the use of a web-based software for real-time video emergency calls (FlagMii® EML, Frequentis). In a randomized order, the dispatchers were confronted with four emergency calls corresponding to four predefined emergency scenarios (Supplement 1). The randomization was organized such that for each subgroup of four consecutive dispatchers, the dispatchers were assigned to a sequence of the four scenarios according to a 4 × 4 Williams design (Supplement 2). Two calls were conducted as audio-only calls, while the other two were conducted as video-assisted calls. The allocation of audio-only vs. video-assisted and of actors (actor 1 vs. actor 2) followed a randomization list prepared by the statistician. Allocation was performed by members of the study team on-site for dispatchers as well as for bystanders depending on the randomization list. For audio-only and also for video-assisted calls, the proQA Medical Priority Dispatch System (MPDS) V0.13 software was used. Dispatchers had to adhere to the MPDS protocols as required for MPDS use. In case of a video-assisted call, dispatchers were allowed to take everything they can see in the video call as given, even if they had not asked the specific question. The four scenarious were designed in an order that each scenario would fit one specific MPDS code only (including chief complaint, response determinant, sub-determinant, and suffix).

Individuals aged between 18 and 70 years with frequent mobile phone use, sufficient German language skills, and no professional medical background were recruited at a shopping center. After providing written informed consent, each participant selected either an iOS or Android device according to personal preference. Following standardized instructions on initiating emergency calls with the assigned device, one of two randomly assigned professional actors simulated an emergency scenario. Participants then contacted the dispatcher, with calls randomized to either conventional audio-only or video-assisted format (CONSORT Flow Diagram, Supplement 3).

All calls were recorded via screen capture at the dispatcher’s workstation and with an external backup camera. Recordings were anonymized prior to analysis. The primary outcome was the total duration of an emergency call, defined as the time from call initiation to hang-up by the dispatcher. Secondary outcomes included duration until alerting of EMS crews, overall duration of video stream, duration from start of the call to established video connection, questions asked per protocol, accuracy, usability and satisfaction ratings provided by both bystanders and dispatchers, as well as documentation of technical issues.

After each scenario, participants and dispatchers rated usability and satisfaction on a 5-point Likert scale. Any technical issues encountered during the trials were documented by the study team.

Qualitative study

Following the simulation study, dispatchers were invited to participate in a moderated 90-minute focus group held in person in German in a conference room of the emergency institution. A predetermined, self-developed question guide was used to explore expectations and concerns regarding video-emergency calls. The topics of discussion were the overall impression of video-emergency calls, own experience with video-emergency calls, doubts and concerns regarding video-emergency calls and the integration into the routine work (Supplement 4). The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) were applied. Prior to the start of the focus group oral consent for participation and for audio recording was provided. A total of nine dispatchers participated in the discussion, including one female and eight male individuals. Next to the participants, only the moderator and two researchers familiar with the project were present.

The focus group discussion was recorded and subsequently transcribed. The transcript was analyzed following the method by Braun and Clarke [8]. In the first step, two researchers independently conducted a thematic analysis. Through an interactive process within the research team the relevant aspects were identified and clustered into themes.

Statistical analysis

As no preliminary data were available for the primary outcome (duration of emergency calls with or without video), an internal pilot study was conducted with 12 dispatchers and 48 bystanders. Based on these data, it was estimated that detecting a mean difference of 20 seconds between video and audio calls (two-sided significance level of 5%, power ≥ 80%) would require a sample size beyond the feasible number of 20 dispatchers and 80 bystanders. It was therefore decided to stop recruitment after this maximally possible number and to publish the results.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) with a two-sided significance level of 5%. Due to right-skewed distribution, the duration outcomes were log-transformed prior to analysis. The primary outcome was analyzed using a linear mixed model, with scenario, actor, and call type (video vs. audio) specified as fixed factors, and dispatcher included as a random factor with a compound symmetry covariance structure. Conditional model residuals were checked for outliers and leverage points. Groupwise means and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) as estimated by the mixed model were back-transformed to the original scale, resulting in geometric means. The back-transformed mean difference is the ratio of geometric means, reported as relative increase (%) with 95% CI.

The accuracy of the emergency code was analyzed as a binary outcome using a generalized linear mixed model with otherwise analogous specification as for the primary outcome, resulting in an estimated odds ratio between groups. A code was counted as accurate only if the chief complaint AND the response determinant AND the sub-determinant AND the suffix were correct.

Primary outcome

As shown in Table 3, the overall median duration of emergency calls was 268 s (IQR 208–342 s). When stratified by study group, a significant difference was observed, with an estimated mean duration of 229 s (CI: 205 – 255 s) in the audio group and 320 s (CI: 287–357 s) in the video group. Video calls were, on average, 40.0% longer than audio calls (95% CI: 25.5%–56.2%; p < 0.001). A sensitivity analysis after removing a gross residual outlier leaves these results practically unchanged (38.1% longer than audio calls, p < 0.001). The mean duration for successful video-calls was 296s (CI: 262-335s). Raw data is shown in Supplement 5.

Secondary outcomes

Further analysis of the time from call initiation to the alerting of an EMS unit revealed significant differences between the audio and video groups. In the audio group, the estimated mean time was 129 s (CI: 118 – 141 s), whereas in the video group it was 239 s (CI: 219 – 261 s). This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) with the video group requiring 84.8% (CI: 62.4–110.3%) more time to alert the emergency services compared with the audio group.

The median time from call initiation to the start of video transmission was 137 s (IQR 119–167 s). The median duration of the video stream itself was 165 s (IQR 111–210 s).

A total of 10 out of 40 (25%) matched response codes were identified in the audio group, while 16 out of 39 (41%) matched codes were found in the video group. However, the observed doubling of the odds for accuracy for video compared to audio (odds ratio = 2.71) did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.085).

The number of questions asked per group exhibited minimal variation, with an average of 17 questions asked in the video group (CI: 16–18) and 17 questions in the audio group (CI: 16–19).

In contrast to the callers, dispatchers evaluated video calls more critically. While satisfaction and comfort levels were very high for audio calls, the proportion of positive responses decreased notably for the video call. For example, 85.0% of dispatchers fully agreed that they were satisfied with audio calls compared to only 56.4% for video calls. Moreover, negative ratings were more frequent in video interactions, indicating that dispatchers were less comfortable and satisfied with video calls than with audio calls. Responses regarding the usability of the emergency call technology are presented in the supplementary material (Supplement 6).

Technical issues

In 25 out of a total of 40 video emergency calls, the video transmission was successful. In the remaining 15 cases, 10 were attributable to user error and 5 to technical issues.

The user-related errors that were identified included, in particular, difficulties in activating the speaker function, which meant that the acoustic instructions for activation could not be used, failure to find the access link, and problems with activating the camera.

The technical difficulties experienced were attributable to disruption of the connection and errors in transmission. In such instances, this resulted in unsuccessful transmission of images to the dispatcher, despite the participants having completed the requisite steps in the correct manner.

Qualitative study

In the focus group, six major topics of interest were identified, namely medical, technical, psychological, human & environmental, legal & ethical aspects and institutional requirements (Fig. 1). Overall, participants agreed that video calls represent a valuable tool for emergency care, but that their success depends on simplicity, flexibility, and awareness for both technical and human limitations.

Thematic aspects of the focus group

In the area of medical aspects, participants highlighted that having a visual overview of the on-site situation is a major benefit, particularly in differentiating the urgency of emergency calls and verifying the correct performance of first aid instructions.

The category of technical aspects discussed challenges related to device compatibility, network stability, and the need for a consistent, intuitive user interface across devices, and software versions. Dispatchers noted that the software should be as simple and intuitive as possible.

Legal and ethical aspects were discussed in the context of data protection, data storage, and cultural sensitivities that might limit the use of video. One dispatcher reported, “If they don’t want that for cultural or any other reasons, then so be it, and I will continue as usual.”

Institutional requirements included the necessity of clear process management, dispatcher autonomy in deciding on video use, and structured training prior to implementation. They stated, “A very useful tool, but only when needed and at one’s own decision.”

The thematic area of human and environmental aspects highlighted practical barriers such as poor video quality, background noise, language barriers, and difficulties for elderly users.

An especially noteworthy finding was related to psychological aspects including cognitive overload from multi-tasking as well as the emotional impact of witnessing distressing situations without the sense of success of being able to help the patient directly, typically experienced by EMS personnel on scene.

Discussion

In this randomized, mixed-methods simulation study, real-time video during emergency calls increased overall call duration and prolonged the time to the actual alert of EMS units compared to audio-only calls. However, the accuracy of dispatch coding was higher when video was used. Dispatchers reported lower satisfaction and comfort with video than with audio, and a relevant proportion of video connections were technically flawed. Qualitative analysis highlighted medical utility alongside technical, legal–ethical, psychological, institutional, and human/environmental concerns. Together, these findings suggest that video can add clinically relevant information at the cost of time and cognitive load, and that implementation must explicitly address reliability and workflow.

Our results align with prior work showing that adding live scene video can change dispatchers’ perception and, subsequently, influence the emergency response. In a large observational study, Linderoth et al. found that live video from callers altered the assessed criticality and changed the dispatched response in more than a quarter of cases [7]. Feasibility and acceptability have also been demonstrated in helicopter EMS dispatch [3] and in a dispatch-center pilot study [4]. Scoping reviews synthesize that video emergency calls are generally feasible and acceptable to callers and dispatchers but emphasize heterogeneity in implementation and persistent knowledge gaps beyond dispatcher-assisted CPR [6, 9]. A before–after study in Norway suggested improved dispatch precision when video was available to the emergency medical communication center [10]. These convergent data support the potential of video to refine triage—consistent with our signal towards higher coding accuracy—while underscoring that benefits are context-dependent.

Mechanistically, video can supply high-value cues (breathing pattern, skin color, scene hazards) and enables verification/coaching of first aid. In cardiac arrest simulations and real-world evaluations, video-assisted dispatcher guidance has been shown to be feasible and associated with improved bystander CPR performance [7], providing a plausible pathway by which video may enhance assessment and early care. Our qualitative results that video helped verify first-aid actions mirror these observations.

At the same time, our data quantify important trade-offs: Even tough longer calls and delayed alerting of resources in this study might partly be explained by the limited routine of the dispatchers in using video for handling emergency calls, these delays probably reflect set-up/connection time and the additional cognitive steps of processing visual input. Such potential time delays in alerting of EMS units when video calls are used, must be considered, if one aims to implement such a system in routine practice. As long as little evidence regarding the risks and benefits of video assisted dispatch, local guidance for dispatchers must be provided when video should not be used due to the risk of delaying the emergency response. In addition, future versions of dispatch systems should be modified and also include video assistance pathways.

Dispatchers’ lower satisfaction with video in our study is congruent with qualitative reports describing technology-related barriers and cognitive overload from multitasking [7]. These considerations argue for workflow rules that prioritize rapid resource activation (e.g., “audio-first, alert-first, video-second”), clear abort criteria for poor connections, and targeted indications (e.g., ambiguous situations, first-aid coaching, language barriers), rather than universal video use. Scoping reviews similarly recommend simple, robust platforms and dispatcher autonomy to decide when video adds value [7, 9].

The technical issues in our simulation echo implementation studies that emphasize network stability, device heterogeneity, and user-interface simplicity as prerequisites [4, 6, 9, 11]. Early implementation should therefore include platform hardening, redundancy (instant fallback to audio), and training that explicitly rehearses video setup while maintaining core call structure.

Strengths of our study include randomization of call modality across standardized scenarios and systematic measurement of timing. Limitations include simulation rather than real-world calls, a possible selection bias due to recruitment of participants in a shopping center, a single-city and high-resource setting with limited applicability to other regions, a fixed device/software choice, and limited dispatcher training/exposure to video before testing; our study was powered for duration, not for accuracy, and did not investigate effects on clinical outcomes. Finally, perceived psychological burden and cultural acceptability warrant ongoing monitoring and staff support.

Additionally, the dispatchers received only one hour of training. This might have an effect on usability. Moreover, only one-dispatch center was included, which limits external validity.

Furthermore, our study was originally powered for the primary duration outcome, but the sample size necessary according to the power calculation of the internal pilot was not attained due to limited ressources (duration still turned out to differ significantly between groups, though). Moreover, the study was not powered for dispatch accuracy. Therefore, the study was hypothesis-generating rather than for confirmation of this outcome. Clinical outcomes were not investigated.

Conclusion

Real-time video emergency calls can support medical dispatchers and may improve assessment accuracy, but at the cost of longer call duration. Video emergency calls appear most promising as a selective adjunct to audio, particularly where visual verification is likely to improve safety or quality. Safe adoption will require alert-first workflows, dispatcher-centered usability, and resilience to technical failure. Future research should test patient-centered and system outcomes (e.g., time-to-alert, correct priority, adverse events, bystander action quality) in real-world deployments, ideally via stepped-wedge or cluster-randomized designs, while measuring dispatcher workload and wellbeing.
