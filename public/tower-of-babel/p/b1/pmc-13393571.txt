Integrating Psychological Care into Pediatric Sports Medicine: Levels of Collaboration and Common Referral Concerns
Authors: Chelsey Bowman, Sabrina Gretkierewicz, Jacqueline M. Pabis, Abigail Bushnell, Rachel Sweenie, Casey Lawless, Jamie Shoop, Morgan Daffin, Emily J. Stapleton, Kirby Knapp, Catherine Butz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is well established that psychological factors impact sport injuries, recovery, and return to sport among youth athletes. Medical associations and sport governing bodies have advocated for the assessment and intervention of mental health concerns among athletes. However, there is less research on methods of incorporating pediatric psychology services within sports medicine (SM) settings. By drawing on the framework of the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration–Health Resources and Services Administration for understanding and implementing medical and behavioral health integration, this paper outlines various approaches to utilizing pediatric psychologists’ expertise in the treatment of youth athletes. Specifically, the paper focuses on how pediatric psychologists can support patient needs, contribute to research goals, and expand reach in SM clinics through coordinated, colocated, and integrated levels of care.Key Concepts(1)Despite calls to assess and address athletes’ mental health, there is little guidance on how to integrate psychology into pediatric sports medicine (SM) clinics.(2)Applying and adapting a preexisting framework for behavioral health and medical specialty integration provides guidance on how to integrate psychology into pediatric SM clinics.(3)Thoughtful relationships and integration between psychology and pediatric SM clinics have the potential to expand access, reduce stigma, and further the reach of SM clinics.

Introduction

Psychological factors impact sports injuries and recovery, with studies finding that premorbid psychological diagnoses can negatively impact postsurgical outcomes, even increasing the likelihood of additional procedures [1,2]. This work has resulted in growing awareness of the importance of incorporating psychological care into pediatric sports medicine (SM) clinics, including screening for mental health needs such as anxiety or depression and supporting return from injury [3]. However, it remains challenging to establish pediatric psychology services within SM settings. It is necessary to illuminate both the scope of practice of pediatric psychologists in SM clinics as well as potential levels of care that detail integration of psychology services within SM settings.

Pediatric psychology is a psychology specialization that focuses on working with youth with medical diagnoses in multidisciplinary teams. Pediatric psychologists are trained in the biopsychosocial model, which emphasizes the overlap among biological, psychological, and social factors in influencing health [4], as well as the social ecological model, which posits that human development is influenced by transactional settings spanning the individual, family and peers, community, and health care system [5]. In SM settings, pediatric psychologists are well positioned to support youth athletes’ unique needs using evidence-based therapeutic approaches while simultaneously addressing health behaviors, as well as addressing the social, cultural, and structural forces that shape their concerns. In the broader pediatric medical literature, studies have shown benefits (eg, patient satisfaction, physician acceptability, increased access to care) of psychology’s integration within medical specialties [6,7]. Given that stigma remains a significant deterrent among athletes needing mental health support [8], integrating pediatric psychologists into SM clinics may help break through stigma and optimize comprehensive care. Pediatric psychologists are well suited to serve as an integral part of an interdisciplinary care team within SM clinics; depending on the institution, they may collaborate with a team including SM surgeons, nonoperative SM physicians, athletic trainers, physical and occupational therapists, registered dietitians, social workers, child life specialists, and sports psychiatrists. Pediatric psychologists can partner with existing multidisciplinary team members to establish their scope of practice, navigate any areas of role overlap particularly with closely related disciplines such as social work and psychiatry, and determine the level of behavioral health integration with the most utility for the unique needs of their institution’s SM clinic.

This paper aims to demonstrate how models of psychology integration could be applied to pediatric SM while delineating benefits and drawbacks of each level. Through case examples, the paper highlights the unique skillsets of pediatric psychologists and how they can employ these skills in SM settings in collaboration with other disciplines.

Coordinated care

Due to its relatively low demands on institutional resources, coordinated care—where patients are referred to an external psychologist—is often employed as the foundational level in medical specialty clinics.

Advantages

Athletes prefer providers who understand the unique demands of sport and value the role of athletic participation in their lives [10]. Given the limited number of psychologists specializing in SM, it is beneficial for medical providers to identify community-based psychologists with relevant expertise to whom they can refer. This model is well suited for addressing general mental health concerns affecting performance or recovery (eg, anxiety, depression) and more severe or persistent mental health conditions (eg, bipolar disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder) beyond sport-specific concerns. The model also offers practical benefits that cater to athletes’ unique schedules. For example, extended appointment times (eg, nights, weekends), increased session frequency, longer treatment duration, and alternative modalities such as telehealth are options that may be limited in other levels but may be ideal for youth athletes juggling school and sports. Stigma can also be addressed by allowing for increased confidentiality and the potential for stronger therapeutic rapport as some athletes may feel more comfortable discussing sensitive issues outside the athletic or health care context.

Disadvantages

On the other hand, some athletes may feel that being referred to a mental health provider outside of the clinic is stigmatizing as it separates their mental and physical health complaints. The lack of immediate cross-disciplinary collaboration and access to shared records limits timely input from psychologists and SM providers, potentially delaying integrated treatment planning. While this model can offer flexibility, scheduling challenges—such as the inability to offer same-day appointments—often require families to commit to an additional visit at another site, which may feel burdensome, especially when concerns are mild or do not require ongoing care. There is also the potential for cost barriers to arise, as referrals need to be sent between systems, and community psychologists may not accept insurance. Medical providers without colocated or integrated psychologists may face pressure to assess psychological concerns and make appropriate referrals, despite limited clinic time or behavioral health training. Furthermore, when referrals lack clear explanation, families may view psychological services as stigmatizing or irrelevant to the athlete’s physical condition.

Coordinated care case example

Fatima is a 13-year-old multisport athlete with a history of performance-related anxiety in academic and athletic settings. Fatima sustained an elbow dislocation in soccer. Following her postoperative rehabilitation, she was referred to an external psychologist due to concerns that anxiety symptoms were impeding her return to sport despite having been cleared by her surgeon.

Fatima is a 13-year-old multisport athlete with a history of performance-related anxiety in academic and athletic settings. Fatima sustained an elbow dislocation in soccer. Following her postoperative rehabilitation, she was referred to an external psychologist due to concerns that anxiety symptoms were impeding her return to sport despite having been cleared by her surgeon.

Initial psychological assessment with Fatima revealed exacerbated anxiety, which contributed to avoidance behaviors (eg, subbing herself out in games), academic difficulties, and social withdrawal. Fatima subsequently engaged in exposure and response prevention, which involves graded exposure to athletic activities and sporting environments that provoke anxiety and managing symptoms until discomfort subsides. Eight sessions occurred biweekly in the evenings after practice, with some telehealth sessions. Given her heightened sensitivity to evaluation by her peers, she preferred meeting with someone not connected to the SM clinic as she had teammates also being treated there. The psychologist also coordinated with school personnel to implement accommodations addressing anxiety-related academic challenges (eg, breaks in class, taking tests alone). Over the course of treatment, she demonstrated progressive improvement in symptom management and functional capacity, including fully reintegrating into sports.

Co-located care

Co-located care, in which psychological services are offered within the same health system as medical services, is a common level of care in pediatric medical settings, particularly primary care [11].

Advantages

Advantages include the potential to have appointments with multiple providers on the same day, enabling coordination with other specialties (eg, physical therapy [PT]), further supporting a multidisciplinary approach. Co-located care can also address stigma-related barriers and health inequities. For example, hospitals tend to accept the same insurances across provider specialties and mental health providers affiliated with hospitals are significantly more likely to accept public insurance [12], increasing access to care. Research shows that, compared to coordinated care models, colocated care is associated with increased follow-up rates [13]. Co-located care offers timely psychological services with providers who consult directly with SM providers. Through collaboration and referral experience, psychologists also gain specialized experience with athlete-specific injuries and physicians can deepen their understanding of how mental health concerns can interfere with the recovery process.

Disadvantages

Co-located care places higher demand (eg, staff, space, scheduling) on the SM clinic. When services are located within the same hospital system but not the same clinic, additional personnel are needed to schedule and obtain preauthorization, and families may need to travel to another clinic for psychology. Unlike integrated care, psychologists and SM medical providers’ work remains relatively siloed. Thus, SM patients may feel stigmatized when they see a psychologist on a referral basis at the discretion of the medical provider compared to psychology visits being standard for all clinic patients.

Co-located care case examples

Blake is a 15-year-old volleyball player with surgically treated shoulder instability referred to a co-located psychology clinic due to avoidance of challenging PT exercises and subsequent lack of progress on return to sport metrics. At the initiation of treatment, the psychologist explained confidentiality, its limits, and explicitly discussed how information in session would be shared with non-mental health providers in the SM clinic. Monthly medical, twice weekly PT, and weekly psychology visits were coordinated, minimizing travel time and missed school. During the initial visit, responses on psychological measures revealed significant depressive symptoms and a fear of pain, including hyperfocus on their shoulder, irritable mood, passive suicidal ideation (ie, thoughts of wishing they could go to sleep and not wake up), and avoidance of potentially painful activities (eg, PT exercises). Given the psychologist’s understanding of the centrality of one’s athletic identity, the psychologist helped Blake understand how suicidal thoughts interfered with long-term athletic goals and supported Blake in finding alternative activities where they could continue to grow key athletic skills (eg, leadership, teamwork) while not on the court, which contributed to the decrease of suicidal ideation over time. The psychologist and Blake, with the input of the physical therapist, developed graded exposures based on Blake’s specific fears about pain that would be caused by using their shoulder and then these were done in PT. After communicating with the physical therapist, the psychologist addressed barriers at the next session, and both providers’ treatments benefited from not having to rely solely on Blake’s report. With family consent, the psychologist was able to briefly join a PT session to guide Blake through diaphragmatic breathing as Blake completed weighted external rotations on their repaired shoulder, a specific fear of Blake’s. After being discharged from PT and the SM clinic, Blake continued to have periodic follow-up sessions with the psychologist to ensure maintenance of treatment gains.

Psychologists within the same hospital system as the SM clinic are also able to draw on institutional resources that may not be available to a community psychologist.

Jacob is an 18-year-old basketball player seen in SM for persistent headaches and dizziness in the context of multiple previous concussions. He was referred for psychological evaluation of mood and coping and was also referred to a multidisciplinary syncope clinic within the same institution. While engaging in problem-solving around adhering to nutrition recommendations for headache management, Jacob disclosed significant barriers to obtaining the quantity and quality of food recommended, while denying intentionally restricting his food intake or any body image concerns. He also noted that he has decreased appetite on school days due to taking his stimulant medication prescribed for Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). He reported eating one meal and a couple of snacks per day despite having classes, work, practice, and conditioning, and observed a direct correlation between his physical symptoms and insufficient dietary intake. Jacob acknowledged hesitancy to discuss these challenges in front of multiple providers due to fear of judgment, a desire to be self-sufficient, and previous negative family experiences in the health care system. With his permission, the psychologist consulted with the dietitian and reached out to the medical social worker who helped connect Jacob with additional food resources. The psychologist also supported Jacob in scheduling a follow-up with his psychiatrist to discuss options for alternative ADHD medication options that do not suppress appetite. As his nutrition and overall health improved, further visits focused on improving mood and building coping skills.

Integrated care

Embedding psychology services within SM clinics promotes a biopsychosocial approach to care that may improve recovery and return-to-sport outcomes for injured athletes by addressing physical and behavioral health care needs in tandem [9]. With fully integrated care, patients and families have access to a psychologist at the same time as their medical visit, for consecutive or even joint appointments [9]. Psychologists can offer brief assessments and interventions for patients, as well as consultation to the medical team.

Advantages

The integration of psychology into medical specialties has been shown to significantly increase access to and utilization of psychological services [14]. Additionally, integrating psychology into medical clinics results in high caregiver satisfaction ratings, as well as for physicians and other integrated care team members [6]. By demonstrating that psychology is part of the sport injury assessment and rehabilitation process from the beginning, this model of care can facilitate early detection and intervention for mental health concerns [9,15], as well as reduce stigma. Psychology presence during integrated visits allows for population-based care whereby all athletes with specific injuries are screened [9]. Screening can include brief questionnaires for mood and anxiety (eg, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] [16], Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 [GAD-7] [17]), and athlete-specific questionnaires (eg, Sport Mental Health Assessment Tool-1 [18], Athletic Identity Measurement Scale [AIMS] [19]) that can help identify athletes who are at increased risk for emotional difficulties following injuries [1,15]. SM medical and mental health providers can collaboratively determine how screeners are completed, whether sent electronically as part of the preappointment registration process, completed at a check-in kiosk or on a tablet or computer at the time of check in, or administered during the visit with their medical or behavioral health provider. Psychologists can be available to immediately review and respond to any concerns identified through these screening tools. More generally, psychologists integrated into clinics can provide live and immediate consultation to multidisciplinary team members, which can support joint case conceptualization and treatment planning, collaborative discussions around return to play, and the immediate assessment of and response to any safety concerns. In the event of active suicidality, for example, a psychologist may be able to successfully engage a patient and their family in safety planning and recommend outpatient follow-up with the psychologist outside of clinic, may refer patients to a higher level of care such as a partial or intensive outpatient program, or can assist families in presenting to the emergency department for further evaluation and stabilization of acute safety concerns. Depending on team makeup and institutional resources, evaluation and response to safety concerns may additionally involve collaboration with sports psychiatry and/or social work.

Disadvantages

While research suggests benefits increase as the level of integration increases, there are challenges with the feasibility of integrated care [9,20]. Given the need for significant resources (eg, private space for psychological sessions, staff to schedule patients, staff to obtain preauthorization at some institutions) and stakeholder involvement, there are financial and logistical considerations [20,21]. This model also may not be well suited for more significant psychiatric concerns that require a higher level of psychological care. However, levels of integration can vary and barriers may be reduced by using a blended model of integrated care (ie, psychologist blending integrated and co-located) allowing for more frequent psychological follow-up for patients that need it.

Integrated care case examples

An integrated psychologist may conduct initial evaluations with all athletes scheduled to undergo specific surgeries, such as anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction, based on research demonstrating psychological factors significantly influence recovery and return to sport [22].

Maria, a 13-year-old flag football player scheduled for ACL reconstruction, completed self-report measures on her mood, anxiety, and athletic identity (ie, PHQ-9 [16], GAD-7 [17], and AIMS [19]) during her preoperative assessment, which included an appointment with the surgeon, physical therapist, and psychologist. Her scores revealed mild concerns for mood and high athletic identity. During the interview, Maria endorsed academic (ie, fear of falling behind due to surgery and recovery), logistical (ie, difficulties utilizing public transit for school and appointments while on crutches), and mild mood concerns (ie, sadness and frustration related to activity restrictions) but did not meet criteria for a mental health diagnosis. Given that concerns were mild at initial consultation, the family may have been unlikely to schedule a psychological appointment at a different time or travel to a separate clinic. However, left unaddressed, such challenges could lead to increased adjustment difficulty and functional impairment (eg, decreased school and appointment attendance) over time. With the family’s consent, the psychologist shared Maria’s concerns about activity restrictions with the team and PT noted an ongoing goal of helping Maria brainstorm ways to engage with social support despite her physical limitations. The psychologist worked with the surgeon to compose a letter advocating for the incorporation of temporary accommodations (ie, transportation, support with makeup work) to promote Maria’s return to school and collaborated with the department’s social worker for further support navigating financial and logistical barriers. By assessing and problem-solving Maria’s fears about commuting to school and appointments postsurgery, Maria attended all appointments, increasing her adherence, and returned to school promptly. Lastly, the psychologist taught Maria progressive muscle relaxation to help her cope with frustration and any worry that may arise as she entered surgery. While no psychology follow-up was planned initially, she was seen for follow-up after endorsing significant fear of reinjury on the ACL–Return to Sport after Injury (ACL-RSI) [23] during 9-month return-to-sport testing. The psychologist and Maria were able to immediately address this concern based on their prior rapport, and, given all providers were housed in the same clinic, there were no delays in scheduling or authorization.

Psychology’s involvement in care can promote monitoring and enhance treatment engagement by identifying and addressing barriers to recovery.In an integrated female athlete clinic, the care team (physician, dietician, psychologist, and PT) met with a 15-year-old long-distance runner, Amara. After evaluation, Amara was diagnosed with Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) given her history of multiple stress fractures, low bone mineral density on a dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)scan, and irregular menses. Psychology evaluated possible psychosocial factors inhibiting recovery. Amara shared about disordered eating behaviors and concerns for body image including a desire for a lean appearance impacting nutritional intake, hyperfocus on “clean eating,” anxiety related to letting down her coaches, and subscribing to the sport culture norm of training through pain, which inhibited rest and recovery and likely contributed to overtraining and recurrent injury. While some athletes can simply receive and integrate corrective education on fueling their bodies, many athletes like Amara struggle to implement changes due to sport, team, and societal norms about ideal body types and ineffective beliefs on how to achieve those standards. The psychologist was able to briefly explore these norms with Amara and find language Amara could use to challenge toxic messaging about her appearance and eating with teammates and coaches. The psychologist was able to leverage what was learned in the session to support the SM physician’s follow-up appointments with Amara. Initially, those had focused on the immediate consequences of untreated REDs in an effort to motivate behavior change; however, through assessment, the psychologist identified that Amara was more motivated by the potential longer-term effects on cardiovascular health as she had lost a family member to heart disease. Given the psychologist’s identification of the strong team culture inhibiting Amara’s adoption of the dietician’s recommendations, the dietician began with changing only Amara’s meals eaten without her teammates. Given the significant mental health concerns related to both eating and anxiety, the psychologist referred Amara to a psychologist primarily focusing on eating disorders, who could see Amara weekly, and also referred Amara to a sports psychiatrist for a psychiatric medication evaluation. The SM psychologist coordinated care with the outside eating disorders specialist and continued to provide a consultative role within the SM team.

Additional roles for psychologists

Integration of psychology within SM clinics can also facilitate program development, research, quality improvement, teaching, and outreach. The training background of pediatric psychologists make them well suited to build, evaluate, and iteratively develop multidisciplinary SM programs that can holistically improve patient outcomes. Psychologists can use their skills in research methods, statistics, and academic writing to support SM medical providers in implementing translational research and quality improvement projects that contribute to program growth, patient satisfaction, and institutional reputation. They can also assist in developing, implementing, and evaluating the use of psychometrically valid measures that assess psychological factors that may interfere with return to sport and rehabilitation adherence. For example, pediatric psychologists in SM clinics have supported and published studies on psychology referrals in pediatric SM clinics [24], differences in rates of suicidality among youth athletes [25], and self-reported quality of life following concussion [26]. Many psychologists also engage in outreach such as providing presentations to athletes, caregivers, coaches, and athletic trainers on athlete mental health and consult with local sports teams. Additionally, they provide SM-specific behavioral health and research training for medical students, residents, and fellows and engage in advocacy for mental health policies that support athletes, all of which further the reach and reputation of the SM clinic.

Moving toward implementation

Fig. 3 can support teams in determining which level of care may be most feasible for their needs. SM clinics incorporating pediatric psychology services into their program can begin by reviewing and leveraging the existing resources and services at their institution. Ideally, identifying a psychologist who can partner with SM clinic leadership can support the evaluation of the clinic’s unique needs and develop an initial plan for services whether coordinated, colocated, or integrated. Teams will need to determine the psychologist’s scope of practice, which may include all or some of the referral needs outlined in Fig. 2. If social work and psychiatry are also available, these disciplines can collaboratively determine which patient needs trigger a referral to specific services and establish a coordinated approach for identifying and responding to safety concerns. Teams will also need to determine methods for identifying patients who would benefit from a referral to psychology. Depending on the availability of psychology and the anticipated volume of patients, pediatric psychologists may prioritize short-term services directly supporting injury recovery or return to sport (ie, adherence concerns, fear of reinjury, goal setting) and refer to community mental health providers for longer-term support of more general mental health needs. If psychology will be available within the same institution, teams need to determine what space the psychologist can use for patient care and the administrative support for financial clearance, prior authorizations, and scheduling. Summary

While literature from other medical specialties emphasizes the benefit of an integrated approach for both financial and health outcomes [7,27,28], SAMSHA-HRSA [9] emphasizes the importance of selecting a level of care that logistically fits within the financial, administrative, political, and personnel restraints of the institution. Fig. 3 can help determine which level of care is most feasible for institutions. The preceding examples highlight the variety of and nuances in clinical presentations that may benefit from psychological support in SM and the overlapping biological, psychological, and multilevel social factors that can influence athletes’ recovery. Including pediatric psychologists as part of the SM team, whether through coordinated, co-located, or integrated care, has the potential to improve athlete recovery and contribute to program growth.
