Linear programmed dietary plan education intervention dataset on improving caregivers knowledge, attitude and energy intake among children aged 2–5 years living with HIV in Simiyu region, Tanzania
Authors: Heriet Carin, Kihulya Mageda, Leonard Katalambula, Mariam Munyogwa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV-infected children are more likely to be malnourished compared to normal children due to inadequate dietary intake, nutrition losses, metabolic change, and increased requirements for nutrients [1]. Poor nutrition, particularly low energy intake, has been shown to impair immune responses and accelerate the progression of HIV to AIDS [2,3]. This study is unique in its focus on the effectiveness of a linear programmed dietary plan educational intervention. The aim was to improve caregivers' knowledge, attitude, and energy intake of children living with HIV aged 2–5 years in the Simiyu region. Using this innovative dietary plan could potentially lead to significant improvements in the health and well-being of these children.The raw and analyzed data included 200 asymptomatic children living with HIV aged 2 – 5 years, with 100 assigned to the intervention group and the other 100 assigned to the control group, with the following information: sociodemographic characteristics (child and caregiver's age, sex of child and caregiver, and level of education of caregiver, household size, relations, and caregiver's occupation and marital status). The other data included in this dataset were the baseline and end line of knowledge, attitude, and energy intake. Repeated measures of ANOVA were used to analyze the data. The outcome was energy intake at the end of the intervention. Additionally, Cohen's d was used to calculate the effect size as an estimation of the strength of the intervention. This dataset of HIV programs from similar situations can be utilized to improve energy intake among children living with HIV.Keywords: Dietary plan, Education, Energy intake, HIV-infected children

Background

Children infected with HIV are at a higher risk of experiencing insufficient energy intake as a result of inadequate food consumption, increased nutritional loss, and increased nutritional demands [[1], [6]]. Poor nutrition and deficient energy intake have been shown to impair immune responses and accelerate the progression of HIV to AIDS [[2], [3], [4], [5], [7]].

Locally available food can provide adequate nutrients when the child consumes the required amount [8]. However, no single food can supply the necessary and sufficient nutrients to an individual [9]. Linear programming can be used to interpret nutritional requirements in food to achieve a healthy and well-balanced menu [10].

Despite local food availability in the community, households need more dietary quality and quantity, resulting in inadequate energy intake, which is essential for a robust immune system [11].

Therefore, we formulated a dietary plan according to the available food, and the education was delivered by a community-based organization (Konga) in a randomized clinical trial. Therefore, the dataset was collected to test the effectiveness of the intervention to promote the utilization of Konga in delivering services to children living with HIV.

Data Description

The intervention trial consists of designed questionnaires for data collection that are available as supplementary files in the repository [12]. The linear programed dietary plan training package for training the Konga was attached as a supplementary file.

The first part of the questionnaire contains information on the caregivers' and their children's sociodemographic characteristics. The second part of the questionnaire includes questions that cumulatively tested the caregiver's knowledge regarding nutrition, and it was categorized into three responses. The third part of the questionnaire contained questions that tested the nutritional attitude of the caregiver—the last part of the questionnaire collected responses regarding dietary practices among children living with immunodeficiency virus.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The National Council of People Living with HIV (NACOPHA) is a nonprofit, nongovernmental, national grassroots-based organization of people living with HIV (PLHIV) in mainland Tanzania. Since its establishment, NACOPHA has embarked on coordinating the efforts of PLHIV through their district clusters known as Konga to address the needs of PLHIV. Hence, in this study, we used a community-based organization called the Konga to provide nutrition education to caregivers of children living with HIV.

The study dataset was collected in the Simiyu region from a cluster-randomized clinical trial with concurrent intervention and control groups. The region demonstrates an alarming prevalence of malnutrition among children below the age of five, exceeding the World Health Organization's threshold of 30 % 5 Based on the survey done by the Tanzania National Nutrition Survey, the prevalence of stunting was found to be 31.8 %, wasting was seen at 4 %, and underweight was recorded at 14.6 % [13].

This study dataset was collected from selected asymptomatic children living with HIV aged 2–5 years registered and attending the selected care and treatment center (CTC) and their caregivers. The study utilized care and treatment as the unit of randomization. Twenty care and treatment centers (CTCs) were included in the analysis.

The intervention package consisted of a linear programmed dietary plan to be demonstrated and basic nutrition education. Four distinct stages were involved. (i) Developing a linear programmed dietary plan (ii) Preparation of nutrition education teaching material (iii) training Konga on a linear programmed dietary plan and general nutrition education (iv) Konga to train caregivers on a linear programmed dietary plan (LPDP) general nutrition education.

Nutrition education material for basic nutrition education was adapted from the National Guidelines for Nutrition Care and Support of People with HIV [14]. The nutrition education contents included the link between HIV and nutrition, healthy eating for children living with HIV, and food safety and hygiene.

The intervention implementation involved two stages: the first was to train the community trainers (Konga), and the second was to train the targeted caregivers.

After training, Konga was provided with copies of training package materials for their reference and to be used during the training of caregivers of children living with HIV.

The second stage involved training of the caregivers where Konga conducted the training to the targeted caregivers of children living with HIV within their community. Each group had ten caregivers of children living with HIV, and 2 Konga were conducting training for them. Confidentiality was observed during the meeting at an agreed venue/place. The education was based on a dietary plan made from locally available food, the link between HIV and nutrition, healthy eating, and safety and hygiene. Standard operating procedures were developed and used to provide the Konga intervention. (The standard operating procedure for the Konga intervention is provided as a supplementary file.)

The participants from the control group did not receive the intervention but continued with the routine nutrition education provided by health workers when attending care and treatment centers. The control group was provided with a linear programmed dietary plan as a reference at the end of the study.

After three months of follow-up, end-line information on knowledge, attitude, and energy intake was collected. The information collected after the intervention was compared with the baseline to obtain the effectiveness of the intervention.

There were three dependent variables: knowledge, attitude, and energy intake.

In this study, variables were collected and measured as follows;

(i)Caregivers' nutrition knowledge data was collected using a structured questionnaire comprising 15 questions. Each question had only one correct answer, and a score of one was given to every correct answer and zero to every wrong answer. A total score for each participant was calculated by adding the total number of questions. The minimum knowledge score was 0, and the maximum was 15—Cronbach's alpha coefficient for knowledge=0.81 [13].

A numerical value was assigned to each choice the caregivers gave, which was 0 for strongly disagree, 1= disagree, 2= agree, and 3=strongly agree. The average score was calculated, and the total mean score was calculated. The attitude score was derived, providing a complete assessment of the overall attitude—Cronbach's alpha coefficient for attitude=0.75 [15].

Anthropometric measurements

(a) Weight

Weight was measured by using SECA weighing scale (0–150 kg) using CDC (2012) procedures. The scale was placed on a flat surface and adjusted to zero before taking measurements. Children was weighed bare feet with only light clothes. The child was stood in upright position at the center of the balance with the feet in a V- shape. The weight were recorded.

(b) Height

The height of a child was measured by using a stadiometer with precision of 0.1 cm using the CDC (2012) procedures. The barefooted child was assisted to stand straight on the stadiometer which was placed on a flat surface and to look straight ahead. With the help of caregiver, the heels were maintained together and the body was placed so that the shoulder blades, buttocks and the heels was touching the vertical surface of the stadiometer. The feet were maintained flat on the floor slightly apart, with the legs and backs at the sides. The shoulders were relaxed and in contact with the stadiometer. As the child stood still, the researcher assistant took the measurement.

(c) MUAC

Mid Upper Arm Circumference was measured on the bare skin nearest to 0.1 cm using inextensible measuring tape after marking the mid-point of the left upper arm between the olecranon and acromion process. The measurement was taken in a relaxed position of the arm by trained personnel to maintain technical errors of measurement within standard limit (Onis et al., 2004).

The anthropometric measurements were converted into nutrition indices using 2006 WHO growth standards and STATA igrowup package was convert measurement into weight- for –height score (WHZ –score) for 24 months up to 59 months.

To provide a statistical power of 80 % and a significance level of 95 %, we establish the framework for our inquiry. The Zα number, which represents the critical value for a 95 % confidence level.

Based on our calculations, we have an 80 % chance of detecting the impact of interest (Z = 0.84), which is statistically significant. The intra-cluster correlation was set at 0.01. The design effect (DE), equal to 1.09(1 + 0.01*(10–1)), was introduced to account for this clustering effect. K was determined by dividing the overall sample size (N) by the cluster size (M), which was assumed to be 10. P1, representing the proportional outcome in the control group, was evaluated at 0.6, while P2, representing the proportional outcome in the intervention group, was estimated at 0.38.

The number of clusters was calculated by K = N/M = 19, and when divided by 2, the number of clusters was 9.5; since there is no half cluster, it was estimated to be 10 per arm, i.e., intervention and control. Therefore, 10 Children living with HIV were included from each cluster, where the total sample size was 200 Children living with HIV (100 from each arm) [14].

Data were analyzed by using SPSS version 26. The descriptive analysis was done to determine the distribution characteristics of participants in both the intervention and control groups. The Chi-square test was performed to identify the variables associated with energy intake, knowledge, and attitude. Repeated measures of ANOVA were used to assess if there were differences between the intervention and control groups after the intervention. F-tests and their corresponding p-values were used as measures of improvement.

Limitations

This dataset has some limitations as the data depend on self-reporting and recall of caregivers in assessing the feeding practices, so it was challenging to control bias as the caregivers may give information that they think is usually acceptable. Also, the information from the study cannot be generalizable to the areas with different backgrounds; therefore, further studies in other places with various settings are essential. There was a slight positive change in the knowledge and attitude of caregivers as well as the energy intake of children living with HIV after three months of follow-up in the control group. The change was likely due to the interchange of information among caregivers within three months after the intervention. Also, the intervention duration needed to allow us to evaluate long-term efficacy.
