Determinants of critical-time hand washing practices among mothers of under-five children in West Guji Zone, Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Lechisa Asefa, Tibeso Gemechu, Hailu Lemma, Gedeno Karbana, Habtamu Roba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand washing at critical times among mothers of children under five is essential for reducing infectious diseases. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends five key moments for proper hand hygiene. Despite the proven effectiveness of hand washing, the burden of diarrheal disease remains high in the study area. Therefore, this study aimed to assess hand-washing practices at critical times among mothers of young children and to identify the factors influencing these practices.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April to May 2025, involving 401 mothers. Data were collected through interviews using a pretested questionnaire. The data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with hand hygiene practices at critical times. All statistical tests were conducted at a 5% level of significance. Variables with p-values less than 0.05 were considered to have a significant association.ResultsThe study reveals that 131 (32.6%) of mothers demonstrate good hand hygiene practices at critical times. Factors significantly associated with good hand practices include the ability to read and write (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.32–8.67), availability of hand washing facilities in the home (AOR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.05–6.88), being government employees (AOR = 4.1; 95% CI: 2.43–11.67), having water sources nearby (AOR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.87–9.57), and mothers possessing good knowledge about hand hygiene (AOR = 2.2; 95% CI: 1.21–7.9).ConclusionThe majority of mothers with children under five years old exhibit poor hand hygiene practices at critical times. To improve these practices, it is recommended to enhance maternal literacy, ensure access to water, and encourage households to install hand washing facilities in their backyards.Keywords: Hand washing, Mothers, Under-five children and infection prevention and control

Background

Infectious diseases remain the leading causes of childhood mortality and morbidity, accounting for 65% of all deaths in children under five years of age [1]. According to global GBD estimates, approximately 500,000 children under five die each year from diarrhea-related diseases, which are linked to inadequate and poor water, sanitation, and hygiene [2]. Specifically, due to poor hand-washing practices, about 400 million children are infected with worms [2]. Hand washing with soap and water thoroughly is essential to prevent infectious disease [3]. Therefore practicing hand washing is essential in different setting including health care facility and community. The World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the concept of the “Five Moments for Hand Hygiene” to reduce healthcare-associated infections and improve hand hygiene practices in healthcare settings. These moments include: before touching a patient, before performing aseptic or clean procedures, after exposure or risk of exposure to body fluids, after touching a patient, and after touching the patient’s surroundings [4]. In addition, the WHO has identified five critical hand-washing moments for mothers and caregivers in community and household settings. These include after using the toilet, after cleaning a child’s defecated buttocks, before cooking, before eating, and before feeding the child [5]. Proper hand-washing with soap and water at each of these critical times is one of the most effective means of preventing and controlling infections transmitted via feces, body fluids, and inanimate objects [5].

Proper hand-washing at critical times significantly contributes to the reduction of infectious diseases; however, poor hand-washing practices persist in low-income countries, including Ethiopia [6]. This is supported by previous research, which reveals that proper hand-washing at critical times is not commonly practiced [6, 7]. Mothers play a crucial role in child health; therefore, their knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding hand-washing are very important. According to the current study, maternal hygiene interventions significantly reduce the risk of diarrheal disease in children under five years of age [8, 9]. Additionally, hand-washing with soap and water at critical times can reduce the incidence of cholera and dysentery by 48% to 59% [10, 11].

The practice of hand washing at critical times among mothers of children under five years old varies across countries worldwide. It was reported to be 44.6% in Gedeo, Ethiopia [8], 88.9% in Ekiti State, Nigeria [12], and 76% in Assam, India [13]. Evidence indicates that various factors influence the practice of proper hand washing at critical times among mothers of children under five years of age [8, 14].

In the study area, a high prevalence of diarrhea (36.6%) was reported [15], and hand-washing is essential for reducing the burden of diarrheal diseases. In addition, the study area is a hard-to-reach border region of Ethiopia with predominantly pastoral communities, who are more vulnerable to communicable diseases. However, to the best of our knowledge, no published studies have examined hand-washing practices at critical times and the associated factors among mothers of children under five years old. Therefore, this study aimed to assess hand-washing practices at critical times and their associated factors among mothers of children under five years old, to inform targeted interventions in the study area.

Method and materials

Study setting and period

The study took place in the West Guji Zone from April to May 2025. West Guji, located in the southern part of Ethiopia, is one of the 21 zones in the Oromia Regional State. Its administrative center is Bule Hora, approximately 467 km from Addis Ababa. According to the 2007 Census by the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia (CSA), the total population of the zone was about 1.3 million, comprising 638,648 males and 662,060 females. The zone consists of two town administrations and nine rural woredas, including seven agro-pastoralist and three pastoralist areas. The primary rainy season lasts from April to October, with the heaviest rainfall occurring in May and August. Public health infrastructure in West Guji includes three hospitals, 42 health centers, and 196 health posts.

Study period and study design

A Community-based cross-sectional study was conducted to assess handwashing practices at critical times among mothers with children under five years old in West Guji Zone.

Population

The study population consisted of randomly selected households and mothers with children under five years of age.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Households with mothers who have children under five years old were included in the study. Mothers with hearing impairments or speech disabilities, those who are seriously ill, and mothers who have lived in the study area for less than six months were excluded.

Sampling procedures, techniques, and data collection tools

Three districts were selected from the nine districts in the West Guji Zone using a simple random sampling method, also known as the lottery method. The sample size was then proportionally allocated to each selected district. Finally, study participants were chosen using systematic random sampling techniques (see Fig. 1). Data were collected through face-to-face interviews and an observational checklist using well-designed questionnaires from different literature [16–18].

Study variables

Dependent variable

Hand-washing practice of mothers at critical times.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, educational status, marital status, income, family size, and mother’s occupation), health information, attitude, and knowledge.

Data analysis

The collected data were checked, coded, and entered into Epidata version 3.1, then exported to SPSS version 26.0 for data cleaning and analysis. Bivariate logistic regression was used to analyze the data, and variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 were selected for multivariable logistic regression analysis. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with hand-washing at critical times among women. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, training was provided to data collectors before the commencement of data collection. Daily supervision was conducted by a supervisor throughout the data collection period. Prior to the actual data collection, the questionnaire was pretested through a pilot survey in Yabello town, which shares similar characteristics with the study population. The questionnaire was translated into the local language, Afan Oromo. Data consistency was maintained throughout the data collection, entry, and analysis processes. Validity of questioner was checked (with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81).

Operational definition

Hand-washing practices at critical times were measured using five items: after defecation, after cleansing the child’s defecated buttocks, before cooking, before eating, and before feeding the child. Responses were recorded on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = always, 2 = sometimes, and 3 = never). For analysis, responses were coded as 1 for always and 0 for sometimes and never. Then the mean score was calculated and Practice, knowledge and attitude scores above or equal to the mean score were assigned for good practice. Practice, knowledge and attitude scores below the mean score were assigned for poor practice [8].

Factors associated with hand-washing at critical times

To identify candidate variables for multivariate logistic regression, the association between each independent variable and hand-washing practice at critical times was assessed using binary logistic regression. Accordingly, age, mothers’ occupation, mothers’ educational status, family income, family size, knowledge, attitude, availability of hand-washing facilities, and availability of a water source near the households showed a significant association with hand-washing practice at critical times, with a p-value less than 0.25.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, five variables were identified as significant predictors of good hand-washing practices among mothers with children under five years, after controlling for potential confounders. Specifically, mothers who were able to read and write were 2.1 times more likely to practice good hand-washing at critical times compared to those who were illiterate (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.32–8.67). Mothers in households with available hand-washing facilities were 1.8 times more likely to wash their hands at critical times compared to those without such facilities (AOR = 1.8; 95% CI: 1.05–6.88). Mothers employed as government workers were 4.1 times more likely to have good hand-washing practices than merchants (AOR = 4.1; 95% CI: 2.43–11.67). Mothers with nearby water sources were 3.1 times more likely to practice good hand-washing (AOR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.87–9.57). Finally, mothers with good knowledge about hand-washing at critical times were 2.2 times more likely to engage in good hand-washing practices (AOR = 2.2; 95% CI: 1.21–7.9) (Table 6).

Discussion

Hand washing at critical times is an essential public health practice that significantly reduces the burden of infectious diseases [14]. This behavior is especially important for mothers with children under five years old, as they often have close contact with their young children and are therefore key to preventing the transmission of pathogens within the household. Proper hand hygiene among mothers not only protects their own health but also plays a vital role in reducing childhood morbidity and mortality associated with preventable diseases.

According to recent studies, 32.6% of participants demonstrated good hand-washing practices at critical times. This finding is consistent with data from Gondar, northwest Ethiopia [19]. However, it is lower than the rates reported in studies conducted in Addis Ababa (74.4%) [9], Gedeo (44.9%), Kolladiba town, Northwest Ethiopia (51.2%) [20]. Conversely, it is higher than the rate found in a study conducted in Indonesia (21%) [21]. The discrepancies in hand-washing practices among mothers may be attributed to differences in socioeconomic status, the availability and quality of health services, living standards across study settings, as well as variations in the tools and techniques used to assess hand-washing practices. Besides, when comparing the current community-level study with studies conducted in healthcare settings, a higher prevalence of good hand-washing practices was observed in healthcare settings [19, 20].

In the recent study, occupation, educational status, availability of hand-washing facilities and water near the household, and the mother’s knowledge were shown to be significantly associated with good hand-washing practices at critical times. Mothers who were able to read and write were 2.1 times more likely to practice good hand-washing at critical times than those who were unable to read and write. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Nefas Silk Lafto Sub-City and Kirkos, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [9], Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Gondar town, North West Ethiopia [19], Wondogenet Woreda and Kamrup District, Assam [13]. A possible explanation is that literate mothers are more likely to have better awareness and understanding of hygiene and disease prevention practices, including hand-washing at critical times (such as after using the toilet, before feeding a child, and before food preparation). Literacy enhances their ability to access, comprehend, and apply health information from various sources such as posters, leaflets, media campaigns, and health extension workers. Moreover, literate mothers are more likely to recognize the link between hygiene and child health outcomes, which motivates them to adopt and maintain proper hand-washing behavior. They can also read instructions on hygiene materials (e.g., soap packages, public health messages) and model healthy behaviors for their children [22].

The availability of a hand-washing facility near the household has been significantly associated with mothers’ hand-washing at critical times. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Afghanistan [19], Nifas Silk, Addis Ababa [23], Nifas silk, Addis Ababa [9], and Gedeo Zone, Southern Ethiopia [8]. Having a hand-washing facility nearby greatly facilitates good hand-washing practices among mothers. When hand-washing stations equipped with soap and water are easily accessible and conveniently located, they reduce physical and time barriers to practicing hand hygiene, especially during critical moments such as after defecation, before food preparation, and before feeding children. A nearby facility serves as a constant visual reminder to wash hands and encourages the habitual adoption of proper hygiene behaviors. Furthermore, proximity to a hand-washing facility makes the behavior more feasible and consistent, particularly for busy mothers managing multiple household and childcare responsibilities. In contrast, the absence of such a facility often discourages frequent hand-washing due to inconvenience or lack of immediate access to water and soap.

Furthermore, households with an available water source near the house are 3.1 times more likely to practice good hand-washing at critical times compared to others. This finding is consistent with previous studies [16, 20]. The availability of a nearby water source plays a crucial role in promoting good hand-washing practices among mothers. When water is readily accessible within a short distance, it reduces the burden of fetching water and ensures that mothers have sufficient water for frequent hand-washing, especially during critical times. A nearby water source increases convenience and consistency in practicing hand hygiene, as mothers do not need to prioritize water for other household needs over hygiene. Additionally, easy access to water encourages the establishment of functional hand-washing stations and the routine use of soap and water [3, 21].

Moreover, a recent study found that mothers with good knowledge about hand washing at critical times were 2.2 times more likely to practice hand washing at these times than mothers with poor knowledge. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Kolladiba town [20], and Debark town [24]. Good knowledge about hand washing at critical times is a strong determinant of proper hand hygiene behavior. Mothers who are well-informed about when and why hand washing is important are more likely to recognize its role in preventing the transmission of infectious diseases. Knowledge increases awareness of critical moments and helps mothers understand the health consequences of neglecting these practices. This awareness enhances risk perception and motivation to adopt and maintain proper hand washing behavior. Furthermore, knowledgeable mothers are often better able to translate information into action, as they understand the correct technique and timing of hand washing. They are also more likely to teach and model this behavior to their children and other family members, reinforcing a culture of hygiene within the household.

In the recent study, mothers who were government employees were 4.1 times more likely to practice good hand washing at critical times compared to merchants. This could be due to mothers is that government employees typically have better access to health information, higher health literacy, and more structured work environments. Government institutions often provide regular training, workplace health promotion activities, and exposure to public health campaigns, which can increase awareness of proper hygiene practices. In contrast, merchant mothers often work in informal, busy, and time-constrained environments.

The major strength of this study is the use of primary data collected through a community-based design targeting the most affected population children less than five years of age. However, the study has several limitations. Recall bias may have occurred, and social desirability bias is also possible, as participants may have reported practices in a manner they perceived to be socially acceptable rather than reflecting actual behavior.

Conclusion

In the study area, hand washing at critical times was uncommon, which children particularly those under five years of age are more vulnerable to preventable infectious diseases such as diarrhea, intestinal parasites infections, and other communicable illnesses. This poor hygiene practice may contribute to the high burden of childhood morbidity and undermines efforts to improve child health outcomes. A mother’s occupation, level of education, access to hand washing facilities and water availability, and knowledge were all found to be significantly associated with proper hand washing practices during these important times. This finding highlights the need for interventions to improve women’s hand washing practices by enhancing maternal literacy and improving access to hand washing facilities, thereby reducing the burden of infectious diseases among children less than five years of age.
