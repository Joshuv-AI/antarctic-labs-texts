Bleeding-related quality of life in older adults: Psychometric evaluation of the HEmorrhage Life Impact indeX (HELIX)
Authors: Anna L. Parks, Gregory J. Stoddard, Amy M. Cizik, Elissa M. Ozanne, Margaret C. Fang, Mark A. Supiano, Daniel M. Witt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Older adults treated with anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation (AF) or venous thromboembolism (VTE) face heightened bleeding risk, which can affect physical, emotional and social wellbeing and function. Existing instruments assess treatment satisfaction or generic quality of life but do not capture the specific impact of bleeding in this population. The goal of this study was to psychometrically validate HELIX (HEmorrhage Life Impact indeX), a novel patient-reported outcome (PRO) measure of bleeding-related quality of life in older adults on anticoagulation.Methods:We enrolled adults aged ≥65 years with AF or VTE on oral anticoagulants. Psychometric testing of the previously derived, 19-item HELIX PRO included exploratory factor analysis, internal consistency (Cronbach's α), floor and ceiling effects, construct validity (correlations with PROMIS short forms), test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficients), and responsiveness.Results:Ninety-five participants (mean age 75, 45% women, 87% White; 66% AF, 33% VTE) participated. Factor analysis identified two subscales: bleeding symptoms (6 items) and quality of life (13 items). Internal consistency was high overall (α = 0.91), excellent for quality of life (α = 0.95), and acceptable for bleeding symptoms (α = 0.61). HELIX scores showed no floor or ceiling effects and demonstrated expected convergent and divergent validity with PROMIS measures. Test–retest reliability was good-to-excellent for most items. Responsiveness analysis suggested HELIX was more sensitive to within-person change than PROMIS.Conclusion:HELIX demonstrated strong initial psychometric performance as a bleeding-related quality of life instrument for older adults taking anticoagulants. It complements existing measures and warrants further validation in larger and more diverse populations to evaluate clinical utility and prognostic value.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) and atrial fibrillation (AF) disproportionately affect older adults [1,2]. The mainstay of VTE and AF treatment, anticoagulation therapy, is highly effective at preventing thromboembolism, but advancing age increases risk of anticoagulant-related bleeding [3]. Anticoagulant-related hemorrhage is common and can be fatal, disabling, or lead patients to alter their lifestyle or behaviors, which can impact quality of life [4-6].

With the aging of the worldwide population, the population of older adults taking anticoagulants for VTE or AF is expected to grow [7]. Yet current anticoagulant-related bleeding instruments do not adequately measure the impact of bleeding on older adults' quality of life. Standardized bleeding outcomes in anticoagulation studies include International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) major bleeding, defined by fatal bleeding, symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ, bleeding causing a hemoglobin decrease of ≥2 g/dL, or bleeding leading to transfusion of ≥2 units; and clinically relevant non-major bleeding, defined as overt bleeding that does not meet major bleeding criteria but prompts medical attention, interruption of therapy, or other clinical action [8,9]. Older adults have unique characteristics (multimorbidity, frailty, polypharmacy, physical or cognitive impairment) that influence not only the risk of but also the experience of bleeding, which are not captured by these definitions [10]. For example, existing measures neglect small-volume bleeds (so-called “nuisance bleeding”) and do not encompass the impact of hemorrhage on outcomes important to older adults like maintaining independence and freedom from disability [5,11,12]. There are two patient-reported outcome measures (PROs) that were developed to measure treatment satisfaction with anticoagulant therapy, the Duke Anticoagulation Satisfaction Scale (DASS) and the Anti-Clot Treatment Scale (ACTS) [13,14]. However, these measures have limitations when applied in older adults. Neither was developed or validated in older adults. Furthermore, they do not comprehensively measure bleeding symptoms or their effect on quality of life and instead focus on treatment satisfaction with medications.

Despite the burgeoning public health burden and growing emphasis on incorporating PROs into research and clinical care, we lack a validated measure of bleeding-related wellbeing and function in older adults. The purpose of this study was to psychometrically evaluate a new condition-specific PRO for the effect of anticoagulant-related bleeding on older adults' quality of life, the HEmorrhage Life Impact indeX (HELIX).

Methods

Study design

We adhered to Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and COnsensusbased Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) guidelines for development and psychometric validation of a novel PRO (Fig. 1) [15-19].

We previously described development of the novel HELIX measure [17]. Briefly, development incorporated extensive patient input, including focus groups to refine a hypothesized conceptual framework drawn from literature review and expert input. We then generated items derived from the refined conceptual model to create a draft measure. Using semi-structured cognitive interviews, the draft measure was then revised for clarity, redundancy and relevance. HELIX was then reviewed by multidisciplinary experts to generate the 19-item final version for psychometric validation.

We next conducted psychometric validation, which evaluates whether a PRO instrument measures what it is intended to measure and whether it does so reliably (Fig. 1). This involves examining the instrument's structure (factor analysis); whether a large number of respondents score at extremes of the scale (floor and ceiling effects); whether items that probe similar concepts produce similar responses (internal consistency); correlation with established measures (construct validity); if repeated administrations of a measure produce similar responses (test–retest reliability); and sensitivity to change in established measures (responsiveness). Such testing ensures that the tool is both scientifically rigorous and clinically meaningful [18].

Participants and procedures

To perform psychometric validation, patients aged 65 years or older with a diagnosis of AF or VTE were eligible for study inclusion. Participants were recruited at the University of Utah between June 2023 and December 2024. Potential subjects were identified via chart review of outpatients who met the above criteria and who were prescribed an oral anticoagulant medication (warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants [DOACs]). The initial psychometric validation of HELIX focused on oral anticoagulants because these represent the predominant long-term outpatient anticoagulation strategy for AF and VTE in older adults. Diagnosis of AF or VTE and anticoagulant prescriptions were confirmed via chart review. We excluded subjects who were taking oral anticoagulants for any reason other than AF or VTE. Because most individuals receiving anticoagulation do not have a prior history of major bleeding, and because HELIX was designed to capture bleeding-related impact in a representative anticoagulated population—including nuisance bleeding, anticipatory concern, and behavioral adaptation—prior bleeding events were not required for inclusion. Inclusion of multiple anticoagulation indications and the absence of a bleeding history requirement were intentional design choices to evaluate HELIX across the range of clinical contexts in which bleeding-related burden arises among chronically anticoagulated older adults.

Patients were contacted via email at the time of enrollment with a link to complete the questionnaire online. The questionnaire included the novel HELIX measure and five four-item PROMIS short forms that correspond to HELIX domains (v2.0 Physical Function 4a, v1.0 Fatigue 4a, v2.0 Satisfaction with Participation in Social Roles 4a, v1.0 Depression 4a, and v1.0 Anxiety 4a) to examine construct validity and responsiveness [20]. We sent a second recruitment email seven days later, and we excluded participants who did not respond to two attempts at contact. We assessed test-retest reliability by emailing participants two-to-four weeks after the initial questionnaire to retake the questionnaire, and we assessed responsiveness by emailing participants again 3 months later to retake the questionnaire. A second recruitment email was sent seven days later, and if an individual did not respond after two attempts at contact, they were considered lost to follow-up.

We estimated a sample size of 95 participants (5 participants per item) to generate stable estimates of factor loadings, reliability and validity [21]. This target sample size (approximately 5 participants per item) is consistent with established guidance for exploratory psychometric validation and COSMIN recommendations for initial evaluation of factor structure, reliability, and construct validity [19]. Only completed surveys were included in the final analysis. Data were collected and stored in REDCap, an electronic web-based data capture system, hosted by the University of Utah Clinical and Translational Science Institute. Consent was obtained from all participants before study enrollment, and participants received compensation for completing the study. Approval was granted by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board (IRB_00146636).

Statistical analyses

We present descriptive statistics, including proportions and means with standard deviation (SD). We used multivariate statistical modeling for exploratory factor analysis to determine the final HELIX structure and scoring. The final factor structure was selected based on scree plots and parallel analysis, factor loadings > 0.3, few cross-loadings of items, no factors comprising fewer than 3 items, and reasonable factors based on expert literature review and qualitative data [22]. Internal consistency for the instrument overall and by subscale was assessed using a standardized Cronbach's α [23,24]. We evaluated for floor and ceiling effects by examining the proportion of respondents scoring at scale extremes. Construct validity was determined by examining correlations between HELIX and PROMIS short forms. Reliability was gauged by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). We examined responsiveness using correlation coefficients (r) between changes in HELIX scores and PROMIS measures, with values interpreted as weak (r < 3.0), moderate (0.3 ≤ r < 0.5) or strong (r ≥ 0.5). All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 19.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Among a total of 95 subjects, demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Mean age was 75 (SD 7) years, 45% were female, 66% and 33% had AF and VTE, respectively. Warfarin was used by 11%, while 71% used apixaban, 17% rivaroxaban, 1% dabigatran.

Factor analysis

In exploratory factor analysis, the scree plot indicated two subscales should be retained. Rotated factor loading results are presented in Table 2. Based on the analysis, the first subscale of HELIX is bleeding symptoms, comprised of six specific manifestations of bleeding. The second subscale is quality of life, comprised of 13 aspects of physical, emotional, and social function and healthcare interactions.

Internal consistency

Cronbach's α for HELIX overall and the two subscales are presented in Table 3. Cronbach's α did not substantially increase or decrease after removing any item from the final 19-item version of HELIX, confirming that all contributed towards reliability and should be retained.

Scoring and floor and ceiling effects

The HELIX score was then defined as the unweighted average of scores on items 1 to 19, with scores ranging from 1 to 5, and higher scores indicating greater impairment. The mean score on HELIX was 1.7 (SD 0.82) overall, mean 1.8 (SD 0.60) on the bleeding symptoms subscale, and mean 1.63 (SD 0.93) on the quality of life subscale (Fig. 2). No extreme ceiling and floor effects were observed.

Construct validity

For construct validity, correlation coefficients between HELIX items and validated PROMIS measures are summarized in Table 4. All results were statistically significant (p < 0.05) and indicated weak to moderate correlation. Results revealed higher correlation between related concepts (convergent validity) and lower correlation between unrelated concepts (divergent validity). Of note, lower scores on the PROMIS Physical Function and Satisfaction with Participation with Social Roles measures indicate worse outcomes, which therefore are inversely correlated with HELIX scores as expected.

Test-retest reliability

For test-retest reliability, 51 (54%) of subjects were included in the analysis. Results showed excellent reliability for 11 questions, good for 4 questions, moderate for 2 questions, and poor for 1 question (Table 5).

Responsiveness

Responsiveness was also assessed in 51 (54%) of participants. We found weak to moderate correlation between changes in HELIX scores and changes in PROMIS measures from baseline to 3 months (Table 6). To further evaluate this finding, we examined scatter plots and found that PROMIS scores most often showed no change whereas HELIX scores changed substantially. We also examined scores on individual HELIX items and found substantial change over time in responses, whereas PROMIS item responses remained unchanged. Reviewing the instances in which changes in HELIX did correlate significantly with changes in PROMIS measures, we found that the two measures probed similar concepts. Overall, this suggests that HELIX could very well be more sensitive and responsive in this population, but PROMIS is not the appropriate anchor scale to establish responsiveness.

Discussion

This validation study demonstrates that HELIX—a newly-developed, condition-specific PRO—is a valid and reliable instrument for bleeding-related quality of life in older adults on anticoagulant therapy for AF or VTE.

Exploratory factor analysis of the 19-item HELIX instrument identified two distinct subscales: bleeding symptoms (6 items) and quality of life (13 items) with conceptual groupings across physical, emotional, and social domains. Internal consistency was high overall (Cronbach's α = 0.91) and excellent for the quality of life subscale (α = 0.95), while the bleeding symptoms subscale showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.61). HELIX scores, calculated as the unweighted average of item responses demonstrated no significant floor or ceiling effects. Construct validity was supported by significant correlations with related PROMIS measures, consistent with both convergent and divergent validity expectations. Notably, we found modest correlations between HELIX mobility and exercise items and PROMIS Physical Function. This likely reflects that HELIX captures bleeding-related limitations and behavioral restriction, whereas PROMIS assesses overall physical capability regardless of cause; we interpret the modest correlations as evidence that bleeding-related behavioral restriction represents a construct distinct from global physical function. Test-retest reliability was strong, with 15 of 19 items demonstrating good to excellent stability.

Although changes in HELIX scores over three months did not correlate strongly with changes in PROMIS measures, visual and item-level analyses suggested greater sensitivity of HELIX to within-person change. These responsiveness findings should be interpreted cautiously and are best viewed as hypothesis-generating. Nonetheless, HELIX appeared to detect changes not captured by generic PROMIS measures, consistent with its bleeding-specific focus. Future studies using alternative anchors will be needed to further establish responsiveness. Collectively, these findings support HELIX as a reliable and valid instrument for assessing bleeding symptoms and quality of life in affected populations, while highlighting the need for larger, independent samples to support confirmatory factor analysis, subgroup analyses, and broader validation.

The HELIX condition-specific PRO was developed to be incorporated into clinical care and research to complement existing outcomes and align treatment with older adults' priorities. Many past studies have incorporated generic PROs like the SF-36, which have strengths like enabling comparison of quality of life across different diseases [6]. However, generic measures are less sensitive to subtle but clinically meaningful changes that are caused by a specific condition or its treatment, and guidelines advise using condition-specific PROs developed for particular populations [25]. During development of HELIX, we identified that bleeding symptoms and specific subdomains within physical, emotional and social functioning matter more than others, which may not be captured by generic PROs [17]. In addition, HELIX is distinct from anticoagulant treatment satisfaction measures (DASS and ACTS) [13,14]. Treatment satisfaction is related to quality of life but is a distinct outcome that includes the limitations, hassles, burdens, confidence, reassurance, and satisfaction with anticoagulant medications. Furthermore, DASS and ACTS were not developed in older adults and do not comprehensively measure bleeding's effects on emotional and social wellbeing, a prominent finding observed during the development phase of HELIX [17]. The condition-specific HELIX instrument fills this gap by directly assessing bleeding symptoms and their impact on quality of life, including physical, emotional, and social domains, and has demonstrated strong psychometric performance in the growing population of anticoagulated older adults.

Results of our study should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. Our sample was derived from a single center composed of predominantly White and English-speaking patients, reflecting the demographics of the source population. Limited racial and ethnic diversity may affect generalizability, particularly given known differences in bleeding risk, access to care, and lived experience across populations. At the same time, the cohort reflects contemporary epidemiologic data on indication for anticoagulation and agent selection [26]. Most participants received apixaban, which may limit generalizability to patients treated with warfarin, dabigatran, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and other parenteral anticoagulants. Similarly, although our study design reflects real-world anticoagulation practice, it was not powered to assess differential psychometric performance separately within AF and VTE subgroups. Larger, independent validation studies across diverse populations are required to confirm HELIX performance within indication-specific subgroups, as well as bleeding severity, oral and parenteral agents including LMWH, and the presence of underlying malignancy and its treatments to support broader use.

Secondly, for construct validity, most correlations with PROMIS measures were moderate and some were weak; while PROMIS measures are excellent measures of general and domain-specific health and overall quality of life, the items we compared indicate that these measures do not sufficiently cover the bleeding experience, supporting the need for a dedicated measure. Third, while HELIX demonstrated strong internal consistency and test-retest reliability overall, a small number of items showed only moderate or poor stability over time. In addition, we observed attrition of participants at follow-up, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the sample size available for responsiveness analyses. Although changes in HELIX scores were observed, PROMIS scores often remained unchanged, limiting our ability to confirm responsiveness; future work using alternative anchors would strengthen responsiveness evaluation.

Finally, although HELIX was designed to complement clinical care and inform patient-centered decision-making, this study was not designed to evaluate its use in guiding treatment decisions or predicting clinical outcomes. Future research should examine whether changes in HELIX scores over time can inform individualized anticoagulation management or predict bleeding events, functional decline, or healthcare utilization. Together, these limitations highlight the importance of ongoing validation and refinement of HELIX across diverse populations and clinical contexts, as well as exploration of its role in improving outcomes and shared decision-making in anticoagulation care.

Conclusions

HELIX is a novel, condition-specific PRO that captures bleeding-related quality of life in older adults taking anticoagulants—a population with distinct clinical needs. This instrument demonstrated strong initial validity and reliability and may help identify individuals experiencing impaired quality of life due to bleeding symptoms. Validation in larger and more diverse populations will further define its clinical utility, including its potential role in informing anticoagulation management and shared decision-making in this growing patient population.
