Reversible pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension due to long-standing arteriovenous fistula
Authors: Laura Saldivar, Emily Garber, Khaled Boubes, Elie Homsy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease predisposes patients to the development of pulmonary hypertension (PH) via multiple potential mechanisms. AV fistulas have been found to be superior to AV grafts in terms of maintaining patency, however unlike grafts fistulas can expand significantly over time which predisposes patients to high output heart failure and subsequent PH. This report demonstrates a case of newly diagnosed pre-capillary PH in a young patient presenting with dyspnea. The patient had an AV fistula placed 18 years prior to presentation for CKD and ultimately underwent transplantation. Pulmonary angiography was negative for PE and echocardiography demonstrated an elevated RVSP of 67 mmHg that was confirmed via right heart catheterization. This case was unique in that the patient's pre-capillary PH was reversed after AV fistula ligation and the patient also experienced improvement in renal graft function (Cr 1.43 to 1.2 mg/dL). AV fistulas can have a significant effect on cardiac function and clinicians should survey patients with AV fistulas for PH via screening echocardiography. Fistulas with outputs greater than 1.5-2 L/min should be considered for revision or ligation to decrease the risk of development of PH.Keywords: Pulmonary hypertension, Arteriovenous fistula, Reversible, Transplant-dialysis

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) predisposes patients to development of pulmonary hypertension (PH). Potential mechanisms include endothelial dysfunction, systemic hypertension, volume overload, chronic anemia and left to right shunting through arteriovenous (AV) fistulas or grafts. In a large retrospective study by O'Leary et al., of 1873 patients with stage III through V chronic kidney disease, 68% were found to have PH [1]. Of these, 76% were found to have post-capillary PH and 24% had pre-capillary PH. Most importantly, the study further demonstrated that the presence of PH had a significant impact on mortality in patients with CKD that increased progressively with CKD stage [1]. .

In the management of CKD, AV fistulas have been found to be superior to AV grafts given that they require fewer interventions to maintain access [2]. While the synthetic material imposed in AV grafts can prevent expansion, AV fistulas can dilate with increasing flows over time. While flows of only 600 ml/min are necessary for intermittent hemodialysis, average fistula flow rates are 1200 ml/min [3]. Fistula flow rates over 1.5-2 L per minute have been found to increase the risk for developing PH as well as high output heart failure [4,5].

Review of the literature reveals one case report of fistula ligation resulting in reversal of precapillary PH in a 68-year-old patient [6]. The following case adds to the limited experience with this complication of long-standing fistulas. We also demonstrate improvement in transplanted allograft function following fistula ligation.

Case description

A 38-year-old female with a past medical history of CKD due to reflux nephropathy status post left brachiocephalic AV fistula and renal transplant 18 years prior to presentation as well as autism spectrum disorder was evaluated for new symptoms of dyspnea, palpitations, and dizziness. Brain natriuretic protein was found to be elevated at 277 pg/mL (normal 0-100 pg/mL) and high sensitivity troponin was also elevated at 77 ng/L (normal <34 ng/L). Creatinine was 1.43 mg/dL from a baseline of 1 to 1.2 mg/dL. CT pulmonary angiography showed no PE but did show a dilated pulmonary artery with right ventricular (RV) strain. Echocardiogram showed an elevated RV systolic pressure (RVSP) of 67 mmHg with moderate RV enlargement, mildly reduced systolic function, and high probability for PH with tricuspid annual plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) of 2.36 cm and a tricuspid regurgitant velocity (TRV) of 4 m/s. A ventilation perfusion scan was low probability for PE. There were no signs of autoimmunity, hepatitis, or HIV. The patient subsequently underwent right heart catheterization with fistula study protocol (see supplement).

Right heart catheterization (see Table 1) showed fistula flow rates of about 2300 ml/min and systemic steal with a systemic cardiac index of 1.89. Interestingly, the RA waveform showed large V waves suspected to represent transmitted arterial pulsations from her fistula as tricuspid regurgitation was mild by echocardiography. Hemodynamic testing was consistent with precapillary PH and there was no response to inhaled nitric oxide. Notably, PA pressures improved dramatically with temporary fistula occlusion from a mean of 65 mmHg to a mean of 49 mmHg. The AV fistula was ligated by vascular surgery and the patient was discharged on tadalafil and macitentan. Macitentan was later changed to ambrisentan due to insurance coverage. At 1 month follow up her symptoms had abated and by 3 months her echocardiogram had normalized (normal RV size and function, TAPSE 1.82 cm, TRV not measured). Ambrisentan was discontinued and her creatinine had improved from 1.43 mg/dL (prior to fistula ligation) to 1.2 mg/dL. At 6 months her echocardiogram had ongoing normal RV function and the tadalafil dose was reduced to 20 mg daily before being discontinued at 9 months. Repeat echo at 12 months remained normal (normal RV size and function, TAPSE 2.19 cm, and TRV 2.63 m/s).

Discussion

Longstanding elevated cardiac output particularly with left to right shunting through high flow AV fistulas can lead to precapillary PH. Malin et al. (2024) demonstrated that almost one-third of patients with CKD and AVF had pre-capillary PH and that this risk was increased more than two-fold among subjects with AVF [7]. Postulated mechanisms of PH in CKD include pulmonary vascular remodeling in response to higher blood flows throughout the pulmonary system, reduced nitric oxide production, and increased endothelin production [7,8]. It is important to note that due to vessel size flow rates are higher in upper extremity compared to forearm fistulas, upper extremity fistulas may pose greater risk of PH development, and ratio of flow rate to cardiac output can be useful in enabling risk stratification [9].

PH should be considered in patients with fistulas who present with dyspnea. If there is concern for PH on echocardiography the next step is right heart catheterization. Proper fistula evaluation during right heart catheterization requires obtaining a saturation that does not include fistula flow in order to calculate systemic flow. Hemodynamics should be repeated during temporary occlusion of the fistula. Importantly, the hemodynamic changes noted during temporary fistula occlusion in the catheterization lab do not actually predict long term hemodynamic benefits after ligation as true flow through the AVF is often underestimated. In one study, manual compression reduced the average cardiac output (CO) by 1.1 L/min while permanent ligation reduced CO by 4.0 L/minute [10].

This case demonstrated a reversal in PH after fistula ligation. However, such a fortunate outcome cannot always be expected. Nevertheless, fistula ligation should always be pursued in severe precapillary PH. The decision to ligate or maintain AV fistulas after transplantation should be individualized as there are certainly situations of early graft failure in patients with limited vascular access. However, clinicians should consider at least revising or banding fistulas in patients with stable graft function to achieve flow rates less than 1.2 L/min. Ultrasound can be used to non-invasively estimate flow rates. A recent comprehensive review suggests obtaining surveillance echocardiography every 1-2 years in patients with an elevated PA systolic pressure (>50 mmHg) or sooner if the patient develops symptoms such as dyspnea on exertion [11]. We hope this case raises awareness on the effect of fistulas on cardiac function and stimulates further study on the topic.
