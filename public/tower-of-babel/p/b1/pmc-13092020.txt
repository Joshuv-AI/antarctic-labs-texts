Adoption and implementation of one health in Bangladesh: Governance structures, system strengths, challenges and future directions
Authors: Sukanta Chowdhury, Md Giasuddin, Sohel Rahman, Faisol Talukdar, Mohammad Nizam Uddin Chowdhury, Suman Das Gupta, Sumon Ghosh, Farhana Haque, Mohammed Ziaur Rahman, M. Salim Uzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bangladesh is characterized by a high density of human and animal populations, frequent human–animal interactions, rapid urbanization, environmental degradation, wildlife habitat destruction, poor biosecurity, intense animal farming and climate change. All of these factors contribute to an elevated risk of zoonotic disease emergence and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). These interconnected challenges underscore the need for an integrated One Health approach. Since 2008, Bangladesh has progressively institutionalized the One Health approach through civil society engagement, government endorsement of the One Health Strategic Framework, and the establishment of the One Health Secretariat (OHS). Ongoing refinement of the One Health Strategic Framework, strengthened multisectoral collaboration, expanded laboratory networks, coordinated workforce training and enhanced data sharing reflect steady progress toward a sustainable and integrated approach. Despite these advances, significant challenges and limitations persist that hinder effective and sustainable One Health implementation across One Health sectors. Collaboration among animal, human and environmental health sectors remains largely project-driven, short-term and donor-dependent. Institutional silos, inequitable distribution of responsibilities, inadequate inter-ministerial coordination and limited application of One Health frameworks continue to constrain consistent joint planning and resource sharing. Major challenges include insufficient strengthening of One Health governance; a shortage of empowered mid-level One Health leaders to sustain efforts; inadequate resources and limited long-term financial commitment from the government; insufficient engagement of environmental and climate sectors; limited sustained institutional commitment; uncertainty among stakeholders regarding ownership and responsibility; inadequate coordination at sub-national and community levels, limited integration of One Health principles into academic curricula and community programs; inadequate professional training on One Health and low levels of community awareness. Formalizing and strengthening One Health governance, empowering the institutional authority of the OHS, developing future One Health leaders, ensuring sustained political and financial commitment and integrating One Health principles into national planning instruments are essential for sustainable implementation. In addition, establishing a National One Health Institute with a clear country-specific vision, mission, and long-term commitment would substantially strengthen national capacity to detect, prevent, and respond to One Health threats.

Introduction

Bangladesh is one of the most densely populated countries in the world, with over 165 million inhabitants and a population density of 1119 people per square kilometer [1]. The country faces a unique convergence of public health challenges, including zoonotic diseases, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), climate change and environmental degradation [2], [3]. Close interaction between humans and poultry or other livestock reservoirs occurs frequently in both rural and urban communities, increasing the risk of zoonotic diseases transmission [2], [4]. These challenges are deeply interconnected, demanding an integrated multisectoral approach that bridges human, animal, and environmental health sectors.

Recognizing this need, Bangladesh has emerged as a regional frontrunner in adopting the One Health (OH) approach, pioneering policies and establishing institutional mechanisms to address these public health risk and proactively advancing policies and institutional frameworks to tackle these multifaceted threats. Following the Nipah virus outbreak in 2005 and the avian influenza outbreak in 2007, Chattogram Veterinary and Animal Sciences University (CVASU) initiated a civil society network of professionals and researchers from both animal and human health sectors in 2008. This network led to the establishment of One Health Bangladesh (OHB), a platform with membership open to multi-disciplinary scientists, practitioners, researchers, and activists [5]. A significant milestone in this journey was the government's endorsement of the One Health Strategic Framework 2012 by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), Ministry of Fisheries and Livestock (MoFL) and Ministry of Environment, Forest, and Climate Change (MoEFCC), followed by the establishment and subsequent institutionalization of the One Health Secretariat in 2016, hosted at Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (Fig. 1) [5].Fig. 1Key timeline of One-Health Capacity building in Bangladesh.Fig. 1

Although Bangladesh developed a OH strategy in 2012 and revised it in 2017, implementation continues to face several challenges including weak governance, limited institutional capacity, inadequate funding and infrastructure [6]. The contribution of the environmental sector to the OH movement remains inadequate. To enable functional OH governance, the government plans to develop OH legal framework and expand the governance mechanism to sub-national levels (Strategic framework draft 2025–2030). However, inadequate stakeholder coordination and the lack of required legislation for intersectoral collaboration remain among the most significant barriers [6]. In Bangladesh, ministries and departments of human and animal health sectors often function in institutional silos, with limited inter-ministerial coordination and unclear lines of responsibility [7], [8]. Ministries and departments typically operate with limited communication or shared accountability, and their roles in OH implementation are often overlapping or poorly defined. The environment and forestry, livestock, and health departments usually work independently from one another, constraining strong cooperation and resulting in fragmented and uncoordinated efforts [6]. The absence of legally mandated governance structures limits the formalization of roles and responsibilities among stakeholders, undermining effective coordination and collaboration to address infectious and non-infectious diseases at the human-animal-environment interface. This lack of integrated governance results in weak joint planning, inconsistent implementation and duplication of efforts across sectors.

Strengths

Bangladesh has demonstrated commendable progress in embracing the OH approach through strong multisectoral involvement. Key departments across relevant ministries are actively engaged, including IEDCR under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW); the Department of Livestock Services (DLS) under the Ministry of Fisheries and Livestock (MoFL); and the Bangladesh Forest Department (BFD) under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC). One of the notable achievements of this collaboration has been the initiation of joint outbreak investigation practices, particularly in response to zoonotic disease events such as anthrax, avian influenza and Nipah virus outbreaks. These coordinated responses have highlighted the value of shared responsibilities, timely information exchange and real-time communication among sectors. Moreover, Bangladesh actively participates in regional OH platforms led by international agencies such as the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), and the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH).

The One Health Secretariat (OHS), established in 2016, is operational, albeit with limited capacity and has made progress in multi-sectoral collaboration, coordination and strategic planning. The OHS is housed within IEDCR, bringing together officers deputed (seconded) from the human health, animal health, and forest department to facilitate joint planning and multisectoral collaboration. Within this collaborative framework, tools such as Event-Based Surveillance (EBS) systems have been developed at the OHS, enabling rapid detection of and response to zoonotic outbreaks.

Bangladesh introduced the Field Epidemiology Training Program Bangladesh (FETP'B) in the public health sector in 2016 and FETP,V (Field Epidemiology Training Program for veterinarian) in the animal health sector in 2020, with support from the US 10.13039/100000030Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and EMPHNET (Eastern Mediterranean Public Health Network), respectively. The FETP'B course is run by IEDCR, DGHS, while the FETP,V is run by DLS. In the Wildlife sector, Field Training Program for Wildlife, Ecosystem, Biodiversity, and Environment (FTP-WEBE) is under planning process.

To guide OH implementation, Bangladesh continues trying to strengthen its One Health governance over the past decade through the adoption of the OH strategic framework in 2012, followed by a revision in 2017. Currently, efforts are underway to update the 3rd One Health Strategic Framework 2025–2030. The foundation of OH Strategic Framework established a roadmap for cross-sectoral coordination, with subsequent revisions refining priorities and enhancing collaborative action. In addition, the country has conducted Joint Risk Assessments to collaboratively identify and prioritize zoonotic disease threats, with technical support from the WHO South-East Asia Region.

At the frontline, ongoing training for health workers, veterinarians and field staff has improved their knowledge and skills in disease surveillance, outbreak investigation and risk communication. In addition, investments have been made in strengthening laboratory networks, enhancing diagnostic capacity across sectors, ensuring the timely detection and confirmation of priority pathogens.

Challenges and limitations

Despite significant progress in advancing the OH agenda, Bangladesh continues to face several challenges in ensuring effective and sustained collaboration across sectors. A key limitation is that OH activities remains largely project-based and donor-driven, with limited long-term institutional commitment and national ownership. Many multisectoral initiatives were initiated under externally funded projects, which often lose momentum once the funding cycle ends. The OH Inter-ministerial Steering Committee meetings do not occur on a regular basis, although the OH Strategic Framework recommends that the committee meet twice a year. However, the importance of these meetings is often not taken seriously. Frequent transitions within the secretariats and among ministers also make it difficult to maintain continuity and regularize their work on OH. The ministries and relevant departments often operate their activities in institutional silos, with overlapping mandates and fragmented planning processes. The dilemma over leadership superiority, the exercise of undue power and the unwillingness to share resources often lead institutions to operate in silos. Currently, there is no precise mechanism to ensure a sustained operational budget for the OH Secretariat and for the implementation of the strategic framework through coordinated resource planning and mobilization. The absence of coordinated governance and collaboration results in weak joint planning, inconsistent implementation, and duplication of efforts across OH sectors.

The national OH strategic framework lacks a clear roadmap or strategic plan to encourage young professionals to engage in the One Health movement and develop as future leaders. As a result, the shortage of mid-level One Health leaders in the future may create a critical gap that could hinder sustained progress in advancing the OH agenda. Inconsistent supervision, unclear feedback mechanisms and high turnover among government officials hinder the institutionalization of sustained One Health (OH) practices. Unclear mandates along with limited authority and inadequate budgetary control hinder coordinated actions and joint responses in surveillance, research, and outbreak investigations.

Other important challenges include very limited engagement of the private sector and civil society organizations, which could provide valuable technical expertise, operational resources, advocacy, and linkages from the national to community levels. The absence of a legally binding mandate hampers cross-ministerial coordination and the clarification of institutional responsibilities. In addition, clear mechanisms for OH coordination at sub-national and community levels are lacking. The OH activities remain mostly confined to the national level, with only partial implementation in a few public universities. While the One Health Secretariat exists, its authority to implement actions remains limited and often depends on individual initiatives or project-based activities rather than a formalized mechanism for sustained multisectoral collaboration. Only 2–3 officials are seconded from the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), the Department of Livestock Services (DLS), and the Bangladesh Forest Department (BFD) to work in the OHS. The small number of officers, combined with weak infrastructural and functional capacity constrains its ability to perform effectively. Furthermore, the rotational leadership of the OHS across other departments has not yet been implemented.

The integration of OH principles into human and animal health laws and regulations is either missing or inadequate. The failure to fully incorporate OH principles into key frameworks and laws such as the national Five-year Plans of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), the Communicable Disease (Prevention, Control and Eradication) Act 2018, the Animal Disease Act 2005, Food Safety Act 2013 and Bangladesh Environment Conservation Act 1995 represents a lost opportunity to strengthen governance coherence and enable more effective, strategic resource allocation. One Health inherently requires multisectoral integration, which can be difficult to fully operationalize in highly fragmented governance systems. Dependence on donor-driven projects makes long-term sustainability difficult.

Bangladesh with its high vulnerability to floods, cyclones, salinity intrusion, and rising temperatures amplifies the risks of zoonotic spillovers, AMR, and vector-borne diseases. The complex vulnerability requires engagement of multiple sectors. So far, the environment and climate sectors remain peripheral in OH planning and implementation. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change remains inadequately engaged, and climate adaptation strategies are weakly integrated into human and animal health planning. This gap limits the ability of OH governance to address the full spectrum of human-animal-environment interface, leaving the country exposed to compounding crises driven by both pathogens and climate change.

Insufficient integration of OH concepts into academic and professional curricula across medical, veterinary, public health, environmental, forestry and other relevant disciplines persists. As a result, graduates entering these fields have no prior interdisciplinary knowledge or collaborative mindset essential for effective OH approaches. While a specialized OH graduate program is offered at the master's level, there are no dedicated curricula for undergraduate or doctoral programs. Awareness on OH concept remains limited at local government and community levels, where zoonotic disease risks often originate and where early detection and response are most critical. Without targeted outreach and education, community engagement in OH initiatives remain limited, thereby reducing the overall effectiveness of prevention and control measures.

Strategic priorities for consideration

To strengthen the implementation of the OH approach in Bangladesh, it is essential to move beyond project-based collaboration toward a more institutionalized, sustainable and nationally owned OH Strategic Framework. Equally important is the mainstreaming of OH into national development planning and budgeting processes, ensuring its integration into key frameworks such as the Five-Year Plans, relevant sectoral policies and national SDG implementation strategies. Establishing dedicated budget lines within relevant ministries would provide the financial sustainability required to operationalize long-term OH initiatives. The OH Inter-ministerial Steering Committee serves as the highest authority for guiding One Health (OH) actions through financial and legal support. As the committee is composed of ministers elected from political parties, major political parties can consider incorporating the OH agenda into their election manifestos. The importance of the OH agenda should also be discussed and integrated into broader political discourse.

Strengthening cross-sectoral data sharing systems is another key priority. Enabling real-time data sharing and joint analysis across public health, veterinary and environmental surveillance platforms would significantly enhance early warning, detection and rapid response to emerging threats. In parallel, sustained investment in capacity-building and curriculum reform is needed to develop a workforce equipped with the interdisciplinary knowledge and practical skills required for effective OH actions. This must include targeted training at the local government level, where early detection and community engagement are essential. Moreover, fostering public-private-academic partnerships will be vital to leverage technical expertise, innovation and resources beyond government institutions. Such partnerships can accelerate research, promote technology adoption and strengthen service delivery in line with OH principles. The OHS, established in Bangladesh, is uniquely positioned to evolve into a ‘National One Health Institute or Foundation’ based in Dhaka. Building on its existing mandate and credibility, the OHS can transition from a coordination unit into a permanent center of excellence for strong OH governance, research, policy innovation and capacity building.

Finally, to ensure the successful implementation of the OH multisectoral approach in Bangladesh, it is essential to strengthen its governance through a comprehensive and integrated strategy that addresses several interconnected domains. Key priorities include enhancing sectoral and institutional coordination and collaboration, supported by a clear and functional governance structure. Sustained political commitment and leadership are crucial to ensure national ownership and effective cross-ministerial engagement. These efforts must be backed by adequate financial and human resource allocation, as well as robust systems for data sharing and integrated surveillance, which are critical for effective implementation. Strengthening technical capacity and raising awareness at all levels, especially among the mid-level, frontline workers and local communities will further support operationalization. Long-term sustainability of OH initiatives requires their systematic integration into national planning and budgeting frameworks. Additionally, the establishment of strong systems for monitoring, evaluation, and accountability will be essential to track progress and guide evidence-based informed decision-making. At the heart of these efforts lies the urgent need to strengthen the OHS both institutionally and financially so it can function as a fully empowered national coordinating body capable of leading and sustaining the OH agenda in Bangladesh.

Conclusions

The future progress of OH in Bangladesh depends on the effective institutionalization of multisectoral governance, supported by strengthened coordination, regulatory mechanisms, financing and sustained political commitment. Advancing this agenda requires moving beyond fragmented and project-based collaboration toward an integrated and accountable governance framework. Strengthening governance and institutional resilience provides an important opportunity to adopt a multi-tiered consultation model that actively engages stakeholders working on One Health at national, subnational and local levels. This participatory approach can facilitate inclusive and structured dialogue among key stakeholders, including government agencies, ministries, research and academic institutions, civil society organizations, professional associations, development partners and community representatives. This process will not only promote shared accountability and broad stakeholder engagement but also establish a strong foundation for embedding OH principles into national policy agendas, development plans and sectoral strategies.
