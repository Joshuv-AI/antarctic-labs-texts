Facile and cost-effective synthesis and characterization of hormone stabilized biocompatible gold nanoparticles for biomedical applications
Authors: Rajendran Ganapathy, Murugadas Anbazhagan, Punitha Natarajan, Ancy Jenifer Albin, Nallusamy Duraisamy, Kathiravan Krishnan, Gholamreza Abdi, Maryam Abbasi Tarighat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal nanoparticles possess unique characteristic features suitable for biomedical and other biological applications such as antimicrobial activity to drug delivery. Non-toxic, biocompatible nanoparticles are essential for these applications. Many of previously described methods applied heat or boiling and toxic chemicals like SDS, beta mercaptoethanol, EDTA, sodium citrate for nanoparticles synthesis. The present research emphasizes the synthesis of the gold nanoparticles using a non-toxic reducing and stabilizing agent sodium thyroxine through a facile, cost effective, and eco-friendly method. The formation of gold nanoparticles was confirmed in the UV-visible spectrophotometer with a SPR peak at 522 nm. The synthesized gold nanoparticles were spherical shape and the particles size were found to be < 15 nm in HR-TEM instrumental analysis. Further, physicochemical characterization of the nanoparticles was analyzed by DLS, zeta potential, XRD, and FT-IR. In-vitro hemolytic assay and ESR estimation results revealed that the synthesized gold nanoparticles were more hemocompatible with human RBCs up to 500 µg/mL. Additionally, Vero and A549 cell lines were used to determine the nanoparticles biocompatibility using in-vitro MTT assay, wound healing assay, cell morphology analysis, and DNA fragmentation study. The in-vivo toxicity of the synthesized gold nanoparticles was analyzed by Hydra magnipapillata that result exhibited the biocompatibility of nanoparticles upto 600 µg/mL. Further, the biocompatibility of nanoparticles was confirmed by the morphological studies, feeding behavior, hydra regeneration study, and apoptosis analysis. Therefore, this gold nanoparticle can be utilized for various biomedical applications like drug delivery, gene delivery, photothermal therapy, biosensing, antimicrobial activity and bio-imaging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56133-0.

Introduction

Recently, gold nanoparticles have been practiced as potential key materials and are widely used in various fields including medicine, biological science, material science, physics, chemistry, and so on1. The physicochemical characteristics of metal nanoparticles differ from those of their corresponding bulk materials. These characteristics include size, a high surface to volume ratio, tunable physical and chemical properties, and target binding properties2. Because of its passive effects on several economic sectors, such as consumer goods, biology, pharmaceutics, cosmetics, transportation, energy, etc., metal nanoparticles are being created more often for a variety of applications3.

Amongst the different metal nanoparticles, the gold based nanoparticles are widely studied as attractive materials can be synthesized from different materials such chemicals, biological (microbial biomass), plant leaves extract, etc.4. Gold nanoparticles can be synthesized in different sizes and shapes such as sphere, rod, wires and tubes depending on different biological and biomedical applications such like drug delivery, Photothermal therapy, bioimaging, therapeutics, gene delivery, colorimetric sensing of molecules and so on5,6. The methods like chemical and biological means of synthesis of gold nanoparticles are familiar due facile to synthesis, tunable size and shape, inexpensive and so on. Recently, researchers have used a wide range of chemicals to synthesize gold nanoparticles which includes EDTA7, beta-mercaptoethanol, SDS, glucose, amino acids, vitamins8, nutrient agar, sodium borohydride, and so on4,9. However, among these methods, many of the chemicals are toxic to in vitro and the in vivo system and therefore the gold nanoparticles synthesized by these methods could not be utilized for biological applications. Further, many of the synthesizing process involved high temperature, addition of number of stabilizing agents for gold nanoparticles synthesis, which induces cytotoxicity or hemolysis in the animal system when they are applied. Therefore, synthesis of non-toxic gold nanoparticles and analysis of toxicity effect of gold nanoparticles is very essential. Environmental friendly synthesis method, non-toxic suitable solvent, reducing and the capping agent are the essential key factor of the gold nanoparticles preparation for biomedical applications10–12.

There are a number of experiments available for in vitro toxicity analysis of nanoparticles such as MTT assay, flow cytometry, fluorescence microscopy, and so on9. Similarly, the in vivo toxicity of nanoparticles is mostly analyzed using rat, mice, and rabbit as model organism. Recently, researchers are analyzed nanoparticles toxicity using simple organisms like Zebra fish, Daphnia, Paratya13, and Hydra. They are very easy to maintain, economic, rapidly multiplied, immediate response to testing materials14. Hydra is a simple aquatic invertebrate fresh water organism. The toxicity analysis of metal and metal nanoparticles in Hydra has been documented in a number of papers because of its easy handling, quick reproduction, and regeneration.

Hence, in this study the synthesis of gold nanoparticles under the bright sun light by cost effective, facile method using non-toxic, biocompatible sodium thyroxine as a reducing and stabilizing agent and toxicity of sodium thyroxine stabilized gold nanoparticles was analyzed using Vero and A549 cells. Similarly, in vivo toxicity was analyzed using Hydra magnipapillata in dose and time dependent manner.

Materials and methods

All the experiments in the methodology were carried out in accordance with relevant guideline and regulations and all experimental protocols were approved by the respective institutional committee.

Synthesis of thyroxine stabilized gold nanoparticles (T-AuNPs)

The Chloroauric acid and Sodium thyroxine were purchased from Loba chemie, and Sigma-Aldrich, India respectively. The standardized solutions of aqueous chloroauric acid (0.5 mM) and sodium thyroxine (0.5 mM) were prepared using deionized double distilled water. From the above standard solutions, 3.5 mL of sodium thyroxine was transferred to the 100 mL conical flask containing 50 mL of chloroauric acid. The pH of the solution in the conical flask was adjusted to 7.5. The solution was mixed by stirring at 1000 rpm and further kept under bright sunlight for 20 min and allowed to react (the intensity of bright sunlight was 90 mW/cm2, at Lat 13.033095° Long 80.180252°). There was appearance of visible colour change from light yellow to dark red colour representing the synthesis of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). The synthesized sodium thyroxine stabilized AuNPs (T-AuNPs) was confirmed through surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak estimation in the UV-visible spectrophotometer and stored in a volumetric flask around 4 °C for the further analysis and uses. T-AuNPs was synthesized by different concentration of Sodium thyroxine (0.3 mM to 0.6 mM), different pH (5 to 10), different condition (Room temperature, Boiling method, Sun light) and reaction time (5 to 25 min) and the reaction condition was optimized by T-AuNPs SPR peak analysis in UV-visible spectrophotometer (Supplementary data Fig. 1; Table 1).

Characterization of T-AuNPs

UV-visible spectrophotometer analysis

The optical absorbance property of synthesized T-AuNPs was assessed using the UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1601 spectrometer, Japan) and the data were obtained from the software UV Probe Ver. 2. The synthesized T-AuNPs (1 mL) sample was taken in a 1 cm path quartz cuvette, and placed in the sample holder and double distilled water was taken as the blank. The optical absorbance of T-AuNPs was measured in the wavelength range from 200 to 1000 nm. The wavelength data was plotted in Origin 8.0 software for determining the absorbance peak.

Field emission–scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and EDX analysis

The surface morphology of the synthesized T-AuNPs was analyzed using the FE-SEM. A drop (~ 50 µL) of synthesized T-AuNPs was placed on clean aluminium foil, and let to dry overnight at room temperature and examined under FE-SEM (Hitachi SU6600, Germany). The micrograph was recorded at 500 nm scale bar. An accelerating of 15 kV was applied. In addition, the appearance of gold in the sample was estimated by the energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy attached with the FE-SEM.

High resolution-transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) analysis

The data on size, shape and distribution of the T-AuNPs was acquired through the HR-TEM, (FEI TECHNAI G2 MODEL T-30-S-TWIN, USA). A drop (3 µL) of T-AuNPs solution was deposited on a carbon coated copper grid (mesh size 300) and the grid was dried at room temperature for overnight. After inserting the sample-loaded copper grid into the HR-TEM’s grid holder, the morphology of T-AuNPs was investigated. The instrument was run with a high resolution (UHR) pole piece at an accelerated of 200 kV.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis

The hydrodynamic diameter and particle size distribution of T-AuNPs in the aqueous medium were determined through Zetasizer Nano Series (Malvern Instruments, United Kingdom). About 2 mL of T-AuNPs solution was diluted in double distilled water (1:2) and added into a disposable cuvette without air bubbles and placed in the sample holder. For 60 s, the instrument was run at a count rate of 232 kcps and an accelerating of 100 VA (Volt-Amperes) at 25 °C. The data obtained was plotted in Origin 8.0 software for further interpretation.

Zeta potential analysis

The surface net charge (ζ-potential) of the T-AuNPs was measured in the Zetasizer Nano Series. About 2 mL of T-AuNPs was injected into the folded capillary electrode cuvette (pre-washed with deionized water) and placed in the cuvette holder. At 25 °C and an accelerating of 100 VA (Volt-Amperes), the instrument was operated. The obtained data was plotted in Origin 8.0 software for further interpretation.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The T-AuNPs solution was centrifuged and lyophilized to prepare the powder sample. Sodium thyroxine and T-AuNPs was separately ground with potassium bromide and compressed to a 2 mm transparent disc. The resulting pellets were examined using the FT-IR spectrophotometer (Jasco 5300, Jasco, Mary’s Court, Easton, MD, USA) to determine if sodium thyroxine and T-AuNPs contained functional groups. The percentage of transmission was obtained in the spectral range of 500 to 4500 cm− 1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The GE X-ray diffraction system-XRD 303 TT was used to take the XRD pattern of T-AuNPs. The source was Cu Kα1 radiation (T = 1.5406 Å) with a voltage and current of 40 kV and 30 mA, and the angle was 2θ. After being placed on a sanitized glass slide, T-AuNPs was vacuum-dried. A 2θ range of 30° to 80° was used to scan the glass slide with T-AuNPs put on it.

Hemocompatibility analysis

Hemolytic assay

The Vedakumari et al., 2015 method was used to perform the haemolytic experiment with minimal modifications15. The ethical committee approval was obtained from Institutional Human Ethical Committee, University of Madras, Chennai and the informed consent was obtained from the volunteer blood donor (IHEC Approval No: UM/IHEC/RM/2017-III). The anticoagulated blood (5 mL) was centrifuged for five minutes at room temperature at 5000 rpm, and the supernatant was disposed off. The sterile saline solution was used three times to wash the red blood cell (RBC) pellet. In several Eppendorf tubes, a sterile saline solution was used to create the RBC cell suspension (5% v/v). Different T-AuNP concentrations (100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µg/mL) were added to the RBCs suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. All of the tubes were centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm following the incubation, and the supernatant was measured at 540 nm in comparison to the negative controls. RBCs treated with saline solution and Triton X-100 (1% v/v) were employed as a negative and positive control respectively. The morphology of untreated RBCs and T-AuNPs treated (500 µg/mL) RBCs were observed under the low power objective (10X) in an optical microscope (OLYMPUS CH 20i) attached with Celestron digital microscope imager.

Estimation of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)

The impact of T-AuNPs on the human RBCs was studied using ESR experiment by following the Westergren method16. The anticoagulated blood was mixed with T-AuNPs (500 µg/mL) and loaded into the Westergren tube without any air bubble and cell lysis up to “0” level, which is marked as the test sample (T). The blood sample without T-AuNPs was used as the control (C). Both the tubes were fitted into the Westergren apparatus vertically and allowed to stand for 1 h without disturbance. ESR was recorded at 15 min interval up to 1 h for both the control and T-AuNPs treated sample and the obtained results were plotted as a graph.

In-vitro biocompatibility analysis of T-AuNPs

Cell culture

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s media (DMEM) was used to maintain the normal Vero cell line, while Ham Kaighn’s modification media (Ham’s F-12 K medium) was used to sustain the A549 human lung cancer cell line. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 10% (v/v) and an antibiotic-antimycotic solution at 1% (v/v) were added to the medium. In an incubator with a humidified environment and 37 °C and 5% CO2, the cells were cultivated. For the experiments, cells in the exponential growth phase were utilized, and cells were subcultured according to the confluence.

Cell viability analysis

The influence of T-AuNPs on the viability of Vero and A549 cells was evaluated using MTT assay17[,18. Briefly, 1 × 104 cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were then subjected to varying T-AuNP concentrations (100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µg/mL) and incubated for 24 h. The cells without T-AuNPs addition served as control. Following incubation, 10 µL of MTT (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to all the wells including control and incubated for 3 h. Finally, 100 µL of DMSO was added to all the wells to dissolve the formazan that produce purple colour, which was measured by ELISA reader (BIO-TEK, Power wave-XS) at 570 nm. Microscopic analysis

Microscopic analysis was performed to study the impact of T-AuNPs in Vero and A549 cells. Both the cell lines were cultured in six-well plates with respective culture medium for 24 h. Following their exposure to T-AuNPs (400 µg/mL), the cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Using an inverted light microscope (10X, Motic AE31, Hong Kong), the morphology of the T-AuNPs treated and the untreated control cells was examined following incubation.

In-vitro wound healing assay

The effect of T-AuNPs in cell-cell interaction and cell migration was assessed using wound healing assay19. The A549 cells (5 × 104cell per well) were cultured in six-well plate using Ham’s F-12 K medium at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2, After attaining > 80% of confluence, the monolayer cells were scratched using a sterile micro tip and floating cells were removed from well plate by washing with culture medium and the images were captured using microscope. After marking the areas that had been scratched, the cells were treated with 400 µg/mL of T-AuNPs and incubated for 48 h. Using an inverted light microscope, the wound closure was seen after 48 h and captured on camera (10X, Motic AE31, Hong Kong).

Apoptosis analysis using fluorescence staining

Acridine orange/Ethidium bromide fluorescence stain is used for T-AuNPs mediated apoptosis analysis with minor modification of previous method20. In a six-well plate with a sterile coverslip, the Vero and A549 cells (5 × 104 cells per well) were grown independently at 37 °C with 5% CO2 using DMEM and Ham’s F-12 K medium, respectively. After adding 400 µg/mL of T-AuNPs to both cell lines they were left to incubate for 24 h. The cells that were left untreated served as the control. 2 µL of AO/EtBr stain composition (100 µg/mL of AO/EtBr produced in PBS) were used to stain the untreated and T-AuNPs treated cells. The stained cells in the coverslip were then seen under the green and red filters of a Leica DMi8 (10X) fluorescence microscope.

DNA fragmentation analysis

DNA fragmentation analysis was performed based on the method adopted by Ramakrishnan et al. 2009 with slight modification20. Briefly, the Vero and A549 cells at a concentration of 5 × 104 cells per well were seeded in a T25 flask with DMEM and Ham’s F-12 K medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2. After incubation, the cells were exposed to 400 µg/mL of T-AuNPs for 24 h, and the cells that were not exposed to T-AuNPs served as a control. The cells were trypsinised and then twice rinsed with PBS buffer (pH = 7.4) after a 24-hour period. T-AuNP-treated and untreated control cell lines were used to extract the genomic DNA. For 45 min at 100 V, the DNA (20 µg) was electrophoresed in a 1% agarose gel with EtBr in a gel tank with TAE buffer (pH = 8). Under a UV transilluminator, the gel’s fluorogram was captured on camera (LARK, India).

In-vivo biocompatibility of T-AuNPs in hydra

Culture of hydra

Hydra magnipapillata 105 strain was received from Dr. Angelika Bottger laboratory Ludwig-Maximilians University, Munich, Germany. According to Lenhoff and Brown (1970), the animals were raised in hydra medium that included 1 mM calcium chloride, 0.1 mM magnesium sulphate, 0.1 mM potassium chloride, 1 mM sodium chloride, and 1 mM tris base (pH = 7.4). The culture was kept in glass bowls with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness at 18 °C. Freshly hatched Artemia nauplii were feed to the animals every day. From the stock culture, polyps that had been fasted for 24 h were selected and used in every experiment. All the experiments in the methodology were carried out in accordance with relevant guideline and regulations,

Determination of acute toxicity of T-AuNPs in hydra

In vivo biocompatibility of T-AuNPs in Hydra was studied by method of Trottier et al., 199721. Bud less polyps were selected for in vivo toxicity experiments. About 25 adult live polyps were moved to a sterile 6-well plate with 2 mL of Hydra medium, and they were cultivated for 12 h at 18 °C in a dark and light cycle. The polyps were treated with different concentration of T-AuNPs (50 to 900 µg/mL) and incubated for 96 h. After that any alterations in the morphology of Hydra were observed and recorded at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h using stereo-zoom dissecting microscope (20X) (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) connected with camera (ERc5 s). To determine the biocompatibility of T-AuNPs, the structural changes were recorded at each time point and the scores were assigned according to Wilby’s scale of toxicity testing22. Score 10 was assigned to healthy polyps, score 9 to 5 was assigned based on the grades of morphological changes that are not lethal and score 5 and below was assigned for lethal polyps. Score 0 was assigned to animals that had disintegration (Table 1).

Feeding assay

Feeding assay was carried out according to Ambrosone et al., 201424. The Hydra were treated with T-AuNPs (600 µg/mL) for 24 h. After incubation, treated polyps and untreated control polyps were tested for the ability to catch, kill or ingest the freshly hatched Artemia nauplii. The Axiocam ERc5s camera-equipped stereo zoom dissecting microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) was used to examine the feeding habits of the Hydra.

Analysis of hydra regeneration

The effect of T-AuNPs on the regeneration of Hydra was studied by following the method of little modification25. A group of 15 polyps were cut off in the upper gastric region using a sterile scalpel. The gastric area was put in a sterile well plate with Hydra medium and left with T-AuNPs (600 µg/mL) to reconstruct the missing portion. The regenerating polyps were viewed under a stereo-zoom dissecting microscope.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis in Hydra was analyzed by the method of Cikala et al., 199926. Briefly, 10 polyps were exposed to T-AuNPs (600 µg/mL) for 48 h, then Acridine orange (3.3 µM) was used to stain them for 15 min in a dark environment. The Hydra was washed with Hydra medium and mounted on the 2% urethane. The apoptosis of Hydra was analyzed in fluorescence microscope (Axioskop 2 plus, Carl Zeiss, Germany), and photographed.

Result and discussion

Synthesis of T-AuNPs

The remarkable physicochemical properties of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) such as their tunable size, shape, surface modification, bio-conjugation, optical, electrical, and thermal capabilities, have led to their growing use recently in a number of sectors27. AuNPs have been produced via physical, chemical, and biological methods28. Many of the previously reported methods for AuNPs synthesis require sophisticated instruments, high temperature and pressure. Subsequently, it is essential to adopt effortless, affordable, safe, and environmentally friendly method for synthesizing AuNPs.

Recently sunlight mediated synthesis of NPs is an emerging field, in which sunlight is used as an external stimulating agent in NPs synthesis instead of thermal energy. Sunlight radiation is non-toxic, traceless in chemical process and non-pollutant29,30. Therefore, Sunlight-mediated NP synthesis is more advantageous than the current physical and chemical processes. Moreover, sodium thyroxine is a non-toxic, and naturally secreting hormone. This study is involving a simple, robust sunlight mediated synthesis of AuNPs using sodium thyroxine (0.5 mM), as a reducing and stabilizing agent during the formation of AuNPs. Sodium thyroxine stabilized gold nanoparticles (T-AuNPs) synthesized in the aqueous medium at a biological pH (7.5) under the bright sunlight for 20 min (Fig. 1). After addition of sodium thyroxine, the yellow colour solution of chloroauric acid turned into deep red colour of AuNPs. This colour change is due to the chemical reduction of Au3+ ions to Au0 by sodium thyroxine. Synthesis of T-AuNPs by the sunlight irradiation is very simple, no need of sophisticated instruments, time saving, eco-friendly and non-toxic. Further, T-AuNPs was synthesized in an aqueous medium with a pH of 7.5, which is most favorable condition for the biological applications.

Characterization of T-AuNPs

UV-Visible spectroscopic analysis

UV-Visible spectroscopic analysis was used as a preliminary identification of NPs synthesis31. Depending on the size and shape of the synthesized nanoparticles, AuNPs have a significant capacity to absorb light in the visible region. The distinguishable SPR peak that forms between 500 and 550 nm confirms the synthesis of AuNPs in the UV-visible spectroscopy32. The UV-visible absorbance spectrum of synthesized T-AuNPs showed single narrow peak at 522 nm (1.22 a.u.) (Fig. 2a), which proved the synthesis of monodispersed small sized particles. In this reaction of gold nanoparticles synthesis, sodium thyroxine acts as both reducing and stabilizing agent and it prevents the nanoparticles aggregation. The synthesized T-AuNPs had good stability for more than 120 days at 4℃. Moreover, it had good stability in the different temperature (T-AuNPs incubated for 1 h), which was confirmed by SPR peak analysis in UV-visible spectrophotometer (Supplementary data Fig. 2; Table 2). The optical property of the AuNPs (SPR peak and intensity) is based on the size of the AuNPs. According to Abdelhalim (2012), AuNPs of 10 nm and 50 nm sizes showed an SPR peak at 517 and 532 nm, respectively33. The NPs components, size, synthesis method, shape and surface behaviour can all affect their biological effects and uses34.

FE-SEM and EDX analysis

To validate the NPs surface morphology, FE-SEM analysis is employed35. The results exhibited that, the T-AuNPs formed as spherical shaped particles with dispersed nature in 500 nm scale bar (Fig. 2b). Additionally, the detection of the gold elements peak in the tested sample confirmed the presence of T-AuNPs in the EDX analysis (Au: weight% (74.28%) and atomic percentage (28.35%) (Fig. 2c).

HR-TEM analysis

The HR-TEM is used for the analysis of the nanoparticles size and shape. The carbon-coated copper grid was loaded with the synthesized T-AuNPs and analyzed under the HR-TEM at 100 and 50 nm scale bar. The result revealed that the T-AuNPs was monodispersed, spherical shaped, and the particles size was found to be < 15 nm (Fig. 3). The spherical shaped particles synthesized formed by reduction of Au3+ ions to Au0 metal followed by deposition of reduced metal atoms. Accepting the electron from the reducing agent causes the Au3 + ions to be reduced. Reaction time, medium pH, and reductant concentration all affect the size and shape of AuNPs. The size and shape of nanoparticles plays significant contribution in their cytotoxicity36,37. Previously Ravi et al., 2013 reported sunlight mediated synthesis of AuNPs using fresh lemon juice and the size of the synthesized AuNPs was 10 to 25 nm38. AuNPs with sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm are mostly useful in biomedical applications39.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis

Nanoparticle’s hydrodynamic diameter (average), distribution, and zeta potential can be determined with the aid of DLS analysis40. The synthesized T-AuNPs showed a narrow size distribution range, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 29.71 nm as shown in the Fig. 4a and the polydispersity index value was found to be 0.386. The hydrodynamic diameter size of NPs obtained by DLS is larger than size of TEM analysis, this may be due to the absorbance of biomolecules (water) on the surface of the T-AuNPs.

Zeta potential analysis

Using the NPs direction and velocity, the zeta potential is estimated when they are subjected to a known electric field. One technique for determining the surface charge of NPs in a colloidal solution. The electric potential at the edge of the double layer of counterions that surrounds NPs because of their surface charge is measured by the Zeta potential. The Zeta potential is additionally influenced by particle size, concentration, temperature, solvent, and ionic strength41. The net surface charge of the T-AuNPs was − 16.6 mV (Fig. 4b), it is due the presence of C = O and -OH group on the surface of T-AuNPs. Each particle in the solution is repelled by the observed negative zeta potential that responds to prevent T-AuNPs aggregation and retain long-term stability42.

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR is used to determine whether a molecule has functional groups. FT-IR study was performed on T-AuNPs and sodium thyroxine to determine which chemical group was incharge of AuNP production. The FT-IR spectra of sodium thyroxine (black colour) and T-AuNPs (red colour) is shown in the Fig. 5a. The FT-IR spectra of sodium thyroxine showed peaks at 797 cm− 1 for C-I, 1142 cm− 1 and 1314 cm− 1 for responsible for O-H, 1410.22 cm− 1 is corresponding to N-H group, 1593.5 cm− 1 is responsible for C = O, 3253 cm− 1 is corresponding to N-H stretching. The FT-IR spectra of T-AuNPs showed peaks at 1653.5 cm− 1 is corresponding to C = O and a broad peak at 3300 cm− 1 is corresponding to O-H group. The N-H (1410.22 cm− 1) group present in the sodium thyroxine spectra is disappeared in spectra of T-AuNPs, it serves as a reducing agent and is in charge of T-AuNP production. The presence of C = O in the spectra of T-AuNPs is responsible for negative zeta potential of T-AuNPs. Carbonyl group of biomolecules (sodium thyroxine) have strong binding affinity to metal. Therefore, these group acts as stabilizing agent and prevent the AuNPs aggregation43.

XRD analysis

The synthesized T-AuNPs crystalline nature was determined by means of X-ray diffraction assessment. The XRD pattern of T-AuNPs is shown in Fig. 5b exhibited four strong Bragg’s reflection at 2θ = 38.17, 44.37, 64.56, and 77.54 corresponding to (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of T-AuNPs. The peaks confirmed the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure of T-AuNPs. There were no impurity peaks visible in the T-AuNPs XRD pattern. The XRD peak at 2θ is good agreement with previously reported AuNPs XRD and JCPDS file number 89–369744. This result demonstrated that the Au3+ is reduced to form crystallized AuNPs with the addition of sodium thyroxine.

Hemolytic assay

When administered intravenously, nanoparticles enter the bloodstream right away and can harm red blood cells (RBCs) by membrane ruptures, releasing hemoglobin into the plasma, leading to hemolysis45. Therefore, prior to in vivo biological applications, it is crucial to evaluate the hemotoxicity of the synthesized NPs. The percentage of toxicity of the nanoparticles is directly correlated with the amount of hemoglobin in the medium. Using a hemolytic assay, the effect of the synthesized NPs on RBCs could be investigated46–48. Hemolytic assay is a simple, believable colorimetric assay, used to estimate the amount of hemoglobin released in the medium49. T-AuNPs’ hemocompatibility was examined using human red blood cells (RBCs). Human RBCs were treated with T-AuNPs at varying concentrations (100–500 µg/mL) for an hour at 37 °C. After the incubation, the percentage of hemolysis was calculated from the supernatant of the medium. The hemolysis (%) at different concentration of T-AuNPs is shown in the Fig. 6a. T-AuNPs had exhibited concentration dependent hemolysis where 0.94%, 1.27%, 1.84%, 2.61% and 3.48% of hemolysis was obtained for 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µg/mL concentrations of T-AuNPs respectively. According to the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) standard, hemolysis below 5% is highly hemocompatible, hemolysis between 10% and 20% is hemocompatible, and hemolysis above 20% is non-hemocompatible50. The synthesized T-AuNPs was induced 3.48% of hemolysis in human RBCs at the maximum tested concentration (500 µg/mL). Therefore, T-AuNPs is considered as highly hemocompatible up to 500 µg/mL concentrations. Further this result was confirmed through the morphological examination of RBCs by light microscope. The T-AuNPs treated RBCs are observed as uniform sized round shaped cells without aggregation and cell shrinkage (Fig. 6c). Abnormal RBCs morphology and aggregation is due to toxic of nanoparticles leads to harmful effect in animal system51 and Ramadurai et al., 2020 reported 22 µg/mL copper nanocluster induced 7% of hemolysis on human RBCs52. Previously, Foo et al., 2017 reported that the hemolytic effect of Curcuma mangga extract mediated synthesized AuNPs (CM-AuNPs), which induced 9.9% of hemolysis at maximum tested concentration 50 µg/mL53. Similarly, citrate capped AuNPs induced 2.9% of hemolysis at 50 µg/mL and CTAB capped AuNPs produced 44.7% and 96% of hemolysis at 25 µg/mL, and 50 µg/mL concentration respectively. Devendiran et al., 2014 reported hemolytic effect of Pisonia grandis leaves extract mediated synthesized AuNPs on human RBCs, which induced 2% of hemolysis at 250 µM concentration of AuNPs54. Further, the microscopic examination of CM-AuNPs and citrate capped AuNPs treated RBCs showed normal morphology without aggregation and CTAB capped AuNPs exhibited RBCs deformation. Whereas, T-AuNPs treated RBCs showed normal round shape morphology without any aggregation and shrinkage. CTAB is toxic to human RBCs, which lysis the RBC membrane and release the hemoglobin into the medium. The CTAB capped AuNPs induced drastic hemolysis on RBCs than the rest of AuNPs. While comparing these results, hemotoxicity of NPs depends on the biological properties of the reducing and stabilizing agent.

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) estimation

Hematological tests like ESR, which happen in three stages like rouleaux development, sedimentation, and RBC packing, are frequently employed. RBC rouleaux development is minimal under normal circumstances. The impact of T-AuNPs (500 µg/mL) in ESR estimation was recorded at every 15 min for total period of 1 h and the values were plotted as graph (Fig. 7). The ESR was found to be 1, 2, 3 and 5 mm/h for control blood sample and 1, 3, 5 and 7 mm/h for T-AuNPs treated blood sample for 15, 30, 45 and 60 min respectively. The average ESR value in the Westergren method is 15 mm/h. The blood sample treated with T-AuNPs had an ESR value that was within the normal range. While comparing T-AuNPs treated ESR values with untreated control, there was no significant differences observed. Therefore, the synthesized T-AuNPs is hemocompatible and did not affect normal ESR pattern. The hemocompatibility of cholic acid-capped AuNPs (DCaC-AuNPs, DCaDC-AuNPs, and DCaLC-AuNPs) in human red blood cells was previously documented by Muthukumarasamyvel et al. in 2017. A human blood sample treated with AuNPs elicited ESRs of 8, 10, and 12 mm/h in that order at a concentration of 120 µg/mL55.

In vitro biocompatibility analysis

Cell viability analysis

In recent years, the use of gold nanoparticles in biological and biomedical applications, including drug delivery, therapies, and nanodiagnosis, cell labeling, enhanced magnetic resonance imaging and gene delivery has elevated56. Most of the NPs were synthesized from chemical methods using toxic or hazardous chemicals, is unsuitable for the biological applications57. The in vitro cytotoxicity analysis of NPs is very essential to determine the ideal starting concentration of NPs for in vivo experiments58. The standard MTT assay was used to assess the impact of T-AuNPs on the cell viability of the normal Vero and A549 lung cancer cell lines. The cells were let to interact with varying T-AuNP concentrations (100 to 500 µg/mL) for 24 h was shown in the Fig. 8. In Vero cells, the cell viability was found to be 95.14%, 91.45%, 88.81%, 85.23% and 74.46% for 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µg/mL concentrations of T-AuNPs respectively. Similarly, in A549 cells 96.76%, 91.2%, 89.7%, 86.65% and 76.34% cell viability was obtained for 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 µg/mL concentrations of T-AuNPs respectively. In the respective order, the concentrations of T-AuNPs upto 400 µg/mL did not significantly decreased the cell viability and retained more than 85% of viability in both cell lines. Furthermore, increasing the concentration of T-AuNPs to 500 µg/mL, the cell viability had dramatically decreased to 74.46% and 76.34% in Vero and A549 cells respectively. Generally, the nanomaterial that retain more than 80% cell viability are considered safe for biological applications59. Hence, the synthesized T-AuNPs is considered as biocompatible upto 400 µg/mL concentration and can be used for biological application. Further, the reducing agent Thyroxine is a naturally secreted hormone by the thyroid gland60, is non-toxic and biocompatible. Commercially available as pentahydrate sodium salt, thyroxine is used to treat hypothyroidism61. The LD50 concentration of sodium thyroxine is 11.2 mg/kg (Repeated dosage toxicity, 4 weeks, Rat, Oral). The cytotoxicity of polyoxometalate-stabilized AuNPs in Vero and B16 cell lines was documented by Gabas et al. in 2016 from 0.02 to 0.1 mg/mL concentration. After 24 h incubation, 90% and 72% of cell viability was obtained for Vero and B16 cell line respectively at 0.1 mg/mL concentration62. Vijayakumar and Ganesan 2012 reported that the cytotoxic effect of citrate capped AuNPs in MCF-7 and PC-3 cells have more cytotoxicity at 140 µg/mL concentration than the starch and gum arabic stabilized AuNPs63. Consequently, the synthesis of AuNPs for biological applications requires a non-toxic reducing/stabilizing agent.

Apoptosis analysis by fluorescence staining

Apoptosis related alterations in the cell membrane can be found using the Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide fluorescence Stain64. T-AuNPs were applied to the Vero and A549 cells for a duration of 24 h. After the incubation, both cell lines were stained with AO/EtBr and evaluated on a fluorescence microscope. Acridine orange (AO) stains both living and dead cells, but ethidium bromide (EtBr) bonds with the DNA of cells that lose their membrane integrity65. Normal-shaped, green luminous nuclei are a sign of living cells, while orange chromatin and condensed or fragmented nuclei are indicative of early and late apoptotic cells, respectively. Bright orange chromatin surrounds round nuclei in necrotic cells20. Both Vero and A549 cells treated with T-AuNPs showed green fluorescent nuclei and normal cell shape, with no apparent signs of necrotic or apoptotic cells (Fig. 11a and b).

DNA fragmentation

The occurrence of apoptosis was determined through the cells shrinkage and DNA fragmentation or shearing66. The agarose gel electrophoresis DNA fragmentation assay was used to clarify the biocompatibility of T-AuNPs. Both Vero and A549 cells had their genomic DNA extracted after being exposed to T-AuNPs for 24 h. The isolated DNA was run in the agarose gel electrophoresis along with 1 kb DNA ladder and control DNA. The fluorogram image shows the absence of DNA shearing, fragmentation and the intact DNA was present on the top of the gel (L2, and L3) (Fig. 12). The obtained result was compared with untreated control DNA (L1) and DNA ladder (1 kb). The molecular weight of the intact DNA had more than 10 kb size. The synthesized T-AuNPs did not induce DNA fragmentation and is genome compatibility upto 400 µg/mL concentration.

In-vivo biocompatibility analysis in hydra

Analysis of acute toxicity of T-AuNPs in hydra

The in vivo toxicity of T-AuNPs was analyzed using Hydra magnipapillata. Hydra, the simple organism acts as an efficient for in vivo test system. In the aquatic invertebrate, Hydra is recently emerging as an attractive model system for screening of NPs bioactivity and toxicity67,68. Acute toxicity analysis of T-AuNPs in Hydra provides the effect of T-AuNPs at whole body level. In the present study the impact of T-AuNPs on Hydra magnipapillata is explained in dose and time dependent manner. Following 96 h of incubation, the polyps were subjected to varying concentrations of T-AuNPs (50 µg to 900 µg/mL). The morphology of Hydra (control and treated) was observed at different time point (24, 48, 72, 96 h) and the median score was assigned based on the morphology using22(Fig. 13b; Table 2). Characteristics including an inverted cone-shaped body, whitening, paralysis, and complete polyp disintegration are considered abnormal68. In microscopic examination, the unusual morphology of Hydra was not observed and the Hydra were live and exhibited clear pattern of normal morphology with cylindrical body, and well projected tentacles upto 600 µg/ml of T-AuNPs treatment for 96 h (Fig. 13a). While treated with 900 µg/ml of T-AuNPs, Hydra exhibited extended tentacles and body reactive at 24 h (score 10) and altered the morphology (clubbed tentacles, contracted body) at 48h. Hydra displayed a shortened body and tentacles at 72 and 96 h (scoring 6), and they were completely contracted with no visible tentacles (score 4) (Fig. 13a) resulting in lethal. The synthesized T-AuNPs is biocompatible and did not induce acute toxicity in Hydra upto 600 µg/ml concentrations; further concentration of 900 µg/ml of T-AuNPs induced toxicity in Hydra at 96 h exposure.

The toxicity analysis of T-AuNPs in Hydra provides several advantages over the prokaryotes and cell models. In the aquatic environment, Hydra consumed the T-AuNPs through several modes such as direct ingestion, ingestion through the contaminated prey, inhalation, and water filtration69. The aquatic organism Hydra can be cultured easily and maintained in the laboratory. Additionally, it can readily come into contact with the test sample in the medium via the gastrodermis and ectodermis. Gonçalves et al., 2016 reported toxicity assessment using activated carbon pyrolysed sugarcane bagasse modified silver nanoparticles (ACPB-AgNP) at 0.5 to 8.0 mg/L concentration in Hydra attenutta. ACPB-AgNP showed acute toxicity in Hydra attenutta and caused morphological changes such as shortening and clubbing of tentacles, body contraction. These changes occurred very shortly after exposure of high concentration of ACPB-AgNP but later happened in low concentration also. The LD50 values of ACPB-AgNP was 1.94 mg/L (96 h)70.

Feeding assay

Feeding assay was performed to determine impact of T-AuNPs in feeding behavior of Hydra, specifically the tentacles movement. Feeding behavior is a multi-step process including capturing the prey, killing, opening the mouth and the insertion of prey (Artemia nauplii) through the mouth71. T-AuNPs treated Hydra readily captured and killed their preys by tentacle. Feeding assay was performed to study whether the T-AuNPs provoke structural abnormality of tentacles that may affect the feeding behaviour. Hydra was exposed to T-AuNPs (600 µg/ml) for 24 h, which was tested with catching the prey Artemia nauplii. The microscopic examination of T-AuNPs treated animals showed that the Hydra readily captured the prey using its tentacle and the prey was killed immediately (Fig. 14b). Thereafter, the tentacles were found to bent towards its mouth part and they ingested the prey through the mouth opening (Fig. 14c). While comparing the feeding behavior of T-AuNPs treated animals with untreated animals, there was no significant change in capturing prey, killing and ingestion process in both conditions. T-AuNPs (600 µg/mL) did not cause any adverse effect to feeding behavior of Hydra. Previously, Murugadas et al., 2016 explained the impact of metal copper oxide nanorod (2.5 µM) in Hydra magnipapillata. Copper oxide nanorod affected the feeding behavior of Hydra. Copper oxide nanorod treated Hydra were unable to catch their prey Artemia naupli, which cause adverse effect in the battery cell complex of the tentacles resulting failure of tentacle writhing and inability to catch the prey14.

Regeneration of hydra

In the year of 1740 s, the Swiss scientist Abraham Trembley discovered that the fresh water Hydra has the wonderful regeneration capacity. The fresh water polyps have capacity to regenerate theirs head and feet to form new individuals after cut into little pieces. Therefore, the hydra is used as an advantages model organism for regeneration studies in Lenhoff, H.M. and Lenhoff, S.G., 1988. The hydra regenerates their missing body parts through the morphallactic regeneration. After amputating the polyp, the tentacle will be regenerated within 36 h and head will be formed within 72 h72. However, environmental stress, failure of differentiation, proliferation, and migration of various cell types can impact this dynamic nature. The adult animal was cut off just below the head and just above the upper gastric region in order to evaluate the impact of T-AuNPs on the possibility for polyp regeneration. For 72 hours, the stomach area was left to rebuild the missing portion while T-AuNPs (600 µg/ml) were present. Figure 15 shows that the regenerated morphology of Hydra from T-AuNPs treated and untreated control. About 50% of regeneration was observed in the animal treated for 48 h and complete regeneration was observed at 72 h with well-defined normal structures like cylindrical body, mouth, the tentacles and the basal disk. After 72 h, 100% survival of Hydra was noticed in the T-AuNPs treated sample. The T-AuNPs didn’t affect the normal regeneration of Hydra and the obtained result confirms the eco-friendly and non-toxic nature of synthesized T-AuNPs. The results showed that the synthesized T-AuNPs are biocompatible, didn’t cause toxic effect at high concentration (600 µg/mL) and even at long exposure. Therefore, T-AuNPs could be considered as eco-friendly nanomaterial. The epithelial cell of Hydra is responsible for regeneration of Hydra. The healthy epithelial cells continuously divide, and migrate from one end to another end of the body and contribute formation of new individual by bud formation from the gastric region and detaches from its mother in within 3 days. Ambrosone et al., 2012 explained the toxicity mechanism of colloidal quantum dot in Hydra vulgaris. When, polyps were incubated with 25 nM quantum dots for hydra regeneration study, 35% mortality was observed and improper regeneration of hydra was observed (dosage dependent inhibition of regeneration)25.

Apoptosis analysis

The impact of T-AuNPs on the apoptosis of Hydra was analyzed through Acridine orange (AO) staining. The fluorescent dye AO highlights the apoptotic cells and emits green fluorescent as punctuated marks. The T-AuNPs treated Hydra were stained with AO and they showed uniform light green colour without fluorescent green punctae and appeared similar to untreated control animal. This study showed that T-AuNPs did not induce significant cell death and is non-toxic to Hydra (Fig. 16). Most of the metal NPs produced oxidative stress, is responsible for the cell death73. The excessive oxidative stress induces ROS production like hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radical, which ultimately leads to DNA damage by strand breakage, fragmentation of deoxy sugar and modification of DNA bases and the DNA damage finally cause apoptosis74. T-AuNPs treated Hydra was stained with AO and showed no sign of green punctae of apoptosis similar to that of untreated control. Zeeshan et al., 2017 explained the Eco toxicological assessment of cobalt in Hydra magnipapillata. Cobalt induced ROS mediated apoptosis in Hydra at 8 mg/L and 16 mg/L concentration. The apoptotic effect of cobalt in Hydra was estimated using ROS estimation, DNA fragmentation analysis, Acridine orange fluorescence staining75.

Conclusion

The AuNPs have been successfully synthesized in the bright sunlight using sodium thyroxine (0.5mM) as a reducing and stabilizing agent. The synthesis process is facile, cost-effective, and eco-friendly, also no need to add an additional stabilizing agent. The electron microscopic analysis of T-AuNPs has exhibited monodispersed, globular shaped with a size of < 15 nm. The in vitro biocompatibility of synthesized T-AuNPs is analyzed by various experiments using human RBCs, Vero cell line, and A549 cell line. The T-AuNPs have fine hemocompatibility nature, induced 3.48% hemolysis at 500 µg/mL concentration. The biocompatibility of the T-AuNPs is analyzed by MTT assay using Vero and A549 cell line; about > 85% of the cell viability is observed in both the cell lines up to 400 µg/mL concentrations.

The in vivo biocompatibility of synthesized T-AuNPs is analyzed by using Hydra magnipapillata. The Hydras have exhibited normal morphology up to 600 µg/mL of T-AuNPs at 96 h incubation, further concentration of 900 µg/mL T-AuNPs induced toxicity in Hydra. The biocompatibility of T-AuNPs in Hydra is confirmed by various analysis such as feeding assay, Hydra regeneration, and apoptosis analysis. Overall, the simple single stepped synthesized T-AuNPs possess good hemocompatibility, and biocompatibility property, hence it can be safely utilized in biological and biomedical applications such as diagnostics, Photothermal therapy, targeted drug delivery, recombinant gene delivery, bioimaging, and so on. Particularly, T-AuNPs can be used for targeted drug delivery by encapsulating the therapeutic medications like aptamers, proteins, or antibodies to the tumor site. In addition, T-AuNPs may be reduced the quantity of drugs and its adverse effect and increasing the permeability. T-AuNPs could offer a possible remedy to address bacterial resistance to antibiotics by transport supplementary antibacterial agents, which, when used in conjunction. The pathogenic microbes, protein and enzyme markers can be diagnosed by colorimetric method, rapid test kit using surface modified T-AuNPs (conjugation of respective monoclonal antibodies). The major barriers in these applications will be the surface modification of NPs with suitable conjugates, determine its optimum concentration and maintain the stability of nano-conjugate hybrid.
