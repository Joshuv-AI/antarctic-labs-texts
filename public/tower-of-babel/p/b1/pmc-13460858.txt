The effect of beetroot juice (BRJ) supplementation on exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD): a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Mohammad Beba, Elaheh Dehghani, Mohammad Gholizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to quantify the effect of beetroot juice (BRJ) supplementation on exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD), inflammatory biomarkers, muscle function, and muscle soreness.Methods:The Robvis tool was used for assessing the risk of bias. The mean differences (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of creatine kinase (CK) levels (IU/L), C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin (IL)-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (pg/ml), maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC), counter movement jump (CMJ), and pressure pain threshold (PPT) (% change from baseline) were pooled using either a fixed- or random-effects model. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the χ2 or I2 statistic.Results:Seven trials met the eligibility criteria and were included in the pooled analysis. Meta-analysis indicated that BRJ supplementation significantly reduced serum CK levels [weighted mean difference (WMD) = −98.67 IU/L, 95% CI (−122.14 to −75.21), I2 = 74.2%]. Significant improvement was also observed in MVIC [WMD = 8.93%, 95% CI (7.59–10.28), I2 = 81.2%], CMJ [WMD = 4.72%, 95% CI (4.14–5.30), I2 = 46.3%], and PPT [WMD = 7.22%, 95% CI (5.30–10.93), I2 = 49.4%] from baseline in the intervention group compared to the placebo group. However, no significant change in CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α levels was found. Dose-response analysis revealed that there is a significant non-linear association between the daily dose and antioxidant content (Trolox) of BRJ and the final effect size regarding CK.Conclusion:BRJ supplementation may improve some aspects of EIMD, including muscle damage, muscle function, and recovery.

Introduction

One of the biggest public health issues of the 21st century is the epidemic of physical inactivity[1–3]. Participation in sports events is one way to encourage an active lifestyle, which has many documented advantages[4–8]. However, sports injuries are considered an unfavorable part of this process and may hinder participation in physical activities[9]. In a broader perspective, sports-related injuries may also affect society due to their high economic and health-related costs[10,11] and, therefore, should be avoided as much as possible[12]. Exercise is a strong and complex physiological stress factor since it causes an immediate increase in muscular damage and inflammation, reduces muscular function, and leads to prolonged periods of muscle weakness and soreness after exercise[13–17]. Strategies in this regard include the use of compression garments, cold water immersion, platelet-rich therapies, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and dietary supplements with anti-inflammatory or antioxidant properties, such as vitamin C, vitamin E, curcumin, and (poly)phenols[18–22]. Nowadays, due to the common side effects of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, their use is decreasing, and the use of dietary supplements containing antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents to treat complications related to sports injuries is increasing instead[23–28]. Although vitamin C and vitamin E, which are nutritional antioxidants, have mainly failed to reduce muscle damage, there is emerging support for the use of antioxidant-rich fruit and vegetable beverages as recovery aids[29–32]. Ingestion of dietary supplements containing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agents, such as tart cherries and pomegranates, which are high in phenolic compounds, is a new strategy to reduce inflammation and accelerate the recovery process[23,33–35]. In recent years, many studies have investigated the effect of beetroot juice (BRJ) supplementation on athletic performance. The nitrate anion is possibly the most researched compound and has emerged as a chemical that may have beneficial physiological effects[36,37]. It is now well known that, under specific circumstances, dietary nitrate can be converted first to nitrite and subsequently to nitric oxide (NO), a chemical with regulatory effects on the vascular, metabolic, and, more recently, the immune system[38]. The most popular way to provide nitrate is through BRJ consumption, which also contains several bioactive substances with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties to accelerate muscle recovery and combat muscle damage[14,39–41].

A systematic review and meta-analysis of human interventional trials by Jones et al[42] in 2021 revealed that BRJ supplementation may attenuate some markers of exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD). However, this study carries some drawbacks, including its failure to include one eligible study and its lack of evaluation of the dose-response relationship between BRJ supplementation and various markers of sports injuries. Moreover, the previous meta-analysis performed subgroup analysis only based on participants’ age and gender, type of intervention, and type of exercise, while, in the current study, subgroup analysis was performed based on country, study design, gender, duration of intervention, type of exercise, and daily dose. Therefore, the present meta-analysis sought to assess the effect of BRJ supplementation on different aspects of EIMD in a dose-response manner.

Materials and methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement, an evidence-based protocol specifying a list of items for reporting in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, was followed in the conduct and reporting of the present study[44]. This manuscript has been registered with PROSPERO under ID CRD42023413561.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus up to October 2025 to find the eligible studies by two authors (M.B. and E.D.). The following combination is an example of search terms used in a PubMed search: [((((((((“Beetroot”[tiab]) OR (“Red beet”[tiab])) OR (“Beet*”[tiab])) OR (“B. vulgaris”[tiab])) OR (“Beta vulgaris”[tiab])) OR (“Nitrate”[tiab])) OR (“Nitrite”[tiab])) OR (“Dietary nitrate”[tiab])) AND (((((((“Sport injury”[tiab]) OR (“Muscle soreness”[tiab])) OR (“Muscle pain”[tiab])) OR (“Muscle damage”[tiab])) OR (“Athletic injury”[tiab])) OR (“Sports injury”[tiab])) OR (“Delayed onset muscle soreness”[tiab]))]. A detailed search strategy in all searched databases is presented in Supplemental Digital Content Tables 1 and 3, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B273. Language and publishing time restrictions were not applied to prevent missing related publications. The reference list of the pertinent publications was also examined. All studies were transferred to EndNote (version X9.3.3) in order to manage and facilitate the review process.

Study selection

Studies were selected by applying the following Population-Intervention-Comparator-Outcome-Study design, and they were included if they met the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled clinical trials, (2) studies on adults (≥18 years), (3) original studies examining a short- or long-term BRJ supplementation intervention, and (4) controlled studies that provided the required data to calculate the effect sizes, including the means and standard deviations (SDs) of creatine kinase (CK), C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC), counter movement jump (CMJ), and pressure pain threshold (PPT) for both intervention and control groups. Studies were excluded if they were: (1) trials that were not robust enough (e.g., trials without a control group or involving unblinded participants), (2) ecological studies, cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses, (3) studies that used BRJ in combination with other nutrients or dietary supplements, or (4) studies using nitrate salt-only interventions. These were excluded because the whole BRJ provides a complex matrix of bioactive compounds (e.g., betalains, polyphenols, and antioxidants) in addition to nitrate. Additionally, nitrate salts may not reflect the physiological and metabolic context of beetroot-derived nitrate and may differ in bioavailability, absorption kinetics, and vascular effects. This distinction was made to enhance the homogeneity and translational relevance of the included interventions. After the primary search, all recorded articles found from the electronic search were entered into EndNote software (version X9.3.3) for screening. Titles and abstracts of studies were independently evaluated by two reviewers (M.B. and E.D.) and selected according to the inclusion criteria. Studies that met the eligibility criteria during the title and abstract screening were subsequently assessed in full text.

Data extraction

Two independent investigators (M.B. and E.D.) performed data extraction from each eligible RCT. The following information was extracted: name of the first author, publication year, individuals’ characteristics (mean age, height, weight, BMI, and gender), study design, sample size (control and intervention groups), type of exercise, the dosage of BRJ (volume/day), Trolox content (mmol/L), gallic acid content (mg/day), nitrate content (mg/day), duration of intervention (days), mean changes, and their corresponding SDs of CK, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, MVIC, CMJ, and PPT throughout the trials for the intervention and control groups.

Change scores (mean and SD) were extracted from included studies when reported. For studies that did not report change scores, the mean change was calculated as the difference between final and baseline means (Mean_change = Mean_final − Mean_baseline). The SD of the change was estimated using the following formula:

SD_change = √(SD_baseline2 + SD_final2 − 2 × r × SD_baseline × SD_final)

where (r) is the correlation coefficient between baseline and final measurements. In cases where (r) was not available from the study, a plausible value of 0.7 was assumed according to the Cochrane Handbook[45].

When outcomes were presented only in graphical form, two authors independently extracted the data using WebPlotDigitizer after calibrating the axes.

Data analysis

The pooled effect size was expressed as the weighted mean difference (WMD) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for each parameter in the present meta-analysis. The heterogeneity of the included studies was examined using χ2 tests, and the degree of heterogeneity was estimated using the I2 statistic. A fixed-effect model (when I2 was below 50%) or a random-effects model (when I2 was above 50%) was used for the meta-analysis. Subgroup analysis was performed based on country, study design, gender, daily dose, duration of intervention, and type of exercise to discern the potential sources of heterogeneity. Studies conducted in the UK used a specific type of BRJ, while non-UK countries used different types of BRJ, and since the type of BRJ affects its antioxidant, gallic acid, and nitrate content, we decided to consider country (UK vs. non-UK) as a potential source of heterogeneity. Potential publication bias was explored using Egger’s linear regression test (Egger’s test). All analyses were performed using STATA software (version 12.0), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In articles that reported data in graphical figures, data extraction was performed using a WebPlotDigitizer. Sensitivity and dose-response analyses were performed using “metaninf” and “fracpoly” commands in STATA software (version 12.0).

Results

Description of studies

The initial search identified 242 articles, of which 186 were eliminated due to duplication. Another 45 papers were removed based on their irrelevant titles and abstracts. After a full-text review of 11 potentially relevant articles, 4 papers were further excluded because they did not assess our desired outcomes. Finally, seven publications with an RCT design were chosen for the systematic review and meta-analysis[14,25,39,40,46–48]. The flow diagram of the selection process is presented in Figure 1. These RCTs were published between 2015 and 2020 and originated from the UK[14,25,39,40], Tunisia[46], Indonesia[48], and Denmark[47]. Risk of bias

Data were entered into an Excel sheet for risk of bias assessment, and visualizations were generated using the Robvis tool, and the final result is presented in Figure 2A. Most studies showed a low risk of bias across domains related to deviations from intended interventions (D2), missing outcome data (D3), and measurement of the outcome (D4). However, several studies (Clifford et al., 2015; Clifford (A) et al., 2016; Sinaga et al., 2020) demonstrated a high risk of bias in the randomization process (D1), which also affected their overall judgment. Other studies showed some concerns, mainly in the randomization (D1) and selection of the reported result (D5) domains. Overall, while the majority of included studies were rated as having low risk or some concerns, a few exhibited high risk in specific methodological aspects. Meta-analysis

Effect of BRJ supplementation on muscle damage

The effect of BRJ supplementation on CK activity is shown in Figure 3. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation significantly reduced CK activity [WMD = −98.67 IU/L, 95% CI (−122.14 to −75.21), I2 = 74.2%] compared to the placebo group (P < 0.001). Considerable heterogeneity was detected among studies assessing serum CK levels (I2 = 74.2%, τ2 = 412.0). The 95% prediction interval ranged from −144.7 to −52.6, suggesting that future studies are expected to report effects consistently favoring BRJ, albeit with varying magnitudes. When the Hartung–Knapp adjustment was applied, the CI widened slightly, but the direction and significance of the effect remained unchanged. Due to the high level of heterogeneity (I2 = 74.2%), subgroup analysis was performed based on country, study design, gender, duration of intervention, type of exercise, and daily dose to discern the potential sources of heterogeneity, as shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table 4, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B273. The sensitivity analyses showed that although statistical significance fluctuated with the omission of individual studies, the overall effect consistently indicated a reduction in CK levels, confirming the stability of the direction of improvement. Effect of BRJ supplementation on inflammatory cytokines

CRP

The effect of BRJ supplementation on CRP is indicated in Supplemental Digital Content Figure 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation was not able to significantly reduce CRP levels [WMD = 4.44 mg/L, 95% CI (−16.48 to 25.36), I2 = 0%] compared to the placebo group (P = 0.67). For this outcome, between-study heterogeneity was minimal (I2 = 0.0%, τ2 = 0.00). The 95% prediction interval ranged from −16.5 to 25.3, suggesting that future studies are likely to yield similar findings. Applying the Hartung–Knapp adjustment did not change the results.

Interleukin-6

The effect of BRJ supplementation on IL-6 is depicted in Supplemental Digital Content Figure 2, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation did not significantly reduce IL-6 levels [WMD = −0.05 pg/ml, 95% CI (−0.14 to 0.05), I2 = 68.3%] compared to the placebo group (P = 0.32). Moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed among the studies investigating IL-6 levels (I2 = 68.3%, τ2 = 0.0051). The 95% prediction interval ranged from −0.22 to 0.12, implying that future studies may yield variable effects, both positive and negative. Application of the Hartung–Knapp adjustment resulted in a wider but still non-significant CI (−0.26 to 0.16), supporting the robustness of the overall null finding.

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha

The effect of BRJ supplementation on TNF-α is depicted in Supplemental Digital Content Figure 3, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation was not able to significantly reduce TNF-α levels [WMD = 0.02 pg/ml, 95% CI (−0.21 to 0.25), I2 = 19.6%] compared to the placebo group (P = 0.86). Heterogeneity among studies examining TNF-α was low (I2 = 19.6%; τ2 ≈ 0.0025). The 95% prediction interval (−0.20 to 0.24) indicates that future studies could observe effects in either direction, but of small magnitude. Applying an approximate Hartung–Knapp correction widened the CI (≈ −0.31 to 0.35), reinforcing the conclusion that there is no robust evidence for a consistent effect on TNF-α.

Effect of BRJ supplementation on muscle strength

Maximal voluntary isometric contraction

The effect of BRJ supplementation on MVIC is shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figure 4, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation significantly improved MVIC [WMD = 8.93% from baseline, 95% CI (7.59–10.28), I2 = 81.2%] compared to the placebo group (P < 0.001). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies assessing MVIC (I2 = 81.2%, τ2 = 2.03). The 95% prediction interval (5.81–12.05) suggests that although individual study results may vary, the direction of the effect is consistently positive. After applying the Hartung–Knapp adjustment, the CI slightly widened (7.17–10.69), but the overall effect remained significant, confirming the robustness of the finding. Due to a high level of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was performed based on country, study design, gender, duration of intervention, type of exercise, and dose to discern the potential sources of heterogeneity, which is shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table 5, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B273. The direction of the effect remained consistently positive across all sensitivity analyses, demonstrating stable improvements in MVIC despite minor variations in significance.

Counter movement jump

The effect of BRJ supplementation on CMJ is indicated in Supplemental Digital Content Figure 5, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation significantly improved CMJ [WMD = 4.72% from baseline, 95% CI (4.14–5.30), I2 = 46.3%] compared to the placebo group (P < 0.001). Moderate heterogeneity was identified among the studies assessing CMJ performance (I2 = 46.3%, τ2 = 0.076). The 95% prediction interval (3.94–5.50) indicates that future studies are expected to show consistently positive effects, though with some variability in magnitude. The Hartung–Knapp adjustment produced a comparable CI (3.96–5.48), further supporting the robustness and consistency of the observed improvement. The results of sensitivity analyses confirmed that the overall effect direction remained positive, indicating improved CMJ performance even when individual studies were removed.

Pressure pain threshold

The effect of BRJ supplementation on PPT is depicted in Supplemental Digital Content Figure 6, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272. The pooled analysis showed that BRJ supplementation significantly increased PPT [WMD = 7.22% from baseline, 95% CI (5.30–10.93), I2 = 49.4%] compared to the placebo group (P < 0.001). Moderate heterogeneity was detected among studies evaluating the effect of BRJ supplementation on PPT (I2 = 49.4%, τ2 = 3.50). The 95% prediction interval (1.96–12.48) suggests that future research is likely to observe a positive effect, albeit with variation in magnitude. When applying the Hartung–Knapp correction, the CI widened (1.19–13.25), yet the direction and significance of the pooled effect remained consistent, indicating robustness of the result. Due to the marginal level of heterogeneity, we decided to perform subgroup analysis based on the type of exercise and dose to discern the potential sources of heterogeneity, which is shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table 6, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B273. The effect-direction remained stable and consistently positive throughout sensitivity analyses, suggesting improvement in PPT regardless of individual study omission. A brief sensitivity analysis summarizing how effect sizes change under different models (random-effects model vs. Hartung–Knapp) is presented in Supplemental Digital Content Table 7, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B273.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Results of the sensitivity analysis showed that, although the statistical significance of some outcomes (CK, MVIC, CMJ, and PPT) changed when individual studies were omitted, the direction of the overall effect remained consistent, and the change in effect size did not exceed 20%. Therefore, no single study had a substantial influence on the overall findings. Funnel plots of CK, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, MVIC, CMJ, and PPT are shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figures 7-13, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272, respectively.

Egger’s test showed no evidence of publication bias (P = 0.74), (P = 0.34), (P = 0.38), and (P = 0.66) for CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, and CMJ, respectively. However, significant publication bias was observed (P = 0.012), (P = 0.03), and (P < 0.001) for CK, MVIC, and PPT, respectively. Although Egger’s test suggested possible publication bias for CK, MVIC, and PPT, these results should be interpreted with caution because the number of included studies for each outcome was fewer than 10. Under such conditions, both Egger’s test and visual inspection of funnel plots have limited power and may yield spurious results. Therefore, the apparent asymmetry may be attributable to small-study effects rather than true publication bias.

Dose-response analysis

We tested for a non-linear association between BRJ supplementation and CK, MVIC, CMJ, and PPT to discern the probable association between the daily dose of BRJ supplementation and the final effect size. Results of the dose-response analysis revealed that BRJ supplementation failed to increase MVIC, CMJ, and PPT (P > 0.05) but did reduce CK levels (dose 1: P = 0.008 and dose 2: P = 0.006) in a non-linear fashion. According to the present study, an intake of approximately 500 ml/day of BRJ may be associated with greater reductions in CK levels. Since different BRJs have different contents of antioxidants, bioactive compounds, and nitrate, we decided to test further for a non-linear association between Trolox (mmol/L/day), gallic acid (mg/day), and nitrate (mg/day) to find out which component(s) is/are exactly responsible for this association. Results of the dose-response analysis on the gallic acid and nitrate contents of BRJ revealed that these two components failed to reduce CK levels (P > 0.05). However, the Trolox content of BRJ did reduce CK levels (dose 1: P = 0.012 and dose 2: P = 0.009) in a non-linear fashion. Based on our results, an intake of approximately 4 mmol/day of Trolox may be associated with greater reductions in CK levels; however, these findings are preliminary and should be interpreted cautiously. The results of the dose-response analyses are shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figures 14 and 15, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B272.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we summarized published evidence from seven randomized double-blind clinical trials that investigated the effect of BRJ supplementation on of muscle damage, inflammation, muscle function, and muscle soreness. The present study revealed that BRJ supplementation elicited a significant decrease in CK levels (−98.67 IU/L) and a significant increase in MVIC (8.93%), CMJ (4.72%), and PPT (7.22%) from baseline compared to the placebo group. Additionally, no significant changes in CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α levels were observed following BRJ supplementation. Pooled analyses for CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α showed no significant effects; accordingly, we refrain from inferring anti-inflammatory benefits based on the current evidence. We found significant heterogeneity pertaining to CK (I2 = 74.2%) and MVIC (I2 = 81.2%). A marginal level of heterogeneity was also observed in the case of PPT (I2 = 49.4%). Heterogeneity for CK and MVIC was substantial, consistent with diversity in BRJ formulations and exercise protocols; we therefore interpret these pooled estimates with appropriate caution. Regarding CK and MVIC: country, study design, gender, duration of intervention, type of exercise, and daily dose; and in the case of PPT: type of exercise and daily dose, were each identified as potential sources of heterogeneity. It seems that country, type of exercise, and daily dose are the most significant sources of heterogeneity in the present study. The country was considered a potential source of heterogeneity because our included studies were conducted across different countries that vary in dietary patterns, training environments, supplement formulations, and methodological practices. Such contextual differences may influence physiological responses to supplementation and contribute to between-study heterogeneity. Therefore, examining country as a potential moderator allowed us to explore whether broader contextual or methodological factors might explain part of the observed variability across trials.

In our meta-analysis, the pooled effect indicated a 7.22% improvement in PPT from baseline following BRJ supplementation, which is often interpreted as a favorable neuromuscular or analgesic adaptation. Increases of approximately 15–33% in PPT are generally considered clinically meaningful in musculoskeletal and exercise-related pain contexts[49]. In studies similar to ours, PPT increases of ≈ 10–25% were interpreted as functionally relevant[14,40]. Therefore, although the 7.22% pooled increase in PPT reached statistical significance in our study, it is still below the commonly recognized threshold for clinically meaningful improvement. Accordingly, we emphasize that the observed change should be interpreted as statistically detectable but still functionally modest.

Although funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were used to explore potential publication bias, it should be emphasized that these methods are inherently unreliable when fewer than ten studies are included. Therefore, any observed funnel plot asymmetry in the present analysis is likely to be artifactual and driven by random variation, between-study heterogeneity, or limited statistical power rather than true small-study effects or publication bias. As such, these findings should not be interpreted as strong evidence of bias, and conclusions should be drawn with caution.

Dose and duration of intervention are the most important factors that can affect the final result in clinical trials, and in the case of BRJ supplementation, it seems that dose is more important than the duration of intervention. The proof of this claim is that studies with higher doses[14,48] had a greater impact on the final effect size than studies with a longer duration of intervention[46].

Our included studies employed a variety of daily doses, ranging from 140 to 750 ml/day of BRJ. However, after dose-response analysis for MVIC, CMJ, and PPT, no significant difference was found between the low dose (140 ml/day) and the high dose (750 ml/day) of BRJ. For CK, however, the result of the dose-response analysis revealed that a BRJ intake of approximately 500 ml/day might be associated with greater effects. Results of another dose-response analysis indicated that there was no significant difference between lower and higher contents of gallic acid and nitrate. In the case of Trolox content, however, the result of the dose-response analysis revealed that an intake of approximately ~4 mmol/L of antioxidant may be associated with greater effects. These findings affirm our earlier claim that the daily dose and antioxidant content of BRJ are more important than the duration of intervention. However, such findings should not be interpreted as the optimal dose, given that this estimate is largely driven by a single high-dose study and is accompanied by wide confidence bands. Moreover, the overall evidence base remains limited, comprising only seven small randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with heterogeneous protocols. As such, the dose–response findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger, well-designed trials employing multiple dose levels.

Unfortunately, no previous study has investigated the safety and maximum dose of BRJ supplementation; however, results from previous studies indicated that 750 ml/day (a 250 ml bottle of BRJ taken three times a day) was completely safe and did not cause any significant side effects[14,42].

CK, or creatine phosphokinase, is an enzyme whose appearance in the blood has generally been considered an indirect marker of muscle damage. However, its validity in reflecting muscle damage as a consequence of the level and intensity of physical activity is controversial. Nonetheless, reducing CK levels is essential for the muscle recovery process to begin [50]. An RCT-designed study indicated that administering BRJ after physical exercise can alleviate the rise in CK levels. The proposed mechanism of action to reduce EIMD is through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties[48]. In this regard, it is well known that BRJ contains a variety of phytochemical substances, such as ascorbic acid, phenolic compounds, carotenoids, and betaine, as well as betalains, which are bioactive pigments that function as antioxidant agents[51,52]. Betaine also acts as an anti-inflammatory agent by inhibiting protein kinase B (Akt) and nuclear factor k-B (NFk-B) activation[53,54]. In addition, according to previous investigations, betalains exhibit pharmacological properties by acting as antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents[51]. Betalains are also effective at scavenging free radicals and oxidant compounds[55]. According to the results of previous research, betalains prevented carrageenan-induced lipid peroxidation, superoxide anion production, and elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-1[56].

Nonetheless, the potential protective effects of BRJ supplementation on muscle damage may be due to multiple interacting mechanisms of nitrate, betalains, and other antioxidant agents[42]. Nitrates increase NO bioavailability, leading to improved blood flow, oxygen delivery, and removal of metabolic waste[42]. Betalains act as potent antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents, scavenging reactive oxygen species and reducing cytokine-mediated inflammation[57]. Together, they may help preserve muscle fiber integrity, reduce oxidative damage, and enhance recovery[58]. Human trials support these mechanisms, showing improved strength recovery, reduced subjective muscle soreness, and lower or quicker normalization of markers such as CK or LDH when supplementation includes both nitrate and non-nitrate bioactives, or when betalain-rich extracts are used[40].

Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses [42] inherited some limitations that we tried to overcome. These limitations included: (A) they did not include Scopus in their search, and one study is only indexed in Scopus. Since our search includes Scopus, one of the strengths of the present study is that it incorporates more clinical trials than the previous one; (B) sub-group analysis was performed only based on participant’s age and gender, type of intervention, and type of exercise, while in the present study, sub-group analysis was performed based on country, study design, duration of intervention, and daily dose in addition to those mentioned before, to clarify the sources of heterogeneity in a more comprehensive way; and (C) to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the effect of BRJ supplementation on EIMD in a dose-response manner. Therefore, the present study sought to assess the effect of BRJ supplementation on different aspects of EIMD in a more comprehensive way.

Limitations

We acknowledge that our study has inherited some limitations that need to be addressed.

(A) The small number of studies and their limited, small sample sizes may influence our final effect size. Although included trials reported that approximately 10 participants per group were sufficient to achieve 80% power at an alpha level of 0.05, we acknowledge that the small sample sizes may limit both generalizability and statistical power.

(B) Considerable heterogeneity level (I2 < 50) was observed for CK and MVIC, and no further methodological steps were feasible to reduce it. Therefore, we conducted multiple subgroup analyses to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Factors such as country, study design, gender, duration of intervention, type of exercise, and daily dose were each identified as partial contributors. This substantial heterogeneity reduces confidence in the pooled effect estimates. Moreover, the small number of included studies precludes a reliable assessment of publication bias, and therefore, no firm conclusions regarding publication bias can be drawn.

(C) The noticeably low number of female athletes (N = 21) compared to males (N = 153) limits the generalizability of the findings to females.

(D) No significant effects were found on CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α. This may reflect either insufficient available data or a true absence of effect.

(E) Muscle soreness was assessed using PPT, a subjective and technique-dependent measure that may introduce variability related to the assessor's skill and the participant's perception.

(F) Evidence of potential publication bias was identified for CK, MVIC, and PPT. As a result, we interpreted these findings cautiously and downgraded the certainty of evidence. Ensuring prospective registration and complete reporting of all trials (including null or negative results) is essential for reducing bias in future research.

(G) The BRJ supplements used across trials varied in brand and formulation, which may have contributed to interstudy variability.

Future studies should prioritize larger sample sizes, longer intervention durations, and greater inclusion of female participants. Additionally, using standardized BRJ formulations may improve consistency across trials and strengthen the reliability of future findings.

Optimal supplementation dose

The findings of this meta-analysis suggest that supplementation with approximately 500 ml/day of BRJ (containing ~4 mmol/L of Trolox) may be associated with reductions in CK levels; however, this observation is hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution, given the limited number of small trials, variability in formulations, and short follow-up durations.

An umbrella review of performance-focused BRJ supplementation trials in 2025 reported that nitrate doses of 515–1017 mg/d (8.3–16.4 mmol/day) were associated with performance improvements, whereas lower doses of 285–515 mg/day (4.6–8.3 mmol/day) did not produce such effects[59]. These nitrate ranges are broadly comparable to the amounts provided by the BRJ used in our study, supporting the physiological plausibility of the supplementation effects observed.

Conclusion

The present study provides evidence that BRJ supplementation may significantly improve factors involved in the process of EIMD, including muscle damage, muscle function, and muscle soreness through anti-inflammatory pathways. Therefore, BRJ supplementation appears to represent a safe and effective dietary strategy to attenuate EIMD and improve muscle function and athletic performance. Results of the dose-response analysis of the present study indicate that 500 ml/day of BRJ containing at least ~4 mmol/L of Trolox is the optimal dose to reduce CK levels. Nevertheless, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution, as the certainty of the evidence ranged from low to moderate according to the GRADE assessment.

Due to the short duration of most studies, as well as a comparable lack of female participants, it is suggested that further well-designed studies using standardized BRJ formulations with a larger sample size and longer duration of intervention focusing on female athletes be conducted to firmly establish the clinical efficacy of this plant.
