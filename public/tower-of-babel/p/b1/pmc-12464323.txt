Pattern functions of the Astrometric Gravitational Wave Antenna
Authors: Federica Santucci, Mariateresa Crosta, Mario Gilberto Lattanzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since the first detection of gravitational waves (GWs), the field of experimental gravitation is steadily working on improving the current detectors as well as developing new instruments in order to expand the range of observable frequencies and improve the reconstruction of the GW direction and source parameters. In such a context, the Astrometric GW Antenna, consisting of at least three pairs of lines of sight assembled in a very compact configuration, represents a promising concept to achieve the above goals. Therefore, its detection capabilities and performances should be characterised. We derive the pattern functions of the Astrometric GW Antenna to estimate its directional response, allowing initial comparisons with other detectors and among different configurations. Our analysis shows that the Astrometric GW Antenna is almost equally sensitive to GWs coming from any direction, and better suited for detecting relatively nearby events. The implication is that the Astrometric GW Antenna can be an optimal detector for GW sources located within our Galaxy and can boost the instantaneous sky coverage of current ground-based interferometers. Moreover, the complementarity with the observations of LISA space antenna is emphasised. Finally, the detection range can be further expanded by combining simultaneous observations of a few replicas of the Astrometric GW Antenna.

Introduction

Since the first direct detection of gravitational waves (GWs)1, we have witnessed a significant advancement in GW astronomy: to date, the sensitivity of existing facilities, LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) network2–4, based on laser interferometry, has improved by a factor of 10 and the number of events detected in the high frequency range of the GW spectrum has increased to about 2005.

Furthermore, new detectors are being developed both on ground, as Einstein Telescope6 and the Cosmic Explorer7, and in space, as the space interferometer LISA8. The target signals for space observations are in the low frequency range of the GW spectrum, from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{-4}$$\end{document}Hz to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{-1}$$\end{document}Hz. New windows are opening up in the ultra-low frequency range, between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{-9}$$\end{document}Hz and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 10^{-6}$$\end{document}Hz, thanks to the observations of the International Pulsar Timing Array (IPTA)9–12.

For an overall review on GW sources and detection methods we suggest the work by13 and references therein.

In order to fully exploit the huge potential of GW science, two crucial challenges continue to persist in the field of experimental gravitation: i) the extremely large observational range of amplitudes and frequencies to be probed, and ii) the improvement in sensitivity for determining the direction to GW emitting sources14.

The new concept of an Astrometric Gravitational Wave Antenna (AstroGraWAnt)15 offers the opportunity to improve upon the above issues. Indeed, relativistic astrometry16, the science of deriving positions and motions of celestial bodies by repeated measurements of the angles among their directions, is another promising way to detect GWs. As matter of fact, tracing back light paths travelling from star-like sources toward a space-born observer is a powerful method to investigate spacetime curvature.

The study of GW effects on the observed direction of light began in the 1970s with the papers by Grishchuk17 and Braginsky18. The first to suggest that GWs from compact sources can induce angular deflection on light rays was Fakir in 199419, even though he mistakenly overestimated the size of the effect. Just a few years later, Fakir’s calculations were corrected and the GW effect on photon trajectories properly modelled in a series of papers by, among others, Damour and Esposito-Farèse20.

With the advent of highly accurate astrometry in space, this detection method became increasingly relevant: in anticipation of its astrometric potential, back in 1998, Crosta et al.21 presented a detection model of light deflection due to GWs as a possible strong science case for the approval of the Gaia space astrometry mission by the European Space Agency22.

However, all previous studies on the astrometric measurement of GWs concerned with the GW deflection on a single light direction and with the use of non-dedicated instruments. Instead, AstroGraWAnt15 is devised as a multiple lines-of-sight (LsOS) telescope specialised in detecting GWs passing at the observer and perturbing its spacetime, by exploiting the measurement of the changing angle between two light directions.

In this article, we concentrate on the basic concept of AstroGraWAnt, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. It must be endowed with a minimum of three lines of sight (3-LsOS) pointing to three close pairs of suitable point-like celestial sources, which are then each re-imaged and multiplexed onto a common digital focal plane for measurement23,24. We mention here in passing that optical designs with more LsOS are already being investigated25.

According to these results, AstroGraWAnt can achieve a very isotropic performance by implementing an orbit around the Sun without further rotations. Therefore, AstroGraWAnt does not need the combined motion required for LISA.

What discussed above is the ideal response of a rotating AstroGraWAnt detector. Actually, since this instrument exploits pairs of close stars as natural sensors, the detailed characterisation of suitable stellar pairs is crucial to distinguish GW signals from angular variations due to other causes, such as the intrinsic luminosity changes of the stars (see Discussion section in15). Thus, a rotating detector implies the need of acquiring and characterising new star-like couples entering the LsOS.

Discussion

The main purpose of the current paper is characterising performances of AstroGraWAnt, as well as highlighting the potentiality of this novel detection principle.

Thanks to its alternative approach and innovative technologies, we expect AstroGraWAnt can contribute to exploring frequency ranges not covered yet by current and future GW detection programs, and improving the resolution in determining the sky location of the GW source.

To address these two challenges, the first step is to study how GW signals are collected by such an instrument, which requires the computation of the pattern functions.

In this paper, we derive the pattern functions of ideal AstroGraWAnt configurations in order to evaluate values of some fundamental characteristic parameters. Therefore, we estimate the ultimate performances of our proposed detector, i.e., how far it can observe GW signals and how isotropic the reachable volume is, without having to go into the details of GW source characteristics and detector noises. Finally, comparisons are reported with ground-based interferometer networks, and the LISA detector.

The most interesting finding is that a single AstroGraWAnt, with three LsOS and the GW free angle equal to optical resolutions routinely achievable in space (0.01as), can explore a region of space more isotropic than any other detectors, whereas it seems not to be able to observe as far as what can be done with the networks of interferometers in the circumstances of our preliminary analysis. Under the current assumptions, the results prove that our approach is especially suitable for GW sources closer to the observer and uniformly distributed in space, such as compact binaries within our Galaxy. This is a complementarity with the LISA detection volume. As matter of fact, the dominant sources in LISA band will be ten thousands of short period double white dwarfs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{GW}\gtrsim 3$$\end{document}mHz) in the Milky Way. As found in cosmological simulation combined with a binary population synthesis model33, these systems are naturally present in all the components of the Milky Way such as the thin and thick disc, the bulge, the stellar halo, tidal streams and satellite galaxies.

This scenario highlights the importance of having an astrometric antenna that can detect signals from, potentially, any direction in the sky. This would ensure a complete coverage of the sky even in that area where LISA is less sensitive, in order to have a comprehensive catalogue of the white dwarf population around the Milky Way.

By evaluating the average response related to the rotation and the heliocentric orbit of AstroGraWAnt, it appears that the detector motion has minimal effect on the detection distance and volume; in addition, serious limitations would come from finding and characterising new suitable reference stellar pairs in the case of a rotating detector.

The second major finding is that the maximum detection distance can be increased by configurations with more than three lines of sight. When combined with the isotropic response of AstroGraWAnt, this outcome provides much higher values of the detection volume that could outperform those of interferometer networks.

Despite its exploratory, ideal, nature (although based on actual values from state of art technology), this study has brought the innovative principle of using differential astrometry to detect GWs into the observational context addressing directional response of the detector. It has also highlighted the many points in need of future investigations.

In this respect, this work represents the first of a series of articles devoted to the AstroGraWAnt full characterisation.

As already mentioned above, angular variations due to the GW emission of compact Galactic binaries should be analysed and special attention should be paid to the issue of signal overlap, which could easily arise given the large isotropic response of our detector.

In line with the studies carried out for current and planned GW detectors, the next crucial point to address for the different realizations of AstroGraWAnt is the definition of the noise spectral density, S(f), which takes into account the various noise sources of our antenna in different frequency ranges. This will allow us to establish more realistic limits on our detector, and therefore to what extent it can complement and possibly enhance the landscape of GW astronomy in terms of GW source targets and synergies with other detectors.

Finally, the AstroGraWAnt ability of localising the GW source on the sky needs to be established via, for example, analytic methods and the Fisher-matrix approach, as done by the currrent GW-search collaborations.

To conclude, we report a preliminary evaluation on the GW frequency range coverage of AstroGraWAnt based on simple considerations about the sampling frequency and the mission lifetime.

Given the sampling frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_s$$\end{document} and thanks to the Nyquist-Shannon theorem, the maximum frequency that a GW should have to be correctly sampled is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{GW}\le f_s/2$$\end{document}. If we assume as a representative sampling time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{s}=25$$\end{document}ms, i.e. the integration time of the astrometer FGS aboard HST34, we should be able to correctly sample GWs with frequencies lower than 20Hz. This suggests that the value of few Hz can be safely considered the upper limit of the GW frequency range observable by AstroGraWAnt.

The lower limit is related to the total observation time, which can be evaluated, in principle, coincident with the mission lifetime, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{miss}$$\end{document}. For a space mission with 5 years of observation and assuming we want to measure the wave signal for at least two periods, the lower limit of the GW observable frequency range can be safely estimated around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-8}$$\end{document}Hz.

Thus, for the purpose of our preliminary evaluations, we can set the GW frequency range of AstroGraWAnt as22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \quad 10^{-8}\text {Hz}<f_{GW}<1\text {Hz}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}We pointed out that AstroGraWAnt has the potential to observe GWs across a frequency range spanning eight orders of magnitude.

This capability would enable the investigation, in synergy with PTA, of GWs at very low frequencies, such as those produced by cosmological sources and supermassive black holes, as well as at low frequencies in synergy with LISA, including signals from massive black holes and Galactic binary systems.

Naturally, the sensitivity will not be uniform across the entire eight orders of magnitude in frequency, and various noise sources may hinder the detection of GW signals in specific frequency bands.

The design of AstroGraWAnt, envisioned as a future astrometric space mission dedicated to GW detection, should therefore be carefully characterised and optimised with respect to the targeted frequency range, whether the aim is to probe the frequency gap between LISA and PTA, or to enable joint observations with LISA.
