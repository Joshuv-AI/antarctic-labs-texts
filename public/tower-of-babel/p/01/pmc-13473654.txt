Experimental analysis of an enhanced biogas injera baking stove with an inverted conical combustion chamber
Authors: Taha Abdella Geda, Demiss Alemu Ambie, Kamil Dino Adem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional biomass-based injera baking stoves used in rural Ethiopia are characterized by low thermal efficiency, excessive fuel consumption, and significant greenhouse gas emissions, creating major environmental, health, and energy challenges. Despite the increasing interest in clean baking technologies, limited research has focused on optimizing biogas-powered injera baking systems while maintaining the traditional quality and cultural acceptability of injera. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and evaluate an optimized biogas-powered injera baking stove with improved thermal performance and reduced energy consumption.The study employed experimental and analytical methods to design and test an improved biogas stove incorporating an inverted 45° conical combustion chamber, enhanced insulation, and optimized air-fuel mixing mechanisms. Thermal efficiency, flame stability, heat distribution, energy consumption, and injera quality parameters were experimentally evaluated and compared with conventional biomass-based baking systems. The results revealed that the developed stove achieved a thermal efficiency of 36%, which is more than double that of traditional three-stone biomass stoves. The optimized system reduced energy consumption by approximately 40% per injera and demonstrated stable blue flame combustion with uniform heat distribution across the baking surface. Large-scale adoption by 5 million rural households could potentially save about 23,160 TJ (6.43 TWh) of energy annually. Furthermore, the produced injera maintained desirable traditional quality characteristics, including porosity, softness, flexibility, and uniform thickness, confirming the cultural acceptability of the technology. The findings indicate that the optimized biogas injera baking stove is an efficient, sustainable, and culturally appropriate clean baking solution for rural communities. Its large-scale implementation could contribute significantly to energy conservation, climate change mitigation, and improved household health conditions. However, long-term field-based evaluations are recommended to assess durability, operational stability, maintenance requirements, and environmental performance under diverse real-world conditions.

Introduction

Access to clean, efficient, and sustainable cooking energy remains a major global challenge, particularly in developing countries where reliance on traditional biomass fuels is still widespread. Globally, billions of people depend on firewood, charcoal, and agricultural residues for cooking, leading to serious environmental degradation, deforestation, and adverse health impacts due to indoor air pollution. This issue is especially pronounced in Sub-Saharan Africa, where rapid population growth and limited access to modern energy technologies continue to intensify pressure on natural resources and hinder progress toward sustainable development goals1.

In Africa, rural communities are the most affected, as they rely heavily on inefficient traditional cooking systems2. These practices not only contribute to forest depletion but also impose significant socioeconomic burdens, particularly on women and children who are primarily responsible for fuel collection and cooking. Despite growing efforts to introduce clean cooking technologies, adoption remains limited due to economic, technical, and cultural barriers.

In Ethiopia, the situation is particularly critical, as the national energy mix is dominated by traditional biomass, accounting for approximately 89% of total energy consumption, while modern energy sources such as petroleum and electricity contribute only 7.7% and 3%, respectively3. Rural households are especially dependent on biomass fuels, primarily for cooking staple foods such as injera4. Injera, a spongy flatbread made from teff, is Ethiopia’s staple and culturally significant food, consumed daily across nearly every household. Its preparation is highly energy-intensive and traditionally carried out using wood-fired stoves, particularly inefficient three-stone stoves, which result in excessive fuel consumption and significant environmental degradation5.

Given these challenges, there is an urgent need to explore sustainable and locally appropriate alternatives for cooking energy, particularly for rural communities in Ethiopia, which form the focus population of this study. Among the available options, biogas technology presents a promising solution due to its renewable nature and local availability6. Ethiopia has substantial potential for biogas production, with an estimated 56 million cattle generating about 280,000 tons of manure daily7. Household-scale biogas systems can produce between 0.5 and 1.2 m3 of biogas per day8, and co-digestion with agricultural residues such as cereal straws and coffee husks can further enhance methane production9.

Previous studies have demonstrated the technical and economic feasibility of biogas systems in Ethiopia, including their potential to reduce fuelwood consumption and improve environmental sustainability10. However, limited research has focused on optimizing biogas stove designs specifically for injera baking, particularly in terms of heat distribution, thermal efficiency, and compatibility with traditional cooking practices. Existing biogas injera stoves often face challenges in providing uniform heat, which directly affects the quality of injera11. Furthermore, alternative clean cooking technologies, such as solar cookers, electric stoves, and improved biomass stoves, have limitations related to reliability, affordability, and continued dependence on biomass resources12.

The point of entry for this research is therefore based on addressing these identified gaps by developing and evaluating an optimized biogas stove tailored for injera baking in rural Ethiopian contexts. This study is informed by existing literature and aims to bridge the gap between technological potential and practical applicability. Specifically, it focuses on improving thermal efficiency through enhanced heat transfer mechanisms, optimizing the air–fuel ratio to maximize fuel savings, ensuring uniform heat distribution suitable for traditional injera preparation, and assessing environmental benefits such as firewood reduction and CO₂ emission mitigation.

This research aims to evaluate an optimized biogas stove specifically designed for injera baking, targeting four key areas: improving thermal efficiency by enhancing heat transfer mechanisms, maximizing fuel savings through air-fuel ratio optimization, achieving uniform heat distribution compatible with traditional injera preparation methods. The stove is intended to serve as a practical and culturally aligned solution for both household and small-scale commercial applications in rural and peri-urban Ethiopia.

Method and materials

Design and construction of the biogas-powered injera baking stove

The biogas-powered injera baking stove was designed and constructed from mild steel, galvanized mild steel was used for the gas pipe. The stove was fabricated using a combination of rolling, cutting, drilling, and welding techniques. The design features a conical combustion chamber at the top of the stove, where combustion occurs. For insulation, gypsum was selected due to its low thermal conductivity and availability, which helps to reduce heat loss and improve overall efficiency.

A key component of the stove is its inverted conical combustion chamber, situated directly below the baking pan (mitad). This chamber features a top diameter of 450 mm and is fixed at a 45° inclination, designed to enhance flame spread and direct thermal radiation uniformly over the baking surface. The conical geometry encourages better air-biogas mixing and guides the flame toward the pan, promoting complete combustion and reducing flame impingement losses.

Three symmetrically placed exhaust ports are located at the upper section of the chamber to vent combustion gases efficiently, maintain airflow, and prevent thermal accumulation inside the stove body as shown in fig. 1..

The burner is centrally located at the base of the conical combustion chamber and plays a crucial role in achieving uniform flame distribution. Fabricated from perforated mild steel, the burner features cylindrical size of 150mm diameter and 200mm height. This layout conforms to the chamber’s conical profile.

Biogas is introduced through a centrally positioned nozzle connected to a household-scale biogas digester. A manual control valve is integrated to regulate gas flow as needed. Air for combustion enters through strategically placed natural draft air inlets at the lower body of the stove, ensuring a proper air-fuel ratio for clean, efficient, and stable combustion Fig. 1.Fig. 1Biogas injera baking stove.

Instruments, testing protocol, and data collection

A comprehensive evaluation protocol was established to assess the performance of the biogas-powered injera baking stove, integrating controlled laboratory experiments with real-world field observations13. The study employed a comparative experimental design, utilizing standardized 45 cm diameter clay mitad baking surfaces. All tests were conducted in triplicate and followed the ISO 19867-1:2018 testing standards to ensure repeatability and reliability.

Key performance metrics included thermal efficiency—determined using a modified Water Boiling Test (WBT)14—and biogas pressure, which was continuously monitored using water-filled manometers. Baking time was precisely recorded using digital timers, and surface temperature across the mitad was measured using both an infrared thermometer and a thermal imaging camera for spatial heat distribution analysis15.

Injera quality was assessed through a combination of quantitative and qualitative measures. Pore uniformity was evaluated visually and recorded as 15 ± 2 pores/in2, while thickness consistency (2.0 ± 0.3 mm) was measured using digital calipers. Mechanical integrity was examined through standardized folding tests to determine flexibility and resilience16.

An economic analysis was performed using established cost-benefit modeling techniques to evaluate fuel savings and payback periods. This analysis adhered to the Clean Cooking Alliance’s Performance Testing Guidelines for ensuring quality assurance and reporting consistency10.

The instrumentation and materials employed included a digital weighing scale for measuring batter and injera mass, a biogas digester system as the primary fuel source, a plastic storage tank for batter, a traditional sefied tool for lifting cooked injera, and other ancillary items necessary for field deployment.

Heat transfer between the pan and the stove’s side wall

In the upper section of the stove, heat transfer plays a critical role in determining the overall thermal performance. This region consists of a cylindrical mild steel pan, which is surrounded by layers of insulating gypsum. The radial heat transfer from the combustion chamber to the external environment occurs through a combination of convection, conduction, and insulation resistance mechanisms,

Specifically, hot combustion gases transfer heat convectively to the inner surface of the steel wall. This is followed by radial conductive heat transfer through the steel and gypsum insulation layers, and finally, convective heat loss from the outer steel surface to the ambient air. This layered structure leads to a composite heat transfer process, which is critical for minimizing thermal losses and maintaining efficient stove operation.

Combustion stoichiometry, air–fuel equivalence ratio (λ) and flame color

Biogas combustion involves the mixing of air with the fuel gas, followed by ignition of the resulting air–gas mixture. In this study, the biogas used consists of approximately 60% methane (CH₄) and 40% carbon dioxide (CO₂). The chemical reaction governing the combustion process primarily reflects the complete oxidation of methane, as CO₂ is inert in combustion.

To optimize combustion performance, a stoichiometric analysis was performed based on the biogas composition. The theoretical air requirement for complete combustion of methane was calculated, resulting in a stoichiometric air-to-biogas ratio of approximately 5.72 m3 of air per 1 m3 of biogas. This value ensures sufficient oxygen for complete combustion of methane, minimizing unburned hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide emissions33.17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CH4 + 2(O_{2} + 376 N_{2} ) \to CO_{2} + 2 H_{2} O + 7.52 N_{2}$$\end{document}

The equivalence ratio λ provides insight into combustion efficiency and flame characteristics. A value of λ = 1 indicates ideal stoichiometric combustion. Values greater than 1 imply excess air (lean combustion), while values less than 1 indicate a fuel-rich mixture. These conditions also influence flame color—blue for efficient combustion and yellow or orange when combustion is incomplete.

Combustion regimes were classified as follows:

λ = 1: Stoichiometric (ideal, blue flame),

λ < 1: Fuel-rich (incomplete combustion, yellow flame, soot),

λ > 1: Fuel-lean (cool flame, lower efficiency).

Result and discussion

Thermal efficiency

The experimental evaluation of the biogas-powered injera baking stove demonstrated substantial thermal performance improvements compared to conventional biomass-based stoves. The system achieved a measured thermal efficiency of 36%, representing more than a two-fold increase over traditional three-stone stoves, which typically operate at 10–15%5,41.

This notable improvement was primarily the result of several targeted technical innovations, including a 45° conical combustion chamber, 3 cm thick gypsum-based insulation, and optimized air-fuel mixing42, where the excess air coefficient was maintained near λ ≈ 1, ensuring efficient combustion with minimal heat loss29,43.

In particular, the conical combustion chamber geometry promoted uniform flame distribution and stable blue flame characteristics, indicative of complete combustion, while the insulation minimized conductive losses to the stove body44. The integration of these design features enabled the system to reach a thermal efficiency comparable to or exceeding modern improved cookstoves used in other biomass- or gas-based applications45.

The stove’s energy requirement per injera was also significantly reduced. On average, the biogas system required only 700 kJ per injera, compared to 800–1,200 kJ typically consumed by firewood-based baking systems such as the Mirt stove or traditional three-stone arrangements35 . This represents an energy reduction of approximately 12% to 40%, further emphasizing the stove’s efficiency and sustainability advantages.

In addition to improved fuel utilization, the biogas stove contributes faster baking cycles, and enhanced user comfort, making it a compelling clean cooking solution aligned with national clean energy strategies and international climate mitigation frameworks19 .

Flame and combustion quality

The color of the flame serves as a visual diagnostic for combustion efficiency and completeness46. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the optimized biogas stove was designed to maintain a stable blue flame, signifying high combustion efficiency and effective thermal transfer to the baking surface. This feature not only improves overall energy utilization but also reduces indoor air pollution and enhances the quality of the injera. Visual flame assessment therefore serves as a practical and immediate indicator of combustion quality in household energy systems. A blue flame typically indicates complete combustion, characterized by optimal air-fuel mixing and a high-temperature, clean-burning reaction that results in the efficient conversion of fuel to heat energy. This combustion state also ensures lower emissions of unburned hydrocarbons. Temperature profile during stove operation

The temperature profile observed during the operation of the biogas injera baking stove reveals three distinct thermal phases: preheating, active baking, and idle. As shown in Fig 7a, the preheating phase is characterized by a gradual increase in surface temperature from ambient conditions (21°C) to the target baking temperature of 230°C within 20 minutes. This rapid thermal response highlights efficient biogas combustion and effective heat transfer between the burner and the pan surface42. Achieving baking temperature quickly is a key indicator of low energy loss and strong thermal coupling47.Fig. 7(a) Surface temperature profile over time; (b) Thermal imaging showing uniform heat distribution.

(a) Surface temperature profile over time; (b) Thermal imaging showing uniform heat distribution.

In the active baking phase, the pan temperature stabilizes around 230°C with minimal fluctuation. Such thermal stability indicates a consistent combustion process and uniform heat output, both of which are crucial for producing injera with even texture and well-distributed eyelets3. Maintaining this steady temperature minimizes batch-to-batch variations, which is essential for household acceptability.

During the idle phase, a gradual temperature decline is observed. However, the pan surface remains above 120°C for an extended period, enabling subsequent baking without the need for complete reheating—thereby reducing biogas consumption38 . This heat retention reflects the stove’s high thermal inertia and efficient insulation, both of which enhance energy efficiency and user convenience48.

Overall, the thermal behavior across the three phases—rapid preheating, stable baking, and extended heat retention—demonstrates the stove’s strong energy performance and its practical suitability for continuous injera baking in domestic settings49 . Thermal imaging further confirmed uniform heat distribution across the entire 45 cm clay pan surface, with temperature variations consistently kept within 25°C—a notable improvement compared to the 55°C differentials observed in earlier stove models11.

This improved uniformity, attributed to the conical combustion flame geometry of the optimized burner (Fig. 5), ensures even thermal exposure—a critical factor for achieving high-quality injera structure and texture. The enhanced heat transfer and minimized temperature gradients not only improve injera consistency but also reduce the risk of undercooked or scorched sections, commonly seen in traditional stoves45.

The temperature variation of only 25°C across the pan surface is significantly lower than the 55°C variation observed in previous biogas injera baking stoves11, reflecting the benefits of improved burner design and pan contact efficiency. Such thermal consistency enhances not only product quality—resulting in uniformly browned, evenly cooked injera—but also contributes to energy savings, as less heat is wasted or unevenly distributed50.

Moreover, the stable thermal profile reduces the likelihood of undercooked or burnt sections, which often occur with irregular heat zones in traditional setups. These improvements confirm that targeted burner geometry and improved heat transfer mechanisms can significantly elevate both the efficiency and performance of injera baking stove45.

When compared with prior investigations on biogas-fueled injera stoves, the system developed in this study exhibits marked improvements in thermal and operational performance. Chala51 reported an initial heating time of 25 minutes, a baking duration of 4 minutes per injera, and a reheating interval of 4 minutes. In contrast, the optimized stove presented in this study achieved a reduced initial heating time of 20 minutes, a baking time of 3 minutes, and a reheating duration of 3 minutes. These enhancements underscore the improved thermal responsiveness and efficiency of the system, primarily resulting from advancements in burner geometry, combustion control, and heat retention capabilities.

Energy-saving benefits of the biogas injera baking stove

The analysis reveals a substantial energy saving when transitioning from traditional biomass (wood-fired) stoves to biogas-powered stoves for injera baking in rural Ethiopia. For a population of 5 million households consuming a total of 7.375 billion kilograms of injera annually, the traditional wood-fired stoves require approximately 3.944 billion kilograms of wood, equating to a total energy consumption of 59,160 (TJ). In contrast, the same demand can be met using 1.800 billion cubic meters of biogas, which corresponds to only 36 billion MJ of energy. This translates to a net energy saving of 23,160 TJ per year, or roughly 6.43 TWh. These savings stem from the higher combustion efficiency and better heat transfer characteristics of biogas stoves, as well as the absence of energy losses associated with moisture content in firewood. The 39% reduction in energy use not only underscores the superior thermal performance of biogas stoves but also indicates their potential to significantly lower the national energy burden associated with household cooking. Furthermore, this improvement in energy efficiency directly supports climate goals, resource conservation, and household energy resilience in off-grid rural areas.

Economical feasibility

The economic feasibility analysis demonstrated that the improved biogas injera stove is financially viable under realistic rural household fuel-saving conditions. The calculated Net Present Value (NPV) was approximately 895.75 USD, indicating positive long-term economic benefits over the 20-year project lifetime. The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) was estimated at about 21%, confirming the attractiveness of the investment. Furthermore, the Payback Period (PBP) was approximately 4.4 years, demonstrating relatively rapid recovery of the initial investment cost. These results suggest that the improved biogas stove technology can provide both economic and environmental benefits for rural households through reduced biomass fuel consumption and improved energy efficiency.

Challenges in promoting the biogas stove adoption

While the improved biogas stove demonstrated notable technical advantages—such as enhanced thermal efficiency and reduced baking time—its broader adoption remains constrained by several non-technical challenges. A major barrier is the high initial investment cost associated with installing biogas digesters and fabricating the stoves, which can be prohibitive for many households, particularly in rural or low-income settings. User acceptance also plays a crucial role, as cooking practices are often deeply rooted in cultural traditions. Resistance to adopting new technologies may arise from unfamiliarity, skepticism about performance, or limited awareness of long-term benefits.

Another significant challenge is the variability in biogas supply, which depends on factors such as consistent feedstock input, proper digester maintenance, and suitable ambient temperatures. Seasonal fluctuations, especially during colder periods, can reduce microbial activity in the digester, compromising gas production and overall stove performance. Moreover, the technical upkeep and maintenance of both the biogas stove and digester systems require skilled personnel and access to spare parts—resources that are often scarce in remote areas.

To overcome these barriers, a combination of technical, educational, and institutional strategies is essential. For instance, community-based biogas hubs can reduce the financial burden on individual households while improving reliability and efficiency through shared resources. Additionally, targeted training programs for users and local technicians can build confidence, enhance operational knowledge, and support long-term system functionality. These integrated measures—alongside continued innovation and supportive policy frameworks—are critical to ensuring the sustainable and scalable adoption of biogas stove technologies.

Conclusions and recommendation

This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of an optimized biogas-powered injera baking stove, highlighting its significant improvements in thermal efficiency, energy savings, and product quality compared to conventional biomass-based systems. The stove achieved a thermal efficiency of 36%, more than double that of traditional three-stone stoves, primarily due to design enhancements including a precision-engineered 45° conical burner and optimized combustion air-fuel ratios.

The system demonstrated exceptional energy savings—reducing energy consumption by approximately 23,160 TJ or 6,430 GWh. annually across 5 million rural households. This translates to a 40% reduction in energy use compared to biomass-based baking systems. Additionally, the stove’s ability to maintain a stable blue flame and uniform heat distribution across the baking surface ensures efficient energy utilization and consistent injera quality.

The biogas stove also produced injera that met traditional sensory and cultural quality standards, including optimal porosity, flexibility, softness, and uniform thickness, affirming that technological advancement need not compromise culinary authenticity.

Overall, the findings strongly support the biogas stove as a sustainable and culturally appropriate clean cooking solution. Its deployment at scale holds the potential to simultaneously address critical energy access, environmental, and health challenges in Ethiopia and other regions with similar socio-economic and energy contexts. Future work should explore integration with AI-based optimization systems, localized biogas supply chains, and policy frameworks to enhance scalability and adoption.

Although the present study confirms the short-term thermal efficiency and performance improvements of the improved biogas injera baking stove, it is important to recognize that long-term usage evaluations are necessary to fully understand the system’s durability, operational stability, and environmental impact under real-world conditions. Such extended testing would help identify potential material degradation, changes in performance over time, user adaptation patterns, and maintenance requirements. Additionally, monitoring long-term emissions and resource use could provide stronger evidence for the environmental sustainability of the technology. Therefore, future work should incorporate longitudinal field studies across diverse geographic and socioeconomic settings to generate comprehensive data that can inform policy decisions, funding mechanisms, and large-scale adoption strategies for biogas stove technologies.
