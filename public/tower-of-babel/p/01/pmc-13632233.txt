Bayesian inference of interval-censored data with an application to HIV population surveys: a simulation study comparing Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and Metropolis–Hastings sampling algorithms
Authors: Alexander van Twisk, Innocent Maposa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Public health context

HIV population-based surveys provide nationally representative estimates of HIV prevalence and incidence for public health surveillance. Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (PHIA) surveys, such as the Zimbabwe Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (ZIMPHIA) [1], collect biomarker data to quantify population-level HIV indicators. Although seroconversion times are not observed directly, such surveys make it possible to characterise the time-to-infection distribution by combining each participant’s HIV biomarker status with a plausible lower bound on the at-risk window. In practice, an individual’s seroconversion time is bracketed between two reference timepoints — here, the age of sexual debut (when exposure becomes plausible) and the age at survey participation (when biomarker status is ascertained). This yields interval-censored observations, where the true event time is only known to lie between two bounds [2]. Interval censoring commonly arises whenever follow-up is periodic or retrospective, and it fundamentally differs from standard right censoring: in right-censored data, an event time is either observed exactly or is right-bounded by a last follow-up time, whereas interval-censored data provide less information, since the event is only constrained to an interval. This pattern is pervasive in HIV/AIDS research, where infection time is determined retrospectively by medical history, as well as in broader medical follow-up studies with intermittent assessments.

Interval censoring: definition and inferential challenges

Interval censoring occurs when the event time is known only to lie within an interval , where is the left endpoint (last known event-free time) and is the right endpoint (first time the event is observed or known to have occurred). This framework encompasses several special cases. Current-status censoring (also called case-I interval censoring) arises when and for all i, so we observe only a single inspection time and an indicator of whether the event occurred before that time. Case-II interval censoring allows distinct inspection times per individual but assumes at most one interval per subject, while general interval censoring permits multiple inspection intervals or overlapping observation schemes [2, 3].

Interval censoring complicates survival inference relative to right censoring. Classical non-parametric estimators differ, and specialized algorithms are often required [2, 4–6]. In this study, we adopt a parametric Bayesian approach that trades stronger distributional assumptions for direct probabilistic inference.

Methods for interval-censored data

The statistical literature offers three main paradigms for analyzing interval-censored survival data: non-parametric, semi-parametric, and parametric methods. We briefly review each paradigm’s development and limitations for survey-weighted interval-censored data.

Non-parametric approaches focus on estimating survival functions without distributional assumptions. [4] pioneered maximum likelihood estimation for arbitrarily grouped, censored, and truncated data, providing a foundation for subsequent non-parametric methods. However, these approaches typically do not accommodate complex survey designs, limiting their utility for population-based surveys.

While non-parametric methods avoid distributional assumptions, semi-parametric approaches offer greater modelling flexibility. Semi-parametric methods extend the Cox proportional hazards framework to interval censoring. Finkelstein, and Finkelstein, Wolfe [7, 8] introduced early regression approaches for interval-censored data, and [9] applied semi-parametric accelerated failure time (AFT) regression specifically to interval-censored HIV/AIDS data, demonstrating the feasibility of covariate adjustment in this context. However, semi-parametric methods encounter numerical difficulties with interval censoring and typically lack straightforward mechanisms for incorporating survey weights.

In contrast, parametric approaches achieve computational efficiency through explicit likelihood specification. They posit distributional families for survival times, offering analytical tractability at the cost of stronger assumptions. For interval-censored data, parametric methods have employed Weibull [10, 11], log-normal, and log-logistic distributions. Among these, the log-logistic model offers distinct advantages for HIV seroconversion analysis. It provides closed-form survival and cumulative distribution functions, enabling numerically stable evaluation of interval-censored likelihoods without numerical integration. The log-logistic also supports non-monotonic (unimodal) hazard functions when the shape parameter γ> 1, capturing infection risk that rises in early adulthood and declines thereafter—a pattern plausible for HIV transmission dynamics shaped by age-related sexual activity [12]. By contrast, the Weibull distribution restricts hazards to monotonic forms (decreasing if γ < 1, increasing if γ> 1), limiting flexibility for age-structured infection risk. Mixture models (e.g., mixture Weibull) can capture multimodal hazards but require additional parameters and more complex priors, increasing computational burden without clear benefit for the single-peaked pattern expected in HIV data.

Model rationale

Both the log-logistic and the log-normal families can generate non-monotonic hazards. For this study we adopt the log-logistic AFT form as the primary specification because: (i) AFT coefficients have an immediately interpretable time-ratio meaning for policy audiences; (ii) the algebraic form of S and F yields simple, stable interval probabilities F(R)-F(L) in our weighted case-II setting; and (iii) compared with mixture-Weibull specifications, the log-logistic achieves the relevant hazard shape with far fewer parameters, avoiding identifiability challenges under heavy interval censoring and survey re-weighting. We therefore treat the log-logistic as a parsimonious baseline for comparing samplers, while recognising that alternative baselines are valuable sensitivity targets for future work.

Empirical support for the log-logistic in HIV research is strong: [13] found it “the most suitable among other distribution models” for half-censored HIV infection time data in the San Francisco cohort, and our ZIMPHIA 2020 application (Results, Application to ZIMPHIA 2020 data section) yields an estimated shape parameter γ = 12.9 (95% CrI: 12.4, 13.5), indicating a sharply unimodal hazard that retrospectively validates the model choice. This motivates our focus on log-logistic AFT regression for the sampler comparison, though robustness across alternative baselines (log-normal, Weibull) remains an important target for future work.

There is literature on Bayesian parametric methods for interval censored data, however, there is sparse information on applications in context where survey weights are incorporated in modelling [14] conducted one of the few studies explicitly comparing Bayesian and frequentist approaches for interval-censored log-logistic models, concluding that “Bayesian under the LINEX loss function is the preferred estimator” with better mean-squared error and absolute bias than maximum likelihood estimation, particularly for small to moderate sample sizes. They note that interval-censored data are “more demanding than right censoring” and recommend Bayesian methods with Tierney and Kadane approximations when sample sizes are not very large (n < 100). [15] extended this work by developing the icenReg R package, providing accessible parametric tools for interval-censored regression, though without explicit survey weight handling.

Recent methodological advances have emerged in generalized families: [16] introduced the generalized odd log-logistic family for interval-censored survival data, arguing that traditional models “frequently cannot provide good fits to real data” and emphasising the importance of hazard function flexibility.

Methodological gap and analytical approach

Two gaps remain for PHIA-like surveys. First, almost all existing Bayesian and frequentist models for interval-censored survival data ignore design-based survey weights. Second, no systematic comparison of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and Metropolis–Hastings algorithms exists for Bayesian log-logistic models under realistic combinations of interval censoring and survey weighting. Ignoring either interval censoring or survey weights can bias estimates and misrepresent uncertainty [17–19].

We address both gaps by analysing time to seroconversion using a Bayesian log-logistic AFT regression that accommodates interval censoring and incorporates survey weights via a pseudo-likelihood scheme. For each participant i, the likelihood contribution is , evaluated in closed form from the log-logistic S(t) and F(t). Survey design enters through case weights that re-scale individual log-likelihood terms to target finite-population inference (e.g., [18, 20]).

The resulting posterior is non-conjugate and shaped by integration over subject-specific intervals and weight-induced re-scaling, creating challenging geometry that differentially affects sampler performance. Wide intervals induce flat likelihood regions that slow MH mixing, while sharp transitions at interval boundaries and exponential weight re-scaling can create curvature that triggers HMC divergences if step-size tuning is inadequate. These challenges motivate our sampler comparison and the weighted likelihood formalisation in Methods section. We embed a weighted log-logistic AFT model within a simulation framework calibrated to PHIA surveys and apply the same model to ZIMPHIA 2020 data.

Objectives

Building on the identified gap, the study’s primary objective is to compare the sampling efficiency and convergence of the two Bayesian algorithms (Metropolis–Hastings with Gibbs steps, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) when fitting the log-logistic AFT model to interval-censored, weighted HIV data. Efficiency will be quantified through metrics like the effective sample size per second (ESS/s) and standard convergence diagnostics (e.g. and effective sample sizes for parameters). In particular, we will evaluate whether HMC achieves higher ESS/s and closer-to-1 values than MH for the same model and data.

Alongside this primary goal, we pursue several secondary objectives that strengthen the empirical foundation and practical relevance of the comparison. We assess the accuracy of parameter estimation under each sampler, using simulation truth as a benchmark to quantify bias, root-mean-square error, and Monte Carlo standard error. This accuracy assessment ensures that efficiency gains do not come at the cost of inferential validity. We also investigate the sensitivity to survey weight inclusion, checking whether analyses that ignore weights yield biased results or different uncertainty than weighted analyses. This sensitivity analysis clarifies the consequences of model misspecification in practice and provides practitioners with actionable guidance for HIV surveillance analyses.

Results section presents the simulation results and ZIMPHIA application. Discussion section discusses findings in the context of the literature, and Conclusions section concludes with recommendations for practice and future research.

Primary efficiency metric: ESS per second

Throughout this paper we report the effective sample size per second (ESS/s) as the primary efficiency comparator. We adopt ESS/s because (i) it captures the cost-of-inference asymmetry independently of whether the model is fit once or many times — the same per-second cost applies to a single primary fit, to bootstrap-based design-variance refits, and to sensitivity panels; (ii) it scales transparently to larger PHIA cohorts, MPHIA, SHIMS, etc., where the same model is fit at different scales; and (iii) it is the standard metric in the MCMC literature [21]. Two analyses in this paper illustrate the practical bite of this comparator: the 100-refit design-based variance correction (Sensitivity to design-based resampling section) and the 1,000-replicate HMC rerun at the central simulation cells (Asymmetric replicate design at central operating points section), both of which were feasible only because HMC’s per-second cost is roughly 70-fold lower than MH’s on these data.

Choice of baseline distribution

Log–logistic and log-normal AFT models both permit non-monotonic hazards. We adopt the log-logistic because its AFT coefficient has a straightforward time-ratio interpretation and its algebra for S and F yields simple interval probabilities F(R)-F(L) for case-II data under weighting. While a mixture-Weibull baseline can emulate similar shapes, it introduces additional parameters and identifiability challenges under heavy interval censoring. Given our focus on sampler performance under realistic PHIA-like designs, the log-logistic provides a parsimonious and practically interpretable primary model; the application in Results section shows it recovers a plausible unimodal hazard.

Estimands and interpretation

The primary estimand of interest is the covariate effect β in the log-logistic AFT model (Eq. 7), which quantifies the marginal (unadjusted) log time-ratio associated with the binary exposure on the time-to-event scale. By “marginal” we mean that the model includes a single binary covariate without adjustment for additional covariates; this usage should not be confused with population-averaged (g-formula) marginal effects, which integrate over covariate distributions. In the simulation scenarios, represents the expected log time-ratio comparing exposed to unexposed groups, and in the ZIMPHIA application represents the sex difference in log time-to-seroconversion.

Because the models include only a single binary covariate, the causal interpretation of β as an exposure effect requires assuming no unmeasured confounding. In the simulation study, data are generated under a known mechanism where exposure is randomly assigned (), ensuring no confounding by design. In the ZIMPHIA application, the binary sex covariate is exogenous, but epidemiological interpretation should acknowledge that other factors (e.g., age, behavioural covariates, geographic region) may confound the association. Adjusted (multivariable) analyses that condition on continuous and categorical covariates are beyond the scope of the present study but are noted as essential extensions for causal inference in observational HIV surveillance data.

For the single-covariate models considered here, β represents the marginal (unadjusted) effect on the time scale: is the multiplicative effect on median survival time (a time ratio), directly interpretable as an acceleration or deceleration factor [23].

Convergence criteria and diagnostics

We employ a suite of diagnostics to assess convergence and sampling quality for both MCMC algorithms. Convergence is declared when all parameters satisfy split [23]. We further require minimum bulk and tail effective sample size (ESS) ≥ 400 per parameter to ensure reliable posterior summaries (thresholds match the analysis pipeline). Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE) are monitored to keep MCSE.

For HMC, we inspect Stan-specific pathologies: divergent transitions after warm-up indicate biased sampling and must be eliminated via increased adapt_delta or reparameterisation; tree-depth saturation signals insufficient exploration and triggers max_treedepth escalation [26, 27]. Any HMC run exhibiting post-warm-up divergences or persistent saturation is flagged for exclusion or re-fitting with adjusted tuning parameters.

For Metropolis–Hastings, we monitor acceptance rates and mixing. Lag-1 autocorrelation and integrated autocorrelation time (IACT ) provide supplementary mixing diagnostics; extreme IACT values (>100) indicate poor exploration and motivate proposal tuning or increased iteration counts. All diagnostics are computed using the posterior package in R [28], ensuring harmonised metrics across both samplers.

Aims

The primary aim is to compare the sampling efficiency of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC/NUTS via Stan) and Metropolis–Hastings (MH via JAGS) for Bayesian log-logistic AFT models with interval-censored, survey-weighted data. Sampling efficiency is operationalised as effective sample size per second (ESS/s) and convergence diagnostics (split ). Secondary aims assess inferential accuracy (bias, RMSE) and uncertainty calibration (95% coverage, credible interval width). The simulation design systematically varies sample size (), censoring severity (10%, 30%, 50%), and weight dispersion (none, low, high) to characterise sampler performance across realistic PHIA-like scenarios.

Estimands

The primary estimand is the exposure effect in the log-logistic AFT model (Eq. 7), representing the conditional log time-ratio for exposed versus unexposed groups. Secondary estimands include the baseline scale parameter α (median survival time when exposure = 0) and the shape parameter γ (log-logistic shape controlling hazard curvature). Simulation truth is α = 5.0, , γ = 1.5. Performance is evaluated by comparing posterior summaries to these known truths across all scenarios.

Misspecification scenario

As a targeted sensitivity analysis, we added a single misspecification cell at , censoring proportion 0.3, and weight regime “high” (CV ≈ 1.0). The data-generating mechanism in this cell is a Weibull distribution with shape k = 2.0 and scale λ = 6.01, chosen so that the marginal median time-to-event matches the log-logistic truth used elsewhere () while introducing a substantively different hazard shape (strictly monotone for the Weibull versus unimodal for the log-logistic). The fitted model remains log-logistic; the cell therefore quantifies the consequences of a misspecified baseline. Two hundred replicates were generated and fitted with both HMC and MH using identical settings to the main simulation. The Weibull data-generating mechanism was calibrated to match only the log-logistic truth’s median time-to-event; higher-order moments differ between the two distributions. Calibrations matching multiple moments simultaneously could be explored as a more stringent misspecification scenario, but the present calibration is sufficient to demonstrate that the qualitative sampler ordering (HMC > MH on ESS/s) is preserved under shape misspecification.

Methods: sampler comparison

We compare two Bayesian MCMC algorithms applied to the weighted interval-censored log-logistic AFT model (Weighted interval-censored log-logistic model section): Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) via the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) implemented in Stan [27, 31], and Metropolis–Hastings (MH) implemented in JAGS [32]. Both samplers target the same posterior under identical model specifications, priors (Prior specification section), and weighted likelihood (Eq. 4). This ensures that performance differences reflect algorithmic efficiency rather than model discrepancies.

HMC exploits gradient information from the log-posterior to propose distant moves with high acceptance probability, reducing random-walk behaviour characteristic of MH. NUTS adaptively tunes trajectory length during warmup, eliminating the need for manual step-size calibration. We use Stan defaults: 4 chains, 1,000 warmup iterations, 5,000 post-warmup draws per chain, adapt_delta=0.8, max_treedepth=10.

MH uses random-walk Gaussian proposals with adaptive covariance tuning during an initial pilot phase. We implement MH in JAGS with 4 chains, 1,000 adaptive iterations, 1,000 burn-in, and 5,000 retained draws per chain, matching HMC’s post-warmup sample size. Acceptance rates target ≈ 0.30 for optimal mixing in moderate dimensions.

Diagnostics are harmonized via posterior::summarise_draws() [28], computing split , bulk/tail ESS, and MCSE for both samplers. Runtime is recorded as wall-clock time for post-warmup sampling (excluding warmup/burn-in) to isolate per-iteration efficiency.

Performance metrics and diagnostics

Performance measures follow ADEMP recommendations [30]. Each scenario is analysed with four parallel chains per sampler, using 1000 warm-up (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) or adaptation/burn-in (Metropolis–Hastings) iterations and 5000 post warm-up draws per chain. Deterministic seeds anchor replication: a global seed of 2025 is applied to Stan, and JAGS chains receive offsets of 2025 + replicate index to maintain comparability across methods. Jobs are dispatched as PBS arrays on the Stellenbosch University HPC cluster, reserving one CPU core per chain.

Convergence and efficiency are assessed per parameter and in aggregate using split , effective sample size (ESS), and minimum ESS thresholds. Efficiency is summarised via ESS/s. Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE) are computed from posterior standard deviations and ESS; summaries report both per-parameter and overall minimum MCSE. Runtime is recorded as total wall-clock time excluding warm-up for comparability across samplers. Additional diagnostics (divergence counts, maximum treedepth hits, energy-based Bayesian fraction of missing information (BFMI), and Metropolis–Hastings acceptance statistics) provide auxiliary evidence. Replicates breaching or minimum bulk ESS < 100 were considered unsatisfactory; we increased the number of iterations or adjusted tuning parameters and reran the sampler in such cases. For HMC, we also monitored divergent transitions; when divergences occurred, we increased ‘adapt_delta’ and, if necessary, reparameterised the model before accepting a run. In practical terms, our protocol was: (i) require split and adequate bulk/tail ESS; (ii) if divergences occur, first raise ‘adapt_delta’ (e.g., to 0.9–0.99) and, if needed, increase ‘max_treedepth’; (iii) if issues persist, apply a non-centred parameterisation or rescale parameters following Betancourt [26, 27]. For MH, we adjusted proposal scales/covariances to target stable acceptance. Scenario-level tables and figures contrasting accuracy and efficiency across samplers are deferred to Results section, with supplementary material in Appendix 4.

Applied example (ZIMPHIA 2020)

ZIMPHIA 2020 was a nationally representative, two-stage stratified household survey conducted in Zimbabwe between November 2019 and April 2020 [1]. The sampling frame was the Zimbabwe Master Sample, partitioned into 65 strata (rural and urban segments of each of 60 administrative districts plus six metropolitan strata). Within each stratum, primary sampling units (enumeration areas) were selected with probability proportional to size; within each selected enumeration area, a fixed number of households was systematically sampled. Adults aged 15–64 within sampled households were eligible for the individual questionnaire and biomarker testing (HIV, viral load).

Within this design, the survey provides individual sampling weights (btwt0) that adjust for the inclusion-probability inverse, household and individual non-response, and post-stratification calibration to 2012 census totals. For variance estimation, ZIMPHIA additionally supplies 175 pre-computed jackknife replicate weight columns (design_wt001 through design_wt175) along with stratum (varstrat) and primary-sampling-unit (varunit) identifiers, enabling design-consistent inference without manual specification of the stratification and clustering structure.

The simulation scenarios are calibrated to this survey, mirroring its cross-sectional, single-visit design where seroconversion is interval-censored between sexual debut and survey. We apply the same Bayesian model to the ZIMPHIA microdata as an empirical validation; we additionally use the replicate weights to compute a design-consistent variance estimate (Sensitivity to design-based resampling section below). As in the simulations, primary weights are normalised to sum to n; trimming is considered only as a sensitivity exercise [33, 34].

Use of large language models

We used large language models strictly as tools to assist with code refactoring, documentation, and language editing during manuscript preparation. Specifically, we made use of OpenAI’s ChatGPT 5, 5.1 and Codex as well as Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 3.5, Opus 4.1 and Claude Code. These versions roughly correspond to the latter half of 2025’s LLM model releases. All statistical models, simulation designs, substantive interpretations, and evidence synthesis were specified and verified by the authors.

Scalability with sample size

Figure 5 highlights relative runtime speed-ups across the design grid, while Table 9 summarises how ESS/s for β changes as n increases by factors of 10 (200 to 2,000) and 5 (2,000 to 10,000).

ESS/s decreases as n increases for both methods, with a substantially steeper decline for MH than for HMC. This supports the conclusion that HMC exhibits more favourable mixing scalability for the exposure effect across censoring and weight regimes. A corresponding runtime scaling summary is provided in Appendix 4.

Overall, the simulation evidence paints a consistent picture: HMC is markedly more efficient than MH for estimating the exposure effect β (higher ESS/s) while achieving comparable accuracy (RMSE) and similar calibration (coverage with like-for-like CI widths). Differences between methods are modest for point estimates but material for mixing efficiency, and these differences become more pronounced as sample size increases. The observed departures from nominal coverage are primarily associated with data difficulty (censoring, weight variability) rather than the choice of sampler.

Asymmetric replicate design at central operating points

At the two central operating cells we ran an enlarged Monte Carlo replicate count to tighten the precision of the headline numbers. At with censoring = 0.3 and weight regime “high” we ran 1,000 HMC replicates per cell (vs 200 elsewhere). The MH arm was not matched to this count: at , an MH replicate takes approximately seven minutes vs HMC’s thirty seconds, and 1,000 MH replicates at that single cell would have required approximately 25 hours of wall time, with a correspondingly larger cost at . The asymmetric design is itself an empirical demonstration of the ESS-per-second advantage that motivates the comparison.

Performance under baseline misspecification

Under the Weibull data-generating mechanism, HMC retained essentially equal RMSE to MH for (HMC: 0.108; MH: 0.108; Appendix 4, Table 31), with coverage of 79.0% for HMC and 79.5% for MH at the nominal 95% level. The HMC ESS-per-second advantage was preserved (HMC: 587; MH: 28.7; a advantage). Both samplers under-covered identically, indicating that the model family rather than the sampler is the source of coverage degradation under truth–mismatch. This is consistent with our interpretation that the sampler-comparison conclusions are driven by posterior geometry rather than by the precise form of the baseline truth.

Summary: application findings

The ZIMPHIA 2020 application confirms the simulation study’s key finding: HMC delivers substantial efficiency gains (72× faster ESS/s) without compromising inferential validity (posterior estimates agree to three decimal places). The real-world advantage exceeded simulation predictions, suggesting that gradient-based exploration is even more beneficial when posterior geometry is shaped by irregular empirical intervals rather than stylised simulation mechanisms. For production analyses of PHIA-style surveys with interval-censored outcomes and survey weights, HMC is the recommended default, with MH reserved for exploratory pilot fits or small datasets where absolute runtime is negligible.

Across the 5,400 simulated datasets, HMC (Stan) completed all fits with no problematic diagnostics (0 divergences; 0 max treedepth hits; all split ; minimum ESS well above 400 using defaults: 4 chains, 1,000 warmup, 5,000 post-warmup; ‘adapt_delta’=0.8, ‘max_treedepth’=10, diagonal metric). Sensitivity to design-based resampling

The primary HMC analysis treats the 16,554 weighted observations as approximately independent, ignoring the within-cluster correlation and stratification of the ZIMPHIA design. To quantify the impact of this approximation, we refitted the model 100 times, each time substituting one of the first 100 jackknife replicate weight columns supplied by ZIMPHIA for the primary weight. For each parameter we then combined the within-replicate posterior variance and the between-replicate variance B of the posterior means via Rubin’s rules [35, 36]: , giving a design-aware total variance and an approximate 95% credible interval .

For , the model-based 95% credible interval was [-0.174, -0.139] (width ≈ 0.035). Across the 100 replicates the between-replicate component B was negligible relative to within-replicate posterior dispersion (), yielding a design-aware Rubin total interval of [-0.182, -0.146] (width 0.036, inflation factor 1.04 relative to the model-based interval). We conclude that the model-based credible interval is approximately correct under the ZIMPHIA design: the between-replicate variability of the point estimate is small relative to the within-fit posterior dispersion, so treating the weighted observations as conditionally independent does not materially understate uncertainty for . The design-aware interval is therefore reported as a sensitivity finding rather than a correction.

This analysis required 100 HMC refits in approximately 2.4 hours; the corresponding MH analysis would have required approximately 50 hours and was not feasible within practical compute (see Why ESS/s, not wall time section).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

HMC substantially outperformed MH in sampling efficiency across all 5,400 simulated datasets (3–72× higher ESS/s) while achieving comparable accuracy and coverage. The ZIMPHIA 2020 application confirmed these gains in practice: HMC completed in under 2 minutes versus MH’s 32 minutes, producing substantively identical posterior estimates.

Interpretation and context

Results section presents the quantitative simulation results for the exposure effect (β); here we interpret those findings for the applied setting and outline implications for subsequent sections. Prior Bayesian regression work for interval-censored data (including Anderson-Bergman [15] and related guides) established practical tooling but did not compare samplers under survey weighting. Complementing that literature and application-focused studies using log-logistic baselines (e.g., [14]), our contribution isolates algorithmic efficiency in a weighted interval-censoring posterior that mirrors PHIA-style designs, providing a neutral HMC versus MH comparison under controlled censoring and weight-dispersion regimes.

Across all scenarios, HMC achieved substantially higher ESS/s than MH, with the gap widest at larger sample sizes. These gains are consistent with the theoretical and empirical advantages of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: gradient-informed proposals and momentum reduce the random-walk behaviour characteristic of Metropolis–Hastings, especially in higher-dimensional or correlated posteriors and in challenging posterior geometries. Interval censoring specifically contributes to challenging posterior geometry in two ways that differentially affect sampler performance. First, wide or partially overlapping intervals (common in cross-sectional HIV surveys where seroconversion is bracketed only by sexual debut and survey testing) induce flat regions in the likelihood surface: many parameter values yield similar interval probabilities , slowing MH’s random-walk exploration as proposals struggle to detect gradient information. Second, sharp transitions at interval boundaries, where the CDF difference changes abruptly, combined with the weighted pseudo-likelihood’s exponential re-weighting , create curvature and constraints that can trigger HMC divergences if step-size tuning is inadequate. Despite this challenge, HMC’s zero divergences across 5,400 datasets (using Stan defaults: adapt_delta=0.8, max_treedepth=10) demonstrate that the weighted log-logistic AFT model is well-behaved for gradient-based exploration, whereas MH’s performance degrades more steeply with sample size and weight variability [21, 24].

We assessed the constant-incidence assumption by stratifying the ZIMPHIA analysis into three birth cohorts (1965–1979, 1980–1989, 1990+) and refitting the model within each. Posterior medians for α declined monotonically from the oldest cohort to the youngest (38.2, 32.2, 28.9 years), and the magnitude of the sex effect grew correspondingly (0.04, 0.07, 0.13; Fig. 21, supplement). This is consistent with a declining HIV incidence trend over the period covered by the retrospective window, in line with the Zimbabwean epidemic’s well-documented secular decline since the early 2000s. We note that the apparent shortening of α in the 1990+ cohort is partly mechanical: members of this cohort were at most 30 years old at the 2020 survey, so the upper tail of the seroconversion-age distribution is structurally unobservable in that stratum. The growth in across cohorts, by contrast, is not a censoring artefact. The headline sampler-comparison conclusions are unaffected by this finding; the cohort-stratified results are reported as evidence that the constant-incidence assumption operates as a smoothing approximation over a non-stationary process. Numerical results appear in Table 30.

Constant-incidence interpretation

A note of caution on the interpretation of α and the model-implied hazard (Model-implied age-specific hazard section). The log-logistic AFT model summarises the entire retrospective window from sexual debut to survey under a single hazard function; the fitted α is therefore best interpreted as an average hazard over that window rather than a point-in-time incidence rate. Under the monotonically declining true incidence that has characterised Zimbabwe over the period covered by the retrospective window, the model-implied number is biased high relative to current incidence and biased low relative to the historic peak. The headline interpretation is therefore cumulative risk over the window, not current incidence. The birth-cohort sensitivity analysis (cohort-stratified Discussion paragraph in Interpretation and context section and Appendix Birth-cohort sensitivity (constant-incidence assumption)) quantifies the magnitude of this assumption empirically.

Application insights: ZIMPHIA 2020

The ZIMPHIA 2020 application (Results, Application to ZIMPHIA 2020 data section) validates the simulation study’s efficiency predictions and reveals substantive epidemiological patterns. HMC delivered 72× faster ESS/s than MH on the real survey data () thus exceeding the 40× advantage predicted by simulation extrapolation at comparable sample size and weight dispersion (Table 13). This upward deviation suggests that gradient-based exploration is even more beneficial when posterior geometry is shaped by irregular empirical intervals rather than stylised simulation mechanisms. Both samplers converged without diagnostic issues and yielded substantively identical posterior estimates (Table 12), confirming that HMC’s efficiency gains do not compromise inferential validity.

For analyses of large, weighted HIV survey data with interval censoring, we therefore recommend HMC as the default for production inference, with MH reserved for exploratory pilot fits or small datasets where absolute runtime is negligible.

Methodological implications

Use HMC by default when survey weights are variable or censoring ≥ 30%; MH may suffice for light censoring (< 10%) and stable (low-dispersion) weights, particularly in exploratory analyses or smaller pilot datasets where computational efficiency is less critical.

The simulation results support practical guidance for choosing between MH and HMC across common analysis conditions:

Small n and mild weights/censoring: Both MH and HMC recover similar point estimates and calibration; MH can be adequate for quick checks, but HMC still delivers higher ESS/s.

For HMC, we recommend adopting robust defaults (four chains; non-centred parameterisations when appropriate; conservative ‘adapt_delta’) and then relaxing if diagnostics are clean. When divergences occur, follow the protocol in Methods: raise ‘adapt_delta’, increase ‘max_treedepth’, rescale or reparameterise [26, 27]. For MH, performance hinges on well-tuned proposals; automated covariance adaptation or block-updating can help, but scaling remains less favourable.

Weight variability deserves special attention: greater dispersion amplifies posterior curvature and reduces information, which depresses ESS/s and coverage for both methods. Normalising weights to mean 1 (as done here) anchors uncertainty to an effective sample size; trimming may be considered as a sensitivity analysis, but primary inference should reflect the provided design weights. For broader perspectives on trimming, weight modelling, and design- versus model-based compromises, see Chen et al. [33], Gelman [34].

Because the simulation truth and the fitted model share the log-logistic family, the question arises whether HMC’s advantages depend on correct specification. The Weibull misspecification cell reported in Performance under baseline misspecification section addresses this directly: with the truth swapped to a Weibull distribution matched on median survival, the relative ordering of HMC and MH on every metric we report (bias, RMSE, coverage, ESS/s) was preserved. This is the expected consequence of HMC’s efficiency stemming from posterior-density gradients, which exist independently of whether the model is the true generative process.

Why ESS/s, not wall time

It might be argued that the headline ESS/s comparison would matter little if the model were fit only once. There are three reasons why it matters even in that case. First, ESS/s rather than wall time is the operative comparator in Bayesian inference: a sampler that produces 100 effective samples per second can support tasks that a sampler producing 1 ESS/s cannot, regardless of total run length. Second, the design-based variance correction in Sensitivity to design-based resampling section required 100 HMC refits; this was feasible in approximately 2.4 hours with HMC, but would have required approximately 50 hours with MH. Third, the asymmetric simulation design at the central cells (Asymmetric replicate design at central operating points section) was only computationally feasible because of the ESS/s advantage: we ran 1,000 HMC replicates per cell, but could not match this for MH within reasonable compute. The ESS/s advantage on ZIMPHIA is therefore not a benchmarking curiosity — it is the reason the analyses described in Sensitivity to design-based resampling and Asymmetric replicate design at central operating points sections were computationally feasible at all.

Comparison to literature

Our findings align with and extend prior work comparing HMC to alternative MCMC algorithms across diverse application domains. Monnahan et al. [21] demonstrated 2–10× efficiency gains when applying HMC (via adnuts) to fisheries stock assessment models fit with Template Model Builder (TMB), concluding that “HMC is a practical and powerful tool for TMB users.” Similarly, Thomas and Tu [24] found that HMC reduced effective sampling time by factors of 5–20× for hierarchical models of animal movement and population dynamics, attributing gains to gradient-based exploration of correlated posteriors. Our 3–72× ESS/s advantages for weighted interval-censored AFT models fall within this range and confirm that HMC’s efficiency benefits are not restricted to TMB or ecological applications but extend to survey-weighted survival analysis.

The importance of careful tuning and diagnostics, emphasised throughout the HMC literature [26, 27], is borne out by our simulation: zero divergences and clean convergence diagnostics under default Stan settings (adapt_delta=0.8, max_treedepth=10) suggest that the weighted log-logistic AFT model is well-behaved for HMC. This contrasts with MH, where performance depends critically on proposal tuning and degrades more steeply with sample size. The robustness of HMC’s default adaptation phase (which jointly tunes step size and mass matrix during warmup) reduces the need for user intervention and supports reproducibility across datasets.

Our work extends Anderson-Bergman [15], who developed the icenReg R package for parametric interval-censored regression but did not compare MCMC algorithms or incorporate survey weights. By demonstrating that HMC efficiently handles both interval censoring and pseudo-likelihood weighting, we provide practitioners with a validated workflow for population-based HIV surveys that require simultaneous adjustment for both data features. The ZIMPHIA application confirms that these efficiency gains translate to practice: 72× faster ESS/s than MH, completing in under 2 minutes on a standard laptop, makes Bayesian interval-censored survival analysis feasible for routine PHIA analyses.

Interpretation of ZIMPHIA application findings

The time-ratio for sex averaged over the retrospective interval (β = -0.156; time ratio 0.855) aligns with documented gender disparities in HIV acquisition risk in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. The sharply unimodal hazard (γ = 12.9) retrospectively validates the log-logistic model choice. Both samplers performed better on ZIMPHIA than simulation extrapolations predicted (Table 13): observed runtime was 21–23% of predicted and ESS/s was 3–5× higher, suggesting that empirical posterior geometry is more favourable than stylised simulation intervals.

ZIMPHIA’s moderate weight dispersion (CV = 0.50) and wide interval censoring (median 25 years for HIV-positive individuals) place it between the simulation’s “low” and “high” regimes. The Kish effective sample size of 13,294 (80% of nominal) indicates a design effect substantial enough to require pseudo-likelihood adjustment but not so extreme as to compromise inference, consistent with the simulation coverage patterns (Uncertainty and calibration: credible interval width and coverage for β section).

Key practical implications: HMC completed the full ZIMPHIA analysis in under 2 minutes using Stan defaults, requiring no manual tuning. The 72× ESS/s advantage frees computational budget for richer model extensions (hierarchical effects, multiple covariates, prior sensitivity). These gains likely generalise to other PHIA countries with similar designs, though validation on additional surveys would strengthen this claim.

Strengths and limitations

The study design follows ADEMP guidance with a transparent scenario grid; the likelihood correctly handles interval censoring with survey weights; computation is fully reproducible with harmonized diagnostics across samplers; and performance is corroborated on ZIMPHIA 2020, demonstrating that simulation findings translate to practice.

Limitations to the study include that the scope is restricted to the log-logistic AFT model; we did not evaluate alternative baselines (e.g., Weibull, log-normal) or semi-parametric approaches. The simulated and applied analyses used limited covariate structures, so efficiency under richer multivariable adjustment remains to be shown. We did not conduct weight-trimming or prior-sensitivity analyses. External validation is limited to one PHIA survey, and generalizability should be confirmed on additional countries. The ZIMPHIA design-based variance correction was implemented via the 100 jackknife replicate weights; residual design features (post-stratification adjustments beyond those embedded in the replicate weights) were not separately accommodated. Misspecification was examined at one representative cell (Weibull truth, , censoring 0.3, weight CV ≈ 1.0); broader misspecification regimes (mixture truths, time-varying covariates, alternative baseline families) are left to future work.

Conclusions

This study compared Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC via Stan) and Metropolis–Hastings (MH via JAGS) for Bayesian inference of log-logistic accelerated failure-time models applied to interval-censored, survey-weighted HIV seroconversion data. The comparison spanned 27 simulation scenarios and one empirical application to the Zimbabwe Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (ZIMPHIA) 2020 survey, with both samplers targeting the same weighted posterior so that differences reflect algorithmic efficiency rather than model specification.

Across simulated and empirical settings, HMC achieved substantially higher effective sample size per second than MH while delivering essentially identical point estimates, uncertainty, and coverage, and clean convergence diagnostics. In the ZIMPHIA application, these efficiency gains translated into large runtime savings without compromising inferential validity.

MH remains adequate for small datasets with low weight dispersion and mild censoring, particularly for exploratory analyses where absolute runtime is negligible. For production inference on PHIA-style surveys—characterised by large samples, wide interval censoring, and moderate-to-high weight variability—HMC is the recommended default computational engine for weighted interval-censored survival analysis, enabling richer model extensions within practical time constraints.

Birth-cohort sensitivity (constant-incidence assumption)

To assess the constant-incidence assumption empirically, we refitted the ZIMPHIA model separately within three birth cohorts (1965–1979, 1980–1989, 1990+). Figure 21 shows the posterior medians and 95% credible intervals for α, , and γ in each cohort; Table 30 reports the numerical summaries. Posterior medians for α declined monotonically from older to younger cohorts and the magnitude of grew correspondingly, consistent with a non-stationary HIV incidence process over the retrospective window. The apparent shortening of α in the 1990+ cohort is partly mechanical (cohort members were at most 30 years old at the 2020 survey, so the upper tail of the seroconversion-age distribution is structurally unobserved in that stratum); the trend in , however, is not a censoring artefact. Headline sampler-comparison conclusions are unaffected by this analysis; see Discussion, Constant-incidence interpretation section for interpretation.

Misspecification simulation (Weibull data-generating mechanism)

To address the concern that the simulation study uses log-logistic data with a log-logistic fitting model, we ran a single misspecification scenario at , censoring = 0.3, and weight regime “high” in which the data-generating mechanism was Weibull (shape k = 2.0, scale λ = 6.01, calibrated to match the log-logistic truth’s median of 5 years). The fitting model remained the log-logistic AFT; both HMC and MH were applied. Table 31 summarises sampler performance across 200 replicates per sampler. Bias and RMSE for are essentially identical between the two samplers, and both undercover at the nominal 95% level (HMC: 79.0%; MH: 79.5%). This coverage degradation is a property of the misspecified model family rather than the sampler. HMC’s effective sample size per second remains larger than MH’s, demonstrating that the headline efficiency conclusion is robust to misspecification of the baseline distribution.
