Subject-specific atlas for automatic brain tissue segmentation of neonatal magnetic resonance images
Authors: Negar Noorizadeh, Kamran Kazemi, Seyedeh Masoumeh Taji, Habibollah Danyali, Ardalan Aarabi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Developing advanced systems for 3D brain tissue segmentation from neonatal magnetic resonance (MR) images is vital for newborn structural analysis. However, automatic segmentation of neonatal brain tissues is challenging due to smaller head size and inverted T1/T2 tissue contrast compared to adults. In this work, a subject-specific atlas based technique is presented for segmentation of gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from neonatal MR images. It involves atlas selection, subject-specific atlas creation using random forest (RF) classifier, and brain tissue segmentation using the expectation maximization-Markov random field (EM-MRF) method. To increase the segmentation accuracy, different tissue intensity- and gradient-based features were used. Evaluation on 40 neonatal MR images (gestational age of 37–44 weeks) demonstrated an overall accuracy of 94.3% and an average Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.945 (GM), 0.947 (WM), and 0.912 (CSF). Compared to multi-atlas segmentation methods like SEGMA and EM-MRF with multiple atlases, our method improved accuracy by up to 4%, particularly in complex tissue regions. Our proposed method allows accurate brain tissue segmentation, a crucial step in brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) applications including brain surface reconstruction and realistic head model creation in neonates.

Introduction

Expert and intelligent systems for qualitative and quantitative analyses of brain magnetic resonance (MR) images are extensively used in routine clinical diagnosis of many neurological disorders as well as in neuroscience research1. The segmentation of brain MR images into different tissues such as GM, WM, and CSF is a crucial processing step for surface reconstruction and realistic head model creation2. Manual delineation and labeling of brain tissues would provide highest accuracy, however, it is laborious and time-consuming in large-scale. Furthermore, it is prone to human errors and lacks of reproducibility.

Infancy is critical time in mental health and thus, measuring brain tissues volume and changes in brain of neonates is required for studying neurodevelopment. These requires automatic and accurate labeling of brain tissues from MR images3. However, in comparison with adults, there are challenges in automatic brain tissue segmentation from neonatal MR images because of lower signal to noise ratio, reduced tissue contrast, poor spatial resolution and partial volume effect resulting from larger voxels relative to brain size4.

Compared to the numerous methods developed for brain tissue segmentation in adults, there are few tools introduced for brain tissue segmentation in neonatal MR images4. These methods can be classified into: unsupervised methods, parametric methods, classification methods, atlas fusion methods, and deformable models5. Due to challenges such as noise and intensity inhomogeneity in neonatal MR images, unsupervised methods have been for pre/post-processing proposes. In atlas fusion approaches, label fusion method is used to combine aligned atlas to segment brain tissues. However, applying this method is limited due to large developmental changes in neonates in different ages and limited number of labeled atlases. In classification methods, a classifier is trained using MR images and segmentation masks (i.e., ground truth labels) to assign a class label to each voxel. Random forest (RF), support vector machines (SVM) and more recently convolutional neural networks (CNN) are example of classification methods that are used for neonatal brain tissue segmentation6–11. Although classification based methods widely used in adult brain tissue segmentation, however, due to rapid brain development and heterogeneity in morphology and texture, which is caused by the rapid brain development, using classification base methods, e.g. deep learning, can be expensive, cumbersome, and time-consuming4.

In parametric methods, segmentation problem is solved by fitting a model to the data. Typically, Gaussian mixture model (GMM) in conjunction with a probabilistic brain atlas or atlas fusion as spatial prior information, is used for tissue segmentation. Normally, expectation–maximization (EM) with combination with Markov random field (MRF) to model spatial smoothness and overcome noise and bias field correction is used to fit the model to the data. Due to robustness to differences in anatomy, this approach has been used in majority of neonatal brain tissue segmentation methods5,12. Accuracy of this approach can be affected by applied atlas. In neonates, the multi-atlas based algorithms provide a better solution for brain tissue segmentation by overcoming problems caused by poor contrast regions using prior information from atlases. The key point in multi-atlas based methods is to incorporate information from atlases into the tissue segmentation procedure. In general, the multi-atlas based tissue segmentation approaches13–18 use preselected training images to create an atlas for tissue segmentation. The performance of these methods varies significantly from region to region in any target image, depending on local similarities between the atlas and the target image. The accuracy of the multi-atlas based segmentation methods can be improved using subject-specific atlases created based on characteristic features of target images. The subject-specific atlas can improve the tissue segmentation performance by significantly reducing dissimilarities between the atlas and the target image, especially in regions with a high degree of tissue complexity.

In the past decade, a few neonatal brain tissue segmentation methods have been developed. Gui et al.19 proposed an image-based method for the segmentation of cortical/subcortical gray matter (GM), myelinated/unmyelinated white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), from T1- and T2-weighted MR images of neonates. However, the image-based methods are sensitive to the shape and size of structural elements. To improve the segmentation performance, Wang et al.20 proposed a neonatal deformable surface-based tissue segmentation method by integrating local information, spatial prior, and cortical thickness constraint in a level-set framework They also extended their method to the longitudinal image segmentation of neonates21. In comparison with the image-based methods, the deformable surface-based methods are sensitive to noise. Their segmentation performance also depends on the initial location of evolving surface. In an effort to further improve the brain tissue segmentation performance, Cardoso et al.22 introduced a single atlas based method based on intensity, shape, and spatial information obtained from atlases. The single atlas based methods suffer from registration errors due to differences in brain structures between subjects. To overcome this problem, the multi-atlas method proposed by Wang et al.23 incorporated multiple atlases as training data to train global classifiers required for label assignment in target images. Due to the ambiguous tissue intensity distribution caused by local intensity changes, the global classification methods have shown lower accuracies for neonatal brain tissue segmentation. To tackle this problem, Serag et al.,13 developed an automatic segmentation approach (SEGMA) incorporating a sliding window strategy and a multi-class random forest classification method.

In this paper, we presented an automatic subject-specific brain tissue segmentation method to segment GM, WM, and CSF in T2-weighted neonatal MR images. In this method, a set of training atlases was first selected uniformly from the entire library of the training images for each target image, which was then partitioned into non-overlapping windows. A subject-specific atlas was created using a multi-atlas label propagation strategy and RF classifiers trained on different windows. The subject-specific atlas was finally used as prior knowledge in the EM-MRF method for brain tissue segmentation.

Materials and methods

The neonatal database

We evaluated the performances of our proposed method using the first release of the developing human connectome project (dHCP) database which is publicly available. The image acquisition was done using 3 T Philips Achieva including 32 channel phased array head coil24. By anatomical imaging of 40 non-sedated term neonates (gestational age of 37–44 weeks), the T2-weighted MR images were acquired in sagittal and axial slice stacks within plane resolution 0.8 × 0.8 mm2 and 1.6 mm slices overlapped by 0.8 mm. Other parameters are: TR = 12 s; TE = 156 ms; SENSE factor: axial = 2.11, sagittal = 2.58. Furthermore, we applied the proposed method to segment brain tissues of 40 neonates with the same age range at acquisition time from dHCP data release four (dHCPR4)25. This release includes images of 783 neonates with age range 23–44 weeks (gestational age) which have been acquired with the same imaging parameters of the first release.

The motion corrected, reconstructed T2-weighted images were bias corrected using the N4 algorithm26. Manual atlases, annotated by a neuroanatomist expert, were registered to the T2-weighted images, and their labels were fused in the subject space to derive structure priors. Subsequently, segmentation was performed employing an EM scheme27 to extract 87 regions, which were further consolidated into 8 tissues, including cortical gray matter (cGM), deep gray matter (dGM), WM, CSF, ventricle, cerebellum, brainstem, and hippocampus. The GM was created combining the cGM and dGM tissues. GM, WM, and CSF masks were then reviewed by neuroanatomists and any mis-segmentation was subsequently manually corrected in all scans and utilized as the “ground truth”. These pre-processed T2-weighted images and their corresponding “ground truth” images were then used to assess the accuracy of the proposed methods proposed in this study.

Step 2: Non-overlapping windowing: In neonatal MR images, WM contains high water content in some regions (e.g. near to ventricles) and therefore its signal intensity is similar to that of CSF in these regions31. The classification methods based on classifiers trained using global information have shown to perform poor on tissues with similar intensities (like CSF or WM) due to the ambiguous tissue intensity distribution14,15,32. To overcome this weakness, local classification methods have been introduced33. With a relatively high computational load, the local strategy has been shown to provide a higher segmentation accuracy by training independent classifiers in a voxel-wise manner. Discussion

In this study, an automatic subject-specific brain tissue segmentation method was developed and evaluated for automatic segmentation of GM, WM, and CSF from MR images of neonates. In this method, a subject-specific atlas was created for each target image using non-overlapping windows, the multi-atlas label propagation strategy and the RF classifier. The subject-specific atlas provided more spatial information about tissues especially in regions with high complexities in comparison with probabilistic atlases, which do not provide enough anatomical details required for accurate segmentation. For classification, the window-specific spatial information was taken into account by training an independent RF classifier for each window. This strategy prevented misclassification usually occurred in segmentation using global classifiers. The window-based classification also reduced the computational load by simultaneously processing all voxels in each window instead of single voxels.

As shown in Table 2, the proposed method outperformed the existing multi-atlas based brain tissue segmentation methods, in which the atlases were only used as prior knowledge or training data in global/local classification-based methods14,15,33. Due to the ambiguous tissue intensity distribution caused by local intensity changes, the global classification methods have shown lower accuracies for neonatal brain tissue segmentation14,15. In comparison, the local classification methods can better handle local intensity changes in single voxels, though with significantly increased computation time and complexity required for voxel-wise processing33. To be able to compare the performance of our method with that of SEGMA and the EM-MRF algorithm using three different atlases (AtlasI, AtlasII, and AtlasIII), we tested the accuracy of these segmentation methods on the dHCP database42. Compared to SEGMA, our method achieved better performance on GM and WM and comparable segmentation results on CSF. Overall, our method outperformed SEGMA by 2% improvement in overall tissue segmentation accuracy by employing subject-specific atlases used as a spatial constraint in the EM-MRF method.

Relatively lower performance of CSF in all of the methods can be argued due to the difficulty in identifying boundary between CSF and GM. CSF regions in the brain, such as the ventricles and sulci, often have smaller volumes compared to GM and WM. The limited spatial resolution of MRI can lead to partial volume effects, where a single voxel contains a mixture of different tissue types (e.g., CSF, GM, and WM). The contrast inversion of GM/WM in newborn images worsens the partial volume problem at the interface between GM and CSF. Since CSF has the highest intensity in T2-weighted images in neonates, and the resolution of their MR images is limited, some voxels between CSF and GM (e.g., in the sulcus) are brighter than GM and darker than CSF. Although applying subject-specific atlases and EM-MRF in the proposed method decreased the number of misclassified voxels, the accuracy of the segmented CSF is still lower than that of GM and WM. Furthermore, the size, shape, and distribution of CSF-filled spaces (ventricles, sulci, etc.) can vary significantly between individuals, even in healthy subjects, which results in different segmentation accuracies between subjects compared to the accuracy of GM and WM segmentation. This anatomical variability increases the standard deviation of the segmented CSF in a population.

In our work, the use of both the subject-specific atlas and the windowing strategy was found very efficient for incorporating local information into the segmentation process. In order to investigate the effect of subject-specific atlases on the efficiency of the EM-MRF method, we also compared the proposed method with the EM-MRF algorithm with three different atlases (AtlasI, AtlasII, and AtlasIII). In comparison with the population-based atlases (AtlasI, AtlasII, and AtlasIII), the EM-MRF method performed brain tissue segmentation more efficiently using the subject-specific atlases especially when the intensity contrast between different tissues was low e.g. nearby ventricles showing a low contrast between WM and CSF. Overall, the proposed method using subject-specific atlases could improve the overall tissue segmentation accuracy by up to 4% compared to the EM-MRF method using population-based atlases.

We also assessed the effect of number of trees (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TRE$$\end{document}TRE) on the RF classifier performance (used in the subject-specific atlas creation stage) and window size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w \times w \times w$$\end{document}w×w×w) on the segmentation accuracy. Increasing the number of trees led to an increase in subject-specific atlas creation accuracy up to a certain level. Further increases in the number of trees did not significantly improve the performance but it increased the RF classifiers’ training time. In our study, an optimal number of trees of 20 provided the optimal trade-off between the accuracy and computational time. This finding is consistent with the number of trees used in previous studies13–15.

Given that our proposed method is a multi-atlas based approach, it allows for the replacement of the entire library with different age subjects such as preterms. Furthermore, our approach can be generalized to various databases, regardless of age, modalities (such as T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and multi-modal), or data from different scanners. Conclusion

In the present study, an automatic subject-specific brain tissue segmentation method for segmentation of GM, WM, and CSF in MR images of neonates was developed. In this method, a subject-specific atlas was created using the UAS atlas selection strategy followed by non-overlapping windowing and RF classification for each target image. The EM-MRF method then used the subject-specific atlas for brain tissue segmentation. In comparison with probabilistic-based atlases, the subject-specific atlas provided more spatial information about tissues by significantly reducing dissimilarities between the atlas and the target image, especially in regions with a high degree of tissue complexity. The subject-specific atlas based brain tissue segmentation outperformed other existing neonatal brain tissue segmentation methods by significantly reducing misclassification errors. The subject-specific atlas-based strategy could efficiently decrease the computational complexity compared to the methods that use local classifiers for each voxel.

In this study, we utilized the first and fourth release of dHCP database, a well-established standard database for neonatal brain segmentation. As dHCP is currently the only standardized database available for neonates, our proposed method was specifically applied to this database. While we acknowledge the importance of testing the algorithm on additional databases, the lack of other standardized databases for neonatal brain imaging represents a notable limitation of our work. The proposed method can be used in neonatal and preterm brain image analysis and applications such as surface reconstruction and realistic head model creation.
