Changes in Self-Efficacy During COVID-19 Among Adolescents With Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Nkemjika Okonkwo, Erin M Bergner, Sarah S Jaser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:The purpose of this longitudinal, qualitative study was to examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on self-efficacy and diabetes management among youth diagnosed with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM).Methods:We conducted two semi-structured interviews with adolescents diagnosed with T1DM (n = 18) and their maternal caregivers (n = 20) over 6–9 months, to understand how their confidence in the adolescent’s ability to manage their diabetes changed during the pandemic. Interviews were transcribed and coded by four raters, establishing inter-reliability.Results:Four themes emerged: Preventing Adverse Glycemic Events, Useful Glucose Trend Information, Improved Diabetes Management, and Downsides of Technology. Most adolescents (63%) reported increased confidence in their ability to manage diabetes during the pandemic. Over half of these adolescents were already using an insulin pump, and a substantial number (33%) updated their insulin delivery method. Both adolescents and caregivers cited diabetes devices as a factor in their self-efficacy for diabetes management.Discussion:Findings illustrate the importance of diabetes management in the daily lives of adolescents diagnosed with T1DM, and the potential benefits of diabetes devices during disruptions to care.Keywords: COVID-19, Insulin infusion systems, Adolescent, Self-efficacy, Type 1 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Self-efficacy – or an individual’s belief in their ability to execute actions necessary to reach a certain goal – is important in diabetes management and is associated with better glycemic levels and improved health outcomes (Iannotti et al., 2006). Self-efficacy is key factor in the conceptual model of childhood adaptation to type diabetes mellitus (T1DM) (Whittemore et al., 2010); in this framework, self-efficacy is an individual and family response that influences adaptation to T1DM, including glycemic and psychosocial outcomes in youth. Disruptions to routines and medical care during the COVID-19 pandemic prompted an increase in T1DM self-management responsibilities for many adolescents (Fernández et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2022). Studies in families of youth diagnosed with T1DM identified changes in diabetes management early in the pandemic, including both positive changes, such as increased time for meals and physical activity, and negative changes, such as fewer blood glucose checks and lack of structure (Ferguson et al., 2022; Goethals et al., 2022). Little is known, however, about how adolescents handled this shift in responsibility, or how their self-efficacy for diabetes management changed over time.

Despite increased uptake in diabetes technology (continuous glucose monitors, insulin pumps, and automated insulin delivery systems), only 17% of adolescents diagnosed with T1DM from a national sample were meeting glycemic targets in 2019 (Foster et al., 2019). In addition, adolescents are less likely than adults to experience the glycemic benefits of diabetes technology (e.g., increased time in range), which is thought to be due to the inconsistent use of devices among adolescents (Laffel et al., 2020). Finally, studies of the psychosocial impact of diabetes devices find that adolescents diagnosed with T1DM do not report the significant improvements in quality of life and reductions in diabetes distress that are observed in adults (Bratke et al., 2022; Hommel et al., 2014; Huhn et al.). Qualitative studies highlight the perceived benefits and burdens of diabetes device use (Messer et al., 2018), but these were conducted prior to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Thus, the purpose of this longitudinal, qualitative study was to examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on self-efficacy and diabetes management among youth diagnosed with T1DM. Findings have the potential to inform the best ways to support adolescents diagnosed with T1DM and their families when future disruptions to management occur, including natural or man-made disasters.

METHODS

To understand adolescents’ and caregivers’ experiences of diabetes management and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, a phenomenological approach was chosen to explore participants’ perspectives. The goal of a phenomenological approach is to develop a comprehensive description of “how” people [adolescents] experience a phenomenon in the context of daily life (Creswell, 2016). The current study is a supplemental qualitative study implemented after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. Interviews were conducted with adolescents diagnosed with T1DM and their maternal caregivers who were participating in a clinical trial evaluating the effects of a cognitive behavioral intervention for distressed mothers (Jaser et al., 2019). Interviews were conducted with participants in the comparator group (diabetes education) to avoid contamination from the active intervention. Participants gave informed consent to take part in the optional interview, in line with the protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB# 182217). All of the participants who completed initial interviews (conducted between April 2021 and May 2022) were invited to complete follow-up interviews that further examined their experiences managing diabetes during the pandemic, approximately 6 to 9 months later.

Interviews with adolescents and their caregivers explored the ways adolescents managed their diabetes over the course of the pandemic, to understand the impact of COVID-19 on diabetes management among youth diagnosed with T1DM. The interviews included a question about adolescents’ level of confidence in their ability to manage their diabetes. Specifically, adolescents were asked “How confident do you feel about your diabetes management on a scale of 1 (not so confident) to 10 (extremely confident)?” followed by a prompt to explain their reasoning for giving that rating. Similarly, using the same scale, caregiver interviews explored caregivers’ level of confidence in their child’s ability to manage their diabetes (see Supplemental materials for the interview guides). Hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) values and diabetes device use were extracted from adolescents’ medical records at baseline.

Interviews were transcribed and uploaded to NVivo for analysis. Techniques of thematic analysis were used to code and identify themes in the interview transcripts (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Four raters developed an initial codebook, which was applied to a subset of transcripts. The team reviewed these codes, and any discrepancies in the application and definitions of codes were discussed until consensus was reached, establishing inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.78). The four raters then coded the remaining transcripts independently. Throughout the coding process, the team met regularly to review coding, and any discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached.

RESULTS

Both adolescents (63%) and caregivers (51%) reported increased confidence in their ability to manage diabetes over the course of the pandemic, with adolescents reporting higher confidence in their self-management than caregivers. Several participants cited use of diabetes devices as an important factor in their self-efficacy. Almost all adolescents (95%) were using CGMs at the time initial interviews were conducted, and 100% were using CGM by the end of the study period. Of the adolescents that reported an increase in confidence in their ability to manage diabetes, 50% were already using an insulin pump as their insulin delivery method, while 38% changed their insulin delivery method during the pandemic. Specifically, several participants initiated insulin pump use, and one started using an smart insulin pen (InPen), a device that can track the timing and amount of insulin administered and can communicate with phone apps that provide bolus calculators and integrate CGM data (Sy et al., 2022). All adolescents who reported a decrease in confidence in their ability to manage diabetes were using insulin injections as their insulin delivery method and made no change over the pandemic. Similarly, adolescents that reported no change in confidence in their ability to manage diabetes also made no change to their insulin delivery; however, 83% were already using insulin pumps. We did not observe a significant change in HbA1c over the study period.

Four themes emerged from the interviews that illustrate how diabetes devices contributed to adolescents’ self-efficacy (Table 2). Over the course of the pandemic, CGMs and insulin pumps helped adolescents to (1) prevent/limit the occurrence of adverse glycemic events (2) receive information about their glucose trends so they could accurately adjust their treatment, and (3) adhere to recommendations regarding glucose monitoring and administering insulin, all of which led to perceived improvements in glucose levels. Additionally, participants, especially caregivers, noted potential (4) downsides to technology that led them to report a lower rating for their confidence in their child’s self-management abilities. Preventing Adverse Glycemic Events

During the pandemic, adjustments in daily routine may have decreased adolescents’ self-monitoring. Research indicates that infrequent blood glucose monitoring is a common issue reported across pediatric populations and is associated with higher HbA1c levels (Helgeson et al., 2011). The COVID-19 pandemic likely exacerbated this problem for adolescents diagnosed with T1DM. However, those using advanced diabetes technology were able to take advantage of its ability to monitor adolescents’ glucose levels and alarm adolescents when glucose readings fall below/above the target range. Alarms help to promote regular glucose monitoring and early recognition and prevention of impending hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia, and subsequent diabetes ketoacidosis events (Bode et al., 2017). As one adolescent explained, “I know if I eat a sandwich and some chips and a coke, I need 20. But the thing is checking it. That’s why I got the monitor on because I know it checks it for me. And so, before, I didn’t because if I felt high, then, I’d be thirsty or whatever, and I’d just wing it.” The alarm component of the technology is advantageous for stressful life circumstances, such as the pandemic, that could impact adolescents’ ability to follow recommendations regarding glucose monitoring. Adolescents believed it contributed to them feeling more confident in their self-management abilities.

Useful Glucose Trend Information

Another benefit of diabetes devices that participants mentioned was the additional information about glucose trends offered by technology. The app allowed them to review data, identify patterns, and make necessary adjustments to their treatment on their own, at a time when diabetes clinic visits were inconsistent. One adolescent on an insulin pump reported, “I have a lot of control over my treatment and within that if every couple of weeks I try to look at my trends and adjust my basal or whatnot, it’s all a fight to flatten that line out.” Assessing glycemic trends and status can lead to better insulin adjustments and improved glycemic control (ElSayed et al., 2023). For some adolescents, the glucose data provided gave them a sense of security and stability, thereby reducing potential worries about diabetes.

Improved Diabetes Management

Several adolescents reported that diabetes technology helped them adhere to recommendations regarding insulin dosing and glucose monitoring during quarantine, leading to improvements in glucose levels and an overall increase in self-efficacy. For some, the COVID-19 pandemic altered their routine, which led adolescents’ glucose levels to fluctuate. As one adolescent explained, “I was at the house, and I was like ‘I’ll do a shot here in a minute,’ and then I’d wait like 30 minutes to do it, then it’d be jacked up. I just got too settled down I guess.” Those with updated diabetes devices were better able to adjust their routine to comply with any changes the pandemic may have brought since they required less effort from the adolescent. On the other hand, more time at home may have improved diabetes management for some families; as one caregiver noted, “I didn’t notice if he was doing it any more or any less. I just don’t feel like we had as many alarms because we were eating more at lunch and dosing at lunch so therefore it was holding him over. And we probably ate dinner earlier too just because I was at home and able to cook it and we were eating at 6 instead of 7.”

Additionally, adolescents who updated their insulin delivery method reported better comfort with new devices (i.e., no longer having to inject insulin as frequently). An adolescent reported, “I did get a new pump, so that really helped me a lot. It’s a pod, so I’m not hooked up to a tube all of the time...now I can go in and change my settings by myself. I can count my carbs and calculate everything, do the ratios, just everything that I need to do.” Updating technology improved many adolescents’ overall quality of life and made them more willing to regularly monitor glucose levels and administer insulin. Moreover, both adolescents and caregivers reported that after switching to an updated form of insulin delivery or CGM, and using the devices consistently, they saw an immediate improvement in their HbA1c, glucose levels, and overall well-being.

Downsides of Technology

Despite the benefits of diabetes technology mentioned, a downside noted by participants was increased family conflict. Some caregivers reported that their adolescent altered their devices in order to prevent caregivers from observing their glucose levels, which decreased caregivers’ trust in their adolescent and increased tension within the caregiver-adolescent relationship. One caregiver said, “Well, he had a CGM Dexcom... and he used it as a manipulation thing. So, he figured out how to make (alerts) stop... he does not want me knowing his glucose levels because I’m on him.” Additionally, CGM and insulin pump alarms were sources of diabetes-related conflict for some families. Caregivers noted that adolescents became reliant on alarms for their diabetes management. Some adolescents fell into the habit of waiting for their device to alert them that their glucose levels were out of range, rather than proactively checking their levels on their own. Many caregivers mentioned feeling less confident in their adolescent’s self-management due to having to repeatedly notify them to respond to alarms.

Another downside to the technology was the fear that the technology would not accurately depict adolescents’ blood glucose levels and the possibility of device failures. One caregiver described, “What if the CGM fails?,” or “What if she has an epic low in the middle of the night and for whatever reason the CGM doesn’t pick it up… I think that’s an underlying fear for every type 1 parent….I mostly trust the technology but sometimes I just need to go prick her finger and make sure that everything’s doing what it says it’s doing.” Participants who recently updated their diabetes devices expressed feeling apprehensive that their health would be compromised due to random CGM or insulin pump failure.

DISCUSSION

Understanding factors that contribute to improving adolescents’ self-efficacy in diabetes management during the COVID-19 pandemic is important for identifying protective factors that may promote better diabetes management and improved health outcomes. The current study highlights the significant role that diabetes technology and devices played in promoting self-efficacy among youth diagnosed with T1DM, especially during stressful life situations, and adds to the literature illustrating the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on youth diagnosed with T1DM and their families (Ferguson et al., 2022; Goethals et al., 2022). Under normal circumstances, adolescents diagnosed with T1DM often struggle to follow their treatment regimens, and changes in routines brought on by the pandemic most likely exacerbated these issues (Hood et al., 2010). Despite this, many adolescents in the current study successfully increased their self-efficacy for self-management responsibilities, which may have served as a protective factor for diabetes outcomes over the course of the pandemic (Whittemore et al., 2010).

This study highlights adolescents’ and maternal caregivers’ perspectives on the protective role diabetes technology can play in facilitating glycemic outcomes. When used correctly and consistently, diabetes devices have the potential to improve glycemic outcomes, including time in range and HbA1c (Laffel et al., 2020). Additionally, studies show that for children diagnosed with T1DM using insulin pumps, the devices can provide more flexibility and contribute to a greater sense of control in their everyday lives, especially during major life events, compared to non-users (Low et al., 2005). This is likely due to the device’s ability to ease the process of insulin administration, which may allow adolescents to be consistent with their regimen, even during stressful times, such as the pandemic. It is important to note, however, that factors other than technology, such as emotional and behavioral self-regulation, also played a role in adolescents’ ability to manage diabetes during the pandemic (Miller et al., 2022).

While findings from the current study support the potential benefits of diabetes technology for adolescents diagnosed with T1DM, its potential drawbacks, such as increased family conflict and diabetes distress were also described. The majority of caregiver–adolescent conflict centers on self-monitoring of blood glucose behaviors (Carroll et al., 2011), and with the ability to monitor their child’s glucose levels remotely, diabetes technology has the potential to increase disagreements within the parent-child relationship. Our findings are in line with those of a qualitative review (Messer et al., 2018) suggesting a lack of trust by caregivers as to whether adolescents were using CGMs appropriately, which may result in caregivers interrogating adolescents about their CGM numbers. Additionally, as more adolescents diagnosed with T1DM become reliant on diabetes technology, many still express doubts surrounding the accuracy and dependability of the devices (Messer et al., 2022). CGM and insulin pump failures can be a source of diabetes distress for adolescents, by disrupting adolescents’ ability to adequately self-manage their diabetes, which in return diminishes their overall quality of life and adversely effects their glycemic outcomes.

Strengths of the current study include the longitudinal nature of the qualitative interviews, allowing for us to understand changes over time. However, while our sample had economic diversity, the majority of adolescents identified as non-Hispanic White, and future studies are needed in diverse samples to determine whether results differ in minoritized racial and ethnic groups.

CONCLUSIONS

Results from the current study highlight the potential of diabetes devices – specifically initiating the use of an insulin pump or smart insulin pen – for adolescents diagnosed with T1DM to promote self-efficacy for diabetes management. Given the role that self-efficacy plays in adaptation to T1DM (Whittemore et al., 2010), adolescents may be encouraged to use diabetes technology that helps them feel more confident in their ability to manage T1DM. Findings from this study add to understanding of the ways adolescents managed diabetes during the COVID-19 pandemic, and results have the potential to help prepare for future disruptions to diabetes management, including man-made or natural disasters.
