Role of LRRK2 in physiological activities, diseases, and therapy
Authors: Zhuoran Wang, Qingfang Li, Lingxi Yan, Mingyu Du, Xiawei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) is a critical target for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and potentially other diseases. LRRK2 is involved in intracellular signaling, immune response, and inflammation, with key roles in both the central nervous system and peripheral tissues. LRRK2 mutations are linked to cellular dysfunction including mitochondrial and neuronal damage and can disrupt signaling pathway balance, thereby contributing to PD and other disorders. Inhibiting LRRK2 kinase activity shows potential for treating PD by correcting cellular imbalances and reducing neuronal damage. However, risks associated with regulating a multifunctional protein must be addressed. Further research on the molecular partners and tissue-specific functions of LRRK2 is essential for developing targeted therapies and improving treatment options for related diseases. This review offers a comprehensive analysis of LRRK2, with a focus on its physiological functions, disease involvement, and emerging therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), a high-molecular-weight and multifunctional protein encoded by the PARK8 gene located on chromosome 12p11.2–q13.1, has a full length of 286,000.[1] As a member of the Roco family of proteins, LRRK2 exhibits a complex structure with multiple functional domains, including armadillo repeat motifs (ARM), ankyrin repeat sequences (ANK), leucine-rich repeat sequences (LRR), Ras-of-complex (ROC), C-terminal of ROC (COR), kinase (KIN), and WD40 domains, thereby demonstrating both kinase and GTPase activities [Figure 1].[1,2] Under normal physiological conditions, LRRK2 exists in both monomeric and dimeric forms; however, under pathological conditions, it aggregates into filamentous structures in a microtubule-dependent manner, particularly when interacting with microtubules.[3,4]

Since its initial discovery in 2004 that LRRK2 gene mutations are a key factor in the development of Parkinson’s disease (PD), research into the relationship between LRRK2 and PD has remained a focal point in the scientific community. Over the past two decades, the association between LRRK2 and PD has been extensively studied. As research has advanced, scientists have uncovered the broader role of LRRK2 in peripheral organs and tissues beyond the central nervous system (CNS). We now understand that LRRK2 is not just a regulator of CNS activity but a key player found throughout multiple organs and systems, where it helps maintain normal physiological function.[5,6] LRRK2 is now known to be widely expressed in various tissues, where it plays a pivotal role in regulating key cellular processes, particularly cell signaling and dynamics. Its function extends beyond the brain, impacting other organs such as the kidneys, lungs, and immune system, with significant alterations observed in its expression.[78910] These findings suggested that LRRK2 may have a vital role in various physiological and pathological processes. LRRK2 is further implicated in regulating intracellular membrane transport and protein breakdown pathways, essential for the healthy operation of neurons.[11,12] Neurons critically depend on precisely regulated intracellular transport mechanisms to efficiently deliver signaling molecules and cellular components to distal axons and dendrites. Dysregulated activity of LRRK2 may disrupt the transport of these intracellular substances, thereby impacting the function of neurons.

In recent years, the in-depth study of the functions and pathological mechanisms of LRRK2 has led to the emergence of novel therapeutic strategies targeting LRRK2, bringing new hope for the treatment of related diseases. However, many gaps and challenges remain in the current research findings that need further exploration. Therefore, this review aims to summarize current research progress on LRRK2, provide valuable references and guidance for future studies, and facilitate further advancements in this field. Specifically, this article will cover the activation mechanism of LRRK2 and factors influencing its activity, its effects on cell function, the mechanisms by which LRRK2 mutations cause diseases, and therapeutic strategies targeting LRRK2. By providing a comprehensive overview of these areas, we aim to elucidate the complex roles of LRRK2 in both physiological and pathological contexts, examine the common pathological pathways shared by various LRRK2-related diseases, and highlight the latest advancements in LRRK2-targeted therapies, emphasizing their potential to mitigate neurodegenerative and other associated disorders. Through this detailed analysis, we hope to offer valuable insights for researchers and clinicians working in the field of LRRK2 biology and related therapeutic development.

Role of LRRK2 in Cellular Regulation: Activation Mechanisms and Functional Consequences

In recent years, a growing body of research has illuminated the role of LRRK2 in modulating downstream signaling pathways through interactions with specific Rabs (Ras-related proteins). For instance, a study probing the molecular structure of the interaction between Rab32 and LRRK2 revealed that the Rab32 subfamily interacts specifically with the ARM domain of LRRK2, indicating that LRRK2 may be an effector of the small guanosine triphosphate (GTP) enzyme in this subfamily [Figure 2].[13] Another finding demonstrates that Rab29 recruits LRRK2 into the trans-Golgi network and significantly enhances its kinase activity [Figure 3].[14] Notably, pathogenic LRRK2 mutants, such as R1441C/G and Y1699C, exhibit enhanced GTP binding, facilitating their recruitment by the Golgi complex and subsequent activation by Rab29 more readily than the wild-type (WT) LRRK2.[14] Multiple studies have reported that Rab29 is a major regulatory factor of LRRK2, responsible for controlling its kinase activity, localization, and phosphorylation of potential biomarkers. Although Rab29 is frequently seen as an activator of LRRK2, the latest research indicates that after Rab29 siRNA knockdown, LRRK2 can still express activity within the cells,[15] implying that Rab29 is not essential for the activation of LRRK2, which is significant for comprehending the role of LRRK2 in cellular function and related diseases.

LRRK2 is a multi-domain protein kinase capable of self-phosphorylation, with its phosphorylation activity primarily dependent on the C-terminal regions of the protein, rather than the N-terminal regions. Research has shown that two key residues in the activation loop of LRRK2 are crucial in regulating its self-phosphorylation process.[3] LRRK2 predominantly exists as a dimer in vivo, with multiple interaction points between each monomer. Interactions have been identified among various regions of LRRK2, including the LRRs, the region preceding the ROC domain, the WD40 domain, and the N-terminal region. These interactions have been confirmed through GST pull-down and co-immunoprecipitation experiments, indicating that the topology of the LRRK2 dimer is intricate and that the interaction between monomers is likely robust. In addition, the LRRK2 complex may incorporate heterologous interacting proteins.[3] For instance, Gloeckner et al[16] discovered that the chaperone protein Hsp90 associates with LRRK2; similarly, Dachsel et al[17] reported a substantial number of potential interactors for LRRK2 when it was expressed in heterologous cell lines. Moreover, a truncated form of LRRK2 lacking the N-terminal region (ROC–COR–kinase–WD40) exhibited high autophosphorylation activity. In contrast, proteins containing only the ROC–COR-kinase or solely the kinase domain were stable when overexpressed but entirely incapable of autophosphorylation.[3,17] Interestingly, it was demonstrated that the LRRK2 kinase domain undergoes trans-autophosphorylation when expressed as a recombinant protein in Escherichia coli. This finding may be attributed to differences in the topology of the protein compared to its mammalian counterpart, possibly due to the presence of domains outside the kinase region or variations in post-translational modifications that occur in mammalian cells. The formation of dimers is a common phenomenon among protein kinases. In several serine-threonine kinases, such as mitogen-activated protein kinases, p38, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and their respective upstream kinases, allosteric regulation is facilitated through the process of homodimerization.

The fundamental cellular functions of LRRK2 are based on its interactions with specific Rabs, such as Rab32 and Rab29. These interactions not only modulate the kinase activity, localization, and phosphorylation of LRRK2 but also influence intracellular material transport and signal transduction by interacting with intracellular membrane structures such as the Golgi complex and lysosomes. As a major regulatory factor of LRRK2, Rab29 recruits LRRK2 into the trans-Golgi network and significantly enhances its kinase activity. In addition, the ability of LRRK2 to self-activate on the cell membrane structure without the need for endogenous Rab29 increases the complexity of its functions. The phosphorylation activity of LRRK2 primarily depends on its C-terminal regions, with two key residues in the activation loop playing a crucial role in regulating its self-phosphorylation process.

Impact of Physiological and Mutant LRRK2 on Cellular Function

LRRK2 holds a pivotal position in cellular signaling pathways. Although the precise role of LRRK2 is still being further explored in various tissues and cell types, existing research results have revealed its multifunctionality in cell biology. Current research indicates that LRRK2 contributes to numerous biological processes through its kinase activity and GTPase activity. These processes include membrane transport, maintenance of lysosomal morphology, and regulation of mitochondrial function. LRRK2 is also implicated in the intracellular autophagy pathway under physiological conditions and can influence specific membrane microdomains, demonstrating its pivotal role in intracellular signaling.[1213181920]

Membrane transport

Under normal physiological conditions, WT LRRK2 plays a crucial role in regulating the activity of various Rabs through direct interaction and phosphorylation. These Rabs are key regulatory factors in the intracellular transport system, including endocytic transport, which is essential for maintaining cellular function.[21222324] Specifically, LRRK2 modulates multiple important molecules by phosphorylating Rabs, such as the adaptive protein AP2 complex μ subunit AP2M1. AP2M1 is a core component in vesicle-mediated endocytosis, regulating vesicle formation and cargo selection. Latest research has revealed the low-temperature electron microscopy structure of LRRK2 kinase and GTPase domains, showing that they exhibit a closed conformation and can bind to microtubules.[25] Although LRRK1, which is closely related to the function of LRRK2, is structurally similar, it was found that LRRK1 does not interact with microtubules.[26]

In contrast, mutations in LRRK2 are closely linked to abnormalities in these transport pathways. LRRK2 mutations associated with PD disrupt the normal regulation of Rabs, leading to aberrant function of molecules such as AP2M1. This disruption can impair the normal process of endocytosis and may be associated with the development of PD.[27] A series of key amino acids mediating microtubule binding has been identified in the GTPase structure of LRRK2, and their mutations can damage microtubule binding function but do not affect the kinase activity of LRRK2.[28] G2019S LRRK2 increases endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in dopamine neurons, resulting in eventual cell death in PD. The mutation is found to cause excessive ER stress by inducing interaction between THBS1 and TGF-β1.[29]

Maintenance of lysosomal morphology

LRRK2 negatively regulates the microphthalmia family (MiT-TFE) transcription factor, inhibits the expression of a variety of mRNA encoding lysosomal proteins, negatively regulates the content of many lysosomal proteins, and inhibits the degradation activity of lysosomal proteins in human and mouse macrophages.[30] Given the central role of LRRK2 and lysosomal biology in PD, these findings may be relevant to disease pathology and highlight the interaction between organelles in this model.[31] LRRK2, together with the PD-related gene Rab7L1, functions in evolutionarily conserved genetic modules in different cellular contexts. In Caenorhabditis neurons, homologs of LRRK2 and Rab7L1 act synergistically to regulate axon elongation. The AP-3 complex, which is considered a physiological downstream effector of LRRK2 and Rab7L1, is a regulator of axon morphology and intracellular protein trafficking to the lysosomal compartment. Other cell-based studies have implicated LRRK2 in the intracellular trafficking of AP-3 complex-associated lysosomal membrane proteins. In mice, deficiency of Rab7L1 or LRRK2 in the absence of clear CNS pathology results in an apparent age-related lysosomal defect in renal proximal tubular cells.[32] In addition, LRRK2-mediated phosphorylation of Rab35, Rab10, and Rab8 regulates lysosomal function by affecting the recruitment of the motor protein JIP4 onto the lysosomal membrane. JIP4 facilitates the generation of LAMP1-negative tubules, aiding the release of lysosomal contents and thereby influencing cellular metabolic pathways.[33] Transcription factor EB (TFEB) regulates lysosome function. Its activation relies on CD38 and LRRK2, which form a complex on the cell membrane of B lymphocytes and macrophages and regulate the function of lysosomes in these two types of cells.[34]

In the Rab4 cell compartment, which is repaired by active Rab8A,[35] the conjugation of ATG8 to single membranes (CASM)–LRRK2 pathway is considered to be one of the cellular defense systems that respond to lysosomal stress and maintain its homeostasis.[36] A complex and multifaceted relationship exists between LRRK2 and the glucocerebrosidase gene (GBA), in which they affect the structure and function of lysosomes in the process of interaction.[37] LRRK2, when directed to lysosomes and early endosomes, triggers its autophosphorylation and directly phosphorylates its substrates Rab10 and Rab12. Rab10 was found only on perinuclear lysosomes, whereas pRab12 was found on both peripheral and perinuclear lysosomes, suggesting that lysosomal localization may influence the LRRK2-dependent Rab phosphorylation process.[38] The upstream molecule Rab29 promotes the lysosomal recruitment of LRRK2. Rab8A and Rab10 specifically accumulated on overloaded lysosomes that depend on LRRK2 phosphorylation. The regulation of lysosomal function through LRRK2-mediated phosphorylation of Rab35 and Rab10 is affected by these mutations, potentially impacting cellular metabolic pathways.[33] Rab7l1-mediated targeting of LRRK2 by lysosomes alleviated stress-induced lysosomal enlargement and promoted lysosomal secretion, while LRRK2-stabilized Rab8 inhibited stress-induced lysosomal enlargement and Rab10 promoted lysosomal secretion.[39]

LRRK2 maintains neuronal homeostasis by regulating the clearance of metabolic waste and degradation of damaged organelles within neurons.[40,41] Mutations in LRRK2 induce modest but significant changes in lysosomal morphology and acidification, as well as reduce basal autophagic flux compared with WT neurons. These changes are associated with the accumulation of detergent-insoluble α-synuclein and increased release from neurons, independent of nuclear protein content.[42] The Drosophila LRRK2 homolog, dLRRK2, regulates gliomolysosome morphology and dopamine (DA) neurodegeneration in a kinase activity-dependent manner. Reducing the expression of dLRRK2 in glial cells can increase the size and decrease the number of lysosomes. The specific mechanism is that the expanded lysosomes lacking LRRK2/dLRRK are mistakenly acidified. In microglia, LRRK2 mediates lysosomal membrane penetration, and LRRK2/dLRRK deficiency induces glial apoptosis and is involved in DA neurodegeneration and dyskinesia.[43] LRRK2 mediates age-dependent changes in lysosomal autophagy in renal cells. With the increase in age of the mouse model, lipofuscin in renal cells accumulated, and the content of autophagy damage markers increased[44]; this phenomenon has also been demonstrated in the nematode model.[45] The G2019S LRRK2-mediated endolysosomal transport defect can be repaired by the Rab11–Rabin8–Rab8A cascade. In some experiments, Rab8A knockdown was used to mimic the G2019S LRRK2 mutation-mediated endolysosomal transport defect. Knockdown of LRRK2 or Rab8A resulted in epidermal growth factor (EGF) accumulation in the Rab4 compartment, and G2019S LRRK2 resulted in insufficient epidermal growth factor receptor recycling. After active Rab7A expression, the mistargeting of EGF into the Rab4 compartment and the defective endocytic recycling mediated by G2019S LRRK2 or Rab8A knockdown were restored. Dominant negative Rab7A leads to defects in EGF accumulation and endocytic recycling.

Mitochondrial function

Under normal physiological conditions, WT LRRK2 plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial function and morphology, which are essential for cellular material and energy metabolism. It regulates mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy, ensuring the proper removal of damaged mitochondria and preserving cellular homeostasis.[464748] Interestingly, LRRK2 can induce autophagy in mitochondria through the activation and recruitment of dynamin-like protein 1 (DLP1) molecules or hyperphosphorylation of certain molecules, such as tau and Bcl-2.[4950515253] Thus, the effect of LRRK2 on mitophagy may be bidirectional: While preventing dysfunctional mitophagy, it partially enhances normal mitophagy damage. The changes in mitochondrial structure and ultrastructure due to the changes in some mitochondrial functions (such as protein synthesis) may lead to the appearance of polymorphic mitochondria in LRRK2 mutant cells, which may be one of the important characteristics of LRRK2 mutation at the non-genetic level.[52,54,55] Alterations in mitochondrial structure usually include the presence of cristae swelling, disorganization, and severely condensed mitochondria.[53,56] The dysfunction of mitochondria is accompanied by abnormal Ca2+ transport structures in biofilms, and mitochondrial calcium overload is one of the important causes of their damage.[575859]

In contrast, LRRK2 mutations associated with pathological changes typically result in abnormalities in mitochondrial function and morphology. These abnormalities include reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, decreased rates of oxidative phosphorylation, reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, and impaired mitophagy, which are often accompanied by abnormal mitochondrial morphology such as mitochondrial fusion and fragmentation.[464748]

Some differences in the pathological changes of mitochondria exist between idiopathic PD (iPD) and LRRK2-associated Parkinson’s disease (LRRK2-PD). Mitochondrial changes are of great significance in the differentiation of LRRK2-PD.[60] LRRK2 mutations that may cause mitochondrial changes mainly include G2019S and R1441G.[47,61]

The mitochondrial abnormalities caused by LRRK2 mutations eventually lead to the degeneration and apoptosis of some cells by inhibiting autophagy and enhancing oxidative stress.[62,63] Mitochondrial dysfunction is characterized by the following: Mutations in several genes associated with LRRK2 were shown to lead to mitochondrial loss and decreased energy synthesis. In cells with mutated genes, ATP reduction, mitochondrial enzyme activity reduction, and accumulation of intermediates of oxidative metabolism were found.[64] In addition, autophagy of damaged mitochondria is affected, causing intracellular accumulation of damaged mitochondria. Miro, a mitochondrial outer membrane protein that immobilizes mitochondria to microtubule motors, is removed to prevent mitochondrial movement as an early step in the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria. LRRK2 forms a complex with Miro to promote its hydrolysis. However, the LRRK2 G2019S mutation impairs this function and delays the activation of the mitophagy mechanism.[65]

Actin function and microglial activation

LRRK2 is crucial for the structural integrity and function of neurons, not only through its kinase domain function, but also through its influence on actin-based cytoskeletal tissue. The molecular interactions and physiological functions of LRRK2 are closely related to actin-based cytoskeletal tissue.[66] Actin is a key component of neuronal development and an important structure for maintaining normal neural function. This suggests that LRRK2 may maintain the structural integrity and function of neurons through a potential special pathway. LRRK2 also maintains neuronal homeostasis by regulating the clearance of metabolic waste and degradation of damaged organelles within neurons[40,41] while also modulating microglial activation to participate in neuroinflammatory processes.[67]

The G2019S LRRK2 mutation may have a negative effect on the role of actin in maintaining the stable morphology and functional homeostasis of neurons. In addition, such mutations might impair the ability of LRRK2 to modulate microglial activation and neuroinflammatory responses. These combined effects could lead to neuronal dysfunction and contribute to neurodegenerative diseases.

Regulation of oxidative stress and apoptosis

LRRK2 exhibits strong protective effects in cellular responses to oxidative stress, such as that induced by H2O2, which is consistent with its potential anti-inflammatory properties.[68] As the target gene of interferon-γ (IFN-γ), LRRK2 affects the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases, including Crohn’s disease (CD);[69] especially, the high kinase activity of G2019S LRRK2 promotes tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Its release enhances the influence of LRRK2 on the inflammatory process.[70] Moreover, silencing LRRK2 can increase the expression of genes and proteins associated with cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in T cells by inhibiting the activation of the JNK signaling pathway. These effects induce T-cell cycle arrest and apoptosis and diminish T-cell migration and proliferation, thereby conferring beneficial modulation on T-cell function in thyroid cancer.[71] Such findings imply that inhibiting LRRK2 may hold therapeutic potential for regulating T-cell activity. In addition, both in vivo and in vitro models of traumatic brain injury have been shown to significantly elevate the expression of LRRK2 and hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) in neurons. Experimental evidence indicates that HIF-1α directly interacts with the promoter region of LRRK2, thereby augmenting its expression and exacerbating neuronal cell death in damaged neurons.[33,72]

LRRK2 G2019S mutation may impair antioxidant response in neural cells, which is related to abnormal oxidative stress response induced by NRF-2 and glutathione redox reaction.[73] This could disrupt its crucial roles in maintaining neuronal structural integrity and function.

Mechanisms Underlying Tissue-Specific Expression and Functional Regulation of LRRK2

LRRK2 exhibits a remarkably tissue-specific expression pattern, predominantly enriched in parenchymal and immune cells of the brain, liver, kidneys, and lungs, while showing relatively lower expression levels in muscle and cardiac tissues.[74] This differential expression pattern enables LRRK2 to perform similar fundamental biological functions (such as regulating autophagy and inflammatory responses) across different tissues while demonstrating distinct organ-specific mechanisms of action.

As the primary site of LRRK2 expression, the CNS has the most clearly defined regulatory mechanisms. Given that we have already thoroughly discussed the role of LRRK2 in the CNS in the previous sections, we will not elaborate further here. This functional specificity stems from the brain’s unique regulatory network: Neuron-specific chromatin-accessible regions drive its transcriptional expression, microRNAs (miRNA) such as miR-205 regulate mRNA stability by binding to the 3′-UTR,[75] and kinases and phosphatases dynamically modulate phosphorylation at Ser910/935, influencing its interaction with 14-3-3 proteins and subcellular localization.[76] The JNK signaling pathway serves as a critical downstream effector, mediating the regulatory role of LRRK2 in mitochondrial quality control.[77]

In hepatic tissue, LRRK2 participates in metabolic regulation through the JNK signaling pathway and modulates Kupffer cell activation via the IFN-γ/STAT1 axis, thereby influencing hepatocyte apoptosis and inflammatory responses.[78] This regulatory mechanism shares similarities with the nervous system, both involving mediation by the JNK signaling pathway.[79,80] The expression pattern of LRRK2 in pulmonary tissue resembles that in the liver, where it primarily regulates localized inflammatory responses through alveolar macrophages. However, its unique function lies in maintaining pulmonary immune barrier function and defending against respiratory infections. Of particular significance, alveolar type II epithelial cells (AT2 cells) represent another important site of LRRK2 expression in the lung tissue. Similar to the liver, IFN-γ upregulates LRRK2 expression in pulmonary macrophages through signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1).[79] Nevertheless, the specific regulatory mechanisms governing LRRK2 in AT2 cells remain to be elucidated.

In contrast, research on the physiological functions of LRRK2 in renal tissue remains relatively limited. Current evidence suggests that it may protect against acute kidney injury by maintaining mitochondrial function.[81] Given the regulatory role of LRRK2 in lysosomal function, it is plausible that modulating the lysosomal pathway to facilitate the clearance of abnormal substances in renal tubular epithelial cells may represent another potential function of LRRK2, though this aspect currently remains understudied. Clinical observations have revealed a significant correlation between LRRK2 levels in urinary exosomes and PD.[82] This secretion is regulated by 14-3-3[83] and offers new insights into the non-invasive diagnosis of LRRK2-related disorders. The mechanism by which collecting duct epithelial cells secrete LRRK2 via exosomes may become an important indicator for future disease monitoring.

Although some regulatory pathways, such as JNK and IFN-γ/STAT1, are shared among tissues, the existence of organ-specific regulatory mechanisms suggests that LRRK2 contributes to the pathogenesis of various diseases through distinct regulatory networks. Future research should prioritize addressing the following key questions: (1) precise regulatory mechanisms of LRRK2 in AT2 cells; (2) elucidation of renal physiological functions of LRRK2; (3) molecular basis of tissue-specific regulatory networks; and (4) development of organ-specific targeted therapeutic strategies. Advancements in these research areas will significantly enhance our understanding of the biological functions of LRRK2 and facilitate precision medicine for related diseases.

The elevated level of LRRK2 in PD and CD pathology may not only become a biomarker for increased PD risk [Table 1], but also a potential therapeutic target, especially in the interaction between LRRK2 and immune cell function. PD

The cumulative study of the LRRK2 gene reveals its key role in various diseases, especially in PD and CD.[84] In the nervous system, LRRK2 influences the pathological processes, including mechanisms related to inflammation and damage to dopaminergic neurons.[85] Under inflammatory conditions or following infection, IFN-γ upregulates the expression of LRRK2. This response may be linked to changes in LRRK2 activity.[69] Especially in the presence of the most common pathogenic mutation LRRK2 G2019S, the IFN-γ generated by infection may exacerbate the phosphorylation inhibition of the AKT signaling pathway and the activation of NFAT (activated T-cell nuclear factor), which may increase the sensitivity of neurons to immune stress.[86] Patients with PD and LRRK2 mutations often exhibit the aggregation of α-synaptic (α-syn) nuclear proteins and tau proteins, which is closely related to abnormal deposition and neurodegeneration.[87888990] G2019S mutations significantly reduce the dynamic transport capacity of autophagosomes within neurons in a kinase-dependent manner, which may lead to the accumulation of metabolic waste and harmful proteins, thereby causing cellular dysfunction.[91] Therefore, G2019S mutations may indirectly promote the neuropathological progression of PD by damaging autophagosome function. Furthermore, LRRK2 mutations disrupt the normal tetrameric structure of endogenous factor α-syn nucleoproteins, which play a crucial role in regulating intracellular homeostasis.[92,93] The abnormal aggregation of α-syn nucleoproteins is not only related to the formation of Lewy bodies, but also one of the main pathological features observed in PD.[19,87,94] Microglia recognize and transfer α-syn fibrils to immature microglia via a cellular network. This process leads to the clearance of α-syn and reduces inflammation in the CNS. However, in G2019S LRRK2 cells, this response is impaired.[95] In PD models, LRRK2 mutations can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, dysfunction of biofilm transport, oxidative stress disorder, abnormal cellular signaling pathways, and dysregulation of autophagy processes.[12,68,70,71,77,91,96,97] These effects may be related to the formation of neurodegenerative features in PD.

Future research directions on the mechanisms of LRRK2 in PD include elucidating the specific substrates that are excessively phosphorylated by LRRK2 mutations and their impact on downstream signaling pathways, particularly those involved in neuronal survival, synaptic function, and axonal transport. Future research will use a multifaceted approach to gain a deeper understanding of the role of LRRK2 in PD, including identifying specific substrates, comprehending the impact on cellular pathways, and developing targeted therapeutic strategies. By advancing our understanding of the role of LRRK2 in PD, we can pave the way for the development of more effective treatments and improved patient outcomes.

CD

In immune cells, LRRK2 has been shown to regulate inflammatory responses, which may help explain why abnormal LRRK2 function is also associated with the development of other inflammatory diseases, such as CD. This mechanism is reflected in type 1 response (T1R) in patients with leprosy, suggesting that LRRK2 is involved in the occurrence of T1R by regulating the pathological pro-inflammatory circulation [Figure 4].[98]

The importance of LRRK2 and nucleotide binding oligomerization domain 2 (NOD2), two genetic susceptibility factors, has increasingly been confirmed through recent discoveries that highlight the immune regulatory role of LRRK2. They collectively affect Paneth cells, which are specialized cells located in the intestine responsible for producing and secreting lysozyme into the crypt space of the intestine, maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier.

LRRK2 plays a multifaceted role in the pathogenesis of CD. Its dysfunction can disrupt immune regulation in the gut, interfering with the signaling pathways of immune cells and thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses. In addition, LRRK2 may compromise the function of Paneth cells, leading to reduced secretion of antimicrobial peptides and weakening the integrity of the intestinal barrier. By modulating the expression of cytoskeletal and tight junction proteins, LRRK2 can further damage the intestinal epithelial cells. These alterations can trigger an imbalance in the gut microbiota, contributing to dysbiosis and worsening inflammation. Ultimately, the abnormal activity of LRRK2 may perpetuate the chronic nature of the inflammatory response, sustaining ongoing intestinal inflammation. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms drive the development and progression of CD.

The pathogenic mechanisms of CD related to LRRK2 can be connected to the relationships between other gastrointestinal diseases and LRRK2. Although LRRK2 has been studied to some extent in relation to CD, its role in ulcerative colitis (UC) still needs further exploration. UC is also an important type of chronic colitis (inflammatory bowel disease [IBD]) and shares some common pathogenic mechanisms with CD. Meanwhile, the relationship between LRRK2 and intestinal immunity may also be an important factor leading to intestinal inflammation. A lack of research remains in this area, which is an important issue that needs to be addressed urgently.

Leprosy

LRRK2 has been identified as a susceptibility gene for leprosy in Han populations.[99] Mutations in the LRRK2 gene are implicated in the immune control of intracellular Mycobacterium leprae infection through the NOD2-mediated signaling pathway. The interplay between LRRK2 and NOD2 can augment human susceptibility to Mycobacterium leprae, with this susceptibility often clustering in families. In LRRK2 knockout cells infected with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), apoptosis was significantly higher compared to WT cells. Conversely, apoptosis in cells expressing the LRRK2 R1398H mutation was significantly reduced, suggesting that specific LRRK2 mutations (such as R1398H and R1628P) play a key role in the interaction between LRRK2 and NOD2.[100101102] LRRK2 mutations wreak havoc on the immune system, diminishing its capacity to fend off Mycobacterium leprae. These genetic alterations sabotage the NOD2 signaling pathway, a critical mechanism for detecting bacterial elements and triggering immune defenses. Moreover, specific LRRK2 mutations, such as R1398H, curb apoptosis, thereby enabling the bacteria to sidestep immune detection and linger within host cells. Often, leprosy susceptibility tied to LRRK2 mutations crops up in family clusters, underscoring the hereditary nature of this vulnerability.

The pathogenesis of leprosy may be significantly linked to the inflammation mechanisms mediated by LRRK2. At the same time, the infection of Mycobacterium leprae and its pathogenic processes within the body may also have important associations with the immune responses mediated by LRRK2. Currently, there are relatively few studies on the pathogenic mechanisms of LRRK2 related to leprosy and its impact on prognosis. Research in this direction holds great potential.

Pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary tuberculosis

In a bleomycin-induced mouse pulmonary fibrosis model, a significant decrease in LRRK2 expression in mouse alveolar type II epithelial cells is closely related to the exacerbation of lung function impairment. Abnormal epithelial cells lacking LRRK2 exhibit accelerated cell aging and impaired autophagy, thereby promoting the aggregation of fibrotic cells and macrophage-related profibrotic responses.[103] A statistical meta-analysis of human gene expression in response to latent and active pulmonary tuberculosis, conducted across nine datasets, revealed a significant decrease in LRRK2 expression levels in patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis.[104] Loss of LRRK2 in macrophages results in elevated basal levels of IFNs and IFN-stimulated genes, accompanied by reduced biological responsiveness to IFN. These effects are linked to LRRK2-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, which ultimately impairs immune function and hinders Mycobacterium tuberculosis clearance. Consequently, the prognosis for pulmonary tuberculosis remains poor.[105]

Given the significant association between LRRK2 and immunity as well as chronic inflammation, there is a potential link between lung tissue and inflammation and immune surveillance-related diseases in terms of the occurrence and prognosis of LRRK2. However, there is currently a lack of research on other chronic inflammation-related lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Studies on the intrinsic connections between these diseases and LRRK2 will further our understanding of the mechanisms of LRRK2 in the lung tissue and its impact on lung physiological functions. Moreover, this will also help us improve the treatment plans for these diseases.

Occurrence of tumor

LRRK2 also plays a vital role in the occurrence, development, and treatment response of various cancers. Different cancer types exhibit different biological phenomena related to the LRRK2 gene status. Numerous studies have identified associations between LRRK2 and cancers such as skin cancer, ovarian cancer, lung cancer, and MM.[106107108109110] In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), reduced or absent LRRK2 expression correlates with cancer progression and promotes apoptosis in cells, enhancing immune response and cellular autophagy. Low or absent LRRK2 expression may disrupt immune surveillance, allowing cancer cells to evade immune detection, survive, and proliferate.

In contrast, in skin and ovarian cancers, high LRRK2 expression is linked to advanced cancer stages or poor prognosis, suggesting that LRRK2 functions differently in these tissues compared to LUAD. In ovarian cancer, it has been found that inhibiting LRRK2 or its absence increases the sensitivity of cancer cells to olapanib, indicating that the regulation of LRRK2 activity may be a potential cancer treatment strategy. In MM, although LRRK2 somatic mutations and truncation have been observed in a few cases, LRRK2 expression is usually not detected or shown to be downregulated in most primary samples and cell lines, indicating that LRRK2 may play an under-recognized role as a tumor suppressor in MM. At the same time, LRRK2 mutations exhibit different impacts on the risk and prognosis of low-grade gliomas and skin melanoma.[109] Elevated LRRK2 levels appear to be associated with an increased risk of low-grade gliomas, while in skin melanoma, high LRRK2 expression correlates with poorer survival. This highlights the diverse biological roles that LRRK2 may play across different cancer types.

Interestingly, when LRRK2 expression is evaluated as a biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitors and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in metastatic melanoma, a multigene analysis identified LRRK2 as an irAE marker. Approximately 90% of patients with abnormal LRRK2 expression did not experience irAEs, which contrasts with prior research.[111] Furthermore, LRRK2 has been integrated into the programmed cell death index for renal clear cell carcinoma (CCRCC), where it has shown promise in prognostic analysis. High LRRK2 expression is generally associated with better prognosis in CCRCC, while LRRK2 knockdown significantly reduces cell proliferation in CCRCC tissues.[112,113]

A trial protocol combining sorafenib and radiation therapy in patients with stage IV liver cancer identified a novel LRRK2 K1316N mutation that exhibits sensitivity to radiation. Cancer cells harboring this mutation showed increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide and H2O2, leading to enhanced cellular damage and apoptosis.[114]

LRRK2 exhibits diverse roles in different tumors due to its interactions with various factors. Its function is influenced by cell type and tissue-specific environments, which can either promote or inhibit tumor progression. Genetic mutations in LRRK2 can alter its activity, leading to different outcomes depending on the tumor type. In addition, the involvement of LRRK2 in multiple signaling pathways implies that its effects can vary based on the specific pathways activated in different cancers. The dynamic tumor microenvironment and disease stage also impact how LRRK2 contributes to cancer development. These multifaceted influences highlight the complexity of the role of LRRK2 in oncology and underscore the importance of personalized approaches in cancer treatment.

Overall, LRRK2 function exhibits complex conditional dependencies in different types of cancer, and its specific mechanism still needs further investigation. These research findings not only reveal the multifaceted role of LRRK2 in tumor biology but also provide new directions for possible future cancer treatments.

Common pathogenic mechanisms of LRRK2-related diseases

Abnormal immune regulation and mitochondrial dysfunction mediated by LRRK2 are common pathogenic mechanisms in PD, CD, and some cases of pulmonary tuberculosis.[104,115,116] These mechanisms not only cause neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative changes in the nervous system but also lead to chronic inflammation and tissue damage in the digestive and respiratory systems.[117,118] Specific LRRK2 variants, such as M2397T, are associated with an increased risk of both PD and CD, indicating a potential shared inflammatory mechanism between these two seemingly unrelated conditions. Furthermore, certain amino acid variations in LRRK2 and NOD2 have been linked to early-onset leprosy.[119,120] Variants of LRRK2 associated with leprosy are connected to Type 1 Reaction (T1R), with LRRK2 potentially playing a role in T1R through its regulation of a pathological pro-inflammatory cycle. This inflammatory mechanism may also be relevant to LRRK2-PD and CD.[121122123]

Autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction are important mechanisms linking neurological diseases such as PD and pulmonary fibrosis.[103,124] In PD, LRRK2 mutations can lead to impaired autophagosome transport and lysosomal dysfunction, affecting the degradation of intracellular proteins and metabolic waste, which in turn leads to abnormal aggregation of proteins such as α-syn and the formation of Lewy bodies. In pulmonary fibrosis, decreased LRRK2 expression similarly affects autophagy and lysosomal function, causing obstacles in intracellular material degradation and circulation, promoting fibroblast aggregation and macrophage-related profibrotic reactions, and ultimately leading to pulmonary tissue fibrosis.[103]

Moreover, dysfunction of the lysosomal system further leads to protein homeostasis imbalance, which is a common pathogenic mechanism between PD and various cancers, such as skin cancer, ovarian cancer, and lung cancer. In PD, lysosomal dysfunction caused by LRRK2 mutations affects normal protein degradation, leading to the aggregation of harmful proteins. In various cancers, abnormal expression or mutations of LRRK2 may affect its interactions with tumor suppressor genes and cell cycle regulatory proteins, thereby affecting intracellular signaling and cellular functions, promoting the occurrence and development of tumors.[125] Disruption of signaling pathways caused by LRRK2 through interference with membrane transport is also a common pathogenic mechanism between PD and cancer.[34] In PD, changes in the kinase activity of LRRK2 affect the activation or inhibition of downstream signaling pathways, leading to cellular signaling disorders and affecting cellular growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolism. In cancer, abnormal expression or mutations of LRRK2 affect signaling pathways related to cell proliferation, apoptosis inhibition, and invasion and metastasis, promoting the occurrence and development of tumors.[126,127] Some signaling pathways associated with cancer are also believed to be involved in the pathogenesis of CD, such as the signaling mechanisms downstream of CD38, which indicates that the impact of LRRK2 on signaling pathways is a common mechanism by which it causes disease.[34]

Preclinical and Clinical Applications of Drugs Targeting LRRK2

Kinase inhibitors that can stabilize the open conformation reduce the interference from LRRK2 to microtubules, reduce the formation of LRRK2 filaments, and provide potential strategies for the treatment of PD. In general, the impact of LRRK2 deletion or reduced activity on the human body is significantly less severe than that of mutations that enhance LRRK2 activity. This insight provides a crucial foundation for the development of LRRK2 inhibitor drugs as therapeutic options.[128]

LRRK2-IN-1 was the first inhibitor with appropriate selectivity; however, its application was limited by poor brain permeability.[135] To address this problem, inhibitors with improved brain permeability, such as HG-102-102-01, have been developed. MLi-2 and ptfe-360 have also garnered attention owing to their excellent affinity and selectivity.[136] Although no type II selective inhibitors have been used in clinical trials, type II inhibitors, such as ponatinib, GZD-824, and rebastinib, which have a wide range of kinase target profiles, exhibit high affinity for LRRK2.[137,138] LRRK2 kinase inhibitors have shown beneficial effects in neuroinflammation models, as they can alleviate the increased TLR2 specificity α-syn activity of LRRK2 and the neuroinflammatory response of microglia prevent neuronal degeneration, slow down motor dysfunction, and dopaminergic neuronal degeneration.[139] At the biochemical level, DNL151 specifically binds to the ATP-binding pocket of the LRRK2 kinase domain. This interaction disrupts the ability of LRRK2 to bind ATP, inhibiting both its autophosphorylation and the phosphorylation of other Rabs.[140]

LRRK2 inhibitor 68 has demonstrated significant in vitro activity by reducing the interaction between lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and LRRK2, thereby reducing phosphorylation of MAPK and inhibitor κB-α (IkB-α). This process leads to a decrease in the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. Therefore, inhibitor 68 demonstrates potential therapeutic value in LRRK2-related immune diseases.[141] Further research has revealed the role of 68 as the first LRRK2 GTP-binding inhibitor, which can effectively inhibit the GTP-binding ability and kinase activity of LRRK2, thereby reducing the pathological changes related to neurodegenerative diseases. However, a significant challenge remains: the limited efficacy of this inhibitor in vivo, particularly due to its poor brain penetration, which hampers its ability to achieve the desired therapeutic effect in the CNS.[26,142]

Some specific phosphorylation sites within LRRK2 (Ser910, Ser935, Ser955, and Ser973) are closely related to the function of LRRK2. Experiments have shown that LRRK2 binds 14-3-3 protein isomers by phosphorylating Ser910 and Ser935. Mutations that inhibit LRRK2 from interacting with Rab29 or GTP significantly inhibit phosphorylation of these important biomarker phosphorylation sites.[14,76,143] The dephosphorylation of these sites has been considered a triggering factor for their ubiquitination and subsequent degradation in recent years. This mechanism may explain why LRRK2 gene knockout, kinase inactivation mutations, or loss of functional phenotype after treatment with kinase inhibitors occur in the peripheral tissues of rodents and primates.[144]

New drugs targeting mitochondrial deformation and functional inhibition, targeting LRRK2 mutations or other sites of action, are constantly being discovered. Genipin is an uncoupling protein inhibitor that restores mitochondrial membrane potential and oxygen use efficiency by preventing mitochondrial uncoupling induced by the LRRK2 G2019S mutation.[145] Edaravone is a potent antioxidant capable of significantly alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis caused by the LRRK2 G2385R mutation. Edaravone protects mitochondria by reducing oxidative stress and significantly improves mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis.[146] Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction caused by the LRRK2 G2019S mutation through multiple mechanisms and is a promising candidate for future neuroprotective trials.[147] Diapocynin, a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant, was able to significantly prevent early PD symptoms caused by the LRRK2 R1441G mutation.[148] Mito-apocynin, an antioxidant derivative that specifically targets mitochondria, prevents mitochondrial dysfunction caused by the LRRK2 R1441G mutation by reducing oxidative stress within mitochondria, showing a significant improvement in mitochondrial function and neuronal health.[149]

To design alternative strategies that can generate therapeutic effects against LRRK2, protein hydrolysis-targeted chimeras (PROTACs) targeting LRRK2 have been developed. This type of strategy directly involves the degradation of corresponding pathological proteins, rather than simply inhibiting their kinase activity. Under this strategy, XL01126 was found to be an effective LRRK2-degrading agent that can rapidly and effectively degrade LRRK2 in various cell lines. Importantly, XL01126 shows a bioavailability for export, which means it can enter the organism orally. Moreover, whether administered orally or parenterally, XL01126 can penetrate the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which is crucial for the treatment of CNS-related diseases.[150] LRRK2 antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) can reduce LRRK2 protein levels and prevent fibrillar induction and the formation of α-syn inclusion bodies to avoid adverse phenotypes associated with loss of peripheral expression of LRRK2. The decrease in endogenous LRRK2 is expected to decrease the formation of α-syn inclusion bodies, suggesting that LRRK2 ASOs serve as a strategy for treating PD.[151]

Recent research has made significant progress in understanding the structure and function of the LRRK2 protein, particularly its relationship with microtubules and its role in the pathogenesis of PD. Multiple LRRK2 kinase inhibitors have been identified and demonstrated to be effective in the treatment of PD and other neurological disorders by inhibiting LRRK2 activity. Among them, some inhibitors have entered the clinical trial stage, indicating that the scientific community is struggling to translate laboratory research results into therapeutic applications.

By correcting monogenic mutations in the LRRK2, the abnormal depletion of dopaminergic neurons and its subsequent effects can be significantly attenuated. To achieve this, multiple gene therapy tools have been investigated, including zinc finger nucleases, helper-dependent adenoviral vectors (HDAdV), and bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) DNA.[152] Specifically, BAC-DNA–mediated correction, as a gene-editing therapy, has the potential to reverse cellular dysfunction and restore key pathways, including autophagy, the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, and vesicular trafficking pathways.[153]

Despite significant progress in developing LRRK2 inhibitors for PD and related disorders, several limitations hinder their clinical efficacy. A major challenge is poor BBB penetration, as seen with early inhibitors such as LRRK2-IN-1,[135] limiting their CNS bioavailability. In addition, many inhibitors lack sufficient selectivity, leading to off-target effects,[134] while long-term suppression of LRRK2 raises concerns about peripheral organ toxicity, albeit milder than gain-of-function mutations. Current biomarkers also require refinement for better patient stratification.[154]

Future drug development must prioritize enhanced brain delivery, improved selectivity, and disease-modifying effects. Strategies include optimizing small-molecule properties,[130] developing allosteric or dual kinase/GTPase inhibitors,[35] and using PROTACs for targeted LRRK2 degradation.[150] Combining LRRK2 inhibitors with mitochondrial protectants or Rab pathway modulators may synergistically address neurodegeneration. Personalized approaches guided by genetic profiling and dynamic biomarkers will be critical, alongside advanced models to predict clinical outcomes. Ultimately, next-generation therapies must balance efficacy, safety, and CNS accessibility to transform PD treatment from symptomatic relief to disease modification.

Conclusion

In recent years, research on LRRK2 has advanced rapidly, revealing its diverse cellular functions and highlighting its close association with immune responses and inflammation. This association was first confirmed in the nervous system and is considered a key pathological process in PD caused by LRRK2 mutations. Meanwhile, increasing evidence suggests that LRRK2 is widely expressed in organs and tissues throughout the body, potentially playing a role in inflammatory responses and their adverse consequences, such as fibrosis and carcinogenesis.

Given the low abundance of LRRK2 in cells and its significant expression variability across tissues, explaining the substantial physiological impact of a small amount of LRRK2 remains a question. As LRRK2 is an important component of multiple intracellular signaling pathways, further investigation into its upstream and downstream molecular partners may help explain the tissue-specific biological effects exhibited by it.

Meanwhile, the development of therapeutic strategies targeting LRRK2, with a more comprehensive understanding of its interactions with other cellular proteins, is becoming a focal point for future drug development. Inhibiting LRRK2 kinase activity may help adjust its phosphorylation functions, correct imbalanced cellular pathways, and alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal damage, becoming a significant direction for treating PD and other related diseases. However, given that the normal physiological function of LRRK2 is not yet fully understood, directly regulating a protein involved in multiple cellular functions could pose unknown risks. Therefore, in-depth research into the physiological function of LRRK2 is a crucial prerequisite for further developing related treatment methods and their clinical application.

In summary, the diverse cellular functions of LRRK2 within cells and its role in disease development not only offer profound insights for scientific research but also demonstrate its great potential as a therapeutic target. Future investigations will focus on how to use this knowledge to develop treatment strategies targeting LRRK2, providing more effective treatment options for patients with PD and inflammatory diseases. As our understanding of the functions of LRRK2 and its role in diseases deepens, and treatment research advances, new therapeutic methods will continue to emerge, heralding new hope to patients.

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the crucial role of LRRK2 in various diseases, particularly PD. It explores the cellular functions of LRRK2, its physiological roles, and the dysfunctions caused by mutations, and compares common and distinct features of LRRK2-associated diseases. By deepening our understanding of LRRK2, this review aims to guide future research directions and address key unresolved questions.
