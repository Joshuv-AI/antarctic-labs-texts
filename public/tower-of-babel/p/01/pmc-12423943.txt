Preoperative Factors Associated With Falls Following Total Hip Arthroplasty in Older Adults
Authors: Craig P Hensley, Abel N Kho, Andrew Kritselis, Jing Song, Jungwha Lee, Alison H Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFalls are common after total hip arthroplasty (THA) and can cause serious complications. Understanding preoperative factors linked to falls post‐THA can inform discharge planning and management to lower fall risk. We aimed to identify preoperative factors associated with falls occurring within one year following THA in older adults.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using records within a large hospital system. Adults aged ≥65 years with a recorded THA between March 2012 and March 2022 were included. The primary outcome was a fall occurrence (yes vs no) within one year post‐THA. A fall was identified through International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth Revision codes, documentation for fall‐related emergency room visit(s), or mention of a fall in the medical record. Purposeful selection of variables in logistic regression models identified preoperative factors associated with a fall.ResultsAmong 4,501 patients (age 72.7 ± 6.0 years), 683 (15.2%) experienced at least one fall within one year post‐THA. The strongest risk factors were a fall within one year pre‐THA (odds ratio [OR] = 2.67, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 2.22–3.22) and depression (OR = 1.95, 95% CI = 1.62–2.35). Other factors included neurologic conditions, insomnia, revision or conversion hip arthroplasty, knee pain, lumbar conditions, body mass index (BMI) <20 kg/m2, and older age. A BMI of 25 to 29.9 was protective against falls.ConclusionThis longitudinal cohort study identified multiple independent factors associated with fall occurrences within one year post‐THA. Findings highlight the opportunity to address modifiable factors and provide individuals with tailored discharge planning and management strategies (eg, fall prevention programs) to reduce fall risk.

INTRODUCTION

In the United States, approximately 500,000 total hip arthroplasties (THAs) are performed annually; this number is projected to rise to 1,429,000 by 2040. 1 Falls are a significant concern after THA and affect 23% to 42% of patients within the first year after surgery, with older adults at highest risk. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The risk of multiple falls within two years post‐THA is twice as high as those undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) 6 and nearly 2.5 times higher than age‐matched controls. 4 Falls can lead to serious consequences like fractures, hospitalization, and death, resulting in substantial costs. 7

Identifying preoperative factors associated with falls after THA can improve fall screening and inform discharge destination, medical equipment prescription, and rehabilitation that lower fall risk. Two recent systematic reviews have examined factors associated with falls after lower extremity joint arthroplasty. 8 , 9 These reviews suggested that older age, history of falls, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), previous TKA, psychiatric conditions, living alone, and postoperative complications were associated with falls after THA or TKA. However, most studies included in these reviews were limited by small sample sizes and focused on the immediate postoperative period, inclusion of mixed‐age populations, and failure to differentiate between factors specific to THA and TKA.

Considering the elevated risk of falls in older adults following THA, focusing exclusively on this population is crucial to better understand the preoperative factors contributing to falls. Unlike TKA, THA poses distinct biomechanical and functional challenges that may influence fall risk differently. 10 In addition, it has been suggested that those undergoing THA are more likely to experience low back pain (LBP) 11 and to report three or more troublesome hip/knee joints, 11 , 12 underscoring the importance of evaluating the influence of other painful sites when assessing fall risk in this population. To our knowledge, preoperative factors associated with falls following THA have not been investigated in a large cohort with extended follow‐up. To fill this gap, we determined the fall rates and identified preoperative factors associated with falls within one year after THA in older adults using electronic medical records (EMR) within a large hospital system.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent THA. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies In Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. 13

We collected de‐identified EMR data from an enterprise data warehouse (EDW). The EDW is a repository of patients who receive care within a large hospital system throughout Chicago, Illinois, and surrounding areas that contains records of over 6.6 million distinct patients. Approval was provided by Northwestern University Institutional Review Board, which was determined to be exempt from informed consent because the study involved research from routine care.

Study patients

We included adults aged ≥65 years who underwent THA (Supplementary Table 1) between March 2012 and March 2022. If a patient had multiple recorded THRs (ie, multiple hip surgeries during the study time frame), we only included data from the first record. Exclusion criteria were: (1) uncertainty as to whether a fall occurred; (2) missing or implausible body mass index (BMI), age, or sex data; and (3) patients who were deceased within one year after THA.

Main study outcome

We identified patients who had at least one fall between the date of THA surgery and one year post‐operatively by using three strategies:

Searching the patient's active problem list, past medical history, visit diagnoses, and/or billing diagnoses for International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10) codes for falls (Supplementary Table 2).

The primary author reviewed all selected texts to determine if a fall occurred. To assess interrater reliability of fall identification with this strategy, an additional reviewer, blinded to the primary author's results, independently examined the first 100 observations in which text data were used to determine fall occurrence. There was a 96% between‐rater agreement in determining a fall occurrence through free text.

Fall history within one year before THA. This was determined using the same methods for identifying falls post‐THA. There was a 98% between‐rater agreement in determining a fall occurrence through free text.

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics, including counts (%) and mean (SD), summarized baseline patient characteristics and were reported separately for the entire cohort and for those with primary care physicians (PCP) within the hospital system. We included patients within the hospital system who met inclusion criteria regardless of whether they had a recorded visit in the previous year. To account for any potential gaps in fall documentation if care was received outside the system, we conducted a subgroup analysis of patients with a PCP within the hospital system. Those with PCPs within the system were more likely to have comprehensive documentation of visits and care both before and after THA. 15

To examine the associations between preoperative factors and fall within one year (yes versus no) post‐THA, we used a purposeful selection of variables in logistic regression. 16 This started with univariable analyses to identify candidate factors and was followed by multivariable modeling containing all candidate factors and then deriving smaller models identified via an iterative process using likelihood ratio tests. This approach balances clinical relevance and statistical rigor to produce a parsimonious and interpretable model. The Akaike Information Criterion was applied for model selection to achieve a parsimonious final model. Previous research has shown a U‐shaped association of BMI with falls. 17 Therefore, we analyzed BMI as a categorical variable. BMI was categorized into five groups: <20 kg/m2, 20 to 24.9 kg/m2, 25 to 29.9 kg/m2, 30 to 34.9 kg/m2, and ≥35 kg/m2. These cut points were selected based on previous studies and to ensure a balanced distribution across the five groups. 18 Furthermore, factors with low prevalence were grouped together in the analyses: revision and conversion THA were combined; seizure, Parkinson's disease, stroke, and dementia were grouped as neurologic conditions; macular degeneration, cataracts, and glaucoma were grouped as visual conditions; and heart failure, myocardial infarction, and COPD were grouped as cardiopulmonary conditions. Anxiety and sleep apnea were excluded from the regression model to mitigate multicollinearity, given the high correlations between depression and anxiety as well as insomnia and sleep apnea.

We conducted a Chi‐square test to compare the proportions of falls between the PCP and the non‐PCP subgroups. In addition, we repeated the logistic regression subgroup analysis and restricted it to those with a PCP within the hospital system.

Results from the final parsimonious logistic regression models are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A 95% CI excluding 1.0 is considered statistically significant. ORs greater than 1.0 indicate increased likelihood, and ORs less than 1.0 indicate decreased likelihood. The area under the curve (AUC) is also reported. Data were analyzed using R version 4.3.2.

RESULTS

Study sample

Figure 1 shows study participant sample derivation. The final sample included 4,501 patients with THA occurring from March 2012 to March 2022. Of these 4,501 records, 4,016 (89.2%) had PCPs within the hospital system. As shown in Table 1, the overall cohort had a mean (SD) age of 72.7 (6.0) years and a mean (SD) BMI of 28.5 (5.3) kg/m2; 2,662 (59.1%) were women; 6.6% were Black or African American, 2.1% Hispanic, and 85.6% White. The majority had primary THA (92.9%), 4.7% had revision THA, and 2.4% had conversion THA. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (71.9%), lumbar conditions (50.0%), knee pain (39.7%), and depression (19.4%).

Proportion of patients with falls

Table 2 shows that 683 (15.2%) patients had at least one fall recorded in the overall cohort. The proportion of patients with reported falls was significantly higher among those with vs without a PCP within the hospital system (641 of 4,016 [16.0%] vs 42 of 485 [8.7%), P <0.001]. Among the 683 patients with recorded falls, 197 (28.8%) were identified through the patient active problem list, past medical history, visit diagnoses, and/or billing diagnoses with ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes; 34 (5.0%) through emergency room visits without ICD‐9 or ICD‐10 codes; and 452 (66.2%) through free‐text documentation.

Preoperative factors associated with falls

Table 3 presents the results from the multivariable logistic regression model. A documented fall within one year before THA was associated with the greatest odds of fall occurrence within one year after THA (OR = 2.68, 95% CI = 2.22–3.23, P <0.0001). Other significant factors associated with a fall included depression (OR = 1.94, 95% CI = 1.61–2.34, P < 0.0001), neurologic conditions (OR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.39–2.03, P < 0.0001), insomnia (OR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.20–1.77, P < 0.0001), knee pain (OR = 1.37, 95% CI = 1.16–1.62, P = 0.0002), lumbar conditions (OR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.14–1.62, P = 0.0006)], revision or conversion THA (OR = 1.47, 95% CI = 1.13–1.92, P = 0.004), and having a BMI < 20 kg/m2 (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 1.01–2.27, P = 0.044). Falls were more common with older age (OR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.02–1.05, P < 0.0001). A BMI between 25 and 29.9 kg/m2 was protective against falls (OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.93, P = 0.044). The PCP subgroup analysis yielded similar preoperative factors associated with a fall, although being underweight was no longer statistically significant (OR = 1.50, 95% CI 0.98–2.29, P = 0.062) (see Table 4). The AUC was 0.73 for the overall cohort and 0.72 for the PCP subgroup, indicating moderate discriminatory power of the model regarding the likelihood of a fall based on the included factors. 19 We ran an additional sensitivity analysis including those who died within one year after THA because they may have had higher fall risk or underlying frailty, but results did not differ significantly.

DISCUSSION

Using EMR data, we found that a history of falls within one year pre‐THA, depression, neurologic conditions, insomnia, revision or conversion THA, knee pain, lumbar conditions, BMI <20 kg/m2, and older age were each independently associated with falls within one year post‐THA in older adults. In contrast, being slightly overweight (not obese) was protective against falls. The proportion of fallers was 15.2%. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date that specifically examined preoperative factors associated with falls occurring within one year post‐THA in older adults.

Depression

Nearly 20% of our cohort had depression. This aligns with rates observed among individuals experiencing chronic arthritic pain. 20 Depression has been linked to other fall risk factors, including frailty 21 ; social isolation 22 ; and reduced physical activity, function, and reaction time. 22 Previous reviews have shown that depressive symptoms and psychiatric conditions are associated with an increased likelihood of falls following lower extremity joint arthroplasty. 8 , 9 , 14 However, neither review specifically examined the association between falls and THA. One study in Denmark reported that individuals treated pharmacologically for psychiatric conditions were 2.8 times more likely to be readmitted for a surgery‐related fall following joint replacement. 23 However, that study examined only falls occurring within 90 days post‐operatively. Given that most falls after THA occur between 6 to 12 months after surgery, 3 our study provides the first evidence linking depression to fall risk over a full year after THA.

Insomnia

Insomnia was present in nearly 18% of our cohort, consistent with previous reports of insomnia in older adults with osteoarthritis. 24 To our knowledge, no studies have specifically examined the link between insomnia and falls post‐THA. Insomnia has been linked to greater fall risk in older adults. 25 Insomnia can lead to daytime hypersomnolence, impaired motor and cognitive function, difficulty concentrating, and slowed responses, all of which could contribute to falls. 26 , 27 Additionally, sleep aid usage may contribute to increased fall risk. 25

Lumbar conditions and knee pain

In our cohort, 50% reported a lumbar condition, and nearly 40% reported knee pain. Previous studies have shown that up to 60% of individuals undergoing THA experienced LBP before surgery, 28 and approximately 50% of those with hip osteoarthritis had knee pain. 29 However, to our knowledge, no previous studies have examined a link between preoperative knee pain or lumbar conditions and fall risk after THA. Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting independent associations of falls with both LBP 30 , 31 and knee pain. 31 , 32 Chronic musculoskeletal pain has been consistently linked to a greater likelihood of falls. 33 Notably, both greater pain intensity and a higher number of painful sites are also associated with an elevated fall risk. 34 This observation may be attributed to the cumulative effects of severe and widespread pain. Proposed mechanisms underlying the connection between musculoskeletal pain and fall risk include muscle weakness, impaired neuromuscular control, range of motion deficits, diminished physical and cognitive function, and fear of falling. 33 , 35

History of a fall within one year before THA

In line with previous research, a history of a fall within one year preceding lower extremity joint arthroplasty was associated with falls following joint arthroplasty. 8 , 9 Our results align with broader evidence indicating that past falls are a strong predictor for future falls in community‐dwelling older adults. 36

Contrasting factors with previous research

A BMI <20 kg/m2 was associated with an increased likelihood of falls in our cohort. Similarly, previous studies have linked low BMI in older adults to an elevated fall risk. 17 Low BMI may serve as a marker for frailty, a well‐established risk factor for falls. 37 On the contrary, a BMI between 25 and 29.9 kg/m2 appeared to be protective against falls. This observation suggests that being slightly overweight in older adults may be beneficial. 38 , 39 , 40 Reviews examining the relationship between BMI and fall risk after lower extremity joint arthroplasty have yielded conflicting results. Lo et al found no relationship between BMI and falls, whereas Liu et al reported that a BMI ≥25 kg/m2 was associated an elevated fall risk. 8 , 9 However, the association reported by Liu et al was skewed by two studies with short follow‐up time in inpatient settings. 9 It is plausible that early post‐operatively, reduced muscle strength may make it difficult for patients with a high BMI to maintain balance because of additional mass. Moreover, health care providers may face greater challenges in assisting heavier patients.

Our study found no association between preoperative hypertension or diabetes and postoperative falls. The study cohort predominantly consisted of White individuals, among whom rates of blood pressure 41 and glycemic 42 control are generally higher compared with Black or African American and Hispanic populations. Because uncontrolled hypertension 43 and poor glycemic control 44 are known fall risk factors in older adults, it is plausible that effective management of these conditions within our study accounted for the lack of association.

We did not observe an association between previous TKA and post‐THA falls, contrary to findings from an inpatient study. 45 This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in follow‐up durations because longer periods may allow for the recovery of mobility and balance control. Additionally, previous lower extremity joint arthroplasty could improve mobility and balance, offering protection against falls. 14 Last, visual conditions were not linked to falls, possibly reflecting the success of corrective eye surgeries in mitigating fall risk. 46

The fall rates in our cohort were lower than that reported in other studies. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 This discrepancy is likely due to underreporting because patients may fail to report falls and health care providers may insufficiently assess for them. Notably, less than 50% of older adults discuss falls with health care providers. 47 Although fall screening assessments are available, 48 they are not consistently performed for individuals at risk for falls. 49 We believe that most falls resulting in serious injury were accurately captured in our study, particularly within the PCP subgroup. Patients who had a PCP within the hospital system had a greater rate of falls compared with those without. We hypothesize that patients were more inclined to return to the hospital system for care where they have an established PCP, leading to better fall documentation. On the other hand, falls among patients without a PCP within the hospital system may have been underreported in our records.

Clinical implications

Given the potential consequences of falls, it is imperative for health care providers to screen for fall risk before and after THA. In older adults undergoing THA who present with preoperative factors associated with falls, a multidisciplinary approach to fall risk management may be warranted. This could involve collaborations among the surgical team, primary care providers, nursing staff, pharmacists, dietitians, occupational therapists, and physical therapists to develop individualized care plans tailored to the patient's specific needs.

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths. It included the largest patient sample to date for examining preoperative factors associated with falls within one year post‐THA. Although systematic reviews examining fall risk after joint arthroplasty have included both THA and TKA cohorts, they have generally not delineated procedure‐specific factors associated with falls. By enhancing the traditional method of extracting data from EMR, which typically relies on ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes, we incorporated free‐text analysis within clinical notes. This innovative approach enabled us to capture a substantial number of falls that traditional coding would have missed. This is the first study, to our knowledge, that has employed such a technique to identify falls post‐THA through EMR. Additionally, by tracking fall occurrences for a full year post‐THA, this study provides a more comprehensive view of patient outcomes over a longer term than those that focus on immediate postoperative periods.

Limitations of our study should be recognized. First, we relied on previously collected EMR, which may contain missing or inaccurate information. This could have led to misclassifications and biased estimates, including a potentially lower fall frequency compared with previous reports. Additionally, we excluded individuals whose fall status could not be determined. Although this group was small, their exclusion may have introduced selection bias. Third, the definition of a fall may have differed across providers and patients and may not have consistently aligned with the World Health Organization's definition. 50 Fourth, our results should not be generalized to those under the age of 65. Fifth, we were unable to account for preoperative factors that may have been resolved or managed after the surgery. We also did not capture fall prevention and rehabilitation strategies that may have been implemented during the postoperative period. Our sample was drawn from a single US hospital system and predominantly consisted of White individuals, which may limit generalizability to more diverse US populations. 51 Finally, because our study focused exclusively on patients with THA, we were not able to directly compare fall risk factors between THA and TKA cohorts. However, our findings support the need for future research that distinguishes risk profiles by procedure type.

Among 4,501 older adults who underwent THA, we identified preoperative factors associated with a fall within one year after surgery. Notably, our study is the first to identify associations between falls within one year post‐THA and preoperative depression, insomnia, knee pain, and lumbar conditions. These findings extend beyond the shorter follow‐up periods used in previous research and offers new targets for screening and prevention. Future studies should investigate whether fall prevention strategies that incorporate balance and strength training decrease fall incidence, particularly among older adults with preoperative factors associated with falls post‐THA.
