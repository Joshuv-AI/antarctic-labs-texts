Topical Imiquimod for Oral Cavity Leukoplakia and Dysplasia: A Scoping Review
Authors: Megan K. Scharner, Angelica M. Walker, Shaun A. Nguyen, Alexandra E. Kejner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveImiquimod, a toll‐like receptor 7 agonist, is currently FDA‐approved for treatment of actinic keratoses, superficial BCC, and external genital warts. It has also demonstrated promise in off‐label use in the treatment of oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia. The aim of this scoping review is to summarize the current literature on this novel treatment strategy.Data SourcesCINAHL, Cochrane Library, PubMed, and SCOPUS.Review MethodData sources were searched from inception through December 2024. The search was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis Extension for Scoping Reviews. Studies reporting on oral leukoplakia or dysplasia treated with topical imiquimod were included, with primary outcome being lesion resolution or reduction.ResultsA total of 9 studies reporting on (N = 89) patients with (n = 99) lesions were included. The average age was 48.4 (range 43‐66), and 48.6% were male. 5% topical imiquimod was used in varying treatment regimens, most commonly for 6 weeks in duration (7/9 papers). 34.3% of lesions had complete resolution, 43.4% had incomplete resolution, and 22.2% had no change in the lesion. The most commonly reported adverse effects were burning/soreness at the site (73.0%), fatigue (22.5%), oral lichen planus development or flare (11.2%), and flu‐like symptoms (2.2%).ConclusionTopical imiquimod for the treatment of oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia is a promising new treatment strategy. Efficacy has been demonstrated in reducing lesion size and even complete lesion resolution, with minimal adverse effects. Further research is needed on this innovative therapeutic approach.

Oral leukoplakia is a potentially malignant oral disease with a recognized risk of progression to squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) ranging from 7.2% to 11.5%. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 This risk becomes significantly higher for patients diagnosed with dysplasia, reaching up to 32.2% transformation to SCC. 3 , 5 As malignant transformation occurs over a variable timeframe, long term surveillance and biopsy is necessary for all patients with oral leukoplakia. 3 , 4 Existing treatment strategies include removal of risk factors, chemotherapy agents, photodynamic therapy, and surgical excision; however, these are limited by recurrence risk, decreased efficacy, and side effect burden. 6 , 7 Due to these deficiencies, there exists a need for both non‐invasive and effective therapeutic approaches to manage premalignant oral disease.

Imiquimod is an immune response modifier that functions primarily as an agonist of toll‐like receptor 7 (TLR7). 8 Currently, imiquimod is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of superficial basal cell carcinoma (BCC), actinic keratoses, and condyloma acuminata. 9 As its mechanism of action activates various immune pathways, imiquimod has been shown to have potential in treating some premalignant and malignant cutaneous disorders. 10 , 11 Utilization of imiquimod in the area of premalignant cutaneous disease is currently off‐label, though its antiviral and antitumor effects make it a promising candidate for additionally treating mucosal premalignancy.

Growing numbers of case reports and small‐scale clinical studies have examined off‐label use of topical imiquimod for oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia. The aim of this review is the explore the effect of imiquimod on lesion regression and dysplastic change, while highlighting its promise as a nonsurgical, immunomodulatory treatment option. By synthesizing the current body of literature, this study evaluates the efficacy, risk profile, and existing off‐label treatment protocols associated with this novel treatment strategy for oral cavity pre‐malignant disease.

Methods

Stage 1: Literature Search

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines provided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) and was registered under PROSPERO ID #42024626950. 12 In developing the research question, the PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) framework was utilized. A comprehensive literature search of CINAHL (EBSCO), PubMed (National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health), Scopus (Elsevier), and Cochrane Library was performed from inception through December 2024. The search strategy was created utilizing Medical Subject Headings (MESH) and keywords including imiquimod, leukoplakia, dysplasia, oral cavity, and tongue. The original search strategy was developed for PubMed and was then reformatted for CINAHL, Scopus, and Cochrane. These search strategies are outlined in Supplemental Appendix S1, available online. All collected references were then uploaded to the Covidence systematic review management software (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd.).

Stage 2: Study Selection

Two authors (MKS and AMW) independently screened articles based on title and abstract, and then by full text review to identify all articles meeting inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by a third author (SAN). Inclusion criteria were (1) oral cavity leukoplakia or dysplasia, (2) human subjects, (3) topical application of imiquimod, and (4) outcomes including lesion resolution or change in lesion size. Case reports and series were included due to the extremely limited body of literature on this topic. Exclusion criteria were (1) lesions other than leukoplakia or dysplasia, (2) lesions located outside the oral cavity, (3) interventions other than topical imiquimod, (4) non‐English studies, and (5) nonhuman subjects. Stage 3: Study Appraisal

Level of evidence for all included studies was assessed using the Oxford Center for Evidence‐Based Medicine criteria (LOE). 13 The risk of bias for all included studies was assessed utilizing either the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports (JBI‐Case Reports), Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series (JBI‐Case Series), updated Risk of Bias in Non‐Randomized Interventional (ROBINS‐I), or the revised Cochrane risk‐of‐bias tool for randomized trials (RoB‐2). 14 , 15 , 16 Both the level of evidence and risk of bias were determined independently by two authors (MKS and AMW) with disagreements settled through discussion with a third author (SAN).

Stage 4: Data Extraction

Two authors (MKS and AMW) independently extracted the data into a standardized Excel template and compared for accuracy, with discrepancies resolved by a third author (SAN). Author, publication year, country of origin, and patient demographic information were collected. Treatment data including topical imiquimod concentration, duration of each application, application frequency, and duration of total treatment regimen were collected. Outcome data including full lesion resolution, partial lesion resolution, no lesion change, and lesion increase in size were collected. Finally, adverse events were collected.

Stage 5: Statistical Analysis

Relevant information from the included studies was synthesized narratively in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for summarizing findings when meta‐analysis was not feasible. Study results and key findings are presented using descriptive statistics—frequency (%) for categorical variables, and mean or median with range or 95% confidence intervals (CI) for continuous variables. Risk of bias figures (Figure 2) and funnel plot (Figure 3) were generated using Cochrane Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020, United Kingdom).

Results

Study Characteristics

The literature search returned 55 unique articles. During title and abstract screening, 46 articles were excluded, resulting in 9 studies that underwent full‐text review. All 9 studies met inclusion criteria and were included for this scoping review. The PRISMA diagram (Figure 1) illustrates the screening process. 17

The included studies were classified as level 3 and 4 evidence according to the Oxford Levels of Evidence and included one pilot randomized controlled trial, two cohort studies, one case series, and five case reports. Patient Demographics

Across the 9 included studies, data were reported for 89 patients and 99 lesions. The mean reported age of patients was 48.4 years (range 43‐66) and 48.7% (n = 36) were male. Histologic confirmation was performed in all cases. Dysplasia was the most common histologic characteristic which was reported in 70.7% (n = 70) of lesions. Due to heterogeneity in reporting of dysplasia severity, specification of dysplastic severity was unable to be evaluated as a cohort. The gingiva was the most common lesion site (71.7%, n = 71). Treatment Regimens

Most studies (88.9%, n = 8) specified the concentration of topical imiquimod used, with 5% imiquimod being utilized in 97.8% (n = 87) of cases. Application with custom tray or retractor was utilized for 64.6% (n = 64) of lesions. Reported application durations ranged from 20 to 60 minutes, with 60 minutes being the most frequently used (71.9%, n = 64). Treatment frequency varied, with five applications per week most common (74.2%, n = 66). Total treatment duration ranged from 6 weeks to 6 months; 96.6% (n = 86) of cases were treated for 6 weeks. Treatment Response

Across the 99 lesions included in the review, outcomes varied. Post‐treatment, all studies (n = 9) utilized clinical evaluation for treatment response, and (n = 6) of the nine studies utilized histologic evaluation for determination of treatment response. Complete resolution was observed in 34.3% (n = 34) of cases, with relapse reported in 3 (8.8%) of these. An additional 43.4% (n = 43) of lesions demonstrated partial or incomplete resolution. Notably, one lesion with partial response to treatment demonstrated eradication of high‐risk HPV strains (18, 31/33, and 45) on histologic evaluation after treatment. 24 No observable response to treatment was noted in 22.2% (n = 22) of cases. Adverse Events

Adverse events were variably reported across studies. The most common adverse effect was localized irritation described as pain, burning, or sensitivity at the application site which 73.0% (n = 65) of patients reported. Additional reported symptoms included fatigue in 22.5% (n = 20), flare or new development of oral lichen planus in 11.2% (n = 10), headache in 10.1% (n = 9), chills in 7.9% (n = 7), and flu‐like symptoms in 2.2% (n = 2). Meta‐Analysis of Available Data

Three studies, encompassing 82 patients and 91 lesions, provided sufficient detail to permit descriptive meta‐analysis. 25 , 26 , 27 Of these patients, 49.3% (95% CI 37.0%‐61.6%) were male. Complete lesion resolution was observed in 37.0% (95% CI 26.9%‐48.4%) of lesions, while 23.4% (95% CI 15.8%‐33.4%) showed no response to treatment. Although limited by small sample sizes and study design heterogeneity, these pooled estimates offer preliminary insights into potential treatment outcomes. Discussion

Oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia are premalignant lesions, with reported annual risk of malignant transformation between 1% and 2% and recognized lifetime risk of progression to SCC ranging from 7.2% to 11.5%. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 28 , 29 In patients with histologic evidence of dysplasia, the risk is even higher. 3 , 5 As a result, these lesions are commonly managed with interventions such as risk factor modification, chemotherapeutic agents, photodynamic therapy, and surgical excision. 6 , 7 , 28 However, these approaches carry limitations, including potential recurrence, variable efficacy, and adverse side effects. Moreover, some patients may not be candidates for surgery or may prefer noninvasive options with a lower side effect burden. Nevertheless, appropriate treatment remains essential for reducing malignant potential.

Topical imiquimod is currently FDA‐approved for several pre‐malignant and malignant cutaneous lesions, including external anogenital warts, actinic keratoses, and superficial basal cell carcinomas. 9 , 30 , 31 It acts as a toll‐like receptor 7 agonist, stimulating anti‐viral activity and anti‐tumoral immune respones. 8 , 30 , 31 Given its mechanism of action and demonstrated cutaneous efficacy, its off‐label use has been explored for mucosal lesions such as oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia. The first published use of topical imiquimod for this indication was by Allam et al, who described two cases of oral cavity leukoplakia treated with topical 5% imiquimod for six and twelve weeks, respectively, resulting in one complete and one partial response. 24

While the existing literature is limited, additional case reports and small studies have since evaluated this application, as summarized in this review. In total, 66.6% of treated lesions demonstrated some degree of clinical improvement, with 33.3% achieving complete resolution. These findings suggest a potential role for imiquimod in managing oral cavity premalignant lesions, particularly in patients who are otherwise not surgical candidates. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the predominance of case reports and small series, which are subject to bias and heterogeneity.

Adverse effects were frequently reported but generally tolerable. Local irritation at the application site was the most common, affecting 73.0% of patients, followed by fatigue, oral lichen planus flare, and headache. No included studies reported treatment discontinuation due to adverse events, which may indicate acceptable tolerability in most patients. However, formal assessments of quality of life and long‐term safety were limited.

A subset of three studies permitted pooled descriptive synthesis, providing preliminary estimates of treatment outcomes. 25 , 26 , 27 Among these, complete lesion resolution was reported in approximately 37.1% of cases, while 23.8% had no response. These data highlight both the potential and the variability of response to imiquimod. However, due to the small number of studies and inherent methodological limitations, no definitive conclusions can be drawn.

This scoping review provides an overview of the available literature on topical imiquimod for oral leukoplakia and dysplasia and identifies key gaps. The findings underscore the need for more robust prospective studies, including controlled trials and standardized treatment protocols, to better evaluate efficacy, durability of response, and safety profiles. In addition, future research should aim to clarify which lesion subtypes or patient populations may be most likely to benefit from this treatment approach.

Limitations

This study is limited primarily by the small body of literature on the use of topical imiquimod for oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia. To capture the breadth of existing evidence, we included case reports and case series, which inherently carry a higher risk of bias and limit generalizability. As a result, meta‐analysis could not be performed on the entire dataset. Additionally, treatment protocols varied significantly across studies, with differences in imiquimod concentration, application duration, frequency, and total treatment length.

Two of nine included studies did not specify the concentration of topical imiquimod used, and regimens ranged from daily application and every other day. Nonetheless, the majority of patients (97.8%, n = 87) were treated with 5% topical imiquimod, and most (96.6%, n = 86) received treatment for six weeks, allowing for some descriptive synthesis. Another important limitation is the limited reporting of relevant clinical data, including risk factor profiles such as alcohol and tobacco use. This impedes efforts to determine whether certain patient populations may derive greater benefit from treatment. Finally, follow‐up durations were not consistently reported, making it difficult to assess the durability of treatment response and long‐term recurrence.

Conclusion

Topical Imiquimod represents a promising nonsurgical approach for the management of oral cavity leukoplakia and dysplasia. The available literature suggests that it may contribute to lesion reduction or complete resolution in a subset of patients, with an acceptable safety profile. However, the current evidence base is limited, and findings should be interpreted cautiously. Additional well‐designed prospective studies are needed to further evaluate efficacy, define optimal treatment protocols, and identity patients most likely to benefit from this therapeutic strategy.
