Impact of intraoperative specimen computed tomography imaging on grossing accuracy and margin assessment in pulmonary wedge resection
Authors: Kentaro Miura, Takahiro Yanagihara, Nicholas Bernards, Shinsuke Kitazawa, Fumi Yokote, Kenta Nakahashi, Yusuke Fujibayashi, Hironori Hinokuma, Dai Sonoda, Noriko Hayama, Tomokazu Omori, Elliot Wakeam, Laura Donahoe, Jonathan Yeung, Marcelo Cypel, Marc De Perrot, Andrew Pierre, Thomas Waddell, Shaf Keshavjee, Michael Cabanero, Yukio Sato, Kimihiro Shimizu, Kazuhiro Yasufuku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether specimen computed tomography imaging (2- or 3-dimensional) influences the accuracy and decision-making in pathological grossing for pulmonary wedge resection.MethodsIn this randomized prospective study, pulmonary wedge resection specimens were divided into 3 groups: control (no imaging), 2-dimensional computed tomography, and 3-dimensional computed tomography. Pathology assistants performed grossing with or without intraoperative computed tomography guidance per group assignment. A 5-point Likert scale questionnaire assessed perceived usefulness in tumor localization, cut-line location, cut-line angle, and overall grossing impact. Margins measured on 2- and 3-dimensional computed tomography with actual cutting lines were compared with pathological margins. Subgroup analyses were performed for lung cancer and metastatic tumors.ResultsAcross all specimens (n = 45), no parameter reached statistical significance. However, 3-dimensional computed tomography tended to score higher for cut-line angle impact (median 4 [3-4]) and overall grossing impact (median 4 [3-5]) than 2-dimensional computed tomography. In lung cancer cases (n = 16), 3-dimensional computed tomography significantly impacted cut-line angle compared with 2-dimensional computed tomography (median 2.5 [2–3] vs 4 [3–4.25], P = .03). In metastatic tumors (n = 29), 3-dimensional computed tomography showed a nonsignificant trend toward higher overall impact. Correlation analysis revealed strong associations between margins measured on 2-dimensional computed tomography and pathological margins, and between margins reconstructed from actual cutting lines on 3-dimensional computed tomography and pathological margins (r = 0.87 and 0.92, respectively). These correlations were slightly weaker in lung cancer than in metastatic tumors.ConclusionsIntraoperative computed tomography imaging demonstrated high accuracy in margin estimation and provided reliable guidance for pathological grossing. Selective computed tomography evaluation may be advantageous for lesions difficult to palpate, such as deeply located small tumors.Key Words: computed tomography, grossing, lung cancer, metastatic lung tumors, pulmonary wedge resection, surgical margin

3D-CT improves accuracy of cut-line angle and location during lung cancer grossing.

Central Message

Intraoperative 3D-CT enhances margin estimation and cut-line planning versus 2D-CT, supporting selective use for nonpalpable or deep lung lesions.

Perspective

Accurate margin assessment in wedge resection is vital for lung cancer and metastasis surgery. Intraoperative 3D-CT improves margin estimation and cut-line planning versus 2D-CT, supporting selective use to enhance pathological evaluation and surgical quality for nonpalpable or deep lesions.

In recent years, the surgical strategy for small primary non–small cell lung cancers has been shifting from lobectomy to sublobar resection. This is driven by the results of high-impact clinical trials.1,2 The Japanese Clinical Oncology Group 0802/West Japan Oncology Group 4607L randomized controlled trial demonstrated that segmentectomy offers superior overall survival and postoperative pulmonary function compared with lobectomy in patients with stage IA non–small cell lung cancer.1 Likewise, the Cancer and Leukemia Group B 140,503 trial reported comparable findings, showing that the prognosis in the sublobar resection group was better than in the lobectomy group.2 However, a major concern with sublobar resection is the higher rate of local recurrence.1 Preventing such local recurrences remains a critical challenge for thoracic surgeons. Previous studies have shown that insufficient surgical margins after wedge or sublobar resection are significantly associated with increased recurrence rates.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Therefore, securing an adequate surgical margin is one of the key factors for successful sublobar resections.

Although a precise cut line during grossing is crucial for accurate margin assessment, achieving this remains challenging due to factors such as tumor size, shape, and location, features such as ground-glass nodule (GGN) components, and the complexity of the staple line. Recent studies have explored intraoperative computed tomography (CT) guidance and 3-dimensional (3D) reconstruction algorithms to improve the accuracy of specimen sectioning and margin evaluation, particularly in sublobar lung resections.8,9 At our institution, we have developed an intraoperative workflow method for evaluating surgical margins using CT imaging.10 This technique enables real-time assessment of surgical margins during sublobar resection and has demonstrated both accuracy and clinical utility. Beyond the intraoperative impact, it also allows for image analysis before formalin fixation of the specimen. As such, we hypothesized that intraoperative CT image references could assist pathological grossing by informing cutlines, cut angles, and ultimately the pathological margin length. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a prospective randomized study focusing on wedge resection cases.

Patients and Methods

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the institutional Research Ethics Board of the University Health Network (#24-5334) on December 4, 2024. All participants gave informed consents.

Study Participants (Cases)

We included patients who underwent therapeutic wedge resections for suspicious malignant lung tumors between December 2024 and October 2025. Patient participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older and scheduled for a lung wedge resection for primary lung cancer or metastatic lung tumor, including suspected lesions. Patients were excluded if they were unable to provide informed consent, if the wedge resection was performed for nontherapeutic purposes (eg, purely diagnostic procedures), or if the specimen had been cut before formalin fixation.

Randomization was performed at the specimen level (not patient) using a pregenerated randomization table to ensure unbiased allocation. Before grossing, eligible specimens were randomly assigned to 1 of 3 groups (control, 2-dimensional [2D]-CT, or 3D-CT) to evaluate the efficacy of intraoperative imaging in assisting pathological grossing. The primary end point of this study was to determine whether providing intraoperative CT imaging of resected lung specimens influences gross assessment during wedge resection, as evaluated by pathologists using a structured Likert-scale questionnaire. The optimal sample size was calculated as 11 using G power 3.1 with effect size 0.7 (correlation coefficient in the previous study), α error 0.05, and power 0.8; therefore, we set the sample size of this study group as 15. Secondary end points included the assessment of the accuracy of intraoperative specimen CT imaging by examining the correlation between the CT-derived surgical margins and the corresponding pathological margins.

Surgical Procedures

All radical wedge resections for suspicious malignant tumors were performed via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), robot-assisted thoracic surgery, or thoracotomy. Preoperative marking using microcoil was performed at the discretion of the surgeon. In cases where a microcoil was inserted, resection was guided by intraoperative fluoroscopy to identify the coil location.11 All wedge resections were carried out using surgical staplers (Endo GIA Universal Staplers and Reloads, Medtronic). In cases of VATS or robot-assisted thoracic surgery, after resection, the specimen was retrieved from the chest cavity using a small specimen bag.

Image Acquisition and 3-Dimensional Reconstruction

Study flow is shown in Figure 1. The procedure for specimen handling and CT imaging was consistent with our previous study.10 In short, as soon as an investigator obtained the wedge resection sample in the operating room, they transported the sample from the operating room to the CT room in the Spatio-Temporal Targeting and Amplification of Radiation Response innovation center located in a connected building. The lung specimen was inflated by injecting air using appropriate size syringe and 25-gauge needle. Inflation pressure (<25 mm Hg) was monitored using a manometer (Compass Universal HG, Mirador Biomedical, Inc). This helped improve contrast between the tumor and lung parenchyma.

Study flow. Workflow of specimen handling: After wedge resection, the specimen was inflated (<25 mm Hg) and scanned by CT. After formalin fixation, a PA marked the initial cut line on a grid sheet. For 2D-CT or 3D-CT group, the PA revised the cut line after reviewing imaging. PA, Pathologist assistant; 2D-CT, 2-dimensional computed tomography; 3D-CT, 3-dimensional computed tomography.

The specimen was then placed on a grid-marked sheet (Figure 2, A), and its contour was traced with a pen. The specimen was positioned with its staple line perpendicular to the CT axial plane (identified by a landmarking laser emitted by the CT scanner) to facilitate margin measurement.10 Then a CT scan was acquired using a 64-row multidetector CT scanner (Revolution Frontier, GE Healthcare). The 2D-CT–based margin defined the minimum distance from the tumor edge to the outer edge of the staple line (Figure 2, B). After CT acquisition, the resected specimen was gently deflated to remove air and fixed by 10% formalin injection.

Measurement of margins using 2D-CT and 3D-CT. A, Specimen before CT. B, 2D-CT margin: shortest distance from tumor edge to staple line) in axial view (red arrow). C, 3D-CT cut-line margin: distance along actual cut line and angle using 3D slicer (yellow arrow).

3D reconstruction and image analysis were performed based on DICOM data using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2; https://www.slicer.org), an open-source software platform for medical image computing.12 By carefully adjusting the threshold range, key anatomic and surgical features—including tumors, staple lines, lung parenchyma, and, when present, microcoils—were visualized. The resulting 3D images could be easily manipulated on screen and observed from multiple angles. Assessment of Image Evaluation in Specimen Grossing

Eight experienced pathologist assistants (PAs), each with at least 2 years (2-12 years) of professional experience, participated in this study to assess the impact of image review on grossing accuracy. Study flow is shown in Figure 1. Initially, the PA in charge of grossing palpated the formalin-fixed, inflated specimen to localize the tumor. After removing the staple lines by a pathology assistant, the parenchymal surface was inked after staple removal to facilitate measurement. The specimen was positioned on the same grid sheet used during CT imaging, aligning it with the premarked reference lines. Using a ruler, the PA indicated the initial cutting line before image review and marked it on the grid sheet with a pen. In the control group, the specimen was sectioned along this initial line. In the 2D-CT and 3D-CT groups, the PA reviewed the corresponding images in detail and was allowed to modify the location or angle of the cutting line based on image reference. In the 2D-CT image group, the PA reviewed the uploaded CT images using a tablet (Surface Pro 9, Microsoft) and an image viewer software (RadiANT DICOM VIEWER, Medixant). In the 3D-CT image group, the PA reviewed the 3D-CT images using the same tablet and an image viewer software 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2; https://www.slicer.org). The cutting angles before and after image reference were recorded and compared to evaluate the influence of imaging on grossing decisions.

A postgrossing questionnaire (Table E1) was administered to assess the impact of image guidance on specimen handling. Participants responded to 5 questions using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), evaluating tumor identification by palpation (Q1), impact on tumor identification (Q2A), impact on cutline angle (Q2B), impact on cutline location (Q2C), impact of image reference on grossing (Q3), and the contribution of image reference to cut at the center of the tumor (Q4) and cut in the line with closest margin (Q5).

The 3D-CT–based cut-line margin was measured using 3D Slicer by measuring the angle between the actual cut line (marked during grossing) and the CT axial line (Figure 2, C). Subsequently, the 2D-CT margin and cut-line margin were compared with the pathological margin to assess the correlation.

Questionnaire results and margin correlations were further analyzed in subgroups, dividing cases into those with lung cancers and those with metastatic tumors.

Pathological Margin Assessment

Tissue slices were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm using a Shandon Finesse ME + microtome (Thermo Scientific). The sections were mounted on glass slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The shortest distance from the tumor to the outer stapled resection line was assessed by a pathologist and recorded as the pathological margin. The pathological margin was defined as the measured parenchymal margin plus the measured thickness of the removed staple line.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software). Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test for 3 groups or the Mann–Whitney U test for 2 groups. The correlation between the 3D-CT margin and the pathological margin was assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient, and linear regression analysis was performed to visualize the relationship.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Figure E2 illustrates the flowchart of this study. Between December 2024 and October 2025, a total of 50 patients who provided consent to participate in this study and underwent pulmonary wedge resection at our hospital were enrolled. After excluding 1 patient who could not undergo specimen CT scanning, 7 due to failure of specimen collection caused by prior sectioning, 2 due to changes in the surgical procedure, and 1 due to unclear CT image, the final cohort consisted of 39 participants with a total of 45 specimens. These specimens were randomly assigned into 3 groups (control, 2D-CT, and 3D-CT) of 15 specimens per group.

Table 1 summarizes the patient characteristics. Of the 45 specimens, 16 cases were primary lung cancer and 29 cases were metastatic lung cancer. The mean tumor diameter was 11.7 ± 4.7 mm, and the mean distance from the pleura was 14 ± 7 mm. Of the 45 lesions, 32 were pure solid nodules. In contrast, 9 were part-solid GGNs, and 4 were pure GGNs. Preoperative marking was performed in 13 specimens, all of which involved microcoil insertion. Most procedures were conducted using minimally invasive techniques, including VATS (n = 41) and robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (n = 1); however, 3 cases were resected via thoracotomy. After CT image review, the 2D-CT group showed an average change in the cut-line angle of 6.5 ± 6.5°, whereas the 3D-CT group showed a change of 8.2 ± 11.1°.

Questionnaire Results

Questionnaire responses regarding the perceived impact of imaging on specimen handling are summarized in Figure 3 and Table 2. Across all specimens (n = 45), differences were not statistically significant for any parameter; however, 3D-CT tended to achieve higher scores for impact on cut-line angles (Q2B) (median 4 [3-4]) and impact on grossing (Q3) (median 4 [3-5]) than 2D-CT. In the lung cancer subgroup (n = 16), 3D-CT demonstrated favorable scores for impact on tumor identification (Q2A) (median 4 [2-4.25]), impact on cut-line location (Q2C) (median 4 [3-4.25]), and overall impact on grossing (Q3) (median 4 [2.75-5]). Especially, impact on cut-line angle (Q2B) was significantly favorable (median 4 [3–4.25]) compared with 2D-CT (median 2.5 [2-3]) (P = .03). Summary of lung cancer specimens are shown in Table E2. In contrast, in the metastatic tumor subgroup (n = 29), although 3D-CT tended to achieve higher scores for overall impact on grossing (Q3), there was no clear benefit of image review for tumor identification (Q2A), cut-line angle (Q2B), or cut-line location (Q2C). Correlation Between Computed Tomography–Based Margins and Pathological Margins

Figure 4 shows the correlation between CT-based margins and pathological margin evaluated in 45 specimens, including lung cancer (n = 16) and metastatic tumors (n = 29). For 2D-CT margins, correlation coefficients were r = 0.87 (all cases), r = 0.84 (lung cancer), and r = 0.91 (metastatic tumors). For 3D-CT cut-line margins, correlations were even stronger: r = 0.92, r = 0.9, and r = 0.95, respectively. In all comparisons, the correlation was slightly weaker in lung cancer cases than in metastatic tumor cases.

Discussion

This randomized prospective study demonstrated 2 key findings. First, intraoperative imaging, particularly 3D-CT, enhanced tumor localization and enabled pathology assistants to make more assured and accurate grossing decisions. Second, the margin evaluated using intraoperative CT images showed a strong correlation with the pathological margin, reaffirming its validity as a reliable approach.

Most tumors in this study were palpable; therefore, reviewing intraoperative CT imaging did not significantly influence tumor identification during grossing. However, a trend toward greater usefulness was observed for lesions that were difficult to palpate. As demonstrated in representative case 2, there were instances in which tumor palpation was extremely challenging. These findings suggest that although the strategy did not show a statistically significant difference in solid tumors, it may have a greater impact on tumors that are challenging to palpate, such as GGNs, potentially improving patient care. Nevertheless, the present study did not directly demonstrate whether changes in the cut-line angle based on intraoperative CT imaging translated into measurable differences in pathological margin distances. Although prior knowledge of tumor localization may facilitate more accurate gross sectioning in difficult to palpate or irregular lesions, this potential benefit remains speculative and should be validated in future studies with margin-based oncological end points.

Additionally, when considering the determination of resection lines and angles, a greater number of cases were perceived to benefit from 3D images compared with 2D images, especially in lung cancer cases as opposed to metastatic cancer. This observation is supported by the slightly higher average rate of angle adjustments in the 3D group. Beyond this, the proportion of cases in which imaging was considered to have influenced the final resection approach was higher in the 3D group, indicating that the ability to visualize tumor location and staple lines in 3 dimensions may offer a distinct advantage. When evaluating whether the tumor was cut through its center or at the site of the narrowest margin, no significant differences were observed among the groups, including the control group. This outcome likely reflects the fact that most tumors were grossly palpable during specimen handling. Overall, although routine image review may not be necessary for every case, our findings suggest that selective use of the intraoperative imaging method, particularly 3D evaluation, could be beneficial in cases where tumor palpation is difficult, such as those involving small ground-glass opacity components that are located deep within the lung.

The margin evaluated by CT image showed a strong correlation with the pathological margin. However, some variability was observed, and the margins did not perfectly match, especially in lung cancer specimens. Several factors may account for this discrepancy.

1.Tissue shrinkage during formalin fixation: Previous studies have reported that lung parenchyma and tumors shrink during fixation.13,14 Kamimura and colleagues13 observed an approximate 15% shrinkage of margins after formalin fixation in wedge resections.

Additionally, lung cancer cases exhibited greater variability compared with metastatic tumors.15, 16, 17 We believe this is largely due to the irregular contours of lung cancers, which make precise delineation on CT images more challenging. Addressing this limitation remains one of the key tasks for improving CT-based margin evaluation methods.

Previously, we have reported the utility of intraoperative CT imaging using both preclinical model and clinical specimens.10,18 In this study, we further extended our investigation to evaluate whether such imaging could be beneficial during pathological specimen processing. Although several studies have assessed surgical margins using intraoperative CT imaging,8,13 to our knowledge, this is the first report to apply imaging reference specifically at the time of pathological grossing. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommends an optimal margin of at least 20 mm or a margin equal to or greater than the tumor diameter for sublobar resections.19 Likewise, the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons recommends a margin of at least 10 mm or a margin-to-tumor diameter ratio of 1 or greater.20 In cases where the margin is insufficient, additional resection or postoperative radiation therapy may be considered to prevent local recurrence. Therefore, accurate measurement of the pathological margin is critically important. Achieving this requires precise localization of the tumor during specimen handling and resection along the shortest possible margin path. This study demonstrates that imaging reference, particularly using 3D reconstruction, may serve as a practical and efficient tool for margin evaluation. The results suggest that 3D imaging provides distinct advantages in accurately identifying tumor locations and guiding the grossing of wedge resection specimens, thereby contributing to more reliable pathological margin assessments.

Limitations

There are some limitations in this study. First, although there were cases in which the resection angle and line were altered based on intraoperative imaging reference, it remains unclear whether these changes had a direct impact on the actual pathological margins. In the present cohort, with a median follow-up of 7.2 months (range, 0.1-13.2 months), no patients showed clear locoregional recurrence. Nevertheless, whether such CT-guided sectioning contributes to reducing the risk of local recurrence at the surgical margin or whether this should influence decisions regarding additional treatment warrants further investigation in larger cohorts with longer follow-up. Second, the questionnaire results obtained from 8 PAs are inherently subjective. Although all 8 participants were skilled professionals, the possibility of bias due to the limited sample size cannot be excluded. Although the resection technique was standardized within the department, individual interpretations may have influenced the responses. Third, in some cases, preoperative insertion of microcoils resulted in metal artifacts, which may have affected the delineation of tumor margins. These image artifacts could have influenced the accuracy of margin measurements, and their impact should be considered in future studies. Additionally, the study is exploratory and underpowered, which may limit the ability to detect certain effects or differences. In particular, for the lung cancer subgroup (n = 16) distributed across 3 arms, the small sample size further limits the interpretability of statistically significant findings in individual questionnaire domains. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and provide more definitive conclusions. Furthermore, specimens from the same patient were treated as independent cases for statistical analysis, which may violate the assumption of independence due to clustering within patients, the same surgeon, and the same grossing environment. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results. Finally, the use of such CT scanners may not be feasible in all institutions, which could limit the generalizability of this approach. As a next step toward broader applicability, we are planning a follow-up study using cone-beam CT, which is increasingly available in operating rooms at general hospitals.

Conclusions

We concluded that intraoperative imaging, particularly 3D-CT, enhanced tumor localization and enabled PAs to make more assured and accurate grossing decisions. In certain cases, it enabled clear localization of tumors that were otherwise difficult to palpate, thereby facilitating more appropriate margin evaluation. In addition, the margin evaluated using intraoperative CT images showed a strong correlation with the pathological margin, reaffirming its validity as a clinically reliable approach. We believe that this method, when applied to appropriately selected cases, such as small, deeply located nodules, can improve the precision of specimen grossing and contribute to more accurate pathological assessment.
