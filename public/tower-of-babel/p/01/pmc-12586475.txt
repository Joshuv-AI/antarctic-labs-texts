Study on the effect of unstable surface balance training on lower limb dynamic balance ability and stroke effect of table tennis players
Authors: Han Li, Yifan Liu, Xiaolu Zhou, Zengrui Yang, Yi Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to investigate the effect of unstable surface balance training (USBT) on the dynamic balance ability of the lower limb and stroke effect of table tennis players. A double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT) design was conducted in this study. A total of 103 table tennis players were randomly divided into an experimental group (ExG, n = 52) and a control group (ConG, n = 51). The ExG engaged in their regular table tennis training along with an additional 8-week USBT program. The ConG only participated in regular table tennis training. Both groups underwent the Y-balance test (YBT) and stroke effect test before and after the intervention. Before the intervention, there were no significant differences in the YBT and stroke effect test results between the ExG and ConG. After eight weeks of intervention, there were significant differences in YBT and stroke effect between the ExG and ConG (all p < 0.01). The ExG showed significant differences in YBT and stroke effect test indicators between the pre- and post-intervention (all p < 0.01), while the ConG did not show significant changes in any test indicators (all p > 0.05). Introduction

Table tennis is a sport that requires high levels of physical coordination and agility for players, characterized by fast ball speed, strong spin, and unpredictable placement. Before hitting the ball, table tennis players need to prejudge the incoming ball’s trajectory, quickly move to the appropriate hitting position, and adjust their body posture and the body center of gravity to prepare adequately for the stroke1. An effective stroke in table tennis involves complex spatial movements, including quick movements, acceleration, deceleration, and changes in direction. This necessitates a good dynamic balance ability to maintain body stability during rapid movements and strokes, which in turn helps players achieve good stroke effect. Therefore, a table tennis player’s dynamic balance ability is crucial for enhancing stroke effect2.

Studies have shown that improved dynamic balance ability can effectively enhance athletic performance such as body postural control, quick movements, and changes in direction et al.3. Excellent dynamic balance ability is a key prerequisite for becoming a high-level athlete4. Furthermore, as table tennis is a multiple-set sport, players need stable body postural control during matches and daily training to execute continuous strokes and maintain good movement patterns1. Dynamic balance ability is a fundamental neuromuscular skill that can be enhanced through training5. Players’ kinetic perception ability can be improved by dynamic balance training, which contributes to better athletic performance6.

The hitting process of table tennis is a ‘segment-chain’ movement, in which, the coordination movement of various body joints plays a crucial role in achieving a more synchronized movement pattern7. As the starting point for energy transfer, the lower limb can effectively transmit energy along the segment chain to the upper limbs, providing the power needed for the stroke8. The dynamic balance ability of the lower limb is crucial for athletes to perform complex sports tasks9. Improving dynamic balance capability in the lower limb contributes to enhancing the efficiency of energy transfer in the open-chain movement of striking, thereby increasing the striking power of the arm, enhancing the friction between the racket and the ball, and consequently improving the stroke effect8. During the entire striking process of a table tennis player, the coordination and control of the body’s dynamic posture are essential. Only by maintaining the body stability of their dynamic posture can athletes effectively transfer the power from the lower limb to the upper limbs, thereby achieving high-quality strokes10.

Balance ability refers to the body’s capability to maintain a stable posture both at rest and during movement or when subjected to external forces11. Balance ability includes static balance ability and dynamic balance ability. Static balance ability is the capacity to keep the body center of gravity and posture relatively stable while at rest12. Dynamic balance ability is the natural capacity to adjust one’s body to keep a stable posture while in motion or when subjected to external forces. This capability is affected by factors like the height of the body center of gravity and the size and stability of the supporting surface. It relies on the central nervous system’s ability to coordinate visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular inputs, in addition to controlling motor effectors10,13.

Balance ability has been proven to improve athletic performance in many sports items, such as tennis, volleyball, and soccer, et al. Hrysomallis et al. suggested that balance ability is related to the quality of technical actions and competition performance, and athletes with stronger balance ability perform technical actions more smoothly4. Shi et al. concluded that improving dynamic balance ability can enhance the precision of footwork, optimize body control, and stabilize joints during rapid movements in tennis players, thereby increasing the ball speed and stability of returns10. Myer et al. argued that dynamic balance training can reduce force attenuation during single-leg jump landings in volleyball players, thereby reducing impact forces, preventing injuries, and improving posture control, which in turn enhances athletic performance14. Dynamic balance training, as a crucial part of dynamic neuromuscular coordination training programs, can significantly improve athletes’ neuromuscular coordination and stability. It is also effective in increasing the lower limb neuromuscular strength and control14. Medeni et al. believed that improving the dynamic balance ability of the lower limb can enhance the stability of the body center of gravity during landing after jumps in volleyball players, thereby affecting their performance15. Evangelos et al. found that balance training can significantly improve the technical level of short and long passes in amateur soccer players (p < 0.05), thereby increasing the accuracy of their passes16. Yaggie and Campbell observed that balance training on unstable surfaces significantly enhanced individual mobility and posture control abilities, specifically improving agility in shuttle run and various movements such as forward and backward3. Zech et al. concluded that although balance training is less effective than traditional strength training methods in improving lower limb muscle strength, it can more effectively enhance the body posture and neuromuscular control ability17.

Common balance training methods include stable surface balance training and unstable surface balance training (USBT)18. Stable surface balance training, such as standing on one foot with eyes open or standing on one foot with eyes closed, is a form of static balance exercise. Its main purpose is to enhance the body’s ability to maintain static balance by keeping the body balanced on a supporting surface while relatively stationary.

USBT involves balance training that leverages changes in the stability of the supporting surface, symmetrical imbalance due to movement, and imbalances in internal and external forces acting on the body caused by unexpected external forces19. This can be achieved by performing various degrees of unstable support exercises on equipment such as a BOSU trainer, balance pad, half-foam roller, DynaDisc, wobble board, and Swiss ball. These exercises simulate unstable environments, enabling the body to more effectively mobilize and adjust muscle groups in response to external disturbances to maintain balance. This type of training is particularly effective in improving dynamic balance abilities in complex situations3,20,21. Additionally, in a study by Attar, the training method where subjects practiced passing and catching a ball while balancing on one leg to challenge dynamic balance control improved athletes’ dynamic balance ability22.

Training for balance abilities on unstable surfaces can improve athletes’ dynamic balance capabilities. However, the degree of instability varies with different unstable devices21. Stanek et al. assessed the balance performance of subjects after training with four types of unstable devices. The results indicated that, compared to the balance pad and the half-foam roller, the BOSU trainer and the DynaDisc had higher difficulty levels, with the BOSU trainer providing the most unstable environment23. Badr created an unstable condition for the experimental group by introducing the BOSU ball, while the control group trained on a stable ground environment. The experimental results showed that unstable condition training with the BOSU ball was more effective in enhancing the technical actions and physical fitness of handball players24. Yaggie conducted a four-week intervention training with the BOSU ball and found that BOSU balance training significantly improved subjects’ dynamic stability and postural control abilities3.

Through USBT, athletes can better perceive the position and status of their lower limbs and maintain stable lower limb support during rapid movements and racket swings, thus improving the stability of their body’s dynamic posture. Borreani et al. believed that USBT can effectively enhance sensorimotor control of the soft tissues around the knee and ankle joints, thereby improving the stability of these joints19. Shi et al. suggested that by enhancing the activation of the ankle and knee joints in perceiving body position and regulating balance, the muscle proprioceptors and nervous system can better control the body during movement10.

The explosive power of the lower limb serves as the energy source for the entire stroke of table tennis players8. Iino and Kojima noted that athletes with better lower limb explosive power often complete strokes with better quality25. Moreover, strong lower limb muscle strength provides the necessary power support for athletes to maintain body posture stability and dynamic balance during quick movements and strokes. Myer et al. found that USBT can significantly increase the open-chain isokinetic hamstring strength, and additionally, the hamstring to quadriceps strength ratio (H/Q ratio) also significantly improved14. Anderson and Behm demonstrated that squatting on unstable surfaces activates the soleus muscle to a higher degree compared to stable surfaces18. Therefore, USBT intervention can improve the strength of lower limb muscles, thereby enhancing the dynamic balance ability of the lower limbs during exercise and improving the stroke effect.

The testing of dynamic balance ability primarily includes the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) and the Y-Balance Test (YBT) modified based on SEBT26,27. SEBT has been proven to be a reliable method for measuring dynamic postural control and can identify the dynamic balance ability of the lower limb28–30. YBT has been widely used in recent years due to its advantages of easy operation, shorter time consumption, and more standardized processes31,32. This method has high reliability32.

In summary, dynamic balance training has been widely recognized for its significant impact on the athletic performance of players in sports such as volleyball, tennis, and gymnastics. However, the effect of USBT on the dynamic balance ability and athletic performance of table tennis players remains unexplored. Studies related to table tennis predominantly concentrate on the strength, speed, agility, etc33,34. Elite table tennis players not only need to possess high-level technical and tactical skills, but also require good dynamic balance ability to ensure athletic performance. Therefore, the impact of USBT on the dynamic balance ability of table tennis players and its relationship with their athletic performance merits further investigation. This study primarily focused on the dynamic balance ability of table tennis players and its influence on the stroke effect. Based on the USBT methods of other sports and combined with the characteristics of table tennis stroke movements, this study designed targeted lower limb USBT methods for table tennis players to enhance their dynamic balance ability and stroke effect. Simultaneously, given the crucial role of the lower limb in the segment-chain movements involved in table tennis strokes, this study aims to examine the effect of an 8-week USBT program on the lower limb dynamic balance ability and stroke effect of table tennis players. The findings will contribute new scientific data to the existing literature, provide scientific training methods for enhancing the dynamic balance ability of table tennis players, and ultimately improve their competition performance.

Methods

Participants

A total of 80 participants were needed for this study to reach a power of 80% at an alpha level of 0.05 based on a statistical power analysis to detect an effect size of ES = 0.2535. A total of 103 table tennis players (male = 51, female = 52) were recruited from the Table Tennis College and randomly divided into an experimental group (ExG, n = 52) and a control group (ConG, n = 51) according to a computer-generated randomization list (see Fig. 1 for the recruitment process). The recruitment of the participants began on May 28, 2024 and ended on June 3, 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: table tennis players with technical skill levels of National Grade 2 or above, right-handed (dominant hand), aged 18–28 years, in good health with no diseases, no injuries in the past year, and capable of engaging in moderate or higher-intensity sports training. The exclusion criteria included: history of lower limb surgery, history of injuries or diseases affecting balance function, and any physical conditions or injuries preventing high-intensity sports training. The basic information of all subjects was shown in Table 1. This study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and the regulations of China and was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (Approval Number: 102772024RT066). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study.

Study design and blinding

A double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT) design was conducted in this study. Neither the evaluators nor the participants were aware of the grouping assignments. The ExG and ConG received identical instructions regarding the requirements and precautions during testing. All testing and training in this study were conducted in the training hall on the third floor of China Table Tennis College.

Experimental protocol

The study was conducted in three phases, specifically: the pre-test was conducted from June 10, 2024 to June 14, 2024, an 8-week USBT intervention was performed from June 17, 2024 to August 9, 2024, and the post-test was implemented from August 12, 2024 to August 16, 2024.

In the first stage (pre-test), measurements were conducted on body characteristics, lower limb dynamic balance ability, and stroke effect.

In the second stage (intervention), both the ExG and ConG underwent daily table tennis training, which included forehand and backhand drives, forehand and backhand loops, random full-court loops, attack after serve, and attack after receive. In addition to the daily table tennis training, the ExG also received USBT aimed at improving lower limb dynamic balance ability. To achieve the effect of dynamic balance training, USBT adhered to the dose-response relationship for balance training on balance performance36. Specifically, dynamic balance training was conducted three times a week over 8 weeks, with each session for about 30 min. Additionally, this study followed a progressive principle, changing the training regimen every two weeks (Supplementary Table 1). This progression involved increasing the challenge by reducing support surface area, increasing deviation from a stable position, transitioning from static to dynamic balance exercises, and decreasing visual input (eyes closed) to gradually escalate difficulty37. USBT utilized unstable equipment such as a BOSU ball and balance disk to simulate unstable environments.

In the third stage (post-test), the two groups repeated the measurements from the first stage.

Instrument

Stroke effect test.

Stroke effect test.

Before the formal test, each participant was required to warm up for 5 min. Then, each participant was asked to perform a high-quality combination of backhand push followed by forehand backspin for 10 consecutive shots. The backspin balls were delivered by a Serving machine (V-989 H, Nittaku) at a frequency of 40 balls per minute. As the players performed the strokes, a Speedometer (BUSHNELL Company, USA) was used to measure the ball speed of the stroke. In order to evaluate the stroke accuracy of players, five rectangles with a different area of 10 cm by 10 cm to 50 cm by 50 cm were drawn at the left-side bottom corners of the table tennis table. The five regions correspond to 1–5 points respectively. The smaller the area, the higher the points players scored. A higher score represents a higher degree of stroke accuracy. The experimental set-up was shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental set-up of the ball shooting speed and accuracy.

(2) Dynamic balance training

Dynamic balance training

The BOSU ball and the DynaDisc (Joinfit, PRC) were used for subjects to perform lower limb dynamic balance exercises.

(3) Dynamic balance ability test

Dynamic balance ability test

The dynamic balance ability of subjects was evaluated by the YBT. The participants stood barefoot on the Y balance board (Y Balance Test KitTM, Danville, VA) with the tip of their big toe on the starting line. To prevent the arm swinging from influencing the test results, subjects were required to place both hands on the waist. Then, from a single-leg standing position, the participants bent the supporting leg into a squat and extended the other leg’s tiptoe as far as possible in three directions (anterior A, posteromedial PM, and posterolateral PL) along the board (Fig. 3). The subjects were instructed to look forward throughout the test. If the supporting leg lifted or moved off the testing board, the blocks were kicked, the testing leg touched the ground, or the subject lost balance and could not return the testing leg to its initial position, the trial was considered a failure and was retested.

Y-balance test.

Before the formal testing, participants practiced three trials in each of the three directions (A, PM, and PL) with both the left and right legs32. Each direction was tested three times, with a 30-second rest between different directions and trials. Both legs were tested separately, starting with the left leg, followed by the right leg. The average value of the three trials was used to evaluate the participant’s balance performance in that direction32.

The overall performance (OP) of balance ability for the left and right legs can be calculated using the results from three directional tests. The specific formula was as follows: OP = (a + b + c) / (3 * len) * 10038, where a, b, and c represent the YBT test results for the three directions of either the left or right leg and the len denotes the length of the participant’s lower limb (centimeter): from the anterior superior iliac spine to the most distal portion of the medial malleolus.

Data processing

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26. Data were normally distributed. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the mean and standard deviation for all variables. A 2 by 2 (group × time) mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures on the time factor was conducted to assess potential differences between groups and times for each variable. The F-value and partial eta squared (η²) were also calculated. The Bonferroni correction method was applied to locate differences in statistically significant interactions. A probability level of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered significant in all statistical analyses.

Differences of the stroke effect

According to Table 3, there were no significant differences in the ball speed and placement between the ExG and ConG in the pre-test (all p > 0.05). After eight weeks of training, however, there were significant differences in the stroke effect between the ExG and ConG (all p < 0.01).

According to Table 4, significant differences in the stroke effect were observed between the pre- and post-test for the ExG (all p < 0.01). For the ConG, there were no significant differences in the stroke effect between the pre- and post-test (all p > 0.05). However, the post-test results showed improvements compared to the pre-test, indicating that USBT can also significantly improve athletes’ stroke effect.

Discussion

This study examined the effect of 8-week USBT on the lower limb dynamic balance ability and stroke effect of table tennis players. The study found that after 8-week USBT intervention training, both the lower limb dynamic balance ability and stroke effect of the ExG showed significant improvements compared to the pre-test results. The ConG also showed improvements in lower limb dynamic balance ability and stroke effect, however, the differences were not significant. The ExG exhibited significantly higher lower limb dynamic balance ability and stroke effect compared to the ConG at the end of the intervention.

The impact of USBT on the dynamic balance ability of the lower limb

The post-test results of YBT show significant improvements in all test indicators and the OP of dynamic balance ability in both the two legs of the ExG. This aligns with previous studies that found unstable balance training programs can enhance athletes’ dynamic balance ability39,40. Encarnación-Martínez et al. found that participants’ dynamic balance ability was significantly improved through USBT using BOSU balls39. The findings of Mitrousis et al. indicated that adolescent soccer players could enhance their dynamic balance ability through an 8-week USBT program40. They also argued that the improvement in balance ability was mainly due to the enhanced adaptation of the nervous system (development of neuromuscular coordination and increased neuromuscular control), resulting from the decreased excitability of the H-reflex and myotatic reflex in closed-chain kinetic exercises40, which may also explain our results.

The impact of the lower limb dynamic balance ability on stroke effect

The post-test results of the stroke effect indicated that the ExG showed significant improvements in ball speed and placement. This is consistent with previous research findings that enhancing athletes’ dynamic balance ability can improve their stroke effect10. Stroke stability relies heavily on the dynamic balance of the body, and only with stable support can the upper and lower limbs coordinate their strength effectively, thereby improving the stroke quality41. After executing a backhand push, athletes need to promptly adjust their footwork to move to an appropriate hitting position. They must also adjust their body posture, timing, hitting power, racket angle, and swing speed, et al. to fully prepare for the subsequent forehand topspin stroke, ensuring the accuracy and stability of the subsequent stroke42. This rapid footwork change and body posture adjustment require strong lower limb dynamic balance ability. Chen et al. suggested that the balance training contributed to enhancing the players’ ability to quickly adjust body posture and respond to changes in ball trajectory, speed, and spin43. Excellent lower limb dynamic balance ability enables table tennis players to move quickly and adjust their body posture accurately to respond to the balls coming from different directions and at varying speeds during matches. Enhancing this ability helps athletes choose better hitting positions, thereby increasing their stroke effect. According to Shi et al., improving the dynamic balance ability, footwork accuracy, body posture control rationality, and joint stability can be effectively improved during rapid movements of tennis players, which in turn enhances the ball speed and placement stability of their returns. This can also explain the results of our study10.

The difference of the dynamic balance ability between dominant and non-dominant legs and its impact on stroke effect

Tracey et al. examined the correlation between the balance ability of soccer players on single-leg with the speed and accuracy of shooting with the other leg and found that the balance ability of the supporting leg (left leg) plays a crucial role in the shooting accuracy of the dominant leg (right leg)44. This is primarily because soccer players often rely on their dominant leg during daily technical training, thereby enhancing the stability and balance ability of the supporting leg.

Table tennis players predominantly use their dominant leg (right leg) for force generation during daily training, which explains why the dynamic balance ability of the right leg overall appeared better performance than the left leg in the pre-test results of YBT. This is determined by the characteristics of table tennis techniques and the movements of the players. Interestingly, the post-test results of the YBT showed a more significant improvement in the overall performance of the non-dominant leg (left leg) (p < 0.001) compared to that of the dominant leg (p < 0.01). This finding may indicate that while the dominant leg plays a critical role in athletes’ lower limb dynamic balance ability, the improvement in dynamic balance ability of the non-dominant leg is equally important for a player’ athletic performance. It contributes to balanced development and overall enhancement of athletes’ lower limb dynamic balance ability, enabling them to stabilize their bodies more stably during striking, reducing power loss of striking due to the swinging of the lower limb, and thereby improving striking speed and accuracy.

Practical implications

It is essential to incorporate the USBT into the daily training of table tennis players. By adopting unstable balance equipment such as BOSU ball and DynaDisc to simulate the unstable scenarios of competition, and formulating targeted dynamic balance training plans, table tennis players can improve their ability to maintain body balance under unstable conditions, thereby enhancing their dynamic balance ability and stroke effect.

Limitations

The dynamic balance ability of the upper limbs is also crucial for the athletic performance of table tennis players. The experiment only tested the dynamic balance ability of the athletes’ lower limbs, neglecting the potential impact of the upper limbs’ dynamic balance ability on the stroke effect. Future studies could incorporate the upper limb dynamic balance ability into the testing indicators to explore its relationship with stroke effect.
