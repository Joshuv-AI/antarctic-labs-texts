Fluence to dose equivalent conversion coefficients for ICRP110 voxel phantoms with aluminum and polyethylene shielding using GEANT4
Authors: Ran Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluence-to-dose-equivalent conversion coefficients are essential for assessing astronaut radiation exposure in space. In this study, we calculate these coefficients for the ICRP110 male and female voxel phantoms under multiple shielding configurations: aluminum shells with mass thicknesses of 5, 10, 20, and 30 g/cm2, and a polyethylene shell with 30 g/cm2. Simulations are performed using the GEANT4 Monte Carlo toolkit for all ions from Z = 1 to 28 over a kinetic energy range of 1 MeV/n to 100 GeV/n. Dose equivalents are computed using both the ICRP60 and NASA quality factors. Organ-specific dose coefficients and their energy dependence are presented, providing reference data for spacecraft shielding design. At solar maximum, when the GCR flux is at its minimum, the effective dose equivalent is reduced by up to 31% and 18% using the ICRP60 and NASA quality factors, respectively, for the thickest shielding considered.

Introduction

Galactic cosmic rays (GCRs) constitute a persistent and highly penetrating radiation field that poses significant health risks to astronauts on long-duration missions beyond low Earth orbit1. Comprising all ions from hydrogen to nickel with energies extending up to several TeV/n, GCRs induce complex particle cascades within spacecraft materials and human tissue, leading to both direct ionization and secondary particle production. Unlike solar particle events, which are intermittent and anisotropic, GCRs are quasi-isotropic and continuously present, making them the dominant contributor to the cumulative dose equivalent on missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond2.

Passive shielding remains the primary practical countermeasure against GCR exposure. Spacecraft structures traditionally employ aluminum due to its favorable mechanical properties and extensive flight heritage. However, aluminum’s moderate atomic number and density limit its ability to attenuate high-energy GCR ions. Furthermore, nuclear interactions within the shielding can produce secondary neutrons and fragments that may even increase organ doses3. Consequently, alternative materials with higher hydrogen content, such as polyethylene, have attracted considerable attention. Hydrogen-rich materials enhance ionization energy loss per unit mass and efficiently moderate neutrons, potentially offering superior shielding performance at an equivalent areal density4,5.

Quantitative assessment of shielding effectiveness requires accurate fluence-to-dose-equivalent conversion coefficients, which relate the incident particle fluence to the dose equivalent received by specific organs. The reference data provided by ICRP Publication 1232 cover unshielded conditions and were calculated using the PHITS Monte Carlo code6,7. Subsequent studies have extended these calculations using other toolkits such as GEANT48 and have considered some limited shielding configurations9. However, systematic investigations across multiple shielding thicknesses and direct comparisons between aluminum and hydrogenous materials at an equal mass thickness remain sparse. In particular, the dose reduction achieved by increasing aluminum thickness from typical spacecraft hull values (5–10 g/cm2) to heavier configurations (20–30 g/cm2) has not been comprehensively documented. Furthermore, the relative performance of polyethylene at the same mass thickness has not been quantified with a consistent human phantom model and modern physics lists.

This study aims to fill these gaps by calculating fluence-to-dose-equivalent conversion coefficients for the ICRP110 adult male and female voxel phantoms10 under five shielding configurations: aluminum shells with mass thicknesses of 5, 10, 20, and 30 g/cm2, and a polyethylene shell of 30 g/cm2. Simulations are performed with the GEANT4 toolkit11–13 using the QBBC physics list14, and dose equivalents are evaluated with both the ICRP6015 and NASA3 quality factors. The resulting organ-specific coefficients are integrated with a recent GCR model16 to estimate dose equivalent rates. These data provide a reference for spacecraft shielding design and enable more accurate astronaut radiation risk assessments.

Methods

All Monte Carlo simulations were carried out using the GEANT4 toolkit (version 11.2.2 with its associated physics datasets)11–13, an open-source code widely used for particle transport and dosimetry calculations in space radiation environments. Unshielded GEANT4-based dose coefficients and dose rates have been calculated and compared with ICRP Publication 123 in a previous study8. Here the QBBC physics list14 was employed throughout this study, as it is specifically recommended for space and medical applications and provides a balanced treatment of electromagnetic and hadronic interactions. This physics list combines the Binary cascade model for light hadrons at low energies with the Bertini cascade at higher energies, and uses the standard electromagnetic package with the G4EmStandardPhysics option. Validation against other physics lists (e.g., FTFP_BERT) has shown agreement within a few percent for dose equivalent rates8.

The simulations covered all positively charged ions from hydrogen to nickel (Z = 1 to 28), which constitute the major components of the galactic cosmic ray spectrum. For each ion species, calculations were performed at 36 logarithmically spaced kinetic energy points for protons and 27 energy points for all other ions, spanning the range from 1 MeV/n to 100 GeV/n. This energy interval encompasses the peak of the GCR flux as well as the high-energy tail where nuclear fragmentation becomes significant. The number of primary events per energy point was adjusted to balance statistical accuracy and computational cost (several 104 CPU hours per shielding configuration): 3 × 106 events for protons, 106 for helium, 3 × 105 for oxygen, silicon, and iron, and 105 for all other ions. For energies above 5 GeV/n, where the copious production of secondary particles slows down the simulation, the statistics were reduced but always exceeded 104 events, ensuring that the statistical uncertainty of the effective dose equivalent remained below 0.2%8 as propagated from the root-mean-square fluctuations of the simulated doses for each ion species, energy point, and organ/tissue. The default GEANT4 random number seed 1 was used.

The ICRP110 adult male and female computational voxel phantoms10 were implemented as the target geometry. These phantoms consist of millions of voxels, each tagged with a specific organ or tissue identifier and its corresponding elemental composition. The more recent ICRP Publication 14517 refines the same underlying human bodies18 to mesh-based phantoms for better resolution, but here we choose to align with previous works2,8,9 for direct comparison. Dose calculations were performed for 28 organs/tissues, comprising the 27 radiosensitive organs with non-zero tissue weighting factors defined in ICRP Publication 10319 and the eyes for deterministic cataract risk. The shell dimensions ensured that the entire phantom was enclosed within the uniform shielding layer, as the diameter was larger than 176 cm, the height of the male phantom. These thicknesses are representative of current spacecraft hull designs (5–10 g/cm2) and of heavier configurations proposed for storm shelters or long-duration habitats (20–30 g/cm2)3.

Isotropic irradiation was simulated by sampling particle directions uniformly over the 4π solid angle. For each direction, the emission point was randomly chosen from a circular beam cross-section with a radius of 100–125 cm, centered on the phantom’s mid-point. This radius exceeded the shell’s lateral dimensions, guaranteeing that the phantom receives dose contribution from all particles traversing the shielding shell. The particle directions were sampled isotropically over 4π steradians, with the azimuthal angle uniformly distributed in [0,2π ] and the cosine of the polar angle uniformly distributed over [-1,1].

Dose scoring was implemented by modifying the GEANT4 source code to record energy deposition in each sensitive organ at every simulation step, following the procedure detailed in reference8. Two additional accumulation variables were introduced: one for the dose equivalent using the ICRP60 quality factor and another using the NASA quality factor. For all incoming GCR elements, the unshielded case exhibits finite coefficients even at low energies, allowing for some dose contribution. In contrast, for the shielded cases, the coefficients consistently vanish below certain threshold energies, as particles lose their energy via the Bethe-Bloch mechanism and are fully stopped within the shielding material. As the shielding thickness increases, the minimum energy at which the coefficients become non-zero increases, and the peak values of the coefficients decrease. Conversely, at energies above the peak, the shielded dose coefficients can exceed the unshielded values, with this enhancement generally increasing with shielding thickness (with some counterexamples, such as for iron when using the dose-equivalent definitions). This is attributed to the production of secondary particles during interactions with the shielding material, which become energetically possible at high energies. However, this enhancement is of limited practical concern because the GCR spectrum is significantly suppressed in this high-energy range.

At the same mass thickness of 30 g/cm2, polyethylene provides better shielding than aluminum, particularly for high-Z GCR elements. This superior performance stems from two main factors. First, the Bethe-Bloch energy loss per unit mass thickness is influenced by the electron density of the material. Hydrogen-rich materials like polyethylene, having nuclei with a higher proton-to-neutron ratio, offer a higher electron density per unit mass. Second, for light particles like protons and neutrons, hydrogen nuclei (protons) are kinematically the most effective targets for energy transfer, allowing for up to 100% energy transfer in a single collision.

Conclusion

We have calculated fluence-to-dose-equivalent conversion coefficients for ICRP110 phantoms shielded by aluminum (5–30 g/cm2) and polyethylene (30 g/cm2) using GEANT4. The dose equivalent reduction at the maximum considered mass thickness varies with GCR solar modulation conditions, shielding material, quality factor definition, and target organ, but does not exceed 47%. Shielding is less effective during solar maximum due to the higher fraction of high-energy particles, which are more difficult to attenuate. At an equal mass thickness, polyethylene provides superior dose reduction due to its enhanced ionization energy loss and light particle moderation capabilities. A larger reduction fraction is achieved with the ICRP60 quality factor definition than with the NASA definition. The coefficients presented in this work are publicly available to support spacecraft shielding design and improve the accuracy of astronaut radiation exposure assessments.
