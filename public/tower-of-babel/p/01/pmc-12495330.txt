In vitro innate immune stimulation using beta-glucan in head kidney adherent macrophage-like cells derived from rohu, Labeo rohita
Authors: David Waikhom, Laishram Soniya Devi, Janmejay Parhi, Tanmoy Gon Choudhury, Sagar Chandra Mandal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•β−1,3-glucan upregulated cytokines (il6, tnfα, ifnγ) in Labeo rohita head kidney adherent macrophages-like cells.•mtor, hif1α, and hdac12 mRNA expression increased post β−1,3-glucan exposure.•Metabolite changes include elevated lactate and LDH, and decreased glucose, indicating innate immune memory in these adherent macrophage-like cells.Keywords: Cytokines, Epigenetic, Metabolites, Macrophage-like cells

Introduction

Rohu (Labeo rohita), a key species of Indian Major Carp, is vital in the global aquaculture market, especially in Southeast Asia, where demand is high. However, many factors are associated with an outbreak of diseases, including emerging and reemerging diseases such as Aeromonas spp., Edwardsiella tarda and Acinetobacter pittii infections and many more that threaten carp, leading to mass economic losses. Interestingly, various measures can be taken to address the problem faced by disease outbreaks. One of these prophylactic measures is vaccination, that targets specific pathogens. Therefore, to overcome a wide range of pathogens that can cause disease, various vaccines are required that have limited cost of production.

The use of β-glucan as a stimulating agent to enhance the innate immune system promotes the development of innate immune memory, providing cross-protection against a wide range of pathogens [[1], [2]]. Innate immune memory is acquired by epigenetic and metabolic alteration, which has short to long-lasting immune memory and non-specific cross-protection in nature [2]. This innate immune memory is induced by the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRR) and the actions of cytokines on innate immune cells that have the capacity to persist for weeks to months [[2], [3]]. Besides, TLR ligand-induced trained immunity enhances innate defense and survival by engaging tissue-specific NK cell subpopulations in the kidney and liver to mediate differential protective responses [4].

Classically, non-memory innate immune cells do not retain such functional changes; they respond to each new encounter in the same manner as the first, with no long-term enhancement once the initial response has resolved has been shifted to establish innate immune memory [1]. Innate immune memory, also called trained immunity, refers to a heightened and faster response of innate immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophil and NK cells after an initial exposure to a stimulus like β-glucan or a pathogen [1,5]. This enhanced reactivity upon re-exposure, even to unrelated pathogens, is driven by lasting epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming that boosts functions such as cytokine production, phagocytosis, and antimicrobial activity [6].

Macrophages are one of the innate immune cells which are considered professional phagocytes, releasing reactive species that could kill or eliminate pathogens in a non-specific manner. Macrophages in teleosts can be polarized into M1 and M2 types [7]. M1 macrophages are pro-inflammatory, characterized by their ability to produce cytokines and reactive oxygen species to combat pathogens, whereas M2 macrophages are anti-inflammatory, involved in tissue repair, wound healing, and regulating immune responses [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Diverse stimuli—including β-glucan, type I interferon, Astragalus polysaccharide (APS), LPS, and poly (I:C) —can activate macrophages, triggering a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, which leads to elevated lactate production [11]. This metabolic reprogramming activates glycolytic enzymes [12], which in turn activate transcription factors like mTOR and HIF1α, leading to the establishment of a trained macrophage phenotype [[13], [14]]. These transcription factors can induce chromatin changes, promoting the enrichment of histone markers such as H3K27 and H3K4me3 in the promoters and enhancers of proinflammatory genes, including TNFα, IL6, and IFNγ. Besides, APS (Astragalus polysaccharides) stimulation enhanced early pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in A. hydrophila-infected bass [15], consistent with β-glucan-induced innate immune memory or trained immunity in turbot [16]. This elevated response upon re-exposure reflects innate immune memory, suggesting APS as a potential inducer of trained immunity in fish [15].

Trained immunity is driven by key shifts in cellular metabolic, epigenetic changes, and upregulated gene expression, resulting in elevated cytokine levels (tnfα, il6, ifnγ) in macrophages. These responses boost broad, non-specific defense against repeated infections by similar or unrelated pathogens [17,18]. Various studies have been reported on trained immunity in invertebrates and vertebrate including mammals such as human, mice, dog, and goat macrophages [1,19,20]. Regarding teleosts, there were reported on common carp [21,22], tilapia, sevenband grouper, pufferfish and zebrafish [[4], [23], [24], [25]]. However, further exploration of trained immunity in rohu is required. There is limited research on head kidney macrophages possessing innate immune memory in Labeo rohita. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate immune expression patterns including one of epigenetic markers (hdac12) and metabolic changes in head kidney adherent macrophages-like cells post β−1,3-glucan treatment in rohu, L. rohita.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All experimental protocols involving the sacrifice of fish in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC), College of Fisheries, Central Agricultural University (Imphal), Lembucherra, Tripura.

Experimental animals

Rohu, Labeo rohita weighing 125±0.5 g was used for the experiment. The fish were procured from a fish farm under the Department of Fish Genetics and Reproduction, College of Fisheries, CAU (Imphal), Lembucherra, Tripura, India, and transported to its wet laboratory. Upon arrival, they were disinfected with 3 mg L⁻¹ KMnO₄ and acclimated for 15 days in 1000 L circular tanks with continuous aeration. During the quarantine period, the fish were fed a formulated feed produced by the college at a dose of 3 % of their body weight, and any signs of disease were monitored. The fish were apparently healthy and disease-free before use in the study.

Isolation of head kidney macrophages

Isolation of adherent macrophage-like cells from the rohu head kidney was performed using Histopaque-1077, as per our previous method [22] with slight modification. Briefly, rohu was euthanized using clove oil (0.05 mL in 500 mL water), and the head kidney was aseptically collected. The extracted head kidney was homogenized in ten volumes of PBS (Phosphate-buffered saline) supplemented with a 2 × concentration of an antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Himedia, India) containing streptomycin and penicillin and gently passed through a sterile 100 µm nylon net filter (Merck Millipore, Ireland). The resulting cell suspension was layered onto Histopaque-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) and centrifuged at 410 × g for 30 min at 25 °C without brakes. Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were harvested from the interface, diluted with PBS, and centrifuged at 260 × g for 10 min. Subsequently, MNCs were seeded at a density of 3.4 × 10⁷ cells mL⁻¹ in 25 cm² culture flasks (Tarsons, Korea) containing 10 mL of l-15 (Leibovitz-15) media (Gibco, New Zealand) enriched with 15 % heat-inactivated FBS (fetal bovine serum) post passed through 0.22 µm sterile syringe filter, 10 µg mL⁻¹ streptomycin and 10 µg mL⁻¹ penicillin (Himedia, India). After 24 h of incubation at 28 °C, nonadherent cells were removed by aspirating the supernatant, and attached cells were maintained in a complete l-15 medium for further studies.

Experimental design

24 h cultured adherent macrophage-like cells were grouped into three treatments viz., treatment 1 (T1; control as only 10 µL DMSO), treatment 2 (T2; stimulation with 10 µL of β−1, 3-glucan at a dosage of 50 μgwell−1;Sigma-Aldrich,USA) and treatment 3 (T3; restimulation, i.e., twice doses of 10 µL each of β−1,3-glucan at a dosage of 50 μgwell−1, with 7 days of interval between the first and second dose). The culture supernatant and cells were harvested at several time points (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h) and then processed for analysis of the metabolites and expression profiles study.

Evaluation of cell metabolic activity using resazurin reduction assay

The resazurin reduction assay was performed for treatments 1, 2, and 3 at various time points outlined in section 2.4 to evaluate cell metabolic activity throughout the experiment, following the protocols described by Riss et al․ [26] and Waikhom et al․ [22]. In brief, cells (2.4 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹) along with the test compounds were dispensed into a 96-well plate (covered with aluminum foil) with a final volume of 100 µL per well. Wells without cells served as blank controls. The plate was incubated for 24 h, after which 20 µL of resazurin was added to each well, followed by a further 4 h incubation at 37 °C. Absorbance was then recorded at 560 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Evaluation of metabolites production

Evaluation of lactate and LDH activity

Lactate concentration and LDH activity were estimated as described in section 2.4 using commercial kits (Himedia, India; Sigma-Aldrich, UK) following the manufacturer's instructions and Waikhom et al․ [22]. For LDH activity, 10 μL of culture supernatant was mixed with 100 μL of LDH reaction mixture and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. A maximum LDH control was prepared by lysing equivalent cell numbers for 15 min before sampling. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Thermo Scientific microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA), and LDH activity was calculated as a percentage of the maximum control. For lactate estimation, 50 μL of supernatant was mixed with assay buffer, lactate probe, and enzyme mix, incubated for 30 min, and measured at 570 nm. Lactate levels were calculated in μM using an l-lactate standard curve.

Evaluation of glucose consumption

The estimation of glucose consumption was performed using the glucose kit (Coral, India), following the collection of cell culture supernatants at different time points specified in section 2.4 from different treatment groups (T1, T2, and T3). In brief, 10 μL of each cell culture supernatant, glucose standard, and distilled water were thoroughly mixed with 1000 μL of glucose reagent respectively as a test, standard, and blank sample and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Following incubation, absorbance was measured at 505 nm wavelength using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). Total glucose in mg/dL was measured as absorbance of the test sample against the blank divided by the absorbance of the standard against the blank and multiplied by 100.

mRNA relative quantification of markers expression using qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from rohu-derived head kidney adherent macrophage-like cells post β−1,3-glucan treatment to the cells at different time points mentioned in section 2.4 using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The isolated total RNA was treated with DNase I (Thermo Scientific, USA) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination, which was subject to present in RNA samples prior to cDNA synthesis. First-strand cDNA synthesis was synthesized using cDNA synthesis kits (2 μg of treated RNA; Thermo Scientific, USA) containing M-MuLV reverse transcriptase following the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time PCR was carried out on a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystem, UK) using a 48-well plate. Each reaction (10 µL) contained 5 µL of 2 × SYBR Green reaction mix (Biorad, USA), 2 µL each of 10 pmol/µL forward and reverse primers (Table 1), 1 µL of 100 ng cDNA, and nuclease-free water to complete the volume. The thermocycling conditions included 1 cycle at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C and 1 min at 60 °C. Gene expression was normalized to the β-actin reference control, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2(-ΔΔCt) method [27].

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Comparison of all the treatment groups after β−1,3-glucan treatment head kidney macrophage-like cells was performed by repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc multiple comparison test.

Results

Evaluation of cell metabolic activity through resazurin reduction assay

The optical density (absorbance) value of the head kidney adherent macrophage-like cells culture supernatants in treatments T1(control), T2 (stimulation), and T3 (restimulation) through the resazurin reduction test was shown in Fig. 1. During the course of time-lapse post-β−1,3-glucan treatments to the cell; there was a trend of reducing resazurin level in T2 and T3. The resazurin reduction assay measured the conversion of resazurin to resorufin, indicated by a pink color change. There was a statistically significant difference in T1 and T2 treatment groups when compared to T3 at the 1 h time point. Further, there were high statistically significant differences among treatments T1, T2, and T3 at the remaining selected time points, such as 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Lower absorbance was found in T3 (restimulation) compared to other treatment groups (T1 and T2) at all the selected time points, indicating that the cells were metabolically active (Fig. 1).

In vitro trend of metabolic activity of head kidney adherent macrophage-like cells

The LDH production level was found to be increased from 1 h to 72 h (Fig. 2). More production of LDH was recorded in T3 compared to T1 and T2 at 1–72 h time points (Fig. 2). The highest production of LDH was observed at 72 h in T3. This assay was based on measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity released from viable cells. LDH catalyzed the conversion of lactate to pyruvate with concomitant reduction of NAD⁺ to NADH. The NADH formation was then coupled to a colorimetric or spectrophotometric reaction, allowing quantification of cell viability. Similar results were observed for lactate production at different time points (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h in T1, T2, and T3) (Fig. 3). This assay was based on its enzymatic oxidation by lactate oxidase to pyruvate and hydrogen peroxide. The generated hydrogen peroxide reacted with a probe in the presence of peroxidase to produce a colorimetric or fluorometric signal, directly proportional to lactate levels in the sample. Considering LDH and lactate production results, suggested head kidney adherent macrophage-like cells had remained healthy and metabolically active, depicting heightened glycolysis following training using β−1,3-glucan on head kidney adherent macrophage-like cells (Fig. 2–3). Moreover, glucose level trends to be decline in T1, T2, and T3 at various sampling time points mentioned in section 2.4. However, there was a significant difference between T1 and T2 at 48 h time point. Additionally, a significant difference was observed at 72 h time point (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Head kidney-derived macrophages are innate immune cells in fish, typically found in aggregates, and play a crucial role in initiating immune responses near the site of antigen uptake [28]. Numerous studies have shown that polysaccharide derivatives such as glucan trigger trained immunity both in vitro and in vivo by reprogramming innate immune cells—especially macrophages—toward a heightened pro-inflammatory state [[1], [22], [23], [25], [29], [30], [31]]. Trained immunity, or innate immune memory, refers to a form of immunological memory that amplifies the immune response non-specifically, regardless of the primary stimulus [22]. Various polysaccharide derivatives, including β-glucan, chitosan, and Astragalus polysaccharides (APS), are increasingly used in aquaculture as potential ligands to improve resistance to parasites, bacterial and viral infections in different species [[2], [15], [32], [33]]. Studies have shown that significant shifts in cellular metabolism, notably the transition from oxidative phosphorylation to increased aerobic glycolysis, are a hallmark of trained macrophages [29]. This metabolic change, referred to as the Warburg effect, is triggered by various immunological signals and plays a crucial role in driving the epigenetic alterations associated with trained immunity [34]. Additionally, epigenetic reprogramming, which corresponds with changes in cellular metabolism, is another key aspect of trained immunity [1]. In this pilot study, we observed increased lactate and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) production and decreased glucose levels, suggesting high glucose consumption by the cells. We observed increased mRNA transcript expression of key trained immunity or innate immune memory markers—mtor, hif1α, and hdac12—in macrophages following β−1,3-glucan exposure. This upregulation intensified upon restimulation, reinforcing earlier evidence of trained immunity in bony fish. A hallmark of trained immunity is the metabolic shift to aerobic glycolysis, driven by the Akt-mTOR-HIF1α signaling pathway [1]. Additionally, the increased expression of HIF1α post-infection in trained cells further indicates the activation of innate immune memory [13]. The resazurin assay effectively measures cell viability by preserving cellular integrity. In our study, reduced absorbance values indicated greater resazurin reduction, pointing to enhanced survival and metabolic activity in the cultured and stimulated adherent macrophage-like cells. On the other hand, a lower reduction of resazurin suggests impaired cellular metabolism [35]. Viable cells with active metabolism are capable of reducing resazurin to the fluorescent pink product, resorufin [26].

In our study, the mRNA transcript profiles of mtor, hif1α, hdac12 and cytokines such as tnfα, il6 and ifnγ indicate characteristic cellular changes associated with immune stimulating, providing evidence of trained immunity in rohu fish. The epigenetic alterations in the primed chromatin allow for a rapid expression of proinflammatory genes upon re-exposure, facilitating a more effective response and quicker control of pathogens [1,36].

Primed macrophages mount a stronger inflammatory response by releasing cytokines like TNFα, IL6, and IFNγ, providing enhanced protection against subsequent exposure to PAMPs from both homologous and heterologous pathogens, compared to unprimed immune responses [[1], [37]]. Cheng et al․ [13] demonstrated that human monocytes acquire trained immunity with high glucose consumption, increased lactate production, and a high NAD+/NADH ratio, reflecting a metabolic shift towards glycolysis driven by mTOR activation via the Dectin-1–Akt–HIF1α pathway when exposed to β-glucan from the Candida albicans cell wall. Angulo and Angulo [38] also showed that in vitro β-glucan treatment induces metabolic changes in calf monocytes, enhancing lactate generation and glucose consumption upon LPS restimulation. Interestingly, β-glucan-induced trained immunity alleviates LPS-induced septic liver injury in zebrafish by inhibiting hepatocyte pyroptosis via H3K4me3-mediated activation of mitophagy-related genes [16]. DrDectin-1, a C-type lectin receptor, orchestrates this epigenetic rewiring [16]. These findings reveal tissue-resident trained immunity's role in liver homeostasis and offer an in vivo model for immunotherapeutic strategies.

Our results align with these findings, demonstrating increased mRNA transcript expression of transcription factors, epigenetic markers linked to innate immune memory, and metabolic alterations following in vitro β−1,3-glucan treatment, reinforcing the presence of trained immune responses in fish. Similar observations were reported by Petit et al. [21] and Paris et al․ [19] in carp and dogs, respectively, following β-glucan stimulation. Further, extracellular HSP70 enhances β-glucan-induced trained immunity in macrophages by activating the TLR2–NF-κB signaling axis, contributing to sepsis suppression through heightened inflammatory readiness and innate immune memory [39]. Additionally, our previous research demonstrated the induction of innate immune memory in head kidney macrophages in common carp [22] and tilapia [25]. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo studies have validated key characteristics of trained immunity in carp macrophages [21,22]. More recently, β-glucan and mannan induced trained immunity in pufferfish, enhancing lactate production, reducing bacterial load, and elevating inflammatory gene expression upon Vibrio harveyi re-infection [24].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study indicates that homologous β−1,3-glucan restimulation of macrophage-like enriched cells induce metabolic and transcriptional reprograming in vitro, indicative of trained immunity in L. rohita. This is marked by the upregulation of key metabolic markers and other trained immunity-related markers, including hdac12, hif1α, mtor, and other cytokines, suggesting a robust immune state. The implications highlight β-glucan's potential as a sustainable disease prevention strategy in aquaculture. However, the study's limitations include a lack of focus on long-term effects and specific immune challenges and dose optimization. Future research should investigate the longevity and specificity of the trained immunity state in fish macrophages or macrophage-like cells and practical applications of β-glucan-based immunoprophylaxis, including dose optimization.
