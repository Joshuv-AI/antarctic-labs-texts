Reliability of remote self‐administered Web‐based digital cognitive measures and comparison to in‐person neuropsychological tests: Stricker Learning Span, Symbols Test, and the Mayo Test Drive Screening Battery Composite
Authors: Morgan A. Hughes, Ryan D. Frank, Rita L. Taylor, Winnie Z. Fan, Teresa J. Christianson, Walter K. Kremers, John L. Stricker, Mary M. Machulda, Jason Hassenstab, Michelle M. Mielke, John A. Lucas, Paula A. Aduen, Gregory S. Day, Neill R. Graff‐Radford, Clifford R. Jack, Jonathan Graff‐Radford, Ronald C. Petersen, Nikki H. Stricker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe describe the reliability of remote self‐administered digital cognitive measures completed via the Mayo Test Drive (MTD) web‐based platform.METHODSA total of 1846 participants (mean age = 70, SD = 12, range 31–101; 48% male; 96% White; 99% non‐Hispanic; 97% cognitively unimpaired) with two to four complete MTD sessions at ∼7.5‐month intervals were included. Test‐retest reliability was assessed using single‐rating, absolute‐agreement, and two‐way mixed intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals. ICCs for in‐person‐administered traditional neuropsychological measures were compared to MTD for a subset of 244 participants. Analyses also compared reliability across demographic, residential, and session context subgroups.RESULTSReliability was good for the MTD Composite (total ICC = 0.79 [0.77, 0.80]), and moderate‐to‐good for the primary outcome variables for each MTD subtest (total ICCs 0.70–0.83 for Stricker Learning Span and Symbols). The reliability of the remote self‐administered MTD was similar to in‐person‐administered cognitive measures.DISCUSSIONMTD showed moderate‐to‐good reliability, supporting its use in longitudinal monitoring.

BACKGROUND

Mayo Test Development through Rapid Iteration, Validation and Expansion (DRIVE; MTD) is a web‐based, multi‐device platform that enables remote self‐administered cognitive testing with high usability. 1 The MTD screening battery includes the Stricker Learning Span (SLS), a computer‐adaptive word‐list memory test, and the Symbols (SYM) test, a measure of processing speed and working memory, completed in 15–20 min. 1 The MTD composite score (MTDComp) shows robust associations with the Mayo Preclinical Alzheimer's disease Cognitive Composite (Mayo‐PACC) 2 and with amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), tau PET, hippocampal volume, and white matter hyperintensity burden. 3 MTD normative data for remote self‐administration are available. 4

Although the validity of MTD measures is well established, 3 , 4 , 5 reliability is essential for remote digital cognitive assessments, where variability in testing environments may affect performance. 6 Test–retest reliability reflects how consistent or replicable test results are across sessions. 7 Because MTD subtests use randomized item selection, test–retest reliability for MTD subtests also includes alternate‐form reliability. 5 , 13

The aim of this study was to characterize the reliability of MTD. We compared the reliability of remotely self‐administered MTD measures with traditional in‐person neuropsychological tests. We examined whether reliability varied by demographic and socioeconomic factors (age, sex, education, and education/occupation score), residential characteristics (area‐level deprivation), and session context, including device type/input source, subtest interference, 1 and testing location. Demonstrating reliable remote self‐administration would broaden opportunities for monitoring cognition longitudinally.

Method

Participants and study procedures

Participants were primarily recruited from the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (MCSA), a prospective, population‐based study of aging in Olmsted County, Minnesota. 1 , 4 MCSA visits occurred every 15 months (or every 30 months for individuals < 50 years of age) and included a physician examination, Clinical Dementia Rating® (CDR), 8 and in‐person neuropsychological testing. 9 Clinical diagnoses of cognitively unimpaired (CU), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or dementia were assigned by consensus according to established criteria. 10 , 11 MCSA participants were invited to complete an MTD session the week following each study visit and between visits (every 7.5 months; also see Supplementary Material). 1 A subset of participants were recruited from the Mayo Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC; Table 1). ADRC participants completed an MTD session the week after their annual visit. 1 , 4 Email was the primary method of sending MTD session requests and reminders, with supplemental phone calls if a participant did not have an email address or if a session was not completed after three emails as previously described. 1

MTD sessions

MTD subtests and variable derivation have been described previously. 3 , 4 , 12 , 13 The SLS includes five adaptive learning trials and a delayed recognition trial after SYM, with a mean learning‐to‐delay interval of ∼4 minutes. 1 , 5 For each learning trial, words are visually presented individually, then recognition is tested with a 4‐choice format. 5 The primary SLS variable is Sum of Trials (SLSSum: total correct across trials 1–5 plus delay). MTD SYM is adapted from the Ambulatory Research in Cognition (ARC) App. 14 The SYM test requires rapid matching of symbol pairs across four sequential 12‐item trials. 3 The primary outcome variable is average correct item response time (SYMRT). We also provide an accuracy‐weighted primary MTD SYM variable (SYMAW). 3 , 4 MTDComp is the sum of SLSSum and SYMAW. 3 , 4 Study aims focused on these four primary outcomes: MTDComp, SLSSum, SYMAW, SYMRT.

Secondary process variables (Table 2) are expected to have lower reliability because they represent single trial variables or are expected to have non‐normal distributions. 15 These should not be used as primary outcomes but may inform performance patterns, an individual's approach to testing (i.e., “process”), or provide alternatives when interruptions affect primary variables.

Inclusion criteria for primary analyses

Inclusion criteria were: (1) a complete baseline MTD session (i.e., MTDComp not missing) and at least one complete and consecutive follow‐up session; (2) a valid session, as previously defined 1 or per data cleaning/session review (Supplementary Materials); (3) absence of dementia at baseline. MTD sessions completed between 5/25/21 and 6/15/25 were included. Data for a subset of these participants have been reported previously. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5

Sample selection for comparison to in‐person traditional neuropsychological tests

To compare reliability between self‐administered MTD measures and traditional in‐person neuropsychological tests, analyses were restricted to participants newly enrolled in the parent study to ensure they were naïve to both assessment modalities. Because in‐person testing occurred approximately every 15 months, the second in‐person visit aligned temporally with the third MTD session. Primary comparisons focused on reliability across the first two MTD sessions (sessions 1 and 2) to avoid inflating reliability coefficients with additional measurements relative to the two in‐person visits available. Primary comparisons are limited by differing intervals, therefore alternative comparisons are also provided in Supplementary Materials. In‐person measures included the Kokmen Short Test of Mental Status (STMS), 16 a multi‐domain global cognitive screening measure (similar to the Mini Mental State Examination), and the Mayo‐PACC, which averages z‐scores from the Rey's Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) sum of trials, Trail Making Test B (inversed) and animal fluency. 2 We additionally compared measures conceptually aligned with MTD subtests (AVLT sum of trials vs. SLSSum; Trails B vs. SYMAW and vs. SYMRT).

Sample selection for subgroup analyses

To examine whether reliability varied by demographic and socioeconomic factors, residential characteristics, and session context, subgroups were created. These analyses were restricted to CU participants to avoid reliability differences driven by disproportionate representation of individuals with MCI, who are more likely to exhibit longitudinal cognitive change. Dichotomous subgroups were created for age (< 70 vs. ≥70 years), sex, education (< 16 vs. ≥16 years), device/input source consistency (consistent vs. switched types across sessions), and potential test interference (none vs. ≥1 potential distraction or interference endorsed during a session; see Supplementary Materials), 1 and testing location (all MTD sessions at home vs. variable locations). Education/occupation score groups were examined as a proxy for socioeconomic status. 17 , 18 The details of how the education/occupation score is derived is available in prior work 17 ; this score combines a job level score reflecting job attributes and complexity based on the participants’ primary occupation during most of their adult life with self‐reported educational attainment using previously described weightings. 17 For age, education, and education/occupation score, the sample median was the cut‐point. Categorization for device/input source consistency, potential test interference, and testing location were chosen for clinical relevance of the category groupings. To evaluate the impact of residential context on MTD reliability, we compared participants in the lowest and highest deciles of the national Area Deprivation Index (ADI; v3.1) within the sample as a measure of neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation (range 1‐100). 19 Additional details on ADI methods within parent studies and their alignment with the National Institute on Aging Health Disparities Framework 20 are available. 21

Statistical methods

Participant demographics were summarized using counts and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Comparison of data distributions across groups were performed using chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and two‐sample t‐tests for continuous variables. ICCs were calculated as σparticipant2σparticipant2+σresidual2 using random effects models with random effects participant intercepts (unadjusted ICCs) and fixed effects for age at assessment, sex, and education (adjusted ICCs). Unadjusted ICCs, commonly reported in cognitive reliability studies, are sample‐specific; therefore, adjusted ICCs are also provided (e.g., Table S1). Bootstrap methods using 1000 simulations were used to estimate the standard error used in the confidence limits for ICCs. Pearson correlation coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were also computed. Raw scores were Winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentile to lessen the influence of potential outliers. We applied reliability descriptives as defined by Koo et al. 7 (ICCs < 0.5 = poor; 0.50–0.75 = moderate; 0.75–0.90 = good; and > .90 = excellent). Unadjusted and adjusted ICCs (where the adjustment terms were included as fixed effects) were calculated in independent subsets of the overall sample for subgroup analyses (e.g. males vs females) and compared using the properties of variance distributions (i.e., Var(X‐Y) = Var(X) + Var (Y) – Cox(X,Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) in independent samples) and bootstrap methods; results interpretation focused on adjusted p‐value comparisons for subgroup analyses (adjusting for demographic variables). Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1. Statistical tests were 2‐sided; p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Participant demographics

A total of 1,846 participants met inclusion criteria for primary analyses (Supplementary Materials). Participants ranged from 31 to 101 years of age at baseline and completed two to four MTD sessions, with 7–9 months between sessions (Table 1). Nearly all MTD sessions (99.5%) were completed remotely on personal devices (61.9% computer, 22.6% smartphone, 15.2% tablet, 0.3% unknown/other). The sample was predominantly White (95.9%), non‐Hispanic (99.0%), college‐educated (mean years of education = 15.7, SD = 2.3, range 6–20), older adults (mean = 69.7, SD = 12.1) and CU (96.7% CU; 3.3% MCI).

MTD reliability

Reliability was good for MTDComp (total ICC = 0.79; see Table 1 for session‐to‐session ICCs and 95% CIs) and moderate‐to‐good for the primary SLS and SYM variables (total ICC: SLSSum = 0.75, SYMAW = 0.70, SYMRT = 0.83; Figure 1). Weighting SYM performance by accuracy reduced reliability: SYMRT had a higher total ICC (0.83) than SYMAW (0.70). Similarly, the z‐score‐based MTD Composite that incorporates SYMRT instead of SYMAW showed slightly higher reliability than MTDComp (total ICC = 0.81 vs. 0.79). SLSSum had slightly higher reliability (0.75) than separate indices of learning (1–5 total correct = 0.73, max span = 0.70) or delay (0.72). Alternative SYM time‐based variables showed nearly identical reliability (e.g., total ICC = 0.83 for SYMRT and average seconds across all four trials, and total ICC = 0.82 for middle two trials average seconds), and averaging across SYM trials yielded higher ICCs than single‐trial measures. All secondary variables had moderate‐to‐good reliability coefficients. As expected, some secondary process variables based on a single trial or with restricted ranges and non‐normal distributions had poor reliability coefficients (SLS Percent Retention, SYM Accuracy/Total Correct, SLS Trial 1, SLS Trial 2), but most secondary process variables had moderate‐to‐good reliability coefficients. Pearson correlations closely parallelled ICCs; for all primary variables, unadjusted ICCs differed from Pearson correlations by ≤.02 (Figure 1; Tables S2 and S3).

Reliability by CU participant subgroups

Tables S5–S8 provide subgroup ICCs (95% CI), unadjusted p‐values, p‐values adjusted for demographic variables (p adj), and subgroup characteristics. Reliability of primary MTD variables did not differ by education (< 16 vs. ≥16 years; p adj’s ≥ 0.35), sex (p adj’s ≥ 0.30), or education/occupation score groups (p adj’s ≥ 0.18).

Most participants (73%) used the same device/input source combination across all MTD sessions, typically a computer with mouse input (61.9%). Reliability did not differ between consistent and inconsistent device/input source groups for most primary variables (p adj’s ≥ 0.13). The exception was SYMRT, which showed higher reliability in the consistent device/input source group (p adj = 0.049).

Thirty‐three percent of CU participants endorsed potential interference during at least one testing session (interference group). Reliability did not differ between the interference 1 and no interference groups (p adj’s > 0.10).

Most participants (85%) consistently completed all MTD sessions at home. MTDComp and SLSSum showed no reliability differences between consistent and inconsistent location groups (p adj’s > 0.14). However, both SYM primary variables showed higher reliability when all sessions were completed at home (p adj’s < 0.01).

Age had no significant effect on reliability for SLSSum or SYMRT (p adj’s > 0.05). However, participants ≥70 years showed higher ICCs for MTDComp and SYMAW than participants < 70 years of age (p adj’s < 0.03). Notably, participants ≥70 were more likely to complete all MTD sessions at home (χ2 p < 0.001), suggesting location consistency may account for observed age effects.

National ADI scores were available in a subset of participants (71%). National ADI showed no significant effect on reliability when comparing the lowest vs. highest deciles (p adj’s > 0.28; Table S5), nor when using median or quartile splits.

DISCUSSION

This study provides a detailed characterization of the test–retest reliability of the remote, self‐administered digital MTD cognitive screening battery composite and its subtests across two to four test sessions in a large community‐based research sample. Overall, MTDComp demonstrated good reliability. Primary and secondary subtest outcome variables showed moderate‐to‐good reliability, consistent with our prior report of 2‐week test–retest reliability for SLS and SYM subtests. 13

MTD reliability compares favorably to other digital cognitive assessments that use a single‐session approach at each timepoint, whether administered in clinic or remotely. For example, Stricker et al. 22 reported poor‐to‐moderate reliability (ICCs 0.36–0.59) for one card learning (OCL) accuracy, a measure of learning/memory from the in‐clinic‐administered Cogstate Brief Battery over a 7.5 month interval in CU individuals. Similarly, Kochan et al. 23 found poor 1‐month at‐home reliability for Cogstate OCL (ICC = 0.43) and Cambridge Brain Sciences Paired Associates (ICC = 0.30), although cross‐battery global composites improved reliability (ICCs 0.82–0.85). Feenstra et al. 24 observed moderate reliability for word list learning (ICC = 0.60), delayed recall (ICC = 0.49) and recognition (ICC = 0.76), as well as for a TMT‐like measure (Connect the Dots ICCs = 0.61–0.71), and good reliability (ICC = 0.78) for a 7‐subtest composite using the 75‐minute self‐administered Amsterdam Cognition Scan completed twice within 6 weeks at home on a personal computer. Rigby et al. 25 found similar reliability to MTD subtest results in CU and MCI participants using the in‐person National Institute of Health Toolbox‐Cognition Battery (NIHTB‐CB) administered twice within 4 months (e.g., picture sequence memory test ICC = 0.65; pattern comparison processing speed ICC = 0.85; Fluid Composite ICC = 0.85).

Reliability increases with the number of items, trials, tests, and assessments completed. 2 , 26 Consequently, “one‐shot” single session approaches often yield lower reliability compared to paradigms that average performance across multiple short sessions completed several times per day for several days or weeks (i.e., ecological momentary assessment) 14 or longer sessions repeated daily for several days. 27 , 28 Although individual sessions in these multi‐test approaches are brief, cumulative testing time is greater than brief one‐shot assessments like MTD. For example, six daily 15‐min sessions represent 90 minutes of testing, 27 and four daily 30‐ to 60‐second sessions over seven days represent 42–84 minutes. 14 Nicosia et al. 14 demonstrated excellent reliability for the ARC app after testing in cycles of four sessions per day for seven days; ICCs for ARC subtests exceeded 0.85, and composite scores exceeded 0.90 between the initial cycle and both 6‐month and 1‐year follow‐ups. Nicosia et al. 14 also showed that SYMRT reliability reached 0.88 when averaging across three sessions, informing our decision to include four consecutive SYM trials within a single MTD session as an adaptation of ARC Symbols. Similarly, the current study demonstrates that averaging across two or more SYM trials yields ICC values > 0.80, whereas single‐trial SYM reliabilities ranged from 0.69 to 0.76. Weighting by accuracy reduced SYM reliability (possibly because accuracy has a ceiling effect that may in turn attenuate reliability of the weighted variable) but captured non‐speed aspects of performance and facilitated creation of MTDComp. 3

The reliability of the remote, self‐administered MTDComp was comparable to an in‐person mental status screening measure (STMS) and a composite derived from traditional in‐person neuropsychological tests (Mayo‐PACC). At the subtest level, SLS demonstrated reliability similar to AVLT. SYMAW showed lower reliability than Trails B, though SYMRT had similar reliability relative to Trails B. Direct comparisons focused on test 1 vs. test 2 ICCs to match the number of assessments, though this introduced differing intervals (7.5 months for MTD vs. 15 months for in‐person measures), which is an important limitation. Results were broadly consistent when comparing in‐person tests using ICCs across all three timepoints or using MTD sessions 1 and 3 to approximate a similar follow‐up interval. Across comparisons, there was a trend for MTDComp reliability to exceed that of STMS. The STMS reliability observed here aligns with coefficients reported for other global mental status screening measures. For example, Pedraza et al. 29 reported DRS reliability ranging from 0.54 to 0.71 in CU older adults. MMSE reliability is consistently poor, including in 119 CU older adults over 1.6 years (Spearman = 0.31), 30 and in 65 CU participants over 83 days (Spearman = 0.35). 31 To our knowledge, no prior study has compared the reliability of remote and in‐person traditional cognitive measures in the same participants.

Remote digital assessments offer several advantages, including cost‐effectiveness, time efficiency, and convenience. They may also reduce the “white‐coat effect” associated with in‐person testing 32 and facilitate repeated measurements over time. 23 , 24 The potential benefits of remote assessment compared to in‐person measures such as Mayo‐PACC or STMS are illustrated by a post‐hoc power analysis using data from this study. Because MTD can be administered more frequently, fewer participants are needed to detect effects in clinical trials. For example, assuming the same visit or session frequency as in this study, a 5‐year clinical trial would include five in‐person sessions (baseline, 15 months, 30 months, 45 months, and end of study) versus nine remote MTD sessions (every 7.5 months). To achieve 80% power to detect a slope difference of 0.10 SD, MTD would require 328 participants, Mayo‐PACC 380, and STMS 418, assuming α = 0.05 and a subject‐specific random slope variance ratio of 0.1 in a random‐effects model. Thus, MTD reduces sample size requirements by 13.7% (Mayo‐PACC) to 21.5% (STMS).

Despite these benefits, remote digital assessments have limitations. First, reduced environmental control may introduce variability and measurement error. Although our findings suggest that MTD reliability is minimally affected by self‐reported test interference, some types of interference likely exert greater influence on reliability than others and certain individuals may be more susceptible to environmental distractions. See our prior work for a description of the impact of interference and device/response input type on some MTD performances, which are small in magnitude but significant. 4 Thus, any reported interference should be interpreted in the context of the individual test taker. Second, some older adults may lack access to some devices or feel uncomfortable using technology independently. 1 , 33 Multi‐device platforms can help by allowing participants to use familiar devices. While MTD reliability was minimally affected by variation in device or input source across sessions, maintaining consistency in device, input source, and location is recommended when possible. Notably, reliability was higher when sessions were completed consistently at home, suggesting that other environments may introduce more distraction‐related variability that may not be fully captured by self‐report of test interference. The importance of device, input source, and location consistency varied by subtest. SYM showed higher reliability in groups with consistent testing conditions (consistent device/input source group; consistent location group), whereas SLS did not, indicating that measures based on response times may be more sensitive to changes in session context. Prior work by Nicosia et al. demonstrates that simple response time differences of less than 150 milliseconds could be impacted by smartphone make/model and operating system, underscoring the need for further study on whether such timing differences meaningfully affect reliability. 34 MTD outcomes do not depend on simple reaction time metrics requiring millisecond‐level precision, however. See also Patel et al. for a discussion of challenges in automated detection of operating systems and other nuanced device characteristics. 1

Our findings should be interpreted in light of the demographic composition of the sample, which was predominately CU, non‐Hispanic White, and highly educated. While MTD reliability did not significantly differ by sex, education or education/occupation subgroups in this study, as a remote digital assessment, MTD may be susceptible to selection effects associated with digital access and literacy, which can vary systematically by race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. Accordingly, the generalizability of our findings in more diverse and/or less‐educated populations may be limited. It is noteworthy that some recent, innovative cognitive measures for older adults (e.g., NIH Toolbox, Cogstate Brief Battery) report comparable reliability coefficients between Black/African Americans and predominantly White samples. 25 Future studies of MTD are planned to evaluate subgroup reliability in more heterogeneous samples. Exploratory analyses in the present study showed no significant reliability differences across National ADI for the highest and lowest deciles in our sample; however, better representation of individuals living in areas with high deprivation is required to confirm this preliminary finding. Increased representation of individuals from rural communities is also needed to assess urbanicity/rurality reliability differences and to determine whether digital cognitive assessments can help address the gaps in cognitive screening often experienced in rural settings.

In summary, this study demonstrates that the MTD screening battery provides a reliable method for tracking cognition over time, with moderate‐to‐good reliability. MTD reliability is comparable to in‐person neuropsychological tests and other digital cognitive assessments and is favorable relative to in‐person mental status screening measures. Future work should evaluate the sensitivity of MTD for detecting longitudinal cognitive decline.
