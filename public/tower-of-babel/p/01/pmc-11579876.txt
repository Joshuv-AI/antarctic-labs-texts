Butylphthalide inhibits ferroptosis and ameliorates cerebral Ischaemia–Reperfusion injury in rats by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway
Authors: Meilin Sun, Junmin Chen, Fan Liu, Pei Li, Jundong Lu, Shihao Ge, Lele Wang, Xin Zhang, Xiaopeng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate whether butylphthalide can inhibit ferroptosis and ameliorate cerebral ischaemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury in rats by activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) / heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) signalling pathway, known for its antioxidative and cytoprotective properties. Middle cerebral artery occlusion reperfusion (MCAO/R) rat models were established. Male rats were randomly divided into five groups: a sham-operated group (sham), MCAO/R group, MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 (Nrf2-specific inhibitor) group, MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP (butylphthalide) group and MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 ​+ ​NBP group. The effect of butylphthalide on cerebral I/R injury was evaluated using neurological deficit scores. The expression levels of Nrf2, HO-1, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) protein were detected using Western blot. Moreover, the expression levels of GPX4, HO-1 and TfR1 mRNA were determined through real-time fluorescence quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. The distribution of Nrf2, HO-1, GPX4 and TfR1 was detected using immunohistochemical staining. The levels of iron and related lipid peroxidation indexes, such as reduced glutathione, reactive oxygen species, malondialdehyde and nitric oxide, were measured using a kit. The changes in mitochondria were observed through transmission electron microscopy. Butylphthalide treatment significantly improved neurological dysfunction, reduced cerebral infarction volume and mitigated histopathological damage in MCAO/R rats. It induced the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and upregulated HO-1 expression, which was attenuated by ML385. Butylphthalide also attenuated lipid peroxidation, iron accumulation and mitochondrial damage induced by MCAO/R. The expression of GPX4, ACSL4 and TfR1 proteins, as well as their mRNA levels, was modulated through butylphthalide treatment, with improvements observed in mitochondrial morphology. Butylphthalide exerts neuroprotective effects by attenuating neurological dysfunction and ferroptosis in MCAO/R rats through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and inhibition of lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation.

Introduction

Ischaemic stroke poses a considerable global burden, with high rates of disability and mortality [1]. Although intravenous thrombolysis and intravascular interventional therapy are effective treatments for acute ischaemic stroke, their narrow therapeutic window and potential complications underscore the need for novel therapeutic targets [2,3]. Ferroptosis, characterised by iron-dependent, lipid peroxide overaccumulation, represents a non-apoptotic form of programed cell death [4]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a pivotal antioxidant enzyme maintaining cellular redox balance, serves as a key regulator of ferroptosis, utilizing glutathione (GSH) for its peroxidase activity [4,5]. Ferroptosis has been implicated in ischaemia–reperfusion (I/R) injuries in various organs and diseases, including cerebral I/R injury [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Inhibiting ferroptosis has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate cerebral I/R injury.

Butylphthalide, derived from celery seeds, has exhibited neuroprotective effects in ischaemic stroke, including apoptosis inhibition, antioxidation, mitochondrial function improvement and blood–brain barrier protection [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Recent studies suggest that butylphthalide may attenuate cerebral I/R injury by inhibiting ferroptosis via the SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4 pathway [15]. However, the precise mechanism underlying butylphthalide's role in ferroptosis remains unclear.

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a pivotal transcription factor, regulates cellular redox balance and inflammation [16]. It modulates ferroptosis by regulating GSH, lipid oxidation and iron metabolism and activating ferroptosis-related target genes [[17], [18], [19]]. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), an essential enzyme in heme metabolism, exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, and its transcription is regulated by Nrf2 [20]. Activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway has been associated with ferroptosis inhibition [21,22]. Long-term butylphthalide pretreatment has been shown to reduce ischaemic brain injury in mice with cerebral infarction by activating Nrf2 [23]. However, the interplay between the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and butylphthalide in ferroptosis-related cerebral I/R injury remains elusive. Our previous research revealed that butylphthalide alleviated cerebral I/R injury in rats by reducing neuron ferroptosis and oxidative stress [24]. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to determine whether the possible mechanisms underlying butylphthalide's regulation of ferroptosis are related to the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. We aim to provide a new perspective and offer potential therapeutic methods for clinical treatment.

Materials and Methods

Experimental animals

Healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats weighing 200–220 ​g (SPF grade) were purchased from Beijing SiPeiFu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; animal production license number: SCXK [Beijing] 2019–0010). The rats were reared under certified conditions without specific pathogens. All animals were kept in a 12-h light/dark cycle, with controllable temperature (22 ​°C ​± ​3 ​°C) and humidity (60%–70%), and they were able to eat and drink water freely. The rats were adaptively fed for at least 3 days, and the experimental animals were randomly selected using a random number table; the experimental evaluation was conducted by researchers who were blinded to the details of the grouping. All the experimental procedures were approved by the Committee of Experimental Animals of Hebei Medical University (Shijiazhuang, China) (approval no. 2022247) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines.

Before conducting the experiment, the experimental animals were assigned to different groups, and the sample size of each group was similar to that of other MCAO/R studies. Sample size selection Based on the preliminary results of our previous experiments and the experience of the rat MCAO/R model in relevant literature studies, the number of rats required for this in vivo study was determined through power analysis. At an α value of 0.05, to detect a 30% reduction in neurological deficit with 80% power (two-tailed T-test), ∼8–10 rats per group are required; for infarct volume, Western blot, immunohistochemistry, Real-time RT-PCR and kit detection require 4–6 samples to detect 30% changes after MCAO/R with 80% power (β ​= ​0.8, α ​= ​0.05) [[25], [26]]. However, taking into account sample losses (animal mortality, animal exclusion), more rats are needed to ensure a sufficient number of experiments to achieve statistically significant differences among treatment groups. This experiment followed the guidelines of the Animal Protection Committee of Hebei Medical University, and any harm to animals was minimised. An experiment flowchart is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Cerebral Ischaemia–Reperfusion model

The rats were fasted for 12 ​h before the operation and anaesthetised by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 ​mg/kg). The right middle cerebral artery occlusion reperfusion (MCAO/R) model was used, and the intraluminal filament technique was applied. The right common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA) and internal carotid artery (ICA) were carefully exposed and successively separated. The distal end of the ECA was ligated with surgical sutures, and a small hole was cut into the stump of the ECA. From the ECA, through bifurcation of the CCA, a nylon monofilament (Beijing Cinontech Co. Ltd., China) with a rounded tip was inserted into the ICA. Slight resistance indicated that the monofilament had reached the beginning of the middle cerebral artery; further movement was stopped, and the monofilament was fixed. The depth of the monofilament was 18.0 ​± ​2.0 ​mm. The neck incision was closed using surgical sutures. After 1.5 ​h of ischaemia, the monofilament was carefully removed to effect cerebral blood flow reperfusion. The follow-up experiment was conducted 24 ​h after reperfusion. In this instance, the monofilament was not inserted in the sham group; all other surgical procedures were the same as for the MCAO/R model rats. The body temperature of the rats was maintained at 37 ​°C ​± ​0.5 ​°C during and after the operation.

Experimental animal grouping and drug treatment

The rats were divided into groups using the random number table method generated by SPSS 26.0 software. The rats were randomly divided into five groups: sham, MCAO/R, MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 (30 ​mg/kg, I.P.), MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP (According to the experimental results of our previous research and related similar research literature [11,27], the dose of NBP in this study was selected to be 4 ​mg/kg, I.P.) and MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 ​+ ​NBP.

A total of 216 rats were included in the experiment, with 40 rats in the sham group, 47 in the MCAO/R group, 44 in the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 group, 42 in the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group and 43 in the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 ​+ ​NBP group.

Butylphthalide was injected 1 ​h after MCAO/R, and ML385 was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and administered intraperitoneally to rats 24 ​h before MCAO. In addition, the sham-operated and MCAO/R groups were administered equal volumes of normal saline and DMSO, the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 group was administered equal volumes of normal saline and the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group was administered equal volumes of DMSO.

Butylphthalide was supplied by the Shijiazhuang Pharmaceutical Group NBP Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Shijiazhuang, China; purity >99%, production batch: 518190403). The concentration of butylphthalide was 5 ​mg/ml, and the dose used in this study was determined with reference to published studies [11,27,28]; thus, the dose in this experiment was 4 ​mg/kg, administered intraperitoneally. The ML385 was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA, Cas# 846557-71-9; assay: 99.72%). The dose and duration of drug administration were based on information denoted in previous similar studies [29,30].

The rats were anaesthetised 24 ​h after MCAO/R. They were rapidly perfused transcardially with normal saline and then perfused using precooled 4% paraformaldehyde dissolved in a 0.1 ​M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Brain tissue was immediately sampled and preserved at −80 ​°C or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological study.

Neurobehavioral assessment

Neurological deficits were tested according to the Zea–Longa score [31,32], which was calculated by a blind examiner (who did not know the experimental grouping) before MCAO and 24 ​h after MCAO/R. The neurological deficit scores were as follows: 0 ​= ​no neurological dysfunction, normal activity; 1 ​= ​contralateral forelimb cannot fully stretch; 2 ​= ​contralateral circling; 3 ​= ​tumble contralateral side; 4 ​= ​no autonomous crawling, loss of consciousness. Rats with no neurological impairment symptoms after reperfusion were immediately excluded.

The modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) was used to evaluate the neurological function and the degree of injury in rats [33] and consisted of motor, sensory, reflex and balance tests. As with the Zea–Longa score, the mNSS score was calculated by a blind examiner, and the score scale ranged from 0 to 18; the higher the score was, the more serious the nerve defect.

The corner test has been widely used to evaluate sensorimotor disorders in rats [34,35]. The experimental device comprised two boards with an angle of 30° and a gap of approximately 5 ​mm between them and was designed to encourage the rats to move into the corner. As the rat entered the corner, the vibrissae on both sides touched the board, stimulating the animal to move forwards or upwards and try to turn 180° to face the open end of the angle. The probability of a left or right turn in normal rats is almost equal. After an MCAO/R injury, the rats prefer to turn towards the side with the ipsilateral damage. In this study, the test counted the numbers of right turns. The test was repeated 10 times, and the interval between the two trials was 30 ​s.

Evaluation of cerebral infarction volume

The volume of cerebral infarction was observed using 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (2,3,5-TTC) staining. The entire brain tissue of the rats was sampled 24 ​h after MCAO/R. The brain tissue was cut into five pieces approximately 2-mm thick along the coronal plane, stained with 2% TTC solution, immersed in a 37 ​°C water bath for approximately 20 ​min and then fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 ​h. The cerebral infarct volume was assessed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, RRID: SCR_003070). The infarct volume percentage was calculated using the following formula: infarct volume (%) ​= ​{[total infarct volume ​− ​(volume of the intact ipsilateral hemisphere – volume of the intact contralateral hemisphere)]/contralateral hemisphere volume} ​× ​100% [35].

Histopathological examination

The rat brain tissue was fixed with 10% buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin and sliced 24 ​h after MCAO/R, and the thickness of each slice was 3–5 μm. After haematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, the sections were observed through an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan, model BX43), and the target area was selected for photographing and analysis. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on some cerebral cortex specimens for subsequent analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining method

Sections of rat brain tissue were routinely deparaffinized and subsequently subjected to antigen retrieval by autoclaving in a 3% citric acid solution for 10 ​min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ​°C with primary antibodies: GPX4 (1:100, Affinity, Cat# DF6701); HO-1 (1:80, Affinity, Cat# AF5393); Nrf2 (1:100, Proteintech, Cat# 16396-1-ap); TfR1 (1:110, Proteintech, Cat# 11899-1-ap). Subsequently, the secondary antibody (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# PV-6000) was added and incubated at 37 ​°C for 20 ​min. After diaminobenzidine (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Cat# ZLI-9018) colouration and a haematoxylin staining solution (Zhuhai Baso Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China, Cat# BA-4041) treatment, the sections were observed and captured using the optical microscope.

Immunofluorescence

The rat brain tissue sections were treated with 3% H2O2 for 10 ​min and washed three times with 0.01 ​M PBS, each time for 5 ​min. After sealing with 5% BAS at room temperature for 30 ​min, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Nrf2; 1:100) overnight at 4 ​°C. The next day, after three PBS washes, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co. Ltd, Cat# ZF0516) for 30 ​min at 37 ​°C. After three PBS washes, the nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 10 ​min. Images were subsequently observed and captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan, UC90).

Transmission electron microscopy

A fresh brain tissue sample of 1 ​mm3 was taken from the peri-infarct area 24 ​h after MCAO/R and soaked in a 4% glutaraldehyde fixed solution at 4 ​°C. After dehydration, osmotic entrapment, polymerisation, sectioning (50–60 ​nm), 3% uranium acetate staining and 0.1% lead citrate double staining were performed. The ultrastructure of rat lesions was observed through transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan, H-7650).

Western blot

The brain tissue from the peri-infarct area (cerebral ischaemic penumbra) was sampled 24 ​h after MCAO/R to conduct a Western blot test. Protein was extracted from the brain tissue using a radio immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd., China, Cat# R0020), and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration of the tissue samples was determined using a nucleic acid protein quantitative instrument (DeNovix ultramicro ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer, DS-11; USA). The tissue sample was added to a sample loading buffer and denatured at 95 ​°C for 10 ​min. After sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, the protein was transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore Company, item no. IPVH00010). The PVDF membrane was sealed with 5% skimmed milk powder for 2 ​h; then, primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 ​°C. The main antibodies were as follows: GPX4 (1:1,200), ACSL4 (1:1,000, Affinity, Cat# DF12141), TfR1 (1:1,300, Affinity, Cat# AF5343), HO-1 (1:1,000, Proteintech, Cat# 10701-1-ap), Nrf2 (1:1,300), β-actin (1:6,000, Affinity, Cat# AF7018), Histone H3 (1:1,300, Affinity, Cat# AF0863). The next day, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit immunoglobin G (IgG) antibody, 1:3,000, Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Cat# ZB2301; goat anti-mouse IgG antibody, 1:3,000, Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Cat# ZB2305) at room temperature for 90 ​min. Each band was detected with a scanner (Epson scanner, model V19) and analyzed using ImageJ software. The relative expression levels of GPX4, ACSL4, TfR1, HO-1 and plasma Nrf2 were normalized using β-actin antibody. The relative expression level of nuclear Nrf2 was normalized through Histone H3 antibody.

Separation and preparation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins

All procedures were performed according to the instructions of a subcellular structure nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Boster, Cat# AR0106). A tissue sample of 40 ​mg was weighed, and 400 ​μl of cytoplasmic protein extraction reagent A was added. After homogenisation, the homogenate was transferred to a precooled centrifuge tube and placed in an ice bath for 10–15 ​min. Subsequently, 20 ​μl of cytoplasmic protein extraction reagent B was added; the solution was shaken and mixed for 5 ​s, incubated on ice for 1 ​min and centrifuged at 16,000 ​g for 5 ​min. The supernatant was immediately placed into a precooled centrifuge tube and labeled as the cytoplasmic protein. The precipitate was added to 200 ​μl of nuclear protein extraction reagent. After shaking and mixing, the solution was centrifuged at 16,000 ​g for 5 ​min. The supernatant was immediately placed in a precooled centrifuge tube and labeled as the nuclear protein. The samples were stored at −80 ​°C.

Real-time RT-PCR

The total RNA of the brain tissue from the peri-infarct area was extracted with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596–026) 24 ​h after MCAO/R. The concentration and quality of RNA were detected using a nucleic acid and protein quantitative instrument (DeNovix ultramicro ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer, DS-11). Iron determination

Tissue homogenate was taken from the corresponding part of the cerebral infarction 24 ​h after MCAO/R, and the supernatant was collected. An iron assay kit (Abcam, Cat# ab83366) was used to determine Fe2+ according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of reduced glutathione

Tissue sample preparation was conducted as previously described, and GSH was determined using the reduced GSH assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China, Cat# A006-1-1) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of reactive oxygen species

Tissue sample preparation was conducted as previously described; then, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) content in the infarction brain tissue at 24 ​h after MCAO/R was measured using a ROS assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# E004-1-1) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of malondialdehyde and nitric oxide

The tissue samples were prepared as previously described. The manufacturer's instructions were followed to analyse malondialdehyde (MDA) and nitric oxide (NO) using an MDA assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A003-1-1) and NO assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A012-1-2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Data conforming to normal distribution are expressed as mean ​± ​standard deviation; otherwise, they are expressed as median and upper and lower quartiles (M [P25, P75]). One-way analysis of variance was used for comparison between multiple groups. The least significant difference test was used to further compare data with homogeneity of variance. A Dunnett test was used to analyse data with heterogeneous variances. Nonparametric tests were performed with the Kruskal–Wallis H test to calculate the Zea–Longa score. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 16 rats died (6.75%), 7 in the MCAO/R group (12.50%), 4 in the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 group (13.79%), 2 in the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group (3.92%) and 3 in the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 ​+ ​NBP group (5.77%). No cerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage was found in these animals after dissection.

The results of the corner test demonstrated that compared with the rats in the sham group, those in the MCAO/R and MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 groups made an increased number of right turns. Compared with the rats in the MCAO/R group, those in the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group made fewer right turns. Compared with the rats in the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group, those in the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 ​+ ​NBP group made more right turns (P ​< ​0.05; Fig. 1C).

To detect the cerebral infarction area of the rats, TTC staining was applied 24 ​h after MCAO/R. The results revealed no infarction lesion in the sham group, and the infarct volume in the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group was significantly reduced compared with the MCAO/R group. Compared with the rats in the MCAO/R ​+ ​NBP group, those in the MCAO/R ​+ ​ML385 ​+ ​NBP group exhibited an increased cerebral infarction volume (P ​< ​0.05; Fig. 1D). However, no statistical significance was identified between the MCAO and MCAO ​+ ​ML385 groups at 24 ​h after cerebral I/R injury.

The HE staining results showed that compared with the sham group, the cortical brain tissue of the MCAO/R group was significantly necrotic and exhibited a significant decrease in the number of neurons, nuclear pyknosis, deep staining and even dissolution; the color of the cytoplasm was also lighter, and many vacuoles were observed. These histopathological lesions were significantly ameliorated after treatment with butylphthalide. However, ML385, a Nrf2-specific inhibitor, attenuated the beneficial effect of butylphthalide on histopathological damage in MCAO/R rats (Fig. 1E).

These results indicate that butylphthalide has a protective effect on cerebral I/R injury. However, ML385 attenuated the beneficial effect of butylphthalide on cerebral I/R injury.

Iron accumulation is another key feature of ferroptosis after brain I/R [36]. The results in this study demonstrated that compared with the sham group, MCAO/R significantly increased the level of tissue endogenous Fe2+, and compared with the MCAO/R group, butylphthalide treatment significantly decreased the iron accumulation induced by MCAO/R (P ​< ​0.05; Fig. 2E). However, ML385, as a Nrf2-specific inhibitor, aggravated MCAO/R-induced lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation and inhibited the beneficial effect of butylphthalide on these processes (P ​< ​0.05; Fig. 2A–E).

Butylphthalide ameliorates mitochondrial damage in MCAO/R rats

Mitochondrial ultrastructural changes are another key feature of ferroptosis. In this study, the morphological features of mitochondria were observed through transmission electron microscopy. As shown in Fig. 3, the mitochondria of neurons in the sham group had a regular shape and clear outline. After MCAO/R, the neuron mitochondrial cristae had decreased, the outer mitochondrial membrane had broken and the density of the mitochondrial membrane had increased. This study confirmed that MCAO/R induced ferroptosis, which is consistent with previous research findings [15,21]. Importantly, treatment with butylphthalide was found to modestly improve the morphological features of mitochondria in MCAO/R rats, suggesting a potential mitigating effect on ferroptosis-related damage. Butylphthalide regulating the expression of proteins related to ferroptosis through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway in MCAO/R rats

Previous studies have demonstrated that protein expression related to ferroptosis (e.g. of GPX4, ACSL4 and TfR1) is abnormal in MCAO/R rat models [15,21,24]. Notably, GPX4 is considered a key protein in the regulation of ferroptosis, TfR1 is a vital transporter of iron metabolism, helping to maintain intracellular and extracellular iron homeostasis [4,37], and ACSL4 is an important lipid metabolic enzyme. Studies have found that ACSL4 activates lipid membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids and produces lipid peroxides to participate in ferroptosis [38]. Our previous experiment also confirmed that ACSL4 was a target of ferroptosis and that the inhibition of ACSL4 could inhibit ferroptosis and promote the recovery of neurological function in cases of cerebral infarction [35].

Discussion

Previous studies have indicated that butylphthalide has a protective effect on cerebral I/R injury [27,39,40]. In this study, we demonstrated that butylphthalide may ameliorate cerebral I/R injury by inhibiting ferroptosis in MCAO/R rats, which may be related to the regulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway.

Stroke is a serious threat to people's health, and how to reduce the morbidity and mortality of cerebral infarction is an ongoing concern. Therefore, the treatment of cerebral infarction remains the focus of scientific research. The pathological mechanisms of brain injury after cerebral infarction mainly include oxidative stress, inflammatory response, excitatory amino-acid toxicity and apoptosis [41]. In 2012, Dixon et al. identified ferroptosis for the first time as a new non-apoptotic form of cell death [4]. Previous studies have confirmed that ferroptosis is regulated by multiple biological regulatory pathways. Ferroptosis is characterised by GSH depletion [4,5], which leads to a decrease in GPX4 activity and prevents the metabolism of lipid peroxides. Another important characteristic of ferroptosis is the iron-mediated excessive peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids [42]. The excessive accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides causes ferroptosis, and the accumulation of ROS is a key indicator of ferroptosis; in addition, GSH, MDA and NO are important indexes for evaluating the degree of lipid peroxidation [36]. Our results revealed that the MCAO/R model significantly increased ROS, MDA, NO and Fe2+ levels and decreased GSH levels in the cortex of rats. The typical morphological features of ferroptosis caused by I/R injury are a mitochondrial crest decrease and membrane rupture, which indicated its occurrence in our MCAO/R model.

Recently, studies have found that ferroptosis is closely related to several diseases, including Alzheimer's disease [17], ischaemic stroke [35] and tumours [43]. New research reveals that ferroptosis plays a key role in cerebral I/R injury and that the inhibition of ferroptosis can protect brain tissue damage caused by I/R. For instance, astragaloside IV mitigates cerebral ischaemia-reperfusion injury via inhibition of P62/Keap1/Nrf2 pathway-mediated ferroptosis [44]. Moreover, the Elabela–APJ axis attenuates cerebral I/R injury by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis [45].

Butylphthalide is generally used to treat patients with acute ischaemic stroke in clinical treatment. It was originally extracted from celery and its seeds but later produced through chemical synthesis. Studies have explored its neuroprotective potential in cerebral I/R injury and recently revealed that butylphthalide may reduce cerebral I/R injury by inhibiting ferroptosis [15,24]. A recent study suggests that butylphthalide has a neuroprotective effect on focal cerebral I/R, which may be mediated through the ferroptosis-dependent SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 and PDGFRβ/PI3/Akt signalling pathways [15].

In the present study, the results indicated that butylphthalide not only reduces the volume of cerebral infarction and neurological deficit in MCAO/R rats but also attenuates lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation. Moreover, transmission electron microscopy revealed that butylphthalide ameliorates mitochondrial damage in MCAO/R rats. These results indicate that butylphthalide has a protective effect on cerebral I/R injury. However, ML385, as the Nrf2 specific inhibitor, significantly attenuates these protective effects. Notably, lipid peroxides and iron accumulation have been identified as critical factors contributing to the induction of ferroptosis. These results suggest that butylphthalide has an inhibitory effect on ferroptosis. However, the mechanisms underlying the effects of butylphthalide on ferroptosis remain unclear. The findings of this study regarding the potential molecular mechanisms underlying butylphthalide's neuroprotective effect indicate that it is related to the inhibition of ferroptosis, potentially through the regulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway.

As an endogenous antioxidant transcription factor, Nrf2 is a key molecule in the regulation of oxidative stress [46]. It is vital to stabilise Nrf2 activity to maintain the redox balance and brain homeostasis. Studies have shown that Nrf2 participates in the process of cerebral I/R injury and has neuroprotective effects [47,48]. After Nrf2 activation, the transcription of some antioxidant enzymes, such as HO-1, NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), is activated [49]. Notably, HO-1 is one of the crucial antioxidant genes regulated by Nrf2 and plays a particularly vital role in endothelial homeostasis. It is considered one of the strongest endogenous antioxidants for clearing ROS and reactive nitrogen species [50]. In addition, Nrf2 is closely associated with the ferroptosis oxidative stress pathway [49]. Numerous enzymes involved in lipid peroxidation and iron metabolism are Nrf2 target genes, including HO-1 and ferroportin [49,51]. The aforementioned studies indicate that Nrf2 plays a vital role in alleviating ischaemic stroke and cerebral I/R injury, and many studies have indicated that Nrf2 is a valuable therapeutic target in regulating ischaemic stroke.

A drug research project indicates that pentoxifylline protectes against cerebral I/R injury through ferroptosis regulation via the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 signalling pathway [29]. Additionally, kaempferol triggered Nrf2 nuclear translocation and provided protection from OGD/R-induced ferroptosis [52]. Furthermore, long-term butylphthalide pretreatment improved neurological deficits and reduced cerebral infarction volumes in dMCAO mouse models by promoting the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 [23]. In this study, we observed the effect of butylphthalide on Nrf2 in MCAO/R rats. The results showed that nuclear Nrf2 levels increased and cytoplasmic Nrf2 levels decreased after MCAO/R, suggesting that the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 after MCAO/R may be a self-protective response. Butylphthalide treatment facilitated the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 in the cerebral cortex of rats after MCAO/R. However, treatment with the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 attenuated the effect of butylphthalide on Nrf2 in MCAO/R rats.

The Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway is a vital mechanism in the body for resisting oxidative stress [53]. As a target gene of Nrf2, HO-1 has a neuroprotective effect on brain injury. After Nrf2 is activated, it can promote HO-1 expression. The upregulation of HO-1 expression can regulate SOD, GSH-Px and other antioxidant enzymes [50]. Studies have recently demonstrated that the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway is key to reducing lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [21,54], and β-Caryophyllene suppresses ferroptosis through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway in MCAO/R rats [21]. It has been reported that procyanidins alleviate cerebral I/R injury by inhibiting ferroptosis through the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway [54].

The antioxidant enzyme GPX4 is a key regulator of ferroptosis. As an endogenous antioxidant transcription factor, Nrf2 can activate the transcription of several downstream antioxidant enzymes, including HO-1 and GPX4 [49,52,55]. Iron distribution in tissues is associated with endocytosis, which is mediated by the binding of TfR1 and TfR2 [56]. Previous studies have shown that the overexpression of TfR1 can promote ferroptosis by enhancing iron uptake [57,58]. Other studies, including one on cerebral I/R injury [59], have found that the inhibition of ACSL4 in different organs and diseases can inhibit ferroptosis and alleviate I/R injury [35,60]. In the present study, we observed the effects of butylphthalide on the protein levels of TfR1, GPX4, ACSL4 and HO-1 after MCAO/R in rats. The results revealed that GPX4 was decreased and ACSL4 and TfR1 increased at 24 ​h after MCAO/R. Butylphthalide treatment significantly increased GPX4 and HO-1 levels and decreased ACSL4 and TfR1 levels in MCAO/R rats. However, ML385 attenuated the effect of butylphthalide on these protein levels. Real-time RT-PCR and immunohistochemical staining produced the same results.

In this study, we investigated the mechanism by which NBP inhibits ferroptosis and ameliorates cerebral I/R) injury in rats by activating the Nrf2 and HO-1 signallling pathways. The study not only detected the expression changes of related proteins and mRNAs at the molecular level (Western blot, qPCR), but also observed the distribution of the target proteins in brain tissues by immunohistochemical staining, as well as iron content and lipid peroxidation-related indexes by using kits, and finally observed the changes of mitochondrial morphology by transmission electron microscopy. This multifaceted validation approach enhanced the reliability and persuasiveness of the study results. These results indicate that butylphthalide inhibits ferroptosis induced by cerebral I/R, whereas Nrf2 mediates the neuroprotective effect of butylphthalide on cerebral I/R injury in rats. This suggests that the significant neuroprotective effects of butylphthalide in attenuating ischaemic stroke injury are correlated with ferroptosis regulation, at least in part by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway. This finding provides new drug targets and strategies for the treatment of cerebral ischaemia/reperfusion injury.

However, we recognize that there may be limitations in the experimental design itself, such as insufficient sample size and less rigorous control of experimental conditions, which may affect the sensitivity of the statistical results. Future studies can further validate this finding by increasing the sample size and optimizing the experimental conditions. Both Zea Longa scores and mNSS scores rely on the subjective judgement of the observer, and thus may have some variability. Although every effort was made to ensure consistency and accuracy in the scoring process, it is possible that there may be imperceptible biases. In addition, the two scoring systems may not fully reflect the details of all neurological impairments, resulting in an inability to accurately differentiate between groups in some cases. The exact mechanism of action of ML385 as an experimental drug or treatment may not be fully understood. In some cases, it may not directly affect neurologic function scores or infarct volume measurements, but rather indirectly affect the disease process and prognosis through other pathways (e.g., inflammatory response, apoptosis, etc.). In this study, we have mainly used biochemical, immunohistochemical and possibly animal model methods to explore the functions and mechanisms of action of the target proteins or molecules. Although these methods provided us with certain information, they have limitations in revealing the regulatory mechanisms and causal relationships at the gene level. It is planned to explore the use of genetic approaches to further validate and expand our findings in future studies. This includes, but is not limited to, constructing knockout, gene overexpression, or conditional knockout mouse models to more directly observe the role of the target genes in disease development, as well as their effects on specific signallling pathways or cellular behaviours. In addition to this, we will continue to explore the mechanism of action of ML385 in order to provide a stronger scientific basis for its application in the treatment of cerebral ischaemia-reperfusion injury.

In summary, our study clarified that ferroptosis plays a key role in cerebral I/R injury. Butylphthalide inhibited ferroptosis and ameliorated cerebral I/R injury in MCAO/R rats. The potential mechanism of this neuroprotective effect may be related to the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway. Therefore, the pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis may be a new direction for the treatment of ischaemic stroke.
