Impaired trunk sensorimotor control during isometric force reproduction involving rotation and extension in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis
Authors: Jean-Philippe Cyr, Amirreza Kebritchi, Patricia Larouche, Pierre Mercier, Martin Simoneau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) affects 2% to 3% of adolescents and may involve alterations in sensorimotor integration. Although proprioceptive feedback from trunk muscles is essential for spinal alignment, trunk sensorimotor control remains poorly characterized in AIS. This study assessed trunk sensorimotor control in adolescents with AIS using an isometric force reproduction task and determined whether impairments emerge specifically during trunk rotation conditions requiring asymmetric loading.MethodsIn this cross-sectional controlled laboratory study, 24 adolescents with AIS and 20 age- and sex-matched controls produced isometric trunk extension forces at 2 amplitudes (15% and 30% MVC) and 5 orientations (0°, ±10°, and ±20° rotation) with real-time visual feedback, then reproduced these forces without feedback following a 5-second delay. Amplitude and directional force reproduction errors (constant and absolute) were computed and expressed as a percentage of maximal voluntary contraction and degrees, respectively.ResultsIn the neutral position, no group differences were observed. During trunk rotation at 15% MVC, AIS participants exhibited significantly larger amplitude constant errors (overshooting) compared with controls, and performance varied across rotation angles. At 30% MVC, amplitude overshooting persisted in the AIS group. Directional accuracy was modulated by rotation angle in both groups, with larger errors at more extreme rotations (±20°). Exploratory correlations suggested a possible association between younger age and larger amplitude errors in AIS participants, a pattern not observed in controls.ConclusionsAdolescents with AIS exhibited force reproduction deficits specifically during combined extension and rotation, suggesting altered trunk sensorimotor control under asymmetric loading.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), the most common spinal deformity, affects 2% to 3% of adolescents and is characterized by a 3-dimensional curvature of the spine with multifactorial origins, including genetic, biomechanical, and neurological factors [1]. Beyond its structural presentation, accumulating evidence suggests that AIS may involve alterations in sensorimotor integration and processing. Because proprioceptive feedback from the trunk muscles is essential for postural control and spinal alignment [2,3], alterations in sensorimotor processing may contribute to the maintenance or progression of the deformity. A recent meta-analysis reported that adolescents with AIS perform worse than peers without scoliosis while performing joint repositioning tasks across multiple body regions (neck, upper, and lower extremities) and exhibit a higher threshold for detecting movement [4]. Moreover, 2 of the included studies reported prolonged latencies of somatosensory evoked potentials elicited by electrical stimulation of peripheral nerves (posterior tibial nerve), with an interlateral asymmetry characterized by slower responses on the concave side of the spinal curve [5,6]. However, because somatosensory evoked potentials in these studies were elicited by electrical stimulation of peripheral nerves of the lower limb, they reflect global alterations in ascending somatosensory processing and provide only indirect information about trunk-specific sensorimotor control. While the overall level of evidence remains limited, these findings raise the question of whether altered trunk sensorimotor control represents a key factor underlying sensorimotor impairment in AIS.

Multiple studies have used joint repositioning tasks to evaluate limb position sense in AIS. These tests consistently show that individuals with scoliosis exhibit greater errors in reproducing joint positions than healthy controls, indicating impaired position sense [7,8]. Moreover, research indicates that AIS patients rely more on visual and ankle proprioceptive cues and have difficulty integrating ankle proprioceptive information when visual feedback is removed, such as during eyes-closed conditions with ankle muscle vibration [9]. Modern understanding of position sense recognizes that multisensory integration dynamically modulates muscle spindle signals, both peripherally (via gamma-motor modulation) and centrally (via processing and convergence with other sensory inputs) to support movement and body awareness [[10], [11], [12]]. Most research has focused on the limbs, particularly the forearm, where joint manipulation is experimentally convenient. Much less is known about the trunk and axial musculature, although these regions are fundamental for posture and spinal alignment [3,13,14]. The gap in the scientific literature is especially relevant in AIS, given that impaired trunk sensorimotor control could alter spinal orientation or stability.

Despite the clear relevance of trunk sensorimotor control to spinal alignment, no studies have directly assessed its performance using force-reproduction tasks in AIS. Force reproduction tasks provide complementary information to position-matching tasks, as they specifically evaluate the ability to integrate motor commands with sensory feedback to achieve a desired output [15,16]. The present study used an active trunk force reproduction task to test the hypothesis that adolescents with AIS exhibit impaired trunk sensorimotor control compared to healthy controls. We predicted that AIS participants would exhibit greater errors in reproducing trunk forces and that these impairments would be amplified during trunk rotation, due to asymmetric muscular and proprioceptive signals inherent to the scoliotic deformation [17]. By shifting the focus from limbs to axial musculature, our work provides new insights into trunk sensorimotor control in AIS.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-four adolescents with AIS and 20 age- and sex-matched control (CTR) participants were recruited for this research. AIS participants were recruited from a pediatric orthopedic clinic (Clinique d'orthopédie pédiatrique de la Cité Médicale). Pediatric orthopedic surgeons established the diagnosis and determined the Cobb angle and Risser sign. The primary curve type (thoracic, lumbar, or thoracolumbar) and curve direction were also documented (Table). The inclusion criteria were age between 10 and 19 years, a confirmed diagnosis of AIS, and otherwise good health. During a routine medical visit, eligible participants were invited to receive study information. Control participants were recruited from the university community through email, social media posts, and contact information from previous studies. The experimenter performed the Adam forward bend test to ensure that none had scoliosis or other spine deformities. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (CIUSSS de la Capitale-Nationale, 2023-2754). Data were collected from February 2023 to October 2025. Group characteristics included age, height, weight, and self-reported physical activity. We tested for intergroup differences using independent-samples Student t-tests, except for physical activity (due to non-normality). The groups did not differ significantly in any demographic variable. Among AIS participants, 12 had a primary thoracic curve, four had a lumbar curve, and 8 had a thoracolumbar curve. Curve convexity was distributed as follows: 13 right thoracic, 4 left lumbar, 7 right thoracolumbar. Nineteen participants (83%) had compensatory curves. At the time of the experiment, 8 AIS participants were undergoing bracing treatment (Boston: n = 3; Providence: n = 5), 1 was using a shoe lift, and 2 had completed bracing. Overall, the AIS group had a mean quality-of-life score of 62 ± 9%.

Procedures

Positioning and familiarization

Each session lasted approximately 3 hours. At the beginning of the session, participants completed questionnaires about lifestyle habits. Those with AIS also completed the French-Canadian adaptation of the Italian Spine Youth Quality of Life (ISYQOL-F) questionnaire [18,19]. Participants then stood with their arms alongside their bodies and their feet positioned at an intermalleolar distance equal to their foot length. They leaned their back against a rigid structure fixed to the wall, which included a waist-level belt to stabilize the pelvis and isolate trunk movement, and a cushioned surface for comfort (Fig. 1A). A multicomponent force transducer (model MC3A, Advanced Mechanical Technology, Inc.) was positioned 5 cm below the C7 vertebra and interfaced with a 6-channel amplifier (model MSA-6, Advanced Mechanical Technology, Inc.). Participants faced a monitor approximately 1.5 m in front of them, at eye level, that displayed the visual interface providing real-time feedback about the amplitude and orientation of the trunk resultant force (LabVIEW, National Instruments). The trunk force was displayed as real-time feedback as an arrow (Fig. 1A). The length of the arrow corresponded to the magnitude of the force, and its orientation: trunk rotation in the transverse plane. This feedback was congruent with the direction of trunk rotation. Force signals were collected at 1,000 Hz using a digital-to-analog converter (model NI PCIe-6531, National Instruments Corp.) and transmitted to a laptop via an isolated multifunction I/O device (model NI USB-6216, National Instruments Corp.). Before data collection, the experimenter demonstrated the task, and participants completed familiarization trials (as detailed in the Supplementary Materials).

Maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs)

Once participants were familiar with the feedback interface and could reliably control the orientation and amplitude of their trunk force, we assessed maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs). For this task, a different visual display was presented on the monitor (Fig. 1A, MVC display). Three green circles appeared sequentially in a 3-2-1 countdown before lighting up simultaneously, signaling participants to extend their trunk and push maximally against the force transducer. During each MVC, participants were instructed to maintain their maximal effort for 8 seconds while keeping the force vector oriented toward a predetermined position: neutral (0 ± 3°), rotated to the right (+10 ± 3°), or rotated to the left (−10 ± 3°), where 0° represented a vertical line through the center of the monitor corresponding to pure trunk extension with no rotation. Participants performed 2 MVCs in each orientation, with a 2-minute rest interval between trials. The first 2 MVCs were always performed in the neutral position, after which directions alternated between left and right. These MVC recordings were later used to normalize force level across participants for the main experimental trials.

Main task: force reproduction

We used the MVC recordings to determine participant- and direction-specific targets corresponding to 15% or 30% of maximal force. Because MVCs were collected only at 0° and ±10°, the ±20° targets were expressed relative to the MVC obtained at ±10° in the same direction (ie, +10° for +20° and −10° for −20°). Studies of limb force reproduction have shown that force perception accuracy varies nonlinearly across the force range, with systematic overshooting at forces below approximately 25% to 40% MVC and optimal accuracy around 50% MVC [20,21]. While our study was not designed to investigate force-level effects, we selected force levels to capture performance in a range where force perception may be particularly sensitive to task demands, while remaining within the capacity of all participants to produce stable, sustained forces. During each trial, a visual target arrow appeared on the monitor indicating both the required force magnitude (15% or 30% MVC) and orientation (0°, +10°, −10°, +20°, or −20°). The positive and negative signs indicate the direction of rotation (positive = right, negative = left from the participant’s perspective). Participants produced force against the transducer to align their feedback arrow with the target (Fig. 1A, main task display). They were instructed to match both the length and orientation of the target arrow as precisely as possible and to hold this position steadily. After 10 seconds of force production with visual feedback, the target and feedback disappeared simultaneously, and participants were instructed to relax completely and return to a neutral resting position. To limit the effect of memory on reproduction performance [15], we used a constant 5 seconds delay between the termination of force production and the initiation of reproduction. After this delay, a visual cue indicated that participants should reproduce the same force magnitude and orientation, without visual feedback. Participants held their reproduced force for 15 seconds. This sequence was repeated 60 times, with the first 12 trials being in the neutral condition. Then, combinations of force magnitude and orientation were shuffled across trials. Breaks were provided every 6 trials to minimize fatigue. All AIS participants were tested without their brace.

Data analysis

Force signal processing

The direction and amplitude of the visual feedback were calculated from the X and Z forces. The X component represents lateral forces acting along the left-right axis of the participant, and the Z component represents anteroposterior forces acting along the direction of trunk extension. Force data were filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 4 Hz. Then, we calculated a net force (as shown in Fig. 1B, upper panel) from the X and Z force components:Fnet=Fx2+Fz2 and the orientation of the force:θ=atan2(Fx,Fz)

Identification of stable force production

A moving standard deviation of the net force signal was computed using a sliding window of 1 second. The standard deviation of the net force was used to identify when participants stabilized their trunk force production after the initial rise in force (Fig. 1B, lower panel). Participants were considered to have stabilized their force when they maintained a net force standard deviation of less than 5 N for at least 1 second. Visual inspection of all trials confirmed that this criterion reliably identified periods of stable trunk force production. Once trunk force stabilization was detected, 1 second of data was extracted, beginning at the stabilization time point. This time window was used for all subsequent analyses of both the force-production phase (with visual feedback) and the force-reproduction phase (without visual feedback).

Quantification of reproduction errors

To quantify the accuracy of trunk force reproduction, we computed errors between the target and reproduced forces. The Z force component (Fz; corresponding to trunk isometric extension) served as the primary indicator of amplitude error (ie, the participant’s ability to reproduce force magnitude). In contrast, force direction was primarily influenced by the lateral component (Fx; corresponding to axial trunk rotation) and was therefore quantified as the angular deviation of the force vector. Amplitude errors were expressed as a percentage of the direction-specific MVC and directional errors in degrees (°) to account for interindividual differences in strength, consistent with standard procedures in force reproduction studies [22]. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted separately for neutral (0° rotation) and rotation (±10° and ±20° rotation) conditions. For the neutral condition, which served as a baseline assessment without directional demands, group differences were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests at each force level (15% and 30% MVC). When normality was violated (Shapiro–Wilk), Welch’s t-test was reported. For rotation conditions, outcomes were analyzed at each force level using a Group × Rotation design. Linear mixed models were used when model estimation was stable (ie, amplitude and absolute direction errors), with participant as a random intercept and fixed effects tested using Type III sums of squares with Satterthwaite approximations. For the direction constant error, mixed-model estimation failed to fit the data. Given the balanced 2 × 4 repeated-measures design, this outcome was analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA. When Mauchly’s test indicated a violation of sphericity, Greenhouse–Geisser-corrected results were reported.

We conducted an exploratory analysis to examine whether force reproduction performance was associated with participant characteristics. This analysis aimed to identify potential moderating factors for future investigation. For each participant, we computed mean errors averaged across all force levels (15% and 30% MVC) and rotation conditions (±10° and ±20°). Given that some authors have hypothesized that alterations in sensorimotor control would affect spine growth only during critical developmental phases [23,24], we specifically examined the relationship between age and force reproduction performance in both groups. Additionally, we investigated the association between the Cobb angle and performance in the AIS group to determine whether the severity of spinal deformity is associated with trunk sensorimotor control. Due to the non-normal distribution of absolute amplitude error in the AIS group, Bayesian correlations were employed for age-related analyses in this group. In contrast, Pearson correlations were used for the control group.

Finally, we conducted AIS within-group analysis to explore the potential effect of bracing treatment and curve convexity. To examine whether bracing status influenced force reproduction performance, we compared AIS participants who were currently undergoing brace treatment (n = 8) with those who were not (n = 16) using Welch’s independent-samples t-tests on participant-level outcomes averaged across rotation conditions. To examine curve convexity and rotation, we conducted an exploratory AIS within-group analysis in which rotation direction was recoded as toward or away from the convex side. Because only 4 participants presented with a left-sided curve, this approach avoided underpowered subgroup comparisons based on curve direction. Linear mixed models were used where model estimation was stable, and repeated-measures ANOVAs were used otherwise. All analyses were performed in JASP (JASP Team, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). For F-tests from repeated-measures ANOVAs and linear mixed models, effect sizes are reported as partial η2. Cohen’s d is reported for group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at α ≤ 0.05.

Rotation conditions

Amplitude reproduction at 15% MVC

Analysis of amplitude constant error during trunk rotation at 15% MVC (Fig. 3, 15% MVC) revealed a significant main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 6.77, p = .01, partial η2 = 0.14), with AIS participants showing larger positive errors compared to controls (mean across rotations: AIS = 4.02 ± 1.53% MVC; CTR = 1.04 ± 0.94% MVC). A significant main effect of Rotation was also observed (F [3, 126] = 6.32, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.13). A planned contrast, averaged across groups, indicated larger amplitude constant errors at extreme (±20°) than at moderate (±10°) trunk rotations (difference = 2.55, t [126] = 4.15, p < .001). The Group × Rotation interaction did not reach significance (F [3, 126] = 2.53, p = .06, partial η2 = 0.06), indicating that the group difference was consistent across target orientations (see Supplementary Table C2 for descriptive statistics). For the absolute amplitude error, the linear mixed model showed no main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 2.58, p = .12, partial η2 = 0.06), but a significant main effect of Rotation (F [3, 126] = 5.15, p < .01, partial η2 = 0.11) and a significant Group × Rotation interaction (F [3, 126] = 3.08, p = .03, partial η2 = 0.07), indicating that rotation angle modulated absolute amplitude error differently across groups (see Supplementary Table C2 for descriptive statistics). To decompose the interaction, group differences were tested at each rotation angle using contrasts with Holm adjustment. Directional reproduction at 15% MVC

For direction constant error at 15% MVC, Mauchly’s test indicated sphericity violation, so Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. There was no main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 0.33, p = .57, partial η2 = 0.01), no main effect of Rotation (F [1.27, 53.26] = 2.46, p = .12, partial η2 = 0.06), and no Group × Rotation interaction (F (1.27, 53.26) = 0.10, p = 0.81, partial η2 < 0.01) (see Supplementary Material Table C2 for descriptive statistics). For direction absolute error at 15% MVC, the model showed no main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 1.78, p = .19, partial η2 = 0.04), a significant main effect of Rotation (F [3, 126] = 3.15, p = .03, partial η2 = 0.07), and no Group × Rotation interaction (F [3, 126] = 0.82, p = .48, partial η2 = 0.02). A planned contrast, averaged across groups, indicated larger absolute directional errors at extreme (±20°) than at moderate (±10°) trunk rotations (difference = 2.47°, t [126] = 2.99, p = .003) (see Supplementary Material Table C2 for descriptive statistics).

Amplitude reproduction at 30% MVC

At the higher force level (Fig. 3 right column, 30% MVC), amplitude constant error showed a significant main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 5.70, p = .02, partial η2 = 0.12) and a significant main effect of Rotation (F [3, 126] = 7.88, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.16), with AIS participants showing larger signed errors (mean ± SD: AIS = 1.05 ± 2.24% MVC; CTR = −1.93 ± 1.89% MVC) compared to controls. A planned contrast indicated larger errors at extreme (±20°) than at moderate (±10°) rotations (difference = 3.99% MVC, z = 4.83, p < .001), averaged across groups. The Group × Rotation interaction was not significant (F [3, 126] = 0.67, p = .57, partial η2 = 0.02) (see Supplementary Material Table C2 for descriptive statistics). For the absolute amplitude error at 30% MVC, the linear mixed model revealed no significant effects of Group (F [1, 42] = 0.01, p = .92, partial η2 < 0.001), Rotation (F [3, 126] = 0.77, p = .51, partial η2 = 0.02), or Group × Rotation interaction (F [3, 126] = 0.62, p = .60, partial η2 = 0.01).

Directional reproduction at 30% MVC

For direction constant error at 30% MVC, because Mauchly’s test indicated violation of the sphericity assumption, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. There was no main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 0.08, p = .78, partial η2 = 0.002) and no Group × Rotation interaction (F [1.31, 54.95] = 0.22, p = .71, partial η2 = 0.01), but there was a significant main effect of Rotation (F [1.32, 54.95] = 32.73, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.44), with more extreme rotations (±20°) producing larger constant biases than moderate rotations (±10°) (see Supplementary Material Table C2 for descriptive statistics). For direction absolute error at 30% MVC, the linear mixed model revealed no significant main effect of Group (F [1, 42] = 0.57, p = .45, partial η2 = 0.01) and no Group × Rotation interaction (F [3, 126] = 0.64, p = .59, partial η2 = 0.01), but a significant main effect of Rotation (F [3, 126] = 18.66, p < .001, partial η2 = 0.31) (see Supplementary Material Table C2 for descriptive statistics). The extreme versus moderate contrast, however, was not significant (difference = −0.40, t [87] = −0.79, p = .43).

Correlation with participants characteristics

Exploratory correlational analyses examined relationships between the mean dependent variables, (ie, amplitude and direction constant and absolute errors) averaged across all conditions, and participant characteristics. Given that results from an animal model demonstrated that alterations in sensorimotor control affect spinal growth only during critical developmental phases [25], we examined the relationship between age and force reproduction performance. Additionally, we investigated the association between Cobb angle (ie, spinal deformation severity) and performance in the AIS group to determine whether spinal deformation severity is associated with trunk sensorimotor control abilities. Regarding age, the distribution of absolute amplitude errors in the AIS group was not normal, so Bayesian correlations were used. A weak negative correlation was observed (Fig. 4, r = −0.18, BF10 = 3.15), providing moderate evidence for the alternative hypothesis and suggesting a probable relationship: younger AIS participants may have greater difficulty reproducing trunk force. In contrast, the control group showed no such relationship (r = 0.08, p = .74). To test whether this association was robust when accounting for possible confounding effect of curve severity, we conducted a Bayesian multiple linear regression including age and Cobb angle as predictors of amplitude absolute error. Adding Cobb angle did not increase evidence relative to the age-only model (BF10 = 0.52), indicating that the age association was not robust when age and Cobb were considered jointly. Cobb angle showed no significant association with trunk sensorimotor control performance in AIS (all ps > .20).

Curve- and brace treatment-specific analyses

Within the AIS group, Welch's independent-samples t-tests averaged across rotation conditions revealed no statistically significant differences for any of the 4 error metrics (all ps > .30, all Cohen’s d < 0.50). Direction relative to curve convexity did not significantly influence amplitude constant error, absolute amplitude error, or absolute direction error at either force level (all ps > .08), although a trend was observed for amplitude constant error at 30% MVC (F [1, 69] = 3.19, p = .08, partial η2 = 0.04). For these metrics, rotation magnitude remained a significant predictor consistent with the main analyses, with greater errors at 20° than at 10°. For direction constant error, the model produced a singular fit at both force levels and repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted. At 15% MVC, no significant effects were found (all ps > .17). At 30% MVC, a significant main effect of direction relative to curve convexity was observed (F [1, 23] = 6.46, p = .02, partial η2 = 0.22), along with a significant interaction with rotation magnitude (F [1, 23] = 7.03, p = .01, partial η2​ = 0.23). Posthoc comparisons with Holm adjustment indicated that direction constant error was significantly lower when rotating toward the convex side compared to away (mean difference = −2.16°, t [23] = −2.54, p = .02, Cohen’s d = −0.85). When rotating toward the convex side, direction constant errors were −0.01 ± 1.12° at 10° and −1.46 ± 3.11° at 20°, whereas rotating away yielded 0.98 ± 2.29° at 10° and 1.88 ± 3.14° at 20°.

Discussion

We investigated trunk sensorimotor control in adolescents with AIS using an active force production and reproduction task. Because spinal deformity involves asymmetric loading and activation of the paraspinal muscles, which may increase sensorimotor processing demands, we hypothesized that adolescents with AIS would exhibit impaired trunk sensorimotor control compared with healthy age- and sex-matched adolescents, particularly during trunk rotation. Our findings partially supported this hypothesis.

While AIS participants did not differ from controls when reproducing trunk forces in the neutral position, they demonstrated systematic overshooting of force reproduction amplitude during trunk rotation at 15% MVC. Directional accuracy, in contrast, was mainly influenced by rotation angle, with more extreme rotations (±20°) producing larger directional errors in both groups. At 30% MVC, force reproduction amplitude overshooting persisted in the AIS group, though directional errors did not differ between groups. An effect of rotation angle was observed in both groups, with more extreme rotations (±20°, ie, with larger lateral force components) producing greater directional errors than moderate rotations (±10°). Overall, these results suggest that alterations in trunk sensorimotor control in AIS worsen when the task requires asymmetric activation of the torso musculature, as occurs during combined trunk extension and rotation.

Interpreting force reproduction in AIS

The comparable performance between groups in the neutral condition suggests that adolescents with AIS can accurately reproduce trunk extension forces when the task involves symmetric trunk muscle activation. The rotation condition, however, required asymmetric activation of the trunk muscles. The systematic force overshooting observed suggests an alteration in trunk sensorimotor control, perhaps reflecting more variable timing of intermuscular oscillations (ie, reduced intermuscular coherence) between torso muscles in this condition [26]. Because the force reproduction task requires both accurate perception of the produced force and the ability to generate a matching motor command, the observed errors could arise from impairments at the sensory (proprioceptive) level, the motor level, or both. However, AIS participants reproduced trunk forces as accurately as controls in the neutral condition, suggesting that the basic motor capacity to reproduce a memorized force is preserved. The emergence of group differences specifically during rotation conditions points instead toward altered trunk sensorimotor control when the task requires asymmetric loading, potentially involving altered processing of trunk proprioceptive information.

This finding contrasts with evidence from limb force reproduction studies, in which increasing the degrees of freedom tends to decrease error and enhance performance [27]. Unlike limb joints, axial proprioception arises from sensory inputs distributed across multiple vertebral levels, including multi-segment paraspinal muscles, intervertebral tissues, and cutaneous receptors. This diffuse sensory organization requires the nervous system to reconstruct trunk orientation from distributed rather than discrete joint signals [3,14]. This distributed organization may make trunk proprioception particularly sensitive to asymmetric axial loading, a condition inherent to both the rotation task and the scoliotic deformity itself.

The larger errors during trunk rotation could also be interpreted through the framework of sensorimotor redundancy. Indeed, the rotation condition required participants to generate trunk extension and axial rotation simultaneously, transforming the task from unidimensional force production into a multidimensional coordination problem. Prior work indicates that rotational tasks are inherently noisier and require greater reliance on central estimation processes, particularly because trunk orientation must be reconstructed from distributed, segmented proprioceptive signals [3,11]. Studies of limb force reproduction have shown that even in healthy individuals, force perception accuracy varies nonlinearly across the force range, with systematic overshooting at forces below approximately 25% to 40% MVC and optimal accuracy around 50% MVC [20,21]. In our study, the target forces were set at 15% and 30% MVC, precisely the range where force perception is suboptimal. This suboptimal range may have made the task particularly sensitive to group differences for trunk sensorimotor control.

In addition, during trunk rotation, the requirement to simultaneously produce extension and lateral forces results in differential activation of axial muscles on each side of the spine. For adolescents with AIS, this coordination challenge at suboptimal force levels is further compounded by structural and altered neural control of their lumbar musculature [26]. Scoliotic deformities modify the passive mechanical properties of the trunk [28] and introduce asymmetries in muscle activation patterns [29,30], thereby degrading the fidelity of proprioceptive afferents from paraspinal muscles. The systematic overshooting observed in AIS and the rotation-dependent increase in directional errors could therefore reflect increased difficulty in processing asymmetric proprioceptive afferents and altered neural control of the torso muscles during trunk rotation.

To determine whether the force reproduction errors observed during trunk rotation might reflect difficulties in force production rather than merely trunk proprioception integration impairments, we conducted a posteriori analysis of the reference (force production) phase during which participants generated forces with continuous visual feedback. The results from mixed model analyses of the 4 rotation conditions did not reveal a significant group difference in amplitude constant error during guided force production, either at 15% MVC (F [1, 42] = 1.57, p = .22) or at 30% MVC (F [1, 42] = 2.08, p = .16). This suggests that in the presence of visual feedback, the trunk sensorimotor control of adolescents with AIS matched that of the controls. However, without visual feedback (i.e, during the reproduction phase), the less accurate force reproduction for targets requiring asymmetrical trunk muscle activation likely resulted from the tight coupling between internal estimates of torso orientation and force production, as orientation estimation error will produce force errors.

Age-related patterns in sensorimotor performance

Exploratory correlational analyses revealed a pattern suggesting that younger AIS participants exhibited larger force reproduction errors, a relationship not observed in controls. While this finding should be interpreted with caution, the relationship between age and trunk sensorimotor control performance in AIS is of particular interest, given the ongoing debate over whether sensorimotor impairment is an etiological factor or a consequence of spinal deformity [1]. If sensorimotor alterations contribute to scoliosis, their effects may be expressed during spinal growth, when asymmetric trunk sensorimotor control could influence skeletal development [24]. The absence of this age-related pattern in controls, who lack the structural asymmetry that may interact with maturational processes, is consistent with this interpretation.

In contrast, if sensorimotor impairment arises secondarily from the structural deformity, errors would be expected to correlate with the severity of the curve rather than with age. Here, spine curvature severity (ie, Cobb angle) did not correlate with force reproduction errors in our AIS group; the strongest observed relationship between Cobb angle and force reproduction performance was constant direction error, r = 0.05, p = .83. This absence of association between spine curvature severity and trunk sensorimotor control performance suggests independence between the 2, consistent with results from Pialasse et al. [23] and indicates that severity does not account for the observed impairment in trunk sensorimotor control. From a developmental perspective, our observation of an age-related pattern in trunk sensorimotor control (ie, force reproduction errors) is consistent with animal work showing that sensorimotor or vestibular asymmetries affect spinal development when they occur during early development [25]. We acknowledge, however, that our protocol was not designed to test the developmental hypothesis. To determine whether the observed age-related pattern arises from developmental processes, longitudinal studies examining trunk sensorimotor control across adolescence in AIS are necessary.

Clinical perspectives

From a clinical perspective, the differences in force reproduction observed under rotational conditions raise the question of whether trunk sensorimotor control during asymmetric loading could be relevant to rehabilitation in AIS. However, the present data do not directly evaluate the clinical utility of the force reproduction task or the efficacy of sensorimotor training. The condition-specific nature of the observed differences suggests that future intervention studies could examine whether exercises incorporating trunk rotation components improve sensorimotor accuracy.

In the exploratory AIS within-group analysis, the direction constant error at 30% MVC was negative when participants rotated toward the convex side of their curve but positive when they rotated away from it, suggesting a direction-dependent bias in the reproduced force orientation under higher task demands. Because this pattern was observed for only 1 error metric at a single force level, it should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, it suggests that the direction of trunk loading relative to curve convexity may be worth considering in future studies of trunk sensorimotor rehabilitation.

Limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the current findings. First, the sample size is small and was determined by the number of eligible participants that could be recruited during the study period. The experimental design did not include force level as a statistical factor to maintain sufficient statistical power to detect group differences in rotation conditions, our main research question. Moreover, these force levels (15% and 30% MVC) fell within the suboptimal range for force perception [20,21], potentially limiting the detection of force-level effects.

Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes inferences about the causal relationship between alterations in trunk sensorimotor control and the onset or progression of scoliosis. Third, the static, isolated nature of the isometric force reproduction task may not fully capture the dynamic, complex demands of sensorimotor control encountered in daily activities. Future studies using longitudinal designs and dynamic trunk movements could help address these limitations and clarify the developmental trajectory of trunk sensorimotor control alterations in adolescents with AIS.

Conclusion

This study shows that adolescents with AIS exhibit force reproduction deficits specifically during combined trunk extension and rotation, suggesting altered trunk sensorimotor control under asymmetric loading conditions. The exploratory observation that younger AIS participants exhibited larger errors raises the possibility that these alterations may be linked to developmental processes, warranting longitudinal investigation.
