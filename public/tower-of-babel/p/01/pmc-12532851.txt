What do dentists do and earn? An exploratory analysis of French dentists with a focus on primary care teams
Authors: Anne-Charlotte Bas, Matthieu Cassou, Carine Franc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccess to dental care in France faces two main barriers: high out-of-pocket costs and a widely uneven distribution of dentists. Multiprofessional primary care groups (MPCGs) are currently in development to improve primary care efficiency and enhance the attractiveness of medically underserved areas. We aim to address the research question: What factors influence the income and activity of self-employed dentists, and how do multiprofessional primary care groups (MPCGs) and local socio-economic conditions shape these outcomes? We use data merging an administrative health insurance database and information from tax declarations of professionals in 2017, which we combined with contextual socio-economic information on the areas where dentists practice. There were 20,961 self-employed dentists, including 201 working in MPCGs, in 2017. We study various aspects of dentists’ practices, including net income, turnover, activity volumes, and case mix among professionals. We analyze their associated factors by conducting standard OLS regression methods. Male, middle-aged, and self-employed dentists with children provided more services and saw more patients than others. In line with market behaviour expectations, the dental bill increases with the local median income, while the volume of procedures decreases in parallel, echoing labour-leisure trade-offs. The activity and annual income of dentists decreased in areas with high densities of dental practitioners. Despite the limited number of dentists in MPCGs, our results advocate for a deeper investigation of the topic, as MPCG dentists provided more dental care than others in terms of volume, with a higher share of young patients and lower extra fees per service.Keywords: Dentistry/dental care; Multiprofessional teamwork; Income, extra fees

Introduction

Geographical and financial barriers to accessing dental care have been well-documented, challenging equitable access in many countries [1, 2]. In France, outpatient healthcare payment for healthcare professionals is mainly based on a fee-for-service system. Regulation primarily involves setting the regulated fees for medical procedures, which serve as the basis for reimbursement by public health insurance. The dentists are allowed to bill above these regulated fees. Usually, extra fees are not covered by public health insurance and are often poorly compensated by complementary health insurance [3]. Finally, patients frequently incur high out-of-pocket costs for dental treatments, averaging € 110 in 2019 [3], and have done so for a long time [4]. As a result, almost one-fifth of the French population postpones or foregoes dental treatment due to financial reasons [5]. At the same time, geographical obstacles are also a major issue in accessing dental care, particularly for dental treatments for which no practitioners are offering alternative care. In France, the number of dentists per capita is relatively low, with an average of 0.6 dentists for every 1 000 inhabitants in 2022, compared with the European average of 0,8 [6]. A third of the French territory is considered underserved in dentists, with fewer than 0.4 dentists per 1 000 inhabitants [7]. In France, as with other medical practices, the establishment of dental practices is not yet subject to geographical restrictions, but the least attractive areas are often rural and have low-income populations. These socioeconomically disadvantaged populations are more likely to have poor oral health yet are less likely to afford costly dental care [8]. As a result, dentists in rural areas are more likely to treat patients with greater oral health needs but lower ability to pay for expensive treatments. The pressure of demand in a context of considerable rationing may be another criterion of unattractiveness. Thus, the incentives for dentists to establish their practice in an underendowed zone are relatively low, and the consequences in terms of workload are uncertain.

In France, dental care accounted for 6.5% of current health expenditure in 2022, totaling €13.9 billion [9]. This share has remained stable for the past ten years. In 2021, the average annual net income of self-employed dentists was €92 542. Female dentists earned 27% less than male dentists [10]. Despite these regular reports, little is known about what drives income and activity differences among dentists. There are few analyses of dentists’ activities, fees, and incomes, which makes it difficult to understand dentists’ strategic choices in terms of their practices. There is a particular need to investigate how professional behaviors vary across different geographical contexts [11]. Studying these characteristics will enable us to identify levers for improving the way care provision is matched to demand.

In outpatient care, the trend is clearly toward group practice for the French health professionals in private practice [12]. In 2021, 54% of dentists practiced in groups with other dentists or with other health professionals, compared to 47% in 2013. Additionally, 30% practiced in individual private practice (down from 43% in 2013), and the remainder typically practiced with a salaried status [7]. Among the groups, multiprofessional primary care groups (MPCGs) have been financially supported by health authorities over the past decade, first to improve the efficiency of primary care and second to increase the attractiveness of underserved areas for professionals, through coordination and teamwork. MPCGs bring together health professionals in a group whose health project has been approved by local public health authorities. This health project, combined with several defined criteria, enables the MPCG to obtain accreditation, which entitles it to funding aimed at promoting the development and ensuring the sustainability of MPCGs. Thus, the group receives annual funding based on its achievement of improving care access criteria, such as extending opening hours, developing teamwork, and an incentive to expand the range of services offered by the group, as well as coordination and establishing shared information systems. MPCG practice appears to be financially attractive for general practitioners, at least in the short term [13]. While MPCGs are most often located in underserved areas [10], they appear to retain physicians even in underendowed territories [13, 14]. MPCGs could thus reduce geographical inequalities in the distribution of health professionals [15] and increase the volume and the range of primary care services available [16]. While the MPCGs usually include a diverse range of health professionals (GPs, nurses, midwives, pharmacists, etc.), the enrollment of dentists is a relatively recent development and remains rather rare. However, the number of dentists in MPCG doubled between 2014 and 2022, accounting for an average of 2.7% of professionals enrolled in the MPCGs in 2022 [17]. MPCGs are not necessarily attractive to dentists given their specificities, such as the very limited delegation of dental tasks. Investments in technical dental equipment cannot be shared with other healthcare professionals and may constitute significant financial barriers that should also be considered. MPCGs may, however, respond to the attraction of young dentists, as they do for general practitioners (GPs), to practice with other health professionals [12]. Diversifying the specialties of health professionals within MPCGs may facilitate the delivery of more comprehensive patient care, including preventive practices.

Prevention in oral health is a crucial issue given that teeth do not heal or grow back, and treating them before caries deteriorate is essential [18]. Dental prevention should be implemented from the earliest age to prevent later dental pathologies. A preventive dental program targeting children has been developed across France. Named Lov’U’r Teeth in English (LUT) (and M’T Dents in French), this program provides free oral check-ups and dental care for children at specific ages (6, 9, 12, and 15 years old). LUT’s objectives are to enhance dental prevention for children in a context where pediatric practice may offer little appeal in dentistry. Indeed, handling children’s behaviors can be challenging, and dental services on temporary teeth are generally procedures with regulated fees. There is a minimal possibility of extra fees in pediatric dentistry.

The regulations governing billing and reimbursement for dental care are therefore likely to strongly influence dentists’ practice patterns and the competitive environment in which they operate. This study explores how the income and activity levels of self-employed dentists in France are related to three main factors: (i) individual characteristics, (ii) local socioeconomic and geographic context, and (iii) participation in multiprofessional primary care groups (MPCGs). To do so, we use an exhaustive dataset to study dental activities of self-employed dentists in France for 2017. We analyze the characteristics of the self-employed activity of French dentists, including their patient case mix and remuneration. We then focus on dentists practicing in MPCGs, assuming that choosing to work in MPCGs is associated with a different practice, as it is for medical doctors [15]. This study will enable the characterization of dentists’ activities and provide a deeper understanding of their specific features, highlighting points that can serve as effective incentives in terms of demographics, productivity, and preventive practice.

Data and empirical strategy

Data

We utilize a database that combines medical activities data from the national public health insurance and tax data from the General Directorate of Public Finances. This database is known as the “CNAM-DGFiP database.” It is exhaustive of all self-employed dentists in 2017 and allows for studying dentists’ incomes, medical practices, and patient case mix.

The scope of this study focuses on self-employed individuals in mainland France in 2017. This was the most recent year for which the role of MPCG practice can be explored. After excluding dentists with missing tax data, we apply a selection criterion to focus on dentists with a standard practice in 2017. The selection criteria are outlined in a flowchart diagram in Fig. 1. The sample comprises 20,961 dentists, including 201 working in MPCGs (≤ 1%) in 2017.

Dependent variables

The (a) variables characterize the dentists’ activity through (a1) the yearly number of services and (a2) the number of patients seen at least once during the year. We also use some activity variables to approximate preventive dentistry, including (a3) the number of dental check-ups for children (Lov U’r Teeth LUT), and (a4) the number of patients with a Long-Term Care (LTC) status. In the French healthcare system, LTC status refers to patients with severe and chronic illnesses, such as cancer, diabetes, or severe cardiovascular disease, requiring long-term and costly treatment. We included LTC status in our study because many chronic diseases are associated with a deterioration in oral health; therefore, individuals with LTC require close dental follow-up.

Based on from dentists’ tax returns, the (e) variables are related to dentists’ earnings and describe (e1) the total annual fees (turnover) corresponding to the sum of regulated fees, extra fees and other lump sums and (e2) the net annual dentists’ self-employed income corresponding the difference between total fees and production costs and potential retrocessions from locums. We also study (e3) the average bill per service corresponding to the sum of regulated fees and the extra fees, if any, over the number of services provided and, finally, (e4) the average extra fee per service. The study of both (e3) and (e4) provides a detailed analysis of the supply of dental care and the conditions of financial access. Indeed, a decrease in the average bill per service may be related to a change in the composition of dental activity, without necessarily being associated with improved access.

Explanatory variables

We used socio-demographic characteristics of dentists known to influence the intensity of medical activity for primary care physicians [19] including, age (continuous), sex, family status (living with a partner, number of dependent children), and being registered in a multi-professional care group in 2017.

In addition, we include in the study different characteristics of the area where the dentists are settled, related to the medical demographics and local demand characteristics. We approach the dental demography by including the density of dentists per 100 000 inhabitants in the area. This variable is categorized into quintiles to allow for balanced group comparisons and to capture potential non-linear effects. To characterize the needs in the area, we used data from the French National Institute of Statistics and Economics Studies (INSEE) and included age structure of the population (5 classes age-related) and the median income of the population in the area (continuous variable).

Results

In France in 2017, the 20 983 self-employed dentists were 48 years old on average (Table 1); 44% were women, 71% were in a couple, and 52.4% had a dependent child.

In 2017, on average, self-employed dentists performed 5 272 services a year (a1) and saw 880 different patients (a2). The average case mix of patients had the characteristics: 13.8% of patients were under 16 years of age, and 14.0% of patients had a chronic disease (LTC) (a4). Total annual dental fees (turnover) reached €225 772 (e1) The net yearly self-employed dental income was €92 874 (e2). The average bill per service was €39 (e4), including €22 of extra fees (e5) (Table 1).

The average density of dentists was 6.7 dentists per 100 000 inhabitants.

Only 201 dentists worked in MPCGs. This accounted for less than 1% of the 20 983 self-employed dentists in France in 2017. These dentists were slightly younger than the average, and women were slightly more numerous than their counterparts who did not work in MPCGs (Table 1). The density of dentists in the areas in which MPCG’s dentists were Based is lower than the national average, reaching only an average of 4.7 dentists per 100 000 inhabitants. Dentists practicing in MPCGs provided a larger total number of services (6 005 versus 5 260 for other dentists, a1) (Table 1). They also saw more patients in 2017 (1 036 versus 878 for other dentists, a2), including a higher number of children and more patients with long-term care needs. In terms of earnings variables, the average bill per service (e4) was €36 for dentists practicing in MPCGs (including 52% of extra fees) compared to €39 for dentists enrolled in another model of practice (solo or monodisciplinary group) (including 55.4% of extra fees). Total annual dental fees (turnover) (e1) for dentists enrolled in MPCGs were €245 908.

We show that the number of services provided (a1) is explained by both the gender of the dentists, as female dentists appear to provide 450 fewer services per year, and by having dependent children. The number of services carried out also strongly depends on the age of the dentists, since the oldest ones appear to provide substantially fewer services than their younger counterparts. Dentists with mixed activities (self-employed and salaried) lower their volume of self-employed activity (−555.4 services provided). The number of services per practitioner decreased gradually by nearly 1 000 annual services between dentists belonging to the lower and upper quintiles of the local density of dentists.

The number of patients seen at least once a year (a2) exhibits similar relationships with our explanatory variables as the number of services provided (a1). This is particularly apparent when focusing on the size of the patient base according to the density of dentists in the area under consideration: the higher the number of dentists per inhabitant (Q5 corresponding to the 20% of areas with the most dentists), the smaller the patient base. Indeed, dentists practicing in these areas (Q5) see an average of 260 fewer patients per year, given that the average number of patients seen at least once in 2017 by a dentist in France is 879 (see Table 1).

The number of LUT (a3) and patients with LTC (a4) all approximate the dentist’s provision of preventive care. Female dentists performed more LUT prevention assessments (+ 4.5) and saw fewer patients with chronic diseases (LTC). Older dentists appear to have seen fewer children but more patients with LTC (Table 2).

As with the number of procedures performed and patients seen, the density of dental practitioners appears to be negatively associated with our variables characterizing preventive care provision, exhibiting high variations. For instance, the provision of preventive dental checkups for children (LUT, a3) is particularly illustrative (−5.6 (Q2) to −18.9 (Q5) checkups compared to the Q1 quintile of less dense areas) (Table 2).

Determinants of earnings and income

The results are presented in Table 3. As expected, the total annual fees (e1) are strongly dependent on both the gender and the age of the practitioner: they were significantly lower among female dentists (-€44 055) than among male dentists. The relation with age is non-linear, exhibiting an inverse U-shape, with an increase during the early career, a maximum in the third age quartile (44–55 years old: €38 028 more than the youngest dentists category) and a decrease at the end of the career. Having at least a dependent child is also strongly associated with an increase in total annual fees by €11 306. This increase is less pronounced for women. Female dentists also raised fees when they had dependent children, but to a lesser extent than their male counterparts. Having a salaried activity in addition to a self-employed one (mixed activities) was associated with a lower total annual dental fee (e1).

Increases in dentist density were associated with a gradual decrease in annual dental fees. The strength of the relationship increases until the higher density quintile, characterizing areas where fees are on average €45 103 less than in the least dense areas.

Regarding the average bill per service (e3) and the average extra fees per service (e4), there was a continuous and positive association with age, up to the oldest age quartile, where the bill increased by up to €10.5 and the extra fees by up to €8.6. Women charged less fees per service than men (-€6.3 on average billed and -€4.8 on average of extra fees). Having children was associated with an increase in extra fees per service, but a decrease when the practitioner is a woman.

Relation between dentists’ activity and their belonging to an MPCG

Tables 2 and 3 both report the estimates of how practicing in an MPCG relates to dental care activity and billing. MPCG dentists provided, on average, a greater volume of activity than other dentists, whatever the dimension considered. For instance, considering (a1), MPCG dentists provided 367 additional services per year corresponding to an additional activity of 5%; beside, they saw more patients (a2), 71 additional patients corresponding to almost + 7% activity, and provided more preventive care, particularly targeted at children, such as the number of LUT preventive dental check-ups (+ 5.8 per year corresponding to nearly 15%)) and finally, they also received more than 12 additional LTC patients per year (+ 8.7%) (Table 2).

Notably, these specificities in the intensity of dental activity for dentists practicing in an MPCG do not really translate into total income and extra fees. In fact, our dimensions of total income from activities studied were not statistically different for dentists practicing in MPCGs compared with other dentists. Besides, MPCG dentists offered better financial access thanks to significantly lower prices with a bill of -€2 per service and additional fees of -€1.7 per service (Table 3).

Discussion

Our results described the determinants of self-employed French dentists’ activities in 2017, particularly for dentists practicing in an MPCG. This work presents a comprehensive analyze on the key characteristics of the activity and incomes of French dental surgeons, a topic that has been lacking in the literature until now.

We found that, for dentists, well-known relations evidenced for general practitioners or more generally for doctors: being a female doctor and having a dependent child are associated with lower turnover, income, and a lower volume of activity [20, 21]. An increase in the density of dentists is related to a decrease in the volume of dental activity per dentist and total dental fees. We also evidenced a positive association between practice in a multiprofessional primary care group and the provision of dental care and oral health prevention.

Individual determinants

We show that female dentists produced fewer dental procedures than men and at a lower price. This result aligns with the existing literature on general practitioners, which indicates that female physicians tend to perform fewer technical procedures and spend more time with each patient [22]. It results in lower earnings compared to their male counterparts. Moreover, women are more likely than men to use ‘compassionate pricing’ for identical services [23], which could explain part of the lower fees, as self-confidence issues about the value of their work are observed in the larger literature on gender differences in personal entitlement at work [24]. The association between having a dependent child and an increase in annual fees can be explained by the need to finance costs associated with raising a child. Leisure preferences are another explanation, suggesting that renouncing family time had to be worthwhile [25]. These findings may help us better understand the activity choices of female dentists in a context of rapid feminization of the profession [10].

We found that older dentists appeared to see fewer children but more patients with long-term conditions (LTC). This is likely because dentists and their patients are ageing together, but it could also be because their experience makes them more comfortable treating patients with complex medical backgrounds.

How does it work in underserved areas?

The results showed that dentists’ incomes and fees were higher in underendowed areas. It comes with a high number of services and patients. This likely implies a greater working time for dentists practicing in these areas, which could offset the attractiveness of the higher incomes. The increased workload can be explained by the shortage of colleagues with whom to share the demand for dental treatment. Furthermore, the rationing of demand may lead to postponed dental consultations due to the limited availability of care providers, thereby contributing to a deterioration in oral health during the waiting period [26].

More than the effects of competition among dentists, local demand drives dentists’ incomes. Indeed, our results suggest that the local standard of living, rather than the density of dentists, has a greater impact on the fees for procedures. Higher average fees per service could reflect a specific behavior in price setting and/or a higher level of complexity in the services provided. It could also be due to a patient’s higher willingness to pay, as wealthier patients have greater care demands for equal needs and are more able to pay for aesthetic and preventive services [27, 28].

Activity of MPCG dentists

Given the specificities of the dental profession and particularly the scale of their technical requirements, dentists would find it less easy to find their place in pluriprofessional teams than, for instance, general practitioners and paramedical professionals. However, we observed that in 2021, almost a fourth of multiprofessional primary care groups included one or more dentists. We have also observed that these pioneering dentists tend to favor preventive practices. The limited representativeness of this group (less than 1% of the sample) necessitates cautious interpretation.

Indeed, these practitioners conducted more dental assessments for children, and they saw a greater number of children and patients with long-term illnesses. This positioning of activity could be related to a selection bias, whereby practitioners who set up practice in an MPCG could also be attracted to prevention [28]. It could also be favored by work in an MPCG, particularly concerning the care of chronically ill patients, as previously observed, which is facilitated by interprofessional groups [29]. The characteristic of local demand is also associated with patient case mix while MPCGs are mainly located in rural, underendowed areas with a low median income. However, the analyses were adjusted for these criteria. We observed, therefore, that practitioners in MPCGs tend to see more patients and perform more services than their counterparts. The average bill per service by MPCG dentists was lower, as were the average extra fees per service. MPCG dentists’ net incomes appear not to be affected, so amortizing a technical platform does not seem to be a greater problem in these practices than elsewhere. We lack information concerning these costs, which would be interesting to examine, as they constitute an important dimension of dentists’ activities and choice of location.

Strengths and limitations

This study relies on a comprehensive medico-economic database, with tax data extractions allowing for highly precise information on each dentist’s household. It is a rare and valuable resource, which enabled us to conduct a detailed assessment of dentists’ activity, income, and their underlying determinants.

Regarding the activity in MPCGs, we examined their specific characteristics but were unable to avoid a substantial selection bias due to the dentist’s characteristics in MPCGs. Indeed, the MPCGs are primarily located in rural and underserved areas, resulting in targeted activities tailored to local needs. It is not possible to separate the effect of dentists’ preferences—those who chose to join a multiprofessional primary care group (MPCG) likely have specific motivational profiles—from the effect of working within an MPCG. Nevertheless, whether it stems from the particular characteristics of these profiles or from the features of areas conducive to the establishment of MPCGs, and despite the relatively small number of dentists practicing in a MPCG, we show that these practitioners provided, on average, a greater volume of activity than other dentists, whatever the dimension considered (services per year corresponding, more patients seen and provide more preventive care). When it comes to the higher level of activity, we were unable to determine whether it is associated with an increase in demand for primary or emergency care. If this is the case, it would signal strong tension in the response to local demand. This tension could require increased working hours and have an impact on the quality of life of health of professionals, two criteria that we were unable to assess in this study.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the economic and professional characteristics of self-employed dentists in France. Income and activity levels were strongly associated with individual factors such as gender, age, and family situation, as well as local dental supply. Participation in multiprofessional primary care groups (MPCGs) was linked to higher preventive activity and lower fees per service, although this model remain. The enrollment of dentists in MPCGs appears favorable in terms of access to care and quality of care. The question is whether others will follow these pioneering practitioners and whether those to come will have the same profile. These issues justify a follow-up study of new generations of dentists joining MPCGs, as the results of our research are promising, suggesting improved oral healthcare and better financial access to dental care in these groups.

These findings highlight persistent heterogeneity in dental practice, particularly in terms of gender and geographic location. They also suggest that new organizational models, such as MPCGs, may contribute to more accessible and prevention-oriented dental care.

Further research is needed to assess the long-term impact of these structures on patient outcomes and health equity. Policy efforts could explore how financial or professional incentives might encourage greater dentist participation in multiprofessional settings, especially in underserved areas.
