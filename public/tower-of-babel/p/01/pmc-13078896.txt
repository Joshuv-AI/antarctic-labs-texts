Defective endomembrane dynamics in Rab27a deficiency impairs nucleic acid sensing and cytokine secretion in immune cells
Authors: Juan Yu, Elsa Meneses-Salas, Jennifer L. Johnson, Susanna Manenti, Mouad Ait Kbaich, Danni Chen, Kasra Askari, Jing He, Aparna Shukla, Binchu Shaji, Rosana Gonzalez-Quintial, Ben A. Croker, Jinzhong Zhang, Hal Hoffman, William B. Kiosses, Catherine Hedrick, Kersi Pestonjamasp, Nathan Wineinger, Roberto Baccala, Sergio D. Catz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYEndosomal Toll-like receptors (eTLRs) are essential for the sensing of non-self through RNA and DNA detection. Here, using spatiotemporal analysis of vesicular dynamics, super-resolution microscopy studies, and functional assays, we show that endomembrane defects associated ith the deficiency of the small GTPase Rab27a cause delayed eTLR ligand recognition, defective early signaling, and impaired cytokine secretion. Rab27a-deficient neutrophils show retention of eTLRs in amphisomes and impaired ligand internalization. Extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling and β2-integrin upregulation, early responses to TLR7 and TLR9 ligands, are defective in Rab27a deficiency. CpG-stimulated Rab27a-deficient neutrophils present increased tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) secretion and decreased secretion of a selected group of mediators, including interleukin (IL)-10. In vivo, CpG-challenged Rab27a-null mice show decreased production of type I interferons (IFNs) and IFN-γ, and the IFN-α secretion defect is confirmed in Rab27a-null plasmacytoid dendritic cells. Our findings have significant implications for immunodeficiency, inflammation, and CpG adjuvant vaccination.

INTRODUCTION

Pathogen-derived DNA and RNA sensing is an essential mechanism for the recognition of non-self and, while impairment in this mechanism leads to infections, exacerbated activation is associated with autoimmunity.1–3 Endosomal Toll-like receptors (eTLRs) play an essential role in sensing and initiating the response to pathogenic oligonucleotides. eTLRs traffic from the endoplasmic reticulum to endolysosomal compartments where they interact with pathogenic-derived oligonucleotide molecular patterns, initiating an innate immune response. While cell-specific differences have been observed in eTLR maturation kinetics and stability,4 the mechanisms by which eTLRs and their ligands traffic and converge in the endolysosomes to initiate signaling are still incompletely understood.

The control of the subcellular localization of eTLRs, essential for accurate signaling, is regulated by vesicular trafficking mechanisms. The accurate transport of eTLRs from their site of synthesis to the site of ligand recognition, endosomal acidification, and eTLR processing are all required for effective oligonucleotide sensing and signaling.5 Several vesicular transport molecules and chaperones help orchestrate the trafficking and processing of eTLRs, although the mechanisms are cell-specific and require further elucidation. In plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and macrophages, eTLRs undergo maturation and stabilization in the endoplasmic reticulum, a process facilitated by the chaperone UNC93B1.6 UNC93B1 deficiency results in decreased expression of TLR proteins, suggesting that the chaperone is important for TLR stability.7 In pDCs, SLC15A4 was recently shown to be required for the trafficking and localization of nucleic acid-sensing TLRs, a process aided by Adaptor Protein-3 (AP3) and necessary for type I IFN production.8

The regulation of eTLR function is characterized by the bifurcation of the signaling mechanisms leading to the activation of independent downstream pathways, which in pDCs are constituted by the IRF7/type I interferon (IFN) response and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The TLR9-signaling bifurcation in pDCs is associated with two cellular features: (1) the time required for the ligand, CpG-containing unmethylated oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG-ODNs), to traffic through the endocytic pathway; and (2) the molecular composition of the TLR9+ compartment. The chemical characteristics of the CpG define their ability to transit through the endocytic pathway at different rates leading to selective secretion of cytokines or type I IFN.9 In pDCs, the retention of CpG-A at early endosomes activates the IRF-7/type I IFN axis, while rapid transit of CpG-B to the endolysosomes leads to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines.9 As for the TLR9+ compartment composition, the traffic of TLR9 to a specialized lysosomal compartment was proposed to favor bifurcation toward the type I IFN branch in pDCs. This process is regulated by AP3, and thus the production of IFN-α but not pro-inflammatory cytokines was found to be defective in AP3 deficiency,10 although others showed decreases of both IFN-α and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in a similar model.11 Finally, in pDCs activated by immune complexes, the bifurcation of the TLR9 signaling is linked to LC3-associated phagocytosis (LAP), in which LC3 recruitment to phagosomes correlates with increased production of IFN-α but not inflammatory cytokines.12

Neutrophils, the most abundant leukocytes in circulation, play a central role in the regulation of immunity. Both human and mouse neutrophils express eTLRs, but many questions regarding the mechanisms regulating the activation of eTLRs in neutrophils remain unanswered. In neutrophils, TLR9 activation requires endolysosomal fusion, a process regulated by the tethering factor Munc13–4 and its interacting soluble N-ethyl maleimide sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) protein syntaxin 7 (STX7).13 Although the fusion of late endosomes (LEs) with lysosomes is Munc13–4 dependent,13 how neutrophils regulate the trafficking of eTLR carrier vesicles and their fusion with endosomes need further elucidation, and the trafficking regulators involved in this process remain elusive. Similar to B cells, pDCs, and macrophages,14 a pool of TLR9 was proposed to reside at the plasma membrane in neutrophils15; however, whether this TLR9 plasma membrane pool is trafficked directly from the biosynthetic pathways or through endolysosomal fusion with the plasma membrane is not well understood. Finally, neutrophils produce a large array of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that help modulate the immune response but also mediate inflammation.16 Neutrophil priming leads to a rapid response to CpG leading to transient extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling activation.13 However, whether this process is linked to the modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines by TLR9 activation is currently unknown, and the molecular mechanisms involved in the putative bifurcation of the TLR9 signaling in neutrophils remain elusive.

Munc13–4 was identified as a regulator of cytosolic granule fusion17 and as an effector of the small GTPase Rab27a.18,19 Together, these two molecules control exocytic events in several immune cells, including neutrophils20,21 and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs).19 Deficiency of either Munc13–4 or Rab27a causes immunodeficiencies in humans (i.e., familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis type 3 [FHL3]17 and Griscelli syndrome type 2 [GS2],22,23 respectively). Patients with FHL3 or GS2 have increased susceptibility to bacterial and viral infections, which are generally attributed to decreased natural killer cell and CTL cytotoxicity. Despite the mutual dependency of Munc13–4 and Rab27a in the process of exocytosis, Munc13–4 is promiscuous and can exert Rab27a-independent functions. These include the regulation of CXCR2 recycling through Rab1124 and endolysosomal maturation required for TLR9 activation.13 Thus, Munc13–4-syntaxin 7 calcium-dependent binding is essential for LE maturation and endocytic TLR9 signaling in neutrophils.13 Importantly, whether Rab27a is also required for eTLR signaling regulation is currently unknown. Furthermore, although infections in Rab27a deficiency are attributed to defective exocytosis, a possible role of Rab27a in pathogen-derived nucleic acid sensing has not been addressed.

Here, we study the regulation of eTLR trafficking and signaling in neutrophils. We show that the small GTPase Rab27a is necessary for endomembrane regulation, and the absence of Rab27a causes defects in receptor compartmentalization, ligand internalization, and early signaling responses. We also show that Rab27a deficiency leads to a bifurcation of cytokine production in neutrophils. Furthermore, we identified Rab27a as a mediator of interleukin (IL)-10 secretion in neutrophils and type I IFN secretion in pDCs. We propose that this small GTPase regulates the maintenance of membrane dynamics and homeostasis, necessary for the correct trafficking of CpG, for maintaining the composition of the eTLR+ endolysosomal compartment in neutrophils, and for cytokine secretion in immune cells.

RESULTS

Rab27aash/ash neutrophils have altered endosomal maturation and impaired eTLR dynamics

Inhibition of late endosome-lysosome fusion in neutrophils leads to an enlarged LE phenotype13 and impaired TLR9 signaling.13 To test whether Rab27a regulates neutrophil endosomal dynamics, we analyzed the morphology of the endolysosomal compartment by quantitative pseudo-total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (pseudo-TIRFM) in neutrophils expressing EGFP-LAMP1.13 Using pure, isolated BM-derived neutrophils (Figure S1A), we show that Rab27a-null neutrophils (Rab27aash/ash; Figure 1A) have enlarged endosomal compartments (Figure 1B). Quantification of the diameter of LAMP1-positive vesicles shows a ~30% increase in the size of endolysosomes in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils compared to wild-type (WT) cells (Figure 1C). These results recapitulate the phenotypes caused by Munc13–4 deficiency or chloroquine (CQ) treatment13 (Figures 1B and 1C), a drug that alkalinizes the lysosome and interrupts the fusion of LE with lysosomes (Lys), suggesting that Rab27a is necessary for LE maturation.

eTLRs are known to traffic from the endoplasmic reticulum through the secretory pathway and further through VAMP3+ specialized intermediate compartments, via AP3; however, mature neutrophils are terminally differentiated cells believed to have limited biosynthetic activity under non-inflammatory conditions, and whether this trafficking mechanism is similar in mature neutrophils has not been elucidated. To analyze eTLR dynamics and localization, we performed live-cell studies in primary mature neutrophils expressing YFP-TLR9 and determined the spatiotemporal behavior of this receptor related to the acidic endolysosomal compartment. Cells were analyzed by pseudo-TIRFM to visualize the trafficking of molecules with a depth of >300 nm in attached neutrophils. We found that exogenously expressed TLR9 localizes entirely at dynamic acidic endolysosomes in both WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure 1D). Analysis of vesicular dynamics shows that TLR9 trafficking is defective in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils, which presented significantly increased numbers of TLR9+ compartments that were either not motile or move at very low speed (percentage of TLR9+ vesicles at <0.01 mm/s: wild type, 8.38% ± 2.26% vs. Rab27aash/ash 14.20% ± 2.38%, n = 15 per group) (Figure 1E; Videos S1 and S2).

TLR9 accumulates at intracellular storage compartments in Rab27a-null neutrophils

Neutrophils were shown to increase the localization of TLR9 at the plasma membrane upon stimulation.15 To analyze whether the impaired dynamics of the TLR9 compartment correlated with its apical movement, and to establish whether the TLR9+ compartment was detectable at or in the proximity of the plasma membrane (PM), we calibrated the detection of endogenous TLR9 by TIRFM (Figures 1F and 1G). Images acquired at 100-nm depth, corresponding to the PM-glass interface, show CD11a, which is constitutively expressed at the PM in neutrophils, as puncta with homogeneous distribution in both WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure 1G). At 250-nm depth, CD11a appears peripheral at the border of the cell, confirming that these images represent a layer below the interface formed by the PM and the coverslip. The ring appearance of CD11a in cells imaged at 250-nm depth shows that it is localized at the PM. Contrarily, TLR9 was scarcely detectable at 100-nm depth in both WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure 1G). Furthermore, at 250-nm depth, TLR9 was detectable in clusters but not in the periphery (no ring formation), again indicating that most endogenous TLR9, similar to YFP-TLR9 (Figure 1D), occupies an area proximal to, but distinct from, the PM. This is supported by quantitative analysis showing that the amount of TLR9 detected in this internal pool (250 nm) was significantly increased in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils stimulated with CpG under primed (granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor [GM-CSF]) conditions compared to WT neutrophils under the same experimental conditions (Figures 1H and 1I), suggesting a possible retention of TLR9 at intracellular compartments in Rab27a deficiency. The enlarged endosomes, the altered eTLR dynamics, and the impaired apical movement in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils suggested defective endolysosomal flux and TLR trafficking, highlighting a possible role of Rab27a in eTLR signaling.

TLR9 and TLR7 are retained in LAMP1+ LC3B+ amphisomes in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils

Because Rab27aash/ash cells present defective late endosomal morphology, we next explored the composition of the TLR9+ compartment in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils. Previous studies showed that pDCs undergoing phagocytosis of beads coated with DNA12 or transfected with vesicles containing CpG-A25 recruit LC3B (microtubule-associated proteins 1A/1B-light chain 3) to the TLR9+ compartment, a process proposed to be necessary for TLR9 signaling upstream of type I IFN (IFN) production. Here, to study the possible association of endosomal TLR9 with an LC3B+ compartment, we analyzed the subcellular localization of endogenous TLR9 by quantitative immunofluorescence analysis in unstimulated and CpG-B-stimulated neutrophils. Rab27aash/ash neutrophils presented significantly increased colocalization of TLR9 with the late endosomal marker LAMP1 compared to WT cells, and high levels of LC3B at TLR9+ vesicles (Figures 2A–2C). To verify that TLR9 was indeed present at LC3+ LEs, we next performed stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) super-resolution microscopy. We showed the formation of tripartite molecular clusters of TLR9, LAMP1, and LC3B in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure 2D). Experiments performed by transfecting Rab27aash/ash primary neutrophils with the expression vector EGFP-Rab27a show that reconstitution of Rab27a expression rescues the TLR9-localization-defective phenotype (Figure 2E, quantified in Figure 2F). Altogether, our data suggest that, in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils, TLR9 is partially retained in amphisome-like structures (i.e., hybrid organelles produced through the fusion of endosomes with LC3B+ compartments).26

To study whether the amphisome-retention phenotype applied to other endosomal TLRs, we analyzed the subcellular localization of endogenous TLR7 in WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils. Like TLR9, the TLR7+ compartment of Rab27a-deficient neutrophils also presented an increased accumulation of LC3BII in amphisomes (Figures 2G–2I). The LAMP1- and LC3BII-associated pools of TLR7 decreased after treatment with the CL097 analog R848 in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils; however, the presence of TLR7 at LC3BII/LAMP1+ puncta in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils remained significantly increased even after TLR7 stimulation. TLR7 presence in a tripartite complex with LAMP1 and LC3B was also confirmed by super-resolution microscopy (Figure 2J).

Impaired early signaling activation in response to eTLR ligands in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils

Next, we tested whether the enlarged endolysosomal phenotype in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils correlates with defects in eTLRs activation, which depends on normal endosomal flux. First, we analyzed the effect of Rab27a deficiency on TLR9-dependent ERK signaling. CpG-dependent stimulation of ERK signaling in neutrophils is rapid and transient and is amplified by priming with GM-CSF.13 Here, we show that TLR9-dependent stimulation of ERK-mediated signaling is impaired in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils (Figure 3A). In WT cells, ERK phosphorylation peaked at 10 min and remained elevated at 20 and 30 min after CpG stimulation. Contrarily, ERK signaling was decreased in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils, thus supporting an association between Rab27a function and early CpG-mediated activation. This was not caused by putative differences in endogenous TLR9 expression, which was similar in WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure S1B). To study whether the defects observed in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils extend to other eTLRs, we stimulated cells with CL097, a small-molecule derivative of the imidazoquinoline R848 known to induce TLR7 responses in human and murine cells.27 Rab27a-deficient neutrophils displayed an impaired response to the TLR7/8 ligand (Figure 3B), further supporting a role for Rab27a in the early response to endosomal TLR activation. Next, we analyzed nuclear factor kB (NF-kB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), STAT1, and TBK1 signaling pathways as they are also associated with TLR receptor-ligand binding. Here, we monitored the phosphorylated state of ERK, p38, p65, STAT1, and TBK1 at 10 min (Figure 3C) and 2, 6, and 16 h (Figures S1C and S1D) after CpG stimulation under primed or unprimed conditions. ERK phosphorylation, again, was significantly impaired in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils at 10 min (Figure 3C). A marked activation of pSTAT was observed in both WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils in response to GM-CSF, confirming no defects in GM-CSF response in Rab27a deficiency. The effect was independent of CpG. We did not detect differential engagement of NF-kB, MAPK, or TBK1 at this time point (Figure 3C). Also, we did not detect engagement of the ERK signaling pathway at late time points (Figures S1C and S1D). p65 phosphorylation was also unaltered at 2, 6, or 16 h. Increased phosphorylation of STAT1 and TBK1 initiated by CpG was detected at 6 and 16 h in both WT and Rab27a-deficient cells (Figures S1C and S1D).

The upregulation of β2 integrins induced by eTLR ligands is defective in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils

To analyze the functional implications of the defective early response to eTLR activation in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils, we studied the β2-integrin subunit alpha M (ITGAM, CD11b). Mobilization of CD11b to the plasma membrane from intracellular stores is largely independent of Rab27a if cells are activated with non-eTLR ligands, such as the phorbol ester Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA).28 Here, we show that CD11b mobilization is impaired in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils stimulated with CpG-B (Figure 3D). The CpG-B-induced CD11b upregulation after GM-CSF priming was also significantly decreased in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure 3D). We found similar defects in CD11b surface upregulation in Rab27a-deficient primed neutrophils when treated with CL097, highlighting again a role for Rab27a not only in TLR9 but also in TLR7 activation (Figure 3E). CD11b mobilization was not affected in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils stimulated with PMA (Figure 3F). Next, because Rab27a interacts with the Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein (WASP) homolog WASH, which also localizes at LEs and counteracts Rab27a function, we studied the effect of WASH deficiency on CpG-stimulated β2-integrin mobilization.29 In Figure 3G, we show that primed neutrophils lacking WASH expression (Wash-cKO)29 have an exacerbated response to CpG compared to WT cells. The effect was specific for CpG because Wash-cKO neutrophils primed with GM-CSF and subsequently stimulated with the bacteria-derived mimetic peptide N-Formyl-Met-Leu-Phe (fMLF) (Figure 3G) or stimulated with PMA29 have normal CD11b mobilization. Altogether, these data support that Rab27a is necessary for the rapid response to ligands that initiate endosomal TLR activation manifested as the mobilization of integrins from intracellular stores, an important neutrophil innate immune response, suggesting that defects in the early response to eTLR ligands have deleterious implications for neutrophil function.

Delayed internalization highlights a Rab27a-dependent defect in CpG presentation

To test whether a putative impairment in CpG transport could be responsible for the defective early ERK signaling and integrin mobilization phenotypes, we monitored the dynamics of CpG trafficking. We show that Rab27aash/ash neutrophils present delayed incorporation of CpG. CpG trafficking was significantly decreased at early time points (10 and 30 min) in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils (Figures 3H and 3I). At 6 h, CpG was detected in intracellular compartments at similar levels to those observed in WT cells (Figures 3H, 3I, and S2). Our data indicate that efficient CpG internalization requires Rab27a. We also observed an increased accumulation of eTLRs at LAMP1+ endosomes in Rab27aash/ash unstimulated neutrophils, a phenotype rescued by Rab27a expression (Figures 2E and 2F). Therefore, it is possible that defective CpG trafficking and eTLR retention in amphisomes both negatively impact eTLR ligand recognition and downstream signaling in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils. Altogether, our data highlight that the absence of Rab27a causes defects in endomembrane dynamics delaying nucleic acid sensing.

CpG-induced cytokine and chemokine secretion in neutrophils is a late response that requires endosomal acidification

Different from ERK signaling and CD11b mobilization, which denote early events of the neutrophil response to CpG, cytokine production is a late event triggered by the TLR9 agonist in these cells. Thus, while cytokine production and secretion were detected at either 6 or 16 h post CpG stimulation, most of these mediators were virtually undetectable in the secretory milieu at time points up to 2.5 h (shown below). Because previous studies support that TLR9 can signal from the PM, but we detected TLR9 exclusively in intracellular acidic organelles (Figures 1F–1I), we next examined whether cytokine production required endolysosomal acidification. Here, we analyzed the ability of neutrophils to produce chemokines and cytokines in response to TLR9 stimulation in the presence of the lysosomotropic alkalinizing drug, CQ. The pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 were produced by neutrophils in response to CpG-B while IFN-γ production was negligible (Figure 4A). Treatment with CQ inhibited both TNF-α and IL-6 production (Figure 4A). The chemokines MIP1α, MIP1β, and RANTES were also induced and secreted by neutrophils treated with CpG for 6 or 16 h and were all inhibited by CQ (Figure 4B). The V-ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) also inhibited the cytokine production in response to CpG stimulation (Figure S3). Neither CQ nor BafA1 treatment significantly affected neutrophil viability (Figures S4A and S4B). These data indicate that endolysosomal acidification is necessary for the late response to TLR9 stimulation in neutrophils.

Rab27a differentially regulates cytokine production and secretion in neutrophils

Rab27a-deficient neutrophils showed impaired endolysosomal trafficking and defective early signaling in response to CpG (Figures 1B, 1C, and 3A). However, efficient cytokine production in response to TLR9 activation is a late signaling event. Furthermore, the activation of TLR9 at specific subcellular compartments defines the bifurcated production of type I IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines in pDCs, in which LC3BII recruitment plays an important role. Because Rab27a-deficient neutrophils presented retention of eTLRs at an amphisome compartment, we next studied the possible role of Rab27a in cytokine and chemokine production by neutrophils. The production of three pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, TNF-α, RANTES, and monokine induced by gamma interferon (MIG, CXCL9), was significantly increased in Rab27a-null neutrophils stimulated with CpG-B (Figure 4C). Other pro-inflammatory mediators presented with production levels similar to those observed in WT cells, including IL-6 and keratinocyte-derived cytokine (KC, CXCL1), while IFN-γ production was negligible in both WT and Rab27a-deficient neutrophils (Figure 4D). Importantly, a third group of cytokines and chemokines including G-CSF, MIP1α, and MIP2 showed significantly impaired production/secretion in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils stimulated with CpG (Figure 4E). Although the production of inflammatory mediators in response to CpG presented different kinetics, with TNF-α, IL-6, and MIP1β showing increased levels at 6 h and MIP1α and RANTES showing larger increases at 16 h, these mediators were undetectable or minimally induced at 2.5 h after CpG-B treatment (Figures 4C–4E). Neutrophil viability was not affected by CpG or GM-CSF treatment at 6 or 16 h (Figures S4C and S4D). These data highlight divergent late responses to CpG and a requirement for Rab27a to produce specific inflammatory mediators in neutrophils.

Discrete but significant transcriptional differences characterize CpG-stimulated Rab27a-deficient and WT neutrophils

To analyze whether differential gene expression could help explain the bifurcation CpG response at the cytokine or the signaling level, we analyzed global gene expression by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE272416). In response to 6-h CpG stimulation, we found significant gene expression upregulation in both WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figures 5A and 5B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis identified similarities in transcriptional responses of genes associated with several cellular functions and signaling pathways (Figures 5C and 5D); however, discrete significant differences in gene expression between WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils were also evident. Thus, we detected significantly increased levels of Tnfa and Ccl5 in Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure 5E), supporting that discrete transcriptional differences may help explain the increased TNF-α and RANTES secretion observed in Rab27a-deficient cells. Genes associated with ERK, NF-kB, and MAPK signaling pathways showed similar profiles between WT and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils (Figure S5). Despite these similarities, 99 genes showed significant differences between WT and Rab27a-deficient cells exclusively in the CpG-stimulated conditions (Figure 5F; Table S1). Several of these genes are known transcription regulators, suggesting that discrete transcriptional regulatory processes may help explain some of the differences observed in cytokine production between WT and Rab27a-deficient cells. Contrarily, decreased cytokine secretion in the selected group shown in Figure 4E was not explained by differences in transcriptional regulation as Ccl3 (MIP1α), Cxcl2 (MIP2), and Csf3 (G-CSF) mRNA induction after CpG treatment was similar in both neutrophil groups (Figure 5E).

Secretion of IL-10 requires Rab27a

Different from human neutrophils, in which an inactive chromatin configuration at the Il10 locus prevents them from producing IL-1030 unless stimulated with specific agonists including LPS-stimulated Treg cells,31 stimulated murine neutrophils produce and secrete the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. However, whether CpG induces IL-10 secretion and the molecular mechanisms regulating this process are currently unknown. Here, we show that Il10 mRNA is upregulated in neutrophils when stimulated with CpG-B for 6 h (Figure 6A) independently of Rab27a expression. Next, we asked whether IL-10 protein production requires Rab27a. To test this, we stimulated neutrophils with CpG and analyzed the accumulation of intracellular IL-10 in permeabilized cells. We show that production of IL-10 was significantly induced by CpG not only in WT but also in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils, albeit mildly decreased (Figure 6B). Next, we analyze IL-10 secretion in supernatants of CpG-treated and -untreated cells under identical experimental conditions. We show that, despite significant Il10 transcriptional induction and IL-10 production, IL-10 secretion was ablated in Rab27a-deficient cells (Figure 6C), thus highlighting a function for Rab27a in the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10.

IFN-α secretion is impaired in Rab27a-deficient pDCs

pDCs are the main producers of type I IFN in response to TLR9 activation.32 Since type I IFNs play a fundamental role in the elimination of viral infections, which are prevalent and recurrent in patients with Griscelli syndrome, and Rab27a was necessary for the secretion of a subset of cytokines in neutrophils, we next studied IFN-α production and secretion by bone-marrow-derived Rab27a-deficient (CD11chi, CD11blow, B220hi) pDCs. pDCs were stimulated with CpG-A for 16 h or left untreated. We show that CpG-A treatment induces increased endogenous IFN-α at intracellular vesicular compartments in both WT and Rab27aash/ash pDCs (Figures 7A and 7B). AP3 binds to TLR9 and controls its trafficking, a requirement for IFN-α production.10 Here, we used super-resolution microscopy to analyze the formation of TLR9-AP3 complexes in WT and Rab27aash/ash pDCs. We show that the association of TLR9 with AP3 is not markedly affected in Rab27a-deficient pDCs under basal or stimulated conditions, despite a mild decrease of the association between TLR9 and AP3 in Rab27aash/ash pDCs observed after CpG treatment (Figures 7C and 7D). Next, we analyzed IFN-α secretion in supernatants of WT and Rab27aash/ash pDCs treated with either CpG-A or CpG-B (as negative control). We found significantly decreased extracellular IFN-α in Rab27aash/ash pDCs, suggesting a defective secretory phenotype in response to CpG-A (Figure 7E). As expected, neither WT nor Rab27aash/ash pDCs produced IFN-α in response to CpG-B. These results indicate a role for Rab27a in the secretion of IFN-α and define this GTPase as an important regulator of cytokine secretion not only in neutrophils but also in pDCs.

CpG-induced inflammation is decreased in Rab27a-deficient mice and is CpG type specific

To directly address the importance of Rab27a in CpG-mediated TLR9 activation in vivo, we treated WT and Rab27aash/ash mice with CpG-A or CpG-B and analyzed IFN-α in plasma 6 h post insult. Rab27a-deficient mice showed significantly lower levels of IFN-α (Figure 7F), indicating that Rab27a deficiency interferes with type I IFN response to TLR9 activation in vivo. IFN-γ production followed the pattern observed for IFN-α, with significantly lower levels being produced in Rab27a-deficient mice treated with CpG-A or CpG-B (Figure 7G). TNF-α levels in plasma were significantly reduced in Rab27a-deficient mice when challenged with CpG-A (Figure 7H), reflecting the specificity of CpG-A for non-myeloid cells, but were similar to those observed in WT mice when challenged with CpG-B, most likely explained by the production of TNF-α by myeloid cells, and, in particular, neutrophils, in response to CpG-B. The production of IL-6 was indistinguishable from WT levels (Figure 7I) and recapitulates the results observed using isolated neutrophils. Our results highlight CpG type-specific mechanisms and highlight the involvement of Rab27a in the secretion of type I IFN and pro-inflammatory cytokines downstream of TLR9 activation in vivo.

DISCUSSION

In this work, we present evidence that endosomal dynamics and composition are regulated by the small GTPase Rab27a to control eTLR ligand internalization and the composition of the eTLR compartment. Rab27a-deficient neutrophils showed impairment of the early eTLR signaling response but mostly normal late signaling and only selective differences in transcriptional responses. Rab27a deficiency caused the bifurcation of the late cytokine response that involves increased TNF-α but impaired IL-10 secretion in neutrophils as well as impaired IFN-α secretion by pDCs in response to eTLR ligands. These newly identified regulatory mechanisms have implications for immunity, inflammatory disease, and vaccine-adjuvant development.

We show that TLR7 and TLR9 localize at amphisome-like acidic organelles in neutrophils. Characterization of the TLR+ endosomal compartments in Rab27a-deficient cells denotes increased levels of LAMP1 and LC3BII, and impaired motility, thus highlighting both altered composition and dynamics of the eTLR compartment. The altered-composition phenotype was rescued upon reconstitution of Rab27a expression, supporting its role in the regulation of vesicular trafficking mechanisms necessary to maintain the composition of the eTLR compartment. Increased retention of eTLR in amphisomes and impaired eTLR ligand trafficking in Rab27a-null cells correlated with decreased early ERK signaling and decreased mobilization of β2-integrins. Furthermore, deficiency of WASH, an actin nucleator that counteracts Rab27a function,29 leads to the exacerbated mobilization of CD11b in response to TLR9 ligands. Restriction of TLR9 response by actin nucleation was attributed to increased actin assembly on endosomes in pDCs.33,34 Based on its dual function as an actin nucleator and negative regulator of Rab27a,29 it is likely that WASH negatively regulates eTLR activation by both increasing actin assembly at TLR9+ endosomes and counteracting Rab27a-dependent trafficking.

Previous studies support the requirement of compartment-specific signaling hubs in the production of type I IFNs or pro-inflammatory cytokines in pDCs. Differences in the ability of CpG-A and CpG-B to induce type I IFN account for the time spent by each CpG oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG-ODN) at these different compartments of the endocytic pathway,9 and genetic alterations that accelerate the trafficking of CpG to the endolysosomal compartment are associated with the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.33 We found that Rab27a-deficient neutrophils present a bifurcation of the cytokine/chemokine production during the late response to CpG, at which point the delivery of CpG into TLR9+ endosomes is normal. The production of TNF-α, RANTES, and MIG was exacerbated in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils stimulated with CpG but they did not respond to GM-CSF, denoting TLR9-specific activation. Given that TNF-α production is manifested at 6 h and that ERK signaling, which takes place minutes after CpG stimulation, is defective in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils, our data indicate a dissociation of the late pro-inflammatory response from the early ERK signaling pathway. While the defective early response to CpG in Rab27a deficiency is explained by impaired CpG internalization and by the retention of TLR9 in amphisomes, the delivery of CpG into specific eTLR+ compartments was normal at late time points. Late signaling in response to CpG did not engage ERK but triggered STAT1 instead in both WT and Rab27a-deficient cells. This may explain why cytokine induction, a late response, was not defective in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils.

The secretion of a selective group of cytokines and chemokines, including G-CSF, MIP1α, and MIP2, showed a clear dependency on Rab27a. Secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was also abrogated in the absence of Rab27a expression. The observation that transcriptional regulation and protein expression of IL-10 were not affected in Rab27a deficiency indicates a role for Rab27a in the secretion of this cytokine, and similar effects were observed for G-CSF and MIP2. Defects in the secretion of these mediators by Rab27a-null neutrophils were manifested when cells were stimulated with CpG-B but also in response to GM-CSF stimulation (not shown), highlighting a dual role of Rab27a in the regulation of endosomal maturation that defines the nature of the eTLR compartment differentially affecting ERK signaling but also a function in the secretory pathway of a defined group of mediators (Figure S6). While CpG-B is associated with the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines in myeloid cells, including neutrophils, as described here, and B cell activation,35,36 CpG-A induces strong pDC activation, type I IFN production,37 and antigen-presenting cell (APC) cell maturation. Here, we show that the production of intracellular IFN-α in BM-derived pDCs in response to CpG-A is not affected by Rab27a deficiency. Instead, we show that IFN-α secretion is a Rab27a-dependent process. IFN-α production after in vivo challenge with CpG-A was also defective in Rab27a-deficient mice, further supporting that pDCs, the main IFN-α producing cell, require Rab27a for ex vivo and in vivo secretion of IFN-α, an essential response against viral infections.

Rab27a deficiency is associated with recurrent viral infections in humans largely attributed to the incapacity of immune cells to form an efficient immunological synapse and the consequent failure to secrete toxic cargoes necessary to kill infected cells. Furthermore, the accumulation of viruses in amphisomes has been shown to impact viral replication38 and to contribute to infection-induced lethality.39 Our observations that, in addition to its role in the trafficking of secretory granules, Rab27a deficiency causes eTLR accumulation in amphisomes in myeloid cells and defective type I IFN secretion by pDCs may have additional implications for human disease as these defects may contribute to the increased susceptibility to viral infections in patients with Griscelli syndrome. Thus, increased TNF-α production but decreased secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 by neutrophils, and decreased IFN-α secretion by pDCs, could contribute to the immunodeficient, yet pro-inflammatory, phenotype associated with Rab27a deficiency. Of note, in vivo TNF-α production by Rab27a-null mice was dependent on the CpG type class, supporting cell-specific functions for this GTPase in the production of this pro-inflammatory cytokine.

Limitations of the study

Although we show that Rab27a regulates CpG internalization, and this has the potential to negatively impact the early TLR9 response in Rab27a deficiency, our data also indicate enlargement of endosomes and altered composition of the eTLR compartment, making it difficult to differentiate their independent contributions to the defective early eTLR response in Rab27a-deficient neutrophils. A similar defect in the composition of the eTLR compartment was observed for TLR7. Furthermore, the early response of Rab27a-deficient neutrophils to the TLR7 ligand CL097, a small molecule whose mechanism of internalization is likely different from that of CpG, is also defective in Rab27a deficiency. In summary, although our study does not fully dissect between defects caused by ligand trafficking from those caused by eTLR localization, both processes are likely to contribute to defective ligand recognition and the early signaling defects caused by endomembrane dynamics alterations in Rab27a deficiency.

In conclusion, we present evidence that Rab27a deficiency is associated with impaired CpG internalization and abnormal composition of the eTLR compartment in neutrophils and with a selective defective cytokine secretion phenotype in neutrophils and pDCs, which independently and negatively impact immunity and inflammation. We identified Rab27a-mediated regulation of endomembrane dynamics as a regulator of these processes and suggest that defective nucleic acid recognition and altered cytokine secretion in Rab27a deficiency have an impact on infection susceptibility and inflammatory disease.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

The Rab27a-deficient mice, (Rab27aash/ash), contain a splicing mutation in the rab27a gene. Rab27aash/ash mice have been extensively utilized for the study of Rab27a deficiency and were described previously.40,41 Rab27aash/ash mice have a null-phenotype. Rab27aash/ash mice are considered a model of Griscelli syndrome and share the cellular phenotype present in human cells lacking Rab27a expression. However, Rab27aash/ash mice breed and age well and do not develop hemophagocytic syndrome unless challenged with certain pathogens. The Wash-cKO mouse model was described previously.29 Mice were maintained in a pathogen-free environment and had access to food and water ad libitum. Housing conditions included a 12-h dark/light cycle (light 6 a.m. to 6 p.m.), ambient temperature 24°C and humidity 50%. The mouse genotype was determined by Transnetyx and the lack of Rab27a protein expression was confirmed by Western blot. Animals were euthanized by over-anesthetization by standard and approved protocols. All animal studies were performed in compliance with the Department of Health and Human Services Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All studies were conducted according to National Institutes of Health and institutional guidelines and with the approval of the animal review board (IACUC) at The Scripps Research Institute.

METHOD DETAILS

Neutrophil isolation and transfection

Bone marrow cells from experimental males and females, 6- to 10-week-old mice and sex and age-matched control mice were collected from the long bones of mouse legs by flushing the bones with 5 mL of phenol red-free RPMI (Life Technologies) using a 30 Gauge ½’’ hypodermic needle and a BD Luer-Lok 10 mL syringe. Bone marrow–derived mature neutrophils were isolated by positive selection using Anti-Ly6G MicroBead Kit or Anti-Ly6G MicroBeads UltraPure purification kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–120-337). Purity of Ly6G + cells was ~99% purity by flow cytometry (Figure S1A). Mouse neutrophil nucleofection was carried out using the 4D-Nucleofector X Unit system (Lonza) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, mouse neutrophils were counted, and 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 20 μL of Lonza P3 solution with 1 μg of DNA and the solutions were transferred into the X Unit and then subjected to nucleofection in the 4D-Nucleofector using program EA-100. The cells were then resuspended in phenol red-free RPMI and seeded into four-chamber 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (No. 1.5 borosilicate coverglass). The cells were incubated at 37°C in a tissue culture incubator (5% CO2) until analysis. Where indicated, cells were labeled for 5 min with LysoTracker (deep-red) before being analyzed by TIRFM.

Neutrophil stimulation and analysis of ERK activation

Mouse neutrophils were treated with 10 ng/mL GM-CSF for 30 min before stimulation with 5 μM CpG for the indicated times. The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and phosphatase inhibitors (Calbiochem, 524–624 and 524–625) and subsequently subjected to Western blotting using the indicated antibodies as described before.42 Briefly, gel electrophoresis was carried out using 4–12% gradient or 12% Bolt Bis-Tris Plus gels (Life Technologies). Proteins were transferred onto 0.45-μm nitrocellulose membranes (GVS Filter Technology, 1–215-484). Total protein was analyzed using Fast Green. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C in the indicated primary antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bio-Rad Laboratories, goat anti-mouse HRP, 170–6516; or goat anti-rabbit HRP, 170–6515). The blots were developed using SuperSignal West Pico (Thermo Scientific, 34–580), clarity ECL Western blotting substrate (Bio-Rrad, 1–709-060) or Femto chemiluminescence substrate systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34–094). For the analysis of phosphorylated proteins by Western blot, phospho-proteins were developed using 20% concentration clarity ECL Western blotting substrate (Bio-Rad, 1–709-060), and long-exposure times (30–60sec). Subsequently, total proteins were detected using 5–10% clarity ECL Western blotting substrate and short exposure times (10–20 s). Uncropped blots and total protein controls are shown in Figure S7. ERK, p65, p38, STAT1 and TBK1 phosphorylation were quantified using ImageJ. The phosphorylated ERK, p65, p38, STAT and TBK1 intensity values were normalized to total ERK, p65, p358, STAT and TBK1 respectively.

Neutrophil stimulation, ELISA, cytokine analysis and flow cytometry analysis

Mouse neutrophils (1 × 106) isolated from the bone marrow of wild-type and Rab27aash/ash mice, were resuspended in phenol red-free RPMI and either stimulated with CpG-B (5μM) (ODN 1826; InvivoGen), CL097 (InvivoGen) or vehicle for the indicated time. Where indicated cells were primed with GM-CSF (10 ng/ml, Shenandoah Biotechnology) or vehicle for 30 min at 37°C before stimulation. The cells were spun down and the supernatants were collected for ELISA or Multiplex analysis. Where indicated samples were diluted (1:2) in PBS before Multiplex analysis. To evaluate the plasma membrane expression of CD11b (clone M1/70; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) or LAMP1 (clone 1D4B, BioLegend, #121610), the cells were blocked in PBS with 1% BSA for 10 min and stained with anti-mouse CD11b or LAMP1-Alexa Fluor 647 for 30 min. TLR9-surface staining was performed in non-permeabilized cells using clone J15A7, BD Biosciences, 565640. For intracellular + surface TLR9 labeling (clone J15A7, BD Biosciences, 565640), cells were permeabilized with 0.01% saponin on ice for 20min, then labeled in the presence of saponin with anti-TLR9 for 1h and washed twice before analysis by flow cytometry. Ly6G staining was used to gate the neutrophil population using anti–mouse-Ly6G-fluorescein isothiocyanate (clone 1A8; BD Biosciences). The cells were then washed, fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde, and the samples were analyzed using the NovoCyte 3000 flow cytometer with BD FACS Diva 6 software, and the data were processed using FlowJo (Ashland, OR) software.

Total Internal Reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy analysis of eTLR distribution and analysis of vesicular trafficking

TIRFM experiments were performed using a 100×/1.45 NA TIRF objective (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY) on a Nikon TE2000U microscope custom-modified with a TIRF illumination module as described.43 Images were acquired on a 14-bit, cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Hamamatsu) controlled through NIS-Elements software. After placing the cells on the stage, the position of the individual laser beams was adjusted with the TIRF illuminator to impinge on the coverslip at an angle to yield a calculated evanescent field depth of 100 or 400 nm for TIRFM and pseudo-TIRFM modes, respectively. For TIRFM, the objective is pre-calibrated for Z-position assignment and for chromatic shift between the channels using 100 nm Tetraspeck beads as specified by Nikon. For pseudo-TIRFM, the calculation of the penetration depth was performed by measuring the position at which the beam is exiting from the center of the objective and subsequently correlated to the penetration depth vs. the displacement of the beam from the center using the exponential decay depth equation described44 and incorporated in the Nikon software. The penetration depth for different wavelengths is estimated at plus or minus 20 nm for a given beam position. For live experiments, the images were recorded using 200–300 ms exposures depending on the fluorescence intensity of the sample. Vesicle dynamics was tracked using surface rendering or spot modules in IMARIS. Quantification of endolysosome size was performed by measuring the diameter of EGFP-LAMP1-positive late endosomes in each neutrophil using ImageJ as before.13

Super-resolution microscopy analysis

STORM was performed as described previously.45 Briefly, cells were labeled with anti-LAMP1, anti–TLR9, anti-TLR7, anti–LC3B and anti-AP3 primary antibodies and Alexa Fluor-Fluor 568, Alexa Fluor 647– and Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibodies. Samples were suspended in freshly prepared STORM buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 10% glucose, 0.1 M mercaptoethanolamine [cysteamine; Sigma-Aldrich], 56 U/ml glucose oxidase [from Aspergillus niger; Sigma-Aldrich], and 340 U/ml catalase [from bovine liver; Sigma-Aldrich]) and imaged on a Nikon Ti super-resolution microscope.46 Samples were imaged using a 100×/1.49 NA Apo TIRF objective either with or without TIRF illumination. Images were collected on an Andor IXON3 Ultra DU897 electron-multiplying CCD camera using the multicolor continuous mode setting in the Nikon Elements software. Power on the 488- and 647-nm lasers was adjusted to enable the collection of 50–300 molecules per 256 × 256 camera pixel frame at appropriate threshold settings for each channel. The collection was stopped after a sufficient number of frames were collected (usually yielding (1–2) × 106 molecules), and the super-resolution images were reconstructed with the Nikon STORM software.46 Quantification of the number of TLR9-AP3 bipartite complexes was performed by analysis of molecular cluster adjacency (distance <0.3μM) as described before.47

Immunofluorescence microscopy analysis

Immunofluorescence analysis of endogenous proteins was performed as described previously.29 Briefly, cells were seeded on untreated cover-glasses using a 4-Chamber 35 mm dish with 20 mm microwells (Cellvis LLC Cat #D35C4–20-1.5-N) and incubated at 37°C for 30 min, then fixed with 3.7% paraformaldehyde for 8 min, permeabilized with 0.01% saponin, and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS. The samples were labeled with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in the presence of 0.01% saponin and 1% BSA. Samples were washed and subsequently incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The cells were then stained with DAPI and mounted with Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech, 0100–01). Where indicated the dynamics of CpG trafficking was monitored by confocal microscopy using fluorescently labeled (FITC) CpG.

Image acquisition and colocalization analysis

All 8-bit/12-bit images were acquired using the full dynamic intensity range (0–256/0–4096) of the specified fluorophores. Stacks (on average 20 slices) were acquired with Nyquist resolution parameters using a 0.3 μm step size and optimal frame size of 1932 × 1932. Images were processed for quantitative colocalization in either Zen Pro in 2D (Zeiss) or Image Pro Premier 10 (IPP10) (Media Cybernetics). For visualization of LC3B, brightness and contrast were adjusted in ImageJ, post-analysis, and without altering pixel value, and equally to all examples. Briefly, maximum intensity projections (MIP) were generated in Zen and then processed using the Zen or IPP10 colocalization modules. Samples stained for secondary antibodies and unlabeled controls alone were used to define thresholds of real signal above background and non-specific signal. In Zen or IPP10, regions of interest (ROIs)s were drawn around each cell, previously defined minimum thresholds were input, two fluorescent channel signals were selected and the software automatically calculated pixel intensity spatial overlap coefficients between them (both Mander’s and Pearsons are scored). Mander’s overlap coefficient (MOC) was primarily used and is described as MOC = ∑i(Ri × Gi)∑iRi2 × ∑iGi2 where Ri is the intensity of the first fluorophore in an individual pixel, whereas Gi is the corresponding intensity for the second fluorophore in the same pixel. Areas of overlap were pseudo-colored in white (binary) to mathematically and spatially defined colocalized zones in cells.

pDC differentiation

BM-derived pDCs were differentiated as described under Mutagenetix (https://mutagenetix.utsouthwestern.edu/protocol/protocol_rec.cfm?pid=21). Briefly, bone marrow cells from wild-type or Rab27aash/ash mice were plated at 3–5 × 106 cells/ml in the presence of hFLT3L at 100 ng/mL. The cells were cultured for 9 days without disturbing the culture. Floating cells and loosely adherent cells were harvested by vigorous pipetting. Cultures were stained for CD11c, CD11b, and B220 and analyzed by FACS for CD11chi, CD11blo, B220hi. IFN-α production was analyzed by ELISA and immunofluorescence.

Antibodies and probes

Primary antibodies used for Western blotting and immunofluorescent staining were as follows: LAMP1 (sc-19992; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA), TLR9 (IF: Novus Biological #24729SS), (FC: clone J15A7, BD Biosciences, #565640)48; TLR7 (Novus Biologicals #27332SS), LC3B (Cell Signaling Technologies, #2775S), phosphorylated p38 (Cell Signaling Technologies, #9211); p38(Cell Signaling Technologies,#9212); phosphorylated ERK (Cell Signaling Technologies, #9101); ERK(Cell Signaling Technologies,#4696); phosphorylated p65 (Cell Signaling Technologies, #3033T); p65 (Cell Signaling Technologies, #6956s); phosphorylated TBK1 (Cell Signaling Technologies, #5483s); TBK1 (Cell Signaling Technologies, #51872s); phosphorylated STAT1 (Cell Signaling Technologies, #9167T); STAT1 (Transduction Laboratories, #S21120),CD11a (BD Biosciences, #553118), AP3 (Proteintech, 16454–1-AP), IFNa (Invitrogen, #PA5–119649), Lysotracker Green DND-26, (ThermoFisher, #L7526), ODN 1826-FITC (InvivoGen, #tlrl-1826f).

In vivo analysis of CpG-induced inflammation

To analyze in vivo response to CpG-induced inflammation mice were treated with a single i.v. injection of CpG-A or CpG-B as described previously.49 Briefly, 2μL of 1μg/μL CpG-A or CpG-B or vehicle were mixed with 98 μL of sterile PBS. CpG was mixed with 12μL DOTAP resuspended in 88μL PBS and the mix was incubated for 20 min at room temperature before proceeding with tail injection. After 6 h, blood was obtained by cardiac blood into the EDTA-containing anti-coagulant tubes. Cytokine analysis was performed after separating the plasma, by multiplex analysis (Millipore). In some experiments, supernatants were diluted (1:2) before analysis.

RNA-seq analysis

Neutrophils from wild type and Rab27aash/ash neutrophils were isolated using Ly6G positive selection. The cells were stimulated with 5 μM CpG-B or left untreated and incubated for 6h at 37°C and RNA was isolated using Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit and analyzed by BGI Genomics. Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format was processed and analyzed using BGI’s Dr. Tom Software. Reads were filtered using SOAPnuke50 to remove those containing sequencing adapters, having a low-quality base ratio (base quality ≤15) exceeding 20%, or an unknown base (‘N’ base) ratio greater than 5%. Afterward, clean reads were obtained and stored in FASTQ format. Clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using Bowtie2.51 Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (v1.2.28)52 obtaining read count, FPKM, and TPM values. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2,53 with genes having a q-value ≤0.05 considered significantly differentially expressed. A heatmap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was generated using pheatmap.54 To gain insights into phenotypic changes, Gene Ontology (GO) (http://www.geneontology.org/open in new window) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (https://www.kegg.jp/open in new window) enrichment analyses were performed on the annotated DEGs using the hypergeometric test implemented in Phyper (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_distributionopen in new window). The significance of enriched terms and pathways was determined using a q-value threshold of ≤0.05.55

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean, and error bars correspond to standard errors of the means (SEM) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was determined using the Student’s t-test or non-parametric Mann–Whitney test for two-group comparisons. We used one-way ANOVA or multiple comparisons test. Analyses were performed using GraphPad InStat (version 3) or Excel software, or Wilcoxon signed rank test, and graphs were made using GraphPad Prism (version 9) software. Each method used is specified in its corresponding figure legend. All measurements were taken from distinct samples. All methods were analyzed using two-tailed analyses unless indicated. For hematological, cytokine, and some ELISA studies, the investigators were blinded to group allocation. A pp-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Assays were repeated at least three times including those shown as representative data unless otherwise stated in the figure legend. The number of samples, cells, or mice per group is indicated in Figure Legends. Super-plots were applied to data when large amounts of cells were analyzed. Each Super-plot includes both single-cell values and mean values from independent experiments. Our research includes predetermination of sample size based on power analysis, blinding of experimentalists to genotype and vehicle or drug when possible; data reporting including all animals; randomization of littermates to the drug or placebo groups; and reporting of positive and negative results.
