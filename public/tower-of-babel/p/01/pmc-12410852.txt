Item generation and establishing face and content validity of a rating scale: A primer
Authors: Snehil Gupta, Sandeep Grover, Vikas Menon, PV Indu, K Vidhukumar, Deenu Chacko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article is the third in a series of learning resources on translation/adaptation of a scale, new scale development and testing its psychometric properties. This paper deals with the process of domain identification and item generation for a new scale and how to perform content validity (CV). During the scale development, the domain of interest/construct must be well-articulated and decided a priori. Item generation should be informed by deductive as well as inductive methods to capture a comprehensive item pool. Items for a scale should be simple, unambiguous, accounting for the experiences of the target population and follow a normal conversation. CV assesses the relevance and representativeness of a scale’s items. It is assessed through face validity (involving the target population), content validity ratio (CVR), content validity index (item and scale level), and interrater agreement (percent agreement, Cohen’s kappa, and Fleiss’ generalized kappa) by involving experts. In this paper, we provide insights and practical guidance on how to generate items for a scale and perform content validity checks. We have provided, and practical guide for conducting analyses and interpreting results on SPSS and MS Excel for analysis.

In previous papers of this series on scale development and psychometric testing, we have discussed the process of scale translation and adaptation[1] and the principles of reliability testing.[2] In this paper, we provide insights and practical guidance on domain identification, item generation for a scale and performing content validity (CV) checks.

DOMAIN(S) IDENTIFICATION

Scale development starts with the identification of domain(s) and item generation. A domain refers to “concepts, attributes, or unobservable behaviors that are the target of the scale.”[3] The domain(s) of interest must be well-articulated and decided a priori; as well-defined domains provide a working knowledge about the phenomena, establish domain/concept boundaries, and ease the process of item generation and CV.[3]

ITEM GENERATION

There are two primary approaches for identifying appropriate items for a scale: deductive and inductive methods.[4] Deductive methods are based on the underlying theory or pre-identified domains or concepts. Items are generated through literature support and inputs from experts and target population. An illustrative example of this approach can be found in the work of Dennis and Faux[5] in the development of the breastfeeding self-efficacy scale.

In contrast, the inductive method begins with a research question and involves collecting qualitative information from field experts and persons with lived experience through in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and field observations. The information is then subjected to thematic analysis to identify relevant themes and generate representative items. The work of Morris et al.[6] on the interpersonal dynamics in the injecting partnerships scale is useful example of this method.

While the literature review provides the theoretical basis for the domains under study, qualitative exercises help translate these abstract constructs into more tangible, observable forms. Therefore, researchers should use both deductive and inductive methods, often referred to as a mixed-methods approach, for comprehensive item generation.[78]

At this stage, the scale developer also needs to investigate the items’ form, their wordings, and the range of responses the questions may induce among the respondents. Importantly, the questions should be able to capture the target population’s lived experiences. Furthermore, to obtain accurate responses from respondents, the questions should be kept simple, straightforward, and follow normal conversation.[17] In Box 1, we have provided some practical tips, while drafting a scale, including instructions to respondents, items, and responses.

CONTENT VALIDITY

CV refers to the “extent to which elements of the assessment tool are relevant and representative of the construct under study for a particular assessment purpose.”[910] It links abstract concepts to observable and measurable matrices. CV provides a preliminary idea of the instrument’s construct validity. However, it is observed that researchers often perform CV superficially or very briefly, which adversely influences the scale development process and psychometric assessment.[7]

CV primarily involves validation by experts. Another component of content validation is its suitability and acceptability to users—this type of validation is usually performed by the target population, which is often referred to as face validity. We will first describe face validity and then discuss CV as assessed by experts.

In the Supplementary Table 1, we have summarized various CV indices and their critical aspects; readers are encouraged to go through it to get a gist of the indices.

FACE VALIDITY

Face validity is the “extent to which respondents or end users judge that the items of an assessment tool are appropriate to the intended construct and objectives of the assessment.”[1] It is considered less analytical compared to a CV involving experts, which typically employs statistical methods. Face validity is primarily concerned with the appearance, attractiveness, and acceptability of an item to the target population. Here, the target population reviews the instrument for their familiarity with the construct and attaches importance to its items; however, can also involve the subject experts.

For face validity, typically, a response sheet is provided to the raters, highlighting the study’s objectives, the targeted construct and its domains, rating instructions, and scoring system for aspects such as importance, simplicity, and understandability. Also, reviewers are encouraged to provide feedback on any crucial phenomenon not covered by the scale, item modifications required in, and the scale’s overall acceptability.

In quantitative terms, an individual item’s impact score (IIS) is calculated; raters are asked to rate each item on a Likert Scale, wherein 1 = unimportant, 2 = slightly important, 3 = relatively important, 4 = important and 5 = very important. IIS is obtained by multiplying the frequency (proportion of raters, who rated an item to be important/very important) and importance (mean importance score of an item) of an item by the raters (a worked example is provided in Table 1). As illustrated in Table 1, items with an IIS of at least 1.5 are usually retained (as it indicates at least 50% of raters (0.5) consider an item to be important (mean = 3).[7]

The raters also assess the items for their completeness and comprehensibility. They comment on the item difficulty level, its desired suitability, the relationship between items and the broader construct, ambiguity, and potential for misinterpretation in the items, and incomprehensibility of words.[39] Moreover, they can also suggest for new items or the removal of item(s) from the initial item pools.

Content validity by expert: In this process, experts evaluate each item’s relevance to the target domain. Typically, the experts should be independent of those involved in the item generation; multiple experts are preferred (5–7 experts). The consensus-building exercise can involve Delphi methods and panel discussion.[911]

VARIOUS INDICES OF CONTENT VALIDITY

Content validity ratio (CVR)

It is primarily used to assess the essentiality of an item, as recommended by experts, for a scale, thereby removing unimportant items from the initial item pool. Those items which are found essential for a given construct or scale are then subjected to item-level and scale–level CV analyses (described later).

The CVR is calculated through two methods - Lynn (1986) and Lawshe (1975) methods.[12] As Lawshe method is more often used, for the sake of brevity, we explain this method for demonstration. CVR is calculated by the formula: (Ne – N/2)/(N/2), where N = number of experts, Ne = Number of experts who rated an item as essential (for scoring refer Table 2). The CVR value ranges from 0 to 1, with the cut-off score dependent on the number of raters. A CVR for an item, for instance, involving 10 experts, should have a minimum value of 0.62 for it to be considered essential for a given construct, according to the Lawshe table.[13]

Scales’ CVR is calculated on the items that are found essential for a given construct/scale. It is calculated by averaging the CVRs of all the items.[12] Notably, the scale’s overall CVR reporting is usually not required, as it often does not convey much additional meaning.

Content validity Index (CVI)

CVI entails the degree to that a scale has an appropriate sample of items for the targeted construct. From a statistical viewpoint, it is a measure of the consensus estimate (among the raters) on the item’s/scale’s relevance for a given construct.[12] While the purpose of the CVR is to (initially) identify the essentiality of an item for a construct, CVI is concerned with the relevance of an item for a construct (I-CVI) and overall relevance of the scale (S-CVI) for that construct.[11]

There are two kinds of CVI commonly reported in the scale development literature - Item-level CVI (I-CVI) and Scale-level CVI (S-CVI).[914] I-CVI informs the tool developer based on the experts’ consensus which item to revise, delete, substitute, or retained as such. Following this, S-CVI is calculated either based on the proportion of the items considered content valid by the experts (Average proportion method and universal agreement method) or the average I-CVIs of the scale (averaging method).

To get an accurate I-CVI, it is essential that the domain/construct is adequately conceptualized, the items are reasonable, the panel comprises quality/representative experts, with clear instructions about their role, and an effective response gathering process is employed.

Item-level CVI

The I-CVI is defined as the proportion of raters who considered an item to be relevant for a given construct. Typically, the responses comprise four options: 1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = relevant, 4 = very relevant. The four points are dichotomized into “not relevant” (1 and 2) and “relevant” (3 and 4) for computational ease.

The formula to calculate I-CVI = number of experts, who endorsed an item as relevant / total number of experts. The final value ranges from 0 to 1. An acceptable value depends on the number of experts involved: if 3–5 raters are involved, an I-CVI of 1 is acceptable, while for 6–10 raters, a value ≥ 0.78 is acceptable.[15]

Pitfalls of the CVI

CVI cannot comment on the comprehensiveness of the item set for a given construct. The CVI is a form of interrater agreement, which can be overestimated due to chance factors. Dichotomization of a multi-point Likert scoring is another issue, as it undermines the nuances of experts’ responses. Hence, the scale developer and users must be aware of this granularity while interpreting CVI.[14]

Interrater agreement as a method of CV – CV can be understood as level of agreement among the experts. There are three agreement methods for the CV: 1) Percent agreement, 2) Cohen’s Kappa, and 3) Fleiss’ Generalized Kappa. Here, we only discuss the initial two kinds of agreement measures as they are often used, and for the latter, readers are requested to refer to Supplementary Table 2 and 3.

Percent agreement – here, percent agreement (% agreement) on each item, among all the raters is calculated. Percent agreement can be used only if there are two response categories (0 or 1, yes or no). A % agreement ≥0.80 is considered acceptable. In Table 3 (column “P”), we have provided sample data comprising a 11-item scale with responses from 13 experts. All the experts rated “item 1” as relevant; hence, the % agreement is 1; for “item 9,” out of 13 experts, only 11 agreed to its relevance (here, the % agreement is 0.84%). Similarly, % agreement among the raters for all items is calculated, and their average is taken. Here, the average % agreement of the scale comes out to be 0.85.[21617]

The major drawback of the percentage agreement is its failure to account for chance agreement (or chance disagreement). Thus, it may overestimate the actual agreement among raters. Therefore, to overcome the issue of chance agreement, Cohen’s kappa is widely used for computing interrater agreement in CV.

COHEN’S KAPPA

Cohen’s kappa is the chance-corrected extent of agreement between observers.[16] It is a ratio of the amount of actual agreement beyond chance and potential agreement beyond chance between raters. Where the numerator represents the amount of observed agreement (Pr(a)) minus the amount of agreement expected by chance (Pr(e)), while the denominator indicates total number of observations minus the number of agreements expected by chance.[1617]

A kappa of “0” represents the amount of agreement expected by chance, “1” indicates perfect agreement, and a “negative” kappa indicates an agreement worse than expected by random chance, or disagreement (warrants item screening and looking for raters’ suitability). Cohen’s kappa is used when there are two raters; for >2 raters, Fleiss’ Generalized Kappa [refer Supplementary Table 2 and 3] should be used.[14] Both Cohen’s Kappa and Fleiss’ Generalized Kappa can be calculated using common statistical software (SPSS, MS-Excel, etc.). CONCLUSION

The development of a new scale begins with domain identification of a construct and generating items to comprehensively capture that construct. A developer must use both deductive and inductive approach, by involving the experts and target population, to generate items. This step is followed by the content validation process – face validity (item impact score and qualitative responses) by involving target population/experts, and content validity (CVR, CVI, and interrater agreement [Cohen’s kappa, Fleiss’ generalized kappa]) to establish the essentiality and relevancy of the scale’s items by involving experts. It is important that the selection of the target population and experts should be properly contextualized; Similarly, cut-off value of the CV indices should be informed by the domain of the study and the number and characteristics of the raters. This paper can be a useful practical guide to the readers and researchers involved in scale development.
