An Escherichia coli strain with recombinant Aspergillus spp. pectinase activity for application in bioscouring
Authors: Anik Biswas, Samayeta Sarkar Tuli, Rasheda Begum Dina, Md Tamzid Hossain Tanim, Reefa Nawar, Tahmia Akter, Kaniz Fatema, Papia Haque, Abu Ashfaqur Sajib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional scouring processes involve chemicals and heat, which have a negative environmental footprint with the potential to harm the environment as well as human health. Enzymes offer an environment-friendly and low-energy-consuming alternative method for textile fabric processing, which is important for sustainable development.ResultsIn this study, molecular biology tools were applied to express an endopolygalacturonase enzyme (pectinase) from Aspergillus steynii in Escherichia coli and assess its pectinolytic activity in vitro and on textile fabrics. Pectinolytic activity on fabric was evident in the wicking test, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, and field emission scanning electron microscopy.ConclusionThis previously unreported enzyme has a relatively shorter sequence, which is supposed to pose less metabolic load in the host, but has a structure characteristic of greater stability under stressful conditions. This recombinant pectinase enzyme holds promise to be a potential bioscouring agent.Keywords: Pectinase, Bioscouring, Endopolygalacturonase, Pectinolytic activity

Introduction

Chemically, cotton is primarily composed of cellulose (88–96 %) in addition to constituents like proteins (1–1.9 %), waxes (0.4–1.2 %), pectins (0.4–1.2 %), inorganics (0.7–1.6 %) and other impurities (0.5–8 %).1, 2, 3 Cotton fiber consists of mainly three layers: cuticle, primary wall, and secondary wall. The cuticle layer contains waxes that are hydrophobic in nature and thus can repel water and prevent textile dye penetration to the interior of the fiber. The primary wall is relatively thin and contains amorphous cellulose, pectins, hemicellulose, proteins, and ions. The secondary wall is multi-layered and consists mainly of cellulose. Upon harvesting from plants, cotton is subjected to a series of steps, called scouring, to prepare the fiber for fabric preparation. Scouring is intended to remove all natural and additive impurities such as oil, wax, dust, dirt, etc. to prepare clean hydrophilic textile material.4 If not removed, these impurities impart a hydrophobic nature and interfere with the downstream aqueous chemical processes, like dyeing and finishing.5, 6 Pectin removal during cotton scouring is essential to make the fibers more hydrophilic, improving their absorbency and wettability, which is critical for uniform dyeing and finishing.

Sustainable development is an absolute necessity for meeting the needs of the present and future generations, which can be accomplished by replacing traditional methods with more efficient and environmentally friendly alternatives.7 The traditional scouring or alkaline boil-off process involves boiling the cotton fabric in a scouring solution that contains sodium hydroxide, sodium silicate, surfactants, detergents, chelating (sequestering) agents, builders (salts), and organic solvents.4, 6 Heat and concentrated alkaline solutions released from scouring cotton fabrics bear the potential to harm the environment as well as human health.8 In addition to removing non-cellulosic impurities, this traditional process results in the deterioration of fabric strength by damaging the cellulose.9 Enzymes offer an environment-friendly alternative, as these provide high catalytic efficiency, specificity, and less toxic effluent.10, 11, 12

Pectinases have drawn attention from researchers around the world because of their application as a biological catalyst in multiple industrial processes, including cotton scouring, fruit juice extraction and its clarification, plant fiber degumming, plant biomass liquefaction and saccharification, wastewater treatment, tea and coffee fermentations, papermaking, and vegetable oil extraction.13, 14 Both endo- and exopolygalacturonase enzymes from a variety of sources have been used.15 Fungi are known to have a wide range of degradative capabilities.16, 17 For example, a 52 kDa Sporothrix schenckii 1099–18 exo-polygalacturonase produced in Pichia pastoris has been shown to work in apple juice processing.18, 19 Endopolygalacturonase enzymes from the Aspergillus genus usually have a right-handed parallel β helix with four parallel β sheets.20 Typically such enzymes are quite stable and suited for industrial use.21 In this study, a relatively smaller (∼37 kDa) endo-polygalacturonase from the Aspergillus genus was targeted for heterologous expression in Escherichia coli, which has been used as the biofactory for many heterologous protein production strategies.22 The bioscouring potential of the enzyme was also tested.

Methods and materials

Preparation of the gene insert

The coding sequence of the polygalacturonase gene [NCBI gene: 36557246, Transcript: XM_024849547.1] of the Aspergillus steynii species was retrieved from NCBI23 and the codons were optimized for E. coli using the JCat software (version 2).24 The entire coding region of the gene was assembled following the Gibson assembly method25 after amplifying 10 overlapping commercially synthesized single-stranded segments in polymerase chain reaction (PCR) ranging in size between 112 and 133 bps having overlaps of 20 bps between the adjacent segments (table 1). The nucleotide sequence that corresponds to the alpha-amylase signal peptide of E. coli, along with a NheI restriction site, was introduced in the forward primer of the first segment. The reverse primer of the last segment contained the restriction site for XbaI. Polymerase chain reaction (5 µl of 5X Q5® Buffer, 1 µl of 10 mM dNTP mix, 1.0 µl of 10 µM forward and 10 µM reverse primer mix, 2 µl of template DNA segments, 0.2 µl of Q5® DNA polymerase, and 14.8 µl of PCR-grade water) was used to amplify the gene segments in Axygen® MaxyGene II Thermal Cycler following a reaction condition as follows: an initial denaturation at 98⁰C for 30 s followed by 35 cycles each at 98⁰C for 30 s, annealing at 58⁰C for 30 s, elongation at 72⁰C for 45 s, followed by an extension at 72⁰C for 5 min. Size and quality of the amplified products were checked following agarose gel (1 % w/v) electrophoresis alongside size-specific markers (GeneOn, 300003). The PCR products were purified using the FavorPrepTM Gel/PCR purification mini kit (FAGCK001, Favorgen Biotech Corp). The Gibson assembly method25 was used to join the segments using their overlapping region using the NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master mix (N2611A, New England Biolabs) in a 20 µl reaction volume at 50⁰C for one hour. The products were purified using the FavorPrepTM Gel/PCR purification mini kit. Polymerase chain reaction (5.0 µl of 5X Q5® Buffer, 1.5 µl of 10 mM dNTP mix, 1.5 µl of 10 µM forward (Seg_1_For) and 10 µM reverse primer (Seg_10_Rev) mix (Table 1), 0.75 µl of purified assembled template DNA, 0.2 µl of Q5® DNA Polymerase, and 16.05 µl of PCR grade water) was used to amplify the full-length of the coding region in Axygen® MaxyGene II Thermal Cycler in a reaction condition as follows: an initial denaturation at 98⁰C for 30 s followed by 35 cycles each at 98⁰C for 30 s, 58⁰C for 30 s, 72⁰C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72⁰C for 5 min. Size and quality of the amplified products were checked. Amplified full length gene construct was purified using FavorPrepTM Gel/PCR purification mini kit (FAGCK001, Favorgen Biotech Corp).

Transformation with the gene construct

pGLO plasmid and the purified gene construct were subjected to restriction digestion by NheI (ER0971, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and XbaI (ER0681, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) separately at 37⁰C for 4 h, followed by inactivation of enzyme at 65⁰C for 15 min. The restriction digestion mixture for the plasmid also contained alkaline phosphatase (EF0651, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Digestion of pGLO plasmid with NheI and XbaI removed the coding sequence for GFP protein. Plasmid and gene construct were incubated at 37⁰C for 4 h, followed by inactivation of enzyme at 65⁰C for 15 min. The ligation reaction was set under the following condition: 10 × T4 ligase buffer 5 µl, purified pGLO digest 30 µl, purified gene digest 10 µl, T4 ligase 0.5 µl, and nuclease-free water 4.5 µl. The ligation mixture was incubated at 14⁰C for 90 min, 22⁰C for 90 min, followed by 65⁰C for 15 min.

Chemically competent E. coli DH5α (C2987I, New England Biolabs)26 cells were transformed following a modified version of the heat-shock protocol.27 Super Optimal broth with Catabolite repression (SOC) media (2 % (w/v) peptone, 0.5 % (w/v) yeast extract, 10 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 20 mM glucose) was used as recovery media after heat-shock transformation. Transformed cells were selected on 2 % (w/v) Luria-Bertani (LB) (610084, Liofilchem) medium containing 1.2 % (w/v) agar (611001, Liofilchem), 0.75 % (w/v) arabinose (DD001, Himedia), and 10 µg/mL ampicillin sodium solution (A0104, Duchefa Biochemie). Colonies that appeared white under UV illumination were subcultured in new plates.

Qualitative assays for pectinolytic activity

Pectinolytic activity of the transformed bacteria was assessed in a medium composed of 2 % (w/v) LB, 1.5 % (w/v) agar, 0.75 % (w/v) arabinose, 0.2 % (w/v) pectin, and 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, bacterial colonies in the culture plates were immersed in 0.1 % (w/v) Congo Red solution and incubated at room temperature for 30 min at 40 rpm on an orbital shaker. Then the plates were destained a few times with 1 M NaCl solution for 1 h.

In addition, Lugol’s Iodine (4 % (w/v) Potassium Iodide and 2 % (w/v) Iodine in distilled water) was used to test for the activity of pectinase.28 Transformed and control bacteria were grown in plates containing 2.0 % (w/v) LB, 0.75 % (w/v) pectin from citrus, 1 % (w/v) arabinose, 1.5 % (w/v) agar, and 100 μg/mL ampicillin. Following 24 h of culture, the plates were flooded with 6 ml of Lugol’s Iodine solution for 5 min on an orbital shaker at 40 rpm. The plates were destained 3 times with 10 ml distilled water for 10 min at 40 rpm.

Detection of the transgene and the pectinase in transformed bacteria

Bacteria with pectinolytic activities were cultured in 2 % LB broth with 100 μg/mL ampicillin. Plasmids from these bacteria were extracted using the FavorPrepTM plasmid extraction kit (FAPDE 001, Favorgen Biotech Corp.), visualized following gel electrophoresis, and used as templates in PCR (5.0 µl of 5X Q5® buffer, 1.5 µl of 10 mM dNTP mix, 1.5 µl of 10 µM Seg_1_For and Seg_10_Rev primer mix, 0.75 µl of the isolated plasmid DNA, 0.2 µl of Q5® DNA Polymerase, and 16.05 µl of PCR grade water) to amplify the transgene in Axygen® MaxyGene II Thermal Cycler following a reaction condition as: an initial denaturation at 98⁰C for 30 s followed by 35 cycles each at 98⁰C for 30 s, at 58⁰C for 30 s, at 72⁰C for 1 min, followed by an extension at 72⁰C for 5 min. Size and quality of the amplified products were checked following agarose gel (1 % w/v) electrophoresis alongside size-specific markers (GeneOn, 300005). In addition, genomic DNA was isolated from bacteria using Chelex® (C7901, Sigma). The bacterial pellet from liquid culture was washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (003002, Invitrogen™) and heated at 95 °C for 10 min in 10 % Chelex® solution. 1 µL of the supernatant was used as template in PCR with 16S rRNA gene-specific primers (forward primer- CTCCTACGGGAGGCAG and reverse primer– GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT). Amplified PCR products were purified using FavorPrepTM Gel/PCR purification mini kit (FAGCK001, Favorgen Biotech Corp) and sequenced through a commercial service. Sequences were analyzed using the Nucleotide BLAST tools at NCBI.29

Proteins from control and transformed bacteria were extracted using RIPA buffer (150 mM sodium chloride, 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 % (v/v) Triton X-100, 1.0 % (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, 0.1 % (w/v) SDS). Protein samples were separated in 10 % SDS-PAGE using 1 × Tris-Glycine running buffer. PageRuler™ Plus Prestained Protein Ladder (26619, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was run alongside the samples. Following incubation in fixative solution (50 % methanol, 10 % acetic acid, 40 % dH2O), the gel was stained (50 % methanol, 10 % acetic acid, 39.75 % dH2O, 0.25 % Coomassie Brilliant Blue) and destained (67.5 % dH2O, 7.5 % acetic acid, 25 % methanol) for protein visualization.

Quantitative test for pectinolytic activity

Cells in PBS were lysed using a sonicator, and pectinolytic activity in cell-free extract was quantified using dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) test to measure the amount of newly formed reducing ends of sugar, which correlates to the number of enzymatic cuts. Glucose solutions were prepared as standards of concentrations 0 mM, 0.05 mM, 0.1 mM, 0.25 mM, 0.5 mM, 1 mM, 1.5 mM, 2 mM, 2.5 mM, 3 mM, 4 mM, 5 mM, 6 mM, 7 mM, 8 mM, 9 mM, and 10 mM using PBS as the solvent. 500 μL of the standard solutions were mixed with 1 mL of the DNS solution and kept in boiling water for 10 min. Optical density values were taken at 540 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. A correlation curve was derived using the Microsoft Excel software. 250 μL of clear cell lysate was mixed with 0.1 % (w/v) pectin (as a 250 μL solution in PBS solution). The crude enzyme-pectin mixture was incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 2 h. The solutions were mixed with 1 mL of the DNS solution and kept in boiling water for 10 min. Optical density (OD) values were taken at 540 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. All experiments in this study were performed in ≥ 2 replicates.

Assays for pectinolytic activities on textile fabric

The wettability of the enzyme-treated cotton fabric was evaluated using the vertical wicking test (AATCC 197), and the level of absorbency of the enzyme-treated cotton fabric samples was determined by measuring the distance travelled by the liquid 10 min after the fabric was submerged into 1 % dye solution. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopic (FT-IR) analysis (ABB MB3000, ABB, Canada) was performed to analyze the pectinolytic activity on fabric samples. The Horizon MB™ software was used for data capture and analysis. Measurements were performed in ATR mode using a MIRacle™ universal ATR accessory equipped with a diamond crystal. The FT-IR spectrometer recorded the infrared spectra for each sample. The tests are done in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1. The average of 5 test runs was taken to ensure clarity and consistency. Data was plotted as a transmittance percentage against a wavenumber (cm−1) graph using the Origin software. The control and enzyme-treated cotton samples were scanned, and the data were analyzed using Origin software (OriginLab Corp.). The transmittance percentages were plotted against wave number (cm−1 values. In this assay particular attention was given to aliphatic C–H stretch indicator at wave numbers of 2,923 cm−1 and 2,885 cm−1 hydroxyl group at 3,288 cm−1 and ester carbonyl at 1,732 cm−1. These groups are present in pectin, as well as the wax and the cuticle layer of the cotton fibers. The control and enzyme-treated cotton fibers were used in a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) at Bangladesh Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (BCSIR) to observe roughness and damage in the fiber which is indicative of the cleavage of pectic substances of the cell walls.

Structure prediction and in silico analysis

The PHYRE2 protein fold recognition server30 was used to predict the structure of the gene-encoded enzyme. PROCHECK software31 and the Ramachandran plot were used to validate the quality of the prediction. AutoDockVina32, 33 was used to compute the affinity of monomeric alpha-D-galacturonic acid and dimeric digalacturonic acid in the predicted protein structure. The ligand structures34, 35 were downloaded from the online database PubChem.36 The docked ligand to the predicted protein structure was visualized using the Discovery Studio software.37 Thermostability of the protein was predicted using PPTStab.38

Results and discussion

In this study, a fungal pectinase was expressed in E. coli. Codon-optimized gene fragments were amplified in PCR and merged following Gibson assembly protocol. Gibson assembly allows the joining of synthesized codon-optimized gene fragments, bypassing the need for source organism and codon editing steps for optimum translation.25 As a result, genes can be constructed, and novel modifications can be added without the need for the source organism.39 In this study 10 individual segments were combined in one step following the Gibson assembly method (Fig. 1).

Synthesis of the gene construct. a) PCR amplified gene segments of polygalacturonase gene of A. steynii. b) PCR amplified product (1176 bp, including the signal sequence and the restriction enzyme recognition sites) of the merged product gene segments. 1.8 % and 1 % agarose gels were used, respectively.

This particular polygalacturonase enzyme was chosen for a number of reasons. First, it was selected because of its shorter length, allowing for easier assembly, better transformation efficiency, and less metabolic load in the host. Second, it was a previously unreported enzyme with sequence resemblance to other Aspergillus endopolygalacturonase already in industrial use. Finally, the parallel β-helix of this enzyme family was predicted to confer greater stability under stressful conditions. To virtually predict the functionality of this enzyme, in silico model prediction and molecular docking analysis were performed. The predicted structure of the enzyme shows a beta-sheet pattern with a groove on the upper-right side (Fig. 2), which is very similar to the structure of rhamnogalacturnoase (RGase A) enzyme of the Aspergillus aculeatus, which contains parallel β sheets.20 This type of enzyme is quite stable and can be suited for industrial use.21 According to the PROCHECK software and Ramachandran plot, the predicted model has over 99.6 % (282/283) residues in the allowed region of φ and ψ angles. Pectic substances consist of long polysaccharide chains with a backbone composed of homogalacturonan and rhamnogalacturonan regions.20 Since the actual substrate was unavailable in an appropriate format, molecular docking was performed using two ligands- alpha-D-galacturonic acid and digalacturonic acid, in conjunction with the predicted protein (Fig. 2).The dimer (−6.9 kcal/mol) had more affinity than the monomer (−6.0 kcal/mol), which is indicative of stronger binding resulting from more interaction with the chemical side groups of pectin and possibly leading to chain breaks. The thermostability of the enzyme was predicted to be 47.40°C.

Predicted structure of the extracellular polygalacturonase gene of A. steynii. (a) Vertical structure: N-terminal starts at the top, and C-terminal is at the bottom. (b) Side view: showing the groove for potential pectin binding. Molecular docking analysis was performed with c) alpha-D-galacturonic acid and d) digalacturonic acid, in conjunction with the predicted protein.

E. coli has been the host for many heterologous protein production strategies.22 The E. coli DH5α strain transformation developed by Douglas Hanahan is one of the most efficient in the case of transformation of plasmids.40 The pGLO plasmid is a very well-known vector carrying the selectable marker β-lactamase gene for ampicillin resistance, green fluorescent protein as a reporter gene, arabinose activator protein, and a multiple cloning site (MCS) under the regulation of the araBAD promoter. Hence, L-arabinose can bind with the activator protein and drive the transcription of any transgene inserted into the MCS. In this study, the pectinase gene was inserted in the pGLO plasmid by replacing the coding region of GFP and under the control of the pBAD promoter and upstream of the transcription termination signals. Following transformation, the colonies were checked for fluorescence before proceeding to the steps of preparing pure cultures. Several of the clones showed pectinase activity in Congo Red assay plates. DNA sequencing of the amplified products in PCR with plasmids isolated from the penctinase activity-positive clone and transgene-specific primers showed the existance of the transgene in the transformed bacteria. 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing confirmed the host to be E. coli (Fig. 3).

Lugol’s Iodine test is a qualitative test which is more sensitive towards a change in polysaccharide concentration. The Lugol’s Iodine solution is made by mixing potassium iodide and molecular iodine. This recipe creates a charge transfer complex forming polyiodide ions of type In-. Electrons in such polyiodide complexes are easily excited to a higher energy level by photons. Therefore, these complexes can absorb photons of a wide range, which makes the solution dark in color.41 The polyiodide ions can slide into polysaccharide coils formed only by intact long chains of polysaccharides. As a result, intact polysaccharide structures in agar plates retain the polyiodide ions and show the characteristic dark color. On the other hand, agar plate regions where the polysaccharides have been cleaved will become transparent. Subsequent washing using distilled water can remove loosely bound polyiodide ions and increase the color contrast. In assessing pectinase activity, clear zones could be seen around the selected clones’ colonies (Fig. 3). The iodine and polyiodide ions can preferentially bind to polymeric carbohydrates, and therefore, the places with degraded polygalacturonic acid are unable to retain the stain, resulting in a lighter zone around the colony. The molecular weight of the pectinase was calculated to be ∼ 37.82 kDa (calculated using the Expasy server.42 Among the proteins extracted from the pectinase activity-positive clone, a band equivalent to the size of pectinase could be observed, which was absent in the untransformed cells and cells transformed with pGLO plasmid (Fig. 3).

The dinitrosalicylic acid test was used to quantify the glycosidic bond breaks. This assay tests for free carbonyl groups (C=O), found in the reducing sugars like glucose and fructose. The change in color corresponds to the aldehyde functional group present in the solution. During the reaction, DNS is reduced to 3-amino-5-nitrosalicylic acid under alkaline conditions43. For quantitation of the enzymatic activity, appropriate standards were used. For this purpose, the appropriate working range of an assay and correlation curves were generated. The standard curve could produce a consistent straight line in concentrations ranging from 0.25 mM to 2.5 mM (Supplementary Fig. 1). Within this range, the linear regression value (R2) was 0.99. Therefore, the absorption values of glucose concentration in the range of 0.25 mM to 2.5 mM were used to correlate the OD values with nascent carbohydrate reducing end concentration. Bacteria transformed with the GFP plasmid was used as the reference to calculate the difference in OD values and estimate the amount of released reducing ends (Supplementary Table 1).

The composition of pectic substance is different in cotton, and thus the bioscouring ability of the pectinase enzyme can be accurately measured from the tests on fabrics. Wicking test, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) analyses are standard tests for fabric quality testing. In the wicking test, dye in pectinase-positive bacterial extract-treated fabric travelled more than the control, and it had the highest wicking distance among all the samples after 5 min (wet condition) and 10 min (dry condition) (Fig. 4). In untreated fabric the dye solution could not rise more than 1 cm because of the outer waxy layer that creates a barrier for hydration.

The average wicking rate was encouraging because of the increased wicking height in the case of enzyme-treated fabric compared to the controls at the same treatment parameters and washing conditions. This enhanced wicking could be attributed to the breakdown of the pectic substances by enzymatic treatment compared to controls.

Conclusion

Enzymatic catalysis is preferable over chemical methods for various benefits, such as higher specificity, more efficiency, less aggressiveness, etc. Development of an E. coli strain that expresses a relatively smaller fungal pectinase was the goal of this study. Activity of the recombinant pectinase was evident in multiple in vitro assays as well as on fabric. This recombinant pectinase enzyme holds promise to be bioscouring agent. However, further research is needed to assess its activity under mild alkaline solutions typical of cotton processing, as well as define its thermal stability and optimum pH.
