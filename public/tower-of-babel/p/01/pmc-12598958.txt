NEOadjuvant Dendritic cell therapy added to first line standard of care in advanced epithelial Ovarian Cancer (NEODOC): protocol of a first-in-human, exploratory, single-centre phase I/II trial in the Netherlands
Authors: Bouke Koeneman, Gerty Schreibelt, Tjitske Duiveman - de Boer, Kevin Bos, Tom van Oorschot, Jeanette Pots, Nicole Scharenborg, Annemiek de Boer, Simone Hins - de Bree, Nienke de Haas, Anna de Goede, Harm Westdorp, Maaike van Ham, I Jolanda M de Vries, Petronella B Ottevanger

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe currently available immunotherapies have failed to meet expectations in inducing durable responses in patients with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC). The low number of somatic missense mutations in EOC necessitates highly potent neoantigen-directed approaches. To this end, we have developed a novel dendritic cell (DC) product that consists of a specialised cross-presenting subset of DC, conventional DC type 1 (cDC1).Methods and analysisWe will conduct the NEODOC study, an investigator-initiated first-in-human phase I/II trial. This study will assess the immunogenicity, safety and feasibility of a cDC1-based, autologous tumour lysate-loaded, DC product. 10 patients with previously untreated advanced EOC (stage IIIb-c, IVa or stage IVb if only supradiaphragmatic or inguinal lymph nodes <1 cm) requiring neoadjuvant chemotherapy will receive DC therapy alongside standard-of-care carboplatin/paclitaxel chemotherapy and interval cytoreductive surgery. The primary endpoint is immune response, and the secondary endpoints are the safety and feasibility of the treatment as well as clinical efficacy.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this trial was granted by the Netherlands Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects. Introduction

Each year, approximately 2 00 000 women die from epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) worldwide.1 It is the most lethal of all gynaecological malignancies, with a 5-year survival rate of 38% for all stages combined. In the Netherlands, the 5-year overall survival (OS) for stage III/IV disease is 25%.2 Most EOC cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage due to the absence of early symptoms. Given the limitations of current screening methods, early detection remains unlikely in the near future. Therefore, research efforts should focus on improving treatment of advanced EOC.

Current initial management of advanced EOC consists of cytoreductive surgery combined with chemotherapy consisting of carboplatin/paclitaxel (CP).3 While a good initial response can be achieved in most patients who undergo this intensive treatment, relapses are common.4 These subsequent relapses tend to be less susceptible to treatment and consequently, most patients eventually die of the disease.

The duration of response after initial treatment is an important predictor for OS. Several treatments that can prolong this interval have been developed in recent years, such as hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase-inhibitors.5 6 Some of these have translated into OS benefit, yet durable responses are still rare. Immunotherapy, known for its potential to induce long-term responses in some cancers, could be the solution to this problem.

Immunotherapy in ovarian cancer

Although EOC is an immunogenic disease, immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has not lived up to the promise that was raised by its success in other cancer types.7,9 One explanation is the genetic landscape of EOC, characterised by a low number of somatic missense mutations.10 Efficacy of ICIs relies on the presence of these missense mutations leading to expression of aberrant proteins, so-called neoantigens.11 Because neoantigens are scarce in EOC, enhancing the spontaneous immune response with ICIs is insufficient to induce clinical responses in most patients. Instead, effective immune therapy for these patients should induce de novo responses to immunogenic antigens in tumour cells.

Such tumour antigens can be either tumour-specific antigens (expressed only in cancer cells), such as neo-antigens, or tumour-associated antigens (TAAs, overexpressed in cancer cells but also present in healthy cells).12 Targeting TAAs provides the distinct advantage of being able to use an off-the-shelf antigen source. However, major downsides are that the immunogenicity of these self-antigens is limited by central tolerance and the use of a single target antigen leaves the door open to immune evasion by downregulation of the chosen target.13

Therefore, we believe that neoantigen-specific approaches should be explored for patients with EOC to ensure a strong and broad immune response.

Next generation dendritic cell therapy

Numerous trials have established antigen-specific immunotherapy with autologous dendritic cells (DCs) as a safe method to induce tumour antigen specific T-cells in cancer.14 DCs are specialised antigen presenting cells with an important role in the initiation of antitumour immune responses. Because the DC function is impaired in cancer patients in vivo,15 16 therapy with in vitro antigen-loaded and matured DCs was developed to circumvent this defect and restore cancer immunity.17 Most DC therapy trials to date have used monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs): DCs produced by culturing monocytes in vitro under conditions that promote their differentiation into DCs. In this (8–9 days long) culture period, they lose essential effector and migration functions, hampering their efficacy.18 In recent years, there has been a trend towards the use of naturally occurring immature DCs isolated directly from blood.19 These cells only require a maturation step of several hours instead of an extensive differentiation process lasting multiple days, which better preserves their functional capabilities.20 21

The specialised DC subset, conventional DCs type 1 (cDC1s), is considered pivotal in cancer immunotherapy because of its capability for cross-presentation, that is, presentation of exogenous material such as tumour cell debris, via Major Histocompatibility Complex(MHC) class I.22 These cDC1s are characterised phenotypically by lack of expression of common lineage markers, high expression of MHC class II and expression of CLEC9A and CD141 (BDCA-3). In tumour-bearing mice, treatment with cDC1s has been shown to be superior in inducing tumour regression compared with treatment with moDC.23 Until now, it has not been possible to isolate cDC1s in sufficient quantities for use in clinical trials due to their rarity (~0.04% of circulating mononuclear cells in blood).24 Recent developments in Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant cell sorting have made it possible to purify these cells from peripheral blood for clinical application.

Here, we describe the NEODOC study, in which we will investigate for the first time the use of cDC1s for neoantigen-directed immunotherapy in cancer patients. Methods and analysis

Study objectives

The primary objective of NEODOC is to determine the immunological efficacy of cDC1s loaded with autologous tumour lysate (hereafter abbreviated as XP-DC) in patients with advanced EOC treated with (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy and interval debulking surgery (IDS). We will perform extensive immunomonitoring by analysing peripheral blood cells as well as tumour tissue and determine antigen-specificity in T-cells cultured from skin biopsies of XP-DC-challenged sites.

Secondary objectives are the safety and feasibility of manufacturing and administering XP-DC in this patient population. Although this single-arm study is not powered to show clinical efficacy, clinical outcome will be reported as part of the secondary objectives.

Study design

NEODOC is an investigator-initiated, exploratory, first-in-human, non-controlled phase I/II trial, which will be conducted at the Radboudumc, a university hospital in Nijmegen, the Netherlands. We aim to include 10 patients with advanced EOC (FIGO stage IIIb–IVb) who will receive a total of five XP-DC administrations during neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed for this exploratory study. The sample size of 10 patients was selected to evaluate immunogenicity, feasibility and safety. This study is not designed to detect statistically significant clinical outcomes. An overview of the study is provided in figure 2 and in online supplemental figure 1. Before chemotherapy is initiated, patients will undergo a surgical tumour biopsy (~ 1 cm3) and a leukapheresis procedure to collect the source materials for XP-DC production. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is administered according to routine care consisting of carboplatin (area under the curve 6) and paclitaxel (175 mg/m2) (CP) intravenously every 3 weeks. From cycle 2 onwards, patients will receive intranodal XP-DC injections on day 14 of each cycle. Patients will undergo IDS followed by three more CP cycles with XP-DC injections. Throughout the treatment schedule and during follow-up, biomaterials will be collected for monitoring of the immune response. Patient recruitment started on 17 March 2023 and is anticipated to be completed by 31 December 2025. The planned study completion date is 31 December 2026.

Study endpoints

Primary study endpoint

The primary endpoint of this study is the number of patients with an immunological response to XP-DC therapy. Immunologically responding patients are defined as: T cells cultured from XP-DC challenged sites (1) can be expanded and (2) express T cell receptors specific for the XP-DC product and (3) show effector functions measured by IFN-gamma secretion or cytolytic activity against tumour antigen expressing target cells.

Study population

Patients with advanced EOC requiring neoadjuvant chemotherapy will be included prior to the start of initial treatment. Eligible patients must have FIGO stage IIIb, IIIc, IVa or IVb disease, provided that supradiaphragmatic or inguinal lymph nodes are ≤1 cm. Eligibility criteria are summarised in table 1 and the full list of eligibility criteria is available in the study protocol (V.5.1, March 2025, provided as online supplemental material 1). Information about this study will be provided to patients after the diagnosis of EOC, and the indication for neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been established. Alternatively, patients with a high suspicion of advanced ovarian cancer who will undergo a diagnostic operative procedure during which biopsies will be taken can be asked to provide consent for taking additional biopsies during this procedure using a separate informed consent form. If such patients are found to meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria, they will receive information about the main study.

Study medication

XP-DC manufacturing

XP-DC manufacturing will take place at the Radboudumc in compliance with GMP for advanced therapy medicinal products. First, autologous tumour lysate used for XP-DC production is obtained. A fresh tumour biopsy is cut into fragments of 1–3 mm3 and digested for 30–40 min with collagenase and DNAse. Tumour cells are lysed by five repetitive freeze-thaw cycles. The end product is stored at ≤−80°C in CryoS vials. Separate aliquots are stored for quality control purposes. Quality control testing of the tumour lysate end product includes viability (no viable tumour cells should be present) and sterility. Furthermore, total protein content of the tumour lysate end product must be ≥40 µg to meet the minimum requirement for producing multiple XP-DC doses.

The leukapheresis product used as starting material in XP-DC production will be tested for total white blood cell and differentiation counts before being stored overnight at room temperature, with a maximum hold time of 22 hours. CD141+ DCs are enriched in two subsequent selection steps using a CliniMACS Prodigy (Miltenyi Biotec). First, in a negative selection step, the product will be depleted of CD19+ B-cells, CD3+ T-cells and CD14+ monocytes. Second, in a positive selection step, CD141+ DCs are selected. After cell amount, viability and purity of the intermediate product are confirmed, CD141+ DCs are sequentially incubated with the control antigen keyhole limpet haemocyanin (KLH) overnight at 37°C, 5% CO2 and subsequently with autologous tumour lysate (minimally 10 µg and maximally 100 µg of protein per 1×106 XP-DC) for 6 hours. During incubation with tumour lysate, nanoplexed poly I:C (BO-112; Highlight Therapeutics) is added to the culture medium as maturation agent. Finally, XP-DCs are harvested and formulated in cryopreservation medium CryoStor CS10 in CellSeal Cryogenic vials for long term storage at <150°C. Quality control testing of the end product consists of appearance, total cell number, cell viability, identity (phenotype), potency, endotoxin and sterility testing. Samples are taken right before cryopreservation, besides sterility test samples.

Product characteristics

An XP-DC product meeting the release criteria is a sterile cell suspension containing 5×105–20×105 viable XP-DC (lineage marker negative, CD141+) per vial. Before cryopreservation, viability is ≥70%, the activation marker CD83 is expressed on ≥50% of CD141+cells and the cells have a T-cell Activation Potency Index ≥2.0 in a mixed lymphocyte reaction.25 Endotoxin concentration is below 7 EU/mL.

Should a batch of XP-DCs be manufactured with an out of specification (OOS) test result, it can be administered in the exceptional case that the risk/benefit ratio of the OOS batch is considered positive by both the manufacturer and the trial physician, and the patient provides informed consent. The Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects via CTIS and the Dutch Health and Youth Care Inspectorate will be notified.

XP-DC administration

A thrombocyte count is obtained prior to DC administration to exclude severe thrombocytopenia due to chemotherapy. XP-DC will be injected in a clinically tumour-free inguinal lymph node under ultrasound guidance by a trained radiologist. Successful intranodal delivery will be confirmed and documented by acquiring a static ultrasound image of the position of the needle at the time of administration. After administration, patients will remain under close clinical supervision for 30 min.

Dose modification

Based on our experience with DC therapy, we do not expect severe treatment-related AEs to occur. In the case of an AE of CTCAE grade ≥3 that is plausibly related to the study treatment, DC treatment will be resumed only after the AE has resolved to grade 1. Subsequent administrations shall take place with a dose reduction of 50% and with DCs generated without the control antigen KLH. If the AE persists despite these measures, DC administrations will be aborted.

Trial assessments

Immunological assessments

Three types of biomaterials for immunomonitoring will be collected at different timepoints in the study: (1) tumour tissue at baseline, during interval cytoreductive surgery and if possible after progression, (2) peripheral blood at regular intervals during treatment and follow-up and (3) skin biopsies from XP-DC challenged sites using a delayed type hypersensitivity (DTH) test.26 A complete schedule of the timing of sampling is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Tumour tissue will be analysed for the presence of different pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory types of infiltrating immune cells using our multiplex immunohistochemistry pipeline.27 It will also be used for analysis of the mutational profile of the tumour using whole exome sequencing.

Safety assessment

We will assess patient safety throughout the study by monitoring WHO performance status, the presence of AEs (reported according to CTCAE V.4.03) during clinic visits and performing physical examination. In addition, we will assess organ function by laboratory testing prior to study treatment and before each cycle of chemotherapy. A complete blood count with leucocyte differentiation and routine biochemical tests including liver function, renal function, electrolyte balance, albumin and C reactive protein levels will be obtained.

Clinical assessment

Clinical assessment will take place according to routine clinical care. Serum Cancer Antigen 125 (CA-125) levels are measured during the treatment phase and follow-up. Radiological evaluation with a CT scan of chest and abdomen will be performed prior to the start of chemotherapy, after two or three cycles to determine eligibility for cytoreductive surgery and after completion of adjuvant chemotherapy. Pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is reported by the pathologist as part of routine care. During follow-up, no routine clinical imaging is performed. When disease recurrence is clinically or biochemically suspected, a CT of chest and abdomen is performed for confirmation.

Data analysis

Because this exploratory study was not designed or powered to test any hypothesis, statistics will be descriptive in nature. Description of the immunological data will include means and SD in case of normally distributed data and medians with 90% CIs or ranges in other cases. Estimations of progression free survival (PFS) and OS after 1 year will be performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. PFS is defined as time from apheresis to the date of disease progression or death, whichever occurs first. Disease progression is defined based on response evaluation criteria using RECIST V.1.1 and/or Gynecological Cancer Intergroup (GCIG) CA-125 criteria, as recommended by the GCIG.28 For exploratory purposes, clinical and immunological findings will be compared with a prospective cohort of comparable patients who do not receive DC therapy.

Patient and public involvement

The Olive Foundation (a patient advocacy organisation for women with gynaecological cancer) was consulted during the conceptualisation and design phase of this trial and they supported the grant proposal. During the conduct and reporting of the trial, patients or the public will not be involved.

Ethics and dissemination

The conduct of this trial will be in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (9 October 2004), the principles of the International Council for Harmonization and the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (1 December 1999). It was also compliant with the European Union Clinical Trials Directive (2001/20/EC) until 2024 when it was transitioned to the Clinical Trials Regulation under EUCT number 2024-512353-24-01. The trial was approved by the Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (medical research ethics committee) under registration number NL75563.000.22 and reviewed by the Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport (competent authority). All patients will provide written informed consent before undergoing any study-related procedures. Examples of both the pre-screening and main study informed consent forms are provided as supplementary material (onlinesupplemental material 2 3 respectively, forms in Dutch). A Data Safety Monitoring Board will not be installed for this exploratory study.

Data storage will be compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation. Clinical data will be captured in an electronic Case Report Form (Castor EDC) in a pseudonymised manner using a unique patient code that can only be traced back to patients using the study log to which access is restricted to directly involved study staff. Immunological data and human materials for translational research will also be stored using the same patient code. The XP-DC product and biomaterials used for XP-DC production (eg, autologous tumour lysate) will be labelled with identifying patient information in a secure location according to GMP requirements. Deidentified individual participant data (IPD) will be made available through the Radboud Data Repository under restricted access. Metadata will be findable and publicly accessible, while access to IPD will be granted to qualified researchers on reasonable request. The trial will be monitored by an independent monitoring team from Radboudumc. Monitoring activities will include verification of informed consent, protocol adherence and completeness of clinical and laboratory data.

Study results will be submitted for publication in an international peer-reviewed open-access journal and presented at national or international conferences.

Discussion

Effective therapeutic immunisation for ovarian cancer requires a robust method to present relevant immunogenic tumour antigens to patients’ naive T-cells. To this end, we propose NEODOC, investigating the immunological efficacy of XP-DCs for cancer immunotherapy. We choose immunological efficacy as the primary endpoint for this first-in-human trial, because the decision to proceed with development of XP-DC depends first and foremost on its ability to induce an immune response. We did not include safety as a (co-)primary endpoint, as would be typical in a phase I study, based on the ample experience with the safety of DC therapy. No major safety concerns were reported in a large number of DC therapy trials spanning over two decades and across various combinations of DC types and antigens.14 29 Although no DC product consisting exclusively of cDC1s has been used in a clinical trial before, we do not expect a sudden change in this safety profile. Nevertheless, we will report safety as a secondary endpoint, along with several other clinical parameters.

In the study design, we have leveraged the knowledge obtained from previous DC therapy studies to optimise the key factors known to influence efficacy: the type of DC and antigen source and the timing and route of administration.

First, to achieve the optimal DC product, we use cDC1s loaded with autologous tumour lysate as antigen source. The potential value of cDC1s for cancer immunotherapy was suspected soon after they were discovered, based on their capability for cross-presentation of extracellular protein antigens to CD8+ T-cells.22 In subsequent preclinical studies, tumour lysate-loaded cDC1s were shown to enhance tumour infiltration with cancer-reactive T-cells and to prevent or halt tumour growth in a murine cancer graft model.30 Another murine study found superior antitumour efficacy of a similar cDC1 product compared with moDCs.23 We will use tumour lysate as antigen source for cDC1 loading rather than an off-the-shelf antigen, to ensure that the patient-specific neoantigens of EOC patients are covered in the DC product.

Second, optimal timing in the disease course as well as delivery of a DC product is crucial to allow for optimal clinical efficacy. Our choice to treat patients in the primary setting is based on the hypothesis that DC therapy may be more effective in this context than in advanced recurrent disease. Advanced recurrent EOC is typically characterised by a higher tumour burden and increased tumour-induced immune suppression, mediated by suppressive immune cells and soluble factors.31,33 Combining DC therapy with standard of care CP has the advantage that chemotherapy not only decreases tumour burden, but also selectively depletes immunosuppressive cells like myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T-cells (Tregs) and leads to release of tumour antigens. These effects combined shift the balance towards a more pro-inflammatory state in which XP-DCs can be more effective. In our study, XP-DCs will be administered 14 days after CP chemotherapy, because after this interval MDSCs and Tregs are still depleted, while levels of immune effector cells have already recovered.31 32 The route of administration of DC matters too. Intranodal injection is preferred because, unlike intravenous or intradermal injection, it ensures more than a small minority of cells reach the lymph node, their site of action.34

The design choices outlined above, aimed at optimising efficacy rather than convenience, will introduce two main challenges during the study. First, the scarcity of cDC1s in blood makes it challenging to obtain sufficient numbers to meet our prespecified criteria. Although our validation runs have consistently shown a sufficient yield in healthy volunteers, it is unknown if the same holds true for our study population. Second, our approach to antigen loading with tumour lysate requires a considerable volume of tumour tissue. While the study protocol does not include a mandatory diagnostic laparoscopy if another tissue acquisition method is feasible, many participants are expected to require laparoscopic surgery for study purposes only. This brings an additional burden to the patient and, from a healthcare system perspective, extra costs. Both these challenges will need to be overcome during the execution of this study as well as towards further development of this approach into randomised trials on a positive result of the study.

Because we take an antigen-agnostic approach in this study, demonstrating antigen-specific T-cells is not as straightforward as it is in studies with known target antigens. We will use two monitoring approaches: an antigen-agnostic assay measuring T-cell activation on stimulation with XP-DC or tumour lysate loaded autologous cells and a known-antigen assay that will involve antigen-prediction based on tumour sequencing. This second method will reveal which (patient-specific) antigens are immunogenic and whether particular antigen-specific immune responses are shared between patients. If immunogenic antigens are indeed unique to each patient, this will support our current choice for producing a DC product based on autologous tumour material. But if antigens are shared between patients, this would provide the rationale for further studies with an off-the-shelf approach based on one or more of the found antigens. Therefore, the results from this study will guide further development of cDC1-based cellular immunotherapy as well as antigen-specific immunotherapy for ovarian cancer in a broader sense.
