Host-pathogen interactions in tuberculosis: from immunological mechanisms to translational applications
Authors: Qiyao Chai, Zehui Lei, Cui Hua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis (TB), which is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains the leading cause of death from infectious diseases globally. The complexity of host-Mtb interactions poses a fundamental challenge to developing effective prevention and control measures. Recent advances in multi-omics, spatiotemporal biology, and single-cell technologies have yielded transformative insights into Mtb infection dynamics and its intricate crosstalk with the host immune system. In this review, we will first introduce the current knowledge of Mtb-host immunological interplay and discuss it from three interconnected perspectives, including the balance between protective immunity and immunopathology, the coordination between local and systemic immunity, and the contribution of Mtb-induced immune dysregulation to TB comorbidities. We then further highlight translational opportunities via targeting the host-Mtb interface, including predictive biomarkers for Mtb infection and disease progression, next-generation vaccines for immune restoration and TB prevention, and precision strategies to overcome drug resistance and improve treatment outcomes. By integrating the latest discoveries and discussing key unresolved questions, we seek to provide an updated overview of TB pathogenesis with an aim to spur the development of transformative tools for TB control.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), a major lung infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains the top infectious killer worldwide, responsible for approximately 1.23 million deaths and 10.7 million incident cases in 2024 (WHO, 2025a). Notably, drug-resistant Mtb is categorized as a critical priority on the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, accounting for approximately 13% of global antimicrobial resistance-related deaths (Farhat et al., 2024; WHO, 2024b). The persistent high burden of TB and the rise of drug-resistant Mtb strains underscore the urgent need for improved diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic tools. Addressing this need, however, will require a deeper understanding of TB pathogenesis.

Mtb is an exquisitely adapted pathogen that has co-evolved with humans for eons, evolving sophisticated strategies to establish lifelong persistent infection. Following aerosol transmission, most immunocompetent individuals develop asymptomatic latent TB infection (LTBI), with bacilli contained in stable, organized granulomas, which are specialized immune cell aggregates. Nevertheless, 5%–10% of latently infected individuals will eventually develop active TB due to granuloma progression and necrosis, with risk increasing markedly in settings of immune dysregulation, such as immunosuppressive therapies or comorbidities like human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection and diabetes mellitus (Andrews et al., 2012; Narasimhan et al., 2013). Notably, accumulating evidence has revealed that Mtb infection presents as a continuous and reversible spectrum of states (Simmons et al., 2018; Zaidi et al., 2023). This spectrum is shaped by the complex interplay between Mtb and host immunity. However, the mechanisms underlying this dynamic infection spectrum are not fully understood, thus hindering the development and optimization of more effective tools for TB prevention and control.

Recent advances in single-cell multi-omics, spatiotemporal biology, and other cutting-edge technologies have profoundly reshaped our understanding of TB pathogenesis, revealing substantial heterogeneity in Mtb populations, host immune responses, and infectious microenvironments. This new knowledge has illuminated complex crosstalk between Mtb and host. In this review, we synthesize current insights into Mtb-host immunological interactions through three interconnected paradigms: 1) the intertwined nature of protective immunity and immunopathology; 2) the bidirectional coordination between local tissue immunity and systemic immune response; and 3) the Mtb-induced immune dysregulation that drives TB comorbidities. We further highlight translational opportunities at the host-pathogen interface, including predictive biomarkers for Mtb infection and disease progression, next-generation vaccines aimed at immune restoration and TB prevention, and precision strategies to overcome drug resistance and improve treatment outcomes. Our purpose is to provide new insights into TB pathogenesis with an aim to accelerate the development of transformative tools to end the global TB epidemic.

Protective vs. pathogenic immunity

Host immunity against Mtb acts as a double-edged sword: while indispensable for bacterial control, it can also drive tissue damage and disease progression due to active manipulation of the host response by Mtb. Here, we first discuss how Mtb subverts host protective immune mechanisms, including evasion of innate immune surveillance, disruption of antigen-specific adaptive responses, and induction of immune exhaustion. We then examine the immunopathogenic consequences that follow these subversive events, including chronic inflammation, tissue destruction, and aberrant remodeling.

Mtb subversion of host protective immunity

Mtb evasion of innate immune surveillance

Innate immune cells constitute the first line of host defense against bacterial pathogens, mounting prompt responses during early infection (Li et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025a). This immune surveillance is enabled by their effective sensing of microbial components via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). For decades, research on innate immune recognition of bacterial pathogens has focused predominantly on classical surface-expressed PRRs, and this body of work has been extensively reviewed (Li & Wu, 2021; Miyake, 2007). More recently, cytosolic immune receptors have emerged as critical players in host defense against intracellular pathogens including Mtb, making this a rapidly evolving field. For instance, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) is a conserved cytosolic DNA sensor that initiates innate immune responses via the adaptor protein mediator of IRF3 activation (MITA), which was independently identified and named stimulator of interferon genes (STING), and is also referred to as N-terminal methionine-proline-tyrosine-serine plasma membrane tetraspanner (MPYS) or endoplasmic reticulum interferon stimulator (ERIS) (Ishikawa & Barber, 2008; Jin et al., 2008; Sun et al., 2009; Zhong et al., 2008). During infection, cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS detects cytosolic mycobacterial DNA to activate antibacterial selective autophagy in macrophages (Watson et al., 2015). cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS signaling is also required for dendritic cell (DC) activation in response to mycobacterial DNA in vitro, but this dependency is largely lost during infection with intact Mtb, probably due to alternative PRR pathways that can compensate to drive DC maturation (Marinho et al., 2018). In parallel, Mtb DNA and secretory products also activate inflammasomes, which are multiprotein complexes composed of cytosolic sensors [such as absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) and NACHT, LRR and PYD domains-containing protein 3 (NLRP3)], inflammatory caspases (primarily caspase-1), and the adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC). Inflammasome activation drives the proteolytic maturation and secretion of the proinflammatory cytokines [interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18], concurrently with the cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) to induce pyroptosis, an immune process that contributes to early control of Mtb infection (Chai et al., 2022; Qu et al., 2020). Thus, both cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS and inflammasome signaling pathways can contribute to host protection against Mtb in specific contexts.

However, as a highly adapted intracellular pathogen, Mtb has evolved sophisticated mechanisms to evade the cytosolic immune surveillance and establish long-term persistence within host cells. Regarding the cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS-autophagy axis, while Mtb initially activates this signaling and even promotes its own capture by autophagosomes (e.g., by directly recruiting ubiquitin via surface protein Rv1468c) at early infection stages, it subsequently deploys the effector PknG to inhibit autophagosome-lysosome fusion to avoid autophagolysosomal degradation (Chai et al., 2019; Ge et al., 2022). This “early activation, late inhibition” temporal regulation probably allows Mtb to maintain its residence within membrane-bound autophagosomes, thereby avoiding detection by cytosolic immune receptors. Notably, although cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS signaling initiates the antibacterial autophagic cascade during the early phase of infection, persistent activation of this axis during chronic Mtb infection may drive sustained type I interferon (IFN) production. This excessive IFN response has been linked to neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) release, which paradoxically enhances Mtb survival and exacerbates pulmonary inflammatory tissue damage (Chowdhury et al., 2024). Similarly, Mtb also encodes multiple effector proteins that potently suppress the inflammasome-pyroptosis signaling pathway. We recently showed that the Mtb eukaryotic-like lipid phosphatase PtpB is allosterically activated by host ubiquitin binding. Activated PtpB then dephosphorylates host plasma membrane phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P) and PI(4,5)P2, thereby preventing the membrane localization of the GSDMD N-terminal fragment and inhibiting pyroptosis (Chai et al., 2022). Another Mtb eukaryotic-like kinase, PknG, phosphorylates the heme-oxidized IRP2 ubiquitin ligase 1L (HOIL-1L) subunit of linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC) (Yu et al., 2025). This phosphorylation blocks LUBAC-mediated linear ubiquitination of ASC, impairing NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, while simultaneously promoting HOIL-1L-dependent K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NLRP3.

Importantly, in phagocytes such as macrophages and DCs, the primary host cells of Mtb, although Mtb resides within membrane-bound phagosomes after phagocytosis, it triggers cytosolic immune recognition via phagosomal membrane damage and consequent leakage of bacterial contents. This damage is mediated by the ESAT-6 secretion system-1 (ESX-1) type VII secretion system, which not only enables Mtb to evade degradation following phagolysosomal fusion but also induces host cell lysis (Osman et al., 2022). The host counteracts this damage via endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-mediated membrane repair and, more recently discovered, via stress granules that serve as “plugs” to seal phagosomal pores (Bussi et al., 2023; Lopez-Jimenez et al., 2018). Despite these advances, the dynamic balance between Mtb-induced damage and host repair mechanisms that governs Mtb-containing vacuole homeostasis remains incompletely understood. Moreover, the key molecular determinants that govern the spectrum of vacuolar damage and cytosolic access states—whether Mtb remains sequestered within vacuoles to establish persistence or disrupts vacuolar membranes to trigger cell lysis and dissemination—remain largely unclear. Answering this question will provide critical insights into the mechanisms underlying the switch between Mtb latent infection and active disease.

Mtb also exploits a spectrum of host non-immune cell populations as privileged intracellular niches to escape host immune surveillance, enhance stress resistance, and enable antibiotic tolerance. A primary example among these reservoir cells is mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), which are recruited to the periphery of TB granulomas during infection (Raghuvanshi et al., 2010). Unlike in macrophages where Mtb mainly resides in phagosomes, Mtb promotes host lipid synthesis and resides within lipid droplets in the cytosol of MSCs, a strategy that supports bacterial latency in these cells (Fatima et al., 2020). In fact, the intrinsically immunosuppressive phenotype of MSCs, coupled with their abundant expression of ABC-family drug efflux transporters, appears to blunt cytosolic sensor-initiated immune clearance and foster drug tolerance (Jain et al., 2020). Adipocytes constitute another potential reservoir for Mtb persistence, since infected mice treated with standard anti-TB drugs failed to clear the bacterium from adipose depots (Agarwal et al., 2014). This finding was further supported by the observation that Mtb residing in human visceral adipocytes highly expresses dormancy markers, highlighting the ability of adipocytes to provide a sheltered niche (Garduno-Torres et al., 2025). Moreover, Mtb has been detected in diverse other non-immune cells, including bone marrow-resident hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), lung epithelial cells, lymphatic endothelial cells, and even neural cells, further expanding the spectrum of protective niches (Davis et al., 2019; Tornack et al., 2017). These non-immune cells commonly possess defective cytosolic immune responsiveness, which fuel persistent bacillary survival and shelter subpopulations that evade both endogenous immune elimination and standard chemotherapy. Collectively, these heterogeneous cellular niches create tissue-specific microenvironments that differentially affect Mtb infection outcomes, tipping the balance toward either long-term asymptomatic latency or the onset of active TB.

In addition, recent years have seen the discovery of numerous novel immune receptors that recognize various invading pathogens across various cell types, such as the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), which senses not only environmental pollutants but also bacterial pigmented virulence factors (Moura-Alves et al., 2014); alpha-protein kinase 1 (ALPK1), a sensor for bacterial ADP-heptose (Zhou et al., 2018); apoptotic protease-activating factor 1 (Apaf-1), which recognizes cytosolic dsDNA (Ruan et al., 2025); and reticulophagy regulator 1 (RETREG1, also known as FAM134B), a cytosolic lipopolysaccharide receptor (Cheng et al., 2026). While these receptors were discovered in diverse infected cells, their identification prompts the question of whether additional, as-yet-unidentified cytosolic immune sensors also exist for Mtb, a pathogen with unique biological features (e.g., its glycolipid-rich cell wall, specialized type VII secretion systems, and extensive repertoire of eukaryotic-like effector proteins). Exploring Mtb-specific sensors and their immune evasion mechanisms in different host cell types represents an attractive direction that will advance our understanding of TB pathogenesis.

Mtb disruption of antigen-specific adaptive responses

Recognition of Mtb by innate immune cells further initiates adaptive immunity, leading to T cell-mediated Mtb-specific immune responses. Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell immune responses play important roles in host control of Mtb infection (Bromley et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2021). During these processes, DCs act as the most critical antigen-presenting cells (APCs), which mediate the initial presentation of Mtb antigens to draining lymph nodes, where they prime naïve T cells to launch adaptive immune responses (Wolf et al., 2008). A hallmark of TB immunity, however, is the profound delay in the onset of adaptive responses, indicating impaired crosstalk between DCs and T cells. Studies have shown that direct pulmonary delivery of activated Mtb antigen-primed DCs to mice vaccinated with Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG), the only licensed vaccine for TB, upon Mtb challenge significantly accelerates the onset of vaccine-induced CD4+ T-cell responses, implicating delayed and dysregulated activation of mucosal DCs as a key bottleneck in antigen presentation and subsequent T cell priming (Griffiths et al., 2016). One explanation for this impaired lung DC activation is that Mtb blocks phagosome acidification and autophagic flux, thereby inhibiting major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II-restricted antigen presentation required to drive CD4+ T cell responses (Jagannath et al., 2009; Saini et al., 2016). Furthermore, DCs have been shown to export Mtb antigens via kinesin 2-dependent vesicular trafficking, which diverts antigens away from the MHC class II pathway and consequently reduces CD4+ T cell priming (Srivastava et al., 2016). Notably, Mtb-dampened CD4+ T cell responses further compromise optimal CD8+ T cell immunity during infection, as CD4+ T cells provide critical helper functions (Lu et al., 2021). Beyond defects in cellular pathways of antigen presentation, the delayed T cell response in TB has also been attributed to impaired DC migration to lung-draining mediastinal lymph nodes (Curtis et al., 2015; Wolf et al., 2008). However, the precise mechanisms by which Mtb subverts DC migration and the key pathogenic mediators involved remain poorly defined. Future studies employing single-cell technologies will be critical for identifying the specific DC subsets responsible for transferring Mtb antigens to lymph nodes.

Another major unresolved question is that even once T cells are activated later in infection, Mtb still prevents infected cells from being recognized and eliminated by these effector T cells. Specifically, Mtb antigen-specific CD4+ T cells (e.g., those recognizing the Ag85B240-254 or ESAT63-17 epitopes) have been shown to recognize infected macrophages in vitro and promote bacterial clearance (Yang et al., 2018). However, in vivo studies using animal models and clinical samples indicate that CD4+ T cells fail to adequately infiltrate TB granulomas, and remain in a suboptimally activated state due to insufficient local antigen exposure in the lung, rendering them unable to effectively eliminate Mtb-infected cells (Chai et al., 2026; Egen et al., 2011). By contrast, the CD8+ T cell response undergoes suppression through distinct mechanisms. Mtb was recently shown to upregulate phosphoserine aminotransferase SerC, which increases D-serine production under hypoxic conditions and subsequently suppresses IFN-γ production by CD8+ T cells in mice (Cheng et al., 2024). Furthermore, in vitro co-culture studies have demonstrated that several well-characterized Mtb-specific CD8+ T cell clones (including those specific for the TB10.44-11, TB10.420-28, and Mtb32A309–318 epitopes) are poor at recognizing Mtb-infected macrophages (Mott et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2018). Intriguingly, these Mtb-specific CD8+ T cells do recognize APCs pulsed directly with the corresponding peptides, and even cells infected with recombinant Listeria monocytogenes expressing these Mtb antigens—a cytosolic pathogen that readily lyses phagosomes and delivers antigens to the MHC class I processing pathway. These findings suggest that Mtb may evade CD8+ T cell recognition by blocking the release of antigens from phagosomes into the cytosol, where they would be processed by the proteasome for loading onto MHC class I molecules. Additionally, the characterized epitopes might act as decoy antigens that divert the immune response. Notably, while recent immunopeptidomics have systematically identified potential antigens presented on MHC-II in Mtb-infected human DCs, and many of them have been validated to elicit CD4+ T cell responses (Leddy et al., 2025), the MHC-I-restricted antigenic peptides discovered so far remain largely unverified as bona fide CD8+ T cell epitopes capable of being recognized on infected cells (Leddy et al., 2023). This discrepancy is intriguing given that both polyclonal CD4+ and CD8+ T cells isolated from the lungs of Mtb-infected mice readily recognize infected macrophages in vitro (Lu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2018). Therefore, it would be valuable to further elucidate how Mtb evades CD8+ T cell recognition and activation, and to systematically identify authentic CD8+ T cell epitopes that drive immune recognition and protection.

Chronic Mtb infection-induced immune exhaustion

Persistent antigenic and inflammatory stimulation in chronic Mtb infection can drive immune cells into a dysfunctional state termed exhaustion, which diminishes both host protective immunity and vaccine effectiveness (Abers et al., 2019; Wykes & Lewin, 2018). Peripheral blood-based single-cell profiling indicates that T cells exhibit exhaustion signatures in TB patients (Pan et al., 2023). However, T cell-targeted checkpoint therapies such as programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) blockade, although beneficial in cancer treatment, paradoxically exacerbate TB progression by inducing lethal immunopathology (Barber et al., 2011; Kauffman et al., 2021). Clinically, PD-1 blockade used for cancer immunotherapy increases the risk of latent TB reactivation (Barber et al., 2019; Langan et al., 2020; Tezera et al., 2020). This discrepancy likely arises from the distinct immune features and pathological mechanisms of T cells in the TB microenvironment compared to those in the tumor microenvironment. Indeed, T cells within lung granulomas do not show significant upregulation of classical exhaustion markers, such as PD-1 and lymphocyte activating gene 3 (LAG-3) (Gideon et al., 2022; McCaffrey et al., 2022; Wong et al., 2018). Thus, TB-associated T cell exhaustion may involve distinct mechanisms. For instance, chronic Mtb infection upregulates the inhibitory receptor T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3) on T cells, and TIM-3 blockade restores T cell function and improves bacterial control in Mtb-susceptible mice (Jayaraman et al., 2016). This indicates that TIM-3 represents an Mtb infection-associated immune checkpoint molecule.

Interestingly, spatial transcriptomic studies of human TB granulomas have revealed that persistent Mtb infection is predominantly associated with exhaustion of the innate immune cells (particularly myeloid cells) rather than T cells (McCaffrey et al., 2022). These paradigm-shifting findings suggest that targeting innate immune checkpoints is a promising alternative strategy for TB immunotherapy. We recently identified the inhibitory receptor leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor B1 (LILRB1) as a critical checkpoint on natural killer (NK) cells, an innate lymphocyte subset capable of directly killing target infected cells in an MHC-independent manner (Wang et al., 2024). Mechanistically, LILRB1 blockade reduces apoptosis and enhances the cytotoxic activity of NK cells isolated from TB patients. Critically, this effect occurs without increasing the production of IFN-γ or tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), two cytokines whose overexpression drives the severe immunopathology associated with PD-1 checkpoint blockade in TB (Sakai et al., 2016; Tezera et al., 2020). Thus, LILRB1 blockade represents a promising strategy to restore NK cell-mediated anti-Mtb immunity without triggering detrimental hyperinflammation. Given that exhaustion phenotypes in TB granulomas are most prominent in myeloid cells, it would be valuable for future studies to further explore the exhaustion mechanisms of additional innate immune subsets and to identify TB-specific innate immune checkpoints, which may help expand the therapeutic options against Mtb infection.

Mtb-driven host immunopathology

Chronic inflammation and immunosenescence

Persistent Mtb infection acts as a chronic stressor that induces sustained low-grade pro-inflammatory signaling. This pathological inflammatory state is characterized by an increased monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio and elevated circulating and tissue levels of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ (Piergallini & Turner, 2018; Sibley et al., 2019). These immunological features closely mirror the hallmarks of age-related “inflammaging” (Franceschi et al., 2018). The induction of premature cellular senescence across innate and adaptive immune compartments represents a key mechanistic basis for Mtb-driven inflammaging-like chronic inflammation. Recent studies have demonstrated that Mtb infection promotes aberrant DNA hypermethylation of multiple genes involved in cellular senescence and inflammation, resulting in durable epigenetic modifications that persist for years even after successful completion of standard anti-TB therapy (Bobak et al., 2022; DiNardo et al., 2020). Importantly, senescent immune cells during chronic Mtb infection acquire a pro-inflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), releasing senescence-associated factors such as IL-6, C-X-C motif chemokine 9 (CXCL9), CXCL10, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) (Bobak et al., 2022; Shee et al., 2026). This SASP may establish a self-amplifying inflammatory loop that sustains both local and systemic inflammation.

The accumulation of senescent cells is not merely a consequence of chronic infection but actively contributes to disease progression. In a TB-susceptible mouse model, lung myeloid cells exhibit elevated senescence markers (e.g., senescence-associated β-galactosidase, p16, and p21) as early as 10 days post-infection, well before necrotic granuloma formation (Shee et al., 2026). Moreover, recent single-cell transcriptomic analysis of post-TB human lungs revealed a pan-cellular senescence signature, especially in pulmonary endothelial cells, which are characterized by increased thromboinflammation and reduced forkhead box O3 (FOXO3) expression (Sun et al., 2025). These senescent endothelial cells secrete SASP factors that may promote vascular leakage, fibrosis, and persistent pulmonary impairment. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Mtb-driven chronic inflammation and the ensuing immunosenescence are actionable drivers of TB pathogenesis. Targeting senescent cells therefore opens new avenues for adjunctive therapies that may accelerate bacterial clearance, mitigate long-term pulmonary damage, and improve treatment outcomes for both active TB and post-TB lung diseases.

Tissue necrosis and destruction

The hallmark pathological feature of active TB is necrotic granuloma formation and inflammatory tissue destruction in the lungs. A key mechanistic driver of this phenotype is the ability of Mtb to reprogram host cell death pathways, favoring excessive necrosis. Indeed, the coordinated interplay and homeostatic regulation of both non-inflammatory cell death (i.e., apoptosis) and pro-inflammatory programmed necrosis (i.e., necroptosis and pyroptosis) are critical for prompt initiation of host immune responses and control of infection (Yuan & Ofengeim, 2024). However, Mtb has evolved specialized strategies to disrupt these cell death pathways, leading to pathological outcomes that favor pathogen persistence and transmission. For instance, Mtb induces lipoxin A4 production, which inhibits biosynthesis of prostaglandin E2, a cytoprotective mediator that functions to prevent mitochondrial damage and repair the plasma membrane, thereby triggering necrosis in infected macrophages (Divangahi et al., 2010). We recently showed that Mtb effector protein mammalian cell entry 3C (Mce3C) binds to host cathepsin B, a lysosomal molecular determinant of cell death plasticity, to inhibit apoptosis while promoting necroptosis, thus enhancing Mtb intracellular survival and exacerbating host inflammatory pathology (Lu et al., 2025). Furthermore, it has been reported that ferroptosis, a form of regulated necrosis driven by accumulation of free iron and toxic lipid peroxides, significantly contributes to Mtb-induced pulmonary necrosis in acutely infected mice (Amaral et al., 2019). We further demonstrated that Mtb PtpA, a eukaryotic-like phosphatase, targets nuclear protein arginine methyltransferase 6 (PRMT6) to epigenetically repress the ferroptosis repressor glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), thus promoting this ferroptotic pathway (Qiang et al., 2023). In addition, the C-terminal domain of Mtb CpnT, an outer membrane protein secreted by the ESX-4 secretion system, functions as an Mtb exotoxin that induces necroptotic cell death by depleting intracellular nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) (Pajuelo et al., 2021).

In recent years, novel forms of regulated cell death, such as cuproptosis, disulfidptosis, and mitoxoperilysis have been continuously discovered (Liu et al., 2023b; Tsvetkov et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). However, whether these newly identified mechanisms play a role in TB pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Moreover, Mtb infection induces distinct cell death modalities that vary depending on the infected cell type, bacterial load, and stage of infection. Thus, it remains to be elucidated whether Mtb has evolved unique, pathogen-specific cell death-inducing mechanisms that are distinct from the canonical paradigms triggered by physiological stimuli.

Besides direct manipulation of host cell death pathways, Mtb also induces dysregulated production of cytokines (e.g., Type I IFNs, IL-17, and TNF-α) that drives excessive inflammatory tissue damage and necrotic granuloma formation. Type I IFNs (IFN-α/β) have emerged as key pathogenic cytokines in TB pathogenesis. Unlike the protective role of type II IFN (IFN-γ), excessive type I IFN signaling during Mtb infection is strongly associated with severe pulmonary necrosis and disease progression in both mice and humans (Berry et al., 2010; Desvignes et al., 2012; Moreira-Teixeira et al., 2020). Mechanistically, Mtb triggers robust type I IFN production through multiple PRR pathways, including cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain 2 (NOD2)-dependent signaling (Pandey et al., 2009; Watson et al., 2015). This aberrant type I IFN response drives the recruitment and activation of neutrophils and inflammatory monocytes that contribute to tissue destruction (Branchett et al., 2025). Of note, type I IFN signaling within neutrophils promotes the formation of chromatin-containing vesicles that allow NET release without compromising plasma membrane integrity, which maintains neutrophil viability while promoting Mtb replication and lung caseation (Chowdhury et al., 2024). Furthermore, the excessive type I IFN response also synergizes with lipid peroxidation pathways to lock macrophages in a state of unresolving stress to promote granuloma necrosis (Yabaji, Zhernovkov, et al., 2025).

Another representative cytokine contributing to TB pathology is IL-17. IL-17 exhibits context-dependent roles in TB, with excessive and prolonged production driving severe immunopathology. While early IL-17 responses contribute to protective immunity by promoting granuloma formation, chronic IL-17 production during active TB exacerbates tissue damage through multiple mechanisms (Maseeme et al., 2026). For instance, IL-17 potently induces the secretion of chemokines (e.g., CXCL1 and CXCL2) that drive massive neutrophil infiltration into the lungs, and these neutrophils are not only inefficient at killing Mtb but also release tissue-damaging proteases and NETs (Lombard et al., 2016; Zlatar et al., 2024). Furthermore, IL-17 directly stimulates epithelial cells and fibroblasts to produce MMPs, which degrade lung parenchyma and promote cavitation (Singh et al., 2018). Recent studies have also identified Ly6G+ granulocytes as a major source of pathogenic IL-17 during chronic Mtb infection, creating a self-amplifying loop of inflammation and tissue destruction (Sharma et al., 2026).

In addition to type I IFNs and IL-17, TNF-α is also a key cytokine implicated in TB immunopathogenesis and tissue damage under certain conditions. TNF-α plays a paradoxical “double-edged sword” role in TB: while essential for protective immunity, it can also drive tissue necrosis when dysregulated. On the one hand, genetic ablation or antibody-mediated neutralization of TNF-α leads to unrestricted intracellular bacterial proliferation and necrotic progression of granulomatous lesions, thus promoting Mtb spread and aggravated pulmonary destruction (Clay et al., 2008; Flynn et al., 1995; Keane et al., 2001). On the other hand, excessive TNF-α production drives macrophage necrosis through a mitochondrial-lysosomal-endoplasmic reticulum circuit, leading to the release of viable bacilli and dissemination of infection (Roca et al., 2019; Roca & Ramakrishnan, 2013). Moreover, TNF-α promotes the expression of MMPs that degrade lung extracellular matrix, contributing to cavitation and tissue destruction (Elkington et al., 2005). In addition, the pathological consequences of dysregulated TNF-α are markedly exacerbated when IL-1 receptor 1 (IL-1R1) signaling is impaired, as loss of IL-1R1 abolishes the TNF-α-dependent antibacterial pathway and results in unrestricted infection and progressive tissue destruction (Di Paolo et al., 2015). Thus, the protective versus pathological outcome of TNF-α signaling in TB is shaped by both its production dynamics and its functional crosstalk with other cytokine axes, highlighting the need for context-dependent modulation of TNF-α bioactivity to balance host defense efficacy and immunopathological injury.

Collectively, both inflammatory cell death and cytokines drive tissue destruction in TB. Notably, inflammatory cell death promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while the synergistic effects of certain cytokines may in turn exacerbate cell death, creating a feedforward inflammatory cascade (Karki et al., 2021; Torres-Huerta et al., 2026). This cascade allows Mtb to transition from a state of immune evasion to aggressive pathogenesis. However, the mechanistic basis by which Mtb orchestrates such a biphasic strategy—early suppression of inflammatory immune responses to establish infection and sustain intracellular survival, followed by a late switch to promote excessive inflammatory cell necrosis and cytokine storm that drive tissue damage and pathogen dissemination—remains to be fully elucidated.

Lung remodeling and fibrosis

Mtb-induced inflammatory pathology triggers extensive pulmonary tissue remodeling, a host defense evolved to contain infection but often co-opted by the pathogen to drive disease progression. This aberrant remodeling directly impairs lung repair. Normal alveolar repair relies on the self-renewal of type II alveolar (AT2) stem cells and their differentiation into gas-exchanging type I alveolar (AT1) cells. However, Mtb induces dysplastic epithelial regeneration in susceptible hosts. In the B6.Sst1S mouse model of post-primary TB, advanced lesions accumulate de-differentiated Nkx2.1+ epithelial progenitors instead of functional alveolar cells, concurrent with robust activation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway (Yabaji, Lata, et al., 2025). These dysplastic epithelial cells secrete type I IFNs, which amplify maladaptive macrophage polarization and further disrupt repair signaling. Moreover, standard anti-TB drugs themselves may exacerbate these regenerative defects. Rifampicin, ethambutol, and levofloxacin reduce AT2 cell organoid-forming efficiency, while isoniazid and levofloxacin alter AT2-to-AT1 differentiation kinetics (Li et al., 2025). Thus, both pathogen-mediated and drug-induced disruption of alveolar stem cell function contribute to incomplete lung recovery despite successful bacterial clearance.

Dysregulated lung remodeling and repair also establish a permissive niche for Mtb replication and dissemination. Elevated MMP activity in TB granulomas, particularly MMP-1, MMP-8, and MMP-9, degrades the extracellular matrix, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of tissue destruction and pathogen expansion (Xu et al., 2018). Furthermore, bronchial occlusion by necrotic debris and fibrotic tissue facilitates bronchogenic spread of Mtb to adjacent airspaces, while hypoxic fibrotic microenvironments promote Mtb persistence and drug tolerance (Datta et al., 2024; Yabaji, Lata, et al., 2025). In addition, Mtb constructs a metabolic permissive niche by subverting host DNA repair pathways in the lung. Its effector urease C (UreC) binds host RuvB-like protein 2 (RUVBL2) to disrupt DNA repair, triggering genomic instability and micronuclei formation, which in turn activates the cGAS-MITA/STING/MPYS/ERIS-IFN-β axis to induce macrophage lipid droplet biogenesis, thus creating a nutrient-rich niche for prolonged Mtb survival (Liu et al., 2023a).

Finally, unresolved aberrant lung remodeling may further lead to irreversible long-term functional impairment. Even after successful bacterial eradication, excessive collagen deposition, alveolar destruction, and airway stenosis persist (Ravimohan et al., 2018). These pathological abnormalities manifest as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)-like phenotypes, characterized by impaired pulmonary diffusing capacity and reduced exercise tolerance (Ravimohan et al., 2018). Furthermore, a significant proportion of patients develop permanent structural lung damage despite curative anti-TB therapy, including cavitation, bronchiectasis, and diffuse fibrosis (Allwood et al., 2021). These sequelae pose persistent, life-altering health risks to survivors. Radiological studies report residual cavities in 20%–40% of treated TB patients, and such cavitation not only predisposes to TB recurrence but also serves as a nidus for secondary infections, such as chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, which occurs in 6%–14% of post-TB patients and is associated with high mortality (Bongomin, 2020; Lee et al., 2011). Despite this substantial clinical burden, the molecular mechanisms governing the chronic phase of aberrant repair and tissue regeneration in post-TB lungs remain poorly characterized. Addressing these underexplored pathways will help resolve two critical unresolved questions: why TB-induced pulmonary damage persists long after bacterial clearance, and why cured TB patients face markedly elevated risks of disease recurrence and lifelong pulmonary comorbidities.

Local vs. systemic immunity

Granulomas represent the primary site of host-Mtb confrontation within the lung, yet immune responses are not confined to this local niche, owing to extensive crosstalk between pulmonary and systemic compartments. Here we first dissect the local immune landscape inside granulomas, including its inherent heterogeneity, Mtb-mediated immune dysregulation, and microenvironmental determinants that shape disease outcomes. We then discuss how Mtb infection drives systemic immune reprogramming, encompassing alterations in circulating immune signatures, inter-organ immune crosstalk, and the establishment of systemic immune memory.

Local infectious microenvironment in the TB granuloma

Granuloma dynamics and heterogeneity

Tuberculous granulomas represent the core pathological and immunological lesions during Mtb infection, undergoing dynamic structural and cellular evolution throughout disease progression. Granulomas exhibit marked phenotypic heterogeneity even within the same host, with individual lesions following distinct trajectories in inflammatory state, cellular makeup, reactivation risk, and capacity for Mtb containment (Gideon et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2014). This lesion-to-lesion variability is tightly coupled to divergent Mtb fitness and stress states within the local microenvironment. Studies using bacterial reporter strains have confirmed that Mtb adopts distinct stress response programs and drug tolerance phenotypes across different macrophage subsets and granuloma microenvironments (Pisu et al., 2021). Single-cell RNA sequencing has further dissected this cellular heterogeneity, identifying multiple macrophage subpopulations, including IFN-responsive macrophages (CD163+CD206low) that are enriched in early-forming, high-burden granulomas and associated with disease progression, whereas CD163+CD206+ macrophages are more abundant in low-burden lesions during latent infection (Esaulova et al., 2021; Gideon et al., 2022). In addition to cellular heterogeneity, spatial mapping studies have uncovered additional structural diversity in human TB lungs. Alongside necrotizing granulomas, there are abundant non-necrotizing leukocyte aggregates adjacent to necrotic regions, such as B and T cell-enriched lymphoid aggregates, also known as tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) (Chai et al., 2026; Sawyer et al., 2023). Although their precise functional contributions during Mtb infection remain incompletely characterized, these TLSs are thought to orchestrate local adaptive immune responses, facilitating T cell-B cell cooperation and the localization of follicular helper T-like cells within the infected lung (Swanson et al., 2023). Collectively, the granuloma heterogeneity reflects a spectrum of local infectious niches that critically determine Mtb persistence, immune evasion, and treatment responsiveness, highlighting the need for lesion-directed therapeutic strategies.

Local immune dysfunction within the granuloma

Beyond compositional and structural diversity, the granuloma microenvironment functionally evolves into an immunosuppressive niche that progressively dampens protective host immunity and promotes Mtb persistence. This dysfunction is driven by extensive immunometabolic reprogramming across multiple immune cell populations. For instance, granuloma macrophages exhibit polarized metabolic shifts characterized by altered heme-iron uptake and catabolism, a state that probably provides a permissive niche for bacterial survival (Pisu et al., 2021). Moreover, although T cells within granulomas rarely acquire the canonical exhaustion phenotype marked by co-expression of inhibitory receptors, their effector functions are subject to dynamic regulation by local immunomodulatory circuits (e.g., B cell-mediated IL-10 signaling) and co-stimulatory signals (e.g., CD30 co-stimulation), resulting in compromised anti-mycobacterial cytokine secretion and diminished proliferative activity (Foreman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023a; Wong et al., 2018). This sustained immune suppression within granulomas allows continuous Mtb replication and persistent antigen stimulation, thus triggering chronic inflammatory cascades and exacerbating local immunopathology. The ensuing dysregulation of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine networks, marked by elevated IL-10, TGF-β, and type I IFN signaling, promotes progressive necrosis and caseation (Chowdhury et al., 2024; McCaffrey et al., 2026). Concurrently, metabolically reprogrammed macrophages, characterized by lipid accumulation and enhanced glycolysis, generate excessive reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, further amplifying oxidative tissue damage (Chai et al., 2026; McCaffrey et al., 2026). This self-reinforcing vicious cycle, which links immune dysfunction, bacterial persistence, and progressive tissue damage, ultimately consolidates active TB lesions and drives their deterioration.

Microenvironmental factors driving granuloma pathogenesis

Accumulating studies have identified a network of interconnected microenvironmental factors that orchestrate local immune responses within TB granulomas. Among these factors, aberrant lipid accumulation emerges as a key driver of local immune dysfunction within TB lesions. Our recent work demonstrates that Mtb induces dysregulated cholesterol trafficking in granuloma macrophages, driving lysosomal cholesterol accumulation that sequesters MHC-II molecules and thereby impairs local antigen presentation to enable optimal effector function of Mtb-specific CD4+ T cells at infection sites (Chai et al., 2026). In addition to mediating immune evasion, lipid overload drives the formation of foamy macrophages laden with triacylglycerols and cholesterol esters, constituting a nutrient-rich niche that supports Mtb persistence and promotes antibiotic tolerance (Daniel et al., 2011; Peyron et al., 2008). Notably, pharmacological restoration of cholesterol homeostasis during late-stage infection augments protective T cell responses and improves bacterial control (Chai et al., 2026), highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting host lipid metabolic pathways to reinstate local immune function and boost TB treatment efficacy.

Hypoxia represents another critical driver of granuloma pathogenesis. Multimodal spatial analyses of TB granulomas in nonhuman primates have revealed that the myeloid core bifurcates into normoxic and hypoxic metabolic zones, with the latter characterized by glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT)-expressing macrophages associated with high bacterial burden and limited T cell infiltration (McCaffrey et al., 2026). This hypoxic compartment seemingly exacerbates local immunosuppression by physically excluding lymphocytes from regions where Mtb-infected macrophages reside. Cellularly, hypoxia promotes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) stabilization in both myeloid cells and T cells with cell-type-specific effects. Specifically, in macrophages, HIF-1α fosters an immunosuppressive phenotype that impedes effector T cell infiltration into granuloma cores; in CD4+ T cells, HIF-1α intrinsically impairs their proliferation and effector function by antagonizing c-Myc signaling and upregulating inhibitory receptors (Bucsan et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022).

Microbial effectors and byproducts also actively shape granuloma pathogenesis through diverse structural and metabolic mechanisms. Mtb can form mechanoresilient, biofilm-like cords that physically disrupt intercellular junctions, facilitating bacterial dissemination into new tissue niches (Mishra et al., 2023). Concurrently, mycobacterial cellulose-anchored biofilms encase bacilli in a protective extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating evasion of host immune clearance and promoting drug tolerance (Chakraborty et al., 2021). Moreover, in the respiratory mucosal environment, metabolites (e.g., butyrate) generated by fermenting oral anaerobes accumulate in HIV-infected lungs, where they suppress IFN-γ and IL-17A production and thereby increase Mtb susceptibility (Segal et al., 2017).

In addition to host-intrinsic microenvironmental drivers and bacterial determinants, exogenous lifestyle and environmental exposures also profoundly reshape pulmonary immune landscapes and modulate granuloma fate. Tobacco smoke, a leading preventable risk factor accounting for ∼20% of global active TB cases, drives robust recruitment of immature, pro-inflammatory monocytes into the alveolar spaces via the chemokine axis, thereby establishing permissive niches for Mtb growth (Corleis et al., 2023). Chronic alcohol consumption, another well-established TB risk factor, augments type I IFN production by pulmonary myeloid cells, which further promotes necroptosis in alveolar macrophages and disrupts local immune barrier function, leading to increased mortality in Mtb-infected mice (Tripathi et al., 2018).

Despite substantial mechanistic progress, the spatiotemporal coordination and synergistic interplay among host, bacterial, and environmental determinants throughout granuloma evolution remain largely uncharacterized at single-cell spatial resolution. Future research that integrates spatial multi-omics, intravital dynamic imaging, and prospective clinical cohorts will help dissect the multilayered regulatory networks of granuloma pathogenesis and will provide a critical foundation for developing precision host-directed therapies.

Systemic immune reprogramming in TB

Infection status-associated circulating immune signatures

While the lung serves as the primary site of Mtb infection, disease progression drives broad alterations to peripheral immune cells and plasma immunometabolic mediators via the circulatory system, providing a readily accessible readout of systemic immune state. Active TB patients exhibit a distinct whole-blood transcriptional signature dominated by type I IFN-inducible and neutrophil-related genes, which correlates with radiographic disease severity and largely resolves following successful treatment (Berry et al., 2010; Moreira-Teixeira et al., 2020). At the proteomic level, plasma profiling reveals elevated proteins related to acute-phase response, lipid transport, and iron assimilation, reflecting systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation (Garay-Baquero et al., 2020; Mateos et al., 2020). Metabolomic analyses further show that TB patients have a markedly elevated kynurenine/tryptophan ratio and decreased retinol levels, which together constitute a metabolic signature (Collins et al., 2025). At the cellular level, TB patients exhibit a significant reduction in circulating NK cells (Roy Chowdhury et al., 2018). Furthermore, the remaining NK cells upregulate the inhibitory receptor LILRB1 and display impaired cytotoxicity alongside enhanced apoptosis (Cai et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024), highlighting the biomarker potential of specific cell-subset signatures.

Comparisons between active TB and LTBI reveal more nuanced differences. While ATB patients display a pronounced type I IFN signature, individuals with LTBI often show intermediate or heterogeneous transcriptional profiles (Berry et al., 2010; Singh et al., 2018). NK cell frequencies are significantly higher in LTBI than in ATB, and LILRB1 expression on NK cells is elevated only in active disease, suggesting that preservation of NK cell numbers and function may contribute to successful bacterial control (Roy Chowdhury et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024). Furthermore, longitudinal studies have identified potential circulating signatures that predict progression from LTBI to ATB. For instance, a 16-gene blood transcriptional risk signature predicted TB progression in the 12 months preceding diagnosis, with a sensitivity of 66.1% and a specificity of 80.6% (Zak et al., 2016). Similarly, metabolic changes, including an increasing kynurenine/tryptophan ratio and declining retinol, are detectable in progressors months prior to disease onset (Collins et al., 2025; Weiner et al., 2018). These findings collectively demonstrate that infection status-associated immune and metabolic signatures in peripheral blood not only reflect ongoing pathophysiology but also hold promise for early risk stratification and monitoring of TB progression.

In contrast to these progression-associated immune profiles, resistance-associated signatures are recently detected in a unique subset of highly Mtb-exposed individuals, referred to as TB resisters. These individuals sustain intense and repeated Mtb exposure yet remain persistently negative for both the tuberculin skin test (TST) and IFN-γ release assays (IGRA), and have not been observed to develop active TB disease during the available follow-up (Simmons et al., 2018). The persistent negativity of immunological diagnosis in response to Mtb antigens suggests that resisters may possess efficient innate immunity enabling early clearance of Mtb infection. Supportively, resisters' circulating monocytes exhibit superior anti-Mtb ability, characterized by altered transcriptional profiles involving epigenetic histone acetylation pathways (Seshadri et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021). We recently identified a unique plasma exosomal lncRNA profiling in resisters, among which we further characterized TB resister-derived CLOCK regulator 1 (TRCR1), which is an exosomal long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) secreted by lung epithelial cells upon Mtb antigen stimulation, and can be taken up by monocytes to promote CLOCK-mediated epigenetic histone acetylation of immune genes, thereby enhancing their anti-infectious capacity (Yu et al., 2026). Furthermore, the resister phenotype may also benefit from an IFN-γ-independent T cell response. While classical IFN-γ production is minimal upon Mtb stimulation, CD4+ T cells from resisters express CD40L and other non-canonical cytokines, indicating alternative modes of T cell help (Lu et al., 2019). Concurrently, a distinctive humoral response emerges. Resisters exhibit detectable immunoglobulin M (IgM) and class-switched IgG and IgA antibodies against Mtb-specific antigens, with enhanced antibody avidity and distinct IgG Fc-glycan profiles relative to LTBI individuals (Lu et al., 2019). In addition, metabolic and genetic factors may also contribute to the resister phenotype. Metabolically, resisters display a monocyte transcriptional program characterized by free fatty acid metabolism and adenosine 5′-monophosphate kinase (AMPK) signaling, distinguishing them from LTBI individuals (Simmons et al., 2021). Genetically, a genome-wide association study identified a protective locus at 10q26.2, with predicted functional consequences that enhance Th1-type responses, supporting a genetic basis for resistance (Quistrebert et al., 2021). Notably, TB resister phenotype is even observed in HIV-positive individuals. Bronchoalveolar lavage from HIV-positive resisters reveals M1-like pre-activated alveolar macrophages and a striking enrichment of poly-cytotoxic T lymphocytes co-expressing mycobactericidal granulysin, granzyme B, and perforin (Dallmann-Sauer et al., 2025). Collectively, these unique circulating immune signatures of TB resisters add a previously missing dimension to the spectrum of infection status-associated immune phenotypes, shedding light on natural protective mechanisms against Mtb and expanding the translational value of peripheral biomarkers for TB prevention and intervention.

Inter-organ immune crosstalk

In addition to the circulating immune signatures that reflect systemic host responses, Mtb infection also drives a broader orchestration of immune reprogramming that extends from the pulmonary niche to distal organs, establishing an intricate inter-organ crosstalk network. The lung-draining lymph node (LN) serves as a primary hub within this network, where the adaptive immune response is initiated in close connection with the lung. Chronic Mtb infection profoundly remodels the mouse mediastinal LN architecture, characterized by a disproportionate expansion of B cells that invade the paracortical T-cell zone. This B-cell accumulation, which occurs independently of CD4+ T cell help or IFN-γ signaling, displaces CCL21-expressing fibroblastic reticular cells and sequesters Mtb-containing myeloid cells, thus impairing activation of naïve CD4+ T cells in response to mycobacterial antigens (Daniel et al., 2022). Furthermore, Mtb remodels naïve lymphocyte trafficking to the lung-draining mediastinal LNs. During chronic infection, the canonical L-selectin-dependent entry pathway via high endothelial venules becomes functionally compromised; consequently, a substantial fraction of naïve T cells instead adopts an L-selectin-independent route through afferent lymphatics from the lung, a process sustained by infection-induced lymphangiogenesis (Daniel et al., 2023). In addition to subverting LN architecture and lymphocyte trafficking, Mtb also directly exploits lymphatic endothelial cells as a replicative niche by forming large intracellular cords that evade autophagic recognition, thereby enabling bacterial persistence (Lerner et al., 2020). Concurrently, a recent study reveals that Mtb also establishes monocytic niches within the LN to evade T cell surveillance, further perpetuating bacterial persistence and undermining the efficacy of local immune control (Shamskhou et al., 2026).

The gut-lung axis represents another critical dimension of this crosstalk. Pulmonary Mtb infection induces a rapid, pronounced gut microbial dysbiosis, featured with a loss of diversity and depletion of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing taxa, such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae (Winglee et al., 2014). This dysbiosis, independent of direct gut infection, appears driven by immune signaling from the lung to the gut. In turn, the altered gut microbiome actively modulates pulmonary immunity. For instance, gut dysbiosis in mouse models impairs lung DC function and Th1-type protective responses, thus increasing susceptibility to Mtb (Khan et al., 2016; Negi et al., 2019). This phenotype is reversible via fecal microbiota transplantation, highlighting the functional relevance of the gut–lung axis. Specific commensal species such as Bacteroides fragilis have been shown to mediate anti-TB immunity by regulating host lncRNA expression and subsequent IFN-γ production (Yang et al., 2022). Moreover, gut microbiota-derived metabolites, including SCFAs and indole propionic acid, may directly modulate both Mtb growth and host immune responsiveness (Negatu et al., 2019; Segal et al., 2017).

Beyond lymphatic and intestinal circuits, Mtb engages in direct crosstalk with the host nervous system. The mycobacterial glycolipid sulfolipid-1 directly activates transient receptor potential vanilloid 1+ (TRPV1+) sensory neurons via G protein subunit alpha q/11 (Gαq/11)-coupled G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling, which mobilizes intracellular Ca2+ stores and increases neuronal excitability, thereby providing a molecular mechanism for the cough reflex, a primary driver of Mtb transmission (Naik et al., 2026; Ruhl et al., 2020). Taken together, these coordinated inter-organ interactions, encompassing the lung-LN axis, the gut-lung microbial-metabolic axis, and the lung-neural axis, demonstrate that TB is a systemic disease of immune dysregulation rather than a localized pulmonary pathology, in which pathogen success relies on reprogramming systemic immune homeostasis and even host behavior across multiple organ systems.

Trained immunity and systemic immune memory

Upon Mtb infection, the host develops specific immunological memory, which has long been considered as the cornerstone of protective immunity. Historically, this Mtb-specific memory has been largely attributed to adaptive T cells. Circulating central memory T cells sustain long-term systemic immunosurveillance, while effector memory T cells mediate rapid recall responses in peripheral tissues; at pulmonary mucosal sites, tissue-resident memory T cells form a frontline defensive pool that confers immediate protection upon pulmonary re-exposure (Cheon et al., 2023). Additional specialized subsets, including stem cell-like memory T cells and long-lived mucosal memory CD8+ T cells maintained via in situ proliferation, further extend the durability and functional breadth of adaptive immune memory (Mpande et al., 2018; Perdomo et al., 2016). However, accumulating evidence from BCG vaccination and natural infection cohorts demonstrates that adaptive T cell memory alone cannot fully explain the heterologous protective effects and sustained myeloid functional alterations observed in Mtb-exposed hosts (Steigler et al., 2019).

This conceptual gap has driven the discovery of trained immunity as a critical component of systemic anti-TB immunity. BCG vaccination is the prototypical inducer of trained immunity in humans. At the systemic level, BCG vaccination imprints long-term functional programs on HSCs in the bone marrow, giving rise to circulating monocytes with enhanced antimicrobial responsiveness (Cirovic et al., 2020; Kaufmann et al., 2018). Moreover, circulating metabolic mediators (e.g., succinate and malate) and exosomal lncRNAs (e.g., TRCR1) act as systemic regulators that shape trained immunity responses, linking host metabolic and epigenetic status to innate immune memory potency (Koeken et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2026). At the local pulmonary level, subcutaneous BCG vaccination induces trained alveolar macrophages via both direct mycobacterial stimulation and gut microbiota-coupled metabolic signaling, establishing a durable tissue-resident innate memory niche that accelerates Mtb control (Jeyanathan et al., 2022).

In contrast to BCG, virulent Mtb has evolved mechanisms to subvert trained immunity and promote persistence. Mtb reprograms HSCs via the type I IFN response, which dysregulates iron metabolism, depolarizes mitochondrial membrane potential, and induces necroptosis in myeloid progenitors, consequently depleting the myeloid pool and generating macrophages with impaired anti-mycobacterial capacity (Khan et al., 2020). Notably, heat-killed Mtb preparations are sufficient to elicit trained immunity in human monocytes, suggesting that certain Mtb components alone can drive innate immune reprogramming (Minute et al., 2024). However, the specific bacterial determinants and host signaling pathways that govern this process remain to be further characterized.

Collectively, these findings suggest that host systemic anti-Mtb defense relies on an integrated network combining adaptive immune memory and trained innate immunity. Notably, while trained immunity confers early protection against Mtb by enabling rapid and robust activation of innate defenses, the non-specific nature of this epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming also carries potential pathological trade-offs. Aberrant induction of trained immunity has been linked to persistent inflammatory damage in autoimmune disorders (Ochando et al., 2023), raising the critical but unanswered question of whether dysregulated trained immunity similarly fuels pathological inflammation and contributes to lung injury during active TB. Additional unresolved questions include how trained immunity and adaptive memory synergize during recall responses, how Mtb immune evasion mechanisms can be counteracted to enhance vaccine efficacy, and whether non-canonical immune subsets beyond the canonical monocyte-macrophage lineage can acquire protective trained phenotypes against Mtb. Addressing these questions will inform the design of next-generation TB vaccines that optimally harness both arms of immunological memory.

Immune dysregulation in TB comorbidities

The immunological impacts of Mtb infection extend beyond the lung, disrupting systemic immune homeostasis and increasing susceptibility to a range of comorbid conditions. Here, we examine how TB-associated immune dysregulation contributes to chronic lung diseases (e.g., COPD and lung cancer), metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes and atherosclerosis), and co-infections with other pathogens, highlighting their underlying pathological interconnections and clinical implications.

TB and chronic lung diseases

Persistent Mtb infection alters the pulmonary immune microenvironment and drives tissue architectural destruction, substantially increasing the risk of multiple chronic lung diseases. Among these, COPD is particularly prevalent in TB survivors: a recent meta-analysis estimates that ∼16% of active TB patients globally progress to COPD (Feng et al., 2025). This elevated risk is largely attributable to Mtb-induced lung remodeling, which causes irreversible functional impairment, including reduced diffusing capacity and diminished exercise tolerance, which persists even after successful bacterial clearance (Ravimohan et al., 2018). Conversely, patients with COPD have a 3-fold higher relative risk of developing active TB than healthy controls (O'Toole et al., 2015). Several potential mechanisms underlie this increased susceptibility. First, in COPD, proteases, such as macrophage-derived MMPs and neutrophil-released elastases, degrade elastic fibers, generating elastin fragments that act as self-antigens to trigger autoimmune responses (Dong et al., 2024). This autoimmune inflammatory milieu accelerates lung parenchymal destruction and airway remodeling while simultaneously impairing host defenses against mycobacteria. Second, COPD patients exhibit defective phagocytosis of alveolar macrophages and dysregulated T cell immunity, including imbalanced T helper type 1/17 (Th1/Th17) polarization and impaired regulatory T cell (Treg) function, which may facilitate chronic bacterial infection and dissemination (Barnes, 2016). Third, inhaled corticosteroids, an anti-inflammatory therapy for COPD, dose-dependently increase susceptibility to mycobacterial infection, likely by impairing alveolar macrophage antimicrobial effector functions (Borremose et al., 2026). Notably, the bidirectional pathogenic crosstalk between COPD and mycobacterial infections has been linked to convergent immune dysregulation and shared molecular pathways. Integrative transcriptomic analysis of public datasets has revealed that common differentially expressed genes between the two conditions are predominantly enriched in sustained pro-inflammatory signaling cascades (Kotlyarov & Oskin, 2026). Therefore, future research should prioritize precision immunomodulatory strategies targeting the shared pathways rather than relying on broad immunosuppression. Such approaches could simultaneously alleviate TB-driven inflammatory pathology and reduce the subsequent risk of COPD.

Lung cancer represents another TB-associated pulmonary disease. Individuals with a prior pulmonary TB episode have a significantly elevated risk of developing lung cancer, with a pooled hazard ratio of 5.01 within the first two years following TB diagnosis (Cabrera-Sanchez et al., 2022). Conversely, lung cancer patients also face an increased risk of active TB, with a meta-analysis estimating a nine-fold higher risk compared to the general population (Cheng et al., 2017). Furthermore, TB and lung cancer share similar clinical features, which may lead to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis (Hammen, 2015). These bidirectional epidemiological and diagnostic overlaps suggest interconnected pathological mechanisms underlying the two diseases. Accumulating evidence suggests that Mtb may promote lung tumorigenesis through intimate host-pathogen interactions. We previously found that Mtb phosphatase PtpA not only suppresses innate immunity in the cytoplasm but also translocates to the host nucleus, where it modulates host gene expression to promote proliferation and migration of lung adenocarcinoma cells (Wang et al., 2017). Mtb antigens further exploit the PD-1 signaling axis to repress Th1 immune responses, enhance Treg activity, and facilitate lung cancer metastasis (Cao et al., 2019). Moreover, Mtb infection induces EMT in alveolar epithelial cells, a critical step in cancer progression (Gupta et al., 2016). Additionally, chronic granulomatous inflammation driven by Mtb generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), causes DNA damage, and promotes fibrotic scarring, all of which are hallmarks of carcinogenesis (Fang et al., 2025). Interestingly, BCG vaccine, while used as an immunotherapeutic agent for bladder cancer, can protect lung tumor cells from TNF-α-induced apoptosis, suggesting a dual role of mycobacteria in tumor biology (Holla et al., 2014). Therefore, future research should further dissect the immune characteristics and mechanistic similarities and differences between TB and tumor microenvironments, and identify which components of Mtb and BCG strains are key pro-tumor or anti-tumor factors, thereby advancing co-morbidity prevention and expanding the application scenarios of vaccine strains.

TB and metabolic disorders

Host metabolic and nutritional homeostasis is a critical determinant of Mtb infection outcome. Metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, have emerged as a key syndemic. Individuals with T2DM have a three-fold higher risk of developing active TB compared to non-diabetic individuals (Jeon & Murray, 2008), and TB itself can induce transient hyperglycemia and worsen glycemic control (Dooley & Chaisson, 2009). From an immunological perspective, hyperglycemia compromises key effector functions of macrophages, the primary niche for Mtb. High glucose conditions reduce the phagocytic capacity of alveolar macrophages and delay phagosome–lysosome fusion, allowing Mtb to persist intracellularly (Lachmandas et al., 2015). Moreover, hyperglycemia drives a shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization, leading to increased IL-10 and decreased IL-1β and TNF-α production, thereby limiting bacterial killing (Ayelign et al., 2019). Diabetic patients with TB also exhibit reduced frequencies of Th1 and Th17 cells, impaired IFN-γ secretion, and an elevated Th2/Th1 ratio, further dampening protective cellular immunity (Al-Attiyah & Mustafa, 2009; Kumar et al., 2013). The altered cellular metabolic pathways also contribute to diabetes-related susceptibility to Mtb infection. In immunocompetent hosts, Mtb infection drives aerobic glycolysis (i.e., the Warburg effect) in macrophages, a metabolic switch critical for IL-1β production and effective bacterial clearance (Gleeson et al., 2016). However, this critical glycolytic reprogramming is impaired under hyperglycemic conditions, leading to reduced lactate production and compromised macrophage bactericidal activity (Cumming et al., 2018; Teran et al., 2022). Notably, Mtb has evolved counteracting mechanisms that hijack host glycolytic machinery, thus suppressing lactate generation and further compromising antimicrobial function (Zhou et al., 2026). These insights have pointed to promising host-directed therapeutic targets. The widely prescribed antidiabetic drug metformin, for example, exerts beneficial effects beyond glucose lowering: it enhances autophagy and mitochondrial ROS production, thereby restricting intracellular Mtb growth and improving clinical outcomes in TB-T2DM patients (Degner et al., 2018; Singhal et al., 2014).

Beyond its impact on glucose metabolism, Mtb also disrupts host lipid metabolism, establishing a pathological link to atherosclerosis. A proposed mechanism is the Mtb-driven formation of lipid-laden foam cells, a hallmark of both TB granulomas and early atherosclerotic plaques (Chai et al., 2018). This process is fueled by persistent, infection-induced oxidative stress, which generates oxidized low-density lipoprotein (OxLDL) within the infectious microenvironment (Vrieling et al., 2019). Moreover, Mtb-infected macrophages upregulate scavenger receptors such as CD36 (Dodd et al., 2016), which further promotes OxLDL uptake and foam cell transformation. Immunologically, the Th1-polarized response required for Mtb control paradoxically exacerbates atherogenesis. The sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IFN-γ and TNF-α, promotes endothelial activation, monocyte recruitment, and plaque vulnerability (Huaman et al., 2015). Furthermore, molecular mimicry via mycobacterial heat shock protein 65 (HSP65) triggers autoantibodies that cross-react with human HSP60 on stressed endothelial cells, fueling vascular inflammation (Huaman et al., 2015). Epidemiological studies confirm that both active TB and latent infection are independent risk factors for acute myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, with systemic inflammation mediating a significant portion of this increased risk (Chidambaram et al., 2021). Intriguingly, BCG vaccination has been shown to lower non-HDL cholesterol and reduce lesion progression in hyperlipidemic mice, suggesting Mtb-specific pathogenic mechanisms in promoting atherosclerotic risks (van Dam et al., 2016). Collectively, these findings position Mtb as a pathogenetic driver of cardiovascular diseases, highlighting the need for integrated management of TB and its long-term cardiovascular sequelae.

TB co-infection with other pathogens

HIV, which causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), and Mtb form a lethal syndemic. Their bidirectional pathogenic synergy arises from intricate host-pathogen interactions that subvert both antimicrobial and antiviral immunity, accelerating disease progression and worsening clinical outcomes. HIV primarily targets CD4+ T cells, the key orchestrators of anti-Mtb immunity. Progressive depletion of Mtb-specific Th1 cells, which are critical for macrophage activation and granuloma containment, compromises bacterial control, triggering reactivation of latent TB and disseminated disease (Sharan et al., 2020). Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies further reveal that, in co-infected individuals, Mtb-specific Th17 cells undergo transcriptional exhaustion and ribosomal stress, thereby exacerbating host immune failure (Zhao et al., 2025b). Conversely, Mtb infection amplifies HIV replication and pathogenesis. Mtb-induced proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) activate transcription factors such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), activator protein 1 (AP-1), and CCAAT/enhancer binding protein (C/EBP), which then bind to the HIV-1 long terminal repeat (LTR), the central regulatory element controlling the viral gene expression, thereby driving viral transcription and replication (Falvo et al., 2011). Mtb also upregulates the HIV co-receptors C-C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) and CXCR4 on lymphocytes, enhancing viral entry (Rosas-Taraco et al., 2006). In myeloid cells, Mtb impairs phagolysosome maturation, creating a permissive niche for both pathogens to co-replicate and spread via tunneling nanotubes (Souriant et al., 2019). Furthermore, Mtb-HIV co-infection rewires immune networks, characterized by upregulated inhibitory checkpoints and reduced co-stimulatory signals in T cells (Zhao et al., 2025b). At the molecular level, Mtb employs its lipoprotein LpqN to target the host ubiquitin ligase Casitas B-lineage lymphoma (CBL), thereby manipulating host cell-intrinsic polarization between antibacterial and antiviral immunity (Penn et al., 2018). Additionally, both pathogens accelerate immunosenescence: Mtb acts via DNA hypermethylation and sustained inflammation, and HIV-1 acts via chronic immune activation and T cell impairment, thereby compromising host anti-infectious immunity and vaccine responsiveness (Singh et al., 2024).

In addition to HIV, Mtb also engages in co-infections involving diverse bacterial, viral, fungal, and parasitic agents. These polymicrobial interactions create complex immunological landscapes that synergistically exacerbate disease pathogenesis and worsen clinical outcomes. Gram-negative bacterial co-infections, most commonly with Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, are particularly prevalent in TB-endemic regions, affecting approximately one-third of all TB patients (Attia et al., 2019). These opportunistic pathogens exploit Mtb-caused lung damage, including cavitary lesions and disrupted epithelial barriers, to establish secondary infections. In turn, these bacterial infections trigger robust neutrophil-dominated inflammatory responses that accelerate tissue necrosis and further impair both Mtb and co-pathogen clearance, creating a vicious cycle of tissue destruction and microbial persistence (Bongomin, 2020; Lee et al., 2011). Viral co-infections, such as with chronic hepatitis B or C virus (HBV/HCV), impair the anti-TB T cell functions and increase the risk of anti-TB drug-induced hepatotoxicity and acute liver failure (Arora et al., 2021; El-Mokhtar et al., 2020). Notably, viruses have also been engineered as potential vaccine tools to promote host anti-TB immunity. For instance, Intranasal infection with a recombinant influenza A virus expressing the Mtb Ag85B240-254 epitope strengthens Mtb-specific CD4+ T cell-mediated protection against subsequent Mtb challenge in mice (Florido et al., 2015). Fungal co-infections, particularly with Aspergillus and Candida species, are increasingly recognized as underdiagnosed TB complications. Mtb-induced cavitary lesions provide an ideal niche for Aspergillus colonization, leading to chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, which in turn synergistically amplifies TNF and IL-1β production, thereby exacerbating persistent inflammation and severe tissue damage (Dewi et al., 2025). Candida species, on the other hand, were found to co-infect with Mtb in individuals carrying bi-allelic loss-of-function mutations in the RAR-related orphan receptor C (RORC) gene, likely due to the combined impairment of both IL-17A/F-mediated mucocutaneous immunity against Candida and IFN-γ-dependent systemic immunity against mycobacteria (Okada et al., 2015). Finally, parasitic coinfections, such as with intestinal helminths or Leishmania, skew immunity toward Th2/Treg polarization, which reduces the production of critical Th1 cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ and IL-12), thus impairing both Mtb control and BCG vaccine efficacy (Li & Zhou, 2013).

Collectively, these findings indicate that Mtb coinfections are not merely additive but involve synergistic pathogen-host interactions that drive immune dysregulation, either impairing protective immunity or fueling excessive inflammation, thus creating a vicious cycle that worsens prognosis and complicates treatment. Future research should focus on elucidating the shared and pathogen-specific immunomodulatory pathways, mapping the spatiotemporal dynamics of polymicrobial immune responses in the lung, and identifying feasible targets to restore balanced immunity.

Translational applications by targeting host-Mtb interfaces

The translational exploitation of Mtb-host interactions is progressively reshaping the strategic landscape of TB control. Rather than viewing the pathogen and the host as separate entities, growing evidence establishes that infection outcomes, ranging from sterilizing immunity and asymptomatic latency to progressive active disease and treatment failure, are governed by dynamic, context-specific molecular crosstalk at the host-pathogen interface. Predictive biomarkers for Mtb infection and progression

A central challenge in TB control is the difficulty to predict which of the estimated one-quarter of the world's population with LTBI individuals will progress to active TB. This clinical heterogeneity arises from the variable nature of Mtb-host interactions. Accordingly, there is a critical unmet need for biomarkers capable of both distinguishing LTBI from ATB and stratifying progression risk in latently infected individuals. Conventional diagnostic approaches fall short of this goal, prompting a shift toward multi-omics strategies that capture the full dynamic spectrum of host immune responses.

As discussed in preceding sections, recent plasma-based profiling studies have unveiled a range of molecular signatures potentially useful for discriminating LTBI, active TB, and healthy controls. However, the precise molecular pathways through which these candidate biomarkers are linked to disease progression remain poorly defined. Moreover, many identified signatures are not mechanistically coupled to underlying TB pathophysiology, thus hampering deeper biological interpretation and clinical translation. Meanwhile, advances in single-cell technologies have uncovered TB-associated immune cell subsets and their corresponding immunometabolic phenotypes. However, whether these cellular signatures are specific to Mtb infection remains to be determined. This limitation is further exacerbated by the high burden of comorbidities in TB patients (e.g., lung cancer and concurrent infections), thereby necessitating rigorous validation of these immune signatures before they can be applied to predict TB progression risk (Chai et al., 2018). In addition, phenotypic characterization of these TB-associated immune subsets has been predominantly based on host-derived markers, whereas pathogen-derived signatures remain largely unexplored. Leveraging emerging technologies such as in situ host-pathogen interaction proteomics offers a promising avenue to simultaneously capture both sides of the host-Mtb interface, which may in turn refine the predictive value of these immune signatures for TB progression (Bogdanow et al., 2023; Wheat et al., 2021).

Looking forward, TB risk prediction could be further advanced by shifting the prognostic window even earlier—to ostensibly healthy, uninfected populations. The recent identification of Mtb-resistant cohorts offers a unique opportunity to investigate host genetic, immunological, and metabolic factors that confer natural resistance (Simmons et al., 2018). By elucidating the key determinants of susceptibility versus resistance, we may uncover critical biomarkers for pre-exposure risk stratification. The integration of multi-omics technologies, coupled with more reliable preclinical models, and the establishment of well-phenotyped clinical cohorts with precisely staged infection status, will be instrumental in advancing this effort. Collectively, these biomarker discovery efforts are paving the way for more precise, personalized approaches to TB management, enabling earlier intervention in those at highest risk and more effective monitoring of treatment response.

Next-generation TB vaccines that prevent disease while preserving immune homeostasis

BCG has been the only licensed TB vaccine for over a century. While BCG effectively protects infants against disseminated TB, its protective efficacy in adolescents and adults is variable and wanes over time (Schrager et al., 2020). This critical limitation has spurred the development of diverse next-generation vaccine platforms, with nearly 20 candidates currently in clinical evaluation, including inactivated, live-attenuated, recombinant BCG (rBCG), subunit, viral-vectored, DNA, and mRNA vaccines. However, most of these candidates have not achieved satisfactory outcomes, with issues of either poor efficacy or excessive toxicity (Zhuang et al., 2023). Among these platforms, rBCG vaccines are particularly attractive, leveraging the well-established safety record of parental BCG while enabling targeted enhancements. However, current rBCG candidates face key limitations. Several strains, such as rBCG30 and AERAS-422, were discontinued due to severe local and systemic reactions linked to the Koch phenomenon (Lei et al., 2025). Moreover, many rBCG candidates inadequately stimulate CD8+ T cells, which are critical for intracellular Mtb control, due to suboptimal MHC class I cross-presentation (Jia et al., 2022).

Recent insights into host-pathogen interactions provide rational avenues for improving rBCG design (Lei et al., 2025). First, protective immunity can be enhanced by augmenting the expression of Mtb effectors that elicit robust and durable antigen-specific T-cell responses, alongside incorporating mechanisms that promote trained immunity, which mediates sustained innate immune memory against infections (Cirovic et al., 2020; Kaufmann et al., 2018). Second, adverse effects should be mitigated by deleting or attenuating BCG homologs of Mtb effectors known to drive immunopathology and immune exhaustion. For example, the Mtb effector PtpA induces ferroptosis-dependent tissue damage via host PRMT6, suggesting that deleting its BCG homolog could reduce vaccine-associated pathology (Qiang et al., 2023). Third, refining delivery routes and adjuvant strategies may further boost protective efficacy. For instance, intravenous or mucosal BCG vaccination induces systemic trained immunity and confers superior lung-resident T cell immunity compared with intradermal administration (Darrah et al., 2020; Vierboom et al., 2021). Moreover, subcutaneous delivery promotes innate immune memory in lung-resident macrophages via the gut-lung axis, suggesting that microbial metabolites may serve as adjuvants to improve BCG efficacy (Jeyanathan et al., 2022). Finally, robust vaccine evaluation requires improved preclinical and clinical assessment frameworks. Assays should extend beyond peripheral blood to incorporate respiratory mucosal immunity, local innate-adaptive immune crosstalk, and gut-lung microbial signatures, all of which correlate closely with in vivo protective efficacy. Such multidimensional assessment will expedite development of vaccines that establish long-term immune homeostasis against TB.

Precision strategies for treating drug-resistant TB

Conventional anti-TB chemotherapy, although effective against drug-susceptible strains, is increasingly hampered by prolonged treatment regimens, poor patient adherence, and the increasing emergence of drug-resistant Mtb strains (Farhat et al., 2024). In response to these challenges, considerable progress has been made in the treatment of drug-resistant TB, with drug susceptibility testing (DST) serving as a key tool for guiding individualized regimen selection throughout clinical management (WHO, 2025b). Guided by DST results, the bedaquiline–pretomanid–linezolid (BPaL) regimen and its moxifloxacin-containing variant (BPaLM) regimens have been recommended by WHO guidelines as the new standard treatments for multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB) (WHO, 2022). These all-oral regimens exhibit better treatment outcomes than conventional regimens (Nyang'wa, 2026). More recently, WHO further recommends a six-month bedaquiline–delamanid-linezolid-levofloxacin-clofazimine (BDLLfxC) regimen, which shows promising therapeutic effects for patients with MDR/RR-TB and pre-extensively drug-resistant TB (pre-XDR-TB) (WHO, 2024a). Nevertheless, these pathogen-targeting antimicrobial regimens face multiple challenges, including the substantial toxicity of linezolid and the emerging resistance to bedaquiline and delamanid (Ahmad Khosravi et al., 2025; Atshemyan et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). Against this backdrop, host-directed approaches serve as potential complements to antimicrobial regimens. Unlike conventional antibiotics that exert direct selective pressure on the pathogen, strategies targeting the host–Mtb interface primarily work by reversing Mtb-mediated immune hijacking and metabolic dysregulation. They can thus restore the host's intrinsic anti-infective immunity while simultaneously curbing pathogenic inflammation, and are expected to be effective against both drug-susceptible and drug-resistant strains, with a substantially lower risk of resistance development. Moreover, given that the host-pathogen interface occurs predominantly on the bacterial surface or within host cytosolic compartments, this therapeutic modality does not necessitate efficient penetration into the intracellular bacterial niche (Chai et al., 2020), and this feature may permit lower effective dosages and improved safety profiles.

The growing mechanistic understanding of Mtb immune subversion has expanded the repertoire of host-targeted interventions. For instance, antibody-mediated blockade of the TB-associated NK cell checkpoint receptor LILRB1 restores NK cell-dependent anti-Mtb immunity in preclinical models, suggesting antibody-based checkpoint modulation as a promising immunotherapeutic strategy (Wang et al., 2024). In addition, drug repurposing agents and microbial metabolites are emerging as leading host-directed therapy candidates. The antidiabetic drug metformin, for instance, improves host defense against Mtb through immunometabolic reprogramming, and may reduce TB risk in patients with diabetes (Bohme et al., 2020). Gut-lung axis research has further identified probiotics and microbial metabolites as potential adjunctive options for clinical TB management (Negatu et al., 2019; Segal et al., 2017).

Notably, the precision application of these strategies requires careful attention to timing and immune context. Early-stage infection may benefit from interventions that boost initial innate immune defense and induce prompt Mtb-specific adaptive responses, whereas late-stage disease, characterized by excessive inflammation and tissue damage, requires immune-balancing approaches that mitigate immunopathology while preserving bactericidal capacity. This temporal precision is exemplified by cholesterol-targeting interventions in TB granulomas: late-stage restoration of cholesterol homeostasis improves anti-Mtb immunity, whereas early-stage cholesterol depletion is detrimental (Chai et al., 2026). Moreover, the complex comorbidity landscape of TB demands combination strategies targeting shared pathogenic mechanisms. Integrating host-pathogen interface-targeting strategies with standard antibiotic regimens holds synergistic potential to accelerate bacterial clearance, shorten treatment duration, and reduce inflammatory sequelae (Shee et al., 2026). However, translating this multifaceted strategy into clinical practice will require rigorous validation of its efficacy, safety, and patient stratification in future studies.

Conclusion

In this review, we summarize current understanding of the immunological mechanisms governing host-Mtb interactions and their translational implications. Advances in single-cell multi-omics and spatiotemporal biology have revealed remarkable heterogeneity in Mtb populations, host immune responses, and granulomatous microenvironments, challenging the conventional one-size-fits-all framework of TB pathogenesis. However, many key unresolved questions remain, particularly regarding how Mtb adapts to heterogeneous infection modes—sustaining persistent latency through immune evasion while actively driving inflammatory pathology to trigger active disease, and how to decipher the integrated innate and adaptive immune networks that mediate long-term protection while avoiding the pathological trade-offs that exacerbate disease. Answering these questions will require interdisciplinary integration of multiple fields and cutting-edge technologies (e.g., spatiotemporal multi-omics and artificial intelligence), coupled with advanced preclinical models that recapitulate human TB heterogeneity (e.g., lung organoid systems). These approaches will further deepen our mechanistic understanding of host-Mtb interactions and facilitate the development of more accurate diagnostics, as well as more effective vaccines and therapies. By bridging fundamental discovery and clinical translation, we can accelerate the development of transformative interventions to end the global TB epidemic.
