Anticoagulation therapy for Lemierre syndrome: a comprehensive review and individual participant data analysis
Authors: Haruka Tsuda, Yusuke Miyazato, Shuri Nakao, Takahiro Tsuge, Norio Yamamoto, Yoshitaka Wada, Yuki Kataoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLemierre’s syndrome is a life-threatening infection characterized by septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and septic embolism. Antimicrobial therapy is an established treatment for Lemierre’s syndrome; however, its role in anticoagulation therapy remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between anticoagulation therapy initiated concurrently with antimicrobial therapy and the clinical outcomes in patients with Lemierre syndrome.MethodsWe comprehensively searched the PubMed, Embase, and Ichushi databases for case reports, case series, and cohort studies. Lemierre syndrome was defined as (1) a preceding oropharyngeal infection and (2) internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis or septic pulmonary emboli confirmed by imaging or pathology. We performed a case-control analysis and estimated odds ratios using multivariate logistic regression after adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsOf the 2051 screened records, internal jugular vein thrombus progressed in 15 patients (7.18% [15/209]) who received anticoagulation therapy and in 21 patients (18.8% [21/112]) who did not. The median age of the patients was 25 years (interquartile range, 18–45 years), and the baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the anticoagulated and non-anticoagulated patients. For the primary outcome of thrombus progression, anticoagulation therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk compared with no anticoagulation therapy (multiple imputation odds ratio 0.34, 95% CI 0.16–0.70). We observed no clear differences in the incidence of new septic pulmonary emboli (multiple imputation odds ratio 0.45, 95% CI 0.18–1.13) or major bleeding events (multiple imputation odds ratio 1.50, 95% CI 0.44–5.08).ConclusionOur study suggests that although anticoagulation therapy is associated with a significantly reduced risk of IJV thrombus progression, it does not confer a clear benefit for other clinically important outcomes, such as new septic pulmonary emboli or mortality. Therefore, routine initiation of anticoagulation therapy may not be supported for Lemierre syndrome, particularly when the thrombus is limited to the internal jugular vein. Background

Lemierre’s syndrome is a life-threatening infectious disease characterized by septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein (IJV) and bacteremia caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum, a gram-negative anaerobic rod [1]. Tonsillitis and peritonsillar abscess occasionally cause Lemierre syndrome in otherwise healthy adolescents and young adults [1, 2]. The primary complication of Lemierre’s syndrome is septic pulmonary embolism resulting from septic thrombosis of the IJV [1]. Additionally, septic thrombophlebitis can extend to intracranial jugular veins, such as the cavernous sinus, potentially causing cranial nerve palsy as a long-term sequelae [2, 3].

Antimicrobial therapy is an established treatment for Lemierre syndrome; however, its role in anticoagulation therapy remains unclear. Previous studies have examined this question, but their findings are inconsistent [2–4]. These studies were limited by variations in the timing of diagnosis, antibiotic initiation, and anticoagulant administration, which may have introduced immortal time bias [3–5]. An updated analysis that standardizes these time points is required to evaluate whether anticoagulation therapy prevents thrombus progression, septic pulmonary embolization, or death.

Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive literature review and analysis of individual participant data. Accordingly, we aimed to estimate the causal effect of anticoagulation therapy on the clinical outcomes of patients with Lemierre syndrome.

Methods

Protocol and registration

We conducted a comprehensive literature review and individual participant data analysis in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines [6]. This review was not formally registered; however, a detailed protocol is available at the Open Science Forum (https://osf.io/7c5w2/?view_only=f8ca5d0967c743cc8243e69eddde9a4d). This study complied with the requirements of the Institutional Review Board/Human Subject Research Committee. Although institutional review board approval was initially obtained to collect individual patient data from the existing cohorts, access to unpublished data was unavailable. Therefore, the analysis was restricted to publicly available data extracted from published reports.

Eligibility criteria

Case reports, case series, and cohort studies involving patients with Lemierre syndrome. We applied no restrictions on the language, publication status, or country of publication. Eligible participants were patients diagnosed with Lemierre syndrome, defined as (1) a preceding oropharyngeal infection and (2) IJV thrombophlebitis or septic pulmonary emboli confirmed by imaging or pathology.

The intervention group included patients who received anticoagulation therapy (heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, or fondaparinux). To reduce immortal time bias, we defined the intervention group as patients who received anticoagulation therapy within one day before or after the initiation of intravenous antimicrobial therapy [5]. We specified time zero as the time of anticoagulant initiation for patients who received anticoagulation therapy within one day before or after starting intravenous antimicrobial therapy (Fig. S-1).

The control group included patients who received IV antimicrobials without anticoagulation therapy. We excluded studies in which the timing or duration of anticoagulation therapy could not be confirmed even after contacting the authors.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were: (1) extension or new thrombus formation in the cranial or neck veins detected by imaging within 30 days after the diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome, (2) new septic pulmonary emboli identified within 30 days of diagnosis, and (3) major bleeding events from diagnosis to discharge, as defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis criteria [7]. For thrombus progression, we classified some cases as “no progression” based on the clinical course, including evidence on symptomatic improvement such as resolution of neck swelling.

Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality within 30 days of diagnosis; new disseminated septic complications such as empyema, septic arthritis, and abscess formation from diagnosis to discharge; and any clinical sequelae observed at discharge.

Information sources and search strategy

We conducted an initial electronic search in September 2023 using the PubMed, Embase, and Ichushi databases (Supplementary Table). To supplement the control cases, we conducted an additional search of the Ichushi database in May 2025 using a modified search strategy (Supplementary Table).

HT and two groups of reviewers (YM and SN; TT and NY) independently screened the titles and abstracts, followed by an assessment of eligibility based on the full texts. We contacted the original authors if relevant data were missing. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion. When consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (YH or YK) acted as an arbiter.

Data collection

We collected data on demographics, comorbidities, primary infections, microbiological findings, thrombus extent, disseminated septic complications, antimicrobial and anticoagulation therapies, and clinical outcomes. Data extraction was performed using a semi-automated approach that combined a large language model and human verification [8, 9]. We employed GPT-4 Turbo (gpt-4-turbo model, Azure OpenAI API version 2023-12-01-preview). All extractions were subsequently reviewed and verified by a human reviewer (HT) [9].

Assessment of risk of bias

As the included studies consisted primarily of case reports and case series, the risk of bias was assessed qualitatively, rather than using a formal quantitative tool. Potential sources of bias, including selection bias, reporting bias, outcome ascertainment bias, and confounding by indication were considered during the interpretation of the findings.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Baseline differences between patients who did and did not receive anticoagulation therapy were assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD).

Although our protocol specified relative risks, we calculated the odds ratios (ORs) because they were directly estimated from the logistic model. Given the low incidence of some outcomes in our cohort, the OR provided a reasonable approximation of relative risk.

We calculated the crude, adjusted, and multiple imputation (MI) ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We prespecified covariates based on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), clinical relevance, and prior literature. We used DAG to identify a minimally sufficient adjustment set for each outcome, including DIC and major bleeding, at the time of Lemierre syndrome diagnosis. We selected covariates based on necessity within the assumed causal structure and measurability. Age was considered an essential confounder for all outcomes. DIC diagnosis was also included for outcomes other than mortality, as it may serve as a proxy for disease severity and is likely to influence the initiation of anticoagulation and thrombotic complications. Other covariates were selected to account for potentially confounding pathways that were not captured by DIC.

Variables considered to lie on the causal pathway (e.g., treatment-related complications) or to act as colliders were not included. We did not use an automated model selection procedure.

To handle missing data, we performed multiple imputations using the chained equation (MICE) approach implemented with the mice package in R, assuming random (MAR). The missing proportions of each variable are listed in Table 1. We generated 200 imputed data sets to enhance the stability and precision of the estimates. The imputation model included the primary exposure (anticoagulation therapy), all outcome variables (thrombus progression, new septic pulmonary emboli, major bleeding, 30-day mortality, new disseminated septic complications, and clinical sequelae at discharge), and all covariates used in the adjusted analyses. These covariates included age, comorbidities, severity at admission, DIC, major bleeding prior to the diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome, preexisting septic emboli, and thrombus extent. Each of the 200 imputed datasets was analyzed separately using the same multivariable logistic regression models, and the results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules to obtain the final ORs and 95% CIs [10]. Convergence of the imputation models was assessed by visually inspecting the trace plots.

Sensitivity analyses were performed using the complete case datasets. We also restricted the analysis to patients with available imaging data to evaluate new septic pulmonary emboli and calculated the crude and adjusted ORs using a complete case analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the relevant packages, including mice, glm, and metafor.

Subgroup analyses of age, thrombus extent, comorbidities, bacterial culture results, and anaerobic coverage during empiric antimicrobial therapy were performed. We calculated the interaction P-values to explore potential effect modifications and generated forest plots for the MI ORs.

Protocol modifications

We screened the reference lists of related systematic reviews and contacted the original authors for unpublished data. However, we did not perform these steps because sufficient relevant studies were obtained from primary screening. Although our protocol specified that two reviewers (HT and YM) would extract the data, we implemented a modified process to handle individual participant data. We first screened the data using Claude 3, and then HT reviewed the extracted information against the original articles. YM verified the unclear cases identified using the HT. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and if needed, a third reviewer (YH or YK) was consulted. In the statistical analysis, our initial protocol specified 1:1 matching of patients. However, to maximize the statistical power by retaining the full sample size, we opted to use multivariate logistic regression models instead of matching. Additionally, we did not perform a risk assessment of reporting bias, evaluation of heterogeneity, or sensitivity analyses limited to studies with > 5 cases, because of the insufficient number of eligible studies.

Results

Search results

After removing duplicates, 373 cases were identified from 325 records during a search conducted in September 2023. For Japanese cases, we identified an additional 28 cases from a separate search conducted in May 2025. Finally, 321 patients were included in this review. Among these, 65% (209/321) received anticoagulation therapy, whereas 35% (112/321) did not (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The median age of the patients was 25 years (interquartile range [IQR], 17–45 years) and 57% (182/321) were male. Clinically severe cases before the diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome accounted for 28% (88/321). The baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the two groups, although thrombi limited to the IJV and septic pulmonary emboli were more common in the no anticoagulation therapy group (70% [62/112] vs. 58% [121/209], SMD = 0.58; 71% [79/112] vs. 48% [101/209], SMD = 0.59). Primary and secondary outcomes

Clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The incidence of thrombus progression was 32.8% (21/64) and 11.5% (15/131) in the non-anticoagulation and anticoagulation therapy groups, respectively. The proportion of new septic pulmonary emboli was comparable between the groups (33.3% [15/45] and 26.5% [18/68], respectively). Major bleeding events were rare. Five deaths occurred within 30 days (EPV = 0.83). Therefore, the multivariate model for 30-day mortality was adjusted for age.

Figure 2 shows the forest plots of the crude, adjusted, and MI ORs for each outcome. Regarding the primary outcome of thrombus progression, anticoagulation therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk than no anticoagulation therapy (MI OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.16–0.70). We observed no clear differences in the incidence of new septic pulmonary emboli (MI OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.18–1.13) or major bleeding events (MI OR 1.50, 95% CI 0.44–5.08).

Regarding the secondary outcomes, there were no significant differences in all-cause mortality within 30 days (MI OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.13–5.15), new disseminated septic complications other than septic pulmonary emboli (MI OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.30–1.36), and complications at discharge (MI OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.25–1.27).

Subgroup analyses

We observed no significant subgroup differences according to age, thrombus extent, comorbidities, bacterial culture results, or anaerobic coverage in empirical antimicrobial therapy (p = 0.12, 0.20, 0.092, 0.65, and 0.94, respectively; Fig. S2-1). Similarly, no significant interactions were observed in the subgroup analysis for new septic pulmonary emboli or major bleeding events after diagnosis (Fig. S2-2, Fig. S2-3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses using complete case datasets yielded adjusted ORs that were consistent in direction and magnitude with the MI ORs across all outcomes. When the analysis was restricted to patients with available imaging for new septic pulmonary emboli, the point estimates remained in the same direction as the primary analysis; however, they were no longer statistically significant: crude OR, 0.57 (95% CI, 0.16–2.14); adjusted OR, 0.53 (95% CI, 0.14–2.07).

Discussion

In this case report-based, case-control analysis, anticoagulation therapy might be associated with IJV thrombus progression, but is not clearly associated with major clinical outcomes, including new septic pulmonary emboli, major bleeding events, all-cause mortality within 30 days, new disseminated septic complications other than septic pulmonary emboli, or complications at discharge in patients with Lemierre syndrome. In this study, we comprehensively searched the PubMed, Embase, and Ichushi databases and screened 2,051 records, retrieving 397 individual patient data points from 349 reports. Considering the inherent limitations of case report-based analyses, causal inferences cannot be made. However, the aggregation of individual-level data may provide a pragmatic overview of current clinical practice and outcomes in rare conditions for which randomized studies are unlikely to be feasible.

Our analysis may not show associations between anticoagulation therapy and major clinical outcomes, except IJV thrombus progression. However, several studies have reported its potential benefits. A previous systematic review reported that anticoagulation initiated before clinical complications might reduce subsequent venous thromboembolism or peripheral septic embolism.3 Two factors may explain the discrepancy. First, this systematic review combined new venous thromboembolism and septic embolisms, including pulmonary and other organs, into a single outcome. Aggregating these outcomes may obscure the relationship between anticoagulation therapy and each outcome depending on the relative frequencies of the outcomes or the magnitude of their effects. Based on our findings, thrombus progression might occur at a notable frequency and be negatively associated with anticoagulation therapy, whereas the associations between anticoagulant use and newly disseminated septic complications in the pulmonary or other organs are less clear. A combination of these distinct outcomes can lead to an overall negative association. Second, their study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics of Lemierre’s syndrome and propose preliminary hypotheses regarding the use of anticoagulants. By contrast, our study focused on rigorously evaluating the association between anticoagulation therapy and clinical outcomes. To minimize bias arising from differences in the timing of diagnosis and treatment, we standardized these time points. Recent evidence is consistent with the findings of this study. A population-based Swedish study found no significant effect of anticoagulation therapy on septic complications, chronic sequelae, or mortality [4]. Similarly, a nationwide Japanese analysis showed no association between early anticoagulation and mortality [11]. However, these studies did not consider the timing of antibiotic or anticoagulant initiation. Therefore, our analysis may provide a more robust estimate of the treatment effects.

Based on our results, antibiotic therapy alone may be a reasonable initial treatment for Lemierre syndrome when the thrombus is confined to the IJV. Because the defining feature of Lemierre’s syndrome is bacterial infection, source control (e.g., drainage of a parapharyngeal abscess), prompt diagnosis, and timely initiation of antibiotics are essential. The potential benefits of anticoagulation therapy may be limited to preventing the further progression of IJV thrombosis. Nonetheless, anticoagulation may be safely initiated without bleeding complications, a trend consistent with those of previous studies [3, 4, 11]. Clinicians may need to individualize anticoagulation therapy decisions based on the extent of the thrombus, including intracranial involvement, disease exacerbation (e.g., progression of thrombus or septic emboli) despite antibiotic therapy, and bleeding risk.

Our findings may not apply to patients with intracranial venous involvement, rapidly progressive thrombosis, or expanding metastatic septic lesions. We were unable to perform subgroup analyses of intracranial venous involvement because of the limited number of available cases; the latter two clinical features were not included in our study. Because Lemierre’s syndrome is a rare disease with diverse clinical presentations, analyzing a sufficient number of cases remains challenging. Additionally, it may be difficult to define covariates that accurately measure disease progression. Furthermore, anticoagulants may be administered for DVT prophylaxis in acute critical illnesses, which complicates interpretation [3, 12]. Due to these complex factors, robust evidence for anticoagulation therapy is lacking. Extensive registry-based studies are required to address these clinical questions [1].

Our study has several limitations. First, it comprised only case reports and series, and we were unable to include individual-level data from cohort studies. Second, publication and selection biases were inherent in the case reports and may have influenced our findings. Case reports often describe severe or atypical cases such as those initially misdiagnosed or caused by uncommon pathogens. In contrast, cases with unfavorable outcomes, including treatment failure or death, may be underreported. Furthermore, reporting bias, potentially compounding publication bias, may have affected the availability and completeness of clinical variables because laboratory and imaging results have been inconsistently reported across studies. Third, outcome ascertainment bias is a major concern as the outcomes were derived from individual case reports rather than from standardized definitions or systematic follow-up protocols. In particular, outcomes, such as thrombus progression and septic pulmonary emboli, depend heavily on the availability of follow-up imaging, which is inconsistently performed or absent across cases. The missingness is unlikely to be completely random, contrary to the assumptions of our analysis and may be informative. This pattern can be explained by several mechanisms. Patients with more severe or atypical clinical presentations may have undergone more frequent or repeated imaging, increasing the likelihood of detecting and reporting these outcomes, and potentially leading to their overrepresentation among severe cases. Conversely, in patients with early mortality, follow-up imaging may not have been performed, resulting in a substantial underascertainment of outcomes in the most severe cases and potentially biasing the results toward the underestimation of complications. In addition, differences in healthcare settings, including access to imaging modalities, healthcare resources, and insurance systems, may further influence outcome assessments and reporting. Furthermore, the outcome ascertainment may differ systematically between anticoagulated and non-anticoagulated patients. Patients receiving anticoagulation therapy may have undergone more frequent follow-up imaging, which increases the likelihood of thrombus progression or embolic complications. Conversely, patients who did not receive anticoagulation therapy may have been closely monitored with repeated imaging to assess potential disease progression, which could have similarly increased outcome detection in this group. Therefore, the overall direction of bias is likely to be bidirectional. In our analysis, crude and adjusted ORs were estimated using complete case datasets. The direction and magnitude of the effect estimates were broadly consistent across the adjusted and MI models, suggesting that the impact of differential outcome ascertainment was limited. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with available imaging findings for new septic pulmonary emboli, crude and adjusted ORs remained in the same direction but were no longer statistically significant. Given the extent of missing data and likelihood of informative missingness, the resulting bias may still be substantial and its precise magnitude cannot be quantified. These factors may have substantially influenced the observed associations and should be considered when interpreting the results.

As we assessed eligibility and extracted data by synthesizing the reported content, the included cases and data may not have been uniform. These limitations may not alter the direction of our results but could reduce their precision and attenuate the association between anticoagulation therapy and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, several clinically important factors, such as rapidly progressive thrombosis, expanding metastatic septic lesions, overall disease severity (e.g., hemodynamic instability and organ dysfunction due to sepsis), timing of diagnosis, and treatment indication (i.e., clinician decision-making, representing confounding by indication), may have acted as unmeasured confounders. These factors may have influenced the treatment allocation and patient outcomes, thereby biasing the results. For example, patients with rapidly progressive thrombosis or more severe infections may have been more likely to receive anticoagulation therapy, while also having a higher baseline risk of adverse outcomes, potentially biasing the observed association with harm. Conversely, if anticoagulation was preferentially administered to patients with a more favorable clinical profile or lower bleeding risk, the estimated effect may have been biased toward the benefits. Additionally, we did not include surgical procedures (thrombectomy and other source-control interventions, such as peritonsillar or parapharyngeal abscess drainage) in our regression model, although these procedures may decrease thrombus progression and risk of septic emboli. This is because thrombectomy is typically not the primary treatment and is rarely performed [2]. Furthermore, the proportion of patients who underwent a surgical procedure was similar between those with and those without anticoagulation therapy (thrombectomy: 3.6% [4/112] vs. 5.4% [11/209]; other surgical procedures: 40.7% [44/112] vs. 41.7% [85/209], respectively). However, the lack of adjustment for surgical procedures, which might act as an unmeasured confounder, may have biased the effect estimate for anticoagulation therapy toward the null, as primary infection source control may prevent thrombus progression and septic dissemination, falsely suggesting that anticoagulation therapy may not be associated with improved clinical outcomes. Finally, the EPV for mortality was substantially below the conventional thresholds, raising concerns regarding model stability and potential overfitting. Model instability cannot be entirely excluded, and the results should be interpreted with extreme caution.

Our study did not fully satisfy the requirements of a formal targeted trial, primarily because of the retrospective nature of the case reports and absence of standardized treatment assignment and follow-up. Nevertheless, we incorporated the key principles of causal inference where feasible within these constraints.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that although anticoagulation therapy is associated with a significantly reduced risk of IJV thrombus progression, it does not confer a clear benefit for other clinically important outcomes, such as new septic pulmonary emboli or mortality. Given the inherent limitations of case-report-based analyses and uncertainty of evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, routine initiation of anticoagulation therapy may not be supported for Lemierre syndrome, particularly when the thrombus is limited to the internal jugular vein. Further well-designed observational studies are needed to define the role of anticoagulation therapy in Lemierre syndrome.
