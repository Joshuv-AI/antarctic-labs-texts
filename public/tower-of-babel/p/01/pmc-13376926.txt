A nutritionally-explicit test of the food-safety trade-off under predation risk
Authors: Avik Banerjee, Maria Thaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredation risk elicits physiological and behavioural responses that enhance prey survival but can constrain foraging. Extensive evidence suggests that prey experience a food-safety trade-off whereby prioritizing safety reduces resource acquisition. However, predation risk also induces physiological stress responses, which increase nutritional demands needed to fuel antipredator responses. Thus, prey are expected to optimize macronutrient intakes towards higher carbohydrate diets while also seeking safety. To assess how predation risk shapes foraging behaviour and macronutrient intakes, we examined foraging choices of Psammophilus dorsalis in large semi-outdoor enclosures. We manipulated macronutrient composition of food [high vs. low CP (carbohydrate: protein)] and access to food options (near vs. far from refuge), and compared the responses of lizards under predation risk and control conditions. Lizards exposed to predation risk had higher (but non-significant) corticosterone levels, reduced activity, and generally foraged from sites near their refuge compared to control groups. Macronutrient ingested was marginally affected by the type and accessibility of food options, but retention of ingested elements shifted with exposure to predation risk. These results demonstrate that while the food–safety trade-off primarily governs foraging decisions under predation risk, nutritional needs may also play a role.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50887-3.Keywords: Carbohydrate, Protein, Prey, Antipredator, Reptile, Foraging, Corticosterone, Stress, Triglyceride, GlucoseSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

To survive in a dangerous world, many animals have evolved the ability to assess and respond to predation risk, but these responses often carry costs1–3. In risky environments, animals make behavioural decisions that help minimise detection by predators, as well as facilitate escape if attacked3,4. These tactics may include changing the allocation of activities, such as foraging to safer times or places and increasing refuge use and vigilance1,5,6. A well-known cost of such antipredator behaviour arises from the balance between ‘the need to avoid being eaten’ and the ‘need to eat’, known as the food-safety trade-off2,3,7. Considerable evidence across a range of taxa has shown that under risky conditions, prey prioritise risk avoidance over resource acquisition, resulting in an overall reduction in food acquisition or a shift in diet composition8–10.

Predation risk also induces lasting physiological changes that are costly when chronic. In vertebrates, direct and indirect encounters with predators elicit a stress response that is mediated by glucocorticoid hormones11–13. Elevated levels of glucocorticoids in prey increase metabolic rates and redirect energy utilization to essential functions, such as escape performance and learning ability, which are necessary for survival during challenging periods12,14. When sustained over more extended periods, stress responses can have adverse effects on other physiological processes, including immune function, tissue growth, and reproduction14.

The induction of behavioral and physiological changes that increase the probability of survival during challenging conditions is energetically costly3,12 as it requires the immediate diversion of stored resources11. To meet the increased energy demands of prey under predation risk, carbohydrates and lipids (or carbon-rich macronutrients) stored in the body are essentially broken down and mobilized11. Additionally, the allocation of protein (or nitrogen-rich macronutrients) towards growth and maintenance is temporarily restricted, and ingested proteins are converted to energy via gluconeogenesis11,15. Furthermore, excess proteins that cannot be immediately utilized for energy production are excreted to avoid the metabolic costs of storing nutrients during challenging periods11. The increased usage of stored and ingested macronutrients necessary to maintain anti-predator responses of prey under predation risk needs to be replenished through foraging. The most efficient way to obtain the necessary energy would be to actively ingest carbohydrate-rich (or carbon-rich) food resources11,15. Hence, physiological responses to the risk of predation can modify prey nutritional demands, thereby shaping their foraging behaviour and dietary choices in the wild11,16.

Physiological responses of prey under predation risk are key mechanistic drivers of behavioural and foraging changes17. For example, grasshoppers (Melanoplus femurrubrum) under prolonged predation risk from spiders (Pisaurina mira) showed higher metabolic rates than those under safe conditions18. Risk-exposed grasshoppers also ingested greater amounts of carbohydrate-rich food18. More recently, a study on desert snails (Xerocrassa simulate) under the risk of predation from beetles (Carabus impressus) found that both the risk of foraging and nutrient demands contribute to determining prey dietary choices, as snails under predation risk consumed more carbohydrate-rich food in safer patches than snails not exposed to risk5. Therefore, foraging decisions of prey may be governed by the food-safety trade-off as well as nutritional needs. This expectation underpins the premise of the change in nutrient demand hypothesis, an extension of the General Stress Paradigm11, which proposes that prey should shift their dietary choices under predation risk to meet new nutritional demands induced by their physiological stress responses5. While several studies have separately tested the food safety trade-off and nutritional shifts in response to predation risk15,19, testing both hypotheses together has been relatively rare5.

Here, we investigated the relative importance of the food-safety trade-off hypothesis and potential changes in nutritional demands in governing the foraging decisions of a vertebrate prey. We used the tropical Indian Rock Agama, Psammophilus dorsalis, as our model system and conducted a controlled experiment in large semi-outdoor enclosures, in which the nutritional quality and accessibility to foraging options were manipulated. Foraging options varied in their nutritional content (carbohydrate-rich or carbohydrate-poor) and location from the safe refuge (near or far from the refuge). Our experiment sets up environments where all foraging options were plentiful and easily accessible (Treatment T1), environments where all foraging options were only available far from safety (Treatment T2), as well as environments where carbohydrate-rich foods were either near safety (Treatment T3) or far from safety (Treatment T4; Fig. 1). Lizards in these environments experienced either chronic predation risk or control conditions, and their foraging decisions were tracked over six days. We determined whether predation risk induced a stress response by quantifying corticosterone levels from plasma and faecal samples (faecal corticosterone metabolite, hereafter FCM)20. Circulating blood glucose and triglyceride levels were also quantified, as we expected these metabolites to be elevated in lizards exposed to predation risk21,22. Glucose is the primary energy metabolite that satisfies immediate energetic needs, and triglycerides represent stored lipid reserves that are mobilised during chronically challenging conditions23,24. Circulating levels of glucose and triglyceride can also be closely linked with body condition and feeding state22,25.

According to the food-safety trade-off, we predicted that lizards under the risk of predation would reduce their overall activity and forage from food sources located near their refuge compared to those in control conditions. We expected risk-exposed lizards to consume less food overall compared to lizards in control conditions, resulting from the trade-off between food and safety. If lizards are also optimising their macro-nutritional intakes under predation risk, we predicted that predation-risk exposed lizards would consume a larger quantity of carbohydrate-rich mealworms compared to lizards under control conditions5,9,15. Further, we also estimated the elemental composition of faecal samples collected during the experiment with the prediction that lizards under the risk of predation would eliminate higher amounts of ingested unused nitrogen and retain more carbon than control lizards. As the first test of these two hypotheses in a vertebrate species, this experiment allowed us to determine whether fear-induced stress responses in animals result in a shift of dietary choices to carbohydrate-rich food (or high CP diets) regardless of where it is present, and whether behavioural responses match expectations from the food-safety trade off, i.e., avoiding risky food options to maximise safety. This manipulative approach provides a nutritionally explicit understanding of the food-safety trade-off, enabling us to assess the relative influence of risk avoidance and stress physiology in shaping prey dietary choices under predation risk. Ultimately, understanding where, what, and how much prey eat under predation risk will provide important insights about predator-prey interactions and trophic ecology11,16,26.

Methods

Rearing of nutritionally different mealworms

We created diets that comprised of mealworms (Tenebrio molitor larvae) that were either carbohydrate-rich (high CP) or carbohydrate-poor (low CP). Breeding colonies of mealworms were reared on oat bran and maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 26–27 °C. When the larvae were at the late instar stage (~ 2 cm in length), they were separated into two groups, with one set reared on pure carbohydrate powder (high CP; Maltodextrin, Nature’s Velvet Lifecare) and the other set on pure protein powder (low CP; Whey protein, Nature’s Velvet Lifecare) for a minimum of 10 days. We further ensured that these mealworm diets differed in macronutrient content by coating the high CP mealworms with the carbohydrate powder and the low CP mealworms with the protein powder, respectively, before they were provided to the lizards as diet options. Continuous batches of mealworms were reared for consistent usage during the experiment. We determined the macronutrient content using the Bradford assay for proteins, the anthrone method for carbohydrates, and the chloroform : methanol extraction method for lipids (as per Cuff et al.27). For the elemental (carbon and nitrogen) content of these mealworms, we used a CNHS analyser (Elementar Analyse Systeme GmbH, Germany). Details of these methods are presented in Supplementary Information 1, Section S1. The resulting macronutrient content of the high CP mealworms was 3.15 ± 0.33 mg carbohydrate, 26.08 ± 1.58 mg protein, and 30.20 ± 0.02 mg lipid per 100 mg dry mass of mealworms. Low CP mealworms contained 0.18 ± 0.05 mg carbohydrate, 39.82 ± 2.78 mg protein, and 27.61 ± 0.02 mg lipid per 100 mg dry mass of mealworms. These mealworms yielded a ratio of ~ 1:8 for the high CP option and ~ 1:220 for the low CP option. See Supplementary Information 1, Table S1 and Fig. S1 for the carbon and nitrogen values of these mealworms.

Capture and housing of lizards

Adult male lizards (N = 56 total) of P. dorsalis were captured by lassoing from the wild (around Bengaluru, India) during the peak activity months of April to September in 2022. Lizards were caught in sets of eight and housed individually in semi-outdoor enclosures (4.8 m long x 1.4 m wide x 1.2 m high), which were enclosed within a large structure with mesh walls and tiled roof that allowed for natural ambient conditions but prevented predator access (Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S2). Each enclosure had walls made from white opaque foam boards and had a natural soil substratum. Enclosures also had concealed openings to introduce and remove food dishes. At one end of the enclosure, two cinder blocks with a shelter were provided as a refuge. Water was provided ad libitum in a Petri dish near the refuge. We measured the body mass (in g) and snout-to-vent length (SVL, in mm) of all the lizards before and after the experiment. Lizards were randomly assigned to the different treatments, such that their SVL (range: 104–140 mm) was not significantly different across the treatment groups (Kruskal–Wallis test: χ² = 11.41, df = 7, p = 0.122). At the end of the experiment, all the lizards were permanently marked with a single toe clip to avoid future recapture and were then released at their original capture sites.

Training period

Lizards were first acclimated in the enclosures for four days, where they were provided with six mealworms daily. Lizards that did not consume mealworms during the acclimation period (n = 8) were excluded from the manipulative experiment. After acclimation, lizards were exposed to an eight-day associative training regimen designed to have them associate a particular colour of the food dish (blue or grey, see Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S3) with the nutritional quality (high CP or low CP) of mealworms presented in it. To standardise handling time during foraging, mealworms were placed in individual wells of a coloured 24-well Petri plate, which ensured that lizards accessed one mealworm at a time while foraging.

During the training regimen, lizards were provided with eight mealworms daily that were either of high CP or low CP. Mealworms of different nutritional value were alternatively provided after every two days (i.e., two days of high CP mealworms followed by two days of low CP mealworms), thus enabling lizards to discriminate between the mealworm types based on taste and associative learning28. Colours assigned for high or low CP mealworms and their sequence of presentation were randomized across lizards. The timing of food presentation was also randomized during the active day hours (between 0700 and 1700 h) to prevent habituation. Lizards that successfully ate all the mealworms from their food dishes progressed to the manipulative experimental phase detailed below. Following the training regimen, the lizards were not fed for 24 h to increase their motivation to feed on subsequent experimental days.

Manipulative experiment

In the manipulative experiment, which lasted for six days, lizards were randomly assigned to either a control treatment or a predator treatment group. In the predator treatment, lizards were exposed to simulated predator attacks, which were created by a mechanical raptor model (65 cm wing span) manually flown over the lizard enclosure. Each attack involved chasing the lizard with the predator model until the lizard sought refuge. Lizards were attacked six times a day (between 0800 and 1600 h), with the direction and timing of the attacks kept random to prevent the lizards from learning. Control treatment lizards were left undisturbed.

Both the control and predator treatment group lizards were also assigned to one of the four foraging treatments randomly (Fig. 1). These treatments presented lizards with foraging choices that differed in nutritional quality (i.e., high CP or low CP mealworms) and location (i.e., near the refuge at 1.2 m away or far from the refuge at 4.6 m away) (Fig. 1). In T1, high and low CP diets were provided both far from and near the refuge. In T2, high and low CP diets were provided only far from the refuge. In T3, high CP diets were provided near the refuge and low CP diets were located far from the refuge, whereas treatment T4 was the opposite, with high CP diets provided far from the refuge and low CP diets provided near the refuge (Fig. 1).

Foraging opportunities during the experiment phase were restricted to a random one-hour feeding session every day when food dishes were introduced into the enclosure. Each food dish contained 12 mealworms of the designated quality (high CP or low CP). Mealworms were weighed before and after each feeding session, and the difference in number and weight was used to determine consumption. The macronutrient values of mealworms were used to estimate the total amount of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids ingested by control and predator treatment lizards across all experiment days21.

All the enclosures were video recorded from overhead CCTV cameras throughout the experiment phase. From these, we measured the daily activity budgets of each lizard between 0800 and 1600 h as the proportion of time spent inside the refuge, near the refuge (within 2.4 m of refuge) and far from the refuge (beyond 2.4 m from refuge). For statistical analysis, we summed the activity times spent in each of these activities over the six experiment days for each lizard.

Measures of corticosterone, glucose, and triglyceride from blood samples

A single blood sample (~ 50–100 µL) was collected from lizards from their retro-orbital sinus using heparinized micro-capillary tubes on the last day of the treatment. Blood was extracted within 3 min of lizard capture during 0600–0700 h, which enabled us to measure the baseline levels of circulating corticosterone, glucose and triglyceride (energy metabolites)29. We used a handheld Glucometer (Contour Next ONE, Bayer/Ascensia) to measure the circulating glucose levels (mg/dL) immediately following blood extraction30. The remainder of the blood sample was centrifuged within 30 min of collection, and the isolated plasma was kept at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. We quantified baseline corticosterone levels (ng/mL) in plasma using an enzyme immunoassay kit (K014-H5, Arbor Assays) which had been previously validated for the species31. Each assay used 4 µL of plasma diluted 1:100, with samples run in triplicate and a lab standard of known concentration included in duplicate. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) for samples ranged between 1.97 and 15.4 (mean ± SD = 8.81 ± 3.4), and the inter-assay CV, calculated from the lab standard, was 4.82 ± 4.04 SE. Triglyceride concentrations (mg/dL) were measured using a standard calorimetric assay (MAK266, Sigma Aldrich), with 6 µL of the plasma run in triplicate. All standard curves showed R-squared values greater than 0.99, and the intra-assay CV ranged from 1.73 to 13.27 (mean ± SD = 4.74 ± 0.03).

Elemental content and glucocorticoid metabolites from faecal samples

We collected faecal samples from lizards every day to determine the elemental (carbon and nitrogen) content of egested faeces and for FCM analysis. FCM values incorporate glucocorticoid variation over a more extended period of time than plasma samples32,33. We analysed samples from three phases of the experiment: ‘before’ (collected just before the experimental phase), ‘during’ (third day of predator or control treatment) and ‘end’ (sixth day of predator or control treatment). All the faecal samples were dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C for at least 6 h, cleaned of soil debris, and stored at −20 °C until analysis.

For elemental analysis, faecal samples were additionally dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C for 6 h, after which their dry weights were recorded. The dried samples were ground to a homogenous mixture, weighed (5 mg) into tin capsules, and analysed using a CHNS elemental analyser (Elementar Analyse Systeme GmbH, Germany) to determine faecal carbon (%) and nitrogen (%) content.

We used a standard enzyme immunoassay kit (K014-H5, Arbor Assays) to measure FCM. The extraction protocol involved mixing dried faecal samples (20 mg) with equal volumes of methanol (200 µL) and phosphate buffer solution (200 µL), which was then vortexed for 16 h, and centrifuged at 1200 g for 15 min to collect the resultant supernatant. We used 100 µL of this supernatant at a 1:2 dilution ratio for the assays. Each assay included faecal samples run in triplicate and a lab standard of known concentration run in duplicate. The intra-assay CV ranged from 0.02 to 18.2 (mean ± SD.= 5.47 ± 1.84), and the inter-assay CV, calculated from the lab standard, was 9.66 ± 2.70 SE.

Statistical analyses

All the following statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.0). We fitted linear models (LM), generalized linear models (GLM), or generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) using the R package lme434 and package glmmTMB35, with the latter used where appropriate to provide greater flexibility in modelling non-normal data. Model assumptions were evaluated using simulated residual diagnostics implemented in the package DHARMa36. Where relevant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means implemented in the R package ‘emmeans’37. Pairwise contrasts were evaluated using z-tests for generalized linear (mixed) models and t-tests for linear models, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Tukey’s method.

To determine the effect of predator treatment on physiological responses (corticosterone, glucose, triglycerides), we fitted separate GLMs using a gamma distribution for positive continuous right-skewed data, with predator treatment (control, predator) and foraging treatment (T1, T2, T3, T4) as fixed factors with an interaction effect. Total food intake was included as a predictor in the models to account for the potential effect of food ingested during experiment. Likewise, for FCM, we fitted a GLMM using a gamma distribution, where predator treatment, foraging treatment, and faecal sample collection time (before, during, and end) were included as fixed effects in a 3-way interaction. To account for repeated measures of FCM data, ‘animal ID’ was included in the model as a random effect.

We analysed activity as the proportion of time spent active (inside refuge, near the refuge and far from refuge) by fitting separate GLMMs for each measure using a zero-inflated binomial model with a logit link. The response variable was specified as the time spent in activity relative to total time. The zero-inflation component accounted for excess zeros in data. Predator treatment and foraging treatment were included as fixed effects with an interaction, and ‘experiment day’ nested within ‘animal ID’ was included as a random effect.

To determine how food options influenced the number of mealworms consumed, we fitted separate GLMMs for each foraging treatment. Models followed a negative binomial distribution to account for overdispersion with the number of mealworms eaten as the response variable. Here, predator treatment and food options (near high CP, near low CP, far high CP, and far low CP) were fixed factors with an interaction, and ‘animal ID’ was included as a random effect. Separate models were run for each foraging treatment because the food options varied across treatments. Any cross-treatment comparisons with respect to foraging treatments are descriptive.

For total amounts of carbohydrate, protein, and combined carbohydrate + lipid ingested during the experimental phase, we fitted separate LMs. Since, macronutrient intake was calculated from total food consumed, we analysed the differences in macronutrient intake as amounts (mg) as well as in proportions which accounts for total food intake. For proportion of carbohydrate, protein, and combined carbohydrate + lipid ingested during the experimental phase, we fitted separate GLMs using beta regression for proportion data. In all these models, predator treatment and foraging treatment were included as fixed effects with an interaction.

Changes in carbon and nitrogen content of faecal samples were analysed with separate GLMMs using a beta regression distribution appropriate for proportion data. Changes in faecal CN ratio were analysed with a GLMM using a gamma distribution for positive continuous right-skewed data. In these models, predator treatment, foraging treatment, and faecal sample collection time (‘before’, ‘during’ and ‘end’) were included as fixed factors in a 3-way interaction and ‘animal ID’ was included as a random effect.

Finally, change in body mass (i.e., final mass at the end of the experiment – initial mass measured before the experiment) was analysed using a LM with predator treatment and foraging treatment included as fixed factors with an interaction.

Results

Physiological responses to the risk of predation

Predator treatment and foraging treatment did not show a significant interaction effect on baseline corticosterone, glucose, or triglyceride levels (Fig. 2, Supplementary Information 2, Table S1). Lizards under the risk of predation tended to have higher, but not significantly different, baseline corticosterone levels than control lizards in all foraging treatments (t39 = 0.929, p = 0.36; Fig. 2, Supplementary Information 2, Table S1). However, predator treatment alone resulted in a marginally significant reduction in glucose levels compared to control lizards (t39 = −1.978, p = 0.05; Fig. 2, Supplementary Information 2, Table S1). Triglyceride levels showed high variation and did not significantly differ between control and predator exposed lizards in all foraging treatments (t39 = −0.442, p = 0.66; Fig. 2, Supplementary Information 2, Table S1). Regardless of exposure to predation risk, foraging treatment had no significant effect on corticosterone, glucose, or triglyceride levels (Fig. 2, Supplementary Information 2, Table S1). Finally, total food ingested during the experiment did not significantly affect corticosterone (t39 = 0.041, p = 0.97), glucose (t39 = −0.999, p = 0.32), or triglyceride levels (t39 = 1.060, p = 0.30; Supplementary Information 2, Table S1).

FCM estimated from faecal samples collected during the experimental phase varied widely between treatments, with no statistically significant interaction effect (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. S4). FCM values were marginally higher in predator exposed lizards than in control lizards in all foraging treatments but this difference was not statistically significant (z = 1.813, p = 0.07; Supplementary Information 2, Table S1).

Behavioural responses to the risk of predation

Control and predator treatment lizards differed significantly in their activity patterns in all foraging treatments (Fig. 3, Supplementary Information 2, Table S2). Lizards under the risk of predation spent significantly more time inside the refuge (~ 74% of activity time) than control lizards (~ 18%) irrespective of the foraging treatment (z = 8.237, p < 0.001; Fig. 3, Supplementary Information 2, Table S2). When active outside the refuge, predator treatment lizards spent significantly less time near their refuge than control lizards irrespective of the foraging treatments (z = − 5.780, p < 0.001; Fig. 3, Supplementary Information 2, Table S2). Similarly, exposure to predation risk significantly reduced the time spent far from the refuge (z = − 8.995, p < 0.001; Fig. 3, Supplementary Information 2, Table S2). ‘Animal ID’ and ‘experiment day’ had negligible effects on the activity patterns of lizards in all the above models (GLMM random effect: SD < 0.8).

Dietary choices under the risk of predation

Predator treatment and food options showed a significant interaction effect on the number of mealworms eaten by lizards in foraging treatment T1 (z > 2.005, p < 0.05), but not for T2 (z = 1.881, p = 0.06), T3 (z = 0.994, p = 0.32) and T4 (z = 0.355, p = 0.72). In foraging treatment T1, where high and low CP food options were present near and far from the refuge, lizards in the predator treatment group ate more mealworms overall than those in the control group (Table 1). Control treatment lizards in T1 ate mealworms from all the food dishes irrespective of their distance from the refuge (all pairwise comparisons, p > 0.05, Supplementary Information 2, Table S3). In contrast, lizards under the risk of predation ate significantly more mealworms from food sources near their refuge than far from refuge (all pairwise comparisons, p < 0.05, Supplementary Information 2, Table S3). There were no significant differences in how many low or high CP mealworms were eaten under risk, regardless of whether the food options were near (z = −0.265, p = 0.99) or far from the refuge (z = 0.198, p = 0.99).

Macronutrient ingestion and elemental egestion under the risk of predation

Predator and foraging treatments did not show a significant interaction effect on the total carbohydrate (t40 < −0.982, p > 0.12), protein (t40 < −1.293, p > 0.08), or carbohydrate + lipid (t40 < −1.476, p > 0.11) ingested by lizards (Fig. 5, Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S5, and Supplementary Information 2, Table S4). Contrary to expectation, predator exposed lizards did not significantly alter their macronutrient intake compared to control lizards (carbohydrate: t40 = 0.409, p = 0.68; protein: t40 = 0.372, p = 0.71; carbohydrate+lipid: t40 = 0.409, p = 0.68; Fig. 5a, Supplementary Information 2, Table S4). However, foraging treatment had a significant effect on the amount of macronutrients consumed (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Information 2, Table S4). Specifically, lizards in T3, who were provided with high CP near their refuge consumed significantly more carbohydrates (t40 = 2.815, p = 0.008), or more carbohydrates+lipids (t40 = 2.445, p = 0.02) than those in T1, who had all food options near and far from their refuge (Fig. 5a). Lizards in T4, who were provided with low CP near their refuge, consumed significantly more proteins (t40 = 2.518, p = 0.02) and more carbohydrates+lipids (t40 = 2.277, p = 0.03) than those in T1.

In terms of proportion of macronutrients ingested, which accounts for differences in total food consumed by lizards, we found that predator and foraging treatments did not show a significant interaction effect on carbohydrate, protein, or carbohydrate + lipid ingested by lizards (all p > 0.05; Fig. 5b, Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S5, and Supplementary Information 2, Table S4). There was also no significant effect of either predator or foraging treatment on the proportion of carbohydrate, protein, or carbohydrate + lipid ingested (all p > 0.05; Fig. 5b, Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S5, and Supplementary Information 2, Table S4). Differences in ingestion, based on amount of carbon and nitrogen ingested are presented in Supplementary Information 1, Section S2 and Fig. S5.

Predator treatment, foraging treatment and faecal sampling time had a significant interaction effect on faecal nitrogen (z = −3.277, p < 0.01), but not on faecal carbon content (z = −0.729, p = 0.47; Supplementary Information 2, Table S5). Lizards under the risk of predation eliminated significantly more nitrogen than the control group during the experiment in foraging treatments T1, T2 and T3 while it was opposite for foraging treatment T4 (all post-hoc p < 0.05; Fig. 6, Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S6 and Supplementary Information 2, Table S5). Carbon estimates revealed no significant difference between the predator treatment and control lizards during the experimental phase (Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S6). The resulting faecal C: N levels were significantly lower for lizards exposed to predation risk during the experiment in T1, T2, and T3, while it was opposite for foraging treatment T4 (all p < 0.05, Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S6 and Supplementary Information 2, Table S5). ‘Animal ID’ had a negligible effect on faecal elemental estimates (GLMM random effect: s.d. < 0.5). The detailed results (for the time periods before and after the experiment) and model outputs are shown in Supplementary Information 1, Section S2 and Supplementary Information 2, Table S5, respectively.

Body mass change

Change in the body mass of lizards was not significantly affected by an interaction between predator exposure and foraging treatment (t40 < −1.459, p > 0.15, Supplementary Information 1, Fig. S7 and Supplementary Information 2, Table S6). Although lizards under the risk of predation tended to show lower body mass in foraging treatments T2, T3, and T4, compared to the control treatment lizards, this difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Information 2, Table S6).

Discussion

Anti-predatory behavioural responses, such as shifts in activity aimed at increasing survival probability, can impose nutritional costs, often leading to a trade-off between food intake and safety from predators2,7. However, prey’s physiological responses to predation risk, which include increased metabolism and shifts in energetic investment to emergency functions, can create additional nutritional demands11. Determining the relative importance of behavioural constraints and physiological demands on foraging behaviour under the risk of predation has been relatively understudied. In our study, we manipulated ‘what to eat’ and ‘where to eat’, and found some evidence for the role of both these mechanisms in governing dietary choices in a wild vertebrate.

The food safety trade-off hypothesis predicts that animals under the risk of predation trade-off between resource acquisition and predation risk avoidance2,19. In our study, lizards under the risk of predation preferred to forage near their refuge and spent most of their activity time (approximately 74%) inside the refuge. In the only treatment where food was unavailable near the refuge (T2), lizards were forced to forage far from the refuge despite the predation risk. Consequently, these lizards under predation risk consumed fewer mealworms than in all the other treatments that offered foraging options near the refuge. These findings support the food safety trade-off as reported for several other taxa5,19,38,39. In contrast, control lizards in all foraging treatments ate indiscriminately from all food sources, regardless of whether they were provided near or far from their refuge. Control lizards were also active across the entire enclosure, spending the least amount of time inside the refuge (only ~ 18%), except in the T2 treatment where lizards spent more time near the refuge. Thus, under safe conditions, activity and foraging decisions were unconstrained.

Foraging decisions of animals are nutritionally explicit, reflecting a balance of macronutrients that meet the animal’s needs40. Physiological responses of prey to predation risk typically involve a series of adaptive metabolic changes, constituting a ‘stress response’12,14. This response increases metabolic rate and energy consumption, which can eventually deplete stored energy resources in chronic predation pressure, thereby creating the need to ingest carbon-rich macronutrients (such as carbohydrates and lipids) to fuel immediate survival functions11. Contrary to our expectation, we did not find an increase in overall carbohydrate and lipid consumption in lizards exposed to a chronic risk of predation compared to the control group. Macronutrient intake targets are dynamic, depending on stored energetic reserves, developmental stage, and reproductive state2,5,40,41. While all lizards were in the adult stage and in their breeding phase, we cannot rule out the effect of stored energetic reserves in governing dietary choices made during the experimental treatment, especially for an ectothermic vertebrate that has remarkable physiological adaptations for torpor23,42. Alternatively, given the current experimental design involving colour associative learning to distinguish between nutritional quality of mealworms, it is possible that lizards were unable to distinguish the macronutrient content of food options or that the differences in macronutrient content between food options were not large enough to be distinguishable by lizards43.

Changes in the macronutrient composition of diets would be expected if animals under the risk of predation had indicators of a higher stress response and were in an energetically demanding state11. We found that lizards under the risk of predation had slightly elevated baseline corticosterone measures, although the differences between risk-exposed and control lizards were not statistically different. We argue that this could be due to the activation of negative feedback mechanisms that suppress the release of corticosterone after initial surges, especially in chronically challenging conditions, since prolonged elevation of corticosterone in circulation can be functionally costly for individuals12. Moreover, baseline corticosterone that we measure here at the end of the experiment also does not capture the increase in circulating hormone that is expected after every predator attack13. It is also likely that captivity itself is stressful and all lizards have elevated corticosterone levels44, which only marginally increase with the addition of predation risk. As a complement to the baseline plasma corticosterone measures, we also measured FCM throughout the experiment. As is typical for FCM values, we found high variation between individuals and across treatment groups45. Despite this variation, FCM values in predator exposed lizards were also marginally higher than those in control lizards in all foraging treatments. These results confirm that predation risk does elevate corticosterone levels to some degree.

Contrary to our predictions, lizards in the predator treatment group had lower levels of circulating glucose than those in the control group, which could indicate depleting energy reserves due to continued heightened energy usage during the stress responses46. Triglyceride levels were not different between the predator-exposed and control groups. We sampled baseline triglycerides in the early morning, before lizards had eaten, which could explain the absence of a pattern, as triglycerides typically increase after food ingestion and are cleared from circulation by adipose tissue over time47. Thus, it is not surprising that total food consumed over the course of the experiment did not significantly affect glucose, triglyceride or even corticosterone levels, which were measured in the morning before food intake. Nonetheless, there may still be some increased energetic usage during the chronically stressful conditions of predator exposure14,48 as lizards under the risk of predation in three of the foraging treatments had slightly lower mass than those under control conditions.

We did find some support for the role of nutrition in risk-taking behaviour. When high carbohydrate food was provided far from the refuge (in treatment T4), we found that predator-exposed lizards took greater risks and ingested a greater number of high CP mealworms (mean ± SE, 38 ± 6.4 mealworms eaten) compared (qualitatively) to predator-exposed lizards who were provided with low CP food far from the refuge (in treatment T3; 25 ± 5.5 mealworms eaten). The best explanation for this difference could be the physiological need to access carbohydrate-rich macronutrients required to meet energy demands induced by stress response to chronic predation pressure5,11,15. Such behaviour, where prey seek high carbohydrate diets under stressful conditions, has been documented in rodents49 and insects18. When the energetic costs of lost foraging opportunities outweigh fitness benefits, prey species have been shown to relax their antipredator responses and take greater risks, such as when starving prey take greater foraging risks6,41,50,51. This is mildly evident in our study, where lizards under the risk of predation took greater risks to access high CP mealworms far from refuge in treatment T4, as low CP mealworms available near the refuge may not have been sufficient to meet the prey’s stress-induced metabolic demands. Chronic predator pressure forced lizards to increase their refuge usage and forage from safer patches, but when coupled with physiological stress responses, the energetic demands to maintain allostasis seem to become more pronounced5,11.

Physiological responses to the chronic risk of predation also involve the elimination of excess ingested proteins via faeces, since storage of nutrients during a challenging state is costly11. Lizards under the risk of predation eliminated a higher proportion of nitrogen than control lizards during the experiment in three of the foraging treatments. Our results are similar to those of previous studies on elk8 and grasshoppers18, which also documented the elimination of greater nitrogen content under conditions of high predation risk. Interestingly, however, in T4, predator treatment lizards eliminated lower amounts of nitrogen during the experimental phase compared to their conspecific control group. This finding is contrary to our predictions but highlights the need to address post-ingestive nutrient processing, allocation, and their consequences on prey body compositions44,52. The amount of carbon eliminated was not different between the treatment groups throughout the experimental phase, suggesting optimal utilization irrespective of physiological needs and corroborates findings from a previous study on the same species44.

In conclusion, we find that the foraging decisions of lizards under predation risk are strongly governed by the food-safety trade-off, with some choices reflecting shifts in nutritional needs5. Contrary to our expectation, we did not find differences in macronutrient ingestion between the control and predator treatment lizards, but qualitative comparisons between foraging treatments suggest some shift to higher carbohydrate diets under the risk of predation. What is clear is that the food-safety trade-off and nutritional optimisation are not mutually exclusive and operate simultaneously in governing prey dietary choices under the risk of predation53. The relative importance of either hypothesis in governing prey’s dietary choices may also depend on other factors, such as stored energy reserves, reproductive demands, and the duration and intensity of chronic stress from predation risk15,41. Regardless of the underlying mechanisms, when predation risk in the wild affects foraging decisions, this can have direct implications on trophic function and food-web dynamics11,16,26. Stress responses to perceived predation risk elevates prey metabolic rates, and when chronic, can alter the amount and composition of resource intake, macronutrient processing, and excretion in individuals. Consequently, predation risk can shift the flow of energy and nutrients through food webs directly influencing trophic transfer efficiency, food chain length, and ecosystem processes such as nutrient cycling11,26. For lizards, however, chronic predation risk exposed over six days does not seem to be stressful enough to induce a shift in macronutrient intake but it does reduce overall food consumption especially in conditions where food is less accessible. Moving forward, studies should expand the conceptual framework and incorporate multiple intrinsic and extrinsic drivers to comprehensively disentangle the dietary decisions of prey under the risk of predation.
