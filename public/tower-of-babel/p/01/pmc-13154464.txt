Effects of interprofessional education on readiness for interprofessional learning in prelicensure rehabilitation science students: a systematic review
Authors: Eric Dixon, Jayden Pannu, Kabir Dhaliwal, Rachel Cheng, Gurpal Deol, Sophie Frangos, Emma Tawil, Ana Oliveira, Sarah Wojkowski, Shirley Quach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly interprofessional education (IPE) facilitates effective interprofessional collaboration (IPC) for high quality patient care, by enhancing students’ knowledge, communication, teamwork and mutual respect. While IPE effectiveness is well explored in medical and nursing students, there is limited information on prelicensure rehabilitation science students.ObjectiveTo examine the effect of IPE on rehabilitation science students’ IPE readiness and attitudes toward collaborative learning, confidence, practice efficiency, and team dynamics. MethodsThis systematic review was informed by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions for data analysis and synthesis. Structured searches were completed across six databases from January 2016 to March 2024. Studies were included if they reported an IPE intervention including multiple prelicensure health professional students, with at least one student from a rehabilitation discipline. Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) and Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) tools were used to report study biases and evidence quality, respectively.ResultsSix studies met inclusion criteria, including 552 students from nine healthcare professions, of which 222 students were from rehabilitation disciplines. Three rehabilitation disciplines were represented across studies: 157 physiotherapy students, 47 occupational therapy students and 18 speech language pathology students. Interventions ranged from single session clinical simulation activities to multi-session interprofessional group projects, using various outcome measures. Overall, students from rehabilitation disciplines had promising changes in their readiness and confidence to engage in IPC. ConclusionIPE interventions may have positive impacts on the readiness and confidence for IPC in pre-licensure rehabilitation science students. Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines interprofessional education (IPE) as collaboration among students from different disciplines to deepen their understanding of each other’s roles to collaboratively provide safe and effective care [1]. IPE can occur in structured activities integrated into the curriculum or arise spontaneously during student placements [2]. IPE aims to enhance collaborative clinical practice by improving health professional students’ knowledge and skills in teamwork, communication and mutual respect to direct effective interprofessional collaborations (IPC) [3, 4]. Therefore, IPE should be considered pivotal in training students to prepare for IPC to provide comprehensive care [5, 6].

In educational settings, students and licensed healthcare professionals can participate in IPE in several ways, which may include small group discussions, simulations, and workshops [5, 7–9]. These IPE interventions improved students’ attitudes and perceptions, confidence, and self-efficacy, shown to enhance team-based performance, communication and patient outcomes [5, 7–9]. However, while effective, Shuyi et al. [8] reported these improvements in IPE attitudes, collaboration and satisfaction towards clinical decision making were only sustained for a short period [8]. Furthermore, past systematic reviews reported very low quality and heterogeneous evidence, lack of randomized control trials, a high proportion of loss to follow-up, and high risk of bias [7, 8]. In addition, the effects of IPE were predominantly reported in nursing and medical students, with very few studies focusing on other healthcare professional students, such as those enrolled in rehabilitation programs [5, 7, 8].

Rehabilitation consists of interventions aimed at optimizing quality of life in individuals with chronic health conditions [10, 11]. Besides increasing independence and promoting participation in meaningful life activities, rehabilitation contributes to healthy aging through disease management and helps reduce morbidity and mortality [12]. According to WHO, rehabilitation professionals include “physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech and language therapists and audiologists, orthotists and prosthetists, clinical psychologists, physical medicine and rehabilitation doctors, and rehabilitation nurses…” [10]. Exploring the effects of IPE on rehabilitation science students is crucial, as effective IPC contributes to improved chronic disease management and overall cohesive patient care [10, 12].

Thus, the primary objective of this systematic review was to explore the effects of IPE on pre-licensure rehabilitation science students’ readiness towards IPC. Secondary objectives were to evaluate the effect of IPE on students’ other IPC skills, such as team dynamics, confidence levels, closed looped communication, and practice efficiency.

Methods

This systematic review was guided by the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and reported as per the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) (supplementary files) [13, 14]. A protocol for this review was registered a priori in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD42024506081) and published with the Journal of Medical Internet Research (JMIR) [15].

Data sources and search strategy

A search strategy was developed in consultation with a health sciences librarian at McMaster University to ensure it captured the research question. Relevant medical subject headings (MeSh) and keywords were identified by examining previous systematic reviews for topics related to IPE, rehabilitation health personnel, and learning (Supplementary files) [6, 16]. An initial search with the MeSh terms and keywords was trialed in Medline, as it is widely used for its extensive collection of biomedical and health-related literature [13, 17]. The search was translated to Embase, CINAHL, ERIC, and AMED as these databases index journals focused on health education and rehabilitation professionals. Reference lists of eligible studies were screened for additional studies and authors were contacted for further information, where appropriate.

Eligibility criteria

Study design and date range

Studies were selected according to the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) framework [13] outlined below. Additionally, studies must have been published after 2016 to reflect discussions incorporating the updated core competencies outlined by the Interprofessional Education Collaborative (IPEC) [18]. Including studies from 2016 onwards was amended after protocol registration and was justified as the 2016 IPEC update emphasized the implementation of IPE competencies in rehabilitation students’ curricula [18]. This increased dissemination was due to new institutional membership in the IPEC during the 2016 update - which included the American Council of Academic Physical Therapy, the American Occupational Therapy Association, and the Association of Schools of Allied Health Professions, amongst other rehabilitation-specific associations [18]. Furthermore, IPE-specific interventions from 2016 onwards likely reflected contemporary educational practices from the IPEC update [18].

PICO and definitions

Studies were included, if the following criteria were met: 1) study designs were interventional (i.e., mixed methods, quasi-experimental, randomized controlled trials); 2) participants were pre-licensure students in entry-to-practice health training programs; 3) IPE interventions included two or more groups of students, including at least one group from a rehabilitation profession; 4) study outcomes quantitatively measured students’ IPE readiness, perceptions, appreciation or confidence for IPC; and 5) outcomes must be reported separately for each professional program.

Rehabilitation disciplines were defined according to the WHO definition, which includes students from physiotherapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), speech language pathology (SLP), audiologists, orthotists, prosthetists, clinical psychologists, physical medicine, rehabilitation physicians and nurses. IPE interventions were defined as an active exchange of information and participation in events aimed at improving communication and collaboration skills [1, 19]. Interventions could occur within various settings including large group classes, small group tutorials, clinical settings, and online platforms and any model of learning was included (e.g. Team-Based Learning (TBL), simulation, and student-led interprofessional clinics) [2]. The primary outcome was students’ IPE readiness, described as students’ preparedness to work with other health professionals which can be measured using various outcome measures [3, 20]. Secondary outcomes were students’ perceptions of IPC, appreciation for the role and scope of complimentary rehabilitation disciplines, closed-loop communication, confidence levels, practice efficiency, and team dynamics. Outcome measures used to assess primary and secondary outcomes were not limited in this review. Details on the inclusion and exclusion criteria are in the supplementary files.

Study selection

Search results from the databases were downloaded and imported into Endnote 21 (Philadelphia, Pennsylvania) Reference Manager for initial duplication removal [21]. The remaining citations were uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Australia) [22] for further duplication removal, followed by title/abstract, and full-text screening.

To ensure consistency in title and abstract screening, the first 30 citations were pilot tested by all authors as a group [23]. After calibration, the remainder of the titles and abstracts were screened by two different authors (ED, GD, JP, KD, RC, SF, and ET). Disagreements for full-text eligibility were attempted by discussion, followed by a decision by a third reviewer (either SQ, SW, AO), if required. The full-text screening process included pilot testing, independent reviews, and disagreement resolution using the same process described above [23]. The abstract and full-text screening tools were developed to facilitate these processes, published with the protocol [15] and available in the supplementary files. Only texts available in English and Portuguese were considered as they were within the limits of the study team’s language capabilities.

Data extraction

Data extraction was pilot-tested on two studies as a group to minimize discrepancies before independent extractions [13, 24], using a pre-determined extraction tool (supplementary files). After pilot testing was completed, two groups of two authors (JP, GD, KD, and RC) completed data extraction of the quantitative results. Disagreements were resolved first by discussion, followed by a decision by a third reviewer (either SQ, SW, AO).

Data analyses and syntheses

Tables were constructed to describe the types of studies, populations, characteristics of the IPE intervention, professions involved in the intervention, comparator, and outcomes. The study results were displayed using an effect direction plot, organized by outcome and ROB. Subgroup analyses were considered if sufficient data was available from two or more studies to examine outcome variability and population affected by the outcome (patient-reported outcomes, clinician outcomes, student outcomes) or exposure variability (setting, frequency, duration; including the potential total number of exposure hours and whether hours came from patient-facing vs. non-patient-facing exposure experiences).

Risk of Bias (ROB) assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB) tool for randomized control trials and the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies (ROBINS-I) for included studies were completed by two authors (KD, RC) [25, 26]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (either SQ, SW, AO).

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)

The GRADE working group methodology was used to assess each study’s quality of evidence across the domains of ROB, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias [27]. Quality was stratified as high (further research is very unlikely to change our confidence in the estimate of effect), moderate (further research is likely to have an important impact on our confidence in the estimate of effect and may change the estimate), low (further research is very likely to have an important impact on our confidence in the estimate of effect and is likely to change the estimate), or very low (very uncertain about the estimate of effect) [27]. GRADE for each study was conducted by two reviewers (KD, RC) independently before meeting to resolve disagreements before consulting a third reviewer (ET).

Results

Across the five databases, a total of 10,692 citations were retrieved in March 2024. From those, 8378 were eligible for abstract and title screening, and 756 for full-text screening (Fig. 1). Studies excluded at the full text screening stage were mostly due to incorrect population, outcomes and study designs. The PRISMA 2020 checklist was utilized to facilitate the reporting of this systematic review [14]. Deviation of this systematic review was mentioned above, where only studies published after 2016 were eligible, reflecting the changes to the updated core competencies by IPEC [18].

Description of included studies

Six quasi-experimental studies (3 pre/post and 3 mixed methods) were included in this systematic review and only one had a comparator group [28]. Five studies were conducted in the USA [29–33] and one in New Zealand [28]. Three (50%) studies were published from 2016 to 2020 and three (50%) after 2020. The setting and structure of IPE interventions varied from clinical simulations involving joint assessments and treatments for standardized patients (4, 67%) and group-based discussion with experiential learning (2, 33%). Five hundred fifty-two students from nine healthcare professions were represented across the studies [28–33] including PT, OT, SLP, dentistry, medicine, nursing, pharmacy, athletic therapy (AT) and emergency medical services. Rehabilitation science students

Each of the six studies reported discrete findings for prelicensure rehabilitation science students such as PT (4, 67%), OT (2, 33%), SLP (1, 17%) and athletic therapy (AT) (1, 17%). Of the 552 students, there were 157 PT students (28%), 47 OT students (8.5%), 18 SLP students (3%) and 45 AT students (8%). Four studies included students in their final year of study [28, 29, 31, 33]. Two of those studies included students in their first, second and final year of their programs [31, 33]. The exception was Cunningham et al. [30] who reported their students were in their first year of study; while Morrell et al. [32] did not mention their participants’ year of study. Mean ages and other sample characteristics were not reported.

Interprofessional education interventions

Four studies had single session IPE interventions [29, 30, 32, 33] while two studies had longer IPE interventions [28, 31]. Pullon et al. [28] had an intervention that lasted five weeks whereas Weir-Mayta et al. [31] incorporated 4 distinct sessions. The topic of the IPE intervention was unique in each of the six included studies; however, many of the activities were similar in development and implementation.

Five studies (83%) involved clinical simulation activities in which students from various health sciences, including rehabilitation science professions, worked together to provide care to standardized patients [29–33]. Morrell et al. [32] and Cunningham et al. [29] directed their interventions towards assessment techniques for an athlete who sustained a spinal cord injury and for an elderly patient with newly acquired delirium, respectively. Cunningham et al. [30] and Weir-Mayta et al. [31] had students provide treatment management for a patient with a recent total hip replacement and signs of abuse, and for patients with speech and respiratory complications, respectively. Ivey et al. [33] included elements of both assessment and treatment in caring for patients with fall history, secondary to Parkinson’s Disease. Pullon et al. [28] was the only study to differ in the IPE activity, utilizing formal projects and placements to facilitate interprofessional interactions, focusing on cultural competency and Indigenous health.

Effects of IPE intervention

Due to the substantial heterogeneity, both clinically and methodologically, a meta-analysis was not performed, and a narrative approach was used to capture the results. Across the six studies, five different outcome measures were used, administered before and after the IPE intervention. Many included studies did not have formal follow-up. One study recorded data one week following the intervention [33] while the others collected data immediately post-intervention with no follow-up [28–32]. One study had separate questionnaires for all students at the beginning and end of an academic year, regardless of when everyone participated in the intervention [28]. Primary outcome measures

Five studies reported on students’ IPE readiness, where four found positive effects from their respective IPE interventions [28–31]. Four different IPE outcome measures were used across the five studies, which were the Interprofessional Socialization and Valuing Scale (ISVS), Jefferson Scale of Attitudes Towards Interprofessional Collaboration (JeffSATIC) Scale and two study specific scales. ISVS and JeffSATIC are scales for measuring interprofessional collaboration, each focused on slightly different IPEC competencies, including interprofessional socialization, perceptions, working relationships and accountability [28, 32, 34]. Pullon et al. [28] and Weir-Mayta et al. [31] created study specific surveys with items geared towards assessing students’ level of readiness to engage in IPC. Pullon et al. [28] adapted their survey from Kirkpatrick’s four levels of evaluation for training [35] and Weir-Mayta et al. [31] created their own survey questions.

PT students across three studies [27, 30, 32] demonstrated increased IPE readiness, knowledge of other health disciplines’ roles, socialization and understanding of interprofessional practice. However, in one study, PT students were reported to not be able to describe one’s professional role on the healthcare team [30]. Another study investigated readiness in OT and AT students and reported no significant change in working relationships, accountability or perceptions on individual and group responsibility pre-post intervention [29]. One study examined readiness in SLP students and showed an increase in preparedness, knowledge and competence in their skills [31].

Secondary outcome measures

One study included a secondary outcome measure. Ivey et al. [33] used the Self Efficacy Interprofessional Experiential Learning (SEIEL) Survey, assessing students’ level of confidence in interprofessional interaction, and interprofessional team evaluation and feedback [36]. PT and OT students were included and showed improvement in their interprofessional interactions, team evaluations and feedback. However, there were no differences reported between PT and OT students. Both the OT and PT students reported liking, benefiting from, and having fun with the experience.

ROB and quality of evidence

A GRADE assessment was performed for each outcome assessed in this systematic review. The level attributed to each construct of GRADE, as well as the quality of evidence, are described in Table 4 and paired with the ROBINS-I tool in Fig. 2. Five of six studies used outcome measures that clearly assessed readiness with the exception of the SEIEL Survey used by Ivey et al. [33] which assessed level of confidence. Overall, the five studies that examined readiness had serious risk of bias, inconsistency and indirectness, likely to have imprecision and undetected publication bias. The study by Ivey et al. [33] that assessed level of confidence also has serious risk of bias and indirectness, no inconsistency, very likely to have imprecision and strongly suspected publication bias. Both readiness and level of confidence were given a very low quality of findings.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review on the effects of IPE on readiness and confidence for IPC in rehabilitation science students. IPE interventions varied in content, context and delivery, lasting from one session to a few months’ time. Clinical simulation and experiential learning were commonly implemented, with at least two or more healthcare disciplines involved. IPE readiness and students’ perceptions for IPC were positive across studies, observed in PT, OT and SLP students. Only one study measured students’ level of confidence for IPE and IPC, which improved PT and OT students’ interactions. Collectively, IPE interventions can improve IPE readiness for IPC and level of confidence in rehabilitation science students by increasing their knowledge of roles, activities, skills, and understanding of other health professionals.

Across studies, study-specific IPE interventions were implemented to meet the curricula’s learning objectives and the available resources of the targeted disciplines [28–33]. Despite heterogeneity of the IPE interactions, the positive results may indicate various forms of IPE interventions can improve IPC readiness, aligning with past research [5, 8, 9, 37–39]. This may be explained by the need for comprehensive IPE interventions that will help students understand their roles and responsibilities, engage in respectful conflict resolutions, develop skillful communication, and collaborative practices for future IPC in the workplace [2, 9, 16, 20]. These objectives can be achieved through classroom activities or simulated environments, which are seen in the presented studies, indicating there is no strict method to implement IPE [2, 28–33]. However, not all studies had positive outcomes, which may be attributed to factors such as participants’ baseline characteristics. Several studies in this review failed to consistently report students’ past IPE experiences, year of study, mean age, and gender, which are possible contributors to their IPE experiences [28, 30, 32, 33]. Past studies have reported differences in IPE readiness and competency across disciplines, likely due to differences in age groups and previous IPE experiences [37, 40–42]. These factors were minimally discussed despite inclusion of students at different timepoints of their programs [31, 33], but these considerations could be informative for IPE implementation [40, 42]. Therefore, a comprehensive IPE intervention that considers target students’ baseline characteristics, their learning needs and institutional resources in rehabilitation may facilitate their overall readiness and confidence in IPC [9, 11, 33, 39, 40, 43].

The development and revision of the IPEC Core Competencies in 2016 was an important milestone in IPE, outlining the crucial components in IPC to achieve the Triple Aim in improving patient care, population health and reduce per capita cost [18]. However, current outcome measurements do not measure the same IPE constructs, and some do not align with the domains presented in the IPEC Core Competencies framework [20]. The IPEC Competency Self-assessment tool is one outcome measure that was informed by the IPEC Core Competencies framework and specifically measures professional values and interactions [44, 45]. Therefore, using the IPEC Competency Self-assessment tool to evaluate different IPE interventions would ensure alignment with the IPEC Core Competencies framework. For example, two short reports by Pinto et al. [46] and Nkonge et al. [47] utilized the IPEC Competency Self-Assessment tool and observed positive IPE responses to the interventions. Unfortunately, the outcome measures in the included studies were heterogenous, preventing quantitative comparisons and meta-analysis, a common challenge across other synthesis reviews [4, 20, 48]. The lack of standardized IPE outcome tools perpetuates the fundamental challenge in understanding and comparing IPE across studies.

Although there is inconsistent use of outcome measures, this may be necessary to evaluate different IPE curricula, their aims and study objectives [28–33]. The included studies used outcome measures evaluating various constructs related to IPE readiness. Which included attitudes towards IPE in the JeffSATIC, perceptions of the value of IPE in the ISVS-21, and self-efficacy for IPE in the SEIEL [20, 34, 36, 49]. Many of these constructs are related to readiness and confidence for IPC, but they can also offer valuable information for specific contexts in their respective studies [3, 20]. A 2024 systematic review by Allvin et al. [20] discussed why standardization of specific IPE outcome measures may not be ideal. To evaluate different domains of interprofessional competencies, multiple IPE outcome measures are available but should be chosen based on the tool’s strength of evidence, its intended purpose, and the psychometric properties, for the specific IPE intervention and objectives [3, 20]. For example, the Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS) is ideal for assessing readiness for IPE readiness for IPC but does not explore perceptions of IPC; while the Students’ Perceptions of Interprofessional Clinical Education (SPICE) or JeffSATTIC tools do [20]. Therefore, as IPE literature continues to grow, future studies must consider the best outcome measures to use under specific circumstances [3, 9, 20, 39]. Investigators should carefully consider which outcome measures will assess the IPEC Core Competencies domains of interest when implementing their IPE delivery. Previous research has shown IPE interventions increased IPC by enhancing communication and understanding of others’ perspectives amongst healthcare professionals [5, 16], especially students in nursing and medicine programs [4, 8, 41, 50]. Similarly, rehabilitation professionals are involved in many aspects of health management, including disease prevention, treatment, and palliation [10, 11]. Therefore, adequate IPE training in emerging rehabilitation professionals is crucial to optimize their function in interprofessional teams to improve patient outcomes [11]. Our review revealed few studies that have reported explicit results of prelicensure rehabilitation science students, with many inconsistently reporting their interventions, outcomes and the lack of follow-up. Nonetheless, rehabilitation science students need to develop the knowledge and skills to improve their confidence and readiness to engage in IPC beyond program graduation [3]. IPE experiences may allow future clinicians to be better advocates for their patients through IPC [4, 5, 51], enhancing patient safety and decreasing time to care [8], and improving workplace satisfaction through higher engagement with other clinicians [3]. Accrediting bodies that regulate and certify professional rehabilitation education programs should continue to advocate for mandatory IPE by implementing policies in alignment with the growing body of evidence [38, 52]. Therefore, more high-quality research on IPE in rehabilitation education curricula is needed but the ideal implementation method for IPE is unclear. Considerations to who the students are, their baseline characteristics and their learning needs must inform the development of IPE interventions to support student interaction, within institutional resources [38, 46, 52]. Current strategies can support IPE readiness and confidence for IPC with their clinical learning needs, such as patient evaluations and assessments, transitions of care and formulating diagnoses and treatment plans [11, 28–33]. Future IPE activities should focus on fostering effective communication, understanding each other’s roles, and fostering trust between students for collaborative learning, measured by the appropriate outcome measure [3, 38, 43, 51].

There are several strengths to this review, including the novel focus on rehabilitation science students, a subpopulation of emerging healthcare professionals dedicated to rehabilitating individuals with underlying chronic diseases. Several methodological factors were implemented from the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic review to ensure rigor and validity of the systematic review process and results [13]. This included PROSPERO registration, protocol publication, pilot screening of study selection and extraction stages [22, 27]. Furthermore, with the refinement of inclusion dates to reflect the IPEC update in 2016 [18], the work published here reflects the current state of IPE interventions in health professional programs, including in rehabilitation science. Therefore, by evaluating IPE interventions within the last 10 years, it provides a strong understanding of the impact of the IPEC and WHO on subsequent IPE curricula. However, there are several limitations to this review. The included studies were low in quality with notable risk of biases. Five studies had a moderate risk of bias, and one had serious risk of bias due to the lack of blinded randomization, control groups, small sample sizes, loss of follow-up and control for participants’ previous IPE experiences [25]. These limitations were also reported in past systematic reviews, stressing the needs for high-quality IPE research to evaluate their long-term effectiveness [5, 38, 51]. Finally, only studies written in English or Portuguese were considered, which may have excluded studies published in regional specific journals.

Conclusion

This review adds to the growing body of evidence in IPE research, focused on prelicensure rehabilitation science students. While these studies showed improved IPE readiness and confidence, the results should be applied with caution due to high study heterogeneity and low-grade evidence. A greater volume of well-designed and controlled studies is necessary to inform the best practices in IPE. Future studies may need to consider standardized approaches and measurement tools to confirm these findings, including follow-up to evaluate long-term effects. However, IPE interventions must be carefully implemented to ensure they meet students’ learning needs and the IPE competencies required by health professional curricula.
