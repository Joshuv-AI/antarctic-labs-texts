Modeling mechanical properties of rubberized concrete using gene expression programming (GEP) and random forest: a comparative study
Authors: Muhammad Sheraz, Muhammad Talha, Muhammad Alam, Muhammad Farhan, Zia Ul Haq, Muhammad Rashid, Hisham Alabduljabbar, Furqan Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRubberized concrete is becoming increasingly popular as a sustainable construction material due to its capacity to repurpose waste tyre rubber (WTR) and reduce the use of natural aggregates. However, experimental testing of its mechanical properties is expensive and time-consuming, emphasizing the importance of reliable predictive tools. To solve this issue, this research uses gene expression programming (GEP) and random forest (RF) models to estimate the flexural strength (FS) and split tensile strength (STS) of rubberized concrete. The development of the models were carried out using a comprehensive dataset that included 112 experimental findings and seven main input parameters. Multiple statistical metrics were used to evaluate model performance, such as R, MAE, RMSE, RAE, and RRSE. Although both models correctly predicted the strength trends of rubberized concrete, RF outperformed GEP with testing R² values of 0.9799 for FS and 0.9817 for STS, compared to 0.9183 and 0.8965, respectively. The error analysis revealed that both models’ prediction errors were within acceptable bounds, with RF models having lower error as compared to GEP. Furthermore, the proposed models were compared to previously published prediction models and outperformed them in terms of accuracy and generality. Furthermore, the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis applied to the dataset revealed the relative importance of input parameters on mechanical behavior. The findings show that machine learning-based techniques are successful at predicting the mechanical properties of rubberized concrete and have the potential to enhance environmentally friendly construction through the efficient reuse of WTR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40897-6.Keywords: Gene expression programming, Rubberized concrete, Random forest, Waste tyre, Machine learning, Mechanical propertiesSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The building sector is expanding quickly and contributes significantly to greenhouse gas emissions since it uses around 33% of the world’s energy1. Concrete is frequently utilized in construction for drainage, rehabilitation, and structural purposes2. Natural resources are used in its production, and demand for it is increasing continuously. The European Union has placed limitations on the use of virgin aggregates and actively promotes the use of recycled materials in construction to reduce the excessive consumption of natural resources3. To solve this issue, the building industry should adopt the concept of sustainability to avoid excessive consumption trends4. As a result, researchers are working hard to find alternatives for aggregates and cement in concrete mixtures to reduce natural resource depletion and energy consumption. Similarly, growing urbanization and technical developments in industries such as transportation, mining, and agriculture have resulted in a significant increase in solid waste generation5. By 2050, approximately 70% of the world’s population is expected to live in cities, which will undoubtedly increase transportation demand and vehicle ownership6. As a result, the production of waste tyre rubber (WTR) has become a major global environmental issue. Shen et al.7 and Siddique et al.8 estimate that 1.5 billion tyres are produced annually worldwide. Approximately 1 billion waste tires are created each year in affluent nations; in these areas, the rate of waste tire output is one tire per passenger5. On the other hand, there are between 200 and 300 million worn-out tyres in the United States9, while Turkey is anticipated to have over 10 million waste tyres10, and the EU roughly 3.4 million11. By 2030, it is projected that there will be 1.2 billion worn tyres, with approximately 4 billion already buried in landfills around the world. According to the European Association of Tyre and Rubber Businesses, 3.2 million tons of worn tyres were disposed of in 2009. The materials recovered from these tyres were remarkably high, accounting for 96% of the total. Of this total, 18% was reused or reprocessed, 38% was recycled, and 40% was converted into energy12. Despite these initiatives, illegal tire dumping and landfilling are still common in many developing countries, bringing major dangers such as vector-borne diseases, soil contamination, and fire hazards13,14.

Fortunately, by incorporating used rubber into products like concrete and asphalt, the construction sector can potentially lessen the impact15,16. This lowers the amount of waste tyres that would have been dumped into the environment and conserves basic materials. Rubberized concrete, which is environmentally friendly and enhances properties like shock and sound resistance, wear and tear resistance, and thermal properties, improves the energy performance of the structures when waste tyre powder is added in the right amounts to concrete mixtures. This lowers the amount of waste tyres that would have been dumped in the environment and conserves basic materials. Rubberized concrete, which is environmentally friendly and enhances qualities like shock and sound resistance, wear and tear resistance, and thermal properties, improves the energy performance of the structures when waste tyre powder is added in the right amounts to concrete mixtures17,18. Despite these benefits, the mechanical properties of rubberized concrete, particularly flexural strength (FS) and split tensile strength (STS) are strongly controlled by a variety of interacting parameters such as rubber content, particle size, and mix proportions. Experimental research of these features is both time-consuming and expensive, which encourages the use of predictive modeling tools. However, a few studies have concentrated on rubberized concrete and attempted to simulate its properties through machine learning (ML). For instance, Jingkui Zhang et al.19 utilized extreme learning machine (ELM) models to forecast 28-day compressive, split tensile, and flexural strengths, incorporating variables such as rubber content, particle size, and polypropylene fiber content, achieving high accuracy and robustness. Another investigation20 employed ML methods to model the mechanical properties of rubberized concrete, including flexural and tensile strengths, highlighting the potential of these techniques in material property prediction. Additionally, Miladirad et al.21 research applied machine learning methods, including neuro-fuzzy system (NFS) and artificial neural network (ANN) to model the mechanical properties of rubberized concrete, including flexural and split tensile strengths, highlighting the potential of these techniques in rubberized concrete properties prediction. Ghanizadehe et al.22 used artificial neural networks (ANN) and support vector machines (SVM) to solve the problem of forecasting the compressive strength of rubberized concrete. Similarly, to model the compressive strength of rubberized concrete, Amlashi et al.23 used four machine learning algorithms: SVM, Multigene Genetic Programming (MGGP), Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH), and Response Surface Methodology (RSM). Out of the three models, they discovered that the SVM model performed better. Amlashi et al.‘s study24 used SVM and adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS) approaches, which were improved by particle swarm optimization, to evaluate the performance of rubberized concrete. The majority of the research in this corpus focused on methods that use neural networks, which by their very nature lack the interpretability and transparency of any machine learning model. Additionally, there is a greater chance of overfitting when using ANN approaches25,26. The majority of current research is on individual modeling approaches and provides limited systematic comparison between interpretable evolutionary models with ensemble learning methods for forecasting the flexural and split tensile strengths of rubberized concrete, despite the increasing usage of machine learning techniques. Moreover, numerous current research depend on very small or heterogeneous datasets, constrained validation methodologies, and insufficient model-agnostic interpretability assessments, hence limiting their practical engineering usefulness. These restrictions draw attention to a glaring research gap in the creation of reliable, comprehensible, and thoroughly verified predictive models for the mechanical characteristics of rubberized concrete.

Gene Expression Programming (GEP) is a powerful machine learning technique that uses symbolic regression to produce explicit mathematical expressions. It is based on Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Genetic Programming (GP). A potent ensemble learning method called Random Forest (RF) combines several decision trees to increase prediction accuracy and generalization while lowering overfitting27,28. RF has been used successfully to forecast many concrete parameters, including compressive, tensile, and durability characteristics29,30. Nonetheless, there exists a lack of research that systematically contrasts GEP and RF models utilizing harmonized multi-source experimental datasets, augmented by explainable artificial intelligence technologies, for forecasting FS and STS of rubberized concrete. To bridge this gap, the current study creates a harmonized experimental database based on multiple published studies and conducts a structured comparative evaluation of GEP and RF models under identical modeling conditions for predicting the FS and STS of rubberized concrete. Key influencing parameters are used as model inputs, and the dataset is split into training and testing subsets. Several statistical indicators and external validation criteria are used to assess the model’s performance. Furthermore, Shapley Additive ExPlanations (SHAP) analysis is used to measure the relative significance of input variables, improving the interpretability and practical usefulness of the model. It is anticipated that the results of this study will offer reliable forecasting instruments and insightful information for the sustainable design and optimal use of rubberized concrete.

Gene expression programming (GEP) approach review

Genetic algorithms (GA) are a probabilistic technique for discovering and optimizing problem solutions using genetic principles38. Cramer developed Genetic Programming (GP) in 1985, which Koza later expanded to incorporate many forms and sizes, boosting its versatility39,40. nlike fixed-length binary strings, GP uses nonlinear patterns known as parse trees, making it a versatile problem-solving tool. It functions similarly to genetic processes, including mutation, crossing, and reproduction41,42. Unlike fixed-length binary strings, GP employs nonlinear patterns known as parse trees, giving it an adaptable problem-solving tool. It works similarly to genetic processes including mutation, crossover, and reproduction42,43. Mutation inhibits premature convergence of the model, ensuring its durability42. Figure 1 shows the overall procedure of the GP modeling method.

GP Flowchart.

Candida Ferreira44 introduced the notion of GEP as an evolutionary approach to computer programming. GEP is notable for its capacity to generate complex, tree-like computer programs or models with qualities akin to live organisms, such as adaptability and learnability. A significant characteristic of the GEP model is the use of simple mathematical expressions developed during the process. This results in accurate predictions and better predicting outcomes45. GEP is an evolutionary version of GP that combines fixed-length chromosomes from Genetic Algorithms (GA) and parse trees. GEP has certain variables with GP, but it varies in that it uses fixed-length strings rather than variable-length parse trees. The GEP algorithm separates genotype and phenotype, accelerating the process by just transmitting genotype to following stages. This method avoids the requirement to replicate and modify the whole structure for mutations46. Furthermore, GEP produces populations with a single chromosome containing several genes, known as head and tail. This leads to a direct one-to-one link between chromosomal symbols and their associated functions42. The chromosomes carry critical information for building causal relationships, which is processed using the Karva language. By supplying the gene sequence, one can determine the specific phenotype, known as Karva expression46. Figure 2 depicts the GEP approach in schematic form. This procedure entails randomly producing fixed-length chromosomes for the entire population, known as Expression Trees (ETs). Each chromosome’s fitness is then assessed. The people most ideal for reproduction are chosen, and this process is repeated with fresh individuals over many generations until the best answer is found. Mutation, crossover, and reproduction are the key genetic mechanisms that help the population evolve.

Random forest (RF) programming review

Breiman initially presented Random Forest (RF) regression in 200128, is a popular ensemble learning technique for classification and regression applications. RF has shown high predictive abilities in modeling nonlinear relationships between input and output variables. Previous research has quantitatively demonstrated that RF can reduce prediction errors compared to classic regression models and single decision-tree techniques28,47. RF generates many decision trees during the training phase and combines their predictions to provide improved predictive accuracy and robustness, as illustrated in Fig. 348. RF has been employed in a variety of industries, including banking to forecast client responses49, medicine/pharmaceutical business50, stock market price direction51, e-commerce52, demonstrating its versatility across multiple domains. RF is created by combining many tree predictors, with a random vector influencing each tree’s decision. This vector is sampled independently and uniformly dispersed throughout the forest28. Each tree in the forest is trained on a unique training set, which may produce somewhat varied outcomes53. During the training phase, each tree is built using a randomly selected subset of the training data, known as bootstrapped samples, which means that certain data points may be repeated while others are deleted. Furthermore, RF uses feature randomization to select a random set of features for each tree node split. This approach reduces overfitting while increasing tree variability.

Schematic of RF generation and prediction.

RF assesses its training performance using the Out-of-Bag (OOB) error estimation technique, which entails evaluating each tree’s predictions for data points that did not appear in the bootstrap sample54. The OOB error provides an unbiased estimate of the model’s generalization error. The final output is obtained by averaging the numerous tree forecasts for regression tasks55,56.

RF has various advantages, as evidenced by prior quantitative studies. Studies have found that RF retains good predictive accuracy while requiring relatively minimum hyperparameter modification when compared to other machine learning models. Furthermore, the independent creation of individual decision trees allows for parallel processing, which considerably enhances computing efficiency and scalability when dealing with huge datasets and high-dimensional data environments57.

Figure 3 depicts the process of the RF model as used in this study. To begin, the generated experimental dataset is separated into training and testing subsets to assess model generalization capabilities. The training dataset is then subjected to bootstrap sampling, which produces several randomized subsets of data. These subsets are utilized to build distinct decision trees, ensuring variation among tree predictors. Random feature selection is performed within each tree during node splitting to reduce tree correlation and improve model robustness. Each decision tree generates an independent forecast based on its bootstrap sample and input variables. The final anticipated answer is generated by aggregating the outputs of all trees using averages. This workflow represents the customized RF framework used in this study, which combines dataset partitioning, bootstrap sampling, random feature selection, and ensemble prediction aggregation to improve predictive accuracy and reduce overfitting when modeling the mechanical properties of rubberized concrete.

Methodology

After specifying the study objectives, a large dataset of reliable experimental investigations was assembled to train and assess the predictive algorithms. The dataset contained 112 data points for both FS and STS. The acquired data was preprocessed, statistically evaluated, and separated into training and testing subsets to ensure balanced representation and consistent model performance. This data splitting technique mitigates model bias and enhances generalization by facilitating independent assessment of model accuracy on novel datasets. The dataset was partitioned into training and testing sections with a 70/30 ratio. The partitioning was executed by random sampling to guarantee an impartial representation of the dataset. Stratified or grouped splitting was not utilized due to the dataset comprising independent experimental findings sourced from various literature. A fixed random seed value of 1 was employed during the creation of the Random Forest model to guarantee the reproducibility of the training process. Two advanced machine learning methods, GEP and RF, were used to create prediction models for evaluating rubberized concrete’s mechanical properties. To attain the best level of accuracy, the models were refined using iterative parameter tuning. The employment of two independent modeling methodologies facilitates a comparison evaluation between evolutionary symbolic regression and ensemble learning procedures, hence enhancing the dependability of model validation. Figure 4 depicts the study’s total workflow, highlighting the important stages of data collection, preprocessing, model construction, performance evaluation using statistical indicators (R, MAE, RMSE, RRSE, RAE), and external validation. In addition to depicting the workflow sequence, Fig. 4 demonstrates the comprehensive validation technique employed in this investigation. The integration of internal statistical measurements with external validation parameters enhances the robustness of performance evaluation and diminishes the risk of overfitting. Moreover, the incorporation of SHAP analysis improves model interpretability by quantifying the impact of various input parameters on strength prediction. This organized multi-phase architecture guarantees that the created models are both precise and transparent, as well as dependable for engineering applications.

Collection of data and input parameters selection

The creation of a dependable dataset is critical to the construction of a machine learning model. To build strong models, a comprehensive database was compiled from internationally published publications on rubberized concrete FS58,60–69,69 and STS58,60–64,64,70,72,72. This database contains 112 data points each for FS and STS. Based on earlier literature research, seven separate input parameters for model creation were identified: fine aggregate (FA), water to cement ratio (W/C),), coarse aggregate (CA), chipped rubber (CHR), crumb rubber (CR), super plasticizer (SP), and age. These input parameters were chosen because of their well-established effect on rubberized concrete mechanical properties and their constant availability across experimental experiments. Despite being influential factors, rubber particle size gradation, rubber surface treatment methods, cement content variations, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), admixture type, density, and air content were not consistently reported in the literature database. Such values would have lowered dataset size and weakened model stability and forecast reliability. Thus, the selected variables are the most consistently reported and statistically reliable parameters in the experimental dataset. The absence of these variables may affect prediction generalization for combinations with advanced rubber treatments or specific cementitious systems, as stated in the Study Limitations section.

The developed prediction models aim to estimate mechanical strength characteristics at different curing ages, rather than being limited to a 28-day strength evaluation, with curing age included as an independent input variable to reflect strength development behavior. Rubberized concrete’s mechanical properties (FS and STS) are given as a function of Eqs. To guarantee consistency and comparability of datasets derived from various literature sources, all quantities of mixture components were standardized before modeling. The doses of CA, FA, CHR, CR, and SP were reported in kg/m³ according to standard concrete mix design standards. The rubber contents initially documented as volumetric replacement ratios or percentage substitutions in the source literature were transformed into corresponding mass-based values to ensure consistent dataset representation. Likewise, superplasticizer dosages expressed as a percentage of cement or binder content were translated into kg/m³ utilizing the relevant quantities of cementitious materials.

The assembled dataset was randomly shuffled before model development to avoid ordering bias and balance input variables across training and testing groups. A predetermined random seed was utilized during dataset partitioning to ensure reproducible random splitting. Duplicate and inconsistent records were thoroughly eliminated during database preparation. The dataset was acquired from numerous distinct experimental experiments, however each data point reflects a unique concrete mixture and independent testing observation. Records were treated as statistically independent samples. Random data partitioning was done after preprocessing and validation to reduce data leakage between training and testing subsets.

Development of the RF model

The Random Forest (RF) model was created utilizing the WEKA machine learning software package. The RF hyperparameters were refined via an iterative tuning approach to enhance predicted accuracy. Throughout the tuning process, several combinations of essential WEKA RF parameters, such as the number of trees (iterations), bag size %, and tree growth configuration, were methodically evaluated. The evaluation of model performance during hyperparameter tuning included statistical performance metrics, including the correlation coefficient (R), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). The ultimate hyperparameter configuration was chosen for its balanced prediction accuracy, lowered error magnitude, and consistent performance across training and testing datasets. The quantity of trees (Number of iterations) was determined to optimize computational complexity and predictive efficacy. The RF model in WEKA was configured with bootstrap aggregation, with each tree trained on randomly generated data subsets. The bag size percentage was established at 100%, guaranteeing that each tree was trained using a bootstrap sampling of the complete training dataset. The maximum tree depth was not constrained (maximum depth = 0), permitting trees to develop entirely and include nonlinear interactions. To enhance transparency and reproducibility, Table 4 presents comprehensive configuration options accompanied by functional descriptions of each parameter utilized in the WEKA RF model. While Random Forest algorithms typically leverage Out-of-Bag (OOB) error for internal validation, this study did not include OOB estimation. Section 3 (Random Forest (RF) programming review) presents the theoretical framework and operational idea of OOB error to elucidate the general operation of the RF method. In this study, model performance was assessed by isolated training-testing data split, supplemented by several statistical performance measures, external validation criteria, and a comprehensive investigation of error distribution. This multi-tier validation methodology facilitates a dependable evaluation of predictive accuracy, generalization capacity, and model resilience across various datasets. This is consistent with findings from Liu et al. (2020), where RF was successfully used to predict the compressive strength of high-performance concrete, yielding robust results98. Additionally, it was demonstrated that RF is more effective in modeling complex mix designs by outperforming other regression techniques in predicting the compressive strength of self-compacting concrete99. RF demonstrated high predictive skills in rice husk ash-infused eco-friendly concrete (with accuracy frequently surpassing Gaussian Process Regression and Decision Tree models)100. Furthermore, while calculating the compressive strength of sustainable self-consolidating concrete that contains SCMs such fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), RF showed excellent reliability101. The appropriateness of RF and its adjustable hyperparameters for forecasting mechanical responses in concrete systems is supported by these investigations. GEP and RF models performance and external evaluation criteria

A range of statistical performance metrics, including the correlation coefficient (R), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE), relative root square error (RRSE), and relative absolute error (RAE), as shown in Eqs. (3–7), were used to evaluate the validity, predictive performance, and accuracy of the developed models. Together, these metrics quantify the degree to which the expected values match the experimental data. Because they can estimate the size of prediction mistakes, RMSE and MAE are the two statistical metrics that are most frequently used in machine learning-based concrete modeling30,102. By squaring the deviations, the RMSE highlights bigger errors, but the MAE offers a more balanced perspective of accuracy by providing a linear measure of the average difference between projected and observed values. Higher precision and dependability of the predictive model are indicated by lower RMSE and MAE values. The strength and direction of the linear relationship between expected and actual outputs are evaluated by the correlation coefficient (R), where values around unity indicate a perfect fit. Similar methods have been used in earlier research on the prediction of concrete strength using GEP103, MEP104, and RF105,106, demonstrating that these statistical characteristics are trustworthy markers for verifying model performance during both training and testing phases. Higher model accuracy is indicated by an R-value near one, which indicates a strong connection between the variables. R should not be used exclusively to assess the model’s performance since it is insensitive to the multiplication and division of output by a constant. Therefore, depending simply on R may lead to inaccurate performance interpretation. Rather, the mean error value is indicated by MAE and RMSE, where values close to 0 imply great model performance. Each error metric gives supplementary information regarding model accuracy. To guarantee clarity in performance interpretation, it is essential to differentiate between the correlation coefficient (R) and the coefficient of determination (R²). The correlation coefficient denotes the strength of the linear relationship between predicted and experimental results, whilst R² signifies the fraction of variance elucidated by the model. External validation metrics (k, k′, R₀², and Rm) offer supplementary assessment of predictive calibration and dependability. Shapely additive explanation (SHAP)

A technique for assessing machine learning models called SHAP (Shapely Additive Explanations) was presented by Lundberg and Lee112. All efforts to develop reliable estimating tools have been captured by machine learning models’ ability to learn from observed data and predict results in new data. However, the majority of ML modeling techniques have poor interpretability and excessive complexity. The explainability of prediction models is enhanced by the SHAP approach. SHAP is used in this study to determine the significance of the input parameters and how they affect the response parameter. Although a lot of machine learning research on building materials has achieved remarkable accuracy in predicting their goals, the interpretation of the ML models is not given enough thought. Several studies use the decision path and intuitive methods to calculate characteristic significance in machine learning models113. A Shapley additive (SHAP) based method was created to assess each input variable’s relative importance to the output and determine whether input variables improve the output in a positive or negative way. A thorough explanation of the SHAP approach can be found in references114,115. Each independent variable’s significance to the response characteristics is shown by the Shapley value. This method is similar to parametric analysis, which evaluates how changes in one input parameter affect the result by changing one variable while keeping other factors constant. In this work, the SHAP is used to provide interpretations of each input variable as well as parameter relevance. It also makes it easier to analyze how input factors either favorably or negatively impact the model’s output112,116,118–123,123. The average proportional contribution of an attribute value over all possible combinations is represented by SHAP, which is based on cooperative game theory. The average anticipated value of a variable with this attribute without the mean forecasted value of observations is called the SHAP number of a character. In order to facilitate interpretability, the output of an ML model is considered to be a linear sum of its input features scaled by matching SHAP values. SHAP analysis

A post-hoc explanation is required when models get more complicated. We used SHAP explanations to comprehend the fundamental logic of forecasts and how each variable affects the outcome. The relevance of the input parameters and their influence on the intended output are provided by SHAP interpretation, as will be covered in the following section.

This work utilized SHAP analysis through the model-agnostic KernelSHAP method, which is appropriate for understanding nonlinear and symbolic regression models, including GEP, as well as ensemble models like RF. KernelSHAP assesses the marginal contribution of each input variable by creating perturbed samples around observed data points and calculating weighted feature contributions to model predictions. The background dataset utilized for SHAP computation was derived from representative samples of the training dataset to maintain the statistical distribution and correlation structure of the input variables. This methodology guarantees that SHAP explanations accurately represent plausible combinations of mixture parameters. To assess the reliability of SHAP interpretations, SHAP values were analyzed over multiple sampling iterations. The relative ranking of feature relevance was similar across many evaluations, affirming the stability and dependability of the SHAP-based interpretability findings.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the use of GEP and RF approaches to develop models for predicting the FS and STS of rubberized concrete and to conduct a comparative study of both the models. The offered models are empirical and derived from a large database containing numerous parameters gathered from experimental research described in the literature. A thorough database was created using published literature, with 112 outcomes for each FS and STS of rubberized concrete. This study looks at the seven most important input variables: CA, FA, CHR, CR, SP, W/C, and age. Various statistical metrics, including R, MAE, RMSE, RAE, and RRSE, were used to assess the efficacy of the provided models, which were also validated using external standards. In addition, SHAP analysis was used to determine the importance and impact of input parameters on response characteristics. Finally, the created models were compared to earlier findings. The suggested models can accurately forecast the strength of concrete containing WTR, making them useful in practical applications. This research aims to repurpose WTR to create green concrete, promoting sustainable construction and environmental safety while reducing disposal costs, greenhouse gas emissions, and construction charges. Furthermore, this study is novel in creating a unified multi-source dataset and systematically comparing GEP and RF models under identical settings, offering a systematic alternative to time-consuming and resource-intensive laboratory testing. The predictive framework developed herein can be directly applied in practice to optimize mix design, contributing to the development of sustainable, high-performance concretes that align with global efforts to reduce carbon emissions in the construction industry.

Study limitations and future research direction

Heterogeneity of Datasets: Despite the robust predictive efficacy of the established Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Random Forest (RF) models, specific limitations must be recognized. The assembled dataset was derived from previously published experimental research, which inherently includes diversity in specimen preparation, curing conditions, source of materials, and testing methodologies. Despite meticulous data screening and standardization to reduce inconsistencies, a degree of dataset variability may still affect model generalization.

Nonetheless, resampling strategies based on cross-validation were not utilized. While many complementary validation metrics were utilized to assess model performance and predicted reliability, repetitive data partitioning or resampling validation methods could offer further statistical validation of model stability over diverse dataset splits. Subsequent research utilizing k-fold cross-validation or repeated resampling techniques may yield further understanding of model stability across various dataset partitions.
