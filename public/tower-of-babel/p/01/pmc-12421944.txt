Influence of biologic sex and obesity on liver recurrence and survival in patients undergoing upfront surgery for pancreatic adenocarcinoma
Authors: Sean J. Judge, Emily Manin, Joanne Chou, Robert J. Torphy, Caitlin A. McIntyre, Vinod P. Balachandran, Michael I. D’Angelica, Jeffrey A. Drebin, Mithat Gönen, William R. Jarnagin, T. Peter Kingham, Eileen M. O’Reilly, Wungki Park, Alice C. Wei, Alice Zervoudakis, Kevin C. Soares
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe influence of obesity and sex on outcomes in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains unclear. The association between obesity (body mass index [BMI], ≥30) and biologic sex (male or female) for outcomes in patients with PDAC undergoing a surgery‐first approach was investigated.MethodsA prospectively maintained pancreatic cancer database at the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center was queried to identify all patients undergoing surgery with a pathologic diagnosis of PDAC. Clinicodemographic variables, outcomes, and tumor mutational analyses for all available patients were collected. Cumulative incidence of first recurrence involving the liver was estimated via a cumulative incidence function. Multivariable Cox regression was used to investigate the association between BMI and sex for overall survival.ResultsFrom 2012 to 2022, 939 patients were identified who underwent surgery with a final pathologic diagnosis of PDAC. Median age was 70 years, 52% were male, and 24% were obese (BMI, ≥30). When dichotomized by sex and obesity status (BMI, <30 or ≥30), females with obesity had the lowest cumulative incidence of liver recurrence at 12 and 24 months postsurgery compared to all other groups (13% [95% CI, 7.2%–20%] and 15% [8.7%–23%], respectively). Females with obesity had the longest median overall survival at 37 months.ConclusionsAfter curative surgery for pancreatic cancer, females with obesity have a significantly lower rate of liver recurrence and the longest median overall survival. This does not appear to be related to surgical quality, receipt of adjuvant therapy, or tumor mutational profile. Investigation into host immune, metabolic, and hormonal parameters is paramount to understanding these differences.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity rates continue to increase nationally and on a global scale, with growing evidence of obesity‐related cancers. 1 , 2 Evidence supporting the impact of obesity on the incidence of pancreatic cancer is evolving, with most data supporting a moderate but positive association between body mass index (BMI) and the incidence of pancreatic cancer. 3 Additionally, being overweight (BMI, 25.0–29.9) or obese (BMI, ≥30) during early adulthood has been associated with a greater risk of developing pancreatic cancer and a younger age of disease onset, whereas obesity developing at a later age has been associated with a shorter overall survival (OS) in patients with pancreatic cancer. 4

Apart from incidence, there are limited and mixed data on survival outcomes in patients with pancreatic cancer on the basis of obesity. A recent matched‐pair survival analysis showed no association between obesity and OS when patients were matched by age, sex, and American Society of Anesthesiologists status, 5 although another study actually showed obesity to be independently associated with improved OS in those undergoing resection. 6 In contrast, for locally advanced and metastatic disease, obesity was associated with worse survival. 7 The impact of patient sex in the setting of obesity on outcomes in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) are also uncertain, although a recent analysis of more than 20,000 patients in a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results–Medicare–linked database showed an association between male sex and worse OS among patients with late‐stage pancreatic cancer. 8

Given the discrepancy in the literature and the growing understanding of the complex interaction of obesity and sex for host immunity and cancer pathophysiology, we hypothesized that there is a difference in outcomes on the basis of biologic sex and obesity status. We therefore investigated the association of obesity and sex for liver recurrence and OS in patients undergoing upfront surgery for pancreatic cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

After institutional review board approval, patients treated at the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) from 2012 to 2022 for resected pancreatic cancer were identified from a prospectively maintained database. Patient consent was not required for this retrospective analysis. We included all patients undergoing upfront surgery for diagnosed or suspected pancreatic cancer with a final pathologic diagnosis of PDAC. We excluded patients who had a final diagnosis other than PDAC upon analysis of the surgical specimen and patients who received any neoadjuvant chemotherapy. TNM staging was completed according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). Surgical margins were considered negative if the tumor was 1 mm or farther from the final surgical margin. Clinical and demographic variables were collected, including age and weight at the time of surgery. Patients were categorized by sex (male or female) and BMI (kg/m2). BMI was used to classify patients into six categories according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and World Health Organization. These classes were defined as the following: underweight (BMI, <18.5), normal (BMI, 18.5–24.9), overweight (BMI, 25.0–29.9), obese class I (BMI, 30.0–34.9), obese class II (BMI, 35.0–39.9), and obese class III (BMI, ≥40). The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated with an online calculator (MDCalc.com/calc/3917/charlson‐comorbidity‐index‐cci) on the basis of variables determined at the time of presentation for surgery. Additional outcome details were obtained from review of the medical record. These outcomes included details on adjuvant therapy and sites of recurrence. For those patients receiving adjuvant therapy outside our health system, internal and external documentation was reviewed to confirm initiation of therapy. Disease recurrence was determined by review of surveillance or diagnostic imaging reports. When radiographic recurrence was likely, the date and first site(s) of recurrence were recorded. Tissue diagnosis was not required for the determination of disease recurrence.

Tumor mutational profiling

DNA from the tumor specimen and matched normal blood or tissue was sequenced via the Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets at the MSKCC (MSK‐IMPACT), a clinically validated targeted next‐generation sequencing array that detects mutations, copy number variations, and selected rearrangements in specific, cancer‐associated genes. 9 , 10 , 11

Statistical analysis

Clinical and demographic characteristics at the time of surgery were summarized with the frequency and percentage for categorical covariates and the median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous covariates. OS was calculated from the date of surgery until the date of death or last follow‐up. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to examine univariate association between clinical/pathologic factors for OS. Multivariable models were constructed to evaluate the independent association between combined obesity and sex and OS. The model was stratified by the use of adjuvant therapy to account for subpopulation differences in baseline hazard, and adjustment was made for clinicopathologic variables that were correlated with OS on univariate analysis: vascular invasion, perineural invasion, pathologic T stage, pathologic N stage, surgical margin, and CCI score at baseline. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by plotting the scaled Schoenfeld residual against the log‐transformed time. Time to first recurrence involving the liver was estimated via the cumulative incidence function. Death without known evidence of recurrence or first recurrence to another site (lung, peritoneum, other site, or local recurrence) was treated as a competing event. To examine the association between combined obesity and sex for the time to first recurrence involving the liver, the competing risks regression model was used. 12

All statistical analyses were performed with R, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Figures and tables were created with Microsoft software and Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). All p values were two sided, and p values of <.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Patient and clinical demographics

Between 2012 and 2022, we identified 939 individuals who underwent upfront surgery with a final pathologic diagnosis of PDAC. Characteristics for all patients and delineated by obesity status and sex are detailed in Table 1. Overall, median age was 70 years (IQR, 63–77 years), 52% were male, and 88% identified as White. Median BMI was 26.2 kg/m2 (IQR, 23.3–29.7 kg/m2). The percentage of individuals in each BMI category was as follows: 2% underweight, 36% normal, 38% overweight, 17% obese class I, 5.5% obese class II, and 1.5% obese class III. Patients were categorized into two cohorts on the basis of BMI: BMI of <30 (“without obesity”) and BMI of ≥30 (“with obesity”). Between the two groups there was a similar distribution regarding age, sex, and race. In the group without obesity, median BMI was 24.9 (IQR, 22.5–27.0), and in the group with obesity, median BMI was 33.0 (IQR, 31.4–35.6). Median CCI score was 6 (IQR, 5–7), and values were similar between the groups with and without obesity and between male and female patients (Table 1).

Perioperative and pathologic variables

Perioperative variables are detailed in Table S1, and most were similar between the subgroups. Overall, 73% underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy, and the grade 3–5 complication rate was 12%. Operative time, estimated blood loss, and length of stay were greater in patients with obesity. There were no differences in the rate of grade 3–5 complications or in the rate of initiation of adjuvant therapy.

Pathologic details of the surgical specimens are detailed in Table 2. Most tumors were moderately differentiated (66%), followed by poorly differentiated (32%) and well differentiated (2%). Most tumor specimens showed vascular invasion (76%) and perineural invasion (92%), and final pathology according to the eighth edition of the AJCC was most often T stage 2 (65%) and N stage 1 (40%) or 2 (34%). A negative surgical margin was obtained in 59% of patients. The median number of counted lymph nodes was 22 (IQR, 16–30), and the median number of positive nodes was two (IQR, zero to four). There was no difference in the distribution of pathologic variables between patients with and without obesity, nor between male and female patients.

Time to first recurrence analysis

Documented recurrence data were available for 900 patients (96%), and there were 641 recurrences: 234 involved the liver, 117 involved the lung, 95 involved the peritoneum, 73 involved other distant site, and 122 were an isolated local recurrence. Those that recurred at more than one site were considered as other distant site and treated as a competing event, and 92 patients died without a known disease recurrence. Cumulative incidence of liver recurrence based on sex and obesity status is shown in Figure 1A. Overall, cumulative incidence of recurrence involving the liver was 20% (95% CI, 17%–22%) and 25% (95% CI, 22%–28%) at 12 and 24 months postsurgery, respectively. When stratifying by sex and obesity, we observed that females with obesity had the lowest cumulative incidence of liver recurrence at 12 and 24 months postsurgery (13% [95% CI, 7.2%–20%] and 15% [95% CI, 8.7%–23%]; p = .027) (Figure 1B). By using females with obesity as reference, we observed a significantly increased risk of liver recurrence for all other groups (females without obesity: hazard ratio [HR], 1.83; 95% CI, 1.05–3.21; p = .034; males without obesity: HR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.15–3.45; p = .015; males with obesity: HR, 2.42; 95% CI, 1.32–4.46; p = .004) (Figure 1C).

OS in nonobese and obese males and females

There were 659 documented deaths in our cohort. For the entire cohort, 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year survival was 83% (95% CI, 81%–86%), 44% (95% CI, 41%–48%), and 26% (95% CI, 23%–29%), respectively. Median follow‐up for surviving patients (n = 280) was 38.3 months (range, 0.3–143 months), with a median OS of 31 months (95% CI, 28–33 months). Median OS was lower for males at 27 months (95% CI, 25–31 months) compared to females at 34 months (95% CI, 31–39 months). OS was similar between those patients without and with obesity (median OS, 31 months [95% CI, 27–33 months] vs. 31 months [95% CI, 27–36 months]). When stratified by obesity status and sex, females with obesity had the greatest OS (median OS, 37 months [95% CI, 30–46 months]). Median OS for females without obesity was 33 months (95% CI, 30–40 months), for males without obesity was 27 months (95% CI, 24–32 months), and for males with obesity was 27 months (95% CI, 23–33 months).

On univariable analysis, there was no association between obesity status and OS, although combined obesity and sex was significantly associated with OS. Compared to females with obesity, males with obesity had an increased risk of mortality (HR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.02–1.93; p = .039). Additionally, male sex (HR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.06–1.44; p = .007), age at surgery (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01–1.03; p < .001), vascular invasion (HR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.41–2.06; p < .001), perineural invasion (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.00–1.03; p = .018), T stage 2 (HR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.25–1.94; p < .001), T stage 3/4 (HR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.54–2.59; p < .001), nodal stage 1 (HR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.17–1.76; p < .001), nodal stage 2 (HR, 2.13; 95% CI, 1.74–2.62; p < .001), margin positivity (HR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.56–2.46; p < .001), CCI score (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.03–1.09; p < .001), and initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy (HR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.44–0.65; p < .001) were found to be significantly associated with OS. In a stratified multivariable model, compared to females with obesity, only males with obesity had significantly worse OS (HR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.12–2.18; p = .009) (Figure 2). Vascular invasion (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.02–1.60; p = .034), perineural invasion (HR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.19–2.44; p = .003), tumor stage 2 (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.01–1.60; p < .045), tumor stage 3/4 (HR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.18–2.06; p = .002), nodal stage 2 (HR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.37–2.20; p < .001), margin positivity (HR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.17–1.62; p < .001), and baseline CCI score (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.01–1.08; p = .008) remained significantly associated with OS on multivariable analysis. The Schoenfeld residual plot did not suggest a nonrandom pattern against time.

Tumor mutational profiling

Genetic sequencing and analysis were performed in 481 patients in our study (51%). Of these patients, results were from 181 females without obesity (53%), 57 females with obesity (52%), 189 males without obesity (50%), and 54 males with obesity (46%). The analysis included both altered genes and pathways, and the most common identified in males and females, with and without obesity, are shown in Figure 3. Overall, KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A, SMAD4, ARID1A, and CDKN2B were the most commonly mutated genes, and there was no difference in mutation frequency between the subgroups. Similarly, pathways related to p53, the cell cycle, TGFβ, Wnt, and PI3K were most commonly altered overall, without any difference in frequency between the subgroups.

DISCUSSION

Results described herein indicate that there is a difference in outcomes when patients undergoing upfront surgery for pancreatic cancer are stratified by biologic sex and obesity. Females with obesity have the lowest cumulative rates of liver recurrence at 12 and 24 months after surgery, and these are significantly different compared to the other three groups. These differences in liver recurrence rates may translate to improved survival because females with obesity had the greatest median OS, although this was only statistically significant on multivariable analysis compared to males with obesity and not the other groups (females without obesity and males without obesity). To our knowledge, this is the first report of such a difference identified in liver recurrence rates and survival on the basis of both obesity and sex.

Investigation into the differential effects of obesity based on sex has been expanding after recent observations that obese males respond better to immune checkpoint therapy for melanoma. 13 Mechanistic studies investigating this observation with lean and diet‐induced obese mice supported this observation and implicated leptin signaling in T‐cell exhaustion, although this was apparent in both male and female mice. 14 In pancreatic cancer, where current immunotherapy drugs are generally ineffective, there do not appear to be any differential effects of conventional or novel chemotherapies on the basis of these variables. The majority of patients with pancreatic cancer are aged older than 60 years, and most females are postmenopausal. Female ovarian steroidogenesis has ceased, and plasma estrogen concentrations are driven by peripheral aromatization of androgens by subcutaneous fat. 15 Unique to our observations is the particular impact of sex and obesity on pancreatic cancer recurrence in the liver, one of the most common sites of PDAC recurrence.

The impact of liver recurrence on outcomes in resected pancreatic cancer is an area of intense research, given that prior studies have found that the site of recurrence is associated with survival, with multisite or liver‐only recurrence having the poorest survival. 16 , 17 The pathophysiology underlying the poor survival observed with liver metastases is unknown, and has been an area of basic, mechanistic research. 18 A recent clinical study investigating the effect of radiation to oligometastatic sites, including the liver, highlights the positive impact of treating the disease, and showed improved OS and alterations of the immune infiltrate in biopsy samples. 19 There is clearly a unique detriment to liver metastases in PDAC, and novel insight into liver metastasis formation and prevention is critical to improving outcomes.

Observations and mechanistic insight into the difference in liver recurrence rates between the sexes were recently reported by Hermann and colleagues 20 with data from the European Cancer Information System. The investigators showed that 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year OS was shorter for males compared to females with pancreatic cancer. Although OS was poor for all patients and the absolute difference between the sexes was small, the investigators observed that females had fewer metastases in the liver compared to males. By using mouse models and clinical samples, the authors attributed this difference in liver metastasis formation to the increased expression of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 in males. Distinct from our cohort, that study examined patients with synchronous liver metastases to reveal a difference in liver metastasis rates (44% female vs. 74% male). Our current study presented here strictly investigated outcomes after upfront surgery, which limits the investigation to metachronous recurrence patterns, and possibly represents a distinct biology compared to those presenting with synchronous metastatic disease. Other studies on the effects of obesity on liver metastasis formation have implicated lipodystrophy associated with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease as the culprit for enhanced metastasis formation in mouse models of breast cancer and melanoma. 21 Although investigations into this observation remain ongoing, the clinical importance of liver recurrence and metastasis formation for prognosis remains paramount.

Results from our study suggest that females with obesity have better outcomes compared to all other groups. We infer therefore that there is something protective within the obese female liver or metabolism that may limit engraftment of metastatic disease, inhibit metastatic cell growth within the liver, or augment the antitumor effects of systemic chemotherapy. There is evidence for a hormonal etiology, given that researchers using mouse models of estrogen receptor–negative breast cancer have shown obese mice having increased tumor growth that could be reversed with exogenous estrogen in ovariectomized female mice. 22 This supports the influence of hormones on tumor growth that may be altered with obesity and potentially reversed with pharmacologic intervention. With regard to the liver, others have shown that consumption of a high‐fat diet and the resultant weight gain lead to an increased incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma and an increased size and number of tumors in mouse models, which were more evident in males compared to females. 23 As the body of work on this subject accumulates, it is likely that the impact of sex and obesity on liver metastasis formation is multifactorial, with many contributing factors.

The limitations of this study lie with the inherent selection bias from a retrospective study, identification of disease recurrence by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, and our lack of knowledge of prediagnosis BMI in all patients. Weight loss is common with the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer and this could alter our cohort, with some proportion of patients with obesity measuring as nonobese at the time of their initial presentation to our center. To minimize the influence of weight loss in our study, we limited the selection criteria to only patients who underwent a surgery‐first approach and excluded those receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Therefore, patients deemed resectable were most likely to be in good physical condition and without significant, unintentional preoperative weight loss that may be indicative of locally advanced disease. As for the detection of disease recurrence, in those with obesity there are radiographic changes in the liver that affect the detection of metastases, particularly smaller lesions. 24 Because most patients initiate systemic chemotherapy after resection, hepatic architectural changes from chemotherapy decrease the accuracy of imaging in detecting metastases. 25 Taken together, the effects of obesity and systemic chemotherapy may create a lead time bias in recurrence. We postulate that the OS differences detected between males and females with obesity negate this limitation, and infer that the detection of metastatic disease is accurate because it portends worse survival outcomes measured between these groups. During follow‐up, we detected 10% mortality without documented cancer recurrence, which is higher than published data. 26 This could influence our results, with some “noncancer” deaths actually attributable to the underlying malignancy or the adjuvant therapy. We are limited, however, in making conclusions about recurrence or cause of death when the data are not available. Devastating outcomes such as cancer‐associated thrombosis may underlie some of these deaths (myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, and pulmonary embolism), although we do not have documented recurrence to definitively state that a recurrence occurred.

In conclusion, this study is the first to identify the differential impact of obesity on the basis of sex after upfront resection of PDAC. The impact of obesity and sex on liver recurrence rates may allow for personalized prognostication among patients with resected pancreatic cancer, and provides a framework for preclinical investigations into the mechanism of liver metastasis formation on the basis of clinically relevant variables, including biologic sex and obesity status.
