Pharmacy faculty students’ knowledge and attitudes towards HIV/AIDS: a multicentre cross-sectional study in Türkiye
Authors: Mefküre Durmuş, Aslınur Albayrak, Sena Güzel Karahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of pharmacists in delivering counseling and care services for patients with HIV/AIDS is becoming increasingly significant. The knowledge and attitudes of pharmacy students regarding HIV/AIDS are essential for the quality of future professional practices. This multicentre cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate knowledge and attitudes related to HIV/AIDS among senior pharmacy students in Türkiye.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among senior pharmacy students from three universities in Türkiye (n = 326). Data were collected using the Turkish HIV/AIDS Knowledge and Attitude Scale, whose validity and reliability have been previously established. HIV/AIDS-related knowledge and attitude levels were summarized using descriptive statistics, and associated variables were evaluated using multiple linear regression analysis. The threshold for statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 326 students from three pharmacy faculties were included in the study (Median age: 22; IQR: 22–23). Participants had median HIV/AIDS knowledge and attitude scores of 12 (IQR:9–15) and 51 (IQR:41,75 − 55) respectively. Overall, the findings demonstrate inadequate HIV/AIDS knowledge among students, notably in transmission and prevention domains and continued presence of negative attitudes toward individuals living with HIV/AIDS. Age (β = 0.604, p < 0.001), grade level (β = 1.282, p = 0.013), and having previously read anything about AIDS (β = 1.264, p = 0.047) were all positively associated with higher levels of knowledge. Being female (β = 3.842, p = 0.004) was associated with higher attitude scores, while 5th-grade status (β = -2.884, p = 0.011), religious belief (β = -6.988, p = 0.006), and low income (β = -3.779, p = 0.031) were significantly associated with lower (more negative) attitude levels.ConclusionIntegrating structured educational strategies into pharmacy curricula may help address existing gaps. Future interventional studies using controlled and pre-post designs are needed to evaluate their impact on professional readiness in HIV/AIDS-related care.

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and the associated acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) constitute a significant public health burden due to their chronic course and long-term care requirements. While a decline in newly diagnosed HIV cases has been observed at the global level, a marked increase in HIV diagnoses in Türkiye has drawn particular attention [1].

Access to healthcare services and continuity of care among people living with HIV/AIDS are influenced by multiple factors, including inadequate knowledge levels, stigmatization and socioeconomic conditions. These factors may restrict individuals engagement in diagnostic, treatment, and follow-up processes, thereby adversely affecting both individual clinical outcomes and the effectiveness of preventive interventions at the population level [2–5]. Pharmacists, as one of the fundamental components of healthcare services, are increasingly engaged in more frequent and active interaction with this patient population due to the growing demands for care [6]. The development of pharmacists’ competencies in the care of patients with HIV/AIDS during professional education has brought attention to need to examine pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding this issue. However, within the context of Türkiye, studies assessing pharmacy student’s knowledge levels and attitudes toward people living with HIV/AIDS remain limited.

Pharmacists play a critical role in global HIV/AIDS prevention strategies, and strengthening their knowledge and attitudes is essential for confident, effective, and sustainable contributions [7]. The importance of strengthening pharmacists’ roles in HIV/AIDS care is further supported by recent and interventional evidence. In the study conducted by Heelon et al., pharmacist involvement in the care of patients living with HIV was shown to improve treatment adherence, reduce treatment burden, and enhance medication safety [8]. Ma demonstrated that pharmacist-led interventions significantly improved medication adherence, CD4 + cell counts, and viral load outcomes among patients living with HIV/AIDS [9]. These findings emphasize that pharmacists’ contributions extend beyond dispensing to include active patient management and communication.

Existing studies demonstrate significant variability in pharmacy students knowledge and attitudes toward HIV/AIDS, with differences being linked to geographical, sociodemographic, cultural and educational determinants [10, 11]. Studies published in Saudi Arabia in 2024 have demonstrated that pharmacy students posses insufficient knowledge regarding HIV/AIDS, exhibit limited or neutral attitudes toward the disease and display risk perceptions that are not fully aligned with scientific evidence [12, 13]. Similarly, a study conducted in Türkiye in 2024 among medical, dentistry, and pharmacy students reported that students knowledge levels regarding HIV/AIDS were below expectations; however, the majority demonstrated a professional attitude toward patients [14]. Ahmet el al. assessed attitudes and risk perceptions toward HIV/AIDS among Malay and Chinese pharmacy students in Malaysia and found that discriminatory attitudes and perceptions were notably prevalent [15].

The limited availability of data examining pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes toward HIV/AIDS in Türkiye underscores the need for methodologically well-structed studies in this field. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate knowledge and attitudes related to HIV/AIDS among senior pharmacy students in Türkiye.

Methods

Study Design

This cross-sectional online survey was conducted in three pharmacy faculties in Türkiye between January 02 and January 15, 2026. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Karadeniz Technical University Health Sciences Scientific Research Ethics Committee (approval date: 17/12/2025, approval number:2025/197). This study included fourth and fifth-year undergraduate students over the age of 18. Undergraduate students in other years and graduate students were excluded.

Sample Size

The number of fourth and fifth-year students in the three faculties was 570. The survey included 104 students (57.7%) from İnönü University Faculty of Pharmacy, 98 students (53.2%) from Süleyman Demirel University Faculty of Pharmacy, and 124 students (60.19%) from Karadeniz Technical University Faculty of Pharmacy. Sample size calculations were performed using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7. The sample size was determined to be 244 students. A power analysis for multiple regression was conducted assuming a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s f² = 0.111, calculated from R² = 0.10), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a desired power of 95%.

Survey instrument and data collection

In this study, the previously validated Turkish HIV/AIDS Knowledge and Attitude Scale was used [16]. In our dataset the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the knowledge scale was 0.839, indicating good internal consistency. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the attitude scale was 0.825, showing satisfactory reliability. Permission to use the scale was obtained from the first author of the study. The survey consisted of 55 questions in total: the first 17 questions pertained to sociodemographic characteristics, 21 questions addressed HIV/AIDS knowledge, and 17 questions focused on HIV/AIDS attitudes.

The survey was created using Google Forms. All questions except the “If your answer is yes” follow-up questions were marked as required to prevent the submission of incomplete responses. In addition, the “limit to one response” option was activated to avoid duplicate entries. It was administered to students before classes. Before participating in the survey, students were informed about the purpose of the study, that participation was optional, and that data would be kept confidential. It was stated that refusing to participate would not constitute any pressure or punishment and would not have any academic consequences. Those who clicked the “I have read and agree” option in the survey consented to participate.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Social Sciences Statistics Package (SPSS) 20.0. Descriptive statistics used included median-interquartile range (IQR), number, and percentage. The normality of the data was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test. Since the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the two groups. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare three or more groups. Spearman’s rho correlation was used to examine the relationship between non-normally distributed continuous variables. Multiple linear regression models were established to identify the factors influencing knowledge level and attitude scores. The significance of the models, residuals, outliers, multicollinearity, and autocorrelation were examined. For the knowledge questions, the correct answer was coded as 1, while incorrect answers and “I don’t know” were coded as 0.

A 5-point Likert scale was used for attitude levels. A positive response receives 5 points, and the scores decrease sequentially (4, 3, 2, 1, respectively). A high score from this section represents a positive attitude. For the knowledge scale, questions 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, and 19 were reverse-coded. For the Attitude Scale, questions 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 13, 15, 16, and 17 were reverse-coded.

Results

This study was conducted in three pharmacy faculties in Türkiye, and 326 students participated. The majority of participants were female students (74.2%), and the median age was 22 (22–23). The median age of participants was 22 (IQR: 22–23). Most participants (60.9%) were fourth-year students. Most participants (%69.3) had grown up in urban areas. The education level of the participants’ mothers was mostly primary/secondary school (42.3%), and the education level of their fathers was mostly university (42%). The vast majority (%94.5) of participants had a religious belief, and most perceived themselves as middle-income (%81.9). Most participants (%85.6) had never had sexual intercourse before. Very few (%6.4) participants had previously been tested for sexually transmitted diseases or AIDS. The vast majority of participants had heard of or read about AIDS before (96.3% and 78.8% respectively). The median HIV/AIDS attitude score was 51 (IQR: 41,75–55). 37% of participants agreed that if they had AIDS, they would hide it from their friends. Very few participants agreed with the idea of living in the same house and marrying someone who has AIDS (11.7% and 4.9% respectively). 29% of participants said they would not want to work in the same workplace as someone with AIDS, and 36% of participants stated that if they were an employer, they would not hire someone with AIDS. Very few (%12.9) participants thought that having AIDS was something to be ashamed of, and very few participants (%15.6) stated that they would be embarrassed to go to the doctor to get tested for AIDS. Discussion

In this study, pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding HIV/AIDS were evaluated, and their sociodemographic characteristics were examined in relation to HIV/AIDS-related knowledge and attitudes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicentre study to assess HIV/AIDS-related knowledge and attitudes among pharmacy students in Türkiye. Although previous studies in Türkiye have evaluated HIV/AIDS-related knowledge and attitudes among healthcare students across multiple disciplines, including pharmacy, none have specifically focused on pharmacy students as a primary study population or employed a multicentre design [14].

More than 70% of the students correctly answered questions related to sexual transmission, HIV-associated immune system impairment, and the availability of antiretroviral medications. However, knowledge regarding other transmission routes, as well as awareness of the absence of an AIDS vaccine and the role of antiretroviral therapy in delaying disease progression, was insufficient among pharmacy students. These findings indicate a need for targeted educational interventions on HIV pharmacotherapy, HIV transmission routes and current preventive strategies to improve HIV/AIDS-related knowledge.

In a study conducted by Akbulut et al., investigated HIV/AIDS knowledge and attitudes among dentists, 57.7% of participants said “yes” as they thought they had adequate knowledge about HIV and its transmission, and their awareness of parenteral and sexual transmission routes was high (91.4%) [17]. Compared with that study, although most pharmacy students in our study were aware of sexual transmission, their knowledge of other transmission routes was comparatively lower. This difference may reflect variations in professional training exposure, as dentists are generally considered to be at higher occupational risk for HIV/AIDS. However, given the cross-sectional design of both studies and differences in study populations, direct causal explanations cannot be drawn.

In our study, HIV/AIDS-related knowledge was most commonly acquired through university-based education, followed by the internet. This finding is consistent with the study conducted by Akbulut et al. [17].

In the study by Özenci et al., the statements “Saliva can be a vehicle for the transmission of AIDS.” and “HIV can be transmitted through aerosols” were correctly answered by 48% and 11% of pharmacy students, 59% and 16% of dentistry students, and 56% and 21% of medicine students respectively. Additionally, “HIV/AIDS patients can live with others in the same place.” and “HIV/AIDS patients can lead a normal life.” were responded correctly among pharmacy, dentistry and medicine students at rates of 70%, 78% and 72% and 73%, 75% and 73% respectively. For comparable items, knowledge and attitude levels in our study were generally lower, ranging approximately between 37 and 40% for knowledge-based questions and 22%-42% for attitude-related items [14]. However, differences between studies should be interpreted cautiously due to variations in sample composition and institutional contexts. The Özenci et al. study was conducted in a single university and included a relatively smaller subgroup of pharmacy students, which may limit direct comparability.

HIV/AIDS-related knowledge levels among pharmacy students in our study appeared to be lower than those reported among nursing students. Nursing students demonstrated higher recognition of incorrect transmission routes -such as coughing, sharing personal items, swimming in the same pool, and kissing- at rates of 79%, 68%, 79%, and 64%, whereas correct responses among pharmacy students in our study were lower (58.6%, 43.6%, 34%, and 37.7%, respectively). Notably, a substantial proportion of pharmacy students incorrectly believed that an AIDS vaccine is available, whereas nursing students showed higher awareness on this issue. However, comparisons between studies should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in sample size, institutional context, and educational background. In particular, the nursing study included only 28 participant, which may limit direct comparability [18].

In a Malaysian study assessing pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding HIV/AIDS, female students constituted 79.6% of the sample, comparable to our study population (74.2%), and mean ages were also similar (23.7 vs. 22 years). However, correct responses to questions concerning AIDS transmission such as mother-to-child transmission, airborne spread, casual contact, kissing, shaking hands, and sharing utensils—were substantially higher in the Malaysian cohort (93%–98%) compared with Turkish pharmacy students (37.7%–64.4%). Importantly, although that study was conducted in 2009, reported knowledge levels were higher than those observed in our 2025 cohort, suggesting that differences in educational context and curriculum structure may contribute to variations in HIV/AIDS-related knowledge levels. In that cohort, there were no significant differences by gender except female students were more knowledgeable about post-exposure prophylaxis, while male students had better knowledge of the progression from HIV to AIDS. Additionally in that study, male students had positive attitudes regarding willingness to take care of HIV patients, females were more positive regarding confidence in their professional education to assist HIV patients [15]. In our cohort, older age, higher grade (5th ) and prior exposure to HIV-related information were predictors of higher knowledge levels, and female gender was a predictor of high attitude levels. We may therefore conclude that female students were more likely to exhibit positive attitudes toward people living with HIV.

AIDS attitudinal patterns were reported in a multicentre cross-sectional study that included university nursing students from the United Kingdom, Greece, and Italy, in which female students constituded 85.4% of the sample. Agreement with statements reflecting reluctance to work with HIV/AIDS patients ranged from 5.6% to 43.5% [19]. In our study, female students constituted 74.2% of the sample, similar to the distribution reported in that study. Comparable agreement rates of approximately 30% in our study suggest that Turkish pharmacy students’ attitudes may be broadly comparable to those of European nursing students; however, the persistence of stigma-related attitudes highlights the potential value of targeted educational strategies. In that multicentre study lower university grades (1st versus 2nd versus 3rd or 3rd versus 4th year) were associated with higher AIDS attitude scores. Additionally, only among Greek students, female students were found to have more positive attitudes on the negative emotions subscale [19]. Some results were consistent with ours, as we found female students and academic year (4th versus 5th ) were independently associated with more positive attitudes. In the study by Akbulut et al., female participants more frequently reported that it is necessary to be informed about whether a patient is patient living with HIV/AIDS [17]. This attitude may be related to perceptions of infection control and patient safety considerations rather than necessarily indicating discriminatory intent. Additionally, younger age was reported to be a predictor of feeling sufficiently knowledgeable about HIV and its transmission in the study by Akbulut et al. [17]. In contrast, we found that older age was independently associated with higher HIV knowledge levels. However, our study differed from the literature in that found religious belief and low income level associated with lower attitude scores.

Halpern et al. reported overall positive attitudes toward individuals living with HIV/AIDS across healthcare groups, with female students exhibiting more favorable attitudes in terms of fear and moral judgment. These findings are consistent with our observation that female students exhibited more positive attitudes than male students in our sample [20]. Although females represented a larger proportion of the sample, female gender remained an independent predictor of higher attitude scores after multivariable adjustment in the multiple linear regression model in our study.

A study conducted in Türkiye reported the presence of negative attitudes among nursing students toward individuals living with HIV/AIDS with the statements such as “Given a choice, I would prefer not to work with AIDS patients” and “I would rather work with a better class of people than AIDS patients” at the rates of 40.8% and 25.1% respectively [21]. Similarly, in our sample, a notable proportion of students expressed discomfort with contact, as reflected in agreement with statements such as “Working with someone who has AIDS makes me uncomfortable” and “I wouldn’t want to work in the same workplace as someone who has AIDS at the rates of 30.7% and 29.1% respectively.

Early research, as summarized in previous reviews, reported inadequate knowledge and negative attitudes toward HIV/AIDS among healthcare professionals and students, with fear of contagion and moral judgment identified as key determinants of discriminatory responses. These findings also emphasized the limited scope of formal education at the time and the potential role of structured training and clinical exposure in improving knowledge and attitudes [22]. The study by Frain reported that educational interventions among nursing students have demonstrated improvements in HIV-related knowledge and reductions in stigma [18]. In our study we didn’t analyze the impact of educational interventions on pharmacy students regarding this issue. However, we identified substantial knowledge and attitudinal gaps among pharmacy students, so we thought education on HIV/AIDS should be strengthened among pharmacy students. Lewis et al. found that although heterosexual college students demonstrated adequate knowledge about HIV/AIDS, they didn’t engage in protective behaviors such as condom use [23]. In our study, 70% of sexually active students reported condom use. However, no relationship was found between condom use and HIV/AIDS knowledge or attitude scores.

In another study, Peate et al. reported variability in attitudes and willingness to care among British nursing students, with associations observed between attitudes, homophobia, and prior caregiving experience. Furthermore, in Peate’s study, all nursing students correctly identified HIV/AIDS as a disease affecting the immune system, whereas correct responses to this item in our sample were lower (78.8%). Knowledge regarding the absence of transmission through casual contact also appeared lower among pharmacy students in our study (43%-63%) compared with the nursing students reported by Peate et al. (82%). Additionally, attitudinal patterns differed between the samples; in Peate’s study, 35% of nursing students reported no concern about contact with individuals living with HIV/AIDS, while nearly half of pharmacy students in our sample indicated a preference to avoid such contact [24]. These differences should be interpreted cautiously, given variations in study populations, settings, and measurement approaches. In that study, females comprised 83% of the sample, and both female gender and older age were reported to be associated with more cautious attitudes toward sexual risk behaviours [24]. In comparison, we found that older age was an independent predictor of higher HIV knowledge levels, whereas female gender constituted the majority of the sample and was independently associated with more positive attitudes toward people living with HIV.

In our study, not all students were aware that effective antiretroviral therapies can slow disease progression, and knowledge of transmission routes remained suboptimal. Attitudinal improvements are also necessary, given the close social and professional interaction required for patient education and adherence counseling. One plausible explanation for these gaps may relate to the limited recognition of pharmacists’ responsibilities in HIV/AIDS patient management within the community, as well as perceptions that pharmacists play a minimal role in HIV/AIDS care in Türkiye. In our study, religious belief and lower income level were independently associated with less positive attitudes. To our knowledge, this finding has not been previously reported. Furthermore age, 5th year of the faculty and having previously read anything about AIDS were independently associated with higher knowledge levels. To the best of our knowledge, previous studies have not comprehensively evaluated the independent determinants of knowledge and attitudes among pharmacy students, suggesting that our study adds valuable insight to the existing literature.

Evidence from the Global HIV/AIDS Initiative Nigeria (GHAIN) project suggests that structured integration of pharmacists into HIV/AIDS care, supported by education, defined practice standarts, and enhanced access to clinical information, can strengthen pharmacy practice capacity and patient-centered services [25]. Taken together, our findings highlight the need for targeted educational and role-oriented initiatives in Türkiye to address identified knowledge gaps and promote greater professional engagement in HIV/AIDS care. In this context, structured educational strategies such as targeted seminars, curriculum-integrated workshops, and scalable training models could be implemented to reduce existing deficiencies. Furthermore, future research would benefit from interventional study designs, particularly pre-post and controlled educational evaluations, to assess the impact of such initiatives on knowledge acquisition, attitude change, and professional preparedness in HIV/AIDS-related care. Additionally, future studies should incorporate structured educational variables such as curriculum content or infectious diseases coursework to better asses their impact.

A major limitation of this study is that only 4th- and 5th-year pharmacy students were included, preventing evaluation of the curriculum’s impact across all academic years. Although gender remained independently associated with higher attitude scores after multivariable adjustment, the predominance of female participants should be considered when interpreting gender-related differences. Given the inclusion of three universities and use of non-probability sampling, the findings should not be interpreted as nationally representative. Generalizability to all pharmacy students in Türkiye is therefore limited. Participation was voluntary, which may have introduced selection bias, as students with greater interest or awareness regarding HIV/AIDS may have been more likely to respond. Causal inferences cannot be established due to cross-sectional design of the study. Social desirability bias may have influenced the self-reported responses, particularly in attitude-related items. Additionally, our study did not include specific educational exposure variables such as curriculum content or infectious diseases coursework. However, the multicentre design and large sample size represent important strengths of this study. A methodological strength of this study is the use of multiple linear regression analysis with attitude scores treated as a continuous variable, allowing a more precise evaluation of independent associations.

Conclusion

This study found that pharmacy students’ knowledge and attitudes toward people living with HIV/AIDS in Türkiye were not at the desired level. This is concerning, as pharmacy students will become healthcare professionals who are expected to play an active role in HIV/AIDS patient management, including treatment counseling and the delivery of unbiased, patient-centered care. Moreover, our findings indicate that attitudinal gaps among pharmacy students are primarily driven by fear-based perceptions and social distancing tendencies toward individuals living with HIV. These findings suggest that educational efforts should not only focus on increasing knowledge but also address stigma-related beliefs, particularly misconceptions regarding HIV transmission. Curriculum-integrated stigma-reduction strategies, patient-centered communication training, and structured interactions with people living with HIV may help foster more professional, empathetic, and evidence-based attitudes in future pharmacists.

There is a notable lack of studies in Türkiye evaluating pharmacists’ knowledge and attitudes toward people living with HIV. Addressing this gap is essential to identify educational needs, reduce potential stigma, and support the effective integration of pharmacists into multidisciplinary HIV care.
