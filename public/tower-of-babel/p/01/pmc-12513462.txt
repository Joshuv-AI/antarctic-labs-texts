Targeting the ATM-TRMT10A-BRCA1 axis confers synthetic lethality to PARP inhibition in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer
Authors: Ying Yang, Qiang Liu, Xinyan Li, Hua Zhang, Xin Xu, Qi Ma, Somaira Nowsheen, Khaled Aziz, Ye-Xiong Li, Zhenkun Lou, Qiuzi Zhong, Min Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) progresses aggressively and resists existing therapies. Although poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis) benefit a subset of patients with mCRPC and BRCA1/2 deficiencies, therapeutic options remain limited for those without such mutations. Here, we uncover a critical role for the ATM-TRMT10A-BRCA1 signaling axis in regulating homologous recombination (HR) repair and PARPi sensitivity. We demonstrate that ATM phosphorylates TRMT10A at serine-28 after DNA damage, promoting BRCA1 recruitment and efficient HR repair. TRMT10A deletion disrupts HR repair, sensitizing cells to PARPis. Moreover, TRMT10A is up-regulated in mCRPC through stabilization by USP10. Targeting USP10 with spautin-1 induces TRMT10A degradation and enhances tumor sensitivity to PARPis in cell-derived xenografts and patient-derived xenograft models. These findings identify TRMT10A as a therapeutic vulnerability in mCRPC and demonstrate that combined inhibition of PARP and USP10 offers a promising synthetic lethal strategy for a broader group of patients lacking classical BRCA mutations.

INTRODUCTION

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death in men worldwide (1). Treatments targeting androgen receptor (AR) axis, such as enzalutamide or abiraterone, are initially effective but ultimately fail due to drug resistance, resulting in almost all patients eventually progress to metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) (2–5). mCRPC remains a highly fatal disease, with median overall survival (OS) rates ranging from 31 to 36 months (6, 7). Therefore, treating PCa, especially mCRPC, presents notable challenges in clinical practice, underscoring the critical need for targeted therapeutic strategies (8, 9). Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis) specifically target and inhibit PARP1 and PARP2 enzymes, crucial for repairing single-strand breaks (10, 11). By trapping these enzymes at the damage sites, PARPi prevent the repair of DNA damage, leading to the accumulation of double-strand breaks (DSBs) (12, 13). In cancer cells deficient in homologous recombination repair (HRR), such as those without functional BRCA1 or BRCA2, these breaks cannot be effectively repaired (14, 15), resulting in genomic instability and cell death. This mechanism, known as synthetic lethality (16), allows PARPi to selectively target HRR-deficient (HRD) cancer cells, offering a potent and specific antitumor strategy with reduced impact on healthy cells. Genomic studies have reflected that 20 to 30% metastatic prostate tumors exhibit alterations in DNA damage response (DDR) genes, such as BRCA1, BRCA2, and ATM (17–19), making them susceptible to PARPis (20). Clinical trials using PARPi such as olaparib, rucaparib, niraparib, and talazoparib have shown notable survival benefits for patients with mCRPC and BRCA1/2 mutations (21–24). However, not all HRR gene defects respond uniformly to PARP inhibition, as seen with ATM, CDK12, and CHEK2, indicating the variability in response depending on the specific HRR gene defect (25–28). Yet, BRCA1/2 deficiency only accounts for 10 to 15% in mCRPC (29), limiting the broad use of PARPi and hindering the advancement of precision therapies. Hence, exploring other approaches for treating mCRPC without BRCA mutations is critically important.

TRMT10A is a tRNA methyltransferase responsible for catalyzing the formation of N1-methylguanosine (m1G) at position 9 (m1G9) in tRNAs (30, 31). Deficiencies in TRMT10A function have been linked to developmental disorders, including intellectual disability and microcephaly (32). In our study, we identify TRMT10A as a crucial regulator in the HR pathway, independent of its known methyltransferase activity. TRMT10A deletion disrupts BRCA1 foci formation and inhibits HR repair, increasing the susceptibility of mCRPC to PARPis. In addition, we found TRMT10A to be up-regulated by the deubiquitinase USP10 in mCRPC. Targeting USP10 with spautin-1 reduced TRMT10A levels, leading to HRD. Combining PARPi with spautin-1 efficiently delays tumor growth in both patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models. Therefore, our findings advocate the combination of PARPi and spautin-1 as a promising strategy to extend the benefits of PARPis to mCRPC without BRCA mutations, representing a substantial advancement in precision cancer therapy.

RESULTS

TRMT10A is up-regulated in patients with mCRPC and is associated with PARPi response

Genes with phosphorylated SQ/TQ sites, which are ATM/ATR-mediated phosphorylation sites critical for DDR signaling, are linked to DDR and may play a role in chemo- and radiosensitivity. To identify such genes in aggressive PCa, we intersected a DNA damage dataset (33) (pSQ/TQ phosphorylation) with three PCa-related datasets: GSE62872 (34) (a gene expression dataset comparing PCa tissue to normal prostate tissue), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) (providing genomic and transcriptomic data highlighting TRMT10A up-regulation in aggressive cancer samples), and the TCGA high Gleason score subset (scores 8 to 10, representing advanced disease). This analysis identified 23 candidate genes, including TRMT10A, which emerged as a potential regulator of the DDR pathway (fig. S1A). Further analysis of the TCGA database revealed that TRMT10A expression was substantially higher in PCa tumors compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1A), with similar results in paired samples (fig. S1B). TRMT10A expression increased with tumor stage severity (Fig. 1B) and was substantially elevated in patients with metastatic PCa (GSE6099 dataset; Fig. 1C) and CRPC (Fig. 1D).

To validate these findings, we measured TRMT10A protein levels in 54 patient samples using immunohistochemistry (IHC). TRMT10A was predominantly localized in the nucleus (fig. S1C) and was expressed at higher levels in malignant versus nonmalignant tissues (Fig. 1, E and F, and fig. S1D). Furthermore, patients with Gleason scores 8 to 10 exhibited more intense TRMT10A staining compared to those with scores 6 to 7 (fig. S1E). High TRMT10A expression was correlated with substantially shorter OS (Fig. 1G, P = 0.014), indicating its potential as a prognostic marker. Genomic alteration analysis revealed that TRMT10A amplification and deletion occur in 1.22 to 5.26% of mCRPC cases across multiple cohorts, with no observed mutations (fig. S1, F and G). Collectively, these findings suggest that high TRMT10A expression is associated with aggressive clinical features and worse prognostic outcomes in patients with PCa.

TRMT10A expression correlated positively with key HR genes, such as BRCA1, BRCA2, RAD51, and PALB2, in tumor tissues (Fig. 1H), suggesting a potential role within the HRR pathway. Further analysis of cBioPortal data revealed no substantial co-occurrence or mutual exclusivity between TRMT10A alterations and BRCA1/2 mutations, indicating that TRMT10A functions as an HRR-related gene independent of BRCA1/2 mutations (fig. S1H). This suggests that TRMT10A may operate as an HRR pathway–related gene distinct from BRCA1/2 mutations and could be linked to PARPi sensitivity in PCa. To test this hypothesis, we quantified TRMT10A expression levels across a panel of PCa cell lines (35, 36) and assessed their sensitivity to olaparib (Fig. 1, I to K). We found that cell lines with higher TRMT10A expression exhibited increased resistance to olaparib, whereas those with lower TRMT10A expression demonstrated greater sensitivity. These findings suggest that TRMT10A may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting olaparib response in PCa.

TRMT10A deficiency sensitizes PCa to PARPis

To validate the relationship between TRMT10A and PARPi response, we conducted both knockdown and overexpression experiments across multiple PCa cell lines. First, we knocked down TRMT10A in 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 2A) and assessed cellular sensitivity to the PARPi olaparib. As shown in Fig. 2B, TRMT10A knockdown substantially increased olaparib sensitivity, producing a response similar to BRCA1 knockdown. Consistent results were observed in DU145 (Fig. 2, C and D) and C4-2 (Fig. 2, E and F) cell lines, confirming that TRMT10A depletion enhances cellular sensitivity to olaparib. Conversely, we overexpressed TRMT10A in PC-3 cells, which naturally exhibit low TRMT10A expression, by transfecting them with increasing doses of TRMT10A expression constructs (Fig. 2G). Clonogenic survival assays demonstrated that TRMT10A overexpression decreased sensitivity to olaparib (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that TRMT10A plays a critical role in modulating PARPi response in PCa.

To investigate whether TRMT10A affects PARPi sensitivity in the context of BRCA1 deficiency, we silenced TRMT10A in BRCA1 knockdown 22Rv1 cells (fig. S2A). BRCA1 knockdown alone reduced cell survival upon olaparib treatment; however, TRMT10A knockdown in these BRCA1-deficient cells did not further decrease cell survival (fig. S2B). This suggests that TRMT10A modulates PARPi sensitivity through the BRCA1-mediated HR pathway.

To further validate this finding in vivo, we subcutaneously injected DU145 cells expressing shCtrl or shTRMT10A into BALB/c nude mice to generate xenograft tumors (Fig. 2I). Treatment with olaparib suppressed tumor growth in the TRMT10A-deficient group compared to the control group (Fig. 2, J to L). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed substantial lower Ki-67 levels and higher cleaved caspase-3 and γ-H2AX levels in the TRMT10A knockdown group, indicating enhanced antitumor activity (fig. S2, C to F). No notable changes in body weight were observed between the groups (fig. S2G). These data demonstrate that TRMT10A deficiency sensitizes PCa cells to olaparib both in cells and in vivo.

Given the established role of TRMT10A in modulating HR, we investigated whether its deficiency would also confer sensitivity to ATR inhibition and chemotherapy of paclitaxel. We observed that TRMT10A knockdown in both 22Rv1 and C4-2 cell lines enhanced sensitivity to ATR inhibitors (fig. S2, H to K), suggesting a synthetic lethal interaction between TRMT10A loss and ATR inhibition. Furthermore, TRMT10A depletion markedly increased cellular sensitivity to paclitaxel (fig. S2, L and M). Collectively, these results demonstrated that TRMT10A also regulates cellular sensitivity to ATR inhibition and chemotherapy of paclitaxel.

TRMT10A deletion impairs BRCA1 recruitment and HR repair

Given that TRMT10A contributes to PARPi sensitivity, we hypothesized that it may regulate PARPi response by modulating DNA repair processes. To test this hypothesis, we assessed DNA repair efficiency following ionizing radiation (IR) by monitoring the dynamics of γ-H2AX foci, an established indicator of DNA DSBs. Specifically, we quantified γ-H2AX foci at two critical time points post-IR: at 1 hour to evaluate the immediate DDR and at 8 hours to measure subsequent DSB repair kinetics. ATM knockdown was included as a positive control given ATM’s well-known role in DNA damage signaling and repair. Our analyses revealed that the initial number of γ-H2AX foci formed at 1 hour post-IR was similar between TRMT10A-depleted and control cells, indicating that TRMT10A loss does not affect the immediate generation of DSBs. However, by 8 hours post-IR, although γ-H2AX foci were efficiently resolved in control cells, TRMT10A knockdown cells exhibited persistent γ-H2AX foci, indicating impaired DSB repair (Fig. 3, A and B). Collectively, these results indicated that TRMT10A affects DSB repair processes.

To further investigate the specific DNA repair pathway regulated by TRMT10A, we used a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter assay system (Fig. 3C). This system includes two distinct reporters: the SceGFP reporter, which measures HR repair efficiency, and the EJ5SceGFP reporter, which evaluates nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) activity. In TRMT10A knockdown cells, we observed a marked reduction in HR repair efficiency, as indicated by decreased GFP expression in the SceGFP reporter assay, and a concurrent increase in NHEJ activity, as shown by elevated GFP expression in the EJ5SceGFP reporter assay (Fig. 3, D to F). These findings highlight a shift from HR to NHEJ repair in the absence of TRMT10A. Notably, BRCA1 knockdown served as a positive control and produced similar results, further supporting the involvement of TRMT10A in the HR repair pathway. Notably, TRMT10A depletion did not alter cell cycle progression, as confirmed by cell cycle analysis (fig. S3A). This indicates that the observed defects in DNA repair are directly attributable to impaired HR repair rather than indirect effects mediated through cell cycle perturbations.

In addition to HR and NHEJ repair pathways, Microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) repair has been implicated in modulating PARPi sensitivity (37). Therefore, we also assessed the impact of TRMT10A depletion on MMEJ repair efficiency. Using an established MMEJ-specific GFP reporter assay, our data demonstrate that TRMT10A knockdown substantially enhances MMEJ activity (fig. S3B). This suggests compensatory activation of the MMEJ pathway in response to the acute induction of HR deficiency.

The HR repair process relies on the precise sequential recruitment of key DDR proteins, including the MRN complex (MRE11, RAD50, and NBS1), MDC1, RNF8, RNF168, BRCA1, and RAD51, to sites of DNA DSBs (Fig. 3G). To determine whether TRMT10A influences this recruitment hierarchy, we performed TRMT10A knockdown in U2OS cells and assessed the formation of DDR foci following DNA damage induction. Knockdown of TRMT10A did not alter the recruitment or foci formation of early DNA damage sensors and signaling proteins, such as γ-H2AX, MRE11, RAD50, NBS1, MDC1, RNF8, or RNF168 (Fig. 3, H to L, and fig. S3, C to P), indicating that TRMT10A functions downstream of the initial DNA damage signaling cascade. As expected, ATM knockdown, included as a positive control, substantially reduced the foci formation of γ-H2AX, MDC1, RNF8, RNF168, FK2, and 53BP1 (Fig. 3, H to L, and fig. S3, I to P), consistent with ATM’s established central role in early DNA damage signaling. TRMT10A depletion selectively impaired the recruitment and foci formation of BRCA1 and RAD51 (Fig. 3, M to Q), key mediators specifically involved in the HR repair pathway. This result mirrors the effect observed with BRCA1 knockdown, which similarly impaired RAD51 foci formation (Fig. 3, M to Q). TRMT10A knockdown did not influence the overall protein levels of BRCA1, RAD51, or 53BP1 (fig. S3Q), suggesting that TRMT10A specifically regulates the recruitment rather than the expression or stability of these proteins. Together, these findings suggest that TRMT10A may regulate HR repair by modulating the recruitment of BRCA1 (fig. S3R).

TRMT10A regulates DNA repair independently on its tRNA m1G9 methyltransferase activity

As TRMT10A is a tRNA m1G9 methyltransferase (38) (fig. S4A), we investigated whether the enzymatic activity of TRMT10A contributes to HR repair and BRCA1 recruitment. To explore this, we used a well-characterized loss-of-function mutant of TRMT10A to assess the role of its methyltransferase activity in DNA repair processes and olaparib sensitivity, based on a prior study (39). Specifically, we introduced a Gly206Arg (G206R) mutation in TRMT10A, which effectively nullifies its m1G9 methyltransferase function. We depleted endogenous TRMT10A and reconstituted cells with either wild-type (WT) TRMT10A or the G206R variant (fig. S4B). Immunofluorescence images revealed that both TRMT10A WT and G206R mutation restored foci formation of BRCA1 and RAD51 compared to the control group (fig. S4, C to L). Moreover, the DR-GFP reporter indicated that both TRMT10A WT and G206R mutation restored HR repair (fig. S4, M and N). This evidence indicates that TRMT10A’s impact on HR repair operates independently of its methyltransferase activity.

ATM phosphorylates TRMT10A at Ser28 and promotes the interaction between TRMT10 and BRCA1

Phosphorylation site data from PhosphoSitePlus indicated that Ser28 of TRMT10A aligns with the conserved pSQ/TQ sequence, a known ATM/ATR substrate motif, suggesting that TRMT10A may be a substrate for ATM/ATR (Fig. 4A). To test this, we transfected human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells with FLAG-TRMT10A, with or without the ATM inhibitor KU-55933, and assessed phosphorylation using a pSQ/TQ ATM substrate antibody. As shown in Fig. 4B, TRMT10A phosphorylation increased following irradiation, and this phosphorylation was blocked by the ATM inhibitor KU-55933, confirming ATM’s role in phosphorylating TRMT10A after DNA damage. To further validate this finding, we performed an endogenous immunoprecipitation (IP) assay in untreated and IR-treated cells. As shown in Fig. 4C, phosphorylation of endogenous TRMT10A also increased after irradiation treatment. Notably, irradiation-induced TRMT10A phosphorylation was substantially diminished upon ATM knockdown and further reduced with ATR inhibition (Fig. 4D). These data indicate that ATM acts as the primary kinase responsible for TRMT10A phosphorylation, with ATR kinase providing compensatory phosphorylation when ATM function is impaired. Consistent with this interpretation, the expression of a kinase-deficient ATM mutant markedly reduced irradiation-induced TRMT10A phosphorylation (Fig. 4E) and impaired HR efficiency (Fig. 4F), reinforcing the direct role of ATM kinase in mediating TRMT10A phosphorylation.

To confirm that Ser28 is the key phosphorylation site, we mutated Ser28 to alanine (S28A) and evaluated phosphorylation. As shown in Fig. 4G, the S28A mutation reduced ATM-mediated phosphorylation of TRMT10A, confirming that ATM phosphorylates TRMT10A at Ser28 after DNA damage.

Previous immunofluorescence analyses have shown that TRMT10A knockdown attenuates BRCA1 recruitment, and we hypothesized that TRMT10A may functionally interact with BRCA1. To test the interaction between TRMT10A and BRCA1, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. The interaction between TRMT10A and BRCA1 increased following DNA damage (Fig. 4H), and this interaction was abolished by the TRMT10A S28A mutant (Fig. 4I). These data support that TRMT10A phosphorylation at S28 mediates its interaction with BRCA1, emphasizing its role in HR repair. Together, these findings demonstrate that TRMT10A binds to BRCA1 via the Ser28 phosphorylation site mainly in an ATM-dependent manner (Fig. 4J).

Phosphorylation of TRMT10A is essential for BRCA1 recruitment and HR repair

Next, we assessed the role of TRMT10A phosphorylation in DNA repair by depleting endogenous TRMT10A and reintroducing either WT TRMT10A or a phosphorylation-defective S28A mutant. BRCA1 and ATM knockdown were used as positive controls (Fig. 5, A to C). TRMT10A WT restored HR repair efficiency, whereas the TRMT10A S28A mutant failed to do so (Fig. 5, D and E), suggesting that the enhancement of HR repair by TRMT10A is dependent on ATM-mediated phosphorylation.

We further investigated whether ATM-mediated phosphorylation of TRMT10A influenced the recruitment of BRCA1 and RAD51 to DNA DSB sites. After depleting TRMT10A and rescuing with either the WT or phosphorylation-defective S28A mutant, we assessed the foci formation of DNA repair factors postirradiation. Although γ-H2AX, FK2, and 53BP1 foci formation remained unaffected across both variants (Fig. 5, F to K), TRMT10A WT facilitated the restoration of BRCA1 and RAD51 foci following ionizing radiation treatment, whereas the S28A mutant did not (Fig. 5, L to O). As a positive control, ATM knockdown resulted in a substantial reduction of γ-H2AX, FK2, and 53BP1 foci (Fig. 5, F to K), whereas BRCA1 knockdown reduced RAD51 foci formation (Fig. 5, L to O). These results indicate that TRMT10A’s impact on the recruitment of BRCA1 and RAD51 is dependent on S28 phosphorylation.

In addition, we examined the effect of TRMT10A S28 phosphorylation on PARPi sensitivity. After depleting endogenous TRMT10A in 22Rv1 cells and reintroducing either vector control, TRMT10A WT, or the S28A mutant constructs (Fig. 5P), we performed colony formation assays to evaluate the impact on olaparib sensitivity. PARP1 knockdown was included as a positive control for assessing PARPi sensitivity (Fig. 5Q). The results revealed that reintroduction of TRMT10A WT reduced olaparib sensitivity, consistent with its role in promoting HR repair. In contrast, the TRMT10A S28A mutant completely abolished this protective effect, indicating that Ser28 phosphorylation is critical for TRMT10A’s function in modulating PARPi response (Fig. 5R). Notably, ATM knockdown did not sensitize PCa cells (22Rv1, C4-2, and DU145) to PARPis (fig. S5, A to F), likely reflecting that ATM loss only partially impairs HR repair capacity. Together, these results demonstrate that ATM-mediated phosphorylation of TRMT10A at Ser28 is essential for BRCA1 recruitment and efficient HR repair, ultimately influencing PARPi sensitivity.

USP10 interacts with and stabilizes TRTM10A

In our previous Co-IP mass spectrometry data, we identified that USP10 may interact with TRMT10A (table S1), suggesting that USP10 might function as a deubiquitinase for TRMT10A, potentially stabilizing it. To verify this interaction, we first confirmed the binding between USP10 and TRMT10A. HEK293T cells were transfected with either FLAG-USP10 or FLAG-TRMT10A plasmids, and Co-IP assays were performed using anti-FLAG beads. As shown in Fig. 6 (A and B), USP10 interacted with TRMT10A, confirming the physical association between these two proteins.

Next, we examined the role of USP10 in regulating TRMT10A ubiquitination and stability. USP10 was knocked down in HEK293T and 22Rv1 cells using two independent short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) (shUSP10#1 and shUSP10#2). USP10 knockdown resulted in a marked reduction in TRMT10A protein levels in HEK293T (Fig. 6C), and this reduction was reversed upon treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 6D). Similar results were observed in 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 6, E and F), indicating that USP10 stabilizes TRMT10A by preventing its proteasomal degradation. To further assess the effect of USP10 on TRMT10A protein stability, we performed a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay. Knockdown of USP10 resulted in a substantial increased degradation rate of TRMT10A protein in both HEK293T and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 6, G to J), confirming that USP10 stabilizes TRMT10A by preventing its proteasomal degradation. To check whether USP10 regulates TRMT10A ubiquitination, we treated these cells with proteasome inhibitor MG132 to stabilize ubiquitinated TRMT10A, which otherwise is rapidly degraded by the proteasome, thus enabling its detection by Western blot analysis. USP10 depletion markedly increased TRMT10A ubiquitination levels under these conditions (Fig. 6, K and L). Together, these results indicate USP10 may deubiquitinate and stabilize TRMT10A.

We observed a correlation between USP10 levels and TRMT10A expression in PCa cell lines (fig. S6A). To extend this finding to clinical samples, we analyzed USP10 and TRMT10A protein levels in tissue samples from five pairs of patients with metastatic PCa. The analysis revealed that both USP10 and TRMT10A protein levels were elevated in tumor tissue (T) compared to paired normal adjacent tissues (N) (fig. S6B). Furthermore, in a cohort of 46 patients with PCa, we examined USP10 and TRMT10A expression levels using IHC. As shown in fig. S6 (C and D), malignant tumor tissues exhibited higher levels of USP10 compared to paired nonmalignant tissues. We found a positive correlation between USP10 and TRMT10A expression levels (R2 = 0.4232, P < 0.0001; fig. S6, E and F). These data suggest a close relationship between USP10 and TRMT10A at the protein level. Together, our findings imply that USP10 functions as a critical deubiquitinase for TRMT10A, preventing its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby maintaining TRMT10A protein stability.

USP10 knockdown leads to HR repair impairment

Because USP10 is an upstream regulator of TRMT10A, we tested whether USP10 depletion affects DNA repair. As expected, knockdown of USP10 impaired HR repair and elevated NHEJ (fig. S7A). In C4-2 cells, USP10 knockdown did not alter γ-H2AX foci formation but reduced BRCA1 and RAD51 foci (fig. S7, B to H). In addition, clonogenic assays demonstrated that depleting USP10 in sensitized the cancer cells to PARPi treatment (fig. S7I). Together, these results suggest that USP10 knockdown impairs HR repair, reduces BRCA1 foci formation, and increases sensitivity to PARPis, which is consistent with the regulatory role of TRMT10A in these processes.

USP10 inhibitor spautin-1 treatment leads to TRMT10A degradation and HR deficiency

Given that TRMT10A is regulated by USP10, we investigated whether inhibiting USP10 with spautin-1 could induce TRMT10A degradation and impair HR repair. First, we treated 22Rv1 cells with varying concentrations of spautin-1 and observed a substantial reduction in TRMT10A protein levels, demonstrating that spautin-1 effectively inhibits TRMT10A stabilization (Fig. 7A). Notably, this effect was rescued by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 7A), confirming that spautin-1 induces TRMT10A degradation through a proteasome-mediated degradation. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis showed that spautin-1 treatment did not alter TRMT10A mRNA levels (Fig. 7B), indicating that the reduction in TRMT10A protein levels is due to posttranscriptional regulation, consistent with ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Given that USP10 regulates TRMT10A expression and TRMT10A affects DNA repair, we hypothesized that targeting USP10 with spautin-1 could affect DNA repair processes and PARPi sensitivity. To test this, we first assessed the impact of the USP10 inhibitor spautin-1 on DNA repair. We observed that HR repair efficiency was substantially reduced upon spautin-1 treatment (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, in 22Rv1 cells, spautin-1 treatment led to a marked decrease in BRCA1 and RAD51 foci formation, whereas γ-H2AX foci remained unaffected (Fig. 7, D to H). Similar results were observed in C4-2 cells (fig. S8, B to G). These results collectively suggest that spautin-1 promotes TRMT10A degradation and impairs HR repair.

Next, we investigated whether spautin-1 treatment could enhance cellular sensitivity to PARPis. We treated 22Rv1 cells with various concentrations of olaparib and spautin-1 and assessed colony formation efficiency. As shown in Fig. 7 (J and K), colony formation rates were substantially reduced in the cotreated groups compared to the controls. To quantify the synergistic effects, we performed synergy analyses using the SynergyFinder online platform (https://synergyfinder.org) to assess the interaction between olaparib and spautin-1 in 22Rv1 cell lines. The combination of olaparib and spautin-1 resulted in highest single agent (HSA) synergy scores greater than 10 in 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 7, L and M). Besides, dose-response matrix showing the most pronounced synergistic effects occurred in olaparib concentrations between 0.4 and 0.8 μM combined with spautin-1 concentrations between 2 and 4 μM. These data confirmed that the effect of the combination was synergistic, rather than merely additive. Similar synergistic effects were also observed in C4-2 cells (fig. S8, H to J).

In addition, considering that 22Rv1 cells harbor a monoallelic loss-of-function BRCA2 mutation (T3033Nfs*11) (40), which could potentially influence cellular sensitivity to PARPi alone or in combination with spautin-1, we generated stable 22Rv1 cells overexpressing WT BRCA2 (BRCA2-WT) (Fig. 7N). These cells were then treated with olaparib alone or in combination with spautin-1. The results showed that, in BRCA2 overexpressed 22Rv1 cells, spautin-1 treatment could still enhance cellular sensitivity to PARPis, as assessed by colony formation assays and synergy analyses (HSA score > 10) (Fig. 7, O and P). These findings suggest that the partial BRCA2 deficiency in 22Rv1 cells does not substantially affect the therapeutic response dynamics observed.

To further validate the synergistic effect of olaparib and spautin-1 in vivo, we established 22Rv1 cells xenograft tumors (Fig. 7Q). Mice were treated with vehicle, olaparib, spautin-1, or the combined therapy. We observed greater therapeutic efficacy in the combination group (olaparib + spautin-1) compared to either monotherapy alone (Fig. 7R). The combination index (CI) of 0.76 indicated an obvious synergy effect (Fig. 7S). This highlights the enhanced impact of the combination treatment on tumor growth suppression. Tumor tissues from the 22Rv1 CDX models were subjected to IHC staining and quantitative analysis. As shown in fig. S8 (K to N), the combined therapy group exhibited a marked decrease in Ki-67 levels and an increase in cleaved caspase-3 and γ-H2AX expressions, compared to the olaparib monotherapy group. Body weights remained similar across all treatment groups, indicating the tolerability of the combined treatment (fig. S8O). These findings confirm the synergistic effect of olaparib and spautin-1 in reducing tumor growth in the CDX model, further supporting the potential of this combination therapy in enhancing cancer treatment efficacy.

Notably, combination therapy exhibited minimal cytotoxicity in nontumor cells, such as human lung epithelial BEAS-2B cells (fig. S8P). In addition to normal cells, we also conducted a comprehensive toxicity assessment and off-target effect analysis in C57BL/6 mice. Given the high expression of USP10 in hematological and muscle tissues and notable TRMT10A expression in testicular tissue and lymphocytes, we conducted thorough histopathological analyses of multiple relevant tissues, including heart, brain, testis, spleen, liver, lung, and kidney. In addition, we assessed hematological parameters and bone marrow–derived cells to examine potential impacts on hematopoietic function. Our expanded analyses confirmed no notable histopathological abnormalities effects (fig. S9A).

Regarding hematotoxicity, fig. S9 (B and C) revealed that olaparib alone led to mild suppression, as indicated by a reduction in hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), white blood cell count (WBC), and neutrophil count (NEU). However, the combination therapy with spautin-1 did not exacerbate these hematological effects, indicating that the combination did not increase hematotoxicity. Similar results were found in bone marrow toxicity analysis (fig. S9D), no notable change in the average cell counts of erythrocytes, granulocytes, lymphocytes, monocytes/macrophages, or other bone marrow–derived cells with combined treatment compared to olaparib alone. This suggests that, although olaparib causes mild bone marrow suppression, the combination with spautin-1 does not worsen bone marrow toxicity.

Furthermore, we also performed flow cytometry analyses on splenic lymphocyte populations, which demonstrated that neither olaparib alone nor its combination with spautin-1 substantially altered the proportions or activation states (CD69 expression) of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets (fig. S10, A to H). These data support a favorable safety profile, providing greater confidence in the specificity and translational feasibility of targeting the USP10-TRMT10A axis.

PARPi and USP10 inhibitor synergistically inhibits tumor growth in mCRPC

Patient-derived tumor xenografts are essential in preclinical studies for evaluating drug efficacy and responses (41). To assess the synergistic effects of olaparib and spautin-1, we used PDX models derived from four patients with mCRPC with elevated TRMT10A expression (Fig. 8A). Among them, PDX models #546 and #1092, which exhibited high TRMT10A and USP10 levels (Fig. 8B), were selected for further investigation. In the PDX#546 model, the combination of olaparib and spautin-1 substantially suppressed tumor growth and reduced tumor weight compared to olaparib monotherapy (Fig. 8, C to E). IHC analysis showed lower Ki-67 levels and increased cleaved caspase-3 and γ-H2AX levels in the combination therapy group, indicating enhanced antitumor activity (fig. S11, A to D). Western blotting of tumor tissue also revealed elevated cleaved caspase-3, cleaved PARP, and γ-H2AX levels and decreased TRMT10A expressions in the combination therapy group (fig. S11E). There were no notable changes in body weight across treatment groups, indicating the tolerability of the combined treatment (fig. S11F). These findings were replicated in the PDX#1092 model, where the combination therapy led to a 42.9% reduction in tumor volume compared to olaparib alone (Fig. 8, F to H). Further IHC and Western blot analyses revealed similar enhancements in apoptosis and DNA damage markers (fig. S11, G to K), with no notable body weight differences between the groups (fig. S11L). Together, these results demonstrate that the combination of olaparib and spautin-1 substantially enhances antitumor efficacy in tumors with elevated USP10/TRMT10A expression, supporting the synergistic potential of this drug combination in mCRPC treatment.

DISCUSSION

Our study uncovers a critical role for the ATM-TRMT10A-BRCA1 signaling axis in regulating HR-mediated DNA repair and demonstrates the therapeutic potential of targeting USP10, the upstream regulator of TRMT10A stability, to induce HRD in PCa cells. Mechanistically, we show that TRMT10A is phosphorylated by ATM at Ser28 in response to DNA damage, facilitating the early recruitment of BRCA1 to DNA DSBs. Although previous studies have elucidated the critical roles of well-established regulators such as the BARD1 and RAP80-ABRAXAS complexes in modulating BRCA1 recruitment and stability at DNA damage sites, our findings introduce TRMT10A as a distinct, upstream mediator of BRCA1 recruitment. Unlike BARD1, which stabilizes BRCA1 at sites of DNA damage through direct ubiquitin recognition and formation of a stable heterodimer (42–45) or the RAP80-ABRAXAS complex (46, 47), which fine-tunes BRCA1 localization via ubiquitin-dependent recruitment, TRMT10A specifically functions through an ATM-dependent phosphorylation mechanism at Ser28, facilitating early-stage BRCA1 recruitment upon DNA damage.

Nevertheless, potential interplay or redundancy between TRMT10A-mediated BRCA1 recruitment and established regulators such as BARD1 and the RAP80-ABRAXAS complex should be carefully considered. Although our data robustly establish TRMT10A as a critical and independent factor driving BRCA1 recruitment, we cannot exclude compensatory interactions between these pathways under specific genetic or cellular contexts. In addition, early BRCA1 recruitment closely coordinates with DNA end resection factors, including CtIP, MRE11, and EXO1, which generate single-stranded DNA overhangs required for HR initiation. Precisely delineating how these DNA end resection components interface with and potentially regulate the ATM-TRMT10A-BRCA1 signaling axis would substantially enhance our mechanistic understanding. Comprehensive studies exploring these potential cross-talk mechanisms and the functional interplay with DNA end resection machinery will be prioritized in our future investigations.

We also acknowledge apparent discrepancies between our findings and previously published data regarding baseline PARPi sensitivity in PCa cell lines. Specifically, earlier studies (36, 48) reported greater intrinsic PARPi sensitivity in LNCaP and C4-2B cells compared to DU145 and PC-3 cells, whereas our analyses revealed the opposite trend. Such discrepancies in cell line responses are well documented (49–51) and can arise from intrinsic genetic differences, experimental conditions, cell passage variations, and other laboratory-specific factors (52). Crucially, despite these baseline differences, our findings consistently demonstrate that TRMT10A depletion enhances PARPi sensitivity across all tested cell lines, underscoring the functional importance of TRMT10A as a universal regulator of PARPi responsiveness in PCa cells.

Our study highlights a previously unidentified therapeutic strategy targeting USP10, a deubiquitinase that stabilizes TRMT10A, thereby indirectly controlling HR efficiency. Although USP10 has multiple substrates, including p53, Beclin-1, and AMPK, we demonstrate that its primary influence on HR repair and PARPi sensitivity arises from stabilizing TRMT10A. Notably, no other known USP10 substrates directly regulate BRCA1 recruitment or HR capacity, distinguishing our approach from existing HR-targeted therapies such as those involving BRCA1/2, RAD51, or ATR inhibition. Our method uniquely induces a “BRCAness” phenotype through the indirect disruption of BRCA1 recruitment, potentially minimizing compensatory DNA repair pathway activation frequently observed with direct HR inhibition strategies. Given the ~10% frequency of USP10 deletions observed in PCa cohorts (SU2C/PCF mCRPC), it becomes critical to consider genetic contexts that might influence therapeutic efficacy. Tumors with deep USP10 deletions inherently exhibit lower TRMT10A levels and may rely on compensatory mechanisms such as enhanced ATR signaling or alternative DNA repair pathways like canonical NHEJ or MMEJ, potentially affecting their responsiveness to further TRMT10A or USP10 inhibition. Consequently, stratifying patients on the basis of USP10 deletion status and evaluating biomarkers like TRMT10A expression and ATM-dependent phosphorylation will be important for optimizing clinical responses.

From a translational perspective, rigorous assessment of potential off-target effects and systemic tolerability of USP10 inhibition is essential. Our extensive toxicity evaluations, including histopathological analyses of vital organs and comprehensive hematological assessments, revealed no notable adverse effects. Moreover, mild hematological suppression induced by olaparib was not exacerbated by cotreatment with the USP10 inhibitor spautin-1, further confirming a favorable safety profile. These findings collectively support the clinical feasibility of targeting the USP10-TRMT10A axis in combination with PARPi therapy, especially when patient selection is informed by genetic profiling and biomarker assessment.

In summary, our study introduces a therapeutic strategy targeting the USP10-TRMT10A-BRCA1 axis to induce robust HR deficiency and PARPi sensitivity in PCa. The detailed mechanistic insights, identification of predictive biomarkers, understanding of genetic contexts affecting therapeutic efficacy, and comprehensive safety evaluations provided here advance our understanding of DNA repair dynamics and present opportunities for improving therapeutic outcomes in patients with PCa.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines

HEK293T cells (catalog no. CRL-1573) and U2OS cells (catalog no. HTB-96) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The human PCa cell panel was obtained from Wuhan Boster Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Boster Bio) and BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC). All cell lines underwent authentication via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (table S5), with catalog numbers as follows: DU145 (CX0116), PC-3 (CX0017), LNCaP (CX0095), 22Rv1 (CX0041), and C4-2 (BNCC338067). HEK293T and DU145 were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS); U2OS was maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium supplemented with 10% FBS; PC-3, LNCaP, and 22Rv1 were grown in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS. All the above cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2.

Reagents

Chemicals, including olaparib (MCE, HY-10162), CHX (MCE, HY-12320), MG132 (MCE, HY-13259), spautin-1 (TargetMol, T1937), KU-55933 (TargetMol, T2685), and ATR inhibitor (TargetMol, T2669) were purchased from the indicated vendors. Anti-TRMT10A antibody (1:1000) used in Western blotting was purchased from Proteintech (17294-1-AP). Other antibodies were used at the indicated dilutions: USP10 (CST, #5553, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804, 1:1000), HA (CST, #3724, 1:1000), KU55933 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML1109, 1:1000), cleaved caspase-3 (CST, #9664, 1:1000), cleaved PARP (CST, #9541, 1:1000), γ-H2AX (Sigma-Aldrich, 05-636, 1:1000), and Ki-67 (Abcam, ab15580,1:1000).

Shortly, lentiviral constructs, which include liposomal transfection reagent and lentiviral plasmid, psPAX2 and pMD2.G (at a ratio of 3:3:1) are cotransfected into HEK293T cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours posttransfection, the supernatant was collected and used for the indicated cell infection, and then puromycin (1 to 2 mg/ml) selection was carried out for 72 hours.

Immunohistochemistry

Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (approval no. K202311-45), and informed consents were acquired from all patients. A total of 54 metastatic PCa tissues and 30 corresponding adjacent noncancerous tissues, all of which were paraffin-embedded samples from patients with metastatic PCa, were included. These tissue samples were collected before any treatment, indicating that the tumors were hormone sensitive at the time of sampling. All patients in our study carried WT BRCA1/2, as confirmed through genomic profiling conducted before this analysis. The sections were incubated with antibodies (TRMT10A, 1:200 dilution; USP10, 1:200 dilution; Ki-67, 1:1000 dilution; cleaved caspase-3, 1:200 dilution; γ-H2AX, 1:200 dilution) by standard IHC procedures. The IHC staining scores were calculated on the basis of the percentage of positive cells and the staining intensity as described below, and the scores were evaluated by two independent experts under the same conditions. The staining intensity was defined as follows: negative = 0: no detectable staining; weak = 1: light staining that is barely perceptible; intermediate = 2: moderate staining that is clearly visible; strong = 3: intense staining that is markedly evident. The proportion of positive cells was defined as follows: <5% = 0; 5 to 25% = 1; 26 to 50% = 2; 51 to 75% = 3; and 76 to 100% = 4. The final score = staining area score x staining intensity score.

HR and NHEJ reporter assays

DR-GFP/EJ5-GFP reporter systems were used to detect HR and NHEJ repair efficiency. In brief, HEK293T cells stably expressing the indicated shRNAs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After 24 hours, cells were transfected with plasmids of 500 ng of DR-GFP or 500 ng of EJ5-GFP, plus 150 ng of mCherry and 500 ng of I-Sce1, which were further mixed with 1.0 μl of Lipofectamine 2000 per well. After 48 hours, the cells were harvested and analyzed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). There were at least 30,000 cells counted, and three independent experiments were performed.

Immunofluorescence staining and foci quantification

U2OS cells were seeded into 24-well plates containing coverslips 24 hours before irradiation treatment [1 gray (Gy)]. For staining, cells were first washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 solution by 10 min at room temperature. Next, samples were blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 hour and were incubated with indicated antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST), cells were incubated with secondary antibody, at room temperature for 1 hour. After washing three times with TBST, cells were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 1 min. After washing with PBS, cells were mounted with antifade solution on glass slides and visualized using a fluorescence microscope. Irradiation-induced foci (IRIF) were quantified by the ImageJ software. For each cell line, 10 random fields of view were selected, each group containing more than 100 cells, to standardize the foci counting and the integration of optical density.

Coimmunoprecipitation

For Co-IP studies, HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with FLAG-tagged TRMT10A or HA-tagged BRCA1 plasmids. The transfected cells were lysed with NETN buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.5% NP-40] containing 50 mM β-glycerophosphate, 10 mM NaF, and aprotinin on ice for 20 min. Whole-cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 30 min, and the soluble fractions were collected and incubated with HA beads/FLAG beads for 2 to 4 hours at 4°C. Beads were extensively washed at least six times with NETN buffer, boiled in 1x SDS loading buffer for 5 min, and then subjected to Western blotting analysis.

Western blotting

Cells were collected with cell scrapers and lysed in cold NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, and 1.0% NP-40 at pH 8.0). Protein quantitation was conducted using the BCA protein assay kit and measured at a wavelength of 562 nm. Protein samples were subjected to electrophoresis in 6.0 to 12.0% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Then, these membranes were blocked with 5% milk and incubated with indicated antibodies at 4°C overnight. After 1 hour of incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature, the protein bands were detected by chemiluminescence. For ubiquitination detection, FLAG-TRMT10A or vector plasmids were transiently transfected in HEK293T cells with USP10 knockdown. Forty-two hours posttransfection, cells were treated with MG132 (10 μM) for 6 hours. Cell lysates were prepared and subjected to Co-IP as described above.

Colony formation assay

The indicated cells at a density of 1000 to 2000 cells per well were seeded in triplicate in 6-well plates. For drug sensitivity assay, cells were exposed to olaparib/spautin-1 at the indicated concentrations. Ten to 14 days later, the clones were fixed and stained with crystal violet. Colony numbers were counted and analyzed.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Biosharp, BS350A). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then treated with various concentrations of paclitaxel (TargetMol, T0968) for 48 hours. Subsequently, 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 to 2 hours. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX). Relative cell viability was calculated as a percentage of the untreated control.

Cell cycle

Cell cycle distribution was assessed by flow cytometry following DNA content staining. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at −20°C for at least 2 hours. After fixation, cells were washed to remove residual ethanol and incubated with propidium iodide (Biosharp, BL114A) and RNase A in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. DNA content was analyzed using a flow cytometer (Sony ID7000), and data were processed with FlowJo software to determine the proportion of cells in G0-G1, S, and G2-M phases.

Irradiation

Cells were irradiated with 1 and 2 Gy for immunofluorescence assays and 10 Gy for Western blot and Co-IP detection. The irradiation doses were chosen on the basis of the prior literature (53, 54) and our experience in studying DNA damage over the years. Typically, cells were harvested 1 hour after irradiationfor immunofluorescence, Western blot, and Co-IP studies.

Tumor models of CDX

Animal studies were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Cancer Hospital (approval no. NCC2023A542). For DU145, shCtrl and shTRMT10A were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of 4-week-old BALB/c nude male mice (Hfkbio, 13001A). These mice were nongenetically modified and used for CDX. For 22Rv1, cells were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of 4-week-old BALB/c nude male mice. Mice were excluded if tumors failed to establish. The mice were treated intraperitoneally with vehicle or olaparib (50 mg/kg) or spautin-1 (20 mg/kg), on the basis of the prior literature (55, 56) or combined therapy for 5 consecutive days per week. Mice were monitored for tumor growth and overall health. After treatments, mice were euthanized, and tumor weight was measured.

Patient-derived xenografts

The collection of PCa specimens was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated People’s Hospital of Nanchang University (no. 2021012). In total, four patient tumor samples (#546, #1092, #1102, and #1267) were collected. Fresh excised tumor tissues (2 mm by 2 mm by 2 mm) were transplanted subcutaneously to 5-week-old male nonobese diabetic (NOD) severe combined immunodeficient (SCID) mice (Hfkbio, 13002A). Mice were monitored for tumor growth every 3 days, and once tumors reached 2 cm in diameter, tumors were aseptically collected and reimplanted into new mice. The initial tumor-bearing mice were termed “passage 0” (P0). The P3 generation of #546 and #1092 PDX models were used. When tumors measured 150 mm3, mice were randomly divided into groups for indicated treatment. Mice were excluded if tumors failed to establish. The mice were treated intraperitoneally with vehicle or olaparib (50 mg/kg) or spautin-1 (20 mg/kg) or combined therapy for 5 consecutive days per week. Tumor volumes of mice were measured every 3 days and recorded by the formula: 0.5 x length x width2.

Co-IP mass spectrometry

HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the FLAG-USP10 plasmid, and posttransfection of 48 hours, cells were collected and subjected to IP with anti-FLAG antibody for 3 hours and SDS-PAGE and Western blot for protein identification. Mass spectrometry proteomic raw data and the results files have been uploaded to the iProX database (http://proteomecentral.proteomexchange.org) with PXD identifiers (PXD059301).

Toxicity assessment in vivo

Five-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Hfkbio,11001A) with nongenetically modified were randomized into groups and treated with vehicle, olaparib (50 mg/kg), spautin-1 (20 mg/kg), or combination therapy for 5 consecutive days per week. Mice were excluded if they showed signs of unrelated illness before treatment.

Histopathological analysis (H&E staining)

After treatments, heart, brain, testis, spleen, liver, lung, and kidney tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 hours, dehydrated in graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were stained using the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (Beyotime, C0105S) using standard protocols. Histopathological evaluation was performed by two independent board-certified pathologists blinded to treatment groups.

Complete blood count

Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA-coated tubes (Bkmbio, BKM-7499) and analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XN-1000). Parameters included hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), white blood cell count (WBC), and differential leukocyte counts (neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes). Calibration and quality control were performed using manufacturer-provided standards.

Bone marrow smear analysis

Bone marrow aspirates were obtained from the sternum under aseptic conditions. Smears were prepared using a push-wedge technique, air-dried, and stained with Wright-Giemsa (Beyotime, C0133). Cellular composition (erythroid, myeloid, and lymphoid lineages) was assessed by counting ≥200 nucleated cells per slide using an Olympus BX53 microscope under oil immersion.

Splenic flow cytometry

Spleens were mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, filtered through a 70-μm strainer, and lysed with RBC lysis buffer (Servicebio, G2015). Cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies: anti-CD3 Percp-Cy5.5 (BioLegend, 100217), anti-CD45 FITC (BioLegend, 103107), anti-CD4 PE-Cy7 (BioLegend, 100421), anti-CD8 APC-Cy7 (BioLegend, 100714), and anti-CD69 Alexa Fluor 700 (BioLegend, 104539). Data were acquired on a flow cytometer (Sony ID7000) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1. Live cells were gated using DAPI exclusion, and T cell subsets were quantified as percentages of total lymphocytes.

Bioinformatics analysis

TRMT10A gene expressions and the corresponding clinical information in PCa were collected from TCGA project (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Differentially expressed TRMT10A counts in prostate and PCa tissues were collected from the GEO database with the accession numbers GSE6099 and GSE28680.

Generation of graphs and statistical analysis

Two-sided Student’s t test was used to compare differences between two groups, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences among multiple groups. Data were analyzed by using GraphPad Prism version 9.4. For cell survival and foci assays, data are represented as the means ± SEM of three independent experiments, and for the CDX and PDX xenograft study, data are shown as the means ± SEM of five mice. Kaplan-Meier curves for OS were plotted, according to TRMT10A IHC staining scores, and the log-rank test was used for analysis. The CI and synergy score under the HSA models were used to represent joint effect of combinational therapy in vivo or in vitro; CI < 1 or HSA synergy score > 10 indicates a synergetic effect.
