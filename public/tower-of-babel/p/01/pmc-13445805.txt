Prognostic impact of lymph node ratio after suboptimal lymphadenectomy in gastric cancer: a retrospective cohort study with interaction analysis
Authors: Yusuf Emre Aytin, Sezer Berkay Gorca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prognostic stratification after gastrectomy is crucial for individualized management of gastric cancer. Although examination of at least 15 lymph nodes is recommended to ensure reliable staging, this threshold is not consistently achieved in routine practice, potentially compromising prognostic accuracy. The lymph node ratio (LNR) has been proposed as an alternative nodal metric; however, its prognostic role in the context of suboptimal lymphadenectomy remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic impact of LNR in patients following suboptimal lymphadenectomy and to assess whether its effectiveness is influenced by the extent of nodal dissection.MethodsThis retrospective single-center cohort study included 210 patients with non-metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma who underwent curative-intent gastrectomy between January 2016 and January 2021, with a predefined 5-year follow-up period. Suboptimal lymphadenectomy was defined as the retrieval of fewer than 15 lymph nodes. Overall survival (OS) was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards models. Multivariable analysis was performed in the suboptimal lymphadenectomy group, and an interaction term (LNR × extent of lymphadenectomy) was evaluated in the full cohort.ResultsEighty-nine patients (42.4%) underwent suboptimal lymphadenectomy. Within this group, LNR independently predicted poorer overall survival (adjusted HR 2.50, 95% CI 1.14–5.47; p = 0.022), together with increasing age. In the overall cohort, a significant interaction between LNR and the extent of lymphadenectomy was identified (p = 0.030), indicating that the prognostic impact of LNR varies according to nodal retrieval adequacy.ConclusionsLNR represents an independent prognostic factor in patients following suboptimal lymphadenectomy and interacts with the extent of nodal dissection. These findings support the incorporation of LNR into postoperative risk stratification when lymph node retrieval is inadequate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04482-5.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Lymph node ratio, Suboptimal lymphadenectomy, Prognostic factors, Overall survival

Introduction

Gastric cancer continues to impose a considerable burden on global health systems and remains a major contributor to cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Despite progress in perioperative management and systemic therapy, curative outcomes still depend largely on surgical removal of the primary tumor accompanied by adequate lymphadenectomy. Precise pathological staging following gastrectomy is indispensable for accurate prognostic assessment and the selection of appropriate postoperative treatment [2].

The extent of lymph node clearance is a decisive factor in determining pathological stage accuracy. Current international guidelines recommend examining at least 15 nodes to ensure reliable nodal evaluation [3]. However, this benchmark is not consistently met in routine surgical practice. Variations in patient anatomy, tumor biology, procedural complexity, and institutional experience can all influence the number of lymph nodes ultimately retrieved and examined [4]. When the nodal yield is low, stage migration becomes more probable, thereby diminishing the reliability of pathological classification and prognostic estimation [5].

Given the inherent limitations of relying exclusively on the absolute number of harvested lymph nodes, increasing attention has shifted toward ratio-based nodal metrics. Among these, the lymph node ratio (LNR)—calculated as the proportion of metastatic nodes within the total nodal yield—has emerged as a clinically meaningful parameter. By simultaneously reflecting metastatic burden and the thoroughness of lymphadenectomy, LNR offers a more integrative assessment of nodal status than simple counts alone [6]. Several investigations have demonstrated its capacity to enhance prognostic stratification and, in selected settings, to outperform conventional pN staging. However, most available data derive from heterogeneous cohorts, and relatively few studies have specifically evaluated its behavior under conditions of inadequate lymph node retrieval [7–9]. Consequently, whether LNR maintains its discriminatory performance when nodal harvest is limited remains incompletely defined.

Moreover, the interaction between LNR and the scope of lymphadenectomy has not been comprehensively examined through interaction modelling. It is still uncertain whether the prognostic relevance of LNR changes according to nodal retrieval adequacy or whether it can compensate for staging inaccuracies in cases with limited node evaluation [9–11].

In light of these considerations, the present analysis focused on clarifying the prognostic relevance of LNR in gastric adenocarcinoma patients treated with suboptimal lymphadenectomy. Beyond standard multivariable survival modelling, an interaction-based approach was implemented to determine whether the impact of LNR on outcomes varies according to the adequacy of nodal retrieval. It was anticipated that LNR would preserve its discriminatory capacity even in the context of limited lymph node dissection.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This analysis was based on a retrospective cohort drawn from a single tertiary care institution. Electronic medical records and pathology archives were screened to identify individuals who underwent curative surgery for gastric cancer between January 1, 2016, and January 1, 2021.

A total of 331 consecutive patients who underwent gastrectomy for gastric malignancy during the study period were initially screened. All patients had non-metastatic disease (M0) at the time of surgery. Patients with non-adenocarcinoma histology (n = 88) were excluded. Among the remaining cases, 33 patients were excluded due to incomplete or inaccessible postoperative follow-up data.

The final study cohort comprised 210 patients with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma and its variants who underwent elective curative-intent gastrectomy and had complete follow-up data available. Surgical procedures included total gastrectomy, subtotal gastrectomy, and total gastrectomy with splenectomy, according to tumor location and extent (Fig. 1). In our institutional surgical practice, the intended extent of lymphadenectomy for operable gastric adenocarcinoma is D2 lymphadenectomy, and D1 dissection is not routinely targeted in this patient group. For the purposes of the present study, however, lymphadenectomy adequacy was evaluated operationally according to the number of examined lymph nodes, with retrieval of fewer than 15 lymph nodes defined as suboptimal and retrieval of 15 or more lymph nodes defined as adequate.

The principal outcome measure was overall survival (OS) over a five-year period. Follow-up data were administratively censored at 60 months after surgery, and any recurrence or death occurring beyond this timeframe was excluded from the survival assessment in accordance with the predefined study objectives.

Clinicopathological variables

Demographic, clinical, and pathological information was retrieved from institutional electronic databases and pathology archives. Collected variables included patient age and sex, the type of gastrectomy performed, exposure to neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy (including regimen details), primary tumor location and size, histological subtype, differentiation grade, and the presence of lymphovascular (LVI), vascular (VI), or perineural invasion (PNI). Pathological staging variables such as T and N categories and the final stage according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification were also documented.

For each case, both the total count of examined lymph nodes and the number of nodes with metastasis were recorded. The lymph node ratio (LNR) was then determined by dividing the number of metastatic nodes by the total number of nodes evaluated.

The extent of lymphadenectomy was stratified according to the quantity of lymph nodes assessed, categorising patients based on the completeness of nodal retrieval.

Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores were calculated for all patients, excluding the score attributed to solid tumour burden in order to avoid duplication of oncological risk. CCI was categorised as 0, 1–2, and ≥ 3 for univariate analyses and dichotomised (< 3 vs. ≥ 3) for multivariable modelling.

Statistical analysis

All statistical procedures were conducted using the Jamovi platform (Version 2.5.5; The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). The distribution of continuous variables was examined both graphically and through descriptive statistics. Variables showing approximate normality were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas those deviating from normal distribution were expressed as median values with interquartile ranges (IQR, Q1–Q3). Categorical variables were described as absolute frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between the suboptimal and adequate lymphadenectomy groups were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data, depending on distributional characteristics. Differences in categorical variables were analysed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval between the date of surgery and death from any cause. Follow-up data were administratively censored at 60 months after surgery. Survival probabilities were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and comparisons between curves were made using the log-rank test.

For graphical survival comparisons, the optimal cut-off value for LNR was determined using maximally selected log-rank statistics, identifying the threshold that maximized separation between survival curves. LNR was primarily analysed as a continuous variable in Cox regression models to minimise potential overfitting associated with data-driven categorisation.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to explore associations between clinical variables and OS. Initially, univariate analyses were performed to identify factors related to survival within the suboptimal lymphadenectomy group. Variables demonstrating clinical relevance or a p value below 0.10 in univariate testing were subsequently entered into multivariable models to determine independent predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and for continuous predictors, HRs were calculated per one-unit increment.

For multivariable modeling, variables showing clinical relevance and/or a p value below 0.10 in univariate analyses were considered for inclusion. To maintain model parsimony and reduce the risk of overfitting, the final multivariable models included age, pT category, vascular invasion (VI), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and LNR. Event counts for the multivariable analyses were 67 in the suboptimal lymphadenectomy group, 91 in the adequate lymphadenectomy group, and 158 in the overall cohort. For additional clinical interpretability, LNR was also rescaled and analyzed per 0.1 increase in supplementary multivariable models.

To assess whether the prognostic effect of the lymph node ratio (LNR) varied with the extent of lymphadenectomy, an interaction term (LNR × lymphadenectomy extent) was incorporated into a Cox regression model that included the entire cohort. A statistically significant interaction term was interpreted as evidence of effect modification.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption underlying the Cox regression models was formally assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests, with no significant violations observed (global test p = 0.194).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 210 patients with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma were included in the final analysis. Of these, 89 patients (42.4%) underwent suboptimal lymphadenectomy (< 15 examined lymph nodes), whereas 121 patients (57.6%) had adequate lymphadenectomy (≥ 15 examined lymph nodes).

The two cohorts were otherwise comparable in terms of sex distribution, exposure to neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy, and comorbidity status as reflected by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Variables considered clinically relevant, together with those showing a p value below 0.10 in the univariate analysis, were incorporated into the multivariable Cox regression model. As summarised in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 2, the lymph node ratio (LNR) retained its role as an independent predictor of overall survival (OS) (adjusted HR = 2.50; 95% CI, 1.14–5.47; p = 0.022). Neither pathological T classification nor Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) maintained statistical significance in the adjusted model, whereas vascular invasion showed a trend toward association with reduced OS. Additional survival analyses were performed in the adequate lymphadenectomy group and in the overall cohort to further assess the prognostic role of LNR. The numbers of death events included in the multivariable analyses were 67 in the suboptimal lymphadenectomy group, 91 in the adequate lymphadenectomy group, and 158 in the overall cohort. In the adequate lymphadenectomy group, LNR remained independently associated with overall survival when modeled per 0.1 increase (adjusted HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.03–1.19; p = 0.007), together with age (adjusted HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06; p = 0.001). In the overall cohort, LNR per 0.1 increase was likewise independently associated with poorer overall survival (adjusted HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.16; p < 0.001), alongside age, vascular invasion, and advanced pT category. Interaction between LNR and Extent of LymphadenectomyInte

To determine whether the prognostic impact of LNR differed according to the adequacy of nodal dissection, an interaction term (LNR × lymphadenectomy extent) was incorporated into a Cox regression model including the entire cohort. As presented in Supplementary Table S2, the interaction term reached statistical significance (HR = 1.82; 95% CI 1.06–3.14; p = 0.030), indicating effect modification according to lymphadenectomy adequacy. These findings suggest that the prognostic relevance of LNR is not uniform across surgical contexts but may vary according to the extent of nodal retrieval.

Discussion

Gastric cancer remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide despite advances in multimodal treatment [1, 12]. Accurate postoperative risk stratification is therefore essential for guiding adjuvant treatment and surveillance. Although TNM staging remains the standard framework, its reliance on absolute lymph node counts may limit prognostic precision when nodal retrieval is inadequate [13, 14]. In this context, LNR has emerged as a complementary nodal metric that integrates metastatic burden with the extent of nodal assessment [15].

Our findings suggest that the prognostic value of LNR may depend on the adequacy of nodal retrieval. Although LNR remained independently associated with overall survival, the significant interaction term indicates that its prognostic effect is modified by lymphadenectomy extent. This supports the interpretation of LNR as a context-dependent nodal marker influenced by both tumor burden and the quality of nodal assessment, consistent with previous studies examining LNR in relation to nodal yield and surgical extent [15–17].

Recent studies have questioned whether conventional TNM staging alone provides adequate prognostic stratification in gastric cancer, particularly when nodal retrieval is limited [18, 19]. Inadequate lymph node assessment may lead to stage migration and reduced prognostic precision [14]. In this setting, alternative nodal metrics such as LNR and LODDS have been proposed to incorporate both metastatic burden and nodal retrieval adequacy, and comparative studies have suggested that these approaches may outperform simple nodal counts in predicting overall survival [20, 21]. These observations are consistent with our findings, which support the added prognostic value of LNR, particularly when nodal harvest is limited.

Supporting this concept, several multicenter cohorts and large-scale retrospective analyses have validated LNR as an independent prognostic factor across diverse clinical settings [10, 22]. Comparative studies incorporating discrimination metrics such as C-index and AUC have reported that LNR exhibits improved prognostic performance relative to conventional pN staging [23]. These findings are consistent with our results, in which LNR retained prognostic significance in both univariate and multivariable analyses. The observed interaction between LNR and lymphadenectomy extent further suggests that its prognostic effect may vary according to nodal retrieval adequacy.

Previous studies have shown that depth of invasion and vascular or lymphovascular spread are important determinants of tumor aggressiveness and prognosis in gastric cancer [24–27]. In our adjusted models, age remained consistently associated with overall survival, while pT category and vascular invasion also contributed prognostic information, particularly in the overall cohort. These findings suggest that LNR captures an aspect of disease burden that complements, rather than replaces, conventional pathological factors. Taken together, the results support a multifactorial approach to prognostic assessment in gastric cancer and indicate that LNR may provide additional stratification beyond established tumor characteristics.

Advancing age also remained independently associated with overall survival, consistent with prior studies demonstrating its role as a patient-related prognostic factor in gastric cancer [27, 28]. Its persistence after multivariable adjustment underscores the contribution of host characteristics alongside tumor burden in determining long-term outcomes.

Nevertheless, variability among published studies persists, reflecting differences in cohort selection, surgical practice, and LNR cut-off definitions [29–31]. Some studies have suggested that the prognostic effect of LNR may vary according to the extent of nodal dissection [32, 33]. In addition, modified staging systems incorporating LNR, including nomograms and composite pathological indices, have shown improved prognostic precision, although large-scale validation remains limited [16, 22]. The cut-off identified in the present analysis reflects the distribution of our cohort and should therefore be interpreted cautiously until externally validated. These considerations underscore the importance of interpreting LNR in the context of surgical quality and support the need for prospective studies to standardize LNR thresholds and clarify their role in prognostic assessment.

The significant interaction identified in this analysis suggests that the prognostic role of LNR should be interpreted in the context of nodal retrieval adequacy. Rather than operating as an isolated marker, LNR appears to reflect both tumor burden and the quality of nodal assessment. These findings are consistent with a more context-aware approach to prognostic modeling in gastric cancer, in which biological and procedural factors are considered together.

From a clinical standpoint, the prognostic relevance of LNR may have implications for postoperative risk assessment. Because the TNM system relies primarily on the absolute number of metastatic lymph nodes, its discriminatory capacity may be reduced when nodal retrieval is limited. In this context, LNR may provide additional prognostic information, particularly in patients with suboptimal lymphadenectomy [22, 34]. Rather than directly dictating therapeutic decisions, LNR could help refine postoperative risk stratification and identify patients who may warrant closer surveillance. Integrating LNR into postoperative evaluation frameworks may therefore support more individualized clinical assessment, especially in settings with variable lymph node yield.

Emerging studies have also explored modified staging approaches that combine LNR with other nodal variables and may improve prognostic discrimination compared with TNM alone [16, 22]. Beyond serving as a numerical index, LNR may contribute to predictive models that account for both biological and procedural factors. Its reproducibility across Western and Eastern cohorts further supports its potential clinical applicability [17].

The study benefits from several methodological strengths, including a homogeneous cohort, standardised surgical and pathological protocols, and a predefined five-year follow-up, all of which reinforce the internal validity of the analysis. The application of multivariable and interaction modeling adds further rigour, providing insights into how nodal adequacy modifies the prognostic role of LNR—a topic seldom addressed in earlier work.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective single-center design introduces the possibility of selection and information bias. Although the sample size was sufficient for multivariable modeling, it may have been underpowered to detect subtle subgroup effects within the interaction analyses. In addition, variation in surgical expertise, pathological evaluation, and adjuvant treatment protocols could not be fully controlled and may have influenced survival outcomes. The single-population nature of the cohort may also limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, disease-specific and recurrence-free survival data were not available, and external validation was not performed. Finally, although the interaction between LNR and lymphadenectomy extent reached statistical significance, its underlying biological basis remains uncertain and should be explored in larger, multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the lymph node ratio (LNR) represents a meaningful prognostic indicator in gastric adenocarcinoma, particularly in settings where lymph node retrieval is suboptimal. The identified interaction between LNR and the extent of lymphadenectomy indicates that its prognostic influence may vary according to nodal adequacy. Taken together, these findings underscore the potential value of incorporating LNR alongside conventional TNM staging to enhance postoperative risk stratification when nodal assessment is constrained.

Although these observations add to the evolving evidence supporting LNR-based prognostic assessment, they should be interpreted in light of the retrospective design and single-center nature of the cohort. Additional confirmation through large-scale, prospectively conducted multicenter studies with extended follow-up will be important to strengthen the evidence base. Such investigations may also help clarify how LNR can be integrated into individualized prognostic frameworks and clinical decision-support strategies aimed at refining adjuvant treatment planning.
