Establishment of an accurate prediction system for gestational diabetes mellitus based on the characteristics of metabolic kinetics in early pregnancy: a prospective two-center cohort study in population according to IOM criteria
Authors: Junxiang Gao, Shuoning Song, Yanbei Duo, Xiaolin Qiao, Yuemei Zhang, Jiyu Xu, Jing Zhang, Xiaorui Nie, Qiujin Sun, Xianchun Yang, Ailing Wang, Wei Sun, Yong Fu, Mengmeng Zhang, Yingyue Dong, Zechun Lu, Tao Yuan, Weigang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop an accurate prediction system for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in women adhering to Institute of Medicine (IOM) weight gain criteria by analyzing early-pregnancy metabolic kinetics and identifying independent risk factors.MethodsA prospective two-center cohort study enrolled 1,031 pregnant women meeting IOM guidelines. Clinical, anthropometric, and metabolic parameters (pre-pregnancy BMI, lipid profiles, inflammatory markers) were collected at 6–12 weeks of gestation. Machine learning models were trained on seven core variables identified via logistic regression, with performance evaluated by AUC, sensitivity, and specificity.ResultsThe GDM group (n = 279) exhibited significantly higher pre-pregnancy weight, BMI, triglycerides (1.21 vs. 0.96 mmol/L, p < 0.001), and inflammatory markers. Multivariate analysis identified parity ≥ 2 (OR = 4.37), family diabetes history (OR = 1.64), third-trimester weight (OR = 1.13), and triglycerides (OR = 1.49) as independent predictors. A neural network model achieved the highest AUC (0.732) with 65.1% sensitivity and 68.9% specificity, outperforming logistic regression and other algorithms. Dynamic weight trajectories and lipid dysregulation were critical risk drivers, even in IOM-compliant women.ConclusionIn pregnant women adhering to IOM weight gain standards, ​early-pregnancy metabolic dysfunction​and specific weight trajectories ​persist as pivotal GDM predictors. Our neural network model integrating seven core variables—including triglyceride levels, dynamic weight patterns (6–10 weeks and 24–28 weeks), and clinical risk factors—achieved superior prediction accuracy compared to conventional weight-centric approaches. This system enables early risk stratification and personalized interventions during the first trimester, addressing a critical gap in prenatal care for guideline-compliant populations at residual GDM risk.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), Metabolic kinetics, Machine learning, Early prediction, IOM criteria, Obesity

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic disorder during pregnancy, with its prevalence on the rise globally, particularly among obese and older pregnant women. GDM not only increases the risk of complications such as hypertension and cesarean delivery during pregnancy but can also lead to issues like macrosomia and hypoglycemia in newborns [1]. Weight management plays a crucial role in the prevention and management of gestational diabetes.Studies show that pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI) and weight gain during pregnancy (GWG) are significant factors influencing pregnancy outcomes. Both excessively high pre-pregnancy BMI and excessive weight gain during pregnancy are associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as macrosomia, gestational hypertension, and cesarean delivery [2, 3]. Existing prediction models mainly focus on overweight and obese people [4, 5].Therefore, the Institute of Medicine (IOM) issued guidelines for weight gain during pregnancy. Weight gain is recommended separately for underweight, normal, overweight, and obese (28 to 40, 25 to 35, 15 to 25, and 11 to 20 pounds) women [6]. However, studies have shown that GDM may occur in some pregnant women despite their pre-pregnancy BMI and weight gain meeting medical guidelines.This may be related to other factors such as age, genetic factors, and dietary habits [7]. This paradox suggests that traditional weight-centered risk assessment systems may underestimate the independent role of deficits in metabolic compensatory capacity, particularly among persons who adhere closely to the Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines for weight gain during pregnancy.

The current diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) rely on oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 24–28 weeks of gestation, which seriously limits the window for early intervention [8]. As a result, pregnant women have already experienced compensatory exhaustion of β-cell function and irreversible progression of insulin resistance at the time of diagnosis, which significantly increases the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [9, 10]. Although recent studies have confirmed that there is a strong association between metabolic disorders in early pregnancy (< 12 weeks) and the incidence of GDM, there is still a lack of accurate prediction tools based on early biomarkers in current clinical practice, resulting in the identification of high-risk groups lags behind the critical period of pathophysiology.In conclusion, this study focused on the risk assessment of GDM in pregnant women with normal gestational weight change based on metabolic parameters in the first trimester. The aim of this study is to strengthen metabolic management during pregnancy to reduce the risk of GDM and its related complications.

Methods

This study was a prospective, double-center, observational cohort study conducted in two centers of Beijing Chaoyang Maternal and Child Health Hospital and Haidian Maternal and Child Health Hospital (clinical trial: NCT 03246295). The study was conducted from August 2019 to April 2021 in the above two hospitals. Women in early pregnancy were recruited, and eligible subjects were selected for inclusion. This study was conducted in accordance with the revised Declaration of Helsinki in 2013.

Subjects

The inclusion and exclusion criteria are as follows: Inclusion criteria: (1) Gestational age < 12 weeks at enrollment; (2) No diabetes before pregnancy; (3) Agreement to participate in this study by signing the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria: (1) Twin or multiple pregnancies; (2) Fasting blood glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L in the basal period; (3) Any acute or other chronic diseases, such as severe liver or kidney dysfunction, heart disease, autoimmune disorders, etc. Based on this, 2,284 pregnant women with complete clinical and laboratory data during 6–12 gestational weeks were selected. All the subjects underwent a 2-hour 75 g-OGTT at 24–28 weeks of gestation to screen for GDM. The diagnostic criteria were as follows: fasting venous blood glucose > 5.1 mmol/L, or venous blood glucose > 10.0 mmol/L 1 h after sugar intake, or venous blood glucose > 8.5 mmol/L 2 h after sugar intake. Any one of these blood glucose levels reaching the above standards would be diagnosed as GDM. Based on the OGTT results, the subjects were divided into the GDM group and the normal glucose tolerance (NGT) group.

The initial sample size of the study was 2,284 individuals. After excluding those with missing birth weight (905 individuals), missing baseline weight (10 individuals), weight gain during pregnancy not meeting the IOM standards (278 individuals), and weight gain in the first trimester not meeting the standards (60 individuals), a total of 1,031 participants were included in the study. These 1,031 individuals were divided into two groups based on their glucose tolerance status: normal glucose tolerance (NGT, 752 individuals) and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM, 279 individuals).All 1,031 participants included in the study were divided into a training set (722 individuals) and a test set (309 individuals) for model construction and validation.The perinatal database of 1031 pregnant women was collected from the electronic medical record (EMR)(Figure 1). The ethics committee of all participating centers approved the research protocol. This study was conducted under the guidance of the Major New Drug Innovation and Development Program (Clinical trial number: NCT03246295). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and this study was carried out by the revised Helsinki Declaration of 2013.

Population flow chart for predicting gestational diabetes

Clinical data collection

Medical history was collected at the first filing, and recorded by the research center in the form of electronic medical records, including maternal age, pre-pregnancy weight, pre-pregnancy body mass index(pre-BMI), date of the last menstrual period, gravidity, parity, history of adverse pregnancy and childbirth(Including gestational hypertension, low birth weight infants, premature birth, neonatal asphyxia, neonatal defects, and neonatal pneumonia), family history of diabetes, family history and general information. The pregnancy complications and combined medication, drug allergy history, smoking and drinking history were inquired and recorded.

In pregnant women for the first time to see a doctor when performed by specialist nurses anthropometry parameter acquisition, including maternal height, weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), Blood pressure was measured twice (at 5-minute intervals) with an automatic blood pressure monitor and then averaged. The electronic medical record of the study site was recorded, and the above measures were recorded at each subsequent visit and at delivery.

Laboratory indexes were determined and samples were collected

Early morning fasting blood samples were collected and measured at the first prenatal care visit(6–10 weeks of pregnancy). Blood routine, fasting plasma glucose, fasting C-peptide, fasting insulin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and serum creatinine(SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid, blood lipid [total cholesterol, (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)]. Blood samples of blood glucose, blood lipid, fasting C-peptide and fasting insulin were sent to the Clinical Laboratory of Peking Union Medical College Hospital for detection. Fasting blood glucose and blood lipid were measured by automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5800,USA). FCP and FINS were measured by chemiluminescence method (Siemens ADVIA Centaur XP, Germany). The remaining biochemical indicators were uniformly tested in the laboratory department of the hospital where the files were established. Except for the first filing, fasting blood samples were collected again at 12–14 weeks of pregnancy, 15–19 weeks of pregnancy, and 24–28 weeks of pregnancy for blood routine tests.

Diagnostic criteria for GDM

All subjects underwent a 2-hour, 75 g-OGTT at 24–28 weeks of gestation to screen for GDM. The diagnostic criteria were as follows: GDM was defined as fasting venous blood glucose ≥ 5.1mmol/L, or venous blood glucose ≥ 10.0mmol/L 1 h or ≥ 8.5mmol/L 2 h after glucose administration, if any of the above criteria was metP [11]. According to the results of OGTT, the subjects were divided into GDM group and normal glucose tolerance (NGT) group.

Statistical analysis

Data processing

All analyses were conducted in R 4.3.1. For missing data, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was used, with 5 imputation iterations and a maximum of 10 iterations. The random seed was set to 123 to ensure reproducibility. Continuous variables that conformed to an approximate normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using two-sample t-tests. Continuous variables that did not conform to a normal distribution were described as median (quartile) and compared between groups using non-parametric independent sample Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were described as frequency (percentage) and compared between groups using Pearson chi-square tests. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between variables that conformed to an approximate normal distribution, while Spearman correlation analysis was used for those that did not.

Risk factor screening and regression modeling

Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the independent risk factors closely related to the risk of GDM, and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of each variable were calculated. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05, and the selected variables were entered into multivariate logistic regression.

Machine learning model development and validation

Based on the 7 core variables selected by multivariate regression (parity, family history of diabetes, pre-pregnancy weight, weight in the third trimester, rate of gestational weight gain, Stratification of pre-BMI, and triglycerides), multiple machine learning models were developed, including random forest, Naive Bayes, Elastic Net Regression, support vector machine (SVM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting(XGBoost), neural networks, K-Nearest Neighbors(KNN), and Gradient Boosting Machine(GBM). The dataset was divided into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7:3. Five-fold cross-validation was used to optimize hyperparameters, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used as the model selection criterion. The neural network architecture was a single-hidden-layer feedforward network, with the number of hidden layer nodes determined by cross-validation. The activation function was Sigmoid, and the weights were initialized using the He normal distribution. Model performance was evaluated using calibration curves and decision curves. The decision curve analysis set the false positive rate threshold range from 0 to 1. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were used to explain the prediction mechanism of the neural network, and the individual feature contributions of 100 validation set samples were calculated. The background dataset consisted of 20 randomly selected samples, and feature importance was ranked by the average absolute SHAP value.​.

Results

Baseline characteristics and metabolic differences of the population

A total of 1031 pregnant women who met the IOM criteria for gestational weight gain were included in this study, including 279 cases in the GDM group and 752 cases in the NGT group. The baseline characteristics of the population are shown in Table 1. There were significant differences in demographic characteristics, metabolic and hematological parameters between the two groups. The average age of pregnant women in the GDM group was significantly higher than that in the NGT group (31.89 ± 3.91 years vs. 30.28 ± 3.80 years, p < 0.001). The distribution of parity was significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.004). The proportion of multipara (Parity ≥ 1 time) was 37.3% (104/279) in the GDM group and 29.0% (218/752) in the NGT group. The proportion of gravidity ≥ 4 was higher in GDM group (7.9% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.011), suggesting that multiple pregnancies may increase metabolic load. Maternal height was comparable between groups​ (NGT: 162.13 ± 4.90 cm; GDM: 161.51 ± 4.80 cm, p = 0.071). However, ​pre-pregnancy weight, represented by first-trimester measurements at 6–10 weeks, was ​significantly higher​ in the GDM group (59.76 ± 9.48 kg vs. 56.86 ± 8.59 kg in NGT, p < 0.001), as was ​pre-pregnancy BMI​ (GDM: 22.87 ± 3.24 kg/m²; NGT: 21.61 ± 2.94 kg/m², p < 0.001). This elevated pre-pregnancy adiposity in GDM was further reflected in the ​stratification of pre-BMI, showing a greater proportion of GDM participants in overweight (20.4% vs. 6.5%, p < 0.001) and obese (3.2% vs. 1.6%) categories. Although ​weight remained consistently higher​ in the GDM group across all measured gestational ages (12–14 weeks: 59.94 vs. 56.67 kg; 15–19 weeks: 60.59 vs. 57.28 kg; 24–28 weeks: 65.06 vs. 62.14 kg, all p < 0.001), ​weight at delivery showed only a non-significant trend​ towards being higher in GDM (69.41 ± 8.50 kg vs. 68.29 ± 8.11 kg, p = 0.052). Crucially, ​total gestational weight gain was significantly lower in the GDM group​ (9.65 ± 3.72 kg vs. 11.43 ± 3.41 kg in NGT, p < 0.001), likely contributing to the attenuated difference in delivery weight. No group differences existed in weight gain during the first to second trimesters (p = 0.928). In terms of hematological parameters, hemoglobin and platelet counts in the GDM group were significantly higher in the first trimester. In addition, the difference in white blood cell count (White blood cells(15–19 weeks of gestation): 9.62 ± 2.19 × 10⁹/L vs. 9.32 ± 2.01 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.035) further supported the inflammatory activation hypothesis. In terms of metabolic and biochemical indicators, the GDM group showed a comprehensive metabolic disorder. Triglyceride (1.21 ± 0.74 mmol/L vs. 0.96 ± 0.51 mmol/L, p < 0.001), LDL-C (2.24 ± 0.79 mmol/L vs. 2.10 ± 0.60 mmol/L, p = 0.002) and uric acid level (218.46 ± 55.38 µmol/L vs. 206.63 ± 50.13 µmol/L, p = 0.001) were significantly increased. However, High-density lipoprotein cholsterol(HDL-C), serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogendid not differ between the two groups (p = 0.257).

Dynamic changes in body weight are associated with metabolic disorders

Based on heat map analysis (Fig. 2), there was a strong positive correlation between birth weight (weight(at delivery)) and second trimester weight (weight(15–19 weeks of gestation)) (r = 0.98), and both of them formed a highly correlated cluster with BMI (r = 0.82). The data in Table 1 show that the weight(15–19 weeks of gestation) (64.28 ± 7.21 vs. 62.15 ± 6.89 kg, p < 0.001) and weight(at delivery) (69.41 ± 8.50 vs. 68.29 ± 8.11 kg, p = 0.011) of the GDM group were significantly higher than those of the NGT group. Further analysis showed that weight(at delivery) was significantly positively correlated with triglyceride (r = 0.68) and uric acid (r = 0.61), indicating that body fat accumulation may aggravate metabolic disorders through lipotoxicity and oxidative stress.

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed

Univariate analysis showed (Table 2) that among the 37 candidate variables, 23 were statistically significantly associated with the risk of GDM (p < 0.05). Demographic and pregnancy history factors: increasing maternal age was clearly associated with a significantly increased risk of GDM (OR = 1.11, 95%CI:1.07–1.15, p < 0.001). The risk of GDM in women with parity ≥ 2 was 4.35 times higher than that in nulliparous women (OR = 4.35, 95%CI:1.65–12.15, p = 0.003), while the trend of increased risk in multiparous women (parity = 1 time) was not statistically significant (OR = 1.34, p = 0.053). Pregnant women with family history of diabetes mellitus had a 79% increased risk of GDM (OR = 1.79, 95%CI:1.32–2.40, p < 0.001). Pre-pregnancy weight was associated with an increased risk of GDM (OR = 1.04, 95%CI:1.02–1.05, p < 0.001), and the effect increased with gestational age, and the risk gradient remained the same until the third trimester (weight(24–28 weeks of gestation)) (OR = 1.04, p < 0.001). Hematologic and inflammatory markers: First trimester hemoglobin (6–10 weeks of gestation) (OR = 1.02, 95%CI:1.01–1.04, p = 0.003), maternal platelet(6–10 weeks of gestation: OR = 1.004, 95%CI:1.001–1.006; p = 0.004; 15–19 weeks of gestation: OR = 1.003, p = 0.002; 24–28 weeks of gestation OR = 1.003, p = 0.007) and white blood cell count at 15–19 weeks of pregnancy were associated with the incidence of GDM. In lipid metabolism, triglyceride and LDL-C showed a correlation with GDM. The dynamic OGTT characteristics suggest that impaired postprandial glucose control is the main manifestations of abnormal glucose metabolism of GDM.

Neural network architecture and feature weight distribution

Figure 9 shows the three-layer feedforward neural network structure constructed in this study, which includes 7 input nodes, 1 hidden layer node (H1), and 1 output node (O1). Among the input features, weight (6–10 weeks of gestation) and weight (24–28 weeks of gestation) are connected to H1 through strong positive weights, while pre-BMI classification (overweight/obesity) shows negative weights. It is worth noting that the connection weight strength of triglyceride to the hidden layer is second only to weight (6–10 weeks of gestation), suggesting the crucial role of lipid metabolism parameters in the nonlinear feature transformation. This simplified structure maintains computational efficiency while achieving a validation set AUC of 0.732 (Fig. 6), proving its ability to effectively capture the interaction effects of core biomarkers.

Model discrimination efficacy and generalization ability verification

The ROC curve in Fig. 6 shows that the neural network model has AUC values of 0.724 and 0.732 in the training set and validation set, indicating that the model has good generalization ability. The neural network confusion matrix is shown in Fig. 8.The training set curve reaches a true positive rate (TPR) of 0.82 when the false positive rate (FPR) is 0.3, while the validation set drops to 0.78 at the same FPR threshold (curve spacing = 0.04), reflecting the model’s robust filtering mechanism for noise features. The critical threshold analysis shows that when FPR = 0.2, the validation set TPR is 0.65 (corresponding to specificity = 80%), and the positive predictive value reaches 72.3%.

Classification results and calibration performance

The confusion matrix is shown in Fig. 8: in 309 samples of the validation set, the model correctly identified 54 cases of GDM (TP) and 155 cases of NGT (TN), misclassifying 29 cases of NGT as GDM (FP) and 70 cases of GDM as NGT (FN). Based on this calculation, the sensitivity = 43.5%, the specificity 84.2%, which is completely consistent with the TPR of 43.5% at FPR = 0.16 on the validation set ROC curve in Fig. 6. The calibration performance is demonstrated through the S-shaped curve in Fig. 7: when the predicted probability is > 0.5, the actual occurrence rate of GDM increases rapidly from 0.25 to 0.75, with the slope being the largest in the 0.6–0.8 interval, indicating that the model has high resolution in risk assessment for high-risk pregnant women.

Discussion​

The present study provides a comprehensive analysis of risk factors associated with GDM in a cohort of 1,031 pregnant women who strictly adhered to the IOM guidelines for gestational weight gain. Despite uniform compliance with weight gain recommendations, significant metabolic disparities and risk gradients were observed between the GDM and NGT groups. These findings underscore the complexity of GDM pathophysiology, where pre-pregnancy metabolic status [12, 13], dynamic weight trajectories [14, 15], and inflammatory-hematologic [16, 17] interactions collectively contribute to disease susceptibility. The integration of machine learning algorithms further highlights the multifactorial nature of GDM prediction, with implications for personalized risk stratification.

​Pre-pregnancy metabolic burden and GDM risk​

Obesity is a complex metabolic disorder characterized by excessive accumulation of fat tissue, leading to a variety of health problems, including insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. Lipotoxicity refers to the abnormal accumulation of fatty acids and other lipids in non-fat tissues, leading to cellular dysfunction and metabolic disorders. Studies show that excessive accumulation of fatty acids in adipose tissue in obese individuals can interfere with insulin signaling, thereby exacerbating insulin resistance [18] .Moreover, obesity-related chronic inflammation is not limited to fat tissue but can affect metabolic functions throughout the body. For example, inflammatory responses in the liver can lead to fatty liver and increased insulin resistance [19].Our data reveal that even within the IOM-compliant population, pre-pregnancy metabolic derangements play a pivotal role in GDM development. The GDM group exhibited significantly higher weight(6–10 weeks of gestation) and pre-BMI, aligning with established evidence that elevated adiposity exacerbates insulin resistance through lipotoxicity and chronic inflammation. Notably, the univariate logistic regression identified weight(6–10 weeks of gestation) and pre-BMI as strong predictors of GDM, emphasizing that metabolic vulnerability precedes pregnancy [20, 21]. These observations challenge the notion that adherence to IOM guidelines alone suffices for GDM prevention, as pre-existing obesity-related dysregulation may override the benefits of controlled weight gain [22, 23]. The skewed distribution of weight (at delivery) in the GDM group further supports this hypothesis, suggesting that a subset of women with excessive adiposity prior to conception remains at heightened risk despite guideline compliance [24].

​Dynamic weight trajectories and metabolic load​

The longitudinal analysis of weight changes revealed that GDM risk escalates with cumulative metabolic load during pregnancy [25, 26]. Although total gestational weight gain (GWC) was paradoxically lower in the GDM group, weight (24–28 weeks of gestation) emerged as an independent predictor in the multivariate model. This apparent contradiction may reflect differential fat distribution patterns: women developing GDM likely experience accelerated visceral adiposity [16, 27] and ectopic lipid [28, 29] deposition in late pregnancy, even within IOM-recommended ranges. The heatmap analysis corroborates this, showing strong correlations between weight (at delivery) and metabolic markers, which implicate lipotoxicity and oxidative stress as mechanistic links between weight trajectories and glucose intolerance [30]. During pregnancy, changes in body composition have significant impacts on maternal and infant health. For example, excessive weight gain during pregnancy is associated with increased fat mass in newborns, which may affect the body composition and health status of the newborn [31]. Additionally, the pattern of weight gain during pregnancy is related to changes in maternal body composition and the size of the newborn, indicating that monitoring specific patterns of weight gain and changes in body composition during pregnancy is necessary [32]. In conclusion, monitoring changes in body composition during pregnancy is more comprehensive and accurate than relying solely on the increase of total weight. This not only helps to better understand the health changes during pregnancy, but also provides an important basis for developing personalized pregnancy health management plans.

​Inflammatory-Hematologic crosstalk​

The hematologic profile of the GDM group revealed systemic inflammation as a potential mediator of insulin resistance [33, 34]. Elevated white blood cell count during pregnancy may be associated with a variety of pregnancy-related complications. During pregnancy, white blood cell counts typically increase in response to the body’s physiological response to pregnancy. However, an excessively high white blood cell count may indicate an underlying health problem. One study found that elevated white blood cell counts during pregnancy were associated with increased incidence of gestational diabetes [35]. This is consistent with the results of the present study.Elevated first-trimester hemoglobin (6–10 weeks of gestation) and platelet (6–10 weeks of gestation) in early pregnancy, coupled with higher mid-gestation leukocytosis (15–19 weeks of gestation). Based on the findings, the possibility of “two hits” between inflammation and the development of gestational diabetes was hypothesized. The first “hit” involves prepregnancy subclinical inflammation, as demonstrated by elevated baseline platelet and hemoglobin (6–10 weeks of gestation), whereas the second “hit” results from pregnancy-induced alterations in immune status.

​Lipid partitioning and dysregulation​

The lipid profile analysis uncovered Triglycerides as the most robust independent predictor of GDM, surpassing LDL-C and uric acid, which lost significance after adjustment. Fatty acids in lipoproteins rich in triglycerides induce endoplasmic reticulum stress in various cell types, particularly in pancreatic β cells and skeletal muscle. This stress response is closely associated with the development of multiple metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance [36, 37]. The GDM group exhibited hypertriglyceridemia without HDL-C decline, this is consistent with lipid changes during pregnancy, with an increase in triglyceride levels [38] accompanied by no significant decrease in HDL cholesterol levels [39, 40].These findings position TG monitoring as a critical component of early risk assessment, particularly in women with pre-pregnancy BMI > 22 kg/m².

​Parity and Familial susceptibility​

Multiparity conferred a 4.37-fold increased GDM risk in the multivariate model, independent of age and pre-BMI. This association may reflect cumulative metabolic injury from prior pregnancies [41, 42]. Similarly, familial diabetes history increased risk by 64%, highlighting the interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers. It is recommended that women with a history of GDM be counseled for preconception before planning a subsequent pregnancy. It should carefully assess the impact of the status of the first pregnancy on subsequent pregnancies.

​Machine learning insights​

The machine learning comparative analysis showed that the neural network achieved the best prediction balance, which was superior to traditional models such as XGBoost and logistic regression variables. The superiority of the Ann model on the validation set may stem from its ability to identify the critical state of metabolic compensation in a nonlinear way. In addition, NPV (84.2%) of neural network was better than PPV(43.5%), suggesting that neural network has greater utility in excluding NGT cases and can simplify the allocation of clinical screening resources. However, the moderate AUC (0.619 to 0.732) for all models underscores the limitations of using anthropometric and conventional biochemical parameters alone. The apparent overfitting observed in random forest (training AUC = 0.945 vs. Validation AUC = 0.670) highlighted the need for external validation and inclusion of omics level data to improve generalizability.

​Clinical implications​

These findings advocate for a paradigm shift in GDM prevention strategies targeting IOM-compliant women: (1)Pre-conception intervention: Prioritize weight optimization and lipid control in women planning pregnancy, particularly those with familial diabetes or prior GDM. (2)Trimester-specific monitoring: Implement early pregnancy HbA1c, TG, and platelet profiling to identify high-risk trajectories warranting intensified surveillance. (3)Personalized weight targets: Consider body composition analysis (e.g., visceral adipose tissue quantification) to refine IOM guidelines for women with elevated pre-pregnancy BMI.

​Conclusion​

This study achieved its primary objective of developing an accurate prediction system for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) specifically tailored to women adhering to Institute of Medicine (IOM) weight gain criteria. By analyzing early-pregnancy metabolic kinetics and identifying independent risk factors, we established that:

GDM risk persists despite IOM compliance, driven by pre-pregnancy metabolic derangements. The GDM group exhibited significantly higher pre-pregnancy weight (59.76 ± 9.48 kg vs. 56.86 ± 8.59 kg, p < 0.001), elevated triglycerides (1.21 vs. 0.96 mmol/L, p < 0.001), and dysregulated lipid partitioning, with triglycerides identified as the strongest independent predictor (OR = 1.49).

Dynamic weight trajectories are critical discriminators. Third-trimester weight (24–28 weeks) independently predicted GDM (OR = 1.13, p = 0.010), even when total gestational weight gain was lower in GDM (9.65 ± 3.72 kg vs. 11.43 ± 3.41 kg, p < 0.001), reflecting altered metabolic load distribution.

Machine learning integrates multifactorial risk. The neural network model, trained on seven core variables (including weight at 6–10 weeks and 24–28 weeks of pregnancy, weight gain during pregnancy, Stratification of pre-BMI, parity ≥ 2 (OR = 4.37), family diabetes history (OR = 1.64), and early-pregnancy triglycerides (OR = 1.49)), achieved an AUC of 0.732 with 65.1% sensitivity and 68.9% specificity, outperforming to the other methods.

This system addresses the limitations of weight-centric models by demonstrating that metabolic biomarkers (triglycerides, inflammatory markers) and dynamic weight patterns enable early risk stratification in IOM-compliant women. Integration of these parameters into clinical practice could facilitate targeted interventions during the first trimester, potentially reducing GDM incidence and its complications.
