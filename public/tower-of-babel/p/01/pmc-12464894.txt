Chronic Pain and Labour Market Affiliation: Effect of Demographic Factors and Comorbidities 5 Years Before and 2 Years After Multidisciplinary Treatment
Authors: Lea Stevnsborg, Magnus Pedersen, Lilli Kirkeskov, Per Føge Jensen, Merete Osler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThis study investigates the association of sociodemographic factors and comorbidities with labour market affiliation among patients with non‐malignant chronic pain treated at multidisciplinary pain centres in Denmark.MethodsThis registry‐based cohort study used data from the nationwide Danish Civil Registration System and Danish National Patient Registry. Patients treated at multidisciplinary pain centres in 2013–2017 were included. Labour market affiliation was assessed 5 years before and 2 years after treatment and at treatment initiation (baseline). Sequence and logistic regression analyses were used to identify associations.Results2375 patients (median age 47 years, female 64%, comorbidity: Mean Charlson's comorbidity score: 0.88, affective disorder: 45%, anxiety disorder: 37.0%, personality disorder: 7.8%) were included. At treatment initiation, only 34.2% of patients were employed, compared to 58.6% 5 years before treatment. Sequence analysis revealed that most patients remained in or transitioned to a ‘no labour market affiliation’ state. Factors significantly associated with labour market affiliation across timepoints included age (adjusted odd ratio [aOR] and 95% confidence interval [CI]age 56–65 = 0.51 [0.38–0.69]), marital status (aORcohabitant = 1.58 [1.30–1.92]), education level (aORhigh = 4.56 [3.51–5.93]), immigrant status (aORnon‐western = 0.35 [0.26–0.45]) and psychiatric comorbidities (aORaffective disorders = 0.67 [0.56–0.87]).ConclusionPatients with high‐impact, treatment‐resistant chronic pain face early labour market detachment, highlighting the need for timely, equitable interventions to support employment, especially among vulnerable groups.Significance StatementThe study offers new evidence on how socioeconomic and psychiatric comorbid vulnerabilities shape long‐term labour market affiliation before relevant multidisciplinary treatment is initiated and suggests that key intervention opportunities for at‐risk patients may be missed under current practice.Keywords: chronic pain, labour market affiliation, multidisciplinary treatment, psychiatric comorbidity, rehabilitation

Introduction

Chronic non‐malignant pain (hereafter chronic pain) poses a significant and complex health challenge. An estimated 20% of Danish adults are affected by chronic pain with varying severity and impact on daily functions, including labour market affiliation (Breivik et al. 2006). Consequently, chronic pain affects not only the individual patient but also incurs considerable costs to society. The Danish Health Agency reported that low‐back pain alone has an estimated annual cost of approximately 28 billion DKK in direct and indirect healthcare costs due to healthcare expenses and productivity loss (Mairey et al. 2023).

Multidisciplinary interventions are considered the optimal treatment approach for chronic pain (Casey et al. 2020; Dale and Stacey 2016; Scascighini et al. 2008). However, patients with chronic pain often undergo numerous examinations and treatments in different sectors before becoming eligible for comprehensive care. Patients seeking treatment at multidisciplinary pain centres in Denmark experience symptoms for an average of 10 years before treatment (Vaegter et al. 2021). By this time, patients experience biological, psychological and social consequences of prolonged pain on their level of function and labour market affiliation (Vaegter et al. 2021). A previous study examining loss of function during the waiting time from referral to treatment initiation demonstrated loss of function during this period; however, it remains unclear at what timepoint between symptom onset and treatment initiation deterioration begins (Lynch et al. 2008).

A systematic review on the effect of multidisciplinary pain treatment on labour market affiliation could not produce conclusive evidence to support multidisciplinary treatment (Wegrzynek et al. 2020). Moreover, research on multidisciplinary treatment of chronic pain has predominantly examined health outcomes and quality of life in long‐symptomatic patients (Jensen et al. 2006). Jensen et al. reported that multidisciplinary rehabilitation was associated with improved self‐reported health and pain intensity, although sustained effects on labour market affiliation were limited. Similarly, a recent Swedish study found that improvements in pain intensity, depression and healthcare utilisation were sustained for up to 3 years after treatment but demonstrated only minor effects on labour market affiliation (Akerblom et al. 2025). Qualitative research (Grant et al. 2019) has identified return‐to‐work barriers among patients with chronic pain, including low self‐efficacy, lack of workplace accommodation, fear of pain exacerbation and perceived stigma from employers and colleagues. However, factors affecting labour market affiliation before multidisciplinary pain treatment are unexplored, and it remains uncertain whether the prognostic factors change over time (Steenstra et al. 2017).

This registry‐based study examined labour market affiliation in a population of patients treated at multidisciplinary pain centres over a period spanning from 5 years before to 2 years after treatment initiation. We examined the influence of socio‐demographic factors and comorbidities. We hypothesised that patients would exhibit low labour market affiliation both at the time of treatment initiation and years before. Furthermore, we anticipated that the influences of risk and protective factors, such as psychiatric comorbidity, low education level and older age, would become more pronounced over time as these factors might interact and increasingly affect patients' labour market affiliation.

Materials and Methods

Sources of Data

This registry‐based cohort study used data from the following Danish nationwide registries that were linked: the Danish Civil Registration System (CPR) (Pedersen 2011), the Danish National Patient Registry (Lynge et al. 2011), the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Registry (Mors et al. 2011), the Danish National Prescription Registry (Kildemoes et al. 2011), the DREAM registry (Arbejdsmarkedsstyrelsen) and the Population Education Registry (Jensen and Rasmussen 2011).

Study Population

The two multidisciplinary pain centres located in the capital region of Denmark provided data on all patients who had completed treatment at one of their facilities in the period from 2013 to 2017 and were alive and living in Denmark during the study period. The data included a unique person identification number, dates of treatment initiation and termination, primary diagnosis and number and type of clinic visits. The data were anonymised and linked at the individual level to the registries on a research server administered by Statistics Denmark.

Patients treated at a multidisciplinary pain centre are selected. The centres accept adult patients (≥ 18 years) with chronic complex non‐malignant pain lasting more than 6 months, meaning that psychological and social factors have a substantial impact on their condition. To be eligible, patients must be diagnostically clarified and have completed relevant medical evaluations and treatments in other specialities prior to referral. Only outpatients are accepted. Patients are not eligible if they present with acute pain, have not been fully evaluated or treated, have active cancer, suffer from primary headache disorders, have current substance use disorders, or have severe unstable psychiatric conditions. Patients with suspected complex regional pain syndrome or complicated neuropathic pain are referred directly to the specialised neurological department. The most common pain location reported by patients is back pain followed by pain in the thorax/abdomen/genital area (Vaegter et al. 2021).

The multidisciplinary team consisted of specialised doctors, nurses, physiotherapists, psychologists and social workers. The treatment process generally takes several months to complete and seeks to optimise the use of pain medications (including discontinuation or reduction of opioids), physical activity levels and psychological coping mechanisms. Furthermore, patients have access to guidance and hands‐on assistance from social workers during treatment. The treatment course ends when the multidisciplinary team determines that no further actions can improve the condition or if the patient does not comply with the treatment (e.g., non‐compliance to opioid reduction). For the present study, patients under the age of 25 years and above 65 years were excluded, as they are likely to be non‐affiliated with the labour market because of enrolment in educational programmes or retirement. Furthermore, patients who completed only one visit to a multidisciplinary pain centre were also excluded to increase the validity of the chronic pain diagnosis. Only patients who completed the treatment regime in the period from 2013 to 2017 were eligible for inclusion. The cohort selection process and sample size are illustrated in Figure 1.

Labour Market Affiliation

Labour market affiliation was assessed using the degree of employment measure which reflects the number of hours an individual has worked per month, based on information from the Danish Tax Authority, where all income must be registered and reported to the DREAM registry. The DREAM registry contains information on social transfers received by Danish residents since 1991, including unemployment benefits, sickness absence benefits, early retirement pensions and disability pensions (Hjollund et al. 2007). Since 2008, the registry also contains data on degree and type of employment not based on the social transfer codes. The registry is administered by the Ministry of Employment and is based on data from the Ministry of Education, CPR registry and Danish Tax Authority.

A degree of employment of zero indicates that no working hours were registered with the tax authority, signifying no labour market affiliation for that month. A degree of employment of one corresponds to full‐time employment, which is defined as working 37 h per week. Values less than one indicate part‐time employment, reflecting fewer than 37 h of work per week. Conversely, values greater than one reflect employment exceeding 37 h per week. This method of describing labour market affiliation has been used in previous Danish register‐based studies on labour market affiliation (Hellmann et al. 2023).

Labour market affiliation was analysed at three timepoints: 5 years before treatment, the year of treatment and 2 years after treatment, using the average degree of employment in the given year.

Demographic and Clinical Variables

Comorbidities

We used information from the Danish National Patient Registry, Psychiatric Central Research Registry and Danish National Prescription Registry to assess somatic and psychiatric comorbidities. The Charlson comorbidity index (Brusselaers and Lagergren 2017) was utilised to assess the extent of somatic comorbidities in the population. The Charlson comorbidity score was calculated based on both primary and secondary diagnoses recorded in the Danish National Patient Registry, which includes data on all inpatient and outpatient hospital contacts. Diagnoses recorded at any time from the inception of the register (1977 for inpatient and 1995 for outpatient data) up to the time of analysis were used in the calculation. The impact of specific psychiatric disorders, including affective, anxiety and personality disorders, was analysed. These categories were chosen as they are known to be important factors for labour market affiliation (Kessing et al. 2023). Patients were classified as having a psychiatric disorder if they had been registered with a relevant diagnoses in the Psychiatric Central Research Registry at any time point prior to the analysis since the inception of the register (since 1995 for inpatient contact and since 2001 for outpatient contacts) or if they had redeemed a prescription for anti‐depressants or anti‐anxiety medications with disease‐specific indication codes within the 5 years preceding each of the three analysis time points: baseline, 5 years prior to baseline and 2 years after baseline. Indication codes were used to distinguish psychiatric use from pain‐related prescriptions, as these medications are also commonly used in the treatment of chronic pain. Specific diagnostic and indication codes are provided in Table S1.

Sociodemographic Factors

Information on age and gender was obtained from the CPR registry. The study population was categorised into three age groups: 25–39, 40–55 and 56–65 years. Gender was defined as either male or female and reflects the registered gender at baseline.

Information on the marital status of the patients was obtained from the CPR registry. Patients were classified as single if they were not married or were in a registered partnership at the time of analysis.

We determined whether the patients were living with a child in the household using information from the CPR registry, which contains data on the number of children in each household. A child in the household was defined as an individual aged under 18 years living with at least one parent, having never been married and having no children of their own. Adoption was equated with biological parenthood.

Information regarding immigration status was obtained from the CPR registry. The study population was divided into three groups: Danish‐born, and Western and non‐Western immigrants. The distinction between these groups was based on a definition by Statistics Denmark (Countries in Social Statistics n.d.). A person can only be registered in the CPR registry as an immigrant if their stay in Denmark exceeds 3 months (6 months if they immigrated from a Nordic country).

Information on the education level of the patients was obtained from the Population Education Registry. This registry is administered by Statistics Denmark and contains information on the education level, as well as the dates of initiation and completion of education. The following groups were defined based on the education level: low level (primary and lower secondary school), medium level (upper secondary school and short‐ and medium‐level higher education) and high level (long‐term higher education such as 3‐year bachelor programmes, graduate programmes, and PhD programmes). In case of missing data regarding education level at baseline, the individual history of education was evaluated, and the patient was placed in the most appropriate group. This method was used in 13 cases at baseline representing < 1% of cases. In all other analyses missing data was kept as “missing” and not grouped separately. Exact number of missing values is reported in Supporting Information tables but never exceeded 4% of the population.

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed graphically using histograms. Baseline characteristics were compared between subgroups (patients with and without labour market affiliation) using the χ 2 test for categorical variables and either analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data. Post hoc comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. The baseline date was defined as the date of initiation of treatment at a multidisciplinary treatment centre. Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of individual covariates (crude analysis) on the likelihood of having no labour market affiliation at different timepoints in relation to the baseline (the year of treatment at a multidisciplinary pain centre). A multivariate logistic regression was performed to estimate the likelihood of not having labour market affiliation, adjusted for the covariates shown in Table 1. Logistic regression analysis was performed at three different timepoints to determine the associations with different covariates over time.

Sequence analysis was performed using the SAS and STATA statistical software. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Sequence analysis was used to examine labour market affiliation in greater detail and illustrate its sustainability and vulnerability among patients with chronic pain (Brzinsky‐Fay 2007). This method has previously been used to describe labour market affiliation over longer periods in a Danish setting (Lindholdt et al. 2017). Sequence analysis applies longitudinal data to illustrate transitions between defined labour market affiliation states over time. With this analysis, it is possible to provide a complete overview of the labour market affiliation of patients with chronic pain over the entire study period. In this study, three labour market affiliation states were defined, which were as follows: ‘no labour market affiliation’, indicating a degree of occupation of zero; ‘part‐time labour market affiliation’, indicating a degree of occupation between zero and one; and ‘full‐time labour market affiliation’, indicating a degree of occupation corresponding to full‐time employment (37 h per week) or more. Data were analysed by visualising the sequence index plots. Basic sequence analysis is an exploratory method that can generate hypotheses; however, it cannot adjust for covariates or confounders without grouping similar sequences, which was not performed in this study.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Danish Patient Safety Authority (case number: 31‐1521‐156) and registered in Privacy, which is the research records of the Capital Region of Denmark. According to the Danish law, ethical approval is not required for purely registry‐based studies.

Results

Descriptive Analysis

We received data from 2983 patients who completed treatment at a multidisciplinary pain centre in the period from January 2013 to December 2017 (Figure 1). A total of 608 patients were excluded because they were younger than 25 years (n = 89) or older than 65 years (n = 491), had completed only one visit to the centres (n = 7), or were not included in the population registry at the baseline (n = 21). The final study population consisted of 2375 patients.

The baseline characteristics of the population stratified by labour market affiliation status are shown in Table 1. Patients affiliated with the labour market at the baseline tended to be younger, more likely to be married or in a registered partnership, and more likely to reside with children. Furthermore, individuals affiliated with the labour market possessed higher education levels. Additionally, those affiliated with the labour market displayed fewer comorbidities, as shown by a lower Charlson comorbidity index and a lower prevalence of psychiatric disorders. The differences between the two groups were significant, except for sex, for which no significant differences were observed. No missing values were observed at baseline as patients were required to be in the CPR registry at this time. Missing values were only present in the education level variable in the analysis 5 years prior and 2 years after treatment but were missing in only 3.6% and 2.6% of patients respectively.

In the year of treatment initiation, 812 patients (34.2%) were affiliated with the labour market, whereas 58.6% (n = 1391) and 33.9% (n = 805) were affiliated with the labour market 5 years before and 2 years after treatment at the multidisciplinary pain centres, respectively. The results of the sequence analysis of the population with different labour market trajectories and the sequence index plot are shown in Figure 2. A large part of the population was consistently not affiliated with the labour market (the ‘no labour market affiliation’ group) throughout the study period. Further, in the years before treatment, a large part of the population transitioned from a ‘part‐time labour market affiliation’ state to a ‘no labour market affiliation’ state, and a smaller group of patients transitioned from a ‘part‐time labour market affiliation’ state to a ‘no labour market affiliation’ state. The trajectories were more stable in the period after treatment.

Association With Sociodemographic Factors and Comorbidities

Figure 3 and Table S4 show that at the baseline, older age was associated with a decreased odds of labour market affiliation (odds ratio [OR] [95% confidence interval CI] = 0.51 [0.38–0.69]). Sex showed no association with labour market affiliation, whereas being married or in a registered partnership was associated with increased odds of labour market affiliation (OR [95% CI] = 1.58 [1.30–1.92]). Living with children showed no association with labour market affiliation in the adjusted model. High education level was strongly associated with labour market affiliation (OR [95% CI] = 4.56 [3.51–5.93]). Western and non‐Western immigrant status were associated with decreased odds of labour market affiliation at the baseline (OR [95% CI] = 0.43 [0.24–0.75] and 0.35 [0.26–0.45], respectively).

Regarding comorbidities, a high Charlson comorbidity index (indicating a high number of comorbidities) was associated with decreased odds of labour market affiliation at the baseline in the crude analysis (OR [95% CI] = 0.92 [0.87–0.98]); however, this effect was not significant in the adjusted model (OR [95% CI] = 0.96 [0.90–1.03]). Psychiatric comorbidities were strongly associated with employment status. In the adjusted model, having an affective, anxiety or personality disorder was associated with decreased odds of labour market affiliation at the baseline (OR [95% CI] = 0.67 [0.56–0.82], 0.61 [0.50–0.77] and 0.61 [0.41–0.92], respectively).

These associations were stable in the analyses at all three timepoints except for non‐Western immigrant status, which was not significant in the analysis of the likelihood of labour market affiliation at 2 years after treatment (Figure 3).

Discussion

This registry‐based cohort study confirmed the hypothesis that low labour market affiliation is present several years before starting multidisciplinary treatment in patients with chronic pain. Our study demonstrated that approximately 34% of the patients treated at a multidisciplinary pain centre had labour market affiliation in the year of treatment initiation. Sequence analysis demonstrated that, once the patients lost their labour market affiliation, they were unlikely to regain it. Overall, the labour market trajectories appeared to indicate a gradual loss of labour market affiliation, and only a small number of patients gained labour market affiliation over the study period. However, the labour market trajectories appeared to be more stable in the 2 years after multidisciplinary treatment for chronic pain, indicating an effect of the treatment on labour market retention. This raises the question of whether early intervention to reduce the duration of chronic pain can help prevent future loss of labour market affiliation. Previous studies have produced conflicting evidence regarding the effect of multidisciplinary treatment on labour market affiliation, demonstrating both positive (Henchoz et al. 2010) and negative effects (Jensen et al. 2011) on labour market outcomes, with systematic reviews producing no or moderate‐quality evidence supporting multidisciplinary interventions (Kamper et al. 2015; Wegrzynek et al. 2020). The impact of the duration of chronic pain on the effectiveness of multidisciplinary interventions in improving labour market affiliation remains unclear. However, previous results suggest that a longer duration of chronic pain is associated with poorer outcomes, with higher degrees of chronicity leading to reduced treatment efficacy (Mills et al. 2019; Moulin et al. 2015). Findings from these studies as well as the current study highlight the potential of earlier treatment initiation to maximise labour market retention.

Contrary to our hypothesis, the effect of demographic and clinical factors was not clearly accentuated by time but influenced labour market affiliation in a similar pattern at all three timepoints, with a few exceptions. Education level was a highly impactful factor in all three analyses; the current study demonstrated a trend towards high education level being particularly protective in the year of treatment initiation. This suggests that patients with high education levels are more likely to utilise treatment benefits to maintain their labour market affiliation. This is supported by results of previous studies demonstrating better participation and treatment outcomes among patients with higher education levels (Gerdle et al. 2020). To promote equitable labour market–oriented rehabilitation in the future, addressing the specific needs of individuals with lower socioeconomic status should be prioritised (Bonathan et al. 2013; Milcent 2024).

Psychiatric comorbidities emerged as a major risk factor in our study. Depression, anxiety and personality disorders were consistently associated with a lower likelihood of labour market affiliation. This association between psychiatric disorders and low labour market affiliation has also been found in previous studies on patients with and without chronic pain (Braden et al. 2008; Pedersen et al. 2023). The relationship between chronic pain and psychiatric disorders is complex, with the conditions affecting each other bidirectionally. Studies have demonstrated that patients with psychiatric disorders are more likely to develop chronic pain, and conversely, patients with chronic pain are more likely to develop psychiatric disorders, such as anxiety and depression (Velly and Mohit 2018). Furthermore, both the development and degree of psychiatric disorders and chronic pain are influenced by sociodemographic factors, such as access to treatment, income and education level, further complicating this issue.

Strength and Limitations

A major strength of this study is its large sample size and registry‐based design which ensured minimal missing data and a high degree of data validity (Sorensen et al. 2023). Furthermore, the rigorous visitation process at the multidisciplinary pain centres ensured a high degree of certainty in the diagnosis of chronic pain. A large portion of the previous research examining the labour market affiliation of patients with chronic pain has used self‐reported data, which can lead to bias (Thorsen et al. 2018).

A limitation of this study is that no reference group from the general population was included. The sociodemographic factors and comorbidities described in this study are well‐known factors influencing labour market affiliation in the general population. However, the size of the effect estimates indicated enhanced vulnerability in this patient group. Furthermore, our study did not reflect the overall Danish labour market trends during that period. According to Statistics Denmark, the degree of labour market affiliation in the Danish population between the ages of 15 and 64 years increased in the study period (from approximately 70.8% in 2013 to 75.0% in 2019) (Labour Force Survey n.d.). Further research is needed to understand how risk and protective factors influence labour market affiliation in patients with chronic pain compared with the general population and other patient groups suffering from chronic diseases.

Moreover, because of conditioning on patients being alive and residing in Denmark during the study period, our findings can only be extrapolated to patients under similar conditions. Our results do not apply to individuals who died or emigrated from Denmark within the study period. Similarly, although patients in this population may experience complications or undergo further interventions, referral to a multidisciplinary pain centre requires medical stability, and such events were not within the scope of this study.

A minor limitation of the study was the possibility of misclassification of educational level at baseline in cases with missing data. This applied to 13 cases corresponding to < 1% of the study population. At the two additional analysis time points (5 years prior and 2 years after baseline), a slightly larger percentage (< 4%) had missing data on educational level, and an individual‐level assessment was not feasible.

Another minor limitation of the study is the possibility of overestimating the effect of having a psychiatric disorder as the variable combined diagnostic codes with redeemed prescriptions with relevant indication codes. Although this approach aimed to capture cases not recorded in hospital data, unmeasured confounding may still inflate effect estimates.

We did not have access to data on pain‐specific measures, such as the severity, duration and location of pain; psychological measures of pain acceptance; and pain catastrophising, which previous studies have shown to affect labour market affiliation in patients with chronic pain (Becker et al. 2000). We initially intended to examine the effects of attention deficit disorder; however, the prevalence of this condition in the population was insufficient for reporting.

Patients treated at multidisciplinary pain centres in Denmark underwent a comprehensive visitation process, ensuring a high degree of diagnostic certainty. However, this may also have narrowed the generalisability of our findings, as they primarily concern a subset of patients with chronic pain who do not respond to other treatments and exhibit lower levels of function. Thus, our results may overestimate the negative impact of certain covariates on labour market affiliation if they are generalised to less severely affected populations.

Perspectives and Future Research

Although this study has some limitations, its results highlight the need for a different approach to labour market rehabilitation in patients with chronic pain. The steady decline in labour market affiliation observed in the years leading up to treatment suggests that delayed rehabilitation can lead to worse socioeconomic outcomes in patients with chronic pain. Early introduction of vocational support has shown promise in previous studies (Reneman et al. 2021; Wynne‐Jones et al. 2018). Systematic early identification and timely referral to targeted rehabilitation programmes for at‐risk individuals, particularly those with psychiatric comorbidities or a lower socioeconomic status, could potentially mitigate the long‐term effects of chronic pain on labour market affiliation. Further strategies could include early integration of rehabilitation programmes with vocational support, mental health services and municipal employment services. Such cross‐sector initiatives linking pain treatment, mental health services and social workers may help to sustain labour market affiliation and lower use of excess health care services (Larsson et al. 2022).

This study highlights the importance of understanding the ideal timing of rehabilitation and incorporating efforts to improve social support and mental health in rehabilitative programmes for patients with chronic pain. Although the multidisciplinary pain centres in Denmark currently employ social workers and psychologists, it is crucial to integrate these elements into earlier rehabilitative programmes to improve labour market affiliation outcomes and to test the feasibility and cost‐effectiveness of such efforts in future studies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that patients with chronic pain experience a persistent decline in labour market affiliation long before initiating multidisciplinary treatment. The gradual loss of employment participation, coupled with difficulty in regaining it, highlights the critical importance of early intervention, particularly for those with lower socioeconomic status and psychiatric comorbidities.
