Red tank aquaculture improves reddish body color, flesh quality and taste of pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei)
Authors: Manman Shi, Xianyong Bu, Qiuxin Yan, Yongtao Liu, Chuanwei Yao, Jinze Zhang, Zhihao Zhang, Lu Zhang, Xiaoru Chen, Kangsen Mai, Yueru Li, Qinghui Ai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe reddish body coloration of Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) is a critical quality attribute that significantly influences consumer preference and market value. Intensive aquaculture practices frequently result in impaired pigmentation, and dietary astaxanthin supplementation, while effective, increases production costs. Considering the intrinsic ability of decapod crustaceans to alter their body color in response to environmental backgrounds, we hypothesized that rearing L. vannamei in red tanks would enhance redness, stimulate astaxanthin distribution, improve muscle texture, and enhance flavor without compromising growth performance. Following a 75-day culture period, shrimp reared in red tanks exhibited a significantly higher redness (a* value) in both raw and cooked forms (P < 0.05), alongside enhanced textural parameters including hardness, gumminess, and chewiness (P < 0.05). Furthermore, increased levels of umami-related amino acids were detected in the muscle of shrimp reared in red tanks (increased by 15.3 %, P < 0.05). Astaxanthin content was elevated in the shell (increased by 25.8 %, P < 0.05) but reduced in the hepatopancreas (decreased by 18.5 %, P < 0.05), indicating a facilitated redistribution of the pigment. This redistribution was corroborated by the upregulation of the red pigment-concentrating hormone (rpch) gene in the hepatopancreas (P < 0.05). Although survival rate was marginally lower (decreased by 7.0 %, P < 0.05), growth remained unaffected. These findings collectively indicate that red tank culture offers an economically viable and feed-free strategy to enhance the visual appeal and sensory quality of L. vannamei, presenting a promising alternative to dietary pigment supplementation.Keywords: Environmental color, Muscle texture, Astaxanthin redistribution, Red pigment-concentrating hormone, Litopenaeus vannamei

Introduction

Shrimp farming is a rapidly growing sector of global aquaculture, with production reaching 9.4 million tonnes in 2022 — a significant recovery from the downturn experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic (Eissa et al., 2024). Notably, owing to its high yield, rapid growth rate, and strong adaptability, the Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) dominates global penaeid shrimp production, accounting for 83.1 % of total yield (FAO 2023). Aquaculture of this species has transitioned from traditional open systems such as semi-intensive earthen ponds to modern recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS), which enable super-intensive production reaching 5.0 kg/m3 at stocking densities of 500 individuals/m3 (Suantika et al., 2020).

Among quality traits, body color is a primary factor influencing consumer preference and market value, often surpassing nutritional composition and palatability attributes in immediate visual impact (Erickson, Bulgarelil, Resurreccion, Vendetti, & Gates, 2007; J. Parisenti et al., 2011). Shrimp with a deep red color—like the “red cherry shrimp” (Neocaridina davidi)—typically sell for more, thanks to their high consumer appeal (Tomas, Sganga, & Lopez Greco, 2020). However, the rise of high-density intensive farming has led to an increased prevalence of abnormal body coloration in shrimp, including paler or bluish pigmentation (Lim, Yusoff, Shariff, & Kamarudin, 2018). The body color of shrimp is primarily determined by the deposition of carotenoids, particularly astaxanthin, in the shrimp shell (Tlusty, Metzler, Huckabone, Suanda, & Guerrier, 2009; Yao, Ma, Chen, Zhao, & Zang, 2023). Dietary supplementation with carotenoids/astaxanthin has been widely demonstrated to enhance pigmentation and meat quality in farmed shrimp (Verlhac Trichet & Amaya, 2022). However, this approach increases production costs and may contribute to environmental concerns due to effluent discharge (Peng, Zhang, Li, & Yang, 2025).

An alternative approach leverages the ability of shrimp to adapt their color to environmental backgrounds—a camouflage mechanism mediated by pigment production and redistribution (Parisenti, Beirão, Mouriño, et al., 2011; Yaqoob et al., 2022). Such color adaptation is not only a survival strategy but also reflects underlying physiological adjustments. The freshwater shrimp Macrobrachium jelskii significantly altered its body color after 30 days to match white, black, and red backgrounds, adapting camouflage that likely enhances survival against visual predators (Araújo et al., 2023). This chromatic adaptation is closely associated with changes in astaxanthin concentration within their tissues. Rearing female Neocaridina davidi on black backgrounds significantly enhanced both astaxanthin content and shell coloration compared to white or red backgrounds, offering a low-cost method to improve their economic value (Tomas et al., 2020). More precisely, it is the redistribution of astaxanthin within tissues that enables shrimp to alter their body color in response to environmental conditions. Shrimp reared in black tanks under red light exhibited higher carotenoid levels in the cephalothorax, despite maintaining a constant total body pigment concentration, highlighting the role of tank color in enhancing shrimp appearance for aquaculture (Díaz-Jiménez, Hernández-Vergara, & Pérez-Rostro, 2018). Notably, manipulating body color through environmental background appears to be a safe approach without adverse effects on production performance. Crayfish reared on black and red backgrounds exhibited significantly deeper body coloration and enhanced growth performance compared to those cultured on white or yellow backgrounds (Zheng et al., 2023).

Although studies have sporadically reported color mimicry in shrimp in response to tank colors, most focus solely on color change without assessing comprehensive quality traits such as meat texture, flavor, or physiological trade-offs. Furthermore, the effects of background color on key performance metrics—including survival, growth, and biochemical composition—remain inadequately evaluated, hindering practical implementation. Therefore, this study was designed to comprehensively evaluate the effects of red tank culture on body redness, nutritional composition, growth performance, muscle texture, and flavor quality of L.vannamei. Utilizing this environmentally induced color adaptation mechanism could provide a sustainable and cost-effective alternative to synthetic pigment supplementation, reducing both production costs and potential environmental contamination.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in strict compliance with the Regulations on the Management of Laboratory Animals (State Council Order No. 676, revised March 1, 2017) and was approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of Ocean University of China.

Shrimp and feeding management

The experiment was conducted at Dongying Tongwei Co. Ltd. (Shandong, China) from August 2023 to November 2023, using shrimp larvae (L. vannamei) obtained from Tongwei Hairen Shrimp Hatchery (Qingdao, China). Prior to grouping, all larvae were cultured in the same square tanks and fed the same commercial diet for acclimatization. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, the shrimp larvae were randomly allocated into 16 octagonal tanks (8 tanks per group). Each group consisted of 8 tanks, so the experimental replication was 8 (n = 8). The initial stocking density was maintained at 1200–1300 individuals per cubic meter across all tanks. During the late-stage sampling, the stocking density was approximately 500 shrimp per cubic meter. To ensure sampling blindness, shrimp were picked up from the bottom of each tank using the same fishing net by the factory workers on duty that day. Crucially, the workers were unaware of the experimental design or group assignments. Of these 16 octagonal tanks, 8 were unpainted (cement-colored, gray) and 8 were painted red. The mean initial weight of randomly assigned shrimp was 0.44 ± 0.01 g in gray tanks and 0.46 ± 0.01 g in red tanks, with no significant difference between groups. Commercial feed “Shrimp Liver Strength A" (9–10 % moisture, 6–7 % crude protein, and 42–44 % crude lipid) from Tongwei Lianyungang Co., Ltd. was provided eight times daily (at 7:30, 10:30, 13:30, 16:30, 19:30, 22:30, 1:30, and 4:30) to satiation. Throughout the entire experiment, the feed and feeding methods were identical for both groups (gray and red). The RGB value of the red tank was (), while that of the gray tank was (91, 95, 96).

Water quality

A recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) was used for the culture experiment. Specifically, a marine water recirculating aquaculture system was adopted, which could automatically achieve a daily water exchange rate of 5.0 %. A handheld YSI meter was used to monitor the parameters of dissolved oxygen (DO), temperature, and salinity once a day. The contents of ammonia nitrogen, pH value, and nitrite were detected twice a week using kits (Henan Nanhua Qianmu Co., Henan, China, Ltd). During the experiment, all procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the kit operation guidelines. The aquaculture system maintained optimal conditions: water temperature at 26–28 °C, salinity at 28–29, pH at 7.4–7.6, ammonia nitrogen <0.1 mg/L, and dissolved oxygen >8.0 mg/L.

Sample collection

On days 0, 25, 50, and 75 of the experiment, 20 shrimp were randomly sampled from each tank (n = 8 tanks per group, biological replicates) for body color measurement, with each individual shrimp measurement considered a technical replicate. At the end of the culture period (day 75), shrimp were sampled from each tank for biochemical analyses. For each tank, 10 shrimp were anesthetized on ice and dissected. The hepatopancreas, shell, and muscle tissues were individually isolated from each shrimp. Tissues of the same type from the 10 shrimp within a tank were pooled to form one composite biological replicate per tissue type per tank. These pooled samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Additionally, hemolymph was extracted from a separate set of 10 shrimp per tank. Hemolymph from the 10 shrimp within the same tank was pooled to form one biological replicate, stored at 4 °C overnight, and then centrifuged at 5180 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was used to determine biochemical indices. Each composite sample was analyzed in triplicate (technical replicates) for the respective assays. The hepatopancreas samples were used for antioxidant enzyme activity, astaxanthin content assays and gene expression, the shells for astaxanthin content determination, and the muscles for analysis of astaxanthin, collagen, amino acid profile, fatty acid composition.

Proximate composition analysis

The moisture, crude protein, and crude fat contents of shrimp muscle were assessed following the standard method (AOAC, 1995). Moisture content was determined by drying the samples in an oven at 105 °C until a constant weight was achieved. The Kjeldahl method (FOSS Kjeltec 8400 Analyzer Unit, Hillerød, Sweden) was employed to determine the crude protein content, while the crude lipid content was determined using Soxhlet extraction (Soxhlet Extraction System B-811, Buchi, Flawil, Switzerland).

Body color measurement

On day 0, 25, 50, and 75 of the experiment, 20 shrimp from each tank were collected for body color measurement. Crucially, the workers were unaware of the experimental design or group assignments. A portable Konida Minolta colorimeter CM-700d (Minolta, Osaka, Japan) was used to evaluate body color at the site between the second and third abdominal segments. Shrimp body color measurement typically focuses on the abdominal segments (Tomas et al., 2020; Tume, Sikes, Tabrett, & Smith, 2009). Our preliminary experiments indicated that color changes in the second and third abdominal segments were most significant. After measuring live shrimp body color, all samples were steamed at 100 °C for 8 min (The preliminary experiment verified that 8 min that counted from the moment the water came to a full boil was sufficient to ensure the shrimp were thoroughly steamed), cooled to room temperature, and re-assessed for body color using the same method. Body color parameters were quantified based on International Commission on Illumination (CIE) standards for L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) (CIE, 1976).

Astaxanthin content analysis

Pigmentation determination was performed based on a previous method with slight modifications (Yuan & Chen, 2000). Briefly, astaxanthin content in the samples was analyzed using HPLC (LC-20 A, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) on an InertSustain C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm; 5 μm) at 480 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 85 % methanol, 5 % acetonitrile, 5 % dichloromethane, and 5 % water (v/v). Approximately 0.2 g of the sample was mixed with an appropriate amount of anhydrous sodium sulfate and 1 ml of acetone, followed by thorough vortex mixing. The mixed samples were then ultrasonically extracted and centrifuged (4 °C, 7104 xg, 5 min) to collect the supernatant. The residues of each sample were centrifuged three times in total, with each time the addition of 1 ml of acetone. The combined supernatant from the three washes was saponified with 2.9 ml of sodium hydroxide-methanol solution and neutralized with 0.1 ml of a 0.6 mol/l methanol-phosphoric acid solution. After filtration through a 0.22 μm organic membrane, the samples were ready for astaxanthin content analysis.

Muscle texture determination

After steaming over boiling water for 10 min, twenty live shrimp were cooled and deshelled. The texture of abdominal muscle segments was assessed using a texture analyzer (TMS-Touch, FTC, Sterling, VA, USA). A compression test was conducted using an 8-mm cylinder probe at room temperature. Samples underwent two compression cycles under the following conditions: test speed 2 mm/s, 50 % deformation, 5-s interval between compressions, and 0.1 N initial trigger force. Hardness, adhesiveness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness were determined (Ginés, Valdimarsdottir, Sveinsdottir, & Thorarensen, 2004).

Fatty acid profile

Shrimp muscle samples were freeze-dried for 48 h using a freeze dryer (FDU-1100, EYELA, Tokyo, Japan). Fatty acids were extracted the method: 100 mg freeze-dried sample was heated at 75 °C with 3 ml 1 mol/l KOH-CH₃OH for 20 min; after cooling to room temperature, 3 ml 2 mol/l HCl-CH₃OH was added and heated again at 75 °C for 20 min, followed by 1 ml n-hexane for extraction. Fatty acid methyl esters were analyzed via GC–MS (QP2010, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a 007-CW capillary column (Hewlett Packard, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and flame ionization detector. Column temperature gradient: 150–200 °C (15 °C/min), then 200–250 °C (2 °C/min) (Zuo et al., 2012).

Free amino acid analysis

Samples for free amino acid analysis were prepared by mixing muscle lyophilized powder with 6 % trichloroacetic acid. Following ultrasonic extraction and centrifugation at 5180 xg for 10 min, the supernatant was obtained. The free amino acid composition was analyzed using Hitachi L-8900 fully automated amino acid analyzer (Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Enzymatic activity assay

An appropriate amount of shrimp hepatopancreas (0.2–0.3 g) was homogenized in pre-cooled saline (9 times the tissue volume) and centrifuged at 3500 g and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. After the shrimp hemolymph samples were left to stand at 4 °C overnight, they were centrifuged using a high-speed refrigerated centrifuge (4 °C, 5180 xg, 10 min). The resulting supernatant was used for the determination of immune-active substances. Glucose (GLU), alkaline phosphatase (AKP), acid phosphatase (ACP), triglyceride (TG), and total protein (TP), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), and hydroxyproline (Hyp) were determined using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Jiangsu, China). All assays were conducted following the manufacturer's instructions. Collagen content was calculated by multiplying the hydroxyproline content by 8 (AOAC, 2000).

Gene expression analysis

The total RNA was isolated from the hepatopancreas and muscle using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) following the standard protocol provided by the manufacturer. The concentration and quality of the RNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) following the manufacturer's instructions. Elongation factor 1-alpha1 (EF1α1) was used as a reference (housekeeping) gene. The levels of mRNA were measured by qPCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan), and the qRT-PCR results were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Results

Effects of tank color on survival and growth performance of L.vannamei

The survival rate (SR) of shrimp cultured in red tanks was significantly lower than that of shrimp in gray tanks, with a reduction of 7 % (P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in final body weight (FBW), weight gain rate (WGR), or specific growth rate (SGR) between the two groups (Fig. 1).

Effects of tank color on body color of L.vannamei

Shrimp reared in red tanks exhibited significantly redder coloration after the 75-day experiment (Fig. 3A and B). This redder hue was retained even after cooking and processing (Fig. 3C and D). Interestingly, post-cooking, shrimp from red tanks displayed less bending compared to those from gray tanks (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting that red tank aquaculture may influence muscle texture.

To further analyze the color changes, three body color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) were measured. On day 0, no significant differences in any of the parameters were observed between the two groups. However, by the 25th day of aquaculture (approximately 3 g per shrimp), shrimp in red tanks exhibited a significant decrease in lightness (L*) (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E) and a significant increase in redness value (a*) (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F). These trends persisted through the 50th day of aquaculture (about 8.5 g per shrimp) (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E and F). Additionally, the yellowness value (b*) of shrimp in red tanks became significantly higher than that of shrimp in gray tanks starting from the 50th day (P < 0.05, Fig. 3G). By the 75th day of aquaculture (approximately 14.5 g per shrimp, market-ready), shrimp in red tanks maintained significantly higher redness (a*) and yellowness (b*) values compared to those in gray tanks (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F and G), while the lightness value (L*) of both groups became comparable (P > 0.05, Fig. 3E).

The color parameters of cooked shrimp mirrored those of live shrimp. The lightness value (L*) of cooked shrimp in red tanks was temporarily lower (25 days, P < 0.05) than that of shrimp in gray tanks during the growth period but ultimately showed no difference in the final product (Fig. 3H). Consistent with the raw shrimp, the redness value (a*) of cooked shrimp in red tanks remained continuously higher than that in gray tanks (P < 0.05, Fig. 3I). Notably, the difference in yellowness value (b*) observed in raw shrimp between red and gray tanks was no longer apparent in cooked shrimp (Fig. 3G and J).

Effects of tank color on nutritional composition of L.vannamei muscle

Moisture content, crude protein, crude lipid, and total collagen levels in the muscle tissue of shrimp reared in red tanks did not differ significantly from those in gray tanks (Fig. 2). The fatty acid composition of shrimp muscle was further analyzed (Table 2). Compared to shrimp from gray tanks, those from red tanks exhibited significantly higher levels of C18:1n-9 and C20:1 fatty acid (P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in the total levels of saturated fatty acids (SFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), or highly unsaturated fatty acids (HUFA) between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Effects of tank color on muscle free amino acid composition of L.vannamei

Glutamate contributes to the characteristic umami taste in food (Kurihara, 2009). Red tank culture significantly increased the muscle glutamate content (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A) and the total amount of umami amino acids (including aspartic and glutamic acid) in shrimp (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). Although no significant difference was observed, the levels of sweet-tasting amino acids (glycine, serine, threonine, alanine, and proline) in shrimp from red tanks were slightly higher than those in gray tanks, while the levels of bitter-tasting amino acids (arginine, histidine, tyrosine, valine, phenylalanine, methionine, isoleucine, leucine, and lysine) were lower in red tank shrimp compared to gray tank shrimp (P > 0.05, Fig. 4B). No significant difference in total amino acid levels was found between shrimp from red and gray tanks, but a slight upward trend was observed in the red tank group (P > 0.05, Fig. 4B). These findings are consistent with the higher protein content reported in the muscles of red tank shrimp compared to gray tank shrimp (Fig. 2B). Overall, the red environment appears to have a positive influence on the flavor profile of shrimp.

Effects of tank color on muscle texture of L.vannamei

The texture parameters of hardness, adhesiveness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness were evaluated to assess the muscle texture of L. vannamei (Ginés et al., 2004). Red tank culture significantly increased the muscle hardness of shrimp (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A), while the adhesiveness of shrimp muscle was significantly reduced under red tank conditions (P < 0.05, Fig. 5B). These changes may explain why cooked shrimp from red tanks exhibited less bending compared to those from gray tanks after 75 days of the trial (Fig. 3C and D). No statistically significant differences were observed in the springiness and cohesiveness of shrimp muscle between red and gray tanks (P>0.05, Fig. 5C and D). However, red tank aquaculture significantly enhanced the gum8 ess and chewiness of shrimp (P <0.05, Fig. 5E and F). Overall, these findings demonstrate that the red tank environment has a positive impact on the texture of shrimp muscle.

Effects of tank color on serum biochemical profile and hepatic antioxidant power of L.vannamei

The serum levels of ACP, AKP, GLU, and TP in shrimp cultured in red tanks were significantly higher than those in gray tanks (P < 0.05, Fig. 6A–D), with no significant difference observed in the serum TG level (P > 0.05, Fig. 6E). These results suggest that shrimp in red tanks exhibited enhanced non-specific immune responses compared to those in gray tanks.

In terms of hepatic antioxidant capacity, red tank culture significantly decreased the total antioxidant capacity of shrimp hepatopancreas (P<0.05, Fig. 7A). However, the activities of the antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), and catalase (CAT) were not significantly affected by red tank conditions (P>0.05, Fig. 7B-D). Effects of tank color on the astaxanthin content of L.vannamei

Environmental color influences the distribution of astaxanthin in shrimp (Tume et al., 2009). Aquaculture in red tanks resulted in increased astaxanthin enrichment in the shrimp shells (P < 0.05, Fig. 8A), which correlated with enhanced redness of their body color (Fig. 3). Although the flesh exhibited varying degrees of redness, the astaxanthin content in the muscle showed no significant difference (P > 0.05, Fig. 8B).

Effects of tank color on astaxanthin content of L. vannamei.

The levels of astaxanthin content in the shell (A), muscle (B) and hepatopancreas (C) of shrimp. Values are means (n = 8 replicate tanks) with standard errors represented by vertical bars. Data were analyzed using an independent t-test and results are presented as mean ± S.E.M (n = 8) (P < 0.05).

Interestingly, in contrast to the findings in the shells, the astaxanthin level in the hepatopancreas of shrimp reared in red tanks was significantly lower than in those from gray tanks (P < 0.05, Fig. 8C). This suggests that shrimp may achieve a redder appearance by transferring astaxanthin from the hepatopancreas to the shell, thereby improving camouflage against a red background.

Effects of tank color on body color-related gene expression of L.vannamei

To further elucidate how background colors induce changes in shrimp body color, the expression of body color-related genes was measured, including carotenoid-binding genes and those involved in carotenoid synthesis and metabolism. Notably, shrimp reared in red tanks exhibited a significant upregulation of rpch (red pigment-concentrating hormone) expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 9). Additionally, the mRNA levels of cyp450 (cytochrome P450) were significantly higher in shrimp reared in gray tanks compared to those reared in red tanks (P < 0.05, Fig. 9). The mRNA expression of cyb5 (cytochrome B5), LvPBP75 (hemocyanin C chain), crcn-a2 (crustacyanin-A2), and crcn-c1 (crustacyanin-C1) exhibited no significant differences between shrimp reared in gray and red tanks (P > 0.05, Fig. 9).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that red tank aquaculture did not affect shrimp growth but reduced their survival rate by 7 %. This phenomenon may be associated with the stress response elicited by the red background. Previous studies have shown that larvae of Macrobrachium amazonicum exhibit lower survival rates in yellow, white, and red tanks compared to black and blue tanks, which more closely resemble their natural environment (Maciel & Valenti, 2014). Similarly, abnormally low survival rates and severe deformities were observed in striped trumpeter (Latris lineata) larvae reared in red tanks (Cobcroft & Battaglene, 2009). The detrimental effect of a red background on shrimp survival might be due to shrimp allocating more energy towards coping with stress rather than towards growth (Merighe, Pereira-da-Silva, Negrão, & Ribeiro, 2004), though further investigation is required to confirm this. While most studies indicate that the background color in aquaculture systems has no impact on shrimp growth (Supriyono et al., 2021), some studies have reported contradictory findings (Maciel and Valenti, 2014; Supriyono et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2023). These conflicting results regarding the effect of background color on shrimp growth have been attributed to the influence of background color on the shrimp's ability to visually locate feed (Freire, Luchiari, & Marques, 2012). For Penaeus vannamei specifically, studies have shown that green and blue backgrounds are more effective in enhancing survival and growth rates compared to black and white backgrounds (Supriyono et al., 2021), whereas yellow and red backgrounds have been found to increase growth rates more effectively than green and blue backgrounds (Freire et al., 2012). According to the present study, red tank aquaculture does not alter growth but reduces shrimp survival rate. Therefore, in scenarios where production efficiency is prioritized over product quality, the use of red tank aquaculture entails certain risks.

Bright red prawns directly and intuitively attract consumers, thus commanding higher prices (Latscha, 1989; Parisenti, Beirão, Tramonte, et al., 2011; Tume et al., 2009). It is well known that many crustaceans, such as shrimp, can effectively adjust their body color to mimic the background for camouflage and self-protection (Tlusty et al., 2009). We therefore hypothesized that if aquaculture tanks were modified to red, shrimp might become redder during growth. However, it remained unclear whether shrimp of comparable quality could still be obtained. In this experiment, 16 tanks were used and divided into two groups. Although the gray tanks and red tanks shared the same dimensions, the intensity of the red color was not quantified. As color intensity might affect shrimp pigmentation, future studies should explore the impacts of red intensity or brightness. In our experiment, as expected, shrimp reared in red tanks significantly enhanced the vibrancy of their body color to adapt to the red background. This color enhan gray was well-received by consumers, with the redder shrimp fetching a price approximately US$0.07 per kilogram higher than those from gray tanks. While raw shrimp are often evaluated based on attributes like color and appearance at purchase, consumer recognition and repurchase behavior are more strongly influenced by post-cooking characteristics (Erickson et al., 2007). Previous studies have shown that dark gray shrimp—previously less preferred by consumers—exhibit a more attractive orange-red color after cooking compared to white shrimp (Parisenti, Beirão, Tramonte, et al., 2011). In the present study, the body redness of shrimp cultured in red tanks was positively correlated with rearing time, and this correlation remained significant even after steaming. However, this retention was limited to redness values: changes in yellowness values disappeared entirely after cooking, suggesting that yellowness may be more susceptible to temperature effects. These results indicate that simply changing the aquaculture tank background to red can visibly alter shrimp body color, making them appear redder. Compared to the production strategy of adding natural pigments to feed to enhance shrimp color (Aréchiga-Palomera, Vega-Villasante, Montoya-Martínez, Mendoza-González, & Badillo-Zapata, 2018), painting aquaculture tanks red is more convenient, cost-effective, and safer.

In addition to body color, the evaluation of shrimp quality also encompasses its nutritional composition, meat texture, and flavor. The present study demonstrated that while red-background aquaculture did not enhance the nutritional composition (such as crude protein, crude lipid, and total collagen content), it did not have any adverse effects either. The texture of shrimp meat is another major factor in consumer choices, as texture influences the sensory attributes and taste of food (Jeltema, Beckley, & Vahalik, 2016). Surprisingly, the present study revealed that shrimp reared in red tanks exhibited overall improved meat texture and flavor compared to those from gray tanks. Consumer satisfaction with cooked meat primarily depends on its tenderness, flavor, and juiciness (Glitsch, 2000). Specifically, after steaming, shrimp cultured in red tanks displayed significantly higher hardness, adhesiveness, and chewiness compared to their counterparts in gray tanks, suggesting a firmer and more resilient meat texture. For instance, wild salmon, a more popular choice, demonstrate greater hardness and chewiness than farmed salmon (Johnston et al., 2006). Similarly, consumers generally prefer shrimp meat that is firm and elastic. Muscle firmness is influenced by various factors (Hu et al., 2013), of which collagen has been shown to positively correlate with muscle firmness in aquatic animals (Cheng et al., 2021). However, the results of the present study did not find any effect of red tank aquaculture on the collagen levels in shrimp muscle. Free amino acids are essential components of meat and a primary determinant of shrimp flavor (Li et al., 2021). Environmental factors, such as salinity and pH, have been shown to significantly influence the free amino acid content in animals (Hsieh et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2024). In the present study, the contents of glutamic acid and total umami amino acids in shrimp meat were significantly higher in those reared in red tanks. Glutamic acid and aspartic acid are crucial for maintaining freshness and are also the primary sources of umami in shrimp (Yinzhe Jin et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2017). All these results indicate that employing a red background in aquaculture can enhance the overall texture and flavor of shrimp meat. This study is the first to report on the effect of background color on the muscle texture and flavor of L. vannamei.

Serum biochemical indices of shrimp are intimately associated with metabolic processes and are extensively utilized to assess nutritional status, health condition, and environmental adaptability (Pan et al., 2022). Results showed that red tank aquaculture significantly increased levels of alkaline phosphatase (AKP), acid phosphatase (ACP), glucose (GLU), and total protein (TP) in shrimp serum. AKP regulates calcium and phosphorus metabolism to promote non-specific immunity during growth and development (Bi et al., 2017). ACP is an important component of lysosomal phagocytosis, serving as a marker enzyme for lysosomes; its elevation suggests enhanced non-specific immunity (Rodríguez et al., 2000; Shen et al., 2020). Blood glucose level is a routine stress marker (Mercier et al., 2006), and an increase indicates an enhanced stress response in shrimp cultured in red tanks, partially explaining the reduced survival rate in this study. Serum TP can serve as a reliable indicator for evaluating shrimp health and protein metabolism (Pascual, Gaxiola, & Rosas, 2003; Rodríguez et al., 2000). Our data on these serum biological markers suggest that red tank aquaculture may affect protein metabolism to some extent. Similar studies showed that combined red and blue light enhances TP levels in trout (Chen et al., 2022). Additionally, total antioxidant capacity in the liver of shrimp from red tanks was significantly lower than in those from gray tanks. This is consistent with decreased astaxanthin accumulation in hepatopancreas of shrimp from red tanks, as astaxanthin is a potent antioxidant (Zhao et al., 2022). Overall, red tank culture slightly enhanced non-specific immunity, likely due to stress responses in shrimp induced by the red background. To thoroughly assess stress levels in white shrimp or other organisms, multiple markers including corticosteroid levels, behavioral changes, and immune function should be considered (Rotllant et al., 2003). Further research is necessary to investigate how aquaculture environment color impacts the health and well-being of L. vannamei.

Finally, we investigated the mechanism by which background color alters shrimp body coloration. Astaxanthin content largely determines shrimp body color (Boonyaratpalin, Thongrod, Supamattaya, Britton, & Schlipalius, 2001; Tomas et al., 2020). Studies indicate that free astaxanthin binds to crustacyanin (CRCN), promoting its accumulation and thereby affecting tissue pigmentation (Wade, Tollenaere, Hall, & Degnan, 2009). In this study, red tank cultivation significantly increased astaxanthin content in shrimp shells while reducing it in the hepatopancreas; muscle astaxanthin content remained unchanged. These results suggest that L. vannamei likely transfers astaxanthin from the hepatopancreas to the shell, thereby promoting shell pigmentation to mimic the red background. Additionally, we analyzed expression of pigmentation-related genes in the hepatopancreas. Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) encoded by CYP2J19 is a key enzyme for carotenoid-to-astaxanthin conversion (Yue Jin et al., 2021; Weaver, Gonzalez, Santos, & Havird, 2020), and such conversion is a primary reason for red coloration in crustaceans (Weaver et al., 2020). Red tank cultivation significantly decreased cyp450 gene expression in the hepatopancreas, indicating that the red background does not induce shrimp reddening by enhancing carotenoid conversion. This also explains reduced astaxanthin deposition observed in the hepatopancreas. The red pigment-concentrating hormone (RPCH) regulates pigment aggregation in chromatophores of Eucarida eyestalks, which contain red/dark pigments (Fernlund & Josefsson, 1968). rpch gene expression significantly increased in the hepatopancreas under red tank conditions, suggesting background redness may concentrate red pigment cells in shells via rpch, enhancing body redness.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that red tank culture can effectively enhance the body redness, meat texture, and flavor of L. vannamei (Fig. 10). Changing the tank color is the most cost-effective method, and it is entirely possible to cultivate shrimp with better quality. On the other hand, the red background may have caused a mild stress response in shrimp, reducing their survival rate by 7 %. Finally, we found that the red background may enhance the redness of shrimp by promoting the transfer of astaxanthin from the hepatopancreas to the shell and increasing the expression of red pigment-concentrating hormone (rpch) in the hepatopancreas.
