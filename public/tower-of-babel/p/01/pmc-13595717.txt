Performance of the Pre-Eclampsia Integrated Estimate of Risk–Machine Learning (PIERS-ML) model in a Kenyan cohort of women with pre-eclampsia- a retrospective test derivation validation study
Authors: Felix Nyagaka, Tünde Montgomery-Csobán, Ingrid Gichere, Mukaindo Mwaniki, Kimberley Kavanagh, Laura A Magee, Peter von Dadelszen, Felix Oindi, And the PIERS Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePre-eclampsia is a cause of significant maternal morbidity and mortality, with delivery initiating resolution. The Pre-eclampsia Integrated Estimate of RiSk-machine learning (PIERS-ML) tool provides individualized risk estimates to guide joint decision-making for women with pre-eclampsia. While it has been externally validated in the UK, our objective was to test PIERS-ML performance in Kenya.DesignRetrospective cohort validation study.SettingTwo tertiary hospitals in Nairobi, Kenya.PopulationWomen admitted with pre-eclampsia who had not experienced any element of the main outcome measure.MethodsTest performance was assessed by stratification capacity, area under the receiver-operator curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), and decision curve analysis.Main outcome measuresAny component of the PIERS primary outcome of maternal death or major maternal organ dysfunction within 48 h of admission.ResultsAmong 2,002 women with pre-eclampsia, 408 (20.4%) experienced an adverse maternal outcome within 48 h of admission (including 4 deaths) and a further 74 (3.7%) between 3–7 days. Introduction

Defined as hypertension identified at ≥ 20 + 0 weeks’ gestation accompanied by either maternal target organ damage or evidence of uteroplacental dysfunction [1], pre-eclampsia is a progressive and unpredictable pregnancy-specific syndrome that complicates approximately 2%−13% of all pregnancies, and is associated with approximately 46,000 maternal and 500,000 perinatal deaths annually.

Delivery of the placenta initiates disease resolution, recognizing that up to 20% of pre-eclampsia is first diagnosed postnatally [2]. Shared decision making regarding the place, timing, and mode of birth is dependent on balancing largely gestational age-dependent perinatal risks with maternal risks [3, 4]. While individual women’s risks are greater with increasing prematurity, the population burden of risk is at term [5, 6].

In Kenya, an expectant approach, including subjective serial integration of clinical parameters and lab tests, is taken in most cases of pre-eclampsia remote from term. Kenyan women can access antenatal care at no cost in public hospitals, however, beyond the standard maternity care, many tests and services are paid out-of-pocket, posing a barrier to access. Due to high volume of patients at these hospitals, even when cost is not a barrier, resources such as trained staff and necessary reagents may not be available, further restricting access to appropriate testing. Private hospitals provide a more comprehensive service at higher cost for women and families with sufficient disposable income.

Maternal risk is not assessed directly to inform timing of birth in either private or public hospitals, rather, affected women are delivered only when observed clinical and laboratory deterioration occurs [7, 8].

The PIERS-ML (Pre-eclampsia Integrated Estimate of RiSk-Machine Learning) tool, intended to be used by maternity care providers in hospital settings, provides individual women whose pregnancies are complicated by pregnancy hypertension and their maternity care providers with personalised risk estimates for severe adverse maternal events within 48 h of admission.

PIERS-ML includes health system, demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables, and was developed and internally-validated in a cohort of 8843 hypertensive pregnant women from Africa (South Africa, Uganda), the Americas (Brazil, Canada, United States), South Asia (Pakistan), Europe (Finland, the Netherlands, United Kingdom), and Oceania (Australia, Fiji, New Zealand); external validation was undertaken in a relatively low-risk cohort of 2901 hypertensive pregnant women in South-East England. Informed by positive (+ LR) and negative (-LR) likelihood ratios, individual women are placed into risk strata, namely very-low risk (-LR < 0.1; ≤ 0.5% risk), low risk (-LR 0.1 to 0.2; 0.6% to 3.0% risk), moderate risk (-LR > 0.2 and + LR < 5.0; 3.1% to 18.6% risk), high risk (+ LR 5.0 to 10.0; 18.7% to 45.5% risk), and very-high risk (+ LR > 10.0; ≥ 45.6% risk). These performance metrics, however, were measured on both retrospective observational data, and on study data with well-defined study protocols.

In routine clinical care, however, the availability of patient data differs substantially from study environments. When data is collected for research, tests are carried out systematically and often more frequently than in routine care [9]. In real-world settings, particularly in low-resource settings in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), data is often missing due to additional factors, such as availability of equipment and trained staff, clinical judgement or patients’ ability to cover the cost of the required tests. Such missingness, while informative, can be challenging for models and methods that were developed on well-defined study data to handle, and they may show lower performance when deployed on incomplete individual patient records [10].

Predictive tools intended for real clinical use therefore must account for the differences in data availability at the development and deployment stages [11], and it has been proposed that validation of the model and missing data management should be concurrent [12].

To the best of our knowledge, no pre-eclampsia adverse maternal risk prediction tool has been validated or implemented for use in Kenya. In view of significant maternal morbidity and mortality due to pre-eclampsia, there is a need for a clinical risk prediction tool that reliably predicts adverse maternal outcomes. Correctly identifying the specific risk to each woman before the occurrence of any of the adverse outcomes due to pre-eclampsia may help guide joint decision making between the patient and the clinician. Moreover, due to various barriers to the availability of blood test results particularly in public hospitals, there is a need to validate such a tool and its missing data management in a low resource setting.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the performance of the PIERS-ML tool in quantifying personalised risks of severe adverse maternal outcomes in a Kenyan cohort of women with pre-eclampsia within 48 h of their initial assessment.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective validation study between January 2024 and February 2025, at The Aga-Khan University Hospital (AKUH) and Pumwani Maternity Hospital (PMH), both in Nairobi, Kenya. AKUH is a private teaching and referral hospital while PMH is a public teaching and referral hospital.

Study population

The study included the chart review of pregnant women and women in the puerperium, admitted between January 2022 and January 2024 with a diagnosis of pre-eclampsia (broadly defined by the 2021 ISSHP criteria) [1]. Charts were reviewed for date of admission, clinical assessment dates, and dates for any occurrences of the elements of the primary outcome (described below). Women whose first adverse outcome occurrence was recorded before their date of admission, or after their date of admission but before their first clinical assessment were removed from the study. After identification of date of admission, charts were only reviewed for the following seven days. In case of multiple admissions, only the first admission to hospital with pre-eclampsia was included.

Sample size

To determine sample size, we used clinical utility (net benefit) for health outcomes [13]. With a conservative minimum outcome rate of 5%, to validate the PIERS-ML model, we needed a minimal sample of 1,800 women with pre-eclampsia of whom at least 90 experienced one or more components of the adverse outcomes.

Hospital record review

Clinical notes and patients’ physical (PMH) or digital (AKUH) hospital records were retrieved to obtain variables of interest. Variables obtained were those relevant to pre-eclampsia during the woman’s first admission only (if there were subsequent admissions). These routinely collected variables included demographic data, maternal past obstetric history, clinical symptoms, signs and laboratory test results. The most clinically relevant abnormal value for each variable was recorded per day, from the first day of eligibility until either discharge or a week from admission, whichever occurred first. These were defined as minimum values for platelet counts, haematocrit, SpO2, albumin, an affirmative response for symptoms, and maximum value for all other repeated variables. Gestational age was determined based on the first day of their last menstrual period or using due date from the first trimester dating ultrasound scan. Adverse maternal outcomes occurring after admission were recorded.

Data were entered into REDCap software. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (2024–10–31 ucrt) [14]. Multiple imputation for missing variables was carried out with the missRanger package [15], and the caret [16] and caret Ensemble [17] packages were used to obtain predicted probabilities and risk strata from the PIERS-ML model.

Primary outcome

The outcome to be predicted is a composite developed by Delphi consensus, including input from the Preeclampsia Foundation (Anne Garrett), and is defined as the occurrence of one or more of the following outcomes within 48 h of admission [8]. The components of the combined adverse maternal outcome were: maternal mortality or one or more serious central nervous system (CNS; eclampsia (≥ 1), Glasgow coma score < 13, stroke or reversible ischaemic neurological deficit, transient ischaemic attack, cortical blindness or retinal detachment, posterior reversible encephalopathy), cardiorespiratory (positive inotropic support, infusion of a third parenteral antihypertensive drug, myocardial ischaemia or infarction, SpO2 [oxygen saturation (pulse oximetry)] < 90%, ≥ 50% FiO2 [fraction of inspired oxygen] for > 1 h, intubation [other than for Caesarean section], pulmonary oedema), haematological (transfusion of any blood product, platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L, with no transfusion), hepatic (dysfunction, haematoma, rupture), renal (acute kidney injury, dialysis), placental abruption, and other adverse events (e.g., severe ascites, Bell’s palsy) (Table S2 for definitions).

Missing data management

Missing data was managed with two distinct strategies: individual imputation and dataset imputation.

The first process was caried out to replicate the process of deploying a predictive model in a digital platform. To such a platform patient information may be entered individually to receive personal treatment recommendations, for which missing data must be handled for without influence of data from other patients in the cohort. In such a platform, an imputation process may be applied to data which was trained on the same training dataset as the model and subsequently used to impute missing values on new, unseen data. To replicate this process, the same multiple imputation algorithm used in the original PIERS-ML was used to predict missing values in the Kenyan data. Each individual observations (i.e. row of the data) was imputed independently and proportionally to the amount of missing data, meaning that if M% of the row was missing, it was imputed M times.

To provide a base for comparison, the validation dataset was also imputed using traditional methods. Data was combined with the PIERS-ML development dataset and imputed M times using multiple chained random forest with the missRanger r package, where M was the percentage of missing data in the combined dataset. The process was also repeated with only data from similar low- and middle-income countries as a sensitivity analysis.

Sensitivity analyses were also carried out using the median value from the PIERS-ML development dataset for variables with > 60% missing in the validation cohort plus multiple imputation for the remaining variables, and by using median values for all missing data as well. Notably, laboratory variables in the validation cohort, such as transaminases and creatinine had missingness exceeding 90%. Complete case analysis was not possible on this data.

Statistical analyses

Predictions were made with the PIERS-ML model on all imputed datasets, and the mean predicted probability was calculated for each woman. Analyses focused on prediction of an outcome within 48 h of admission to match PIERS-ML’s intended use, however outcomes occurring up to 7 days after admission were included in descriptive statistics.

Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, and Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare categorical and continuous variables. ANOVA tests were applied to multiple continuous variable comparisons. The PIERS-ML tool was assessed on its ability to correctly classify women into outcome and no outcome groups by the area under receiver operator curve, and precision-recall curves using this dataset. Decision curve analysis was used to assess the clinical utility of the model. Using the predicted probability from the PIERS-ML model, the women were grouped into various risk strata based on pre-defined risk thresholds. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 2022 and January 2024, a total of 2,010 pre-eclamptic women attended either AKUH (144/2010, 7.16%) or PMH (1866/2010, 94.84%). Eight of these women in PMH experienced an outcome at an unclear time, close to their time of admission, and therefore were removed from the dataset as at the time of analysis it was not possible to determine what variables were collected from these women prior to the occurrence of their adverse event. The contemporaneous Kenyan World Bank per capita gross domestic product (GDP; $US 2097) and WHO estimate of maternal mortality ratio (MMR; 355 per 100,000 livebirths) were shared for all the women in the study. The health system and demographic characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Women who developed adverse outcomes differed from those with no outcomes in a few aspects. These women presented at an earlier gestation, tended to have severe hypertension, severe dyspnoea with low SpO2, proteinuria, thrombocytopaenia, elevated creatinine, and transaminitis.

Missing data

Healthcare system, demographics, past and current medical and obstetrical history and symptoms were available for all patients, while blood pressure measurements and blood oxygen were missing for < 1%. There was a significant amount of missing data in all remaining variables. Patient height and weight were routinely recorded only at AKUH, where only 3.50% of values were missing, however almost no patients had their height or weight recorded at PMH (Table 2). Proteinuria was measured for 30.60% of patients, generally recorded consistently at AKUH and more rarely at PMH, and lab tests were available for only ~ 10% of patients due to less than 5% of patients at PMH having any blood tests recorded. Moreover, mean platelet volume and uric acid were not recorded at either location.

Between patients who did or did not experience an outcome, those with no adverse outcomes had slightly higher levels of missing values, however these differences were not as notable as the differences between locations.

On the individual level, most women were missing 11 (N = 1549) or less (N = 408) of the 18 model variables.

Performance of the PIERS-ML model in this cohort

Risk stratification

Using the predicted probability from the PIERS-ML model imputed on an individual basis, the women in the study were grouped into various risk strata based on pre-defined risk thresholds (Table 3 and Table 4). No women were stratified into the very-low and low risk strata, almost ~ 75% (1537/2002) into moderate risk, and all but one of the remaining as high risk. Stratification capacity within risk strata within 48 h was accurate and comparable to the internal and UK-based external validation cohorts (Table 3). As with the internal and UK-based external validation cohorts, both AUROC (68.12% [95% C.I. 65.03%−71.2%]) and AUPRC (0.397 [95% C.I. 0.349–0.447] vs 48 h outcome rate 0.203) performance were good (Table 3).

Decision curve analysis

Clinical utility of the PIERS-ML model on decision making (treating versus not treating women in the various probability thresholds) was assessed using the decision curve analysis.

A detailed description of the decision curve analysis and explanation of the interpretation can be found in the supplement. Induction of labour for women in high or very high PIERS-ML risk strata was shown to have a higher net benefit than either induction of labour for all or expectant management for all as long as the average acceptable number of inductions to prevent outcome for 1 woman is between 7 and 2.5 (Fig. 1). If it is acceptable to induce more than 7 women to prevent 1 outcome, then it is possible to induce all women and prevent all outcomes, and therefore risk stratification is not needed.

Decision curve analysis. The X-axis represents treatment threshold probabilities while the Y-axis represents the net benefit of each strategy. The solid red line represents a ‘treat all approach’, the solid green line represents a ‘treat none approach’, and the solid blue and purple lines represents the net benefit of treating patients based on their predicted PIERS-ML risk strata (high or very high, or very high only, respectively)

Discussion

Main findings

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing the utility and application of the PIERS-ML model in a low- or middle-income clinical setting. Validation with retrospectively collected data is important as it represents the current clinical practice, allowing us to identify differences in real-life and clinical settings. This study represents two different but important settings the PIERS-ML may be used in Kenya: a public hospital with high volume of referrals servicing women living in informal settlements in Nairobi (PMH), and a well-resourced private hospital afforded by the more affluent population (AKUH).

We observed important differences between these settings in the availability of diagnostics, representing the difference in disposable income of the population attending these hospitals, as many tests are paid out-of-pocket and public hospitals often offer a more limited range of tests than private hospitals to keep costs low [18, 19]. The Kenyan cohort also differed in the distribution of variables from both the PIERS-ML development cohort, and the low- and middle-income subset of this cohort. While this difference could be due to the higher levels of missing data, it is also possible that women are admitted to hospital in Kenya at a more progressed stage of the condition and thus worse measures are more often observed. These differences pose challenges in both the deployment of predictive models in real clinical settings, and the evaluation of model performance.

While the original PIERS-ML study assumed data missing at or completely at random, there are indications that missing data structure was different in the Kenyan population. Missing data carried information on location as data availability was primarily defined by the hospital the woman attended, which then linked to level of income, an indicator previously correlated with baseline health assessments [20, 21]. Although limited, there is evidence linking low socioeconomic status to maternal morbidity and mortality. Inequalities in access to antenatal care have been postulated to be a reason with women who suffer significant morbidity presenting late to hospitals [22–24].

Beyond data issues, we also observed indications that the Kenyan cohort was generally more severely affected by pre-eclampsia than the original PIERS-ML cohort. Outcome rate was substantially higher in the Kenyan cohort (20.4% vs 6.7%) within 48 h of admission, dominant outcomes were more severe (hepatic dysfunction and AKI vs blood transfusion), maternal mortality was higher and occurred withing 48 h. The last issue in particular highlights the need for immediate risk stratification on admission with prediction of outcome within 48 h.

Despite high levels of missing data and indicators that women in this cohort may have been sicker, when applied in the context of a digital platform handling missing data, PIERS-ML still maintained good discrimination (AUROC 68%, AUPRC 39.7%) comparable to the previously reported performances (shown in Table 3). Risk stratification (shown in Table 3 and Table 4) reflected expected behaviour – moderate risk was not informative for ruling in or ruling out the occurrence of outcomes, however showed slightly lower outcome rates than the overall cohort, indicating that these women could be managed under current clinical practice without a detriment to observed outcome rates.

High risk strata doubled the outcome rate seen in the overall cohort, these women could be monitored with greater care and considered for induction of labour to prevent complications. Very-high risk saw a 100% outcome rate, although this is based on a single patient who experienced an outcome. Nonetheless, women classed as very-high risk can be considered for induction of labour as soon as possible, and other interventions such as blood pressure control measures, magnesium sulphate, and antenatal corticosteroids.

Notably, PIERS-ML classified 29.8% of women in the original internal validation into very-low or low risk, while none in the Kenyan cohort. This may be an indication that the Kenyan cohort is already significantly higher risk on admission to hospital, as supported by the more severe outcomes, or that the model’s predictive ability was limited by missing data in important variables which could allow differentiation from moderate risk.

Decision curve analysis [25] indicated that “treatment”, meaning induction of labour in this context, informed by risk strata showed an improvement in net benefit, the prevention of outcomes balanced by early delivery, compared to both treating all patients and treating none. This suggests that even in resource-limited environments, digital deployment of the PIERS-ML model could support allocation of the limited resources to where they will matter the most.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths to note. First, validation was carried out with a good sample size of 2002 unselected women from both public and private hospitals in Nairobi, Kenya. Not only is this study the first external validation of the PIERS-ML model in an LMIC cohort, but it is also the first external validation reflecting deployment conditions. In a healthcare setting with meagre resources, correctly identifying women at high and very high risk of adverse outcomes and prioritising treatment to them should greatly improve their health outcomes. In addition, correct identification would inform shared decision-making on key interventions because of the high level of certainty that these women would suffer adverse events within the crucial 48 h following admission [26].

Rigorous statistical approaches also strengthen our study, including assessment of different imputation strategies and using both standard model evaluation metrics such as AUROC, metrics better suited for unbalanced data (AUPRC) and metrics assessing clinical utility (decision curve analysis).

However, retrospective studies have limitations based on their design. Such studies review data that was not intended for research purposes at the point of capture and, therefore, missing data is common [27]. Our cohort did have a high level of missing data, especially laboratory tests at admission. A large proportion of women in the study cohort were from low socio-economic backgrounds. Some were possibly not able to afford expensive baseline investigations assessing organ function such as liver function tests and kidney function tests. Operational constraints likely contributed to inconsistencies in recording (e.g., high patient-to-provider ratios), which can degrade both data quality and model performance. In addition, some variables (e.g., mean platelet volume, uric acid) are not routinely assessed in the Kenyan setting and, therefore, these were imputed for all participants. Imputation may introduce bias into the study.

Our findings support the feasibility of deploying the PIERS-ML model in a digital platform in low- and middle-income countries even with limited resources, however robust individual patient-based missing data management methods must be included in such a platform. Future work could assess data availability in Kenya in different settings and develop more appropriate approaches to missing data handling, considering the differences highlighted by this study. Future studies could then look at the real-time performance, clinical utility and adoption of such a platform.

This study also highlights a key result for the Kenyan healthcare system – the importance of improved documentation and routine data collection for low-cost variables (such as height and weight) to improve predictive performance of existing models, in turn reducing costly outcomes and freeing resources, especially in public hospitals.

Interpretation

Current models such as PIERS-ML, fullPIERS, and miniPIERS are static tools whose performance degrades beyond 48 h from the initial assessment; the epoch for which they were intended to be used. Currently, neither fullPIERS nor PIERS-ML should be used for consecutive risk predictions. A dynamic model is necessary for continuous prediction. Pre-eclampsia is a life-threatening syndrome with an unpredictable course. Therefore, there is a need for regular re-assessment of patient risk in ongoing care, especially when conservative management is offered for pre-term pre-eclampsia [26].

Conclusion

In a limited-resource setting with high levels of non-random missingness, PIERS-ML demonstrated useful discrimination ability and clinical utility. Understanding of missing data patterns and the development of robust missing data handling methods are crucial to unlock utility of existing predictive models in LMICs, which can enable shared decision making between women, their families, and their maternity care providers to guide management and to optimise maternal outcomes.
