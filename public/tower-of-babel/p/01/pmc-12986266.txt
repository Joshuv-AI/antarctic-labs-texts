Multi-Omics Integration Reveals Temporal Partitioning Between Metabolic Priming and Proliferative Expansion in PGPR-Treated Cherry Plants
Authors: Zhaoqing Tong, Ze Tao, Fangdong Li, Jiali He, Sijun Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) can rebalance growth–defense trade-offs in plants. However, the temporal molecular mechanisms underlying sustained growth promotion in woody fruit crops, particularly cherry (Prunus avium), remain largely unclear. This study inoculated Gisela 6 sweet cherry seedlings with three PGPR strains (Rahnella Y17, Arthrobacter Y37, and Bacillus megaterium P6). Phenotypic and physiological traits were assessed at 60 days (d), while targeted phytohormone metabolomics and root transcriptomes were profiled at 30 and 40 d post-treatment. Our results demonstrated that all three PGPR strains enhanced plant growth, photosynthetic capacity, and root architecture, with Y37 demonstrating superior biomass promotion. Phytohormone dynamics featured consistent ABA (abscisic acid) suppression, coupled with an early elevation of GA (gibberellin) and auxin followed by subsequent cytokinin accumulation. Notably, Y37 uniquely enriched jasmonate intermediates. Comparative transcriptomic analysis uncovered strain-specific trajectories, with integrated co-expression analysis defining modules associated with early metabolism and later structural remodeling. Key hub genes were identified as involved in hormone regulation and cell wall synthesis. Collectively, these findings suggest that Y37 drives a temporal partitioning from metabolic priming to architectural reinforcement by reallocating carbon and tuning hormone pathways, thereby underpinning superior growth and resilience. This study provides novel insights into PGPR-based strategies for sustainable cherry production.

1. Introduction

Sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.) is a globally important fruit crop, valued for its exquisite flavor and high economic returns [1]. The productivity and quality of sweet cherry are heavily dependent on the health and vigor of seedlings during their establishment phase [2,3]. However, young plants are often susceptible to various biotic and abiotic stresses, which can impede growth and compromise subsequent yield [4,5]. Compared to other fruit trees such as apple, cherry trees possess a less favorable root-to-shoot ratio characterized by shallow root distribution [6], reduced fibrous root development, and lower overall root density [7]. This suboptimal root architecture significantly compromises their adaptability to adverse soil conditions, particularly drought and waterlogging [8,9]. Hence, it is imperative to develop economically feasible, highly efficient, and sustainable solutions to enhance root function in cherry plants, thereby fostering their overall growth, and securing the long-term sustainability of the sweet cherry industry [10,11].

The utilization of plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) represents a promising frontier in sustainable agriculture, offering viable alternatives to chemical inputs while enhancing crop performance [12,13,14]. These beneficial microbes, which colonize the rhizosphere, have been shown to enhance plant growth through various mechanisms, including nutrient solubilization, phytohormone production, and the induction of disease resistance [15,16,17,18]. The role of PGPR in promoting healthy plant growth, especially under adverse environmental conditions, underscores their potential as a natural alternative to chemical fertilizers and pesticides, addressing concerns related to environmental sustainability and food security [19]. While numerous studies have documented the positive effects of PGPR on various crops, the intricate molecular dialogue between the host plant and the specific PGPR strain, particularly regarding its temporal dynamics, remains incompletely understood. The plant’s response to PGPR colonization is a highly dynamic process involving complex signaling networks and metabolic adjustments [20,21]. Phytohormones, such as auxin, cytokinins (CKs), gibberellins (GAs), abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), and salicylic acid (SA), act as central regulators, orchestrating the balance between growth and defense, a phenomenon often referred to as the growth–defense tradeoff [22,23]. Understanding how PGPR modulates the host’s hormonal network over time is pivotal to deciphering their mode of action [24,25].

With the advent of high-throughput omics technologies, it is now possible to simultaneously profile changes at the transcriptomic and metabolomic levels, providing a holistic view of the plant’s response [26,27]. Integrating these multi-omics datasets can reveal novel gene–metabolite networks and uncover the key regulatory hubs that govern complex biological processes [28]. Recently, a genomic analysis identified P. palleroniana P6 as a strain capable of phosphorus and potassium solubilization, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) synthesis, and siderophore production [29]. Further transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses confirmed that this strain likely promotes the growth and polyphyllin biosynthesis in P. polyphylla by regulating plant hormone signal transduction and saponin synthesis pathways [29]. Using the genome-wide metabolic pathway annotation and the differential enrichment pathway analysis of multi-omics, Yue et al. [30] elucidated that E. cloacae Rs-35 can promote cotton balance cell ion concentration, stabilize intracellular IAA changes, stimulate the induction of systemic tolerance, and enhance cotton growth under salt stress.

In this study, we hypothesized that different PGPR strains would elicit distinct, time-dependent molecular responses in sweet cherry roots, and that the most effective strain employs a unique strategy to optimize the growth–defense balance. To test this, we inoculated sweet cherry seedlings with three candidate PGPR strains (Rahnella Y17, Arthrobacter Y37, and Bacillus megaterium P6), which possess varying capacities for IAA production, nitrogen fixation, and phosphorus/potassium solubilization. We first evaluated their effects on plant phenotype and physiology at 60 days post-inoculation. To capture the dynamic regulation from active interaction to legacy effects, we designed a time-series experiment involving continuous inoculation followed by a recovery period. Multi-omics of the root transcriptome and targeted phytohormone metabolome analyses were specifically conducted at 30 and 40 days to target the active transcriptional reprogramming phase, prior to the stabilization of phenotypic traits. Through integrated multi-omics analysis, we aimed to: (1) identify the most effective PGPR strain for promoting sweet cherry growth; (2) characterize the temporal changes in the root transcriptome and hormone profiles in response to PGPR inoculation; and (3) elucidate the key molecular pathways and regulatory networks, particularly for the most promising strain, that contribute to its superior growth-promoting effects. These findings will provide novel insights into the temporal molecular mechanisms underlying PGPR-mediated growth promotion and lay the foundation for developing highly efficient, sustainable biostimulants to enhance the productivity and adaptability of the sweet cherry industry.

2. Results

To investigate the growth-promoting mechanisms of PGPR strains, we conducted a pot experiment with a specific focus on the temporal dynamics of plant–microbe interactions. We monitored phenotypic traits continuously at 30, 40, 50, and 60 days post-treatment to assess the long-term growth effects. In contrast, transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles were specifically characterized at the critical signaling stages (30 and 40 days) to capture the early molecular regulation events driving these phenotypic changes (Figure 1).

2.1. PGPR Inoculation Promotes Aboveground Growth and Photosynthetic Performance

2.1.1. Plant Biomass

By 60 d post-treatment, distinct growth promotion phenotypes emerged among the PGPR strains (Table 1). Among the tested strains, Y37 and P6 elicited the most substantial enhancements in plant height, as well as aboveground fresh and dry weights. Notably, the Y37 group exhibited the highest values across these parameters. Furthermore, Y37 also significantly increased stem diameter and root biomass (both fresh and dry weights) compared to the CK group.

2.1.2. Chlorophyll Content and Photosynthetic Performance

Photosynthetic performance was also modulated by PGPR inoculation (Table 2). The Y37 and P6 treatments significantly increased the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and water use efficiency (WUE), which was associated with a significant decrease in intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), suggesting higher CO2 assimilation efficiency. In contrast, while Y17 significantly increased chlorophyll content, its impact on photosynthesis was less pronounced. Overall, none of the three inoculation treatments had a significant effect on the transpiration rate (Tr) and stomatal conductance (Gs).

2.2. PGPR Inoculation Significantly Enhances Root System Architecture and Vitality

As the primary interface for plant–microbe interactions, the root system showed significant improvements. All three PGPR strains (Y17, Y37, and P6) significantly increased the total root length, root surface area and root volume. Among them, the Y37 treatment led to the highest root fresh and dry weights, as well as the greatest number of root tips and links (Table 3), indicating a more complex and extensive root system. PGPR treatments displayed increased root vitality peaking around 50 d compared to CK, which remained consistently lower (Figure 2). Root vitality assays over time revealed that all PGPR treatments enhanced root activity, particularly at 40 and 50 d. At 50 d, the Y17 and Y37 groups maintained higher root vitality compared with P6. Root vitality reached its peak at 50 d before showing a general decrease at 60 d across all treatments. This temporal trend likely reflects natural phenological transitions and the gradual depletion of substrate nutrients towards the conclusion of the experiment.

2.3. PGPR Strains Differentially Modulate Root Respiration Pathways

The magnitude of the root respiration rate can reflect the degree of material and energy exchange between the root system and its surrounding soil environment. As shown in Figure 3, all three strains overall increased the total respiratory rate. Different PGPR treatments had varying effects on the total root respiration rate. P6 displayed the highest total respiratory rate and EMP and PPP fluxes, while the Y17 treatment demonstrated the highest TCA cycle activity. Notably, while Y37 only induced a modest increase in total respiration, it significantly enhanced the EMP and TCA pathway fluxes, indicating a more efficient energy metabolism strategy than that observed in P6-treated plants.

2.4. PGPR Treatments Induce Time-Dependent Shifts in the Root Hormonal Profile

To decipher the hormonal basis of PGPR-induced growth, targeted metabolomics was performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the 60 d phytohormone profiles exhibited a clear time and treatment separation between the CK and PGPR-treated groups along PC1 (33.2% variance), and between the 30 d and 40 d time points along PC2 (14.4%), indicating significant effects of both treatment and time (Figure 4a). PLS-DA separated PGPR-treated roots from controls across time, and the top 20 variables contributing to group discrimination were shown in Figure 4b. Overall, metabolic perturbations were stronger at 30 d than at 40 d, with Y17 exhibiting the most pronounced early shifts. Indole-3-acetyl glycine (IAA-Gly) had the highest VIP score, indicating the strongest discriminatory power among the groups. Unlike most metabolites, IAA-Gly tended to be relatively higher at 40 d than at 30 d. Several phytohormones and related compounds exhibited an early increase followed by attenuation at 40 d, including ABA (with the second-highest VIP score), gibberellin A19 (GA19), GA7, (±) strigol, 3-indolepropionic acid, IAA, N6-isopentenyladenosine-5′-monophosphate (iPRMP), indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), SA, N6-isopentenyladenine, N6-isopentenyl-adenine-9-glucoside (iP9G) and dihydrojasmonic acid (H2JA). In contrast, indole-3-acetyl glutamic acid (IAA-Glu), cis-(+)-12-Oxophytodienoic acid (OPDA), indole-3-carboxylic acid (ICA), dihydrozeatin-O-glucoside riboside (DHZROG), and 2-methylthio-cis-zeatin (2MeScZ) exhibited treatment- and time-specific shifts rather than a uniform decline from 30 d to 40 d. Together, these patterns suggest a transient hormone surge at 30 d, most evident under Y17, followed by partial normalization by 40 d, while IAA conjugates and JA precursors (e.g., OPDA) displayed distinct temporal behaviors across strains.

Heatmap analysis of differential metabolites (|log2FC| > 0.58 and p-value < 0.05) revealed distinct temporal and treatment-specific patterns (Figure 5). At 30 d, free IAA levels increased across all treatments, accompanied by a higher auxin conjugate [Indole-3-acetyl-L-aspartic acid (IAA-Asp)], with Y37 and P6 demonstrating the substantial fold changes. By 40 d, the auxin profile shifted, with conjugated auxins such as IAA-Gly becoming significantly upregulated in all strains. Cytokinin glucosides (iP9G and K9G) increased markedly at 30 d in Y37 and P6 but declined by 40 d. At 30 d, GA19 was the most prominently upregulated precursor in all treatments, and GA7 was especially enriched in Y37 compared to CK. By 40 d, the GA profile shifted from GA19 toward downstream GA20 and the catabolite GA8, particularly in Y37 and P6. Furthermore, all strains exhibited significantly reduced ACC levels compared to CK. Shared features included persistent ABA reduction, transient cytokinin glucoside accumulation, GA progression (GA19 → GA20/GA8), and reduced ACC at the later time point. To ensure stringent selection, 19 metabolites with |log2FC| > 0.58, p < 0.05, and VIP > 1 were designated as DAMs (Tables S1 and S2).

2.5. Root Transcriptomic Differences

Transcriptome analysis of root tissues at 30 d and 40 d revealed a temporal shift in the amplitude of differential gene expression across treatments. PCA of the transcriptome profiles (Figure 6a) revealed distinct clustering of samples by both the developmental stage and treatment. PC1, accounting for 31.59% of the total variance, primarily discriminated the 30 d and 40 d samples, indicating a strong time effect. Meanwhile, PC2 (14.71% of the variation) further delineated treatment-related differences. Within each group, biological replicates clustered tightly with minimal outliers, supporting high data consistency. Notably, one outlier sample from the Y17 group (Y17-1-1) was identified and excluded from the downstream analysis due to its significant deviation from the group centroid, ensuring the statistical robustness of the study (details provided in Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). At 30 d, PGFR-treated samples (Y17, Y37, and P6) were markedly displaced from CK along PC1/PC2, whereas at 40 d the separation was still evident but partially reduced, suggesting convergence or remodeling of transcriptional responses over time. Volcano plots summarizing multiple pairwise comparisons (Figure 6b) demonstrated abundant DEGs under a significance threshold of p-adj < 0.05. Collectively, these results indicate that developmental stage is the dominant source of variance, with treatment-specific transcriptional reprogramming superimposed on this stage effect.

A total of 9319 DEGs were identified across all comparisons (Table S3). The most pronounced transcriptional changes were observed between 30 d and 40 d in the Y37 and P6 groups, encompassing 4999 and 4616 DEGs, respectively (Table S4), indicating a significant transcriptional reprogramming event during this period of these two treatments. Notably, among treatment-versus-control comparisons, the Y37 vs. CK at 40 d yielded the highest DEG count (2304), emphasizing Y37’s substantial influence at the later stage (Table S4). In contrast, Y17 exhibited fewer DEGs overall, particularly at 40 d (275 DEGs vs. CK), implying a more subdued or stabilized response. These results reveal three distinct trajectories of gene expression dynamics: an early surge (P6), a delayed systemic escalation (Y37), and an early strong response followed by rapid metabolic stabilization (Y17).

The top 50 DEGs for each comparison group are presented in Table S5, comprising 450 gene entries representing 273 unique genes. The dominant molecular signals were centered on plastid/photosynthetic machinery, hormone/defense signaling, and cell wall/secondary metabolism. Treatment- and stage-specific nuances were apparent: Y17-associated contrasts prominently featured regulation of light-harvesting complexes and photosystems, whereas P6 comparisons integrated plastid metabolism with defense receptor kinases and stress-responsive programs.

2.6. Integrated Multi-Omics Analysis Unveils Functionally Coherent Gene Clusters

2.6.1. Correlation Analysis of Significant Genes and Metabolites

To map the regulatory landscape connecting transcriptional changes to metabolic outputs, we performed a global correlation analysis between the 9319 DEGs and 19 DAMs. This approach yielded a comprehensive dataset of 63,075 statistically significant gene–metabolite pairs (|r| > 0.8, FDR < 0.05), representing a rich resource of potential regulatory interactions (Table S6). The distribution of these correlations revealed distinct patterns across different comparisons: temporal comparisons (40 d vs. 30 d) showed the strongest associations, particularly for Y37 (20,499 pairs) and P6 (15,947 pairs), while Y17 exhibited the most conservative responses across all comparisons (2397 pairs). Among treatment vs. control comparisons, Y37 vs. CK contrast at 40 d displayed the strongest associations (10,065 pairs), followed by P6 vs. CK at 30 d (7038 pairs). In contrast, the Y17 strain elicited a much more constrained response, with far fewer correlated pairs (863 pairs at 30 d and 1030 pairs at 40 d). This global view underscores that Y37 and P6 trigger profound and dynamic gene–metabolite reprogramming, setting the stage for the targeted functional clustering that follows.

As shown in Table 4, the cytokinin glucoside iP9G emerged as the most recurrent metabolic hub across contrasts (see top pairs in the table). Core glycolytic DEGs, including LOC110761288 (ATP-dependent phosphofructokinase-like), LOC110750332 (fructose-bisphosphate aldolase I), and LOC110764330 (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase A, chloroplastic), exhibited large-magnitude, bidirectional correlations with iP9G, suggesting a tight and dynamic coupling between central carbon flux and cytokinin homeostasis.

2.6.2. K-Means Clustering and Functional Profiles

Elbow analysis supported the classification of DEGs and DAMs into four major K-means clusters, each characterized by distinct temporal/treatment signatures and functional themes (Figure 7 and Table S7). Cluster 1 (late response; Y37-40 d dominant) exhibited strong upregulation at 40 d, specifically within the Y37 treatment. Concordant metabolite features included the accumulation of OPDA (a JA precursor), ICA (an indole intermediate), and the inactive GA15. These molecular profiles are consistent with reinforced specialized metabolism and the structural remodeling observed in Y37-treated roots. Cluster 2 (early response; Y37-30 d dominant) showed peak expression at 30 d followed by attenuation at 40 d, a pattern most evident in Y37. This aligned with early increases in growth-related hormones such as IAA, SA, and active GA7. Cluster 3 (photosynthesis-related) was comprised of genes associated with photosynthesis and chloroplast function. Its expression profile reflects systemic source–sink adjustments facilitated by PGPR colonization. Cluster 4 (signaling and defense; P6/Y17-30 d dominant) exhibited marked gene upregulation at 30 d primarily in the P6 treatment, with a similar but weaker pattern in Y17.

2.7. Identification of Hub Genes Driving the Y37-Mediated Response

To identify the core regulators of the PGPR-induced response, we prioritized hub genes based on a stringent tri-modal selection strategy (Table S8). Subsequently, cnet networks were constructed to visualize regulatory coupling, with representative hubs integrated into a cross-condition heatmap to highlight treatment-specific and time-dependent regulatory modules. By constructing gene–pathway networks, we identified several hub genes within the key clusters.

2.7.1. Cluster 1 Hub Gene Detecting

As shown in Figure 8, Cluster 1 (late response; Y37, 40 d upregulated) reveals a highly coordinated activation axis involving secondary metabolism, cell wall/xylem, and hormone metabolism, including 66 hub genes (Table S8). Several key hubs were identified as central to this network. Specially, the ACL5/thermos spermine synthase (LOC110764722), which is pivotal for xylem development and regulation of hormone levels, was found to be consistent with vascular maturation. In terms of hormonal recalibration, the network featured 3-epi-6-deoxocathasterone 23-monooxygenase (LOC110767804), a brassinosteroid (BR) biosynthetic enzyme, and abscisic acid 8′-hydroxylase (LOC110771684), an ABA catabolic enzyme. The upregulation of the latter indicates a strategic relief of ABA-mediated growth inhibition at the later developmental stage. Furthermore, the cluster included a suite of lignin/phenylpropanoid and wall biosynthesis gene, such as cytochrome P450 98A2-like (CYP98A2-like: LOC110765625 and LOC110765626), cellulose synthase A catalytic subunit 4 (CESA4: LOC110771343), laccase-4-like (LAC4-like: LOC110757310), and xylan xylosyltransferases IRX9/IRX10 (LOC110747799, LOC110768713, and LOC110750703). Together, these hubs support wall thickening, vessel maturation, and structural reinforcement in Y37-treated roots by 40 d.

2.7.2. Cluster 2 Hub Gene Detecting

As shown in Figure 9, Cluster 2 (early response; Y37, 30 d high then attenuated at 40 d) defines a network emphasizing “primary metabolism and transport—JA biosynthesis—C/N assimilation”, including 70 hub genes (Table S8). Within this module, several representative hubs were identified. These include high-affinity nitrate transporters (NRT2.1: LOC110755413 and LOC110755414), amino acid vacuolar transporters (AVT1I family: LOC110755544, LOC110755578, and LOC110760872), and an organic cation/carnitine transporter (LOC110760303), all of which are functionally connected to transmembrane transport and vacuole terms. Core metabolism hubs are represented by phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK: LOC110759237), which is linked to the Calvin cycle and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, alongside glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH: LOC110753145), and Malate dehydrogenase (MDH: LOC110771779). Additionally, the JA pathway was represented by 12-oxophytodienoate reductase 3 (OPR3: LOC110749220), a pivotal enzyme bridging α-linolenic acid and JA biosynthesis. Overall, Y37 at 30 d favors resource acquisition and primary metabolic activity, transitioning by 40 d to structural and signaling remodeling.

2.7.3. Cluster 4 Hub Gene Detecting

As shown in Figure 10, Cluster 4 (signaling/defense; P6 dominant at 30 d) reveals that the P6 strain triggers a strong and early induction of signaling and stress modules, including 76 hub genes (Table S8). Key hubs within this network include components of the MAPK cascade (e.g., MAPKK2-like: LOC110749159), phosphatidylinositol signaling (e.g., LOC110769189), and enzymes involved in sphingolipid/glycerophospholipid metabolism (e.g., LOC110764550), alongside genes associated with ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. Furthermore, the stress markers with the strongest upregulation include a low-temperature-induced protein (LOC110748500) and ethylene-responsive transcription factor ERF110-like (LOC110762897). Collectively, these molecular signatures indicate that P6-treated roots prioritize defense priming and signal readiness at 30 d.

2.7.4. Validation of the Candidate Hub Genes Expression Patterns

To further verify the reliability of the multi-omics analysis and the expression patterns of the identified hub genes, qRT-PCR was performed. We selected six representative genes involved in the key pathways discussed above, including cell wall biosynthesis (IRX9, CESA4, and CYP98A2-like) and hormone metabolism and signaling (OPR3, CYP707A4, and ACL5). As shown in Figure 11, the expression patterns of these genes obtained from qRT-PCR were generally consistent with the transcript abundance changes (FPKM) observed in the RNA-seq dataset. For instance, the upregulation of OPR3 at 30 d and its subsequent downregulation at 40 d in the Y37 treatment were accurately captured by both methods. This consistency confirms the accuracy and reproducibility of our transcriptome results.

2.8. Y37-Mediated Reaction Mechanism Hypothesis

To elucidate how Y37 treatment dynamically regulates plant growth and is different from Y17 and P6, we integrated the most prominent hub genes into four functional modules, Architectural Remodeling, Hormonal Switch, Defense and Meristem Preservation, and Primary Growth. Heatmap analysis captured a critical window of transcriptional reprogramming at 40 d (Figure 12a), which was further validated and extended by qRT-PCR analysis of the core representative genes (Figure 11).

In the Architectural Remodeling module, Y37 and P6 showed a prominent upregulation of IRX9/IRX10 (LOC110747799 and LOC110768713), CESA4 (LOC110771343), and LAC4-like (LOC110757310) compared to the control. These genes synergistically coordinate xylan elongation, cellulose deposition, and lignin polymerization, thereby strengthening root architecture and hydraulic capacity. This structural reinforcement was further supported by the qRT-PCR validation of ACL5 (Figure 11), a thermospermine synthase gene crucial for xylem differentiation, which strengthens root architecture and hydraulic capacity, together with LAC4-like. Concurrently, a qualitative reprogramming occurred at 40 d, especially in Y37, where BR biosynthesis (LOC110767804) was induced alongside accelerated ABA catabolism (LOC110771684) and cytokinin turnover (LOC110773015). The subsequent downregulation of OPR3 (LOC110749220) supported by the qRT-PCR validation suggests a dampening of the JA biosynthesis. Collectively, these changes pivot the system from defense priming toward growth investment.

Regarding Defense and Meristem Preservation, consistent with the qRT-PCR results, Y37 upregulated CYP98A2-like (LOC110765626) and CYP73A100-like (LOC110766184) at 40 d, reinforcing phenylpropanoid-derived structural/chemical defenses, while KNAT2 (LOC110763330) supported the maintenance of meristem activity. The downregulation of GSTU19-like (LOC110765075) suggests a metabolic shift from acute detoxification to longer-term structural defense. Primary Growth markers, including nutrient transport (NRT2.1-like and AVT1I-like) and core metabolism genes (GAPDH and DLAT), were downregulated at 40 d relative to 30 d. This indicates a strategic reallocation of carbon/energy from primary assimilation to cell wall biogenesis and specialized metabolism. Together, the 40 d transcriptomic state, particularly in Y37, reflects a deliberate internal reorganization, temporarily slowing cell expansion while upregulating genes associated with cell wall architecture and hormonal signaling to support sustained root growth.

3. Discussion

The emergence of various biotic and abiotic stresses poses significant challenges to global agriculture, necessitating innovative approaches to enhance plant resilience and yield. Among these approaches, the utilization of PGPR has gained increasing attention due to their ability to stimulate plant growth and mitigate the effects of various stresses. PGPR can enhance nutrient availability, promote root development, and induce systemic resistance against pathogens, thereby playing a crucial role in sustainable agricultural practices [15,16]. Understanding the mechanisms underlying these beneficial interactions is essential for optimizing PGPR applications and improving crop productivity, particularly in the face of climate change and evolving agricultural challenges [31].

In this study, we employed a time-resolved multi-omics approach to dissect the dynamic interplay between PGPR inoculation and cherry seedling physiology. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported the beneficial effects of PGPR on plant growth [15], but extend these observations by revealing that different PGPR strains deploy distinct metabolic strategies. We successfully identified the Y37 strain as a superior growth-promoting agent by elucidating its unique capacity for temporal partitioning of resource allocation, shifting from metabolic priming to structural expansion.

Among the three strains, Y37 stood out by delivering the most pronounced improvements in plant height, stem diameter, and biomass (Table 1). The physiological basis for its superiority appears to lie in a uniquely optimized carbon economy, which becomes evident when comparing the divergent metabolic strategies of the three strains (Table 2 and Figure 3). A notable discrepancy was observed in the Y17 treatment, which exhibited the highest chlorophyll content and a high Pn comparable to Y37 and P6, while the roots displayed a significantly higher total respiration rate, driven by markedly elevated TCA cycle activity. This metabolic intensification contrasts with the extensive transcriptional reprogramming seen in other groups, explaining the notably lower number of DEGs identified in Y17. This suggests that in the Y17 treatment, a substantial portion of the carbon fixed through photosynthesis was consumed for metabolic maintenance and energy production in the roots, rather than being allocated to structural growth. This high-cost strategy is further evidenced by the root phenotype: while Y17 developed an extensive root system (with a large surface area and length), its root dry weight was significantly lower than that of other treatments. In sharp contrast, the P6 treatment, although having a higher total respiration rate, was dominated by the EMP pathway that provided a large amount of carbon skeletons for biosynthesis, coupled with the highest net photosynthetic rate, ultimately leading to a significant increase in biomass. Y37 stands out as the most distinctive group; its net photosynthetic rate was high, yet it maintained a total root respiration rate at a low level similar to the control (CK) group, with a considerable portion of it being used for biosynthesis via the EMP pathway. This optimal equilibrium, where photosynthesis enhances carbon input while respiration minimizes carbon loss, results in the highest carbon use efficiency, enabling the conversion of a greater proportion of photosynthates into maximal biomass accumulation. These findings powerfully suggest that in the context of plant growth promotion, simply enhancing the photosynthetic rate is not the most effective strategy. Instead, refining the overall energy metabolism and resource allocation mechanisms offers substantially greater potential for improving growth and productivity. We postulate that these divergent host response strategies are intrinsically linked to the specific metabolic profiles of the inoculated strains. For instance, the superior growth promotion observed in Y37 appears driven by its dual capacity for high auxin biosynthesis and potassium solubilization. This functional repertoire likely synergized to alleviate nutrient limitations, thereby specifically activating the nutrient acquisition and structural reinforcement modules identified in our omics data. Similar growth-promoting effects have been reported in plum (Prunus domestica), where inoculation with specialized PGPR strains significantly augmented shoot diameter, plant height, and chlorophyll content [32]. These parallels suggest that the optimized carbon economy and physiological enhancement observed in our study are consistent with successful PGPR applications in other stone fruits.

Then we characterized the temporal changes in the root, revealing a critical window of reprogramming between 30 and 40 days post-inoculation. The hormonal and transcriptomic profiles showed a highly dynamic host’s response. At 30 d, PGPR treatments induced a dramatic, system-wide perturbation in the root hormonome, characterized by shifts in ABA, auxins, GAs, and JA precursors (Figure 4 and Figure 5). Given the bacterial capacity for hormone synthesis, the measured hormonome likely represents a collective pool of both plant-endogenous and bacterial-derived metabolites. Crucially, however, the significant activation of plant endogenous signaling pathways—such as the upregulation of auxin-responsive hub genes identified in the Y37 treatment—confirms that these metabolites, regardless of origin, function as effective signaling triggers that stimulate a comprehensive host response. This was mirrored at the transcript level, where thousands of genes were differentially expressed compared to the control. The period between 30 and 40 d represented a major transcriptional transition, especially for Y37 and P6, which exhibited nearly 5000 DEGs each (Table S4), indicating a profound shift in cellular strategy. This confirms that the plant–PGPR interaction is not a static event but a phased process of adaptation and reprogramming.

Our data support a biphasic model of Y37-mediated growth promotion: an initial phase of metabolic priming (30 d) followed by a subsequent phase of structural reinforcement (40 d). This temporal partitioning enables efficient resource allocation—first fueling rapid growth through hormone-driven metabolic activation, then consolidating gains through Architectural Remodeling. This began with Phase 1 (early response at 30 d): metabolic priming and growth initiation. Y37 exhibited a lower ABA than CK. Because ABA normally inhibits auxin/CK-driven cell-cycle and growth programs, its decrease enabled a surge in growth-promoting hormones like IAA and active GAs (Figure 4). Concurrently, the transcriptome revealed an upregulation of genes essential for primary growth and resource acquisition, including nutrient transporters (NRT2.1) and core metabolic enzymes (GAPDH and MDH) (Figure 9 and Figure 12). This phase effectively fueled the cellular machinery for rapid proliferation and nutrient uptake. Following this was Phase 2 (late response at 40 d): metabolic reprogramming. By 40 d, Y37 shifted from rapid proliferation to intelligent consolidation. This phase was characterized by a significant upregulation of genes associated with cell wall biosynthesis (e.g., IRX9, IRX10, CESA4, and LAC4-like), alongside genes for brassinosteroid biosynthesis (Figure 8 and Figure 12). This suggests carbon reallocation to strengthen root architecture and passive defense. This structural reinforcement was tightly coupled with a sophisticated reprogramming of hormone metabolism, including the modulation of OPDA, OPC-6/-4 and ABA that is crucial for growth regulation and stress tolerance [33,34]. This hormonal rebalancing by Y37 effectively fine-tunes the trade-off between growth and stress responses. The deliberate construction of a more complex and robust root system serves as a long-term investment, establishing a superior foundation for sustained nutrient and water absorption, thereby supporting the enhanced shoot biomass observed at 60 d. Consistent with this, studies on Prunus RP-20 rootstocks have shown that rhizosphere microorganisms enhance root architecture through hormonal modulation, serving as a biological alternative to exogenous applications [35]. This reinforces the critical role of the ‘Hormonal Switch’—balancing growth promotion and structural reinforcement—within the Prunus genus.

The results for these two phases, therefore, not only address the functional benefits of PGPR application but also elucidate the complex interplay between plant hormones and growth-promoting mechanisms that can be harnessed for sustainable agricultural practices. The clustering analysis of DEGs and metabolites confirms that the Y37 strain specifically induces this favorable metabolic shift, underscoring the dual role of PGPR in promoting long-term growth while simultaneously preparing the plant for potential stressors [16].

Despite the evident growth-promoting benefits observed in this study, the potential ecological and metabolic trade-offs associated with PGPR inoculation must be considered. The introduction of exogenous bacterial strains may trigger competition with the indigenous soil microbiota for resources and ecological niches. Furthermore, maintaining these beneficial interactions may impose a metabolic cost on the host plant, such as the increased secretion of carbon-rich root exudates to sustain the colonized populations. While our results provide a mechanistic foundation for the ‘Hormonal Switch’ in sweet cherry, long-term field studies remain essential to evaluate the ecological stability of these strains and to ensure that the growth gains are not offset by long-term metabolic burdens or disruptions to the native soil ecosystem.

In summary, our multi-omics analysis reveals that strain Y37 promotes growth through a biphasic mechanism: initial metabolic priming followed by structural reinforcement. This coordinated host response effectively optimizes the carbon economy under nutrient-limited conditions.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Plant Material, Bacterial Strains, and Growth Conditions

Seedlings of Gisela 6 (obtained by the Fruit Cultivation and Physiological Ecology Team of the Horticulture College of Shenyang Agricultural University through plant tissue culture technology), a widely utilized rootstock for sweet cherries, was used as the experimental material. The seedlings were pre-cultivated in nutritional pots containing a growth medium composed of garden soil and sand in a 2:1 ratio. The physicochemical properties of the substrate were as follows: 17.42 g·kg−1 organic matter, 127.33 mg·kg−1 alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, 173.42 mg·kg−1 available phosphorus, and 123.75 mg·kg−1 available potassium. For the experiment, uniform seedlings with new shoots of approximately 10 cm in length were selected as the final experimental materials.

The PGPR strains Rahnella Y17, Arthrobacter Y37 and Bacillus megaterium P6 were respectively isolated from the rhizosphere soil of sweet cherry orchards (using Gisela 6 rootstock) in Dalian City, Liaoning Province, China. These strains were deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) under accession numbers CGMCC 34219, 34220, and 34218, respectively. All the three strains exhibit an IAA-producing capacity. Additionally, Y17 also has the ability to fix nitrogen and dissolve phosphorus, Y37 is capable of potassium solubilization, and P6 exhibits both nitrogen-fixation and potassium-solubilization traits. To prepare the inocula, activated strains were cultured in 50 mL of Nutrient Broth liquid medium until reaching the logarithmic growth stage. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C, washed, and resuspended in sterile water to a final concentration of 107 CFU/mL.

These seedlings were randomly divided into four groups: (1) a control group (CK) treated with 50 mL of sterile water; (2) plants inoculated with 50 mL of the Rahnella Y17 strain (Y17); (3) plants inoculated with 50 mL of Arthrobacter Y37 strain (Y37); and (4) plants inoculated with 50 mL of Bacillus megaterium P6 strain (P6). One seedling was cultured per pot. Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates, with five plants per replicate.

Experimental Design and Sampling Strategy:

The experiment was conducted under natural outdoor conditions at the experimental field of Shenyang Agricultural University, Shenyang, China, from June to September. During this period, the plants were exposed to natural irradiance and temperature fluctuations typical of the season. To ensure environmental homogeneity across treatments, all pots were placed in the same open-air area and arranged randomly. Water and nutrients were supplied uniformly to all plants to prevent abiotic stress other than the intended treatments.

Root inoculation was performed via soil drenching six times at 5 d intervals during the first 30 d of the experiment. This multiple-inoculation strategy was employed to facilitate the stable colonization of PGPR strains and help them overcome competition from indigenous soil microbiota. Subsequently, the plants underwent a 30 d growth without further inoculation to evaluate the long-term legacy effects of the bacterial treatment. The experimental seedlings were watered at 2 d intervals during inoculation. The total experiment duration was 60 d, and the root samples were collected at 30, 40, 50 and 60 d post-initial treatment. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were specifically focused on samples from 30 and 40 days post-inoculation to capture the peak period of molecular reprogramming and metabolic signaling that drive growth promotion. This sampling strategy was based on the biological rationale that transcriptional and metabolic changes typically precede observable phenotypic shifts, which were subsequently validated through morphological measurements at 50 and 60 days post-inoculation.

4.2. Phenotypic and Physiological Measurements

At 60 d post-treatment, various growth parameters were measured. Plant height and stem diameter were recorded using a tape measure and electronic vernier caliper, respectively. Aboveground and root tissues were harvested separately to determine their fresh weights. Subsequently, the samples were oven-dried at 80 °C to constant weight to record their dry weight.

Root system architecture (including the total length, surface area, volume, etc.) was captured using a professional root scanning system (Epson, Long Beach, CA, USA) and subsequently analyzed with WinRhizo PRO2016 software (Regent Instruments, Quebec, QC, Canada) for quantitative analysis [36]. Root topological characteristics were classified as either herringbone or dichotomous branching patterns within the branching structure. The external path length (Pe) was defined as the sum of all path links from each external link to the basal link.

Photosynthetic parameters, including the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), stomatal conductance (Gs), and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), were measured using an LI-6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI6800, LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). Measurements were conducted between 9:00 and 10:00 AM on clear days using three fully expanded mature leaves (leaf plastochron index = 7–9) per treatment.

For chlorophyll content determination, 0.5 g of fresh leaf samples was homogenized in 5 mL of 80% acetone supplemented with a small amount of CaCO3 and quartz sand [37]. The mixture was filtered into a 10 mL graduated cylinder, and the volume was adjusted to 10 mL with 80% acetone. Subsequently, a 5 mL aliquot of the extract was diluted to 25 mL with 80% acetone, yielding a final plant-to-solvent ratio of 1:100 (w/v). The absorbance of the resulting extract was measured at 663 and 645 nm using a spectrophotometer (U-5100, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The concentrations of chlorophyll a (Chla) and chlorophyll b (Chlb) were calculated based on the following formulas: Chla = 1.27 × A663 − 0.269 × A645; Chlb = (2.29 × A645 − 0.468 × A663). The total chlorophyll content (Chla + b) was calculated as the sum of Chla and Chlb.

4.3. Root Vitality and Respiration Assays

Root vitality was measured at 30, 40, 50, and 60 d post-treatment using the triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) reduction method [38]. Briefly, 0.5 g of fresh root samples was weighed and placed in a test tube containing 5 mL of 0.4% (w/v) TTC solution and 5 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, after which the reaction was terminated by adding 2 mL of 1 mol·L−1 H2SO4 solution. The roots were removed, gently blotted dry, and returned to the original test tube with 10 mL of 95% ethanol solution for overnight extraction. Once the solution turned red, absorbance measurements were taken at 485 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Root respiration rates were measured using the OxyTrace+ liquid-phase oxygen electrode automatic measurement system (Oxytherm + R, Hansatech, King’s Lynn, UK). The contribution of different metabolic pathways to total respiration, namely glycolysis (EMP), the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), was determined by sequentially adding sodium fluoride (0.5 mol·L−1), malonic acid (0.5 mol·L−1), and sodium phosphate (0.5 mol·L−1) to the reaction chamber, respectively [39].

For the measurement, 0.05 g of fresh young cherry roots was immersed in 2 mL of phosphate buffer within the oxygen electrode reaction chamber. Once the slope stabilized in the OxyTrace+ software (Version: v1.0.48), the slope was recorded and used to calculate the total root respiration rate (Rtotal) according to the formula Rtotal = −20.001 slope/root fresh weight, based on the rate of decrease in oxygen concentration.

Subsequently, 50 μL of sodium fluoride (0.5 mol·L−1) was added to the chamber, and after slope stabilization, the new value was recorded and applied to the total respiration rate formula. The glycolytic pathway respiration rate was calculated as REMP = Rtotal − RNaF. The TCA cycle respiration rate was determined by RTCA = Rtotal − REMP − Rmalonic acid, and the pentose phosphate pathway respiration rate was calculated as RPPP = Rtotal − REMP − RTCA − RNa3PO4.

4.4. Targeted Metabolome (Phytohormone) Analysis (30 and 40 d)

Root samples from the CK, Y17, Y37, and P6 groups at 30 and 40 d post-treatment (with three biological replicates per group/time point) were collected. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

A total of 109 hormone-related metabolites were quantified. Sample extraction and analysis were performed using a UPLC-MS/MS system (QTRAP 6500+, SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Metabolites were identified and quantified based on authentic standards.

All metabolomic data analyses were performed using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 web platform [40]. Raw data matrices were first quality-filtered by removing metabolites absent (zero or below detection) in ≥80% of all samples, thereby excluding poorly detected or unstable features. The remaining missing values were imputed using the default LoD method (e.g., replaced by 1/5 of the minimum positive value found in that group for the respective metabolite). Subsequently, these data were normalized by the sum (total signal) per sample to correct for global loading/ionization differences, log2-transformed to stabilize variance and approximate symmetry, and underwent Pareto scaling (mean-centered and divided by the square root of the standard deviation of each variable) to balance medium and high abundance hormone signals without over-amplifying noise.

For multivariate statistical analysis, unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was initially conducted to visualize the natural clustering among sample groups and to identify potential outliers. Following this, supervised method Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was employed to maximize inter-group separation and to identify variables contributing significantly to this separation, according to previous method. The robustness and validity of the models were assessed using permutation tests. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores derived from the PLS-DA models were used to rank the contribution of each metabolite to group discrimination.

Identification and visualization of differential metabolites were based on a combination of multivariate and univariate results. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using a threshold of |log2FC| > 0.58 (1.5-fold change), p-value < 0.05 and VIP score > 1.0.

4.5. Transcriptome Sequencing and Analysis

The same root samples used for targeted phytohormone analysis were subjected to transcriptomics analysis. Total RNA was extracted from plant roots using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), while RNA integrity was evaluated using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were generated using the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), with unique indices incorporated into each sample’s sequences for identification. Sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced on an NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw sequencing data were further processed by Sangon Biotech. Raw RNA-seq reads were processed for quality control and alignment using FastQC (v0.11.2) [41] for read quality assessment, and Trimmomatic (0.36) for adapter trimming and filtering [42]. After filtering raw reads, clean reads were mapped to the sweet cherry reference genome (Prunus avium PAV_r1.0, GCF_002207925.1) [43] using HISAT2 (v2.2.0) [44]. Gene-level quantification was conducted using StringTie (v1.3.3b) [45].

We first performed a principal component analysis (PCA) of all samples based on these FPKM using the Metware Cloud, a free online platform for data analysis (https://cloud.metware.cn, accessed on 18 September 2025). The sample Y17-1-1 was identified as a statistical outlier due to its lack of clustering with the two others Y17 replicates in the 30 d dataset. To minimize noise and improve the reliability, the sample Y17-1-1 was excluded prior to DESeqDataSet (Figures S1 and S2).

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.22.1) [46] in R (v3.5.1) with the criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and p-adj < 0.05 on the Metware Cloud. For comparisons between treatments, the CK group at the corresponding timepoint was used as the reference. For temporal comparisons, the 30 d group was set as the reference level.

4.6. Multi-Omics Integration and Bioinformatic Analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and DAMs were combined for an integrated analysis [47]. Pairwise correlation analysis and K-means clustering between gene expression and metabolite abundance were conducted using the Metware Cloud. Pairwise gene–metabolite correlations were calculated using the Pearson correction method on log2-transformed expression/abundance matrices within each comparison. The resulting p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR) procedure. Significant association pairs were retained at |r| > 0.8 and FDR < 0.05.

Functional annotation of the reference genome’s protein sequences was performed using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.13 against eggNOG v5.0 to obtain GO and KEGG annotations [48,49]. Enrichment analysis of each of the K-means cluster genes was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.14.6) [50,51] against a custom GO and KEGG annotation database. Significantly enriched terms were identified using an adjusted p-value threshold of <0.05.

Hub genes were identified using a multi-step filtering strategy: (1) functional enrichment in GO/KEGG terms; (2) stringent expression thresholds with |log2FC| ≥ 2 for Cluster 1 and |log2FC| ≥ 1.58 for Clusters 2/4; and (3) strong gene–metabolite correlations with |r| ≥ 0.8 and FDR < 0.05. Gene–metabolite interaction networks (cnet plots) were visualized for the filtered sets using the Metware Cloud, with representative hubs selected based on connectivity and biological relevance.

4.7. qRT-PCR Validation of the Key Hub Genes

The six candidate genes involved in the functions of Architectural Remodeling and Hormonal Switch, identified from transcriptome data, were validated by qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from plant roots using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a Takara reverse transcription kit (DRR037A) (Takara, Dalian, China). The qRT-PCR assays were conducted on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system using the SYBR Green method in a 10 μL reaction volume with three biological replicates. Specific primers for the target genes are provided in Supplementary Table S9. The elongation factor 1-delta 2-like gene (EF1-delta, LOC110763208) was selected as the internal reference gene for data normalization due to its high stability across different treatments and time points in our preliminary evaluation. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt [52].

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that the Y37 strain of PGPR significantly enhances cherry seedling growth and resilience through a multifaceted approach involving hormonal reprogramming and structural modifications. The strategic allocation of metabolic resources across different growth stages underscores the potential of Y37 as a biological regulator, promoting both growth and defense mechanisms. This dual action is particularly relevant in the context of sustainable agriculture, as it reduces reliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides while enhancing plant health and productivity. The results not only address the functional benefits of PGPR application but also elucidate the complex interplay between plant hormones and growth-promoting mechanisms. This deeper understanding of the PGPR–plant metabolism paves the way for the development of effective biocontrol strategies and enhances crop resilience in agricultural systems.

However, some limitations must be noted. This study was conducted under controlled pot conditions; thus, the long-term efficacy and stability of these strains in complex, heterogeneous orchard environments remain to be verified through multi-year field trials. Furthermore, while hub genes were identified, the inherent difficulty of genetic transformation in P. avium poses a challenge for direct functional validation, necessitating alternative approaches like transient expression or heterologous expression in future studies.

Additionally, regarding the experimental design, while our multi-omics approach successfully captured a critical window of early host–microbe interaction (30–40 d post-inoculation), this design inherently limits our perspective on the initiation and long-term consequences of these molecular events. The mechanisms driving the initial establishment of the PGPR–plant dialog prior to 30 d, as well as the persistence of the observed transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming beyond 40 d, remain open questions. Future studies incorporating earlier time points (e.g., earlier infection stages) and extended monitoring (e.g., 60 d) are essential to construct a complete temporal model of this dynamic interaction.

Finally, the precise population dynamics and colonization efficiency of the three PGPR strains were not directly quantified via bacterial counting or qPCR in this study. However, the persistent and strain-specific improvements in root architecture, coupled with the profound transcriptomic reprogramming observed, provide compelling indirect evidence of the successful establishment and differential functional activity of the inoculated strains in the rhizosphere. Future research incorporating molecular tracking of these specific bacterial populations will be essential to further correlate colonization density with the magnitude of growth-promoting efficacy.
