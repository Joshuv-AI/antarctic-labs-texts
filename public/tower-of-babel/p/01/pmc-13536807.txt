Malignant epithelial states drive immune dysfunction in ampulla of Vater carcinoma

AbstractBackgroundAmpulla of Vater (AoV) carcinoma is a rare malignancy arising at the junction of intestinal and pancreatobiliary epithelium. Its heterogeneous clinical behavior and histological diversity have hindered therapeutic advances, and the cellular basis of this heterogeneity remains unclear. We aimed to construct a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of AoV carcinoma, with a focus on identifying epithelial subtypes and their interactions with the tumor microenvironment (TME).MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing on eight primary AoV tumors and four matched normal tissues. Comprehensive clustering and transcriptomic analyses identified cell-type composition, epithelial heterogeneity, and tumor-immune interactions. Findings were validated using deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data from 62 AoV carcinoma patients.Results Malignant epithelial cells were categorized into four distinct subtypes: Int-Wnt, PB-KRAS, Int-Hypoxia, and Cycling stage. Background

Ampulla of Vater (AoV) carcinoma is a malignancy arising from the ampulla of Vater, the anatomical convergence of the common bile duct, pancreatic duct, and duodenum. This unique anatomical location gives rise to both histological and molecular heterogeneity, reflecting contributions from intestinal and pancreatobiliary lineages. AoV carcinoma is often categorized into intestinal and pancreatobiliary subtypes, which exhibit distinct prognostic and therapeutic characteristics [1, 2]. However, this binary classification does not fully account for the molecular diversity and variable clinical behavior observed among patients [3].

Previous genomic studies have provided important insights into the molecular landscape of AoV carcinoma. Notably, Yachida et al. [4] identified frequent mutations in the ELF3 gene, implicating it as a potential driver of tumorigenesis. Similarly, Gingras et al. [5] reported recurrent ELF3 inactivation and widespread dysregulation of the WNT signaling pathway. While these studies advanced our understanding of AoV carcinogenesis, they were based on whole-exome sequencing and bulk transcriptome profiling, which lack the resolution to capture cell type-specific transcriptional programs and the complexity of tumor-microenvironment interactions. As a result, the biological basis of epithelial heterogeneity and the functional contributions of non-malignant cell populations remain poorly understood.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers a powerful approach to address these limitations by enabling high-resolution, cell-type-resolved transcriptomic profiling [6]. This technology facilitates the identification of cellular subpopulations, and intercellular communication networks that are not accessible through bulk sequencing methods. Despite its potential, scRNA-seq has not yet been applied to AoV carcinoma, leaving fundamental gaps in our understanding of its cellular and transcriptional architecture. Therefore, our study is essential to move beyond bulk-level genomic insights and establish a high-resolution, cell-type-resolved atlas of AoV carcinoma.

In this study, we present the first single-cell transcriptomic analysis of AoV carcinoma. Using scRNA-seq on primary tumor specimens, we identify distinct epithelial subtypes characterized by unique oncogenic and reveal their associations with the immune landscape and clinical outcomes. This work establishes a foundational single-cell atlas of AoV carcinoma and provides new insights into its cellular diversity with potential translational implications.

Methods

Study patients

This study included 70 patients histologically diagnosed with AoV adenocarcinoma between September 2005 and March 2023 at Samsung Medical Center (SMC), of whom eight underwent single-cell RNA sequencing and 62 underwent bulk RNA sequencing. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, tumor stage according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition, tumor differentiation, adjuvant chemotherapy administration, and survival data, were retrospectively collected. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at SMC (IRB No. SMC-2022–06-076) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent for the future research use of their pathology specimens.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Fresh tumor and adjacent normal tissue specimens were obtained from patients with AoV carcinoma before treatment through Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for scRNA sequencing. Acquired tissues are collected in MACS® Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec., Auburn, CA, USA) on ice and processed to the single cell dissociation immediately. Biopsy tissues are placed on a petri dish with a small volume of enzyme mixture solution (Tumor Dissociation Kit, Miltenyi Biotec.) and minced into small pieces under 1–2 mm with surgical blades. Minced pieces are transferred to gentleMACSTM C-tube containing enzyme mixture solution. By choosing an appropriate program of gentleMACSTM Dissociator, tissue pieces are further dissociated three times with two interval incubations for 30 minutes at 37 °C under continuous rotation using the MACSmixTM Tube Rotator. Resuspended samples are applied to a cell strainer (70 μm) and washed with 10 ml of DMEM/F12 media with 1% FBS. After a centrifugation at 400xg for 5 minutes, cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of media and counted with trypan blue staining or acridine orange/propidium iodide staining. Optionally, erythrocytes are removed by red blood cell lysis solution for 10 min before counting. We initiated the cell preparation process by employing the LUNA-FL™ Automated Fluorescence Cell Counter (logos biosystems). Detailed guidelines for cell preparation were sourced from the 10x Genomics Single Cell Protocols Cell Preparation Guide and the Guidelines for Optimal Sample Preparation flowchart. Libraries were meticulously constructed using the Chromium controller in accordance with the 10x Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ v3.1 protocol (10x Genomics). In brief, cell suspensions were diluted in nuclease-free water, aiming to achieve a targeted cell count of 10,000. The diluted cell suspension was mixed with a master mix and subsequently loaded onto a Chromium Next GEM chip, along with Single Cell 3’ V3.1 gel beads and partitioning oil. Within the droplets formed in the chip, RNA transcripts from individual cells underwent unique barcoding and reverse transcription. Pooled cDNA molecules then underwent sequential processes, including end repair, addition of a single ‘A’ base, and adapter ligation. Subsequently, the resulting products were subjected to purification and enrichment through polymerase chain reaction (PCR), ultimately yielding the final cDNA library. To ensure library quality and quantify the purified libraries, we adhered to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA). Furthermore, library qualification was performed using the Agilent Technologies 4200 TapeStation from Agilent Technologies. The sequenced libraries were processed using the HiSeqX platform from Illumina to obtain the requisite data for our study.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Raw sequencing data were processed using CellRanger (v7.0.1; 10x Genomics) [7], with alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome via STAR. CellBender (v 0.2.2) [8] was applied to each sample to eliminate background RNA contamination. Doublets were detected using Scrublet (v 0.2.3) [9], with thresholds determined according to simulation-derived scores. we excluded low-quality cells with fewer than 500 detected genes and a mitochondrial gene proportion exceeding 20%. Seurat (v4.3.0) [10] was used for downstream processing, including normalization, variable gene selection, scaling (regressing out total UMI counts, mitochondrial gene proportion, and cell cycle scores), principal component analysis, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) projection. Clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, with dimensionality assessed via JackStraw and ElbowPlot methods. Cell type annotation was conducted using SingleR (v2.0.0) [11] and further validated through the expression of canonical lineage markers. Major cell types were identified using EPCAM (epithelial cells), COL1A1 (fibroblasts), VWF (endothelial cells), CD3D (T cells), CD14 (myeloid cells), and CD79A (B cells). For more granular annotation of immune and stromal subsets, we utilized the following marker sets: CD4 for helper T cells, CD8 for cytotoxic T cells, FOXP3 for regulatory T cells (Tregs), and NCAM1 and NCR1 for NK cells. Myeloid sub-populations were defined by C1QC (macrophages), LYZ and S100A9 (monocytes), CD1C (dendritic cells), and CPA3 and KIT (mast cells). B cell lineages were further distinguished using CD79A and MS4A1 (B cells), CD38 and CD27 (plasma cells). Subclustering analyses were performed on major immune and stromal compartments following additional doublet removal [12].

Identification of epithelial subtypes using cNMF

To identify transcriptional programs within epithelial cells, we applied consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF) (v0.2) [13] to the normalized gene expression matrix. The number of components (k) was varied from 2 to 15, and the optimal solution was selected based on silhouette scores and Frobenius reconstruction error.

Copy number variation inference

To infer copy number alterations at the single-cell level, CopyKAT (v1.1.0) [14] was applied with default parameters. T cells were used as a reference for normal diploid profiles. CNV scores were computed for individual epithelial cells and summarized across samples to evaluate subtype-specific genomic instability.

Transcription factor regulatory network inference

Regulatory network analysis was performed using the pySCENIC (v0.12.1) [15] pipeline. Regulon activity scores were computed from raw UMI matrices to identify transcription factor-target gene modules, and average activity scores were calculated per subtype for downstream comparative analyses.

Modular score and stemness estimation

The CytoTRACE algorithm (v0.3.3) [16] was used to infer the differentiation potential of malignant epithelial cells. CytoTRACE computes transcriptional diversity and expression correlation to derive stemness scores, ranging from 0 (fully differentiated) to 1 (highly undifferentiated). This approach enables the identification of less differentiated, stem-like cell states at single-cell resolution. To evaluate functional characteristics of epithelial subtypes, including stemness potential, we calculated gene module scores using AddModuleScore function in the Seurat package. This approach determines the average expression of a specific gene program at the single-cell level, which is then normalized by subtracting the aggregated expression of control feature sets. To ensure a robust baseline, all analyzed features are binned based on their average expression levels, and control features are randomly selected from each bin. This process effectively accounts for technical noise and the influence of overall gene expression profiles. The stemness gene set was constructed using previously reported stemness-associated markers [17].

Functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes were selected based on adjusted p-values and fold changes. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using EnrichR (v3.2) [18], employing the hypergeometric test with default parameters to identify overrepresented pathways and biological processes. We employed the hypergeometric test with default parameters to identify overrepresented pathways and biological processes from the MSigDB Hallmark gene set collection.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Ligand-receptor interaction analysis was conducted using CellPhoneDB (v5.0.0) [19] to identify potential intercellular communication events between cell types. Significant interactions were inferred via permutation testing using default parameters.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

To infer the developmental hierarchy of epithelial subtypes, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis using the Monocle2 R package (v2.30.0) [20]. To minimize potential biological variations across patients, we utilized the integrated data generated via the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions from the Seurat package. For trajectory ordering, we selected the top 30 differentially expressed genes from each epithelial cluster. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree algorithm.

Bulk RNA sequencing and processing

For bulk RNA sequencing, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues from an independent cohort of surgically resected 62 AoV carcinoma patients were analyzed. Transcript-level quantification was achieved using RSEM (v1.3.0) [21], aligned to the GRCh38 genome with GENCODE v33 annotation. Gene-level expression matrices were derived for integrative analyses with single-cell data. Proportions of each subtype were estimated in bulk RNA-seq samples using MuSiC (v1.0.0) [22].

Xenium in situ hybridization and spatial analysis

Surgically resected FFPE tissue samples from AoV tumor patients were profiled using Xenium Prime 5K Human Pan Tissue and Pathways Panel (10x Genomics). FFPE tissue sections (5 µm) were prepared on Xenium slides using Xenium Prime Sample Preparation Reagents. RNA quality was assessed by calculating the percentage of RNA fragments > 200 nucleotides (DV200) extracted from tissue sections, and H&E staining were also used to assess tissue morphology and region of interests by a specialized pathologist before performing the Xenium in situ hybridization. Slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated and decrosslinked, followed by hybridization with Xenium 5K Priming Oligos and probes targeting messenger RNA. Probes were ligated, amplified and washed, with subsequent antibody staining for cell segmentation. Autofluorescence quenching and nuclei staining were performed and slides they were stored in phosphate-buffered saline with tween-20 (PBS-T) at 4 °C. The Xenium Analyzer (version 3.1) was used for imaging, decoding, segmentation and cell assignment, following the manufacturer’s protocols.

During the initial quality control and filtering step, cells with a transcript count below 100 were classified as low-quality and excluded from all downstream analyses. For cell type classification, we utilized our own scRNA-seq dataset as the reference. Cell assignment was performed using the FindTransferAnchors and TransferData functions within the Seurat package, employing default parameters. Cells with a prediction score below 0.5 were classified as unknown and excluded from further downstream analyses.

Survival analysis

The clinical relevance of epithelial subtypes was evaluated using survival analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the survival and survminer R packages (v0.5.2). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were implemented using the coxph function to assess associations with overall survival and disease-free survival.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R. Pair-wise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were utilized to analyze the proportional differences between groups within each cell type/subset. Significance is displayed as *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; and ***, p < 0.001. For comparisons among multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to determine global significance, followed by Dunn’s test for pairwise post-hoc comparisons with adjustments for multiple testing. Statistical significance for multiple comparisons is presented as adjusted p-values (Padj), indicated as follows: *, Padj < 0.05;, Padj < 0.01; and ***, Padj < 0.001. Pearson correlation was employed to calculate the correlation between the proportions of each pair of cell subsets, and Spearman correlation was used for the co-ecosystem analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included 70 patients (scRNA-seq: n = 8 and bulk RNA-seq: n = 62) with AoV carcinoma. Median age was 61.5 years (range, 36–83 years) and 33 were male (47.1%). Tumor stage (AJCC 8th edition) was I-III in the majority of patients (67/70, 95.7%), with only one patient classified as stage 0 and two as stage IV. During a median follow-up of 9.7 years (interquartile range, 6.4–12.7 years), 27 deaths (38.6%) occurred (Table S1).

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals diverse cellular composition in AoV carcinoma

To investigate the cellular architecture of AoV carcinoma, we performed scRNA-seq on tumors (n = 8) and matched adjacent normal tissues (n = 4) (Fig. 1a). After quality filtering, a total of 34,672 cells were analyzed and grouped into 18 clusters, which were subsequently classified into six major cell types based on cell type marker gene expression (Fig. 1b–c). UMAP visualization revealed that while immune cells were broadly shared across patients, epithelial cell clusters displayed strong patient-specific transcriptional profiles, consistent with the absence of batch effects and reflecting inter-patient variation driven by tumor-specific copy number alterations [23, 24] (Fig. 1d). We further examined the distribution of cell types across individual samples, revealing substantial inter-patient heterogeneity in cellular composition (Fig. 1e). Some tumors were highly enriched in epithelial or myeloid populations, whereas others showed greater T cell infiltration. These findings highlight the cellular complexity of AoV carcinoma and underscore the diversity of tumor-immune microenvironments across patients.

Epithelial subtyping reveals distinct transcriptional programs and inter-patient heterogeneity

To investigate epithelial heterogeneity in AoV carcinoma, we performed subclustering of epithelial cells. This analysis revealed strong patient-specific clustering patterns (Fig. 2a), indicating pronounced transcriptional differences between tumors. We then applied consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF) to the epithelial transcriptomes, identifying four robust molecular subtypes (NMF-1 to NMF-4) (Fig. S1a-b). Although each subtype included epithelial cells from multiple patients, their relative proportions varied markedly across samples (Fig. 2b). This suggests that, while certain transcriptional programs are shared among tumors, their dominance is highly patient-specific, reflecting substantial inter-patient heterogeneity.

(b) Proportional composition of four transcriptional subtypes (NMF-1 to NMF-4) in each AoV tumor, identified by consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF) clustering of epithelial transcriptomes. (c) Violin plots showing expression levels of well-known marker genes associated with AoV subtypes: CDX2 and KRT20 (intestinal subtype), MUC1 and KRT7 (pancreatobiliary subtype). (d) Functional enrichment analysis of each transcriptional subtype, visualized as a bubble plot. The pathways were identified using the MSigDB hallmark gene set collection. We further examined the expression of established AoV subtype marker genes across the four subtypes (Fig. 2c). Intestinal markers (CDX2 and KRT20) were strongly expressed in NMF-1 and NMF-3, whereas pancreatobiliary markers (KRT7 and MUC1) were predominantly enriched in NMF-2. These findings support the biological relevance of the identified subtypes and their alignment with previously described histological features [2, 25]. Functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct transcriptional programs characterizing each subtype (Fig. 2d and Table S2): NMF-1 was enriched for Wnt/β-catenin signaling (designated Int-Wnt), NMF-2 for KRAS signaling (PB-KRAS), NMF-3 for hypoxia-related pathways (Int-Hypoxia), and NMF-4 for cell cycle-associated signatures (Cycling). We categorize these four programs as three lineage-driven, differentiated subtypes and one progenitor/functional subtype (NMF-4, Cycling).

To further characterize the regulatory landscape underlying epithelial heterogeneity, we performed regulon activity analysis across epithelial cells. This analysis revealed that multiple transcription factor regulons were differentially activated in a subtype-specific manner (Fig. S1c). MSX1 activity was predominantly elevated in the Int-Wnt subtype compared to the PB-KRAS and Cycling subtypes. HNF1B activity reached its highest levels in the PB-KRAS subtype, showing a significant increase over the Cycling subtype and a strong upward trend compared to the Int-Wnt and Int-Hypoxia subtypes. SIRT6 activity was notably enriched in the Int-Hypoxia subtype, while CDX1 was most active in the Cycling subtype, significantly surpassing the levels in the Int-Wnt and Int-Hypoxia groups (Fig. S1d). The MSX1 is a known WNT/β-catenin responsive transcription factor implicated in promoting intestinal tumorigenesis [26]. Similarly, the enrichment of HNF1B in the PB-KRAS subtype is relevant, as its overexpression has been shown to enhance the expression of liver progenitor cell markers and stemness in liver cancer cells [27]. Finally, the activation of the SIRT6 regulon in the Int-Hypoxia subtype is consistent with its known role in enhancing the stability of HIF-1α, a process linked to epithelial-mesenchymal transition under hypoxic conditions [28]. These regulon profiles were concordant with the transcriptional programs defined by cNMF, further supporting the molecular distinction among AoV epithelial subtypes.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that epithelial cells in AoV carcinoma exhibit diverse transcriptional states driven by lineage-specific and oncogenic programs, with subtype composition varying substantially between tumors.

PB-KRAS subtype exhibit distinct genomic instability and stem-like properties

To interrogate the degree of genomic instability underlying each epithelial subtype, we first analyzed copy number variation (CNV) profiles inferred from single-cell transcriptomes. Notably, CNV patterns were strongly patient-specific, indicating inter-patient variability in genomic alterations (Fig. 3a). At the single-cell level, CNV scores were markedly elevated in tumor epithelial cells compared to non-epithelial controls, with the PB-KRAS subtype exhibiting the highest levels of genomic aberrations (Fig. S2a). To validate these findings at the sample level, we averaged CNV scores across epithelial cells within each sample and compared them across subtypes. This analysis confirmed that the PB-KRAS subtype exhibits the highest CNV burden among all epithelial subtypes (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.00013; Fig. 3b). These results indicate that genomic instability is unevenly distributed among AoV epithelial subtypes and is particularly pronounced in PB-KRAS subtype.

Given that high genomic instability is frequently associated with aggressive phenotypes and enhanced self-renewal capacity [29], we next investigated the functional and developmental hierarchy of these subtypes. Based on its highest CNV burden, we specifically focused on evaluating the stem-like properties of the PB-KRAS subtype. We first focused on the Cycling subtype. Given its cell cycle-associated transcriptional program and the enrichment of progenitor-associated regulons, such as CDX1 (Fig. S1d), we hypothesized that it represents a less differentiated, progenitor-like state. To infer the developmental potential across all subtypes, we employed CytoTRACE [16], a computational tool that infers developmental potential using transcriptional entropy. As expected, the Cycling subtype exhibited high CytoTRACE scores (Fig. 3c and Fig. S2b), supporting its identity as a less differentiated, progenitor-like state. Surprisingly, the PB-KRAS subtype, which otherwise presents as a differentiated lineage, exhibited the highest CytoTRACE scores (Fig. 3c and Fig. S2b), suggesting unexpected stem-like features. This combination—a seemingly differentiated state with high developmental potential—defines a non-canonical stemness phenotype. This suggests that, unlike the canonical progenitor-like Cycling subtype, the PB-KRAS subtype represents a distinct, highly aggressive stem-like epithelial state that deviates from the typical differentiation pathway. To further examine this possibility, we calculated stemness scores based on a curated stem cell gene signature [17]. The PB-KRAS subtype exhibited elevated stemness scores that were comparable to those of Cycling cells, while remaining notably higher than the Int-Wnt and Int-Hypoxia subtypes (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.00043; Fig. 3d and Fig. S2c).

To provide dynamic evidence of this developmental hierarchy, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle2 [20]. The resulting trajectory delineated a clear lineage progression originating from the Cycling subtype, passing through the Int-Wnt and Int-Hypoxia subtype, and ultimately culminating in the PB-KRAS subtype at the terminal end (Fig. 3e). Notably, despite its terminal position on the pseudotime axis—which typically denotes a fully differentiated state—the PB-KRAS subtype harbored exceptionally high developmental potential, consistent with its elevated CytoTRACE (Fig. 3f) and stemness scores (Fig. 3g). This paradoxical terminal stemness suggests that PB-KRAS cells do not merely follow a conventional differentiation path but may undergo a coordinated transcriptional reprogramming or de-differentiation process, re-acquiring progenitor-like properties at an advanced stage of lineage development.

We next sought to determine the potential molecular drivers of this phenotype, focusing on KRAS, the defining feature of the PB-KRAS subtype. Expression of KRAS was significantly upregulated in PB-KRAS subtype compared to the Int-Wnt subtypes (Fig. 3h and Fig. S2d), whereas CNV at the KRAS locus showed no significant difference among the subtypes (Fig. 3i). Consistent with this, correlation analysis at the sample level revealed a moderate positive correlation between CNV score and stemness score (r = 0.5, p = 0.014, Fig. S2e). Furthermore, the expression of KRAS showed a strong positive correlation with both stemness score (r = 0.6, p = 0.002, Fig. S2f) and CNV score (r = 0.59, p = 0.002, Fig. S2g). These findings collectively highlight the robust association of both genomic instability and KRAS expression with the enhanced stem-like properties of the PB-KRAS subtype. Since KRAS knockdown in pancreatic cancer cells is known to reduce stemness characteristics [30], these observations suggest that the transcriptional upregulation of KRAS, rather than genomic alterations, is the likely contributor to the stemness signature of the PB-KRAS subtype.

Epithelial subtypes confer distinct prognostic significance and clinical relevance in AoV carcinoma

To evaluate the clinical significance of the four epithelial subtypes (Int-Wnt, PB-KRAS, Int-Hypoxia, and Cycling), we first deconvoluted their proportions in bulk RNA-seq profiles from 62 AoV carcinoma samples using the single-cell-derived subtype signatures. Among these, the Int-Wnt subtype was the most abundant, followed by Int-Hypoxia and PB-KRAS, whereas the Cycling subtype was relatively rare across samples (Fig. 4a and Table S3).

We then assessed whether subtype proportions were associated with clinical variables. PB-KRAS subtype proportions were significantly elevated in patients who experienced recurrence (p = 0.011; Fig. S3a), whereas no significant differences were observed across genders, adjuvant chemotherapy or tumor stages (Fig. S3b-d). Regarding tumor stage, the Int-Hypoxia subtype was found to be more prevalent in Stage I tumors compared to Stage II (Padj = 0.045; Fig. S3d).

Next, we examined the prognostic impact of each subtype on OS. Proportions of Int-Wnt subtype was positively correlated with OS (r = 0.4, p = 0.001) (Fig. 4b), while PB-KRAS subtype abundance was negatively correlated with survival (r = −0.34, p = 0.007) (Fig. 4c). Int-Hypoxia subtype showed a weaker and non-significant inverse correlation (r = −0.21, p = 0.097) (Fig. 4d). Kaplan-Meier analyses further demonstrated that high PB-KRAS subtype content was significantly associated with worse survival (p = 0.001), while high Int-Wnt subtype content showed a trend toward improved survival (p = 0.013).

To determine whether AoV subtypes independently contribute to prognosis, we performed multivariate Cox regression analyses incorporating clinical covariates. Notably, PB-KRAS subtype was significantly associated with worse OS (hazard ratio; HR = 116.6, 95% confidence interval; CI = 5.4–2496.6, p = 0.002) and DFS (HR = 101.1, 95% CI = 3.3–3132.4, p = 0.008), independent of other factors (Fig. 4e–f), while Int-Wnt subtype was significantly associated with improved prognosis (DFS: HR = 0.1, 95% CI = 0.004–0.7, p = 0.028) (Fig. 4f). Consistently, univariate Cox regression analyses supported the prognostic significance of PB-KRAS subtype for both OS and DFS (Fig. S3e-f).

Together, these findings highlight that the composition of epithelial subtypes, particularly PB-KRAS and Int-Wnt subtype, has significant prognostic and clinical relevance in AoV carcinoma. The PB-KRAS subtype is associated with a most distinctly aggressive phenotype and a higher likelihood of recurrence.

We next examined the relationship between epithelial and immune subtypes. At the single-cell level, the proportion of Tc-GZMK cells was strongly correlated with the abundance of PB-KRAS subtype within each tumor (r = 0.98, p < 0.001; Fig. 5c). These Tc-GZMK cells represent a pre-dysfunctional state of CD8+ T cells [31], characterized by the robust expression of effector molecules such as GZMK and the absence of terminal exhaustion markers like HAVCR2, while exhibiting moderate levels of early activation markers such as LAG3 (Fig. S4a). This association was further validated in bulk RNA-seq data, where higher PB-KRAS subtype proportions were positively correlated with Tc-GZMK proportions across patients (r = 0.26, p = 0.037; Fig. 5d). These results suggest a potential interaction between PB-KRAS subtype and Tc-GZMK cells. We also examined whether their abundance was associated with clinical outcomes. Patients with high Tc-GZMK cell proportions exhibited significantly worse OS compared to those with lower levels (Fig. 5e).

We next explored potential epithelial-immune crosstalk using ligand-receptor interaction analysis. PB-KRAS subtype exhibited enriched interactions with Tc-GZMK cells through multiple ligand-receptor axes, including TGFB2, SPP1, and FGF2, which are well-known mediators of immunosuppressive signaling (Fig. S4b). These factors are associated with T cell exhaustion, suppression of cytotoxic responses, and immune evasion, suggesting that PB-KRAS subtype may contribute to shaping a dysfunctional yet Tc-GZMK-rich immune microenvironment.

Spatial profiling reveals an immunosuppressive microenvironment in PB-KRAS subtype regions

To investigate the spatial relationships between cellular subtypes and the tumor microenvironment, we performed Xenium in situ analysis on two representative AoV carcinoma samples, categorized as intestinal type and pancreatobiliary type based on the consensus judgment of experienced pathologists examining the tumor sections (Fig. 5f). We first used single-cell RNA sequencing to estimate the major cell types and then mapped their spatial distribution across the tissue (Fig. 5g). To investigate subtype-specific spatial patterns, we next identified and distinguished specific regions within the tumors, focusing on the AoV-ST1 sample, which are characterized by high proportions of Int-Hypoxia, and the AoV-ST2 sample, which are characterized by high proportions of the PB-KRAS subtype (Fig. 5h). Importantly, the classification of regions derived from scRNA-seq mapping into Int-Hypoxiahigh (AoV-ST1) and PB-KRAShigh (AoV-ST2) categories showed strong agreement with the initial pathological classification. Visualization of the spatial maps revealed a striking co-localization between the PB-KRAS subtype and Tc-GZMK cells (indicated by triangle-shaped dots), with a denser infiltration observed in the PB-KRAShigh sample (Fig. 5h). Quantitative analysis confirmed this observation: the PB-KRAShigh sample exhibited a markedly higher proportion of PB-KRAS cells (among all epithelial cells) and a corresponding elevation in the proportion of Tc-GZMK cells (among all T cells) compared to the Int-Hypoxiahigh sample (Fig. 5i). To further validate the spatial association between these populations, we performed a nearest neighbor distance analysis using the Xenium spatial coordinates. Our results revealed that Tc-GZMK cells were located significantly closer to PB-KRAS cells, with a mean minimum distance of 114.3 µm, whereas the distance to Int-hypoxia cells was nearly three-fold higher at 321.4 µm (Fig. 5j).

To visually confirm the presence of the Tc-GZMK population, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence using MACSima Imaging System (n = 3 per each group; see supplementary methods). Although this population was relatively sparse, pancreatobiliary subtype enriched samples showed higher infiltration of Tc-GZMK cells, whereas intestinal subtype dominant tumors displayed sparse Tc-GZMK infiltration (Fig. S5a-b). Quantification confirmed that the proportion of Tc-GZMK cells was significantly higher in Pancreatobiliary subtype compared to intestinal subtype tumors (p = 0.0375; Fig. S5c). These initial findings collectively suggest that the PB-KRAS subtype is associated with a distinctive and highly infiltrated tumor microenvironment.

PB-KRAS subtype associates with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment

To gain deeper insights into the immune microenvironment of AoV carcinoma, we conducted detailed subclustering analyses specifically focusing on myeloid and B lineage cells. This allowed us to resolve cellular heterogeneity within these immune compartments. Myeloid cells were classified into distinct subpopulations including macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), and mast cells based on canonical lineage markers and differentially expressed genes (Fig. 6a), with neutrophils excluded from this analysis because they were predominantly derived from a single sample (Fig. S6a-b). Similarly, B lineage cells were divided into naive B cells and multiple subsets of plasma cells, each characterized by unique transcriptomic signatures (Fig. 6b). These refined annotations enabled a more precise characterization of cell-type-specific contributions to the tumor microenvironment.

(a-b) UMAP plots showing clustering and annotation of myeloid and mast cell subsets (a) and B/plasma cell (b) subsets based on marker gene expression. (c) Heatmap showing pairwise spearman correlations between the proportions of tumor subtypes and immune cell subsets inferred from scRNA-seq data. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

To assess the relationship between epithelial subtypes and immune cell populations, we performed correlation analysis across epithelial and immune cell proportions. Notably, the PB-KRAS subtype exhibited strong positive correlations with macrophage-SPP1 and Tc-GZMK clusters (Fig. 6c). SPP1-expressing macrophages have previously been associated with immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic functions [32, 33]. In addition to the T cell infiltration, the spatial distribution of pro-tumorigenic myeloid cells also exhibited a marked preference for the PB-KRAS-rich environment. The nearest neighbor distance analysis demonstrated that macrophage-SPP1 were predominantly localized in close proximity to the PB-KRAS subtype, with a mean distance of 352.6 µm, compared to a significantly larger distance of 903.0 µm from the Int-hypoxia subtype (Fig. 5j).

To investigate the molecular mechanisms that may underlie this observation, we conducted a formal cell-cell communication analysis between epithelial subtypes and myeloid subclusters. This analysis identified several significant signaling axes, including PTN-ALK, FGF2–FGFR1, and FGF19–FGFR1, which were specifically enriched in the interaction between the PB-KRAS subtype and SPP1+ macrophages (Fig. S6c). Notably, FGF2 is a well-established mediator known to promote the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward a pro-tumorigenic, M2-like state within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [34]. These findings provide molecular evidence that the PB-KRAS subtype actively modulates the functional phenotype of the myeloid compartment, offering a mechanistic basis that extends beyond mere numerical correlation.

These findings suggest that the PB-KRAS subtype may develop within, or contribute to, a uniquely immunologically conditioned microenvironment enriched in tumor-promoting macrophages and pre-dysfunctional CD8+ T cells. This immune landscape may underlie the aggressive behavior and recurrence-prone nature of this epithelial subtype. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the functional significance of these immune–epithelial interactions and evaluate their potential as therapeutic targets.

Discussion

This study presents the first single-cell transcriptomic analysis of AoV carcinoma, offering a high-resolution landscape of its epithelial and immune cell composition. Previous genomic investigations, primarily based on whole-exome sequencing, have significantly advanced our understanding of AoV carcinoma by identifying recurrent alterations such as ELF3 mutations and WNT pathway dysregulation [4, 5]. However, these bulk approaches lack the granularity required to resolve cell type-specific programs and microenvironmental interactions. Our single-cell approach addresses these limitations and provides novel insights into epithelial heterogeneity, differentiation states, and tumor-immune crosstalk in AoV carcinoma.

Among the four molecular subtypes identified, our data demonstrate that the intestinal subtype itself is not homogeneous but comprises at least two molecularly distinct programs: one characterized by active WNT/β-catenin signaling (Int-Wnt) and another driven by hypoxia-associated pathways (Int-Hypoxia). The Int-Wnt subtype was defined by robust expression of WNT target genes and selective activation of the MSX1 regulon. MSX1 is known to function downstream of WNT/β-catenin signaling, playing a key role in promoting intestinal tumorigenesis. Its loss suppresses ectopic crypt formation in Apc-deficient models, suggesting that MSX1 is a critical mediator of WNT-driven epithelial transformation [26]. These features suggest that the Int-Wnt subtype retains a proliferative, WNT-responsive phenotype reminiscent of early intestinal neoplasia.

In contrast, the Int-Hypoxia subtype displayed prominent enrichment of hypoxia-related gene sets and strong activation of the SIRT6 regulon. This suggests a mechanism involving SIRT6-enhanced stability of HIF-1α, which is known to promote the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related transcription factors like SNAIL and TWIST [28]. The resultant overexpression of SNAIL and TWIST leads to suppression of E-cadherin and induction of Vimentin, hallmark events of EMT [28]. This molecular cascade facilitates increased cellular plasticity and motility, thereby promoting invasive behavior. EMT has been associated with enhanced metastatic potential and poor recurrence-free survival in several cancers, including papillary thyroid carcinoma, and may similarly portend aggressive clinical behavior in the Int-Hypoxia subtype of AoV carcinoma. By uncovering both a WNT-driven and a hypoxia-induced EMT program within the intestinal group, our analysis emphasizes the subtype’s underlying complexity and suggests that these distinctions may carry prognostic or therapeutic significance.

The PB-KRAS subtype emerged as a particularly notable finding in our comprehensive epithelial subtyping. This subtype is defined by elevated KRAS expression and a transcriptional program robustly enriched for KRAS signaling. The PB-KRAS subtype exhibited a pronounced degree of genomic instability, carrying the highest CNV burden among all identified epithelial subtypes. Reflecting these substantial genomic aberrations, which are often associated with aggressive and self-renewal-enhancing phenotypes, we were guided to investigate its developmental hierarchy and specifically focused on evaluating its stem-like properties. Strikingly, the PB-KRAS subtype paradoxically exhibited high developmental potential and stemness scores, comparable to the canonical progenitor-like Cycling subtype. This unique combination of an apparent differentiated PB lineage state possessing high developmental potential defines a non-canonical stem-like epithelial state. Unlike the Cycling subtype, which represents a classical progenitor state, PB-KRAS cells achieve stemness through oncogenic signaling. Specifically, we found that the stemness signature was driven by the transcriptional upregulation of KRAS expression, independent of differential CNV at the KRAS locus. This suggests that elevated KRAS expression forces an aberrant, de-differentiated potential within a seemingly mature lineage. This is consistent with prior findings implicating KRAS signaling in the maintenance of stem-like features in pancreatic cancer cells [30]. Supporting this stem-like phenotype, HNF1B regulon activity was also selectively enriched in the PB-KRAS subtype. As HNF1B overexpression has been shown to enhance the expression of liver progenitor cell markers and stemness in liver cancer cells [27], this activation suggests a conserved role in supporting KRAS-associated epithelial states. Clinically, the PB-KRAS subtype was associated with significantly worse OS and DFS, as well as a higher incidence of recurrence. These findings collectively suggest that the PB-KRAS subtype program, defined by unique genomic instability and a KRAS-driven non-canonical stemness, represents the most clinically aggressive AoV subtype identified in this study, associated with poor outcomes and inherent therapeutic resistance.

In addition to epithelial heterogeneity, our analysis revealed striking differences in the immune landscape across subtypes. PB-KRAS subtype were enriched for a distinct immune microenvironment characterized by abundant GZMK+ CD8+ T cells (Tc-GZMK cells) and SPP1+ macrophages. GZMK enriched cytotoxic T cells, recently described as pre-dysfunctional T cells, represent an intermediate state preceding terminal T cell exhaustion [31]. Their accumulation within the PB-KRAS tumor microenvironment, coupled with spatial proximity to tumor epithelium and signaling through immunosuppressive ligand-receptor pairs (such as TGFB2, FGF2, and SPP1), strongly suggests active immune remodeling by tumor cells. These specific axes are known mediators of immunosuppressive signaling. For instance, TGF-β is known to suppress CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity by repressing a distinct program of cytotoxic gene responses, thereby limiting the antitumor immune response [35]. Similarly, FGF2 has been implicated in promoting T-cell exhaustion by regulating the expression of SPRY1 in esophageal tumor-infiltrating T cells, further impairing T cell function [36]. SPP1 directly suppresses CD8+ T cell proliferation and interferon-γ production, contributing to immune evasion by the tumor [37]. The co-enrichment of SPP1+ macrophages, previously implicated in promoting immune evasion and supporting tumor progression, further supports the presence of an immunosuppressive TME in PB-KRAS subtype. Importantly, high abundance of Tc-GZMK cells was associated with poor clinical outcomes, reinforcing the relevance of the immune milieu in disease progression.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the PB-KRAS subtype defines a biologically aggressive, stem-like epithelial population that may either arise within or actively sculpt an immunosuppressive TME enriched for dysfunctional immune cell states. This epithelial-immune crosstalk may contribute to tumor recurrence and resistance to therapy. In contrast, the Int-Wnt and Int-Hypoxia subtypes are characterized by alternative oncogenic programs and immune environments, with divergent prognostic implications.

A key limitation of this study is that the observed association between the PB-KRAS subtype and the immunosuppressive TME is based on correlational multi-omics analysis rather than direct functional validation. It is well established that activating KRAS mutations can actively modulate the immune microenvironment by promoting pro-tumorigenic macrophage polarization, inhibiting T-cell cytotoxicity, and accelerating T-cell exhaustion. Moreover, KRAS-mediated metabolic reprogramming may create a feedback loop where metabolite accumulation induces further genomic instability and mutational burden [38]. Although our findings regarding the PB-KRAS subtype are highly consistent with these known roles of KRAS signaling, further in vivo studies—such as orthotopic or syngeneic mouse models—and co-culture experiments are necessary to definitively prove the causal role of PB-KRAS cells in shaping this specific immunosuppressive niche. Moreover, while the specific ligand-receptor axes identified in this study, such as TGFB2 and SPP1, represent highly promising therapeutic targets, we acknowledge that their functional significance requires further validation in follow-up translational models utilizing functional blocking with neutralizing antibodies or small molecule inhibitors in patient-derived organoid-immune co-culture systems.

These insights have potential therapeutic implications. Targeting the KRAS-HNF1B axis or the immunosuppressive mediators enriched in PB-KRAS subtype may offer novel avenues for treatment. Moreover, immunotherapeutic strategies aiming to restore CD8+ T cell functionality or reprogram tumor-associated macrophages may be particularly effective in this context. Future studies should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying epithelial-immune interactions in AoV carcinoma and evaluating their druggability in preclinical models. Additionally, although the multivariate analysis yielded an inflated hazard ratio due to complete separation in our small cohort (n = 62), Bootstrap validation (1,000 resamples) confirmed the statistical stability and robust prognostic value of the PB-KRAS subtype. Given the limited number of patients included in the single-cell analysis, validation in larger multi-institutional cohorts will be important to further establish the generalizability and potential clinical utility of the PB-KRAS subtype classification.

Conclusions

Our single-cell atlas of AoV carcinoma reveals profound epithelial and immune heterogeneity and identifies a clinically relevant PB-KRAS subtype defined by KRAS-driven non-canonical stem-like features and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. These findings provide a foundation for precision classification of AoV carcinoma and suggest novel therapeutic vulnerabilities for high-risk patients.
