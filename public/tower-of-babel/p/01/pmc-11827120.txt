Surgical Tips and Tricks for the Anterior-Based Muscle-Sparing Approach During Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Gideon Jia Wei Cheok, Ing How Moo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn orthopaedic surgery, the advancement of surgical techniques aims to enhance patient outcomes and minimize postoperative complications. A notable innovation in this field is the anterior-based muscle-sparing (ABMS) approach to total hip arthroplasty, also known as ABLE or Rottinger approach. ABMS modifies the Watson-Jones approach, using the intermuscular plane between the tensor fascia lata and the gluteus medius to access the hip joint without detaching abductor muscles. This technique offers several benefits including muscle preservation, decreased pain, faster recovery, and lower dislocation rates, similar to the direct anterior approach. However, ABMS distinguishes itself by its lower infection rate, reduced nerve injury risk, easier femoral exposure, and compatibility with various implants. Large-scale studies have confirmed its safety and effectiveness. While mastering the ABMS technique requires some learning, this article provides a concise description of the technique, along with practical insights gained from surgical experience to support safe and effective execution.Keywords: Anterior-based muscle-sparing approach, Total hip arthroplasty, Minimally invasive surgery, Surgical pearls

Introduction

In the realm of orthopaedic surgery, the evolution of surgical techniques has continually sought to enhance patient outcomes and minimize postoperative complications. Among these advancements, the anterior-based muscle-sparing (ABMS) approach to total hip arthroplasty (THA), also known as ABLE or Rottinger approach, stands as a relatively recent innovation that has gained significant attention within contemporary literature.

Initially described by Rottinger et al. [1], the ABMS is a modification of the standard Watson-Jones approach, which uses the intermuscular plane between the tensor fascia lata (TFL) and the gluteus medius (GMed) to gain exposure to the hip joint but without the detachment of abductor muscles [1,2]. This approach gained popularity over the last 2 decades, driven by a growing body of evidence supporting some advantages over more established approaches. Similar to the direct anterior approach (DAA), this approach is minimally invasive and muscle-sparing, so patients suffer less pain, experience faster recovery and fewer dislocations, with similar patient outcome scores [3,4]. However, unlike the DAA, the utilization of a more lateral skin incision away from inguinal folds lowers the Cutibacterium sp. infection rate in the ABMS approach [5]. The risk of injury to lateral femoral cutaneous nerve is also significantly lower [6,7]. Also in contrast to DAA, the ABMS approach requires less posterior soft-tissue release and the femoral exposure is easier. The ABMS approach does not necessarily require special instruments or table and is compatible with any modern implant that best fits the patient anatomy. The technique has also been shown to be safe and effective in large sample studies [[8], [9], [10]].

Transitioning to an ABMS approach for THAs has been shown to be prone to a learning curve. Although it is our experience that transitioning to the ABMS has a relatively gentler learning curve compared to the DAA, the adoption of a new surgical approach inevitably comes with a significant learning in the first few cases. There is paucity in the literature discussing the surgical pearls and pitfalls in ABMS. This article provides a brief description of the technique, focusing mainly on the surgical tips and tricks gained from experience executing this approach to safely and effectively perform the ABMS approach.

Patient selection

While the choice of surgical approach is often dictated by the surgeon’s experience and preference, the ABMS approach is versatile and can be used in almost all types of patients who require a THA (ie, obese patient, stiff or complex hips). The femoral exposure is technically less challenging in ABMS as the incision is more lateral; hence, the proximal femur requires less anterior translation. This can be especially useful when exposing the proximal femur in muscular patients or in those with significantly stiff hip joint. This is in contrast to the DAA wherein femur exposure may be limited even after extension of the incision. In this regard, the ABMS offers a versatile and extensile approach, having been shown to be effective in primary arthroplasties as well as in revision cases [6,9]. It can also be used for proximal femur wiring, plating, trochanteric osteotomies, and abductor muscle advancements.

There has been no correlation shown between body mass index (BMI) and postoperative outcomes in primary THAs, although high BMI is associated with slightly longer operative times [11]. Larger patients potentially add a measure of difficulty to this approach due to the hindrance caused by larger soft tissue mass. The author recommends that the panniculus be taped away from the surgical field and incision location should be placed distal and lateral to it. The skin incision can also be extended in obese patients to minimize tension during retraction while optimizing exposure.

The ABMS approach, however, does suffer from the same limitation as other anterior-based techniques where posterior acetabular wall exposure is limited. In cases where there are large posterior column defects or pelvic discontinuities, choosing a posterior-based approach may be more prudent.

Positioning and bed setup

The ABMS approach can be performed with the patient in either the supine or lateral position. Our senior author routinely uses the lateral position for his THAs. For the ABMS approach in lateral decubitus position, an operating table with a detachable leg plate and peg board for positioning patient is recommended. When positioning the patient in lateral position, the pelvis is leveled and the gluteal fold is placed at the edge of the bed just next to the posterior leg plate so that the operated leg can be maximally extended, externally rotated, and adducted to access the femur (Fig. 1). The contralateral leg is secured to the anterior leg plate in slight hip and knee flexion. One to 2 short pegs are placed anterior to the pubis so that the surgeon can palpate the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) intraoperatively and allow unrestricted leg flexion, and 1 to 2 long pegs are placed at the sacral region. The author also recommends placing long peg anterior and posterior to the thorax to stabilize the entire trunk to avoid changes in the patient position as the leg is manipulated intraoperatively. Hence, 4-6 pegs are routinely used to stabilize the pelvis and trunk. Inflatable sandbag can also be used in addition to the peg for extra stability. After prepping the leg with alcohol-based solution, the drape is then placed such that the proximal femur and ASIS can be accessed. The surgeon stands on the anterior side of the patient. Meticulous positioning is paramount to avoid the variability in pelvic tilt and obliquity especially seen in lateral decubitus position, as that can lead to acetabular cup malpositioning in conventional THA techniques without navigation.

Accurate skin incision and localization of the intermuscular interval

Precise mapping of the skin incision helps to ensure optimal exposure of the intermuscular interval. While simple palpation may suffice for lean patients, we recommend using a 20-gauge spinal needle to palpate bony landmarks as this maximizes accuracy especially in larger patients. The needle is used to locate the tip of the greater trochanter (GT) and 2 points along the anterior border of the femur, while the leg is in neutral abduction and rotation. A point is first marked 2 cm distal and posterior to the ASIS. A second point is marked 5 cm distal to the tip of the GT at the anterior one-third of the femur. An oblique line is drawn connecting these points and this corresponds to the anterior border of the GMed. The resultant incision measures around 12 cm in length, but the overall length of the incision varies with the body habitus of the patient. Extending the incision distally helps with acetabular exposure and extending the incision proximally may improve femoral exposure. The proximal aspect of the skin incision can be altered based on the preferred femoral stem system. A ream and broach femoral stem system will require a straight incision which provides more direct-in-line access to the femur. As the senior author preferred to use a broach-only system with offset handle, a curvilinear incision provides adequate access to the femur while maximizing acetabular exposure.

After making the skin incision, it is critical to accurately localize the intermuscular interval. The fascia overlying the TFL is thinner so the muscle appears as blue hue through the fascia, while the GMed fascia is thicker and will appear white. Furthermore, a perforating vessel can almost always be found at this interval (Fig. 2). However, with experience, one can identify this interval easily at the distal incision just anterior to the femur, and this can minimize soft tissue stripping and avoid postoperative swelling, seroma or hematoma collection. Once this intermuscular interval is identified, an incision is first made at the distal aspect of this interval to visualize the precapsular fat which signifies the correct location. It is important to make the fascial incision 1 cm posterior to this interval over the GMed, which leaves an adequate fascial cuff for closure at the end of the case. Further deepening of the interval should reveal the gateway vessels between the TFL and GMed which are the terminal transverse branches of the lateral femoral circumflex artery. Abducting the leg can relieve tension and allow for an easier definition and dissection of the intermuscular plane. Inadvertent severance of the deep vessels should be identified and ligated prior to proceeding. Finally, as the interval is developed proximally, the terminal branches of the superior gluteal nerve may be encountered; these provide innervation to the TFL and should be preserved by using blunt dissection and gently elevating the GMed off the TFL.

Adequate capsulotomy

Once through the intermuscular interval, pericapsular fat can be removed. Two curved cobra retractors are placed above and below the femoral neck extracapsularly. The rectus femoris is elevated from the capsule medially using a cob elevator and a medial retractor is placed under the rectus femoris. A hibb retractor is placed to retract the vastus lateralis laterally. The leg is slightly externally rotated, allowing good exposure of the proximal femur from anterior acetabular rim medially to the intertrochanteric ridge laterally for the capsulotomy or capsulectomy. While there are several configurations for the capsulotomy, our senior author favors the inverted T-shape configuration, with one limb over the intertrochanteric line and the other proximally up the axis of the femoral neck. The superior flap of the capsulotomy is reflected off the neck until the superior acetabulum is exposed, while the inferior flap is reflected until the proximal edge of the vastus intermedius along the intertrochanteric line. Care should be taken not to extend this too medially or risk injury to the rectus. The limbs of the capsulotomy can be tagged with vicryl 1-0 sutures to help aid in further retraction, exposure, capsular release, and later repair. Now the extracapsular retractors can be placed on the superior and inferior aspects of the femoral neck intracapsularly, thus protecting the posteromedial and anteromedial structures during the following steps.

In-situ neck osteotomy

It is almost impossible to dislocate the hip without making an in-situ neck osteotomy to create space required to dislocate the head when performing this minimally invasive approach. The oscillations of the saw should be between the 2 retractors; the initial neck cut be done as proximal as possible and close to the acetabular rim. This may mean cutting through a portion of the femoral head. Multiple osteotomies of the neck can then be performed moving distally in a “salami-slice” pattern, with oscillating saw and osteotome for completion (Fig. 3). A more proximal initial cut allows a larger portion of the femoral neck to be removed, thus creating more space to remove the head, which is also smaller and easier to dislocate.

In-situ multiple osteotomies of the femoral neck in a “salami-slice” pattern (green arrow).

If there is difficulty in delivering the femoral head at this point, it can be left in the acetabulum, while the definitive neck cut is measured and performed together with further capsular releases. This will afford even more space for manipulation and removal of the head later.

Femoral neck cut and femur mobilization

The femoral neck cut is where the proximal medial aspect of the femoral stem is expected to sit. Ensuring optimal femoral mobilization is the next critical step. Mobilization of the femur with capsular releases allows for optimal exposure of both the acetabulum and proximal femur. With the leg abducted and externally rotated, thereby decreasing the tension of the abductors, a curved sharp Hohmann retractor is placed over the trochanter at the bare area. The superior capsule is then released from the saddle. With the leg placed in figure-of-four position into the anterior pouch, a straight Hohmann retractor is place along the posteromedial femoral neck and the taut inferior capsule is released by electrocautery and this also exposed the lesser trochanter (LT). Once the saddle and LT are visible, the definitive femoral neck cut is performed at a level consistent with preoperative templating. This release allows greater external rotation of the femur and better exposure of the acetabulum. Release of soft tissues around the posterior femur can be performed if further exposure needs to be improved at this point.

Acetabular preparation

For acetabular preparation, the operated leg is bought back to the bed and kept in slight flexion to relax the anterior hip structures. A large towel roll is placed under the leg to keep the leg abducted to relax the gluteus maximus. The ipsilateral thigh is axially loaded, shortened, and elevated to create space posterior to acetabulum so that a right-angle retractor can be easily placed behind the posterior acetabular wall, under the posterior capsule, and lying perpendicular over the femoral neck cut. This allows further leveraging of the femur posteriorly and out of the field for the meantime. A second retractor is placed anteriorly between the anterior labrum and the anterior capsule deep to the iliopsoas. A third curved retractor can be placed over the superior acetabulum to protect the GMed and enhance exposure (Fig. 4). Placement of posterior and anterior retractors is essential in protecting the muscles surrounding the approach interval such as the GMed and TFL during acetabular reaming. If access to the acetabulum is limited, one can adjust the retractors around the acetabulum, perform further capsular release around the proximal femur, and lastly, consider further neck resection by few millimeters if the remaining bone allows. As the acetabular exposure can sometimes be limited, introducing the acetabular reamer while attached to the power tool can be unwieldy and may induce collateral damage to surrounding structures. Hence, one surgical tip is to introduce the acetabular reamer basket into the acetabulum manually and position it for assembly to the power source; thereafter, the shaft of the reamer is introduced and assembled in-situ to the handheld power tool. Similarly, the reamer is removed from the acetabulum by dissociating the reamer basket from the shaft within the surgical field. Otherwise, the acetabular preparation and implantation are straightforward and intuitive.

Femur preparation

To obtain femoral exposure for instrumentation in the lateral decubitus position, the operated leg needs to be maneuvered into extension, adduction, and external rotation by an assistant on the posterior aspect of the operating table (figure-of-four position) and placed inside the sterile pouch. To achieve this, a retractor is placed over the posterior aspect of the GT under the abductors with gentle traction to retract the abductors and a second double-pronged retractor is placed under the calcar proximal to the LT to elevate the femur (Fig. 5). The surgeon needs to ensure that the assistant is not placing excessive force on the retractors as that may cause calcar or trochanteric fractures especially in osteoporotic patients. The placement of the retractor over the GT should be smooth and introduced without resistance. If resistance is met, then the tip of the retractor may be in bone and trochanteric fracture can occur. It is also important to remind the assistant holding this retractor to retract the abductors gently. Using electrocautery, the posterior capsule is released from medial and posterior border of the GT as the leg is brought into figure-of-four position. This requires coordinated movement and synergy between the surgeon and the assistant. The amount of release should be done sequentially and tailored based on patient characteristics (eg, BMI, muscle mass, stiffness, and complexity of the cases). These include release of the posterior capsule and the obturator externus. The release is complete when the femur can be externally rotated such that the tibia is perpendicular to the operating room floor.

Retractor placement during femur preparation; blue star: curved Hohmann retractor placed over greater trochanteric bare area, green star: straight Hohmann retractor on the posteromedial femoral neck, white star: double-pronged retractor under the femur calcar, yellow line: superior border of femoral neck, green line: inferior border of femoral neck, and yellow dot: U-shaped capsulotomy flap tagged with suture.

It is also paramount at this point to ensure that the trajectory of the broach is unhindered by soft tissue, retractors, and the lateral bony ridges over the piriformis fossa. Once the femoral access is adequate, broaching is performed in a standard fashion using an offset handle. It is important to broach in line with the femoral canal. If there is hindrance, first revisit the capsular and soft tissue releases, otherwise the fascial incision may be lengthened distally parallel to the femur shaft. This should allow the TFL to be retracted anteromedially and reduce the tension on the GMed posteriorly. It is important to use either burr or rongeur to clear the lateral ridge of bone at the piriformis fossa to get a straight shot down the canal while broaching. Failure to do so may lead to impingement of the posterior cortex, cortical perforation, varus malposition, and undersizing of the stem.

Trial reduction is done on reaching the final broach size. Reduction can be performed by having the assistant applying longitudinal traction with the leg in neutral rotation and the surgeon lifting the femoral head posterior and lateral over the anterior acetabulum and into the cup. Caution should be taken not to drag or push the trial head over the anterior acetabulum as the torque on the trial stem may lead to fracture of the calcar. The surgeon needs to keep his fingers on the trial femoral head as it can get caught by the rectus femoris and may potentially dislodge and get lost inside the surgical field. After trialling the hip for range of motion, stability, and impingement, the hip is then dislocated. The hip is dislocated using a small langenbeck or a bone hook around the neck of the stem to lift the femoral head out of the acetabulum after placing longitudinal traction on the leg in slight abduction. A series of trials can be performed in standard fashion before final implant is inserted. Our senior author uses the shuck test, drop test, and range of motion in flexion, internal and external rotation to assess stability intraoperatively.

Minimizing perioperative complications

Femoral nerve palsy is a known complication following THAs using anterior-based approaches [3,12]. The femoral nerve is at risk of partial or complete palsy given its proximity to the intermuscular interval during the ABMS approach and during retraction for acetabulum and femur preparation. One precaution to take is to leave at least 1 cm cuff of fascia attached to the TFL. This fascial sleeve protects the femoral nerve from stretch injury. Another tip is to achieve femoral elevation during femur preparation by appointing an assistant to push the femur into adduction and with a gentle cranially directed force. This reduces distraction and tension on the femoral nerve.

Femur-sided complications such as GT fractures or calcar fractures are associated with the learning curves of the more recent anterior-based approaches. These can be prevented by performing adequate soft tissue releases, mainly of the posterior capsule and obturator externus. Calcar fractures can be minimized during trial reduction as previously mentioned and with proper retractor placement during femur preparation.

Summary

With the growing popularity of minimally invasive approaches to the hip joint, many surgeons experience a learning curve when transitioning to an anterior-based approach. These useful tips and tricks were developed from the senior author’s learning points after certain challenging cases in a hope to minimize the learning curve of fellow surgeons and practitioners.
