Association of residential greenness and Life’s Essential 8 with cardiovascular disease in China
Authors: Qiuyu Cao, Dong Li, Hong Lin, Shuangyuan Wang, Mian Li, Yonghua Yu, Ruizhi Zheng, Yu Xu, Min Xu, Tiange Wang, Libin Zhou, Mofan Xu, Yiming Mu, Lulu Chen, Tianshu Zeng, Lixin Shi, Qiao Zhang, Qing Su, Xuefeng Yu, Li Yan, Guijun Qin, Qin Wan, Gang Chen, Xulei Tang, Zhengnan Gao, Ruying Hu, Zuojie Luo, Yingfen Qin, Li Chen, Xinguo Hou, Yanan Huo, Qiang Li, Guixia Wang, Yinfei Zhang, Chao Liu, Youmin Wang, Shengli Wu, Tao Yang, Huacong Deng, Jiajun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryProspective evidence linking greenness and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in rapidly urbanizing developing countries remains limited. Here, among 159,590 adults aged ≥40 years from the nationwide China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort with a median follow-up of 10.1 years, we examine the association between residential greenness, measured by satellite-derived normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) within 500 m of residence, and incident CVD, and evaluate its joint effects with cardiovascular health as defined by Life’s Essential 8. Individuals in the highest quartiles of contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative NDVI consistently show lower CVD risk compared with those in the lowest quartiles, although associations vary across subpopulations. Notably, individuals with high cardiovascular health scores living in low-NDVI areas exhibit similar CVD risk to those residing in high-NDVI areas. These findings highlight the complementary importance of both green infrastructure and healthy lifestyles in reducing CVD risk in rapidly urbanizing regions of China.Keywords: residential greenness, cardiovascular health score, cardiovascular disease, combined effects

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has emerged as the leading cause of premature death and chronic disability globally, particularly in China, where stroke is more prevalent than ischemic heart disease.1,2 The development of CVD is influenced by genetic, behavioral, and environmental determinants,3,4 highlighting the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors for effective prevention strategies. Environmental risk factors (ERFs) now represent a growing driver of cardiovascular disease, accounting for ∼20 million deaths annually and exceeding the burden of many conventional risk factors.5 These observations underscore the public health value of identifying scalable environmental interventions to reduce CVD risk.

Residential greenness has been increasingly proposed as a potential protective factor against cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, by mitigating ERFs exposures and promoting healthier behaviors.6 Although several studies in high-income countries have suggested an inverse association between greenness and CVD risk,7,8 evidence from rapidly urbanizing low- and middle-income countries remains limited. The rapid pace of urbanization, coupled with China’s vast geographical and socioeconomic heterogeneity, may influence these associations in unique ways.9 Moreover, the majority of existing studies were limited by cross-sectional design with small sample sizes, restricting their capability to infer causality between greenness exposure and CVD outcomes.10,11,12 Even among prospective cohort studies, findings are still inconsistent, with some reporting beneficial effects of greenness exposure,8,13 while others demonstrating no associations,14,15 possibly due to differences in sample size, greenness metrics, exposure windows, and outcome definitions. Additionally, few studies have examined the temporal dimensions of greenness exposure, with most studies relying on a single time point or short follow-up window.16 This gap limits our understanding of how contemporaneous, short-term, and cumulative exposure may differentially affect cardiovascular risk.

The cardiovascular benefits of greenness may also vary with demographic, socioeconomic status (SES), individual lifestyles, and environmental contexts. Several studies have reported stronger beneficial effects of greenness among older adults and individuals with low SES,17,18 while interactions between greenness and other environmental factors, such as air pollution and extreme heat, were rather complex.19,20 In addition, Life’s Essential 8 defined by the American Heart Association (AHA) is a composite index designed to assess overall cardiovascular heath (CVH) through a dual framework of health-related behaviors and factors.21,22 However, no studies to date have investigated the potential synergistic effects between residential greenness and individual CVH profiles on CVD outcomes.

To address these gaps, we utilized data from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) to investigate the relationships between contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative exposure to residential greenness and the risk of incident CVD events. Importantly, we also explored how these associations may be modified by individual behaviors and metabolic factors, providing a comprehensive perspective on the potential cardioprotective role of greenness in a rapidly urbanizing population.

Results

Participants characteristics

A total of 193,846 participants aged 40 years or older were enrolled in the 4C study between January 2011 and December 2012. Participants with CVD at baseline (n = 3,961) were excluded. During a median follow-up of 10.1 years, 4,365 (2.30%) participants were lost to follow-up, and 26,200 participants were further excluded due to incomplete information on CVD events during the follow-up period, leaving 159,590 participants for the final analysis (Figure S1).

Among 159,590 individuals, the mean age was 56.8 years (standard deviation [SD] 9.3) at baseline, and 55,425 (34.7%) participants were men (Table 1). Three complementary measures of residential greenness exposure were evaluated, including contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative greenness assessed by normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) values within 500 m buffer of each participant’s residential address (Figure 1A). There was substantial variability in the greenness across the study regions (Figure 1B). The mean contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative NDVI values were 0.31 (SD 0.17), 0.31 (SD 0.13), and 0.30 (SD 0.12), respectively. Individuals living in areas with greater greenness exposure were more likely to be younger and less educated than those in areas with lower greenness exposure. Areas with higher greenness exposure tended to be rural areas, have lower PM2.5 levels, higher artificial light at night (ALAN) levels, and fewer days of heatwave compared with areas with lower greenness exposure. The average overall CVH score was 61.3 (SD 12.5) and was the highest in individuals in areas with higher greenness. The baseline characteristics of participants included in the analysis were similar to those excluded (Table S1).

Association between greenness exposure and incident CVD events

During a median follow-up of 10.1 years, 12,707 incident CVD events were identified, corresponding to an incidence rate of 826 per 100,000 person-years. The incident CVD events included 1,364 non-fatal myocardial infarctions, 9,213 non-fatal strokes, and 3,370 CVD mortality. Table 2 presents hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for CVD events in models fully adjusted for covariates. The HRs (95% CIs) for individuals in the highest quartiles of contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative NDVI values were 0.37 (0.35–0.40), 0.39 (0.37–0.43), and 0.52 (0.48–0.56), compared with those in the lowest quartiles. A 0.1 unit increase in contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative NDVI was associated with 15%, 29%, and 19% reduction in CVD risk, respectively. Higher exposure to contemporaneous, one-year, or cumulative NDVI values was also associated with lower risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, and CVD mortality. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated similar results (Tables S2–S6). The associations between greenness and CVD events were substantially attenuated in the time-dependent Cox models (Table S7). As shown in Figure 2, the restricted cubic spline curves indicated non-linear associations between contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative NDVI and CVD events (all p values for nonlinearity <0.001). The protective effect of greenness plateaued at higher NDVI levels, which was more evident in rural than urban residents (Figure S2). Sensitivity analyses showed similar results for NDVI within 250 and 1,250 m radius (Tables S8–S9; Figures S3–S4).

The association between greenness and incident CVD was mediated by physical activity (1.2% [95% CI 0.5%–1.6%]), obesity (1.9% [95% CI 1.5%–2.3%]), and diabetes (4.9% [95% CI 3.0%–6.9%]), with a smaller contribution from hyperlipidemia (Table S13). No significant mediation effect was found for hypertension. In addition, PM2.5 and heat mitigation accounted for 9.1% (95% CI 7.7%–10.1%) and 5.3% (95% CI 3.7%–6.1%) of the association, suggesting a measurable contribution of environmental pathways.

Combined effects of greenness and CVH scores

The risks of incident CVD consistently increased as the degree of overall CVH scores decreased (Table 4). In fully adjusted models, the HRs of individuals with low overall CVH score were 3.09 (2.63–3.62) for CVD, 3.78 (2.23–6.38) for myocardial infarction (MI), 3.13 (2.62–3.75) for stroke, and 2.80 (1.93–4.07) for CVD mortality. A similar pattern was observed for health behavior and health factor scores, with the association being stronger for health factor scores.

Among those in the lowest quartile of NDVI, the HRs for CVD was 3.58 (3.24–3.96) for participants who had a low overall CVH score, 1.95 (1.77–2.13) for those who had a medium CVH score, and 0.84 (0.63–1.12) for participants who had a high CVH score, compared with participants in the highest quartile of NDVI (Table 5). Discussion

In this nationwide prospective cohort study in China, we found that higher level of contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative exposure to residential greenness were significantly associated with lower risks of incident CVD events, with heterogeneous effects in subpopulations. Furthermore, individual behavioral and metabolic factors had joint effects with greenness exposure on CVD. Individuals with higher Life’s Essential 8 scores in the lowest quartile of greenness had similar risk of CVD as those in the highest quartile of greenness. These findings enhance our understanding of the complex interplay between environment and CVH in rapidly urbanizing settings and also provide valuable evidence to inform green infrastructure planning, urban sustainability policies, and public health interventions aimed at reducing the burden of CVD through the promotion of greener living environments.

Our study identified an inverse association between residential greenness and the risk of incident CVD events, consistent with findings of previous studies conducted in Western populations.7,8 Although relevant evidence from prospective cohort studies in China remains limited, a few studies have reported similar associations. A study of 29,141 participants from a southeastern Chinese city reported that an interquartile range increase in NDVI within 250 m was inversely associated with incident ischemic heart disease.23 Another prospective cohort study involving 748,209 participants from the subset of Guangzhou and Zhongshan cities found that a 0.1-unit increase in annual average NDVI was associated with a 9.3% lower risk of total CVD mortality.17 However, both studies were limited to one or two regions of China and had relatively short follow-up periods (3.5 years and 7.9 years, respectively). A recent study using data from the China Kadoorie Biobank cohort demonstrated that greenness was associated with the incidence of CVD, but the participants were from only 10 regions in China.24 In contrast, our prospective cohort included participants from 16 provinces across China, representing a broad range of geographical, socioeconomic, and environmental contexts, and followed them for a median of 10 years.

We found that contemporaneous, short-term, and long-term exposures to greenness were all associated with lower risks of CVD outcomes. Interestingly, findings from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) study, which focused on the oldest-old population, showed that only contemporaneous NDVI, rather than cumulative NDVI, was associated with mortality, indicating that mortality in the oldest-old population might be more influenced by acute rather than long-term greenness exposures.16 Our study extends this evidence by demonstrating that among middle-aged and older adults (aged ≥40 years), greenness exposure across different time frames, from a few days to a decade, is consistently associated with lower cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Studies on the acute effect of exposure to greenspaces suggested that forest exposure could reduce blood pressure, pulse rate, and anxiety levels.25,26 And repeated acute exposures to greenness were associated with decreased total and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, as well as reduced fasting glucose.27 Our results indicated that short-term exposure to greenness might have stronger association with CVD risk than acute or long-term exposures, and it is likely that some of the cardiovascular and metabolic effects of greenspaces may be cumulative. More studies are needed to understand the potential biological or behavioral mechanisms underneath the short-term and long-term cardiovascular benefits of greenness. However, in comparison with the primary analyses, results from the time-varying Cox model showed attenuated effect estimates. This discrepancy may be partly explained by increased exposure variability introduced by repeatedly updated NDVI measures, which may introduce greater exposure variability and increase exposure misclassification and bias estimates toward the null. In addition, several covariates, including physical activity, adiposity, and air pollution, may lie on the causal pathway linking greenness to cardiovascular outcomes. Because these variables were not fully updated over follow-up, residual time-varying confounding or overadjustment may also contribute to the observed attenuation. These findings underscore the importance of considering both current and long-term greenness exposure in CVD prevention strategies and highlight the potential cumulative benefits of sustained green environments on CVH.

The exposure-response curves indicated that the protective effects of residential greenness on CVD events tended to plateau at higher NDVI levels. Notably, in the CLHLS study, higher cumulative greenness appeared to be detrimental to all-cause mortality.16 Similarly, another study involving individuals with type 2 diabetes observed a protective association between NDVI and mortality, with a threshold beyond which the protective effect slightly stabilized.28 Our study further demonstrated the presence of a plateau effect, suggesting that the cardiovascular benefits of greenness may level off beyond a certain exposure threshold. The plateau effect at higher NDVI levels was more evident in rural areas than in urban regions, and several factors may contribute to this pattern. First, NDVI levels in rural areas are generally higher, and the marginal health benefits of additional greenness may diminish beyond a certain threshold. Second, as NDVI derived from satellite images does not differentiate between vegetation types, higher NDVI values in rural or suburban areas likely reflect agricultural land rather than functional green spaces.29 Such non-differential exposure misclassification may attenuate the observed associations and partially explain the plateau effect at higher levels of greenness. Future studies should incorporate more detailed land-use data, including the classification, accessibility, and utilization of green spaces, to better characterize exposure and elucidate nonlinear exposure-response relationships. Third, structural disadvantages, including limited healthcare resources, emergency services, and chronic disease management capacity in rural areas, may attenuate the potential cardiovascular benefits associated with the greenness. Future studies in more diverse populations are needed to further examine the nonlinear exposure-response relationship between greenness and CVD events.

We observed a stronger protective association between greenness and CVD among participants with more frequent physical activity, consistent with findings from previous studies.17 Physical activity in green spaces has been shown to enhance immune function and promote psychological and related health benefits. Furthermore, the association was particularly significant in urban areas with high GDP and high ALAN. Since ALAN serves as an indicator of urbanization, our results suggested that in more urbanized areas, the protective effect of increasing greenness against CVD might be more substantial, aligning with previous studies.17 In addition, we found that the effects of greenness on CVD events might be stronger among adults living in areas with higher exposure to heatwave days, consistent with a prior study.20 It was possible that extreme heat amplified the benefit of increased vegetation in heat mitigation, making people more inclined to spend time in green spaces. Associations between greenness and CVD were largely consistent across geographic regions. However, heterogeneity was observed for contemporaneous NDVI in southwestern China, where it may be less reflective of stable greenness exposure. This may be attributable to region-specific characteristics, including unique climatic conditions (e.g., high humidity and frequent cloud cover), complex topography, and distinct vegetation patterns, which could increase short-term variability in NDVI and potentially lead to exposure misclassification.30

We demonstrated that higher Life’s Essential 8 scores were associated with reduced risks of CVD. Life’s Essential 8, originally developed by the American Heart Association, has increasingly been applied in non-Western populations, including Chinese cohorts, where higher scores have been associated with reduced risks of CVD.31,32,33 In this study, we adopted population-specific adaptations, including dietary scoring based on Chinese guidelines and BMI classification tailored to Asian populations, to enhance its cultural relevance. These findings support the applicability of Life’s Essential 8 in the Chinese context, although further validation in diverse populations is warranted.

Life’s Essential 8 components may both interact with and partially mediate the effects of greenness. The mediation analysis demonstrated that the beneficial effects of greenness on CVD risk may be partly explained by improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, including physical activity, obesity, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, although the overall mediation proportion was modest. This finding indicates that behavioral and metabolic pathways, as captured by Life’s Essential 8 components, represent a measurable and clinically actionable mechanism linking environmental exposures to CVH. Additionally, PM2.5 and heat mitigation explained a comparable proportion of the association, consistent with established environmental mechanisms.18 Taken together, these results suggest that while environmental pathways remain important, metabolic regulation may constitute a complementary pathway through which greenness exerts its cardioprotective effects.

Our study provides evidence on the combined effects of residential greenness exposure and individual Life’s Essential 8 CVH scores on CVD risk. Importantly, participants in the lowest quartile of greenness who had a high CVH score appeared to have no significant excess risk of CVD events compared with the population in the highest quartile of greenness. These findings suggest that maintaining healthy behaviors and ideal metabolic profiles may mitigate the negative influence of low residential greenness exposure. A cohort study in South China reported that individuals with a healthier lifestyle (non-smokers and non-drinkers) were more likely to benefit from greenness.17 Another cross-sectional study found that metabolic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, overweight/obesity, and hyperlipidemia, mediated the association between residential greenness and CVD.10 The underlying biological mechanisms linking greenness with individual behavioral and metabolic factors remain to be fully elucidated. One possible explanation is that individuals with higher CVH may already have optimized pathways related to inflammation, endothelial function, and metabolic regulation, thereby reducing their vulnerability to adverse environmental exposures.22 These findings highlight the potential benefits of integrating nature-based and behavioral interventions into CVH promotion.

Our study had several strengths. First, it is a nationwide prospective cohort with a 10-year follow-up, incorporating a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle variables to account for potential confounding factors. Second, this study comprehensively explored the associations between contemporary, one-year, and cumulative greenness and CVD events. Third, based on the extensive data from the 4C study, we assessed the influence of a series of environmental, behavioral, and metabolic factors on the associations, enhancing the robustness of our findings. Our findings suggest that residential greenness may serve as a population-level strategy for CVD prevention. From an urban planning perspective, integrating accessible green infrastructure into residential communities, such as neighborhood parks, street trees, and community gardens, may contribute to CVH promotion. Importantly, in resource-limited areas where access to advanced and individualized interventions is limited, greenness-based interventions could provide a relatively low-cost and sustainable approach to improving population health. Promoting equitable distribution of green spaces in underserved communities may help reduce CVH disparities.

In conclusion, higher contemporaneous, one-year, and cumulative exposures to greenness are all associated with lower risks of incident CVD events. The protective effect of greenness was more evident in individuals with higher levels of physical activity and those living in urban areas with higher GDP, greater ALAN, and more heatwave days. Individual behavioral and metabolic factors may have synergistic effects with greenness exposure in reducing CVD risk. Our findings indicate that residential greenness might serve as a protective factor for CVD and that individual behavioral and metabolic factors may have synergistic effects to mitigate the impacts of low greenness exposure on CVD.

Limitations of the study

Our study has some potential limitations. First, even though NDVI is an objective, quantitative measure of vegetative coverage, it does not capture the usability or quality of the green spaces. Similar NDVI values may represent distinct land-use types, such as functional urban green spaces (e.g., parks) versus agricultural land or grasslands, which might have different effects on CVD risk. Further studies should explore the association between CVH and alternative greenness indices, such as tree canopy cover and street tree count. Second, exposure misclassification may occur due to the lack of continuous monitoring of personal exposure and unmeasured residential mobility during the follow-up period. However, these discrepancies are expected to be random and are likely to lead to an underestimation of the effects.34 Third, behavioral CVH components were self-reported and may be prone to recall bias, which could lead to misclassification of CVH categories and reduce power to detect interactions between greenness and CVH scores. Fourth, although the cohort includes participants from multiple regions across China, it was not designed to be nationally representative. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, healthier individuals with higher socioeconomic status may be more likely to live in greener neighborhoods, which may introduce a “healthy-user” bias. Although we have adjusted for local GDP, individual education level, marriage status, and occupational status, the association between greenness exposure and incident CVD could be confounded by unmeasured individual socioeconomic factors. Sixth, environmental noise exposure is not measured in our study. As an important cardiovascular risk factor, noise could confound or mediate the association between greenness and cardiovascular outcomes. Finally, while we accounted for differences in dietary and other behavioral factors, variations in local environmental and socioeconomic conditions may influence the observed associations. Although we did not observe effect modification by northern versus southern residence, cluster analysis by geographical regions, with detailed vegetation types, is required in future research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and population

The 4C Study is a multicenter, population-based, prospective cohort study in China. Details about the study design have been published previously.35,36,37 In brief, a total of 20 communities across 16 provinces covering various geographic regions of mainland China were selected. The selection of communities considered geographic diversity, economic development status, feasibility of centralized sample collection, and the ability of ensuring long-term follow-up. Based on the national criteria for coordinated regional development, these 16 provinces were categorized into central (Henan and Hubei provinces), eastern (Fujian, Jiangxi, Shandong, Shanghai, and Zhejiang provinces), northern (Beijing, Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, and Gansu provinces), southern (Guangxi and Guangdong provinces), and southwestern China (Sichuan and Guizhou provinces).16 Eligible individuals aged ≥40 years were identified through local resident registration systems. Between January 2011 and December 2012, 193,846 individuals were recruited and followed until November 30, 2021. The median follow-up time was 10.1 years (IQR: 9.8–10.3).

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao-Tong University School of Medicine (ethical approval number: 2021280). All study participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrollment in the study.

Method details

Assessment of residential greenness

We assessed greenness through the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), a widely used satellite-image-based vegetation index.38 NDVI ranges from −1.0 to 1.0, with higher values indicating greater levels of greenness. Negative values often indicate blue space or water, and pixels with negative values were excluded from the analysis. We obtained NDVI measurements from the Moderate-Resolution Imaging Spectro-Radiometer (MODIS) on the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s Terra Satellite. By linking NDVI imagery to the longitude and latitude coordinates of each residential address, we calculated the greenness levels within a radial buffer of 500 m, which provides a meaningful representation of nearby green spaces.39 We also analyzed different buffer sizes (250 m and 1,250 m).

Based on previous studies,16,40 we evaluated three measures of greenness exposure: (1) contemporaneous NDVI, reflecting contemporaneous exposure; (2) one-year NDVI, reflecting relatively short-term exposure; (3) cumulative NDVI, reflecting long-term exposure. Contemporaneous NDVI was defined as the NDVI value at the time closest to a CVD event, or the last interview date for those who did not have an event.16 The average duration between the event date and the contemporaneous NDVI measurement was 2.3 days. One-year NDVI was calculated as the average NDVI value over the year prior to a CVD event or the last interview.40 We chose one-year NDVI as the metric for relatively short-term exposure, because it showed the strongest protective association with incident CVD compared with 2-year and 3-year average NDVI (Table S21). Cumulative NDVI was defined as the mean of all NDVI values measured over the follow-up period,16 spanning from the baseline interview (2011–2012) to the date of CVD onset or the most recent follow-up interview. The mean duration of follow-up was 9.8 years (median, 10.1 years).

Calculation of Life’s Essential 8 score

Life’s Essential 8 is a composite index calculated based on eight metrics of health behaviors and health factors. According to the definition proposed by AHA in 2022,22 we scored the CVH score based on 8 components, including 2 domains of health behaviors (nicotine exposure, diet, physical activity, sleep) and health factors (body mass index (BMI), blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure). Each of the 8 components is quantified on a scale of 0–100, based on established thresholds and guidelines, with higher scores indicating more favorable health conditions. The overall CVH score is the unweighted average of the individual component scores, also ranging from 0 to 100. Detailed methods and scoring criteria of CVH were presented in Table S22. The health behavior, health factor, and overall CVH scores were calculated as the average of 8 component metric scores. Higher scores indicated more favorable CVH conditions. Participants with high CVH scores of 80–100 were considered optimal CVH, while medium CVH referred to 50 to 79 and low CVH of 0–49.

Covariates selection

In this study, covariates including demographic factors (age, gender), socioeconomic status (urban/rural residence, geographical region of residence, local gross domestic product per capita (GDP), education level, marital status and occupation status), and lifestyle factors (smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, diet habits). Covariates were selected based on previous study findings.10,13,17 Established risk factors for CVD, as well as factors known to be associated with greenness exposure, were considered as potential confounders and thus included in the adjustment set. Multicollinearity among the covariates was limited (Table S23). We additionally adjusted for hypertension and diabetes in the sensitivity analysis. Local GDP data were obtained from government administrative sources, with low GDP defined as lower than the average in 2011 (36,262 yuan). Education level was classified into three categories as primary school or less, middle school, and high school or above. Marital status was categorized as married or not married (single, divorced, separated, or widowed). Occupational status was classified as employed, or unemployed/retired. For lifestyle factors, physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF).41 Habitual dietary intake was assessed using a semi-quantitative Chinese Food Frequency Questionnaire, recommended by the National Institute for Nutrition and Health of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention.42 This questionnaire recorded the frequency and portion size of typical food items consumed during the previous 12 months. A diet score was calculated according to the recommendation of the American Heart Association and was reported to associate with health outcomes in Chinese.35,43,44

Based on previous study findings, we included three environmental exposures as potential effect modifiers, including air pollution, artificial light at night (ALAN), and heatwave.

Air pollution was quantified as the average particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 μm (PM2.5) concentrations for the year before the occurrence of a CVD event or the last interview, at a spatial resolution of 1-km.2 In detail, the hourly PM2.5 in situ observations were obtained from the China National Environmental Monitoring Center and measured using the tapered element oscillating microbalance or the β-attenuation method. Calculations of daily PM2.5 were estimated based on satellite data at a 1-km2 resolution using space–time extremely randomized tree models, which can be obtained from the ChinaHighPM2.5 dataset (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html).45 The dataset has been rigorously validated, with sample-based 10-fold cross-validation showing good agreement between daily estimates and ground-based measurements (R2 = 0.86–0.90; RMSE: 10.0–18.4 μg/m3; MAE: 6.4–11.5 μg/m3) across different years at the national scale. The model also demonstrated strong predictive performance for historical periods, achieving an R2 of 0.80–0.82 for monthly to annual estimates.45 Participants were allocated PM2.5 values utilizing a nearest-neighbor matching methodology based on their residential addresses. Since the residential addresses of participants were unevenly distributed across the country, it was possible that there were no meteorological stations around the addresses, leading to insufficient environmental data. Therefore, the use of a high-quality meteorological and atmospheric reconstructing dataset could effectively solve the problem.

Annual exposure to ALAN from 2011 to 2021 was derived from the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (NPP-VIIRS)-like Nighttime Light data from a cross-sensor calibration,46 with a resolution of approximately 500 m. Validation against composited NPP-VIIRS data showed excellent agreement at the pixel level (R2 = 0.87, RMSE = 2.96 nW cm−2 sr−1) and city level (R2 = 0.95, RMSE = 3024.62 nW cm−2 sr−1). We used the average ALAN values for the year prior to a CVD event occurring or the last interview conducted.

We also assessed heatwave days from 2011 to 2021. Data of daily maximum temperatures in each year were retrieved from the ChinaHighTEMmax dataset,47 and were interpolated to 1 × 1 km separately for assessment at each participant’s residential address. This daily maximum temperature (Tmax) dataset was validated against observations from over 2,400 meteorological stations using 10-fold cross-validation, showing high accuracy (R2 > 0.98, RMSE = 1.49°C). According to previous studies, we defined a heatwave as a daily maximum temperature exceeding the warm season (1 May to 30 September) 92.5th percentile for at least three consecutive days, and we calculated the total heatwave days for the year prior to a CVD event occurring or the last interview conducted.

Assessment of CVD events

Morbidity and mortality data up to November 30, 2021 were obtained by linking the unique identification number of each participant to the local death registries of the National Disease Surveillance Point System, the registries of cardiovascular diseases, and the National Health Insurance System. Two members of the outcome adjudication committee, blinded to baseline characteristics, independently verified each clinical event. Discrepancies were adjudicated through discussion involving other members of the committee. Incident CVD was defined as a composite outcome, including non-fatal myocardial infarction (I21-I22), non-fatal stroke (I60-I64, I69), and cardiovascular death (I00-I99). When necessary, additional medical documentation was retrieved to support adjudication, including electronic or paper copies of outpatient and emergency visit records, hospital discharge summaries, laboratory and imaging reports, pathology reports, operative notes, and death certificates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables or numbers with proportions for categorical variables.

We used Cox proportional hazard models to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of incident CVD events, myocardial infarction, and stroke associated with NDVI exposure. For CVD mortality, we used Fine-Gray hazard regression models to estimate HRs and 95%CIs, accounting for the competing risk of non-CVD deaths. Person-years were calculated from baseline until the date of diagnosis of incident CVD events, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Covariates included age, gender, urban/rural residence, local GDP, geographic region, education level, marriage status, occupational status, smoking status, drinking habits, physical activity, and diet score. To assess potential nonlinear relationships between greenness and CVD risk, exposure-response curves were fitted using restricted cubic splines with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of NDVI.

We conducted subgroup analyses to assess effect modification by age, gender, education levels, physical activity levels, residence areas, geographic areas, and local GDP. Additionally, we examined stratification by PM2.5, ALAN, and heatwave days. To assess the heterogeneity of effect, we performed likelihood ratio tests by comparing models with and without an interaction term between the categorical factors and the continuous NDVI variable.

In the mediation analysis, we further evaluated the hypotheses linking greenness to cardiovascular health to estimate whether any proportion of the association was mediated by lifestyle, metabolic, or environmental factors. The mediation proportions and 95% CIs were calculated using the parallel mediation models (R package “bruceR”), in which all mediators were included simultaneously.

We used Cox proportional hazard models to assess the association between Life’s Essential 8 CVH categories and incident CVD. The models were fully adjusted for age, gender, urban/rural residence, local GDP, geographic region, education level, marriage status and occupation status. We also estimated the risk of CVD events in individuals stratified by both NDVI and CVH scores, in which we divided all participants into 10 categories as NDVI in quartile 4, and NDVI in quartiles 1–3 with 3 degrees of CVH (high, moderate, and low), with NDVI in quartile 4 as the reference group. We performed likelihood ratio tests by comparing models with and without an interaction term between the continuous CVH score and the continuous NDVI variable.

To examine the robustness of the study results, we performed several additional sensitivity analyses. First, we assessed the association between greenness exposure and CVD events using radial buffers of 250 m and 1250 m around each participant’s residential address. Second, we used the logistic regression model to assess the association between greenness exposure and CVD events. Third, to further test the causal effect of greenness on CVD events, we used a marginal structured Cox model coupled with inverse probability weighting within a causal framework.48 Fourth, we performed a sensitivity analysis using averages of NDVI over warmer seasons (from May 1st to September 30th). Fifth, we additionally adjusted for hypertension and diabetes. Sixth, we additionally adjusted for air temperature, humidity, and PM2.5. Finally, the proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and a time-dependent Cox model was fitted to assess potential time-varying effects of greenness.
