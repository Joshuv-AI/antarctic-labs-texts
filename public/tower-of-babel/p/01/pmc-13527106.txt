RTEDAP framework for real-time event-driven data aggregation and processing in tsunami early warning systems
Authors: M Umadevi, Dhanalakshmi Gopal, V S Nishok, J Dhanasekar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTsunami early warning systems are critical for minimizing the impact of seismic events on coastal communities. However, existing systems face challenges in terms of latency, scalability, and real-time data processing. This research introduces RT-EDAP (Real-Time Event-Driven Data Aggregation and Processing), a novel framework designed to address these issues by enhancing the efficiency of tsunami prediction and alert dissemination. The primary objective of this study is to develop a robust, low-latency, and scalable TEWS that can effectively handle multi-source data streams and deliver timely tsunami alerts. RT-EDAP utilizes Edge Computing and Stream Processing Frameworks, such as Apache Kafka and Apache Flink, to process data locally at edge nodes, reducing latency and optimizing network bandwidth usage. The event-driven architecture prioritizes computational resources for critical seismic anomalies, ensuring fast and accurate detection of tsunami events. The framework integrates real-time data from seismic sensors, tide gauges, and GPS, and employs lightweight edge models combined with centralized machine learning techniques, such as Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs), to improve event classification accuracy. The system’s performance is evaluated using key metrics, including latency, throughput, scalability, and prediction accuracy. The results demonstrate that RT-EDAP achieves high accuracy (95%) and low processing latency (50-60ms), outperforms traditional methods, and can scale to handle high event rates. In conclusion, RT-EDAP is a scalable, fault-tolerant solution that enhances tsunami early warning systems by improving real-time data processing and response times, offering a significant advancement in disaster management capabilities.Keywords: Tsunami early warning system, Real-time data processing, Data aggregation, Stream processing frameworks, Event-driven architectureSubject terms: Evolution, Climate sciences, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards, Ocean sciences

Introduction

One of the most devastating natural disasters that have been causing havoc to the coastal areas, especially in tectonically active areas like the Pacific Ring of Fire, is Tsunamis1. Tremendous waves caused by earthquakes may surge with terrifying heights in just minutes after seabed shaking occurs, and there is hardly any time to evacuate the area, as well as respond to the emergency2. Past occurrences such as the 2004 Indian Ocean and 2011 Tohoku tsunamis reveal that any little delay in warning relay can end up causing disastrous human and infrastructural deaths3. The tsunami early warning systems (TEWS) have to therefore function within stringent real-time limitations and be able to effectively process a heterogeneous data stream that can be generated by seismic sensors, ocean-bottom pressure gauges, tide gauges and GNSS stations4.

The current TEWS methods can be categorized into broad groups of numerical (physics-based) methods and methods that rely on data, each having its own benefits as well as drawbacks5. Numerical models are based on governing equations which may be a formulation of shallow water equations, a Boussinesq formulation, or a solver that uses the Navier-Stokes equations to model tsunami generation, propagation and inundation6. These models offer good physical interpretation and high predictive accuracy especially in inundation mapping and hazard assessment7. Recent innovations have enhanced the calculating speed by shifting CPU to GPU and high-performance computing optimization8. With these improvements however, numerical models are still computationally expensive and may need the centralized supercomputing resources and restrict their applicability to ultra-low-latency, real-time decision making in near-field tsunami events9.

Conversely, machine learning and data-driven approaches have been attracted by the capability to make superfast predictions on the basis of past and real-time sensor information10. Approaches like recurrent neural networks, temporal convolutional networks, ensemble learning, and multi-source observations can be integrated with hybrid AI models, allowing the rapid inference and are capable of inference on multi-source observations11. They are especially useful in the early warning situations because these methods are highly effective in the detection of anomalies and short-term forecasting12. However, data-driven methods are assumed to be less physically interpretable, less dependent on data quality and coverage, and less applicable to unknown seismic conditions13. Additionally, most of the implementations are still based on centralized processing pipelines, which create bottlenecks in communication and single points of failure when subjected to heavy event loads.

Although both types have improved the level of tsunami detection and forecasting, they have major weaknesses when used in real-time working conditions14. Latency and scalability are a limitation of numerical models, and data-driven model have a weakness in robustness, integration, and prioritization of events15. Above all, the currently available systems are mainly focusing on model accuracy as opposed to the system architecture and provide little support to dynamic resource allocation, fault tolerance and real time processing of high velocity data stream generated during seismic crisis16. These restrictions point to the requirement of an architectural change towards distributed, event-driven, and edge-enabled systems that can be used to achieve both rapid analytics and scalable data processing17.

This study will suggest a solution to these problems; RT-EDAP is a low-latency and scalable framework specifically created to support tsunami early warning systems. RT-EDAP uses edge computing as a local preprocessing method, distributed stream processing as a real-time processing method, and event-driven prioritization as a resource allocation method to high-priority seismic anomalies. The framework allows the decoupling of data ingestion, processing, and analytics, thus allowing the simple addition of lightweight edge models and central machine learning models, including Temporal Convolutional Networks, with minimal end-to-end latency. Related works

Studies of tsunami early warning systems have taken several complementary paths, such as the development of numerical physics-based modeling, data-driven and signal-based methods, development of system-level communication and sensing. Although these research works have made a major breakthrough in terms of tsunami detection and prediction, they also indicate the existence of perennial flaws in terms of real time scalability, integration and architectural integrity.

Numerical and physics-based tsunami modeling

Most operational TEWS are based on numerical models because they are highly physically interpretable and have the capability to model the development, propagation, and impact of the tsunamis. Other studies like that of Benazir and Oktari18 use the nonlinear shallow water equations and finite-difference to predict the arrival time of the tsunami in different earthquake conditions and the results are useful in evacuation planning. Likewise, Mori et al.19 show that network of dense ocean-bottom-based pressure gauges along with numerical and analogue modeling prove to be effective tools to predict tsunami within three minutes in Japan.

Takahashi et al.20 discuss CPU-to-GPU migration strategies to solve the computational demands of large-scale simulations: these strategies can reduce the time spent on the model by multiple orders and maintain the model fidelity. These methods of high-performance computing also enhance access to forecasts, but require sophisticated infrastructure and so are not applicable to near field, low-latency warning situations. Moreover, numerical models are accurate but, in many cases, do not have the flexibility necessary to process heterogeneous high-velocity data stream in real-time.

Data-driven and signal-based detection approaches

The use of data-driven approaches has become popular because it can provide quick forecasts based on machine learning and signal processing algorithms. Dharmawan et al.21 compare different variants of the recurrent neural network in predicting near-field tides and show that models based on GRU can predict near-field tidal waves with high precision in less than a few seconds. These methods are very effective in detection that is time critical, but are usually limited in training data heterogeneity and low physical explicability.

Other modalities of sensing have been investigated as well. Xiao et al.22 explore the application of the Underwater Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS) technique with subsea optical fibers, especially its potential to detect the infragravity and tsunami waves on large but spatial scales. Similarly, Fuso et al.23 use GNSS-based Total Electron Content (TEC) perturbations and use machine-learning classifiers to discern tsunami-related ionospheric perturbation within 75 s of earthquakes. Although these approaches offer greater coverage to detection and speed, they generally exist as a lone entity and need to be more integrated into functional TEWS pipelines.

Communication, sensing, and system-level enhancements

A number of studies concentrate on enhanced data acquisition, data transmission, and dissemination instead of prediction on its own. In Indonesia, Ina-TEWS maritime VHF-based wireless communication suggested by Adityawan et al.24, as the alternative to the buoy-based systems, lowers signal propagation time to less than one minute. In the same manner, Park et al.25 propose a Hybrid Duplex Medium Access Control (HDMAC) protocol to streamline the use of heterogeneous under-sea data transmission by enhancing channel usage in real-time monitoring. The protocol improves channel efficiency with a group Automatic Repeat request (ARQ) and variable packet length.

The other works deal with sensor deployment and efficiency of data collection. Purnama et al.26 optimize the location of ocean-bottom pressure gauges with stochastic tsunami simulations and with empirical orthogonal functions providing a better early warning due to improved spatial coverage. The synthetic stochastic-slip tsunami simulations are utilized in lead-time analysis with the help of empirical orthogonal functions (EOF) to extract the high-energy spatial modes as well as analyze bathymetry for sensor placement, as proposed, to add 42 sensors so that warnings occur sooner. Sakalasuriya et al.,27 discuss data aggregation systems of swarm UAVs, which are proven to have a better sensibility as well as fault tolerance in large-scales. Despite increasing reliability and coverage, these studies are still mostly fragmented and do not deal with real-time data processing and intelligent event prioritization.

Real-time and data-driven tsunami forecasting methods

A number of more recent papers have also concentrated on speeding up early warning of tsunamis by skipping the fully resolved physics based simulations in favor of other sensing modalities and data based surrogates. Kadri et al.28 propose an international real-time system of tsunami warning in which the intensity of tsunamis is determined by studying acoustic signals caused by tsunamis through hydrophone devices, and the quicker speed of acoustic waves is used to provide timely evaluation. This scheme shows potential real-time performance without pre-calculated scenarios though performance drops in the far field location and in regions where the land is separated. To supplement this signal-based paradigm, Mulia et al.29 take advantage of dense offshore observation networks and machine learning in order to directly estimate the inundation of coastal tsunami, and their results are as accurate as numerical models with a comparable reduction in computational cost by a factor of nearly two.

Similarly, de la Asunacion et al.30 use deep learning surrogates, trained on extensive tsunami simulations, to quickly compute alert and inundation levels, and can infer thousands of cases in less than one second. Taken together, these investigations point to an evident trend of transition to the latency-conscious, data-driven forecasting of tsunami, which should focus on the speed, the feasibility of its operational implementation, and the quantification of the uncertainty. They are however normally implemented as independent predictive modules and do not have an integrated real-time processing infrastructure that can dynamically combine heterogeneous data streams, prioritize critical events and scale to distributed computing environments, which inspires the development of an event-driven structure like the suggested RT-EDAP.

Comparative analysis and research gap

Table 1 provides a comparative overview of some representative tsunami detection and early warning methods with their methodologies, major findings, and limitations inherent to them. All of these works have shown a high level of advancement in numerical correctness, sensing versatility, and communication effectiveness. However, they mostly pay attention to the components of systems and not the end-to-end real-time processing architectures. Numerical models have a problem of latency and scalability, data-oriented methods have robustness and combination issues, system wide improvements do not have smart coordination with streams of data.

Unlike earlier works, the suggested RT-EDAP framework is at the forefront of the state of the art in that it proposes an event-driven, architecture-driven approach towards tsunami early warning. Instead of enhancing one of the existing models or sensing approaches, RT-EDAP combines edge computing, a distributed stream processing, and dynamic event prioritization to enable the aggregation and processing of multi-source data streams in real-time. This architectural change is a direct response to the constraints noted in the current numerical, data-driven, and system-level frameworks, which allows scaling, fault tolerance and low-latency early warning of tsunamis at a scale appropriate to operational use.

The works listed in Table 1 enumerate the different advancement in tsunami early warning systems and the various detection methods available. Studies consider ocean-bottom pressure gauges, Underwater Distributed Acoustic Sensing, GNSS-based TEC detection, and more complex computational modeling approaches. The improvements are faster warning, better placement of sensors, and better prediction using machine learning and hybrid protocol. Methods of RNN models, EOF analysis, and code migration based on directive show more innovative approaches towards tsunami detection and response. The major drawbacks persist, including maintenance needs, restricted observation areas, and dependency on infrastructure. Together, these studies present positive steps in using advanced technology for tsunami preparedness with the stresses of further applications in real environments, larger fields, and complete integration systems designed to overcome a wide range of challenges in coastal safety.

Proposed RTEDAP framework for tsunami early warning systems

This study suggests a novel framework for the development of efficient Tsunami Early Warning Systems using an event-driven, Edge-Computing, Real-time Stream Processing, and Machine-Learning technologies shown in Fig. 1. Information collection is the first step of the system that mainly involves getting data from seismic sensors, tide gauges, and the GPS. Low power consuming end devices like the NVIDIA Jetson Nano process this data locally where noise is filtered, data is normalized, and TCNs are used to identify anomalies. This edge-level processing lowers latency since it limits the forwarding of the raw data to other centralized servers. The pre-processed data is then transferred to the central processing unit in real-time and for the minimum fault-tolerant level through using Apache Kafka. Apache Flink is used for the distributed stream processing whereby data collected from different locations can be aggregated and synchronized. This makes all inputs synchronized temporally to permit right event analysis. In the centralized system, efficient TCN models take the summarized results to label an event as tsunami or non-tsunami. For the critical events, the system sends out the alarms with certain information like the level of intensity, geographic location and time to mess up. These are to the disaster management authorities; public warning systems and the emergency response teams. To achieve scalable and efficient early warning, continuous feedback is used to update the models, enhancing the prediction of the system, tsunami impact, and enhancing reliability and robustness of the framework.

Proposed system architecture.

Data gathering

The proposed research uses the Global Historical Tsunami Database through Kaggle which publicly is a collection of over 2,259 tsunami related events since 2100 BC to the present across the Pacific, Indian, and Atlantic Oceans, as well as the Mediterranean and Caribbean Seas31. The dataset includes significant areas of oceanic regions and this includes the Pacific, Indian, and Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean and Caribbean seas. Entry includes spatiotemporal information, seismic features as well as tsunami-specific ones. There is the EVENT_VALIDITY field which shows the degree of reliability that is attached to an event that has been recorded. To develop a model, events which were labeled as “Valid, Probable or Questionable” were used, and records which were labeled as Very Doubtful were not used in the training process and only used in analysis of robustness. After elimination of incomplete or inconsistency of the entries, about 1800 events were chosen to undergo a detailed analysis. To estimate a real-time multimodal sensing setting, features were grouped in three major sources of data. The features derived by seismic sensors (about 50%) are earthquake magnitude, the focal depth, the location of the epicenter, the event cause, and the time features like year, month, and day. The features of Tide gauge (approximately 30%) are depicted by the tsunami intensity indicators, inferred sea-level changes, and coastal impact descriptors which reflect the behaviour of tide fluctuations. The ground displacement features (average of 20%) are based on spatial deformation indicators, nearness to the tectonic fault zone, and movement-sensitive geographical descriptors that are deduced by historic data. To assure generalization, the dataset was split into three subsets 70% one of which was used in training, 15% in validation and the final 15% in testing that covered a wide scope of earthquake magnitude, tsunami strength and geographic location. This configuration allows strong model training and assessment in various seismically active regions in the world.

Nevertheless, neither raw sensor observations nor raw historical event records are present in the above dataset but mostly structured historical events and derived features. In order to overcome this limitation and provide the validation to the real-world conditions, more real-world sensor time-series datasets are used, but only in validation purposes. These consist of sea level tsunami wave data of DART buoy stations. (21418, 21413)32 obtained via Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission Sea Level Monitoring Facility, tidal gauge records of coastal sea-level, and ground displacement time-series records of GNSS stations supplied by UNAVCO (Hanakai and Ayukawa)33,34. These are datasets that are only used in validation but not in model training.

Validation set assists in tune of the threshold and refine the model and test set is used to evaluate the final performance. The inherent multimodal structure of this representation is what allows the RT-EDAP framework to easily learn both temporal dependencies and cross sensor interactions of interest to tsunami generation and early warning.

Edge devices

To minimize tsunamis risks, IoT sensors are installed in edge devices located near seismometer installations, tide gauge installations, GPS monitoring sites, and other risky regions. Seismic, tide gauge and GPS data instruments are gathered by these devices on individual and real-time basis so there will be no delay in identifying existence of any anomalies. These edge devices are comprised of Light Weight hardware with the ability to deliver Local Data Processing. The study employs the NVIDIA Jetson Nano as the main edge device for real-time processing tasks owing to its low energy consumption and novel GPU design, as well as compatibility with retrained small-learning networks. The device is used in locations that are close to data sources for example seismometers, tide meters and GPS units with the objective of minimizing latency and performing local data analysis. It runs at power levels of 5–10 W, which make it ideal for use in remote regions. Preprocessing is done locally by Jetson Nano while anomalous events are detected and unimportant events removed before the system sends important events to the central system. Data is pre-processed to clean it and normalize it for further analysis directly from the raw data collected from seismic sensors, tide gauges, and GPS devices. This step takes place on edge devices such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano in a bid to relieve the central system of much work.

(e) Preprocessing of real sensor time-series data

The validation sensor images take a form of real-world sensor images which are pre-processed through a very basic though organized preprocessing pipeline to make them compatible with the trained model but without removing the temporal nature of the data. Because the datasets are not homogeneous, having different sampling rates and noise characteristics, a similar pattern of preprocessing is used by all sensor modalities. First, the temporal alignment is done by resampling all sensor data to a shared resolution of 1-minute intervals by using the linear interpolation method. The missing values arising as a result of telemetry gaps are filled in by the use of linear interpolation where there are short gaps and forward filling where there are isolated missing points. To mitigate noise, low-pass moving averages with window sizes of 3 to 5 samples are used in noise reduction of DART buoy pressure data and tide gauge sea-level data since high-frequency variation has been observed to overwhelm tsunami waveforms. Data of GNSS displacement are rather low-frequency but are smoothed with a simple moving average filter in order to eliminate noise in measurements. In order to give uniformity between the heterogeneous sensor units (pressure, sea level and displacement), all of the time-series are normalized to a range of [0,1] using Min-Max scaling. At last, the processed signals are cut into fixed-length temporal windows in order to fit to the input demands of the Temporal Convolutional Network model.

Event-driven architecture

In this context, events are considered as critical phenomena sensed by the system, including earthquakes or rising sea levels. In the context of disaster prediction systems, EDA is critical in managing data-intensive tasks while incurring minimal latency response time, resource provisioning, and delivering timely alerts during disaster incidents.

Real-time data aggregation

The aggregation of multiple sources of data in real-time is an important requirement for the processing of data streams for early warning of the occurrence of tsunamis. The aim is to guarantee that data originating from several sensors like the seismic sensors, tide gauge, and GPS is acquired, timestamped and analyzed in real time in the best way. This makes it possible to perform precise analysis and detection of events, thus comprising effective issuance of tsunami warning.

Stream processing framework

Due to the tremendous amounts and velocities of the real-time data coming from the seismic, tide, and GPS sensors, the proposed stream processing framework is developed. It services also guarantee that the data is not processed with a lot of delay and that the sources are consistent to support the integrity of the event detection.

Apache Kafka (Message Broker)

Kafka plays the role of an efficient, resilient real-time data feed for gathering and processing data in huge volume35. The physical structure of the data acquisition system that consumes data from several seismometers, tide, GPS sensors and transfers it to the subsequent data processing section. Kafka is designed to guarantee the delivery of the high volumes of data while providing fault tolerance and handling data flow in case of system failure. Kafka ensures that the system can handle the stream of data and if it there is a large influx of data the system will be capable of scaling to serve all the data without failure. Kafka is a data storage system that allows topic-based publishing, replication across nodes for durability and availability and provides partitioned data for scalability and parallelization.

Apache Flink (Distributed Stream Processing)

The RT-EDAP is to work with minimal effectiveness in the case of multi-high-magnitude or close-space seismic events. Event prioritization is a process that assigns dynamic priorities to every identified event, using earthquake magnitude, population density that is exposed, and regional vulnerability to make sure that priority is given to critical events first. The redistribution of computational resources to high-priority events in real time can be performed by dynamically assigning the use of computational resources to the adaptive resource-mapping function. Scalability is also improved by distributed processing where edge nodes do local anomaly detection to minimize the load on central processing and enable simultaneous processing of multiple events. As it is configured, the system can support a maximum of about 50 processing nodes and a combined throughput of about 5,000 events per second without this affecting the end-to-end latency which should not exceed 100 ms. At the limits of the system, load balancing and queuing systems maintain continuity in the generation of critical alerts. Detection is also reliable in the case of multi-sensor fusion and distributed stream processing even in situations of cascading events.

RT-EDAP is implemented to be resilient with partial sensor failures, network delays and communication outages. Kafka-based data replication ensures fault tolerance because incoming data streams are replicated across the multiple nodes to ensure that information is not lost in case of failure. Flink check pointing is used to enable stateful streaming as well as to provide quick recovery when an interruption happens so that operation can be continued. It uses multi-sensor fusion and combines seismic, tide-gauge, and GPS streams, such that when one source fails or lags, detection of events is not interrupted. Dynamic reallocation of the resources shift the computational capacity to high-priority events in case of partial failures. In addition, automated fallback alerts can be used so that warnings can be provided using sensor inputs that are available, and the level of confidence should be adjusted to represent the data availability. All these measures combine to guarantee high levels of real-time Tsunami early-warning even in the face of extreme and degraded operating conditions.

Results and discussion

The demonstration of the potential of the proposed Tsunami Early Warning System with emphasis on real-time data from seismometers, tide gauges and GPS to effectively detect tsunami generating events and categorize them appropriately. The measures in evaluating the system are; the accuracy of event classification and the time interval between the occurrence of the event and the issuance of the alert. By using the concept of edge-level processing and integrating Apache Kafka, Apache Flink, and TCN, efficient, reliable, and low-latency predictions of tsunamis are shown in the work. Further, the system provides a mechanism for the immediate communication of an alert to those authorities that will help to remediate any disaster that may occur.

Traditional tsunami early warning systems based on the use of centralized processing and numerical models normally take several to tens of minutes to provide an alert, especially through the aggregation of data, model execution, and human-in-the-loop decision-making processes. Conversely, the suggested RT-EDAP model shortens the warning latency by doing anomaly detection and initial classification on the edge, low-latency stream processing, and automatic decision-making. The response to event detection results in end-to-end alert generation in about 50–60 ms, which allows much earlier situational awareness than is possible under traditional architectures. In addition to the magnitude of the earthquakes, the suggested system takes into account other focal properties including the focal depth, the epicenter location, the type of fault, the vertical movement of the seafloor, and the patterns of tectonic stress accumulation. These characteristics are better at detecting tsunamis. Based on the geographical position of the epicenter and the depth, the system automatically classifies between offshore and onshore earthquakes, and sends alerts to only events that have a high potential of generating a tsunami. Such an automated method does not require the input of a user and enhances classification and false alarms.

The suggested RT-EDAP model uses deduced tidal and GNSS displacement characteristics based on previous tsunami and earthquake history to simulate multimodal live sensor feeds. Although real time series of operation of tide gauges, DART buoys, or GNSS stations were not provided, the synthesized streams maintain the statistical and time-based features of the measured tsunami-generating phenomena. This method enables the realistic analysis of processing at the edge level, TCN-based anomaly detection, and the transmission of alerts without contradiction to the constraints of publicly available historical data.

End-to-end computational latency of RT-EDAP framework was tested based on emulated real-time streams based on historical tsunami events, simulation of sensor acquisition and telemetry under realistic operation conditions. Stage latency The measurements of stage latency show that the processing is efficient: Data Aggregation 10-20ms was required to integrate seismic, tide, and GPS streams; Central Processing 30-40ms to classify and estimate the likelihood of an event; Alert Generation needed 5-10ms to create notifications; and Alert Dissemination needed 5-10ms to send alerts to stakeholders. The cumulative latency of 50–60 ms proves that the framework is able to provide timely warnings with minimal computation delay. Given that the travel time of the tsunamis of offshore sources can vary between several minutes to hours, this low-latency aspect performance guarantees significant time to react to the emergency with meaningful lead time to evacuate. Active management of resources at edge nodes and central nodes give the high-priority events more priority again, ensuring a quick response during concurrent seismic events, as well as emphasizing the real-time nature of the framework.

Seismic, tide-gauge and GPS data streams employed in the present study are obtained by means of Global Historical Tsunami Database (Kaggle), which contains more than 2,259 historical events in the world since 2100 BC till the current time. Valid, Probable, and Questionable events were kept to train and test the model, whereas the Very Doubtful events were removed or used to test the robustness. In order to test the RT-EDAP framework in real-time, historical data was simulated as a real-time stream, and the event sequences were replicated through replaying the sequence of events based on their initial time sequence. The method allows evaluating system latency, detecting anomalies, and generating events based on the occurrence of an event and maintaining realistic temporal relations. The multimodal record is organized with seismic records (~ 50%) single magnitude, depth, and epicenter coordinates of a tsunami; the tide-gauge records (~ 30%) of sea-level variation and the intensity of a tsunami and the GPS records (~ 20) of ground movement and the proximity of faults. In this arrangement, both accuracy and real-time responsiveness on the early-warning situation can be tested.

The Global Historical Tsunami Dataset that has been used in the study has the events spread in the Pacific Sea, Indian Sea, Atlantic Sea, the Mediterranean and the Caribbean Sea. Due to this global coverage, the dataset has an inherent bias of including tsunami events that occur in tectonically active Pacific-margin environments, including subduction dominated plate boundaries, and also events occurring in other oceanic and coastal environments. The suggested RT-EDAP framework is not based using region- specific rules or tectonic classifications. Rather, any event is processed with the help of shared focal and spatial characteristics, such as magnitude of earthquakes, depth of focal, and offshore or onshore rupture location. This was a feature-based method, which enables the model to acquire generalized tsunami-relevant patterns across different geographic areas represented in the data set.

TCNs have unique strengths when compared to LSTM, GRU, or Transformer-based models when used in early-warning of tsunamis. TCNs use causal and dilated convolutions, which can be conveniently computed concurrently over time, so the inference latency is much lower (50–60 ms) than sequential LSTM /GRU-based models. To illustrate this point, CNN-LSTM and CNN-RNN models have 85% and 88% prediction accuracy at latencies of 100 ms and 120 ms, respectively, whereas hybrid SVM-LSTM and Random Forest models have even higher latencies (150–200 ms) and lower prediction accuracy. Connections between layers in TCNs provide robust training and inference of long-run length sequences like seismic, tide and GPS data, without vanishing or exploding gradients which frequently occur in recurrent networks. TCNs are also more interpretable because the scores of anomalies and convolutional filters can be plotted to discover the most important temporal patterns, in which alarms are raised.

This is contrasted to most of the previous studies that focus only on the prediction accuracy, but this will instead be a combined measure of prediction performance and end-to-end computational latency, which is the key criterion of operational tsunami early-warning systems. The elimination of the latency in RT-EDAP is mainly by applying TCNs that use parallelizable causal and dilated convolutions rather than recursive operations. In comparison to LSTM- and GRU-based models, which are plagued with their inherent sequential dependencies and expensive gate computations, TCNs can be used to perform faster inference and they are more scalable within the limits of real time. Transformer-based models have a high power but involve extra overhead on attention mechanisms, in addition to quadratic complexity with regard to sequence length and are not low-latency edge deployable. On the system level, RT-EDAP uses event-based and edge-enabled architecture where anomaly detection and initial classification are done at the edge devices. That reduces the amount of data sent and only events flagged are sent to the cloud to be fused and end-to-end alert generation is maintained at 100 ms even with multi-event loads. In comparison with centralized systems (like GREAT v1.0), RT-EDAP is a complementary real-time decision layer that has been optimized to local and regional tsunami warning in a short time.

Real-world alert system representation and validation

This reported detection accuracy (95%) has been obtained by a temporally stratified test set containing 20% of the filtered historical events so that the data of any one event would only be present in either the training or the test set. This method maintains time-based relationships and is close to real-life environments of deployment. The RT-EDAP framework makes use of multimodal characteristics, such as magnitude and depth of earthquakes, fluctuations of the tide-gauge, and ground displacement via GPS, enabling the framework to become sensitive to the fine-tuning of the indications of tsunamis. The importance of features analysis shows that the most influential predictors are the earthquake magnitude, shallow focal depth, and rapid sea-level changes. The suggested RT-EDAP framework is planned to be as close as possible to the operational cycle of the actual tsunami early warning systems. Its design is in the form of the standard functional pipeline used by the national and regional warning centers where sensor analogies are captured, real-time events are detected, centralized decision-making, and alert dissemination occurs. The real-time operational conditions of existing tsunami monitoring networks are simulated by generating data streams that are processed in near real time by seismic sensors, tidal gauges and GPS sensors. The alert generation in RT-EDAP is probabilistic and triggered by events as opposed to rule-based.

After a critical event has been detected, an alert message is automatically created with the approximate severity of the event, geographic area of impact, and projected time-to-impact. These notifications are arranged in such a way that they contribute to the dissemination via the existing mechanisms like disaster management authorities, emergency response units, and the official warning systems that are in tandem with the current tsunami warning methods. The validation of a system is carried out with the help of the replay of historical tsunami events as a stream of data ordered in time. This streaming emulation maintains realistic sensor timing and sequence of events and inter sensor dependencies, enabling evaluation of both accuracy of detection and responsiveness of operation. The processing latency (50–60 ms) reported is the time (excluding tsunami propagation time) taken by the computation involving event detection and alert production. This low-latency response, when coupled with physical wave travel times, which may take a few to several minutes or hours depending on the source distance, makes it possible to achieve significant gains in warning lead time by the populations in the coastal areas. Overall, the RT-EDAP framework has shown operational feasibility by incorporating real-world alert logic, realistic data flow and measurable performance metrics, and therefore is applicable to practice in the real-world deployment of the tsunami early warning system. Comprehensively, RT-EDAP is novel not only in its incremental improvements in accuracy, but also in its comprehensive real-time architecture, integrating low-latency TCN-based modeling, edge-cloud interaction, multimodal fusion, and scalable event processing. All these aspects make RT-EDAP viable, operationally more applicable to the current tsunami early-warning systems, especially in cases where quick reaction and computing power are crucial.

Discussion

The proposed Tsunami Early Warning System reveals over twofold enhancement through edge computing with Apache Kafka and Flink stream processors and Temporal Convolutional Networks. The key findings presented focus on the system’s efficiency in detection (95%) of tsunamis, and a modular structure allowing the handling of up to (5000 events/second) while being fault-tolerant.

The reported latency values of 50–60 ms correspond specifically to the end-to-end computational processing latency within the proposed RT-EDAP framework. This includes edge-level preprocessing (noise filtering, normalization, and anomaly detection using TCN), real-time data aggregation via Apache Kafka and Apache Flink, centralized event classification, and alert generation. These latency measurements do not include external system delays, such as sensor data acquisition latency, telemetry transmission delays, communication network overhead, or real-world alert dissemination time (e.g., mobile networks, satellite communication, or public warning infrastructure). These external factors are highly dependent on deployment environments, network conditions, and hardware configurations, and are therefore considered beyond the scope of the current simulation-based evaluation. The primary objective of this work is to evaluate the computational efficiency and architectural performance of the RT-EDAP framework under controlled conditions. By isolating processing latency, the study demonstrates the capability of the proposed system to support ultra-low-latency decision-making, which can significantly reduce overall response time when integrated with optimized communication and alerting infrastructures in real-world deployments. In comparison to the existing methods CNN-LSTM and CNN-RNN, the proposed system can predict the required data more accurately several times with minimum latency, which in turn is beneficial for efficient disaster management. The integration of edge-level processing helps to improve system efficiency, timely identify anomalies and distribute workload, which will be less demanding for centralized servers.

The initial testing was made to be based on a 60 min simulation window to create a balance between computational loads and to illustrate the functionality of the real time system to typical coastal hazards. In the case of faraway earthquakes, the structure relies on the predicted time-to-impact, which is calculated through the distance to the epicenter and the velocity of the tsunami wave, to stretch the monitoring and alerts timelines. This is a dynamic adjustment where all coverage is guaranteed without affecting the low latency performance of the RT-EDAP model. The solution enables deployment on a scale to numerous coastal areas and remain real-time responsive, ensuring successful implementation of both nearshore and offshore tsunamis.

Some of the benefits of the proposed work include fast data rates, which will be essential for tsunami early warning, and real-time performance. Further, the geographic distribution of the system’s components improves scalability and increases the ability to handle faults. Nevertheless, some limitations are the following they include the fact that this system depends on the quality of the sensor attached and in case the attached sensor gives wrong readings then the system is bound to give wrong results. Nevertheless, the work can be considered to be a major improvement on current systems, by pointing the way to improving the breadth and speed of data analysis and decision-making in the case of tsunami early warning systems.

Despite the performance of the RT-EDAP framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. The primary dataset used in this study, the Global Historical Tsunami Database, mainly consists of structured historical records and derived features rather than raw real-time sensor streams, which may limit the ability of the model to fully capture complex temporal dynamics observed in real-world scenarios. Although simulated real-time streams and selected sensor datasets were used for validation, the framework has not yet been extensively validated on continuous large-scale real-time sensor deployments. Additionally, the system assumes relatively stable data acquisition and communication conditions, whereas practical environments may involve sensor noise, missing data, network delays, and partial system failures, potentially affecting performance. The use of predefined thresholds (e.g., magnitude-based filtering) may also introduce sensitivity trade-offs, leading to possible false positives or missed detections under varying seismic conditions. Furthermore, while the framework demonstrates low computational latency, the overall operational latency may vary in real deployments due to external system dependencies such as telemetry and communication infrastructure.

Conclusion and future scope

This work proposes RT-EDAP as an innovative approach to address latency and scalability challenges, which are critical in real-time disaster warning frameworks especially in Tsunami early-warning systems. The framework efficiently leverages edge computing, stream processing frameworks including Apache Kafka and Apache Flink, and event-driven designs to process data locally in edge nodes hence improving system responsiveness. When sensor data from seismic sensors, tide gauges or GPS is received by RT-EDAP, by utilizing efficient lightweight edge models in conjunction with sophisticated centralized deep learning algorithms such as TCNs, are able to provide timely and accurate tsunami warnings. This proposed system only focuses on important seismic anomalies, which leads to less time spent on processing and more efficient use of time, and resources which enhances the disaster response time and accuracy. Based on evaluation outcomes, high prediction accuracy (95%) and low latency (50–60 ms) prove the hypothesis that the proposed system can be used as a robust, fault-tolerant system for real-time tsunami prediction and early warning scalable to a large number of nodes.

Further studies can be oriented towards enhancing the stability of the proposed RT-EDAP in different climatic environments, different levels of seismicity, and non-uniformity of the sensor data. It will be also beneficial to include even more complex anomaly detection methods such as deep learning ones for increasing the accuracy of identified anomalies. Furthermore, enhancing the system with other kinds of sensors or more complex data sources, e.g., ocean sensors, may enhance recognition performance. For example, the gain of more streamlined resource usage in large scale adoption may prove useful in the future concerning improving the system’s adaptability to more geographically spread areas. Implementation in realistic settings and ongoing evaluation will confirm the applicability of the framework in real-world disaster response situations and make the framework more responsive to future advancements in the disaster management domain.
