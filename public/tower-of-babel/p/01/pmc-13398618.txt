Graded traumatic brain injury severity differentially modulates microglial and astrocytic polarization states and response to minocycline
Authors: Yan Yu, Chen Wang, Jinyu Zhu, Jiahui Du, Hao Li, Shuyi Ning, Rui Yang, Yiwen Ma, Mengyuan Yuan, Wei Shen, Huailin Wang, Huihui Jiang, Liping Chen, Haitao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic brain injury (TBI) involves complex secondary injury cascades in which neuroinflammation is a prominent driver. The lack of standardized models capturing a spectrum of injury severities has hindered a systematic understanding of the associated cellular and molecular responses. This study aims to systematically characterize the dynamic responses and phenotypic shifts of neurons, microglia, and astrocytes during the acute and subacute phases following TBI of varying severities. By integrating macroscopic histopathological assessments with microscopic cellular analyses and correlating these with early peripheral biomarker changes, we seek to provide a solid experimental foundation for understanding TBI mechanisms and developing severity‐stratified diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.MethodsMale mice were randomly assigned using a computer‐generated randomization sequence to the following experimental groups: Sham group, mice that underwent only craniotomy (n = 10 per group); Mild group, mice with a 0.5 mm depth impact on the right motor cortex (n = 10 per group); Moderate group, mice with a 1.0 mm depth impact on the right motor cortex (n = 10 per group); Severe group, mice with a 2.0 mm depth impact on the right motor cortex (n = 10 per group). On Days 1, 3, 7, and 14 after injury, tissue damage was assessed using Nissl staining; anxiety‐like behavior was evaluated using the elevated plus maze; cognitive function was assessed using the Y‐maze test; motor function was evaluated using the open field test, balance beam test, rotarod test, and gait analysis; neuronal apoptosis and glial cell polarization levels were assessed using immunofluorescence staining; and changes in peripheral serum markers were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (Elisa). In addition, severely injured mice received minocycline treatment (45 mg/kg, n = 12 per group) from 30 min to Day 3 post‐injury, while the control group received the same volume of saline. For comparisons between groups at a single time point, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used. For comparisons between groups across multiple time points, two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was applied.ResultsImpact depth was directly correlated with histopathological lesion volume and dictated the trajectory of functional recovery. Motor deficits and neuronal apoptosis scaled with injury severity. The neuroimmune response was severity‐dependent: mild TBI triggered a transient, reparative response dominated by M2 microglia and A2 astrocytes. In contrast, severe TBI provoked an early and sustained pro‐inflammatory state, characterized by persistent M1 microglial and neurotoxic A1 astrocytic activation (n = 4 per group, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, severe injury led to significant acute elevations in serum interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and ubiquitin carboxy‐terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1) within 6 h post‐injury (n = 3 per group, p < 0.0001). Minocycline treatment attenuated neuroinflammation, improved motor function, and promoted a shift in microglial polarization toward the protective M2 phenotype (n > 4 per group, all p < 0.05).ConclusionOur findings establish that TBI severity is a critical determinant of the post‐injury neuroimmune microenvironment, with severe injuries driving a maladaptive, chronic inflammatory response. This graded model provides a robust framework for identifying severity‐specific biomarkers and validates the rationale for developing precision immunomodulatory therapies stratified by injury severity.Keywords: biomarkers, glial polarization, neuroinflammation, severity stratification, traumatic brain injury

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI), caused by external mechanical forces, leads to structural and functional impairment of brain tissue. As a leading cause of death and disability among neurological disorders worldwide, TBI poses a significant socioeconomic burden, with estimated annual global losses reaching $400 billion. 1 Pathophysiologically, TBI progresses through two distinct phases: primary and secondary injury. 2 The primary injury occurs at the moment of impact, directly causing cerebral contusion, Blood‐brain barrier disruption, 3 , 4 vascular rupture, and mechanical axonal stretching and disruption. 5 , 6 The subsequent secondary injury unfolds over hours to months as a complex cascade of metabolic disturbances, ionic imbalances, oxidative stress, and widespread neuroinflammation, characterized notably by progressive neuronal death 7 and sustained neuroimmune activation. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Thus, TBI is not an isolated acute event but a dynamic and complex pathological process involving multiple cell types and molecular pathways. 12 Since primary injury is largely irreversible, current research and therapeutic strategies focus predominantly on modulating the secondary injury phase.

Clinically, TBI severity is classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on the Glasgow Coma Scale score, duration of unconsciousness, and imaging findings. 13 , 14 , 15 This stratification is crucial for guiding diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. However, in basic research, particularly in widely used rodent models, a standardized experimental definition aligning with clinical mild, moderate, and severe TBI classifications is lacking. This gap significantly hinders in‐depth investigation into severity‐specific pathological mechanisms, biomarker identification, and targeted therapies. It remains unclear how TBIs of differing severities trigger distinct cellular and molecular responses that lead to divergent functional outcomes.

Several experimental rodent models are utilized to simulate human TBI, with controlled cortical impact (CCI), fluid percussion injury (FPI), 16 and weight‐drop injury (WDI) 17 , 18 are the most commonly used. 19 The CCI model, characterized by precise control over impact parameters, high inter‐experimental reproducibility, and its capacity to mimic key pathological features of human closed‐head injury, is regarded as particularly suitable for investigating TBI pathophysiology across a spectrum of severities. 20 , 21 , 22

TBI elicits complex responses from neurons and glial cells in the central nervous system. Their activation patterns, which vary with injury severity, critically influence neurological recovery. At the neuronal level, injury effects are graded: mild TBI may cause limited cell death but still induce significant dendritic spine loss, synaptic dysfunction, and network disruption. 23 More severe trauma triggers excitotoxicity, where excessive glutamate release over‐activates postsynaptic AMPA/NMDA receptors, leading to abnormal Ca2+ and Zn2+ influx, activation of hydrolases, and ultimately neuronal apoptosis or necrosis. 24 Recent studies in severe CCI‐TBI models highlight the role of mitochondrial dysfunction; for instance, lactate modification of Tufm, a key mitophagy regulator, observed at 6 h post‐injury, suppresses mitophagy and exacerbates mitochondrial failure and neuronal apoptosis. 25 However, how TBI severity differentially regulates the dynamics of key subcellular processes remains poorly understood.

As central immune effector and support cells, glial cells are a double‐edged sword. 26 Microglia and astrocytes play decisive roles in balancing secondary damage and endogenous repair. Microglia polarize into pro‐inflammatory (M1‐like) or anti‐inflammatory/reparative (M2‐like) phenotypes. 27 , 28 Recent evidence indicates that a specific C5aR1+ microglial subset amplifies inflammation via NF‐κB signaling, promotes neurotoxic A1 astrocyte polarization through LGALS9‐CD44 interaction, and recruits neutrophils via CCL3/CCL5 secretion, collectively worsening injury. 29 Moreover, studies have shown that TNF‐α released by activated microglia induces Müller cells to secrete CCL2, which in turn promotes the activation and migration of microglia, thereby aggravating retinal neuroinflammation and retinal ganglion cell damage in glaucoma. 30 Astrocytes undergo reactive gliosis, adopting either neurotoxic A1 or neuroprotective A2 phenotypes. While acute inflammation aids debris clearance, chronic non‐resolving neuroinflammation driven by persistently activated microglia and astrocytes is a key factor in long‐term neuronal damage. 31 , 32 A critical area requiring investigation is how the severity of traumatic brain injury precisely governs the spatiotemporal dynamics of microglial and astrocyte polarization, and how these differential cellular responses ultimately determine the resolution of neuroinflammation and the quality of tissue repair.

Animals were housed at the Animal Center of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences with four to five mice per cage under standard conditions: ambient temperature of 23 ± 2°C, humidity of 55% ± 5%, and a 12/12‐h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animal experiments and related procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (Approval No.: IACUC‐DWZX‐2023‐553).

Controlled cortical impact (CCI)

Mice were weighed and anesthetized via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (6 μl/g body weight). Anesthetized mice were fixed on a stereotaxic frame. The scalp was disinfected with 75% ethanol and iodophor swabs, incised surgically, and the eyes were covered with erythromycin ointment‐coated filter paper to prevent light damage. An incision was made along the sagittal suture, and the periosteum was gently retracted using cotton swabs to dry tissue fluid. A circular craniotomy (approximately 2.0 mm diameter) was performed over the right motor cortex, taking care not to damage the dura mater. The CCI injury was induced using a pneumatic impactor with the following parameters 20 : impactor tip diameter, 2.0 mm; impact velocity, 5.0 m/s; dwell time, 1.0 s. Injury severity was varied by adjusting the impact depth, and male mice were randomly assigned using a computer‐generated randomization sequence to the following experimental groups (n = 10 per group):

Mild: The impact depth is 0.5 mm.

Moderate: The impact depth is 1.0 mm.

Severe: The impact depth is 2.0 mm.

Severe: The impact depth is 2.0 mm.

Sham: Underwent identical surgical procedures, excluding the impact. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36

After CCI or sham surgery, mice were placed on a heating pad for recovery before being returned to their home cages. All time points are expressed as days Post‐injury (dpi).

Nissl staining and lesion volume analysis

To assess cortical lesion volume at various time points post‐TBI, Nissl staining was performed using a commercial kit. Briefly, frozen brain sections (n = 4 per group) were thawed at room temperature (RT) for 30 min, rinsed three times with 0.01 M PBS, and air‐dried. Sections were stained with approximately 100 μL Cresyl Violet (Nissl) stain for 1 min, followed by differentiation. Sections were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 75%, 100%, 2 min each), cleared in xylene (2 min), and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured using a Tissue Genetic imaging analysis system.

Lesion volume was calculated by measuring the area of the intact tissue in the ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres on serial sections using TissueFAXS Viewer (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria, www.tissuegnostics.com). Lesion volume was determined as: (Contralateral hemisphere volume ‐ Ipsilateral hemisphere volume)/Contralateral hemisphere volume × 100%. 37

Open field test

Locomotor activity was assessed using the Open Field Test at 1, 3, 7, and 14 dpi (n = 8 per group). Individual mice were gently placed in the center of an open field arena (50 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm). Their activity was recorded for 10 min using AnyMaze behavioral tracking software. The arena was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues, as described previously. 38

Balance beam test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using the Beam Walking Test at 1, 3, 7, 14 dpi (n = 10 per group) and after minocycline treatment, as previously described. Mice were trained for 2 days prior to testing. A wooden beam (1 cm diameter, 60 cm length) was positioned horizontally 50 cm above the ground. A bright light was placed 20 cm from the start point, and a dark box was placed at the opposite end to motivate the mouse to traverse the beam. The time taken to cross the beam was recorded over three trials. The beam was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials.

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and endurance were assessed using the Rotarod test (n = 10 per group). Mice underwent 2 days of acclimatization training (4 trials per day, 30 min intervals) prior to testing. The Rotarod apparatus was set to accelerate from 4 to 40 rpm over a 5 min period. The latency to fall was recorded, with a maximum cutoff time of 300 s. The rod was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials. 39

Catwalk gait analysis

Gait parameters were analyzed at 1, 3, 7, and 14 dpi using the Catwalk XT system. 40 Following parameter configuration, mice were placed at the entrance of a glass‐walled walkway, and their gait was recorded using the Visugait software. Multiple consecutive step cycles were recorded for each mouse, and parameters were averaged for analysis (n = 10 per group).

Cardiac perfusion and brain tissue sectioning

Mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg, i.p.). Transcardial perfusion was performed sequentially with saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post‐fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h, then cryoprotected in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions. Coronal sections (30 μm thickness) were prepared using a cryostat.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining procedures were performed in accordance with methods previously described in the literature. 41 Frozen sections were thawed at RT, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 1% PBS‐Triton X‐100 (PBST). After washing, sections were blocked with 3% BSA at RT, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing and warming to RT, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies. Following secondary antibody incubation and washing with 0.5% PBST, sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium containing DAPI. Images were acquired using a laser scanning FV‐1200 confocal microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan, www.olympus-lifescience.com). Groups of four mice were used. Four serial brain sections per mouse, selected at representative anatomical landmarks, were quantitatively analyzed to ensure consistency and reproducibility across samples.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cytokine levels were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (n = 3 per group). Briefly, standards and samples were added to the pre‐coated plate and incubated at 37°C for 90 min. After washing, biotinylated detection antibody was added and incubated at 37°C for 60 min. Following further washes, HRP‐streptavidin conjugate was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After a final wash step, TMB substrate was added, and the reaction was incubated for 15 min at 37°C before stopping with stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated using CurveExpert 1.4 software (www.curveexpert.net).

Minocycline treatment

Mice were randomly assigned using a computer‐generated randomization sequence to the following four groups (n = 12 per group):

Sham + Saline: Received only saline treatment without impact.

Sham + Saline: Received only saline treatment without impact.

Sham + Minocyline: Received minocycline treatment without impact.

Sham + Minocyline: Received minocycline treatment without impact.

TBI + Saline: Received only saline treatment with an impact depth of 2.0 mm.

TBI + Saline: Received only saline treatment with an impact depth of 2.0 mm.

TBI + Minocyline: Received minocycline treatment with an impact depth of 2.0 mm.

TBI + Minocyline: Received minocycline treatment with an impact depth of 2.0 mm.

Minocycline hydrochloride (45 mg/kg) or an equal volume of sterile saline (vehicle) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 30 min post‐injury, followed by once‐daily injections for two additional days at 24‐h intervals. 38 , 42 Behavioral tests and tissue collection were performed at 3 dpi.

Y maze

The Y‐maze test was used to assess the learning and memory abilities of mice in each group at 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 dpi (n = 7 per group). The three arms of the Y‐maze were designated as the starting arm, the old arm, and the novel arm. During the training phase, the mouse was placed in the starting arm, with the novel arm of the Y‐maze kept closed to prevent entry, while the old arm was open, allowing the mouse to freely explore the starting arm and the old arm for a total duration of 15 min. During the testing phase, the mouse was again placed in the starting arm of the Y‐maze, with both the old arm and the novel arm simultaneously opened, permitting the mouse to choose between entering the old arm or the novel arm for a total duration of 5 min. The movement trajectories of the mice in the old arm and the novel arm were recorded using behavioral analysis software.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze was used to assess anxiety‐like levels in mice from each group at 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 dpi (n = 7 per group). Mice were moved to the experimental room 2 h in advance to acclimate to the environment. Each mouse was placed in the central area at the intersection of the closed and open arms, facing one of the open arms (with mice in the same experimental batch facing the same open arm), and allowed to freely explore the elevated plus maze for 10 min. Behavioral analysis software was used to record the mouse's activity.

Western blot

The damaged brain tissue of the mouse was collected and lysed according to the instructions of the NE‐PER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents kit to separate the nuclear protein and cytoplasmic protein (n = 4 per group). Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; cat. 23227). The proteins were then subjected to SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA; cat. IPFL00010). After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies and then for 2 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Visualization was achieved using enhanced chemiluminescence (Applygen Technologies, Beijing, China; cat. P1000), and the bands were detected with the MiniChemi610 Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Beijing SageCreation, Beijing, China). The grayscale value of protein bands was measured using ImageJ software version 9.0 (Wayne Rasband, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; https://imagej.net).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk and Levene's tests. For TBI groups of different severities involving multiple time points, Nissl staining, behavioral tests, immunofluorescence staining, and ELISA were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. In contrast, western blot, which was examined only at a single time point, was analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. For the minocycline treatment group, as only a single time point was assessed, Nissl staining, behavioral tests, immunofluorescence staining, and ELISA were all analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Additionally, the beam balance test, rotarod test, and gait analysis were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All procedures from group assignment through data collection to analysis were conducted under blinding.

RESULTS

Impact depth determines the severity and temporal dynamics of traumatic brain tissue damage

To quantitatively evaluate the pathological consequences across varying impact depths, Nissl staining was performed on brain tissues from experimental cohorts during acute (1 and 3 dpi) and subacute (7 and 14 dpi) phases. Analysis revealed progressive exacerbation of tissue loss with increasing impact depth (Figure 1A–C), confirming depth‐dependent injury severity. Furthermore, distinct temporal patterns of tissue repair were observed across groups subjected to different impact depths. Specifically, compared to the Sham group (n = 4 per group, 0.018 ± 0.012%), the 0.5 mm impact group exhibited the least degree of tissue loss, which peaked at 3 dpi and showed no significant deficit by 7 dpi (n = 4 per group, 2.011 ± 1.403%, p < 0.0001, Figure 1B,C). In contrast, the 2.0 mm impact group displayed the most severe damage, with tissue loss being most pronounced at 7 dpi and significant recovery beginning only by 14 dpi (n = 4 per group, 25.780 ± 5.078%, p < 0.0001, Figure 1B,C). The 1.0 mm impact group demonstrated an intermediate level of tissue loss, which was most severe at 3 dpi and showed no significant difference from baseline by 14 dpi (n = 4 per group, 10.930 ± 5.390%, p < 0.0001, Figure 1B,C). These data establish that impact depth dictates distinct spatiotemporal patterns of neuropathology. Consequently, injury models induced by 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mm impact depths were operationally defined as mild, moderate, and severe TBI, respectively, for subsequent analyses.

Motor dysfunction correlates with the severity of TBI

Long‐term neurological dysfunction is a hallmark clinical sequela of TBI, with its underlying complex pathophysiological mechanisms often leading to impaired motor coordination and balance, thereby significantly affecting patients'quality of life. 43 To systematically evaluate the impact of TBI severity on motor function, we conducted a series of behavioral tests at multiple time points following injury (Figure 2A). Results from the open field test revealed that, compared to the sham group (n = 8 per group, 41.280 ± 2.260 m), the total movement distance in the mild TBI group did not differ significantly. In contrast, the severe TBI group exhibited a significant reduction in total movement distance, while the moderate TBI group showed a notable decrease in distance traveled on Day 1 dpi (n = 8 per group, Severe: 23.370 ± 2.162 m, p < 0.001. Moderate: 31.670 ± 3.023 m, p < 0.05, Figure 2B,C). In tests assessing motor coordination and balance, the beam walking test (n = 10 per group, Sham: 6.447 ± 0.206 s. Mild: 7.836 ± 0.241 s. Moderate: 10.300 ± 0.711 s, p < 0.0001. Severe: 16.090 ± 3.132 s, p < 0.0001, Figure 2D) and rotarod test (n = 10 per group, Sham: 294.700 ± 1.489 s. Mild: 285.200 ± 2.542 s.Moderate: 233.300 ± 11.490 s, p < 0.0001. Severe: 172.900 ± 21.110 s, p < 0.0001, Figure 2E) revealed that the mild TBI group did not exhibit significant differences in traversal time or rotarod retention time compared to the sham group. However, the moderate TBI group showed a marked increase in beam traversal time and a significant reduction in rotarod retention time (Figure 2D,E). The severe TBI group displayed more pronounced motor dysfunction, with further increases in beam traversal time and further decreases in rotarod retention time (Figure 2D,E). Notably, over time, both the moderate and severe TBI groups showed partial functional recovery (Figure 2D,E). Additionally, gait analysis using the Catwalk system revealed no significant differences in paw stride and forelimb stride width between the groups. However, during the acute phase of injury, the severe TBI group exhibited a significant reduction in hind limb stride width (n = 10 per group, Sham: 2.272 ± 0.488 cm. Severe: 2.183 ± 0.083 cm, p < 0.05, Figure 2F–J). In summary, these behavioral findings highlight a clear gradation in the relationship between TBI severity and motor dysfunction. More severe injuries result in greater motor impairment, and the recovery dynamics are closely correlated with the extent of the initial injury.

Neuronal damage and apoptosis increase with TBI severity

Cell death and neuroinflammation are pivotal pathological features following TBI. 44 , 45 To explore the cellular pathological mechanisms underlying the severity‐dependent behavioral deficits induced by TBI, we analyzed neuronal loss in the injured region and its proportional relationship with injury severity using immunofluorescence staining (Figure 3A,B). The results demonstrated that, compared to the sham group, TBI resulted in significant neuronal loss, with the extent of loss directly correlating with the severity of the injury. Specifically, the mild TBI group exhibited a marked decrease in neuronal count within 7 dpi, with no significant differences observed by 14 dpi (n = 4 per group, Sham: 1077.0 ± 3.4 cells. Mild: 683.1 ± 106.4 cells, p < 0.0001, Figure 3A,B). The moderate TBI group showed neuronal loss intermediate between the mild and severe groups, with partial recovery over time. In contrast, the severe TBI group displayed nearly complete neuronal loss, characterized by extensive and persistent damage (n = 4 per group, Sham: 1077.0 ± 3.4 cells. Moderate: 535.0 ± 96.2 cells, p < 0.0001. Severe: 356.1 ± 138.1 cells, p < 0.0001, Figure 3A,B).

Neuronal apoptosis is a common early event following TBI, and its extent is closely linked to injury severity. 46 , 47 To quantify this process, we performed immunofluorescence co‐staining for Cleaved Caspase‐3 and NeuN in the injured brain regions. The results revealed that the degree of neuronal apoptosis positively correlated with TBI severity, peaking at 3 dpi. In the sham group, almost no Cleaved Caspase‐3+NeuN+ neurons were observed (n = 4 per group, Sham: 0 ± 0%, Figure 3C,D). The mild TBI group showed a small number of apoptotic neurons, primarily located around the periphery of the injury site. In contrast, the moderate TBI group exhibited a significant increase in Cleaved Caspase‐3+NeuN+ neurons, while the severe TBI group displayed the most pronounced apoptotic signal, which reached its maximum at 3 dpi (n = 4 per group, Mild: 7.460 ± 1.142%, p < 0.05. Moderate: 16.090 ± 1.921%, p < 0.0001. Severe: 32.370 ± 8.104%, p < 0.0001, Figure 3C,D).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the extent of neuronal loss is closely associated with injury severity, highlighting its critical role in the pathological mechanisms underlying TBI‐induced neurological dysfunction. Moreover, our data confirm that neuronal apoptosis is a widespread early cellular response following TBI, with its intensity escalating in parallel with injury severity. These results provide a strong experimental foundation for future research focused on developing severity‐adapted therapeutic strategies that target specific apoptotic signaling pathways.

In the moderate TBI group, the number of Iba1+ cells began to rise significantly at 3 dpi and remained elevated into the subacute phase, accompanied by a shift toward an amoeboid‐like activated morphology (n = 4 per group, Sham: 213.3 ± 6.7 cells. Moderate: 824.8 ± 270.6 cells, p < 0.0001, Figure 4A,D). The severe TBI group exhibited the most pronounced increase in Iba1+ cells, along with typical activated morphological features (n = 4 per group, Sham: 213.3 ± 6.7 cells. Severe: 1133.0 ± 332.5 cells, p < 0.0001, Figure 4A,D). These findings suggest that mild TBI may induce a predominantly M2‐polarized immune response that gradually resolves, whereas severe TBI leads to an early surge and sustained dominance of the M1 phenotype, which may contribute to the development of a chronic inflammatory state.

TBI injury leads to activation of the NF‐κB signaling pathway in brain tissue

Dynamic changes in serum biomarkers following TBI of varying severity

The early clinical management of TBI presents significant challenges. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is susceptible to interference from factors such as alcohol, drugs, or shock, complicating accurate assessment during the hyperacute phase. 53 , 54 This limitation can lead to misclassification of mild cases and the potential omission of critical time windows for neuroprotective interventions in severe cases. To enable early, non‐invasive, and objective evaluation of injury severity, we collected peripheral blood via retro‐orbital bleeding at five key time points—6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post‐injury—and systematically analyzed the dynamic expression of multiple serum biomarkers (Figure 6A,B).

DISCUSSION

The pathological outcomes of TBI exhibit high heterogeneity, with injury severity being a critical factor determining the neuroinflammatory response pattern and long‐term functional prognosis. However, the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study, by establishing a severity‐graded CCI model, systematically revealed the dynamic evolution of neurons, glial cells, and peripheral biomarkers across the spectrum from mild reversible injury to severe persistent damage. Our core finding is that TBI severity precisely regulates the balance of the neuroimmune microenvironment: mild injury tends to induce a transient, repair‐dominated response‐predominant M2‐type microglia/A2‐type astrocytes, whereas severe injury leads to an early surge and sustained dominance of a pro‐inflammatory state‐M1‐type microglia/A1‐type astrocytes.

This polarization disparity is driven by distinct molecular initiators. In severe TBI, substantial DAMP release (HMGB1, ATP) activates microglial TLR4 and P2X7R, triggering NF‐κB and NLRP3 pathways to establish early M1 dominance. 44 Recent evidence indicates that EPHA4 promotes M1 microglial polarization post‐TBI by activating MAPK signaling and amplifying endoplasmic reticulum stress 57 ; Conversely, limited DAMP release in mild TBI favors anti‐inflammatory phenotypes. 58 Intercellular crosstalk further amplifies this imbalance. Severe TBI exhibits synchronized accumulation of M1 microglia and A1 astrocytes, where microglia‐derived IL‐1α, TNF‐α, and C1q drive neurotoxic A1 polarization. 50 Mechanistically, enhanced astrocytic USP11‐PGAM5 interaction promotes Drp1 dephosphorylation and mtDNA leakage, activating the cGAS‐STING pathway. This cycle amplifies neurotoxic astrocyte reactivity and recruits microglia via the CCL5‐CCR5 axis, thereby exacerbating secondary brain injury. 59 Nevertheless, these specific molecular mechanisms require further exploration and validation within our experimental system. Our results confirm that moderate and severe TBI can activate the NF‐κB signaling pathway, which may be the cause of polarization.

A significant strength of this study lies in addressing the limitations of previous CCI models, which often relied on single injury intensities or lacked standardization. Current severity definitions based on histology vary significantly across laboratories, 18 and while cerebral blood flow monitoring offers potential, equipment variability hinders consistent calibration. 33 , 60 , 61 To resolve this, we established and validated a reproducible tri‐graded‐mild, moderate, severe‐injury model based on quantifiable injury depth. This approach allowed us to establish a direct correlation between injury severity and the spatiotemporal dynamics of glial polarization, revealing a causal chain linking injury severity‐immune phenotype‐functional outcome. While TBI‐induced microglial activation is well‐known, 62 , 63 we demonstrate that injury severity acts as a critical switch determining the M1/M2 and A1/A2 balance. Specifically, severe TBI impairs early M2 polarization and drives A1 astrocyte aggregation in the subacute phase, providing a cellular explanation for the chronic neuroinflammation and failed repair seen in severe cases.

At the neuronal level, our results confirm a severity‐dependent increase in neuronal apoptosis. While this finding aligns with general knowledge in the field, this model provides a more refined spatiotemporal profile for this process. Recent studies indicate that in severe TBI, neurons may die through mechanisms beyond apoptosis and necrosis; for instance, one study found that neurons can recruit macrophages via CCL2 and activate the IL‐23R/JAK2/STAT3 pathway, thereby inducing ferroptosis. 64 The extensive and persistent neuronal loss observed in our severe TBI model suggests potential of multiple cell death modalities. Whether TBI of different severities preferentially activate distinct neuronal death pathways need further investigation.

For clinical translation, we characterized severity‐dependent serum biomarker dynamics within the hyperacute phase (≤6 h post‐injury), enabling early pathological stratification. The significant elevation of IL‐6 and UCH‐L1 in severe TBI supports their clinical utility in two key aspects. First, combined detection of IL‐6 and UCH‐L1, reflecting inflammatory intensity and neuronal injury, serves as an objective adjunct to the GCS score, particularly when GCS evaluation is confounded by sedation or intoxication. 53 , 54 Second, the sharp early rise of these biomarkers suggests that intensive monitoring and neuroprotective interventions, such as minocycline, should be initiated within the hyperacute window–6‐24 h to mitigate secondary injury. These findings provide a theoretical basis for shifting TBI management from “time‐based, empirical treatment” to “biomarker‐guided, precision‐initiated therapy.”

We acknowledge that the use of adult male mice, while minimizing variability, limits the generalizability of our findings. The exclusion of females omits a key biological variable, particularly given that sex hormones differentially modulate. 65 Additionally, young adult mice do not model the elderly TBI population, which exhibits higher baseline inflammation, diminished regeneration, and worse outcomes. 66 , 67 Future research must validate these findings in both sexes and aged models to ensure clinical translation. Furthermore, while we characterized parallel glial responses, mechanistic interplay—such as microglia‐astrocyte crosstalk via LGALS9‐CD44 or synaptic pruning—remains unexplored. Advanced approaches like single‐cell transcriptomics and spatial proteomics are needed to resolve this spatiotemporal coordination. Future work must also integrate cognitive and emotional behavioral tests, such as Morris water maze and Y‐maze, to define the links between neuroimmune polarization and functional outcomes. Finally, this study tested only minocycline; exploring novel agents like 3‐monothiopomalidomide, which may act independently of Cereblon to offer safer mechanisms, could leverage our polarization atlas to advance precision, stratified TBI therapies. 68 , 69 , 70

In summary, by constructing a severity‐graded TBI research framework, this study reveals that the differential regulation of the neuroimmune response is a core determinant of injury outcomes. These findings not only deepen the understanding of the heterogeneous pathological mechanisms of TBI, but also establish a solid theoretical foundation for achieving biomarker‐based early diagnosis and developing immunomodulatory therapies targeted to specific injury phases.
