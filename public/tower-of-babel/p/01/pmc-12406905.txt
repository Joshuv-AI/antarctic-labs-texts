Nurse-led cardiac rehabilitation programme on physical capacity and mental health for heart valve patients: study protocol of a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Juan Zhou, Yufeng Zhou, Yurong Huang, Pan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence-based early rehabilitation intervention after heart valve surgery has been proven to have many benefits, but the experience of implementing nurse-led early rehabilitation combining exercise and psychology is still lacking. It is urgent to find a more acceptable and cost-effective method to provide exercise intervention and psychological support. The aim of this study is to design a nurse-led cardiac rehabilitation programme to increase the physical capacity and mental health for heart valve patient based on broaden-and-build theory.Methods and analysisA non-blinded randomised controlled trial will be conducted. A total of 86 adults diagnosed with heart valve disease will be recruited and randomly assigned to the control group and intervention group. The recovery for valvular heart disease intervention, based on the broaden-and-build theory, will be guided one-on-one by a multidisciplinary team and will consist of two main components: physical intervention and psychological intervention. The baseline assessment will be conducted 1 to 2 days after admission, and sequent evaluations will be implemented at post-intervention, 3 months after intervention and 6 months after intervention. The primary outcome is the finding of six-min walk test and mental state. Other outcomes include quality of life, 36-item short-form health survey, Short Physical Performance Battery, stress, coping modes and social support.Ethics and disseminationThis study was conducted following the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the ethics committee of the Zhong Da Hospital, Southeast University (2024ZDSYLL098-P01). The results of this study are scheduled to be published in relevant peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberRegistered at the Chinese Clinical Trials.gov (ChiCTR2400090853).Keywords: Valvular heart disease, Cardiothoracic surgery, Nursing Care

Introduction

Valvular heart disease (VHD) is a significant public health concern, as it contributes to the decline in physical function, quality of life and lifespan. Globally, with the population ageing and the prolongation of the average lifespan, the disease burden of VHD is increasing exponentially.1 Patients with VHD require long-term medication and rehabilitation services, which puts an economic strain on the social healthcare system. In England, the management of symptomatic calcific aortic valve disease imposes substantial societal costs, estimated at £13 000 per patient over 5 years, and surgical aortic valve replacement incurs even higher costs over the same timeframe.2

Surgery is regarded as the most cost-effective intervention for the treatment of VHD; however, disruption of the integrity of the thorax, surgical time, mechanical ventilation, anaesthesia and cardiopulmonary bypass determine longer hospital stays with negative outcomes.3 The effect of cardiac rehabilitation (CR) has been confirmed to reduce the recurrence rate and mortality of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) by providing a comprehensive secondary prevention framework.4 Multiple guidelines5,7 have recommended CR as a strong CVD treatment. However, the current participation rate in CR among various patients with CVD ranges from 10% to 50%,8 9 and the overall participation rate in China is less than 10%.10 Objective challenges such as difficult transportation, insufficient rehabilitation facilities and space, and heavy economic burden are insurmountable obstacles for patients undergoing valve surgery to participate in CR programme in the short term. Under the circumstances, scientific and effective nursing intervention can serve as an alternative measure to prevent the decline in CR levels.11 Nurse-led rehabilitation programmes can provide ideas for improving patient recovery and addressing the shortage of rehabilitation therapists.12 With the cooperation of rehabilitation professionals, this approach will narrow the gap between supply and demand for rehabilitation services while addressing the lack of standardisation or detail in the implementation of nursing interventions.13

Evidence-based nursing practices have seen significant evolution, alongside the burgeoning concept of fast-track surgery, which is geared towards expediting the postoperative recovery timeline.14 Protocols and resources dedicated to facilitating early rehabilitation have gained traction because they promote several benefits, including enhanced physical function, reduced medical costs and all-cause mortality, and prevented immobilisation-related complications, ultimately improving patient care outcomes, alongside other pivotal benefits that cumulatively contribute to a more efficient recovery process. Some strategies have been explored to promote the recovery of cardiac patients.15 For instance, Yayla’s study highlighted the viability and benefits of early mobilisation in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery, including shorter duration of hospitalisation and fewer late complications.16 Sidik’s study showed that engaging in exercise training during the perioperative period significantly aids patients in enhancing respiratory function and is effective in reducing the length of hospital stay in postcardiac surgery.17

Anxiety and depression are typical but frequently undiagnosed psychological distress issues in many patients with VHD.18 Multiple studies have shown that patients who undergo open-heart surgery may require supportive treatment for symptoms of depression and anxiety.19 20 A randomised clinical trial showed that CR after heart valve surgery slightly improved physical function in the short term but did not appear to improve mental health.21 Another systematic review with an evidence map suggests that further research is needed to establish the safety of CR and its impact on patients’ emotional well-being.22 Obviously, surgery changes the patient’s disease pattern and brings predictable medical burdens; therefore, a comprehensive postoperative care plan is needed to help patients cope with potential physiological and psychological issues after surgery. However, few studies have focused on patients’ perioperative psychological status, which may result in a lack of intrinsic motivation for recovery. In addition, existing evidence does not directly indicate that exercise-based CR has an impact on patients’ primary outcomes (mortality, hospitalisation rate and health-related quality of life).23

Therefore, addressing the challenges of traditional centralised CR programmes, such as poor accessibility, limited resources, high costs and the absence of specialised interventions targeting the psychological distress of patients with VHD, this study proposes a nurse-led, convenient, economical and accessible study protocol to evaluate the short-term and mid-term effects of a ‘physical+psychological’ intervention on patients undergoing heart valve surgery, particularly on physical capacity (six-min walk test (6MWT)) (hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS), Chinese perceived stress scale (CPSS), medical coping modes questionnaire (MCMQ), social support questionnaire (SSQ), 3-item short-form health survey (SF-36)) and physical exploratory indicators.

Theoretical framework

The broaden-and-build theory indicates that positive orientation produces positive outcomes, such as certain positive experiences, and positive emotions (eg, contentment, joy, happiness and love), which not only expand an individual’s instantaneous knowing-acting abilities but also enhance their resources, such as physical strength, intelligence and social coordination.24 Positive emotions experienced by individuals can be accumulated and build up many personal resources, which facilitates the individual’s coping with dilemmas. Increased personal resources and effective coping, in turn, enable the individuals to generate positive emotions and a good mental state, thus improving the individual’s quality of life. According to the broaden and build theory, this study proposes a ‘Recovery for VHD’ (RVHD) rehabilitation nursing strategy based on such a virtuous circle, which reinforces positive cognitions and generates positive emotional experiences in patients with VHD (figure 1).

This strategy can stimulate patients’ motivation for recovery and thus improve the patient’s clinical recovery outcomes. Then, we reviewed the literature on early rehabilitation after cardiac surgery to identify the measures most likely to be effective in helping patients recover. Certainly, the construction of a scientific intervention also needs to include the opinions of stakeholders. We took semi-structured interviews with patients with VHD and nurses who provided care to determine the needs of users/implementers for such interventions and judge the feasibility of this. To make sure that all of the materials was included in the evidence-based intervention, we finally accepted the expert recommendations through expert meetings.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study is a two-arm, single-blinded, randomised controlled trial in clinical settings with patients with VHD (figure 2). Eligible patients who signed informed consent will be allocated to the intervention group or control group by using a random number. This study will be accomplished with six sessions of psychological intervention and six sessions of exercise interventions of one-to-one intervention. The primary outcome (6MWT and HADS) and secondary outcomes (SF-36, Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), stress, coping modes and social support) will be collected at baseline, 3- and 6 months post-intervention (table 1). This protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines (figure 2).25 26

If patients decide to participate in the study, they will be asked to return their informed consent form.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated based on the results of the 6MWT from a published study,27 the 6MWT distance of patients with VHD who received the intervention was 319±48.9 and the control group was 286.6±62.7, assuming that α=0.05 and 1-β = 0.9. According to the formula n1 = n2 = 2 * [(μα + μβ)/ (δ/σ)]2 + 1/4μα,2 a total of 68 patients with VHD will be needed. Assuming an attrition rate of 20%, 86 participants will be required.

Randomisation

Patients with VHD who fulfil the eligibility requirements will be randomised 1:1 to either the intervention or control group, and a computerised random number generator will be used to assign a sequence number to each patient. A single-blind design was used because of the research design. Patients were unaware of which group they were in. The data analysers and collectors were not engaged in the creation of the allocated serial numbers or in carrying out the intervention in order to lessen the possibility of measurement bias. Additionally, neither the writers nor the participants know one another.

Intervention

The intervention group

Participants in the intervention group will receive an RVHD intervention administered by our multidisciplinary team. Prior to the first intervention, we will explain the goal of the research and our programme to the participants and gather signed informed consent. The RVHD intervention is composed of two complementary parts, physical intervention and psychological intervention. The physical intervention will adopt the approach of progressive individual training, including five stages: pre-rehabilitation, bed rest period, sitting period, standing period I and standing period II, and each stage has corresponding measures and exercise programmes (table 2). Given the potential risks and individual differences among patients with heart valve disease, the regimen developed in this study strongly emphasises the supervision of exercise intervention. All exercise training will be conducted under medical supervision by a multidisciplinary team consisting of rehabilitation therapists with CR qualifications, nurses and cardiac surgeons. Monitoring indicators include immediate symptoms (chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, etc), heart rate, blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation, ECG, Borg RPE (rating of perceived exertion) score, etc (online supplemental file 1). Once patients have achieved stable physical function, are familiar with the exercise programme and can effectively self-monitor symptoms and heart rate, they may gradually transition to a partially supervised mode under the strict assessment and approval of a multidisciplinary medical team. In this model, patients also need to strictly follow individualised prescriptions and record daily exercise logs (including exercise type, duration, subjective fatigue, heart rate and any symptoms that arise). Any adverse events or changes in symptoms should be reported immediately.

Psychological intervention spans two stages: hospitalisation and home rehabilitation. Through preliminary interviews and literature review, we found that patients prefer an intervention frequency of once a week (63.6%) and 4–6 weeks (54.5%). Therefore, the programme includes 6 weekly sessions, one topic per week, each session lasting about 40 min: the first session will take place 1–2 days after admission, the second session will take place 1–2 days after surgery, the third session 1 week apart from the second and the subsequent sessions are separated by 1 week until the sixth session.

Psychological interventions during hospitalisation are primarily conducted by a nurse with a psychological counselling certification and a psychological counsellor in a one-to-many format. In addition to allowing patients to discuss their current emotional state, the programme uses games to alleviate psychological stress. At the end of each intervention, there is a question-and-answer session, video playback and feedback to enhance patients’ understanding of the psychological intervention. During the home rehabilitation phase, the WeChat platform sends video courses at scheduled times. The platform automatically generates attendance rates and session durations. If a patient does not complete the course within 48 hours, a nurse will conduct a follow-up phone call. Researchers review patients’ emotional logs weekly to promptly identify abnormal psychological states and intervene, with feedback and adjustments made during the next intervention session (table 3).

The control group

The control group will accept routine care of cardiac surgery, including preoperative/postoperative guidance, cardiopulmonary training (deep breathing and balloon blowing), diet guidance, early getting out of bed, oscillating expectoration, limb pressure, etc. The control group received no psychological intervention. Following data collection and analysis, we made the RVHD available to the control group so they might potentially benefit from the programme in the same way.

Data collection

A trained nursing graduate student who will explain this study’s purposes and programmes for the eligible participants will collect the data. Demographics (age, occupation, education, etc) and disease characteristics (blood pressure, left ventricular ejection fraction, B-type natriuretic peptide, etc) will be collected only at baseline. At four different time points—baseline (T0), post-intervention (T1), 3 months after the intervention (T2) and 6 months after the intervention (T3)—physical variable (6MWT), psychosocial variables (HADS, CPSS, MCMQ, SSQ, SF-36) and physical exploratory indicators will be regularly collected (table 1).

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

6MWT

The 6MWT will be carried out in compliance with guidelines.28 For 6 min, the participants strolled at their own pace along a 30-metre walking track. The post-rehabilitation measurement minus the baseline measurement was used to compute the primary outcome, with a positive number indicating improvement.

HADS

The initial edition was compiled by Zigmond and Snaith29 and was translated into Chinese by Ye.30 It has been used primarily to screen for anxiety and depression in general hospital patients. The HADS contains 14 self-assessment items in two dimensions: seven items for depression and another for anxiety. It employs a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 to 21, with 0 to 7 denoting normal, 8 to 10 denoting mild anxiety or depression, 11 to 14 denoting moderate and 15 to 21 denoting severe.

Secondary outcomes

Medical Outcomes Study item SF-36

It was developed from the MOS short form,31 which was used to evaluate patients’ quality of life. The SF-36 contains 36 self-assessment items in eight dimensions: physical function, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social function, role-emotion and mental health and, another indicator, health transition. The final score was determined by averaging the scores across all eight dimensions, with a higher score indicating a better quality of life.

CPSS

It was compiled by Cohen32 and was translated into Chinese by Yang and Huang. It has been used primarily to measure an individual’s perception of stress. The CPSS contains 14 self-assessment items in two dimensions: loss of control and sense of tension. It employs a five-point Likert-type scale. The score for each dimension ranges from 0 to 56, and higher total scores indicate higher levels of perceived stress. The Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.74.

MCMQ

It was developed by Feifel33 and was translated into Chinese by Yang and Huang. It was used to assess the patient’s coping strategies. The MCMQ contains 20 self-assessment items in three dimensions: confrontation, avoidance and acceptance-resignation. It employs a four-point Likert-type scale (1, ‘never’, to 4, ‘always’). The score for each dimension ranges from 20 to 80. The higher the dimension score, the more likely the patient is to use the coping style. The Cronbach’s α of the three dimensions was 0.69, 0.60 and 0.76.

Social support rate scale (SSRS)

It was developed by Xiao.34 The SSRS contains 10 self-assessment items in three dimensions: objective support, subjective support and utilisation of social support. A higher score indicates higher levels of social support. The scale exhibited commendable reliability and validity, and it can accurately assess the social support available to clinical patients.

Physical exploratory indicators

Systolic blood pressure at rest and maximal, heart rate at rest, exercise duration, SPPB and NYHA grade were among the physical exploratory outcomes. By using the Borg RPE scale and heart rate, the intensity of the mobilisation exercises was assessed.

Safety considerations

This intervention will be undertaken by a multidisciplinary team, including experienced nurses, resident doctors, physiotherapists and physicians. The team will strictly follow the initiation and termination criteria of the intervention (online supplemental table 1). Any exercise involving exercise in this study (Table 2) is done by the patient wearing a portable supervision and is fully supervised by a nurse, physician or physiotherapist. To evaluate the safety of RVHD, adverse events were tracked in line with the international principles of Good Clinical Practice.35 All adverse events related to the intervention were recorded by the nurse, and any adverse events that occurred after discharge were self-reported by the patient to the nurse via WeChat 6 months after discharge.

Compliance was evaluated by recording the number of physical sessions and psychological sessions completed. It was determined that at least 80% (four out of five) of the physical session and at least 83% (five out of six) of the psychological session consultations would constitute the predetermined per-protocol level of intervention adherence.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be conducted using the SPSS 23.0 statistics programme. The computer will be equipped with EpiData 3.1 software to input all of the acquired data, and logical error correction and consistency checks will be used to guarantee data accuracy. A significance level of p<0.05 was deemed statistically significant. The measurement data that conform to normally distributed data are expressed as mean and SD, non-normally distributed data are expressed as median and interquartile spacing and the enumeration data are expressed as frequencies and component ratios. The t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test for quantitative variables and X2 tests for categorical variables will be applied to compare the homogeneity of the two groups. The efficiency of the RVHD was confirmed using repeated-measures analysis of variance. Furthermore, missing data are practically a given. At the beginning of the study, we will establish a trusting relationship with patients with VHD to reduce the loss of follow-up, and the questionnaire will be carefully checked for errors and omissions by the researchers. A small proportion of missing values was filled by conditional mean completer. When the proportion of missing values is large, the appropriate interpolation method will be selected according to the characteristics of missing data, and the sensitivity analysis will be carried out.

Discussion

The current CR focuses on improving the cardiopulmonary function of patients with VHD and helping them adapt to postoperative life. Traditional CR is based on evidence-based development of a rigorous exercise prescription that includes aerobic training, strength training (resistance training), flexibility training, coordination training and balance training, which has been shown to play a tremendous role in patient recovery.7 The implementation of these pieces of training must rely on sophisticated medical monitoring equipment and professional CR teams, which means advanced supporting facilities must be in place and more costly. However, in China, inadequate medical facilities, lack of professional rehabilitation personnel, inconvenient transportation and overrun budget are all obstacles to CR for patients with VHD.36

Telerehabilitation can serve as a supplement or alternative to traditional centre-based CR, enabling patients to access rehabilitation opportunities at minimal economic cost.7 Studies by Antoniou et al37 38 have demonstrated the feasibility and safety of telerehabilitation in patients with coronary heart disease. However, to our knowledge, there are currently few online and offline combined interventions that integrate exercise and psychological support for patients after heart valve surgery. This is the first attempt to demonstrate the effectiveness of an intervention programme in improving the physical fitness and psychological health of Chinese patients with VHD and will also test additional variables. The advantages of this intervention are shown in the following aspects: first, due to safety considerations, most CR starts 1 month after surgery. Ennis39 et al verified that CR exercise training that began 2 weeks after surgery was as effective and safe as beginning 6 weeks. Therefore, we extended the front of CR to the first day after surgery, to seek practical evidence of early rehabilitation. Second, this intervention is informed by the broaden-and-build theory and combines physiological and psychological intervention. Physiological intervention can increase patients’ positive emotional experience, and the positive emotions experienced can be superimposed and build many personal resources. Psychological intervention can increase the motivation for rehabilitation, and the two cooperations can promote patients’ rehabilitation. Third, this protocol was developed based on a scientific process: not only on a literature review but also on the recommendations of stakeholders, so it is more in line with the clinical implementation environment in China. Finally, compared with traditional CR programmes, this protocol is simple, convenient and cost-effective, which is suitable for large-scale promotion and can provide necessary support for patients in early rehabilitation.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. First, the study will only recruit patients with VHD from one hospital, so it is challenging to generalise the study’s findings. Second, this study will adopt a non-blinded design due to the nature and design of the study. Thus, selection bias could result from this. Finally, considering the risk of cardiovascular surgery, some participants may not survive long enough to finish the study or may pass away. To reduce this bias, we calculated the wastage rate of 10% and will analyse the intention-to-treat in the process of data analysis.

Implications for policy and practice

Obviously, there are many challenges experienced by nurses in implementing early rehabilitation in cardiac wards such as limited medical facilities, overrun budget and security concerns. This intervention is a labour-saving, easy to implement and ‘low-cost’ early CR programme and makes it possible to support a large number of patients during a critical stage of their illness. Clinicians, nurses and psychologists are likely to be interested in the findings, especially in light of the pressing need to give patients with VHD meaningful exercise-psychological intervention. Additionally, the large-scale implementation of sports and psychological rehabilitation nursing strategies requires the willingness and commitment of nursing and hospital leadership to address the challenges posed by inadequate human resources.
