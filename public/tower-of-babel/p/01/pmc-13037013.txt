Helminth coinfections mitigate clinical, parasitological, and immune outcomes in Mozambican children with malaria
Authors: Inocência Cuamba, Rebeca Santano, Berta Grau-Pujol, Marta Vidal, Ruth Aguilar, Anélsio Cossa, Chenjerai Jairoce, Rojelio Mejia, Augusto Nhabomba, Jose Muñoz, Gemma Moncunill, Carlota Dobaño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClearance of Plasmodium falciparum infection requires a TH1 immune response with production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, IgG1, and IgG3 responses. In contrast, infections with helminths are dominated by TH2/Treg immune responses characterized by production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, IgE, and IgG4, which could potentially interfere with malaria outcomes and immune responses.MethodsWe recruited 441 malaria-symptomatic children aged 2–10 years attending two hospitals in the Manhiça District (Mozambique) and assessed infection by rapid antigen diagnostic test, microscopy, and/or quantitative PCR. Using Luminex, we measured concentrations of 30 cytokines and IgA, IgM, IgE, IgG, and IgG1–4 levels against 12 P. falciparum antigens and total IgE.ResultsAmong 74 children diagnosed with malaria, 22% (16) were coinfected with STH, and 78% (58) had only P. falciparum. Soil-transmitted helminths (STH) coinfection associated with lower P. falciparum density (P = 0.005) and fever (P = 0.047). Coinfected children also had lower concentrations of plasma pro-inflammatory (IL-8, TNF, IFNα, IP-10, MCP1, MIG, MIP1β, and GM-CSF) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-5, IL-10) (P < 0.05). Furthermore, antibody responses to a set of P. falciparum antigens, including SSP2 (IgG1, IgG4, IgG), PTRAMP (IgG3), MSP3 (IgG), RH5 (IgG1), and α-gal (IgG1), as well as total IgE, were elevated in STH coinfected children (P < 0.05).ConclusionsSTH infections were associated with decreased P. falciparum parasitemia, downregulation of inflammatory cytokines, and enhanced antibody responses, potentially resulting in milder malaria episodes, suggesting enhanced protective immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04827-7.

Background

Malaria remains a significant public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, with 94% of cases occurring in Africa, caused mainly by Plasmodium falciparum [1]. Helminth infections, including soil-transmitted helminths (STH) and schistosomiasis, are also highly endemic in low-income countries [2]. Their geographic overlap often results in P. falciparum-helminth coinfections [3–6].

P. falciparum infection initially elicits a T-helper 1 (TH1) immune response, characterized by the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and interferon (IFN)-γ [6]. Additionally, P. falciparum is associated with a dominant IgG1 and IgG3 antibody response [7]. TH1 immune responses are pro-inflammatory, promote cell-mediated immunity, and are essential for controlling intracellular pathogens [8, 9]. These immune responses are essential to protect and clear P. falciparum parasites [10, 11]. Conversely, helminths mainly induce a strong type 2 immune response characterized by the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, and B cell isotype switching to IgE and IgG4 [12–14]. TH2 responses are anti-inflammatory and support humoral immunity against extracellular pathogens [9, 15]. Helminths also induce an immunomodulatory response, allowing parasite survival in the host and dampening of pathogenesis [16]. These responses are characterized by regulatory T cells (Tregs), which mediate their effect through suppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β [17, 18]. This regulatory environment might also affect coinfecting pathogens, including the pathology and immune response to malaria [19].

Epidemiological, clinical, and immunological data on the impact of malaria-helminth coinfections are controversial. Coinfection was associated with protection against anemia [20], reduced P. falciparum density [21], and lower risk of fever in Schistosoma haematobium coinfections [22]. In contrast, other studies found that malaria and STH or S. mansoni coinfections were associated with anemia [4, 23] and clinical or severe malaria [24]. Additionally, children carrying Ascaris lumbricoides, Ancylostoma duodenale, Trichuris trichiura, or S. mansoni, had higher risk of clinical malaria [25–27]. Furthermore, the effect of coinfection could depend on the infecting helminth species. For instance, A. lumbricoides was associated with protection from severe malaria, while hookworm was associated with increased malaria incidence and prevalence [28].

Regarding immune responses, concurrent malaria and helminth infections were associated with increased cytokine levels in some studies [29, 30], while others found no effect [31] or the opposite association [32, 33]. In S. haematobium and malaria coinfections, plasma concentrations of TNF and IFN-γ [29], as well as IL-10 production upon whole blood stimulation with P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes [30], were increased among coinfected individuals. In another study, IL-10 was lower in S. haematobium and malaria-coinfected subjects [32].

Lower anti-malarial cytophilic antibody responses in helminth carriers have also been reported, suggesting an increased risk of malaria infection or disease as a result [34, 35]. For example, anti-MSP3 IgG1 and IgG3 [34], or anti-MSP1 IgG1 [35], were lower in helminth-positive individuals. Pfs48/45 IgG levels were also reduced in individuals coinfected with S. haematobium [36]. In contrast, increased IgG1 [37] and IgG1–3 [38] to P. falciparum antigens were also found in children coinfected with S. haematobium. These conflicting findings highlight the need for further research to understand the impact of helminth coinfections on malaria outcomes and immune responses, particularly in light of the potential adverse effects of concurrent helminthiasis on malaria susceptibility and burden.

We previously found that P. falciparum and helminth coinfection or coexposure was associated with higher P. falciparum parasite density in children, and increased total IgE and antigen-specific IgG levels [39] as part of a community-based study with asymptomatic participants in Mozambique. Here, we assessed the effect of concurrent helminth infection on malaria-related clinical and immunological outcomes in symptomatic children with a systems serology approach against a large breadth of antigens. Specifically, we measured plasma cytokine concentrations, total IgE, and P. falciparum-specific IgM, IgA, IgG, IgG1–4, and IgE antibodies, comparing these biomarkers between helminth coinfected and malaria monoinfected children.

Methods

Study design

This analysis was nested within EcoHeMa, a hospital-based cross-sectional study that recruited 441 children aged 2 to 10 years residing in the Manhiça District, southern Mozambique, who presented at the Manhiça hospital or the Ilha Josina Machel health center, between August 2015 and December 2016. Since the study was designed as an exploratory investigation nested within the EcoHeMa project, the sample size was determined by the number of suitable samples available from the enrolled participants. Children who had ingested anti-helminthics or anti-malarials in the past 30 days were excluded.

At recruitment, 6 ml of blood was drawn for malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDT) (HRP2-based SD Bioline Ag Pf, Standard Diagnostics, South Korea), hematocrit determination, and HIV serological rapid test, and the remaining samples were stored as dry blood spots (DBS) and plasma at the Centro de Investigação em Saúde de Manhiça (CISM) laboratory. Field staff visited the participant’s homes the day after the hospital visit to collect stool samples, transported to CISM at 2–8 °C for helminth detection [40].

This study received ethical approval from the Mozambican health bioethics committee (Ref: 134/CNBS/16) and the Hospital Clinic de Barcelona ethics committee (CEIC, ref: HCB/2016/0447), and written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians of all participating children before starting study procedures.

Study area

The study area has been described in detail elsewhere [41]. The Manhiça District is predominantly rural to semi-rural, with perennial malaria transmission of higher intensity at the start of each year, coinciding with the rainy season, and infections are predominantly caused by P. falciparum. Malaria transmission in the district has decreased over the years [42], but Ilha Josina Machel remains a malaria hotspot with persistently high transmission intensity [43].

Parasite diagnosis

Thick and thin blood smears were stained with Giemsa to detect and quantify P. falciparum parasites by microscopy [44]. Negative samples were then analyzed in duplicate by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting P. falciparum 18S rRNA [45], using DNA extracted from DBS. Parasitemia was quantified by extrapolating cycle threshold (Ct) values from a standard curve generated using P. falciparum positive controls. Samples without amplification were classified as negative.

Microscopic detection of STH was performed using the Telemann technique and remaining samples stored at − 80 °C for multi-parallel qPCR diagnosis [40, 46].

Quantification of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors

Plasma concentrations (pg/ml) were measured using the Cytokine Human Magnetic 30-Plex Panel from Life Technologies™ that includes different functional families. First, TH1-associated cytokines, IL-12 (p40/p70), IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF, IFN-α, and related chemokines IP-10 (IFN-γ-induced protein 10) and MIG (monokine induced by IFN-γ). Second, TH2-associated cytokines, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, eotaxin (CCL11), and RANTES (regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted). Third, TH17-associated cytokine IL-17. Fourth, regulatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-1RA. Finally, cytokines and chemokines involved on inflammatory pathways, as well as growth factors including G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor), IL-1β, IL-2R, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-15, IL-17, MCP-1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein-1), MIP-1α (macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha), MIP-1β (macrophage inflammatory protein-1 beta), EGF (epidermal growth factor), FGF (fibroblast growth factor), HGF (hepatocyte growth factor), and VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), and GM-CSF (granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor). Assays were done following the manufacturer’s instructions with a modification to the protocol (see Additional file 1 [47–63]). Sample acquisition was performed on the Luminex 100/200™ instrument using the xPONENT software.

Quantification of antibodies

Serology was performed against a panel of 12 P. falciparum antigens (SSP2/TRAP, EXP1, AMA1, EBA175, MSP1, MSP2, MSP3, MSP5, P41, RH1, RH5, and PTRAMP) and α-gal, selected based on their importance as vaccine candidates and/or malaria exposure markers (Additional file 1: Table 1). Quantitative suspension array technology (qSAT) assays, which are bead-based immunoassays that allow simultaneous measurement of multiple analytes such as antibodies in a single small-volume sample, were used to measure specific IgG, IgG1–4, IgA, IgM, IgE, and total IgE levels in plasma using the xMAP™ platform (Luminex Corporation, Austin, USA) [49]. For each isotype or subclass, samples were used at different dilutions as predetermined in optimization assays. See Additional file 1 for assay details. Briefly, antigen-coupled magnetic beads were incubated with samples, positive and negative controls. After washing, plates were incubated with corresponding biotinylated secondary antibodies and then with Streptavidin-R-Phycoerythrin. After a final washing, beads were resuspended in Luminex buffer, followed by acquisition on a FlexMap 3D instrument. At least 50 microspheres per analyte were acquired per sample, and results were reported as median fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Statistical analysis

Children were considered P. falciparum- or helminth-infected if positive by any of the diagnostic methods, and were divided into two groups for analysis: malaria and helminth coinfected and malaria monoinfected. Comparisons between groups were performed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and by Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Hematocrit values of < 33% were considered as anemia based on literature [64, 65]. Concentrations of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, parasite densities, and antibody levels were log10 transformed for analysis, and patterns were explored using hierarchical clustering heatmaps, taking into account factors such as infection group, sex, age, site, anemia status, and P. falciparum parasite density. In hierarchical clustering heatmaps, the columns were ordered based on the infection status to highlight group differences, and rows were clustered using Euclidean distance and complete linkage.

Linear regression models were applied to (i) assess associations between helminth infections and malaria-related parasitological and clinical outcomes, (ii) evaluate associations of demographic factors (age, sex, site) and clinical variables (parasitemia, fever, anemia, vomiting, and coughing) with cytokines and antibody levels, (iii) determine the association of coinfection with cytokine concentrations and antibody levels, and (iv) determine associations between antibody levels and cytokine concentrations. Potential confounding factors were accounted for in the models based on relationships identified using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs, Additional file 1: Fig. S1), which are visual tools that depict causal relationships between variables, helping to identify confounders and guide appropriate adjustments in statistical models, and associations observed in the univariable analyses. For interpretation purposes, the β estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) obtained from the models were transformed into percentages (see Additional file 1). P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Raw P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

To analyze if infection group was an effect modifier of the associations of antibody responses with parasitemia and cytokine concentrations with antibody responses, the infection group was analyzed as an interaction term in the regression models. Models adjusted by infection group were compared to the same models with infection group as an interaction term using ANOVA. When regression models with the interaction term were statistically significantly different than models without interaction term, they were stratified by infection group.

Finally, multimarker analysis by partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using the R package mixOmics [66]. Here, the response variable was current infection status, and the predictor variables were the log10-transformed MFI levels of all antigen–antibody pairs and log10-transformed concentrations of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors.

All data analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0) with tidyverse [67], gtsummary [68], ggpubr [69], ggbeeswarm [70], ComplexUpset [71], and pheatmap packages [72].

Children were stratified into two age groups: 2 to 5 years and 6 to 10 years, with the coinfected group having a higher proportion of children aged 6 to 10 (88%, 14/16) than the malaria group (P = 0.007). Across infection groups, no statistically significant differences were found in study site, sex distribution, and prevalence of anemia. Study participants mainly presented with fever (85%, 63/74) and cough (47%, 35/74), followed by vomiting (22%, 16/74), wheezing (2.7%, 2/74), and diarrhea (1.4%, 1/74) (Table 1).

Considering that parasite densities can vary according to age, sex, and site-specific malaria transmission intensities, and confirming they are confounder variables in a DAG (Additional file 1: Fig. S1a), we evaluated the effect of helminth infection on parasite density using a multivariable linear regression model that included these variables. The presence of helminths in individuals with malaria was associated with a 97.9% (95% CI: − 99.7 to − 86.4, P < 0.01) decrease in P. falciparum parasite density (log10 Pf parasites/μl) (Fig. 1b).

Anemia

Coinfected children had higher hematocrit levels, indicating a trend towards less anemia; however, this difference was not statistically significant compared with the malaria-only group (Fig. 1c and Table 1). This result did not change upon considering potential confounding factors (Additional file 1: Figs. S1b and S3a).

Symptoms

Coinfected children had lower median body temperature than those with malaria (P = 0.047) (Fig. 1d and Table 1). In multivariable regression models, adjusted by P. falciparum parasite density, an important factor affecting temperature in malaria episodes (Additional file 1: Fig. S1c), the association was no longer statistically significant (Fig. 1e). This suggests that lower temperature in the coinfected was potentially mediated by the lower P. falciparum parasite density observed in this group of children. When adjusting the models by the other potential confounders, age, sex, and site (Additional file 1: Fig. S1c), coinfection was still associated with a decrease in body temperature, but it did not reach statistical significance (Additional file 1: Fig. S3b). Gastrointestinal (diarrhea and vomiting) and respiratory symptoms (coughing and wheezing) did not differ between coinfected and malaria monoinfected children (Table 1).

When demographic variables were included as covariates in the models assessing the association between coinfection and cytokine responses, the results (data not shown) were consistent with the findings in Fig. 2a.

Helminth coinfection associated with increased anti-malarial antibody responses

We first evaluated the association between antibody responses with malaria parasite density and fever in all children, without considering infection status. The most consistent pattern that emerged was the statistically significant negative association between IgG3 levels to a set of P. falciparum antigens that have been previously associated with protection (SSP2/TRAP, EXP1, MSP1 block 2 and 42KDa, MSP2, MSP3, MSP5, EBA175, PTRAMP, RH1) and parasite density, fever and/or temperature (β estimates ranged from − 89.74% to − 1.22%) (raw P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Additionally, other antibody isotype/subclass responses to some of those antigens, along with AMA1, were also negatively associated with parasite density, fever, and/or temperature. Specifically, IgG1 (MSP3, MSP5, RH1), IgG2 (MSP3, MSP1 block 2), IgG4 (MSP3, MSP5), total IgG (MSP3, MSP5, RH1, AMA1), and IgE (AMA1), with β estimates varying from − 74.3% to − 0.49% (raw P < 0.05). Discussion

Our findings suggest a potential protective effect of STH infection on malaria-related outcomes, as evidenced by the association with lower P. falciparum parasite density and reduced fever in coinfected individuals. This observation suggests that coinfection may potentially attenuate malaria severity by decreasing parasite density, which is a known risk factor for severe malaria [73], resulting in diminished fever, which is a characteristic of acquired clinical immunity and tolerance. In addition, coinfected individuals had a less inflammatory cytokine profile in serum, potentially also due to their lower parasitemia. This profile in the coinfected may also explain the reduced body temperatures in this group, considering fever is mediated by a pro-inflammatory cytokine environment [74]. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF are known to correlate with malaria severity [75, 76]. Thus, the decrease in the concentration of pro-inflammatory cytokines among coinfected participants in this study supports the hypothesis that helminth coinfection may play a protective role in mitigating malaria symptoms. Our study goes beyond the state of the art by simultaneously measuring different immune mediators and integrating them with clinical and parasitological outcomes to provide deeper insights into the effect of concurrent STH infection on malaria disease and immunity, and offering evidence of immune system modulation in the presence of helminths. Additionally, we assessed IgE responses to a panel of P. falciparum antigens, an approach that adds a unique dimension to our understanding of the immune landscape during helminth-P. falciparum coinfections.

Although the underlying exact immune modulation mechanisms were not elucidated in full, it is plausible that the presence of STHs suppresses P. falciparum-induced excessive inflammation and better controls malaria negative outcomes through induction of Tregs and anti-inflammatory cytokines [77]. Indeed, as part of the response to malaria infection, regulatory cytokines like IL-10, which came up as the top contributor associated with malaria monoinfection in our PLS-DA analysis, are elicited after the initial secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, to avoid pathological consequences and promote a balanced response, eventually allowing adaptive immunity to establish. This process could be facilitated by STH coinfections. For instance, it has been reported that helminth-infected children from Ghana had increased production of IL-10 and higher expression of regulatory markers such as FOXP3 in response to in vitro stimulation with malaria antigens [30]. In addition, Treg activity was increased in helminth-infected children in response to malaria antigens, even though Treg frequencies may not be altered [78], indicating that T cells with regulatory functions might be preferentially induced in coinfected individuals. However, our data does not reflect a significant increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines in STH and malaria-coinfected participants, but we do observe a less inflammatory environment. After adjusting for P. falciparum parasite density, the association between STH coinfection and lower cytokine concentrations was no longer evident, suggesting that the observed differences may be attributable to lower parasitemia. Future studies focusing on cellular immunophenotyping and functions in blood samples from these participants could provide deeper insights into immune cell functions and populations involved in modulating malaria responses in STH-coinfected participants.

Consistent with some previous studies [37, 38], we found that coinfected participants had a trend for increased P. falciparum-specific antibody levels. It is well known in malaria naturally acquired immunity that antibody responses associate with reduced parasite burden [79]; however, it is less clear what isotypes or subclasses are the most protective, and what antigen targets they bind to. Our comprehensive systems serology analysis, which included a wide breadth of immunoglobulin types and antigens, provided some clues as to which ones may be more relevant for protection and prioritized for multistage vaccine development. Here, IgG1 to MSP3 and MSP5, and IgG3 to EBA175, MSP2, MSP3, MSP5, and PTRAMP, consistently associated with less parasitemia and body temperature or fever. Of note, these cytophilic antibodies are involved in Fc-dependent effector functions [80, 81]. Among those antigens, EBA175 [82], MSP2 [83], MSP3 [80, 81], and MSP5 [84] have been linked to malaria protection in endemic populations. While this anti-parasite activity appears to be true for malaria-monoinfected participants in our study, we found no protective associations for IgA, IgM, IgG (including IgG1–4), or total IgE, in helminth-coinfected individuals, suggesting the involvement of additional protective anti-parasite mechanisms. In fact, our data indicate that antibody responses may be differently regulated during coinfections, as suggested by the numerous significant interactions showing that antibodies and cytokines were more strongly correlated in coinfected participants.

An alternative potential mechanism of protection from severe malaria in coinfected individuals could be the activation of the FcƐRII (CD23) and nitric oxide (NO) pathway [85]. Helminth infections stimulate IgE production, which can form immune complexes and interact with cells expressing CD23, a low-affinity IgE receptor found on various immune cells [86]. Activation of CD23 has been linked to increased NO production, which is thought to help control P. falciparum parasite growth [87]. The activation of this CD23/NO pathway through the formation of IgE immune complexes has been associated with reduced malaria severity in helminth-infected individuals [28, 85]. Interestingly, total IgE was higher in coinfected children and malaria-specific IgE responses in the coinfected children showed a trend of negative association with P. falciparum parasite density, with IgE to α-gal and MSP3 having a statistically significant negative correlation with parasitemia, even though the levels of antigen-specific IgE responses were not significantly increased in this study group. This suggests a potential protective role of P. falciparum-specific IgE [88] responses in the STH and malaria-coinfected children, which could contribute to the low parasitemia in these coinfected participants. For future cellular assays, it would be interesting to evaluate CD23 expression in cells from these coinfected children, measure NO production in these samples, and assess the association with IgE levels to substantiate this hypothesis, since few studies report this mechanism [87, 89].

Our study had some limitations. First, the small sample size of coinfected children compared to the malaria monoinfected group may have limited the statistical power to identify associations between antibodies and parasitemia in this group, although there were statistically significant interactions detected by infection group, and the few significant associations detected in coinfected were associated with risk. Additionally, the small number of coinfected children may limit generalizability to other populations or settings. Because of the sample size limitation, we were unable to stratify analysis by helminth species, potentially masking helminth-specific associations. Another limitation is that helminth infection intensity was not taken into account, which may have limited our ability to assess potential associations between helminth parasite burden and immunological or clinical outcomes. Also, the cross-sectional nature of the study restricted our ability to establish causal relationships as exposure and outcomes were measured simultaneously. The study design also prevented us from determining whether helminth infections were acute or chronic, limiting our ability to assess how the infection stage might influence immunological and clinical outcomes. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are needed to clarify causal relationships and confirm these observations. We also acknowledge that other studies reported opposing findings, such as increased cytokine concentrations [90] and reduced anti-malarial IgG [36], IgG1, and IgG3 [34] levels in helminth and malaria-coinfected individuals. These differences could be attributed to variations in study designs, methodologies, and study populations, particularly considering that some of these studies were community-based and included asymptomatic participants. The infecting helminth species being evaluated as well as the chronicity of infection at the time of sample collection [91] could also account for the differing findings.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that symptomatic children coinfected with malaria and helminths were associated with decreased P. falciparum parasite density, less fever, and lower inflammatory cytokine concentrations, suggesting a potential mitigation of malaria severity and thus potential protective effects of helminth coinfection. We also detected increased IgG (SSP2, MSP-3), IgG1 (α-gal, SSP2, RH5), IgG3 (PTRAMP), IgG4 (SSP2), and total IgE responses in malaria-helminth coinfected children, suggesting that concurrent helminth infection could influence the humoral response to P. falciparum, likely through a different regulation of the immune responses, as illustrated by the infection group differences in associations between cytokine and antibody responses. From a public health point of view, the findings of this study could be a factor to consider when designing malaria and helminth control strategies. An integrated approach could be used, where mass deworming interventions could be accompanied by increased malaria testing or preventive malaria treatment to avoid a potential rise in clinical or severe malaria cases as a result of increased P. falciparum parasitemia [92, 93]. Additionally, deworming has been associated with enhanced malaria-specific pro-inflammatory cytokine responses [94], and this could potentially increase the occurrence of severe malaria. Future longitudinal studies in high malaria and helminth transmission settings could increase the likelihood of obtaining a larger sample of coinfected participants, thereby improving the statistical power to assess associations and causality. Evaluating cellular immune responses would also allow further understanding of the mechanisms behind malaria and helminth interactions during concurrent infections.
