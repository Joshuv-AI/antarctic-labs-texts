CGRP-targeted migraine treatment and early pathophysiology in experimental subarachnoid hemorrhage
Authors: Emelie P Fungbrant, Jesper P Bömers, Philip V Reducha, Spyridoula Kazantzi, Tiit Mathiesen, Lars Edvinsson, Kristian A Haanes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is a central mediator in migraine and an endogenous vasodilator in the trigeminovascular system. Its role in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), remains uncertain, particularly in patients receiving CGRP-targeted migraine therapy at the time of hemorrhage. This study examined whether CGRP blockade administered shortly before experimental SAH affects outcome and whether early SAH is associated with altered trigeminovascular CGRP signaling.MethodsCGRP-mediated vasodilation was first assessed ex vivo in rat and mouse cerebral arteries using wire myography to identify the most suitable species for SAH experiments. Experimental pre-chiasmatic SAH was then induced in rats. At 24 h, body weight, rotating pole performance, mechanical sensitivity, and capsaicin evoked CGRP release from dura mater and trigeminal ganglion were evaluated in SAH and sham animals. In a separate intervention arm, rats received vehicle or the anti-CGRP monoclonal antibody fremanezumab, 30 mg/kg intravenously, 15 min before SAH. Functional outcome, neurological scoring, body weight, and survival were followed for 14 days.ResultsRat basilar arteries showed markedly greater CGRP-induced dilation than mouse basilar arteries, supporting rat as the relevant model. At 24 h after SAH, body weight was reduced whereas periorbital and plantar von Frey thresholds were unchanged. Capsaicin-evoked CGRP release from dura mater was significantly reduced after SAH, while trigeminal ganglion release was unaffected, consistent with early peripheral trigeminovascular peptide release. Fremanezumab treated animals showed higher cerebral blood flow 5 min after SAH and improved rotating pole performance at 48 h compared with vehicle treated animals. No significant differences were observed in composite survival, defined as freedom from spontaneous death or euthanasia at humane endpoint, body weight trajectory, well-being score, or composite neurological score at the 14 day follow up.ConclusionsExperimental SAH is associated with early impairment of releasable CGRP in dura mater, supporting acute trigeminovascular involvement after hemorrhage. Pre-existing CGRP blockade did not worsen overall 14-day outcome in this model of SAH, but seemingly improved early functional recovery and the temporal pattern of disease progression. These findings suggest stage-dependent effects of CGRP signaling in SAH and support further study of CGRP-targeted therapies in cerebrovascular disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02403-1.Keywords: CGRP, Stroke, Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Anti-CGRP, Fremanezumab

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a type of hemorrhagic stroke and has an incidence of approximately 6–7 per 100 000 person years with a 30-day mortality of approximately 40% [1–3]. The high morbidity and mortality reflect complications such as ischemia related to ictus, early brain injury (EBI), re-bleeding, hydrocephalus and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), which can occur 4–14 days after the initial bleeding [4].

DCI is a diagnosis of exclusion that can lead to poor outcome in patients [5, 6]. DCI involves cerebral vasoconstriction associated with disruption of the balance between vasoconstrictive and vasodilatory signaling pathways. The pathophysiology is multifactorial and involves several processes including disruption of the blood-brain barrier [7], inflammatory activation within the cerebral vasculature [8, 9], microthrombosis [10] and cortical spreading depolarization [11, 12]. Increasing evidence suggests that DCI is at least partly determined by events occurring during EBI [13]. EBI describes the initial phase following ictus characterized by transient global hypoperfusion followed by the initiation of biochemical cascades that contribute to several pathological processes including oxidative stress [14, 15], cerebral edema formation [16, 17] and disturbances of the cerebral microcirculation [18, 19].

These early cascades include upregulation of vasoconstrictive receptors and increased release of vasoconstrictor peptides such as endothelin [20–25]. In parallel, vasodilatory signaling related to nitric oxide and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) appears to be downregulated [26, 27]. Sensory nerve fibers originating from the trigeminal ganglion innervate cerebral and meningeal arteries [28], and release of CGRP from these fibers contributes to dilation of cerebral arteries [29].

Several studies indicate that the trigeminovascular system is altered following SAH. Sensory nerve fibers supplying the cerebral circulation that contain CGRP are reduced after experimental SAH [30]. Lower levels of CGRP have also been detected in cerebral arteries from patients with fatal SAH compared with those with fatal coronary infarction [26]. Recent experimental work has further shown that SAH reduces CGRP immunoreactive fibers and attenuates evoked CGRP release within the trigeminovascular system, supporting the concept that early peptide release may be followed by depletion of CGRP stores [31]. At the same time, CGRP levels measured in cerebrospinal fluid from SAH patients are elevated compared with controls [32]. These findings suggest that SAH may induce an early release of CGRP followed by depletion of peptide stores within trigeminal nerve terminals. There is a clear correlation between release of CGRP and degree of vasospasm after SAH in the external jugular vein and in the cerebrospinal fluid following subarachnoid hemorrhage in man [33, 34].

From a vascular perspective, CGRP is considered a potent endogenous vasodilator. Activation of the trigeminovascular reflex [35] has been shown to prevent vasospasm in experimental models [36]. At first sight, increased CGRP signaling might therefore appear beneficial in a condition characterized by cerebral vasoconstriction. However, CGRP also affects systemic hemodynamics. CGRP can lower systemic blood pressure [37], whereas anti-CGRP therapies have been associated with increases in blood pressure in clinical studies [38, 39]. Available experimental evidence further indicates that stimulation of the trigeminal system can alleviate cerebral vasoconstriction [40, 41]. At the same time, higher CGRP levels have been associated with unfavorable outcomes in some SAH patients [42]. These observations suggest that the effects of CGRP signaling in SAH may depend on the temporal stage of the disease and the balance between cerebral and systemic vascular effects. This has been tested and demonstrated in CGRP infusion in SAH patients with vasospasm measured with transcranial doppler [43].

CGRP is primarily known as a key mediator in migraine pathophysiology and represents an established therapeutic target in migraine treatment [44–46]. Within the trigeminovascular system, CGRP released from C-fibers can activate CGRP receptors on adjacent Aδ-fibers and contribute to nociceptive signaling [47]. While the role of CGRP in migraine has been extensively studied, its potential contribution to headache and meningeal irritation following SAH remains poorly understood. The increasing use of anti-CGRP therapies for migraine introduces an additional clinical consideration. Patients suffering from SAH may already be receiving CGRP-targeting treatment at the time of hemorrhage. Experimental work has further shown that CGRP receptor antagonism with agents such as rimegepant can worsen ischemic stroke outcome in mice [48], indicating that the effects of CGRP blockade on cerebrovascular pathology warrant further investigation across experimental models and species. There is therefore a need to understand how modulation of CGRP signaling may influence the pathophysiology of SAH.

The present study was undertaken in an experimental SAH model to investigate the role of CGRP signaling in this condition. The primary objective was to determine whether anti-CGRP treatment influences neurological outcome following SAH. Secondary objectives were to examine potential early pathogenic mechanisms including whether SAH is associated with CGRP release within the trigeminovascular system and whether experimental SAH induces behavioral signs consistent with pain. CGRP release was assessed in meninges and trigeminal ganglion, while behavioral responses were evaluated using mechanical stimulation with von Frey filaments.

Methods

Animals

Male Sprague Dawley rats and male C57BL/6 mice were used in the study (Taconic, Denmark). Rats were acclimatized for at least 2 weeks before surgery. SAH surgery was performed on a total of 32 rats. Sham surgery was performed on 8 rats. Post-surgery, animals were kept single housed in type III (Eurostandard) cages for 24 h and then returned to their original groups of three animals in type IV (Eurostandard) cages. Nine rats and four mice were used for organ harvest and myograph studies. Animals were housed under a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle. Temperature was maintained at 22 ± 2 °C with a humidity of 55 ± 10%. Animals had free access to water and Altrumin chow (Scanbur). Danish Animal Experimentation Inspectorate authorized all procedures (09.20.2021), which were performed in accordance with national laws and norms (License no. 2021-15-0201-00947). The sample size was calculated to detect a 40% decrease in high performers in the rotating pole test. Using an alpha value of 0.05 and a power of 80%, the group sizes were estimated to be twelve [49].

Experimental design

In the first part, ex vivo vascular contractility was evaluated using wire myography to examine CGRP mediated dilation in cerebral arteries on experimentally naïve animals. Four rats and four mice were sacrificed, and basilar arteries (BA) were harvested from both species while middle cerebral arteries (MCA) were collected only from rats.

In the second part, early effects of SAH were evaluated. SAH surgery was performed on eight rats and sham surgery on eight rats. As the injected blood itself represents the pathological stimulus in this model, no separate vehicle injection group was included. 16 rats were evaluated at 24 h using the rotating pole test, body weight measurement, and von Frey testing. After sacrifice, CGRP release experiments were performed in all animals (SAH n = 8, Sham n = 8) using dura mater and trigeminal ganglion (TG).

In the third part, the effects of anti-CGRP treatment administered immediately prior to experimental SAH were investigated, thereby modeling a worst- case scenario of preexisting CGRP blockade at hemorrhage onset. Fremanezumab was administered intravenously 15 min prior to blood collection and SAH induction to ensure antibody incorporation into the injected autologous blood, given its limited permeability across the blood-brain barrier, while minimizing variability associated with longer-term pharmacokinetic factors, including redistribution, metabolism, and clearance. SAH surgery was performed on 24 rats. The rats were randomly allocated to either a vehicle treatment group (n = 12) or an anti-CGRP treatment group (n = 12). Anti-CGRP antibody (Fremanezumab, TEVA pharmaceuticals, Israel) was administered intravenously at a dose of 30 mg/kg 15 min before induction of SAH to ensure mixing with the autologous blood used for the injection into the subarachnoid space. The 30 mg/kg dose of fremanezumab was selected based on prior acute and long-term studies [50–52]. Follow up assessments were performed using the rotating pole test, well-being score and modified neuroscore at 48 h, 7 days, and 14 days. Body weight was recorded daily. The primary event in the Kaplan-Meier analysis was reaching a predefined humane endpoint requiring euthanasia (see Supplementary scoring system 1), rather than spontaneous death alone, for longitudinal behavioral assessments, animals that reached a predefined humane endpoint or died before a scheduled assessment were not tested further and were excluded from analysis at subsequent time points. Thus, rotating pole and neurological score data at each time point reflect only animals alive and available for assessment at that specific time point.

Surgery

The experimental pre-chiasmatic single injection SAH model was utilized [53]. Following anesthesia with isoflurane, the rat was intubated and mechanically ventilated with 30% oxygen, 70% atmospheric air, and isoflurane 1.5–3%. A heating pad was used to maintain body temperature at 37 °C ± 0.5 °C (TC-1000, CWE Inc., PA, USA). Mean arterial blood pressure (MABP) was measured through a catheter placed in the tail artery. A hole was drilled 6.5 mm anterior to bregma and a cannula was positioned using a stereotaxic frame. Another hole was placed 3–4 mm lateral to the right of bregma partially drilled without penetrating the dura mater, for measurement of cerebral blood flow using laser Doppler flowmetry (single-channel laser Doppler Blood FlowMeter (INL191, ADInstruments connected to a fiber optic probe and PowerLab/LabChart acquisition system).

The cannula was then inserted through the skull between the two hemispheres and placed anterior to the optic chiasm. A volume of 300 µL autologous blood withdrawn from the tail catheter, was injected through the cannula into the subarachnoid space to induce experimental SAH. Surgical incisions were closed with non-resorbable monofilament 4 − 0 suture. No antibiotic prophylaxis was given. The same procedure was performed in sham animals except for insertion of the cannula and injection of blood. MABP were measured continuously during the surgical procedure, however, reduction in cerebral blood flow was used as the primary inclusion parameter of SAH severity. Intracranial pressure measurements were not included as an inclusion criterion in the present study. Animals received carprofen (0.1 mL per 100 g body weight of 5 mg/mL) and 3 mL saline subcutaneously before recovery from anesthesia. For the long-term study, the animals received the same analgesia at 24 and 48 h after surgery.

Rotating pole

For sensorimotor evaluation and initiative, the rotating pole test was used [54]. The rat traverses a wooden pole (45 mm diameter and 150 cm length), either stationary or rotating at a speed of 10 rpm, attempting to reach the opposite end of the pole and a cage containing familiar bedding. Each rat was scored twice for right and left rotation. Performance was scored as low if the animal was unable to cross the pole without falling off, and high if the animal reached the opposite end of the pole without falling [49, 55]. Higher scores therefore indicate better performance. Rats were habituated to the rotating pole apparatus prior to testing to ensure reliable and consistent traversal during the experimental trials.

Von Frey

The electronic von Frey (EVF) method was used to assess mechanical allodynia as previously described [50]. Allodynia was measured using an EVF anesthesiometer (IITC, USA). Rats underwent preliminary testing before the formal experimental phase to allow acclimation to the EVF apparatus. On the day of testing, rats were placed in the examination room 30 min before the procedure.

During testing, the maximum pressure applied was 400 g, which represented the threshold for no response. The applied pressure did not exceed this limit. Rats were gently restrained with a towel by the investigator to minimize movement, while the tested limb remained unrestrained to allow clear observation of withdrawal responses. Three periorbital areas and five plantar areas were tested. Recorded values were averaged for subsequent analysis.

Neuroscore

Neurological evaluation included a well-being score [56] and a modified neurocomposite score (modified neuroscore) [57, 58]. The well-being score (see Supplementary scoring system 2), comprised of the following parameters: body temperature, body posture (normal or curved back), eyes (open, closed, dry, or blood), fur condition (clean or piloerection), vocalization during handling, noise sensitivity (hyperactive), feces (normal, soft, or absent), movement (normal, reduced, or absent), balance, ear color (red or pale), and temperament (passive or aggressive). Each parameter was scored according to severity as follows: normal state = 2, moderate state = 1, and poor state = 0, with higher scores indicating better wellbeing.

The modified neuroscore consisted of eight neurological domains: forelimb use, hind limb use, spontaneous activity, climbing ability, balance, visual function (blinking reflex), side stroking response, and vibrissae touch (turning head in direction of touch). Outcomes were assessed using an ordinal 4-point scale from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating better neurological function (see Supplementary scoring system 3).

CGRP release

A validated model was used for CGRP release [59, 60]. Rats were anesthetized with 70% CO2 and 30% O2 mixture and euthanized through decapitation. Fur and mandibles from the heads were removed. Rat hemi-skulls with intact dura mater and trigeminal ganglia were incubated in oxygenated synthetic interstitial fluid (SIF) containing 108 mM NaCl, 3.5 mM KCl, 5.6 mM glucose, 3.5 mM MgSO₄, 26 mM NaHCO₃, 1.5 mM CaCl₂, 9.6 mM Na-gluconate, 7.6 mM sucrose (pH approximately 7.3), and 11.7 mM NaH₂PO₄. Tissues were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C and subsequently underwent a 4 × 10 min wash using prewarmed (37 °C) SIF. Prewarmed SIF was added for 15 min and used as baseline. This was followed with CGRP release induction using either 60 mM potassium (left tissue) or 100 nM of the TRPV1 agonist capsaicin (right tissue). Samples of 200 µL mixed in 50 µL EIA buffer were collected for CGRP analysis and processed using commercial EIA kits. A validated Human CGRP ELISA kit (SPIbio, Paris, France) was used to quantify CGRP release [61]. The assay shows approximately 120% cross reactivity for rat CGRP α/β and has a detection limit of 0.7 pg/mL. Optical density was measured at 410 nm using a microplate photometer (Tecan, Infinite M200, Switzerland).

Myograph

BA and MCA segments from rat and mouse were mounted on stainless steel wires in a Mulvany-Halpern wire myograph (Danish Myograph Technology A/S, Denmark) as previously described [62, 63]. The myographs were connected to a PowerLab unit, and LabChart software was used for acquisition of recordings. The chamber temperature was maintained at 37 °C and continuously aerated with 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂ to stabilize pH. Vessel contractility was confirmed using 60 mM K⁺ Krebs solution containing 59.5 mM NaCl, 60 mM KCl, 1.5 mM CaCl₂, 5.5 mM glucose, 15 mM NaHCO₃, 1.2 mM MgCl₂, and 1.2 mM NaH₂PO₄. Vessels were equilibrated for 20–30 min and were subsequently precontracted with U46619, a thromboxane A₂ analogue (10⁻⁷ M). Concentration-response curves were generated by cumulative addition of human αCGRP. Each concentration was applied for a fixed exposure time of 4 min before the subsequent concentration was administered. Relaxation responses were expressed as a percentage of the precontraction induced by U46619. Concentration-response curves to capsaicin (10nM – 10 µM) were also generated in the absence and presence of 1 µM fremanezumab.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 software (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Numerical data from two independent groups were analyzed using an unpaired t test. When data were non normally distributed or ordinal, a Mann-Whitney test was used. Comparisons between two measurements obtained from the same animal were analyzed using a paired t test, with results presented as mean ± SEM. Emax values are presented as the maximal vasodilatory response, expressed as a percentage of the contractile capacity induced by the thromboxane A₂ agonist and reported as mean ± SEM. Categorical data were analyzed using Fisher exact test, with results presented as odds ratio and confidence interval. Time to event was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, where the event was defined as reaching a predefined humane endpoint requiring euthanasia or spontaneous death. Animals surviving until day 14 without reaching a humane endpoint were treated as censored observations, meaning that the true event time lies beyond the final observed timepoint of the study. Group differences were assessed using the log rank Mantel-Cox test. A p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To determine the most suitable species for investigating CGRP-mediated vascular effects in stroke, vasodilatory responses were examined in rat and mouse BA using wire myography.

CGRP induced maximal dilation differed significantly between species, with rat BA showing a greater maximal response than mouse BA (n = 4 per group; −69.3 ± 17.2% versus − 9.5 ± 17.2%; p = 0.0133, Fig. 1A).

Capsaicin induced dilation in rat basilar arteries at 10 µM was 16.73 ± 7.12% under control conditions and 2.59 ± 2.70% in the presence of fremanezumab (n = 4 per group), with a significant difference at this concentration (p < 0.05, Fig. 1D).

Acute effects after SAH at 24 h

To evaluate early consequences of SAH and to assess whether it induces changes consistent with headache related mechanisms, basic physiological parameters, mechanical sensitivity, and CGRP release were examined 24 h after surgery. Body weight and sensorimotor performance were first evaluated, followed by mechanical sensitivity testing using EVF. The same animals were subsequently sacrificed and CGRP release was quantified in dura mater and trigeminal ganglion.

SAH animals showed a significant reduction in body weight between baseline and 24 h (p = 0.017; 341.4 ± 15.97 g vs. 327.6 ± 12.25 g). No significant change in body weight was observed in sham animals between baseline and 24 h (p = 0.892, 330.1 ± 13.98 g vs. 329.9 ± 15.48 g). Sensorimotor performance was evaluated using the rotating pole test at 24 h. Poor performance was observed in 8 of 24 SAH animals (33%) compared with 2 of 24 sham animals (8%). This difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.072, OR 5.5, 95% CI 1.0–27.5).

Mechanical sensitivity was evaluated using the EVF test 24 h after surgery. Baseline values did not differ between groups (p = 0.950; 182.3 ± 15.79 g vs. 181.1 ± 10.65 g). Periorbital testing showed no significant difference between SAH (n = 8) and sham (n = 8) animals (p = 0.4636; 178.6 ± 22.47 g vs. 198.5 ± 13.89 g). Within group comparisons did not show significant changes over time. Comparison between SAH baseline and SAH at 24 h showed no significant difference (p = 0.866; 182.3 ± 15.79 g vs. 178.6 ± 22.47 g). Similarly, no difference was observed between sham baseline and sham at 24 h (p = 0.119; 181.1 ± 10.65 g vs. 198.5 ± 13.89 g).

Plantar testing also showed no difference between SAH and sham animals at 24 h (p = 0.888, mean ± SEM; 227.5 ± 12.11 g vs. 224.7 ± 14.98 g). Baseline measurements did not differ between groups (p = 0.808, mean ± SEM; 221.3 ± 11.63 g vs. 217.5 ± 10.49 g). Comparison between SAH baseline and SAH at 24 h showed no significant difference (p = 0.697, mean ± SEM; 221.3 ± 11.63 g vs. 227.5 ± 12.11 g). Similarly, no difference was observed between sham baseline and sham at 24 h (p = 0.680, mean ± SEM; 217.5 ± 10.49 g vs. 224.7 ± 14.98 g).

Capsaicin-induced CGRP release in dura mater differed significantly between groups. SAH animals showed reduced CGRP release compared with sham animals (208.9 ± 14.04 pg/mL vs. 296.4 ± 43.35 pg/mL, p = 0.0379). Baseline CGRP levels did not differ between groups (p = 0.397; SAH 11.20 ± 2.87 pg/mL vs. Sham 6.80 ± 1.85 pg/mL). In trigeminal ganglion, capsaicin induced CGRP release did not differ between groups (SAH n = 8, Sham n = 8; p = 0.798). CGRP release values were 186.6 ± 51.16 pg/mL in SAH animals and 162.5 ± 54.44 pg/mL in sham animals. Baseline levels were also not significantly different between groups (p = 0.279; SAH 12.51 ± 1.67 pg/mL vs. Sham 13.27 ± 4.84 pg/mL).

Discussion

The present study examined how CGRP-targeted migraine treatment influences early pathophysiology after experimental SAH. Importantly, the present design modelled the presence of circulating fremanezumab during the hemorrhagic event rather than chronic clinical anti-CGRP treatment exposure. The short interval between administration and SAH induction was selected to reduce variability related to longer-term pharmacokinetic distribution and clearance. The main finding was a temporal dissociation in antibody effect. Animals receiving the CGRP antibody showed improved early functional performance in the rotating pole test and a later timing of progression to humane endpoint, compared with vehicle treated SAH animals, whereas this advantage was not maintained during the later observation period. At the same time, overall survival and neurological scoring did not differ significantly between groups. These findings indicate that anti-CGRP treatment present in the injected blood causing the modelled hemorrhage, does not adversely affect overall 14-day outcome in the pre-chiasmatic single injection model although it may influence the temporal progression of the disease during the early post- hemorrhagic phase (Figs. 3B and 4).

The vascular experiments were included to determine the most appropriate species for examining CGRP-related cerebrovascular responses in the context of hemorrhagic stroke. Rat basilar arteries showed clear CGRP-induced dilation, whereas mouse basilar arteries did not show detectable CGRP-induced dilation under identical experimental conditions (Fig. 1). Additional pharmacological validation demonstrated that CGRP induced dilation in rat basilar arteries is sensitive to CGRP neutralization, similar to in human [65], with fremanezumab shifting the concentration response (Fig. 1C, reproduced from [64] and modulating capsaicin induced vasodilation (Fig. 1D), confirming functional binding of the antibody to rat CGRP. The rat was therefore selected as the relevant species for the subsequent SAH experiments. This observation is important in relation to previous mouse stroke studies examining CGRP blockade. In mice, CGRP receptor antagonism with olcegepant and rimegepant worsened ischemic outcome and was interpreted as reflecting impaired collateral perfusion [48]. Species specific vascular anatomy may also contribute to these differences. Only a minority of C57BL/6J mice possess a complete circulus of Willis [66, 67], whereas rats have been considered a suitable experimental species for studies of cerebrovascular disease [33, 34, 43, 68]. Sex-dependent differences in CGRP signaling and migraine susceptibility have also been suggested, and the inclusion of only male animals therefore represents a limitation of the present study, although we do not necessarily expect the core pathophysiological mechanisms investigated here to differ substantially between male and female animals.

The early biochemical findings support involvement of the trigeminovascular system during the acute phase after hemorrhage. Capsaicin evoked CGRP release from dura mater was reduced after SAH, whereas release from trigeminal ganglion was not significantly altered at this early time point. These findings are consistent with early peptide release followed by depletion of releasable CGRP in meningeal afferents (Fig. 2E). However, as temporal in vivo CGRP measurements were not performed, alternative explanations including altered afferent responsiveness or impaired release capacity cannot be excluded. Independent support for this interpretation is provided by recent work demonstrating that experimental SAH reduces CGRP immunoreactive fibers in rat dura mater and trigeminal nucleus caudalis, attenuates ex vivo dural CGRP release, and reduces CGRP-related expression in trigeminal ganglion [31]. Earlier experimental work also demonstrated that transection of the trigeminal nerve produces prolonged vasoconstriction in cerebral vessels, illustrating that trigeminal signaling can regulate cerebrovascular tone beyond nociceptive signaling [35]. Together, these observations support a model in which SAH activates trigeminal afferents early after hemorrhage, leading to release of CGRP from meningeal projections followed by reduced peptide availability in the peripheral trigeminovascular system. Earlier experimental and clinical observations showing reduced CGRP-containing perivascular fibers after SAH and altered CGRP levels in human cerebrospinal fluid further support this interpretation [26, 32].

Despite evidence of altered CGRP signaling, mechanical sensitivity was not significantly changed at 24 h after SAH. Periorbital and plantar von Frey thresholds were comparable between SAH and sham animals (Fig. 2C–D). This finding indicates that early trigeminovascular activation does not necessarily translate into measurable mechanical allodynia in this experimental setting. As headache is a dominant early symptom after SAH, the von Frey assessments were specifically focused on the acute injury phase and the thunderclap headache-like period where trigeminovascular and CGRP-related mechanisms were expected to be most relevant. In contrast, long-term sensory sensitization and mechanical allodynia after SAH remain relatively unexplored and may warrant further investigation. Trigeminal afferent fibers densely innervate intracranial and meningeal vessels [69, 70], and these co-store CGRP and substance P [71]. In addition, blood products together with rapid increases in intracranial pressure can activate these fibers after hemorrhage [72–74]. However, these processes do not necessarily produce the same behavioral manifestations observed in chronic headache models [75–77]. Mechanical hypersensitivity is well described in migraine [77, 78], but the absence of early mechanical allodynia in the present SAH model suggests that trigeminovascular activation after hemorrhage may follow a different temporal pattern. It is worth mentioning that in the present study, carprofen was administered as standard postoperative analgesia. Prior experimental evidence does however indicate that perioperative NSAID treatment should not interfere with the subsequent development of mechanical allodynia [79].

For the current study we chose an intervention arm with a design to model a clinically relevant worst-case scenario in which a migraine patient receiving CGRP-targeted antibody treatment subsequently develops SAH while the antibody remains present in the circulation. In SAH, blood enters the subarachnoid space and blood-brain barrier disruption is a well-recognized component of early brain injury [7], making the hemorrhagic subtype, the stroke with the highest likelihood of intracerebral exposure to an antibody treatment. In the confined intracranial compartment created by hemorrhage, edema, and elevated intracranial pressure, diffuse vasodilation increases intracranial vascular volume without increasing intracranial space. Under such conditions widespread vasodilation may reduce effective perfusion rather than improve it. Limiting neurogenic vasodilation through CGRP blockade may therefore stabilize early cerebrovascular dynamics and permit faster recovery of cerebral blood flow. The observation of reduced releasable CGRP in dura mater after SAH together with the findings reported by Masood et al. supports the interpretation that trigeminovascular CGRP release occurs early after hemorrhage and may contribute to these vascular dynamics [31]. It should also be noted that CGRP has been reported to exert protective effects on the blood-brain barrier in models of focal ischemia [80, 81], indicating that the role of CGRP may differ between ischemic and hemorrhagic brain injury.

Systemic vasodilators have been examined previously in experimental SAH models. Administration of nitric oxide donors has been reported to improve cerebral blood flow and reduce neuronal injury in rats despite associated systemic hypotension [82]. However, such interventions act primarily through systemic hemodynamic effects on arterial pressure and cerebral perfusion pressure. In contrast, CGRP blockade limits locally triggered neurogenic vasodilation originating from trigeminal afferents within the cranial compartment. The physiological consequences of these two interventions therefore differ substantially, which may explain why inhibition of local CGRP signaling can produce effects distinct from those observed with systemic vasodilatory therapies.

The later phase of the experiment showed a different pattern. The early functional advantage observed in anti-CGRP treated animals was no longer present at 7 and 14 days, and the Kaplan-Meier curves suggested a shift toward later endpoint occurrence in the antibody treated group (Figs. 3B and 4). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, the pattern is compatible with the well described temporal phases of SAH pathophysiology. Early brain injury after SAH is characterized by acute hemodynamic disturbance, blood-brain barrier disruption, cerebral edema, and reduced cerebral perfusion [7, 16]. Several days later, delayed cerebral ischemia and vasospasm become dominant contributors to neurological deterioration [36, 83]. During these later phases endogenous CGRP can function as an important vasodilatory counterbalance within the cerebral circulation. Experimental work has shown that reduced CGRP signaling is associated with enhanced vasospasm and impaired vascular relaxation after SAH [26, 83]. Prolonged blockade of CGRP signaling could therefore become less favorable during the delayed phase even if early inhibition produces transient benefit.

The neurological scoring systems did not reveal clear treatment differences across the observation period (Fig. 5). Interpretation of neurological scores in experimental SAH models can be challenging, as reproducibility and sensitivity of these scales have been questioned in previous work [84–86]. Although rotating pole performance differed between groups, no corresponding effects were observed in composite neurological scoring or survival. The study was not specifically powered for subtle functional differences across all behavioral endpoints, and the broader neurological scales may be less sensitive to temporal changes in motor coordination and exploratory behavior. In contrast, the rotating pole assay represented the primary functional readout in the present study and integrates multiple behavioral parameters, including motivation, sensorimotor coordination, balance, and initiative behavior, rather than isolated single measurements. The observed differences may therefore reflect subtle functional alterations after experimental SAH, consistent with the observed shift in mortality timing. Body weight loss after SAH observed in the present study is also consistent with previous rodent studies reporting early postoperative weight loss following hemorrhage [87, 88]. Body weight loss and recovery followed similar trajectories in both treatment groups, indicating that the general postoperative course was comparable while the principal treatment effect appeared related to the timing of deterioration rather than the overall magnitude of neurological injury (Fig. 3A).

The present findings indicate that CGRP blockade administered shortly before experimental SAH does not markedly worsen overall 14-day outcome. Survival and general neurological scores were comparable between groups, while early sensory motor function was improved in antibody treated animals. The results instead suggest that CGRP signaling may influence different stages of SAH pathophysiology. Early inhibition of CGRP-mediated neurogenic vasodilation may stabilize acute cerebrovascular dynamics, whereas endogenous CGRP may later contribute to vascular compensation during the phase when delayed ischemia and vasospasm become more prominent. A recent case report describing improvement of chronic post-SAH headache during CGRP antagonist therapy illustrates that CGRP-targeted treatment may influence symptoms after hemorrhage, although such observations remain limited and should be interpreted cautiously [89]. For migraine patients receiving CGRP-targeted therapies, these data suggest that SAH occurring during treatment is unlikely to produce a clearly worse overall course, although the temporal pattern of deterioration during admission could differ and may influence clinical interpretation and management. This may reflect complex interactions between vasodilation, intracranial pressure dynamics, and effective tissue perfusion after SAH. Further studies are warranted to investigate time-related dynamics of treatment effects.
