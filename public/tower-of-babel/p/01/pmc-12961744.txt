Voriconazole as a Secondary Prophylaxis for Invasive Fungal Infections in Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation: Real-world Evidence From a Major Transplantation Center in Taiwan
Authors: Yu-Wen Wang, Chih-Ning Cheng, Chien-Ting Lin, Xavier Cheng-Hong Tsai, Jiun-Da Huang, Zong-Da Hsieh, Xiu-Wen Liao, Fang-Ju Lin, Yee-Chun Chen, Ching-Hua Kuo, Shang-Ju Wu, Shu-Wen Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVoriconazole plays an important role in the treatment of invasive fungal infections (IFIs). Keeping voriconazole concentration at appropriate levels is crucial for drug effectiveness and safety. However, it remains unclear whether drug concentrations influence the clinical outcome of secondary prophylaxis in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT).MethodsIn this retrospective analysis, 83 patients receiving allo-HSCT with histories of IFIs responsive to voriconazole were enrolled. These patients continued voriconazole during and after allo-HSCT. Their characteristics, clinical outcomes, and plasma voriconazole concentrations at (1) Days −30 to −9, (2) Days −8 to −1, (3) Days 0 to 14, and (4) Days 15 to 30 of allo-HSCT were examined.ResultsVoriconazole trough concentrations significantly declined after transplantation and remained low for approximately 4 weeks post allo-HSCT, despite dose adjustment. This variation was observed across subgroups defined by age, sex, diagnosis, conditioning regimens, donor type, administration route, or documented drug interactions. The cumulative incidence of breakthrough IFI was 11.4% at 3 months and 18.6% at 1 year after transplantation. Transient decreases in voriconazole trough levels were not significantly associated with breakthrough IFIs. However, a trend toward a lower risk of proven or probable breakthrough IFIs at 12 months has been observed in patients having average voriconazole trough levels ≥ 1 mg/L after allo-HSCT.ConclusionsTransient fluctuations in voriconazole levels occur after allo-HSCT, independent of most clinical factors. These subtherapeutic exposures did not significantly affect the incidence of breakthrough IFIs in this cohort. Larger prospective studies are warranted to further investigate the clinical impact.

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is widely used to treat various hematologic malignancies in an effort to improve survival outcomes; therefore, the number of patients receiving this treatment with histories of invasive fungal infections (IFIs) is increasing [1, 2]. Uncontrolled IFIs may reduce the survival probability of these patients, and secondary antifungal prophylaxis is well established in this real-world scenario [3, 4]. Voriconazole is one of the broad-spectrum antifungal agents that has been used for the treatment of aspergillosis and other serious fungal infections according to clinical guidelines [5–8]. Voriconazole's nonlinear pharmacokinetic profile, involvement of multiple cytochrome P450 enzymes in its metabolism, drug interactions, and other medical conditions result in high inter- and intraindividual variability in the drug's plasma concentration [9–11]. Therefore, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is strongly recommended to minimize the toxicity and maximize the effectiveness of voriconazole, with the optimal therapeutic range being 1–6 mg/L [12–14]. In allo-HSCT, there is a lack of evidence regarding the interactions between voriconazole and conditioning regimens prior to allo-HSCT, as well as the clinical assessment of the outcomes of a secondary prophylactic strategy. This retrospective study investigated the dynamic changes in voriconazole trough concentration before, during, and after allo-HSCT to reveal the effectiveness of voriconazole-based secondary prophylaxis and explore the impact of plasma trough changes on the risk of breakthrough IFI after allo-HSCT.

METHODS

Study Population

This retrospective study screened hospitalized patients who underwent their first allo-HSCT from a related or unrelated donor at National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH) between January 2009 and December 2015. Adult patients were eligible for inclusion if they received voriconazole as secondary prophylaxis during and after allo-HSCT. Patients were excluded if they (1) had no voriconazole TDM data within 30 days before or after or (2) lacked at least 1 TDM measurement within 30 days after allo-HSCT. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of NTUH (200801019R).

The following procedures represent the standard protocol for allo-HSCT at NTUH. Patients were admitted to clean rooms equipped with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration system before receiving conditioning regimens. These regimens, whether myeloablative or reduced intensity, incorporated busulfan and cyclophosphamide as the backbone and were typically initiated 7–8 days before the stem cell infusion. Prophylactic antibiotics, including fluoroquinolones, acyclovir, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, were administered according to institutional protocols. In patients receiving busulfan, prophylactic phenytoin or levetiracetam was started 1 day before busulfan administration and continued until 1 day after completion of therapy [15, 16]. Voriconazole was switched from an oral to an intravenous formulation in patients who had severe mucositis or other gastrointestinal complications. The target trough concentration of voriconazole was 1–6 mg/L. Immunosuppressant therapy consisted of cyclosporine in combination with either short-course methotrexate or mycophenolate sodium, depending on the type of conditioning regimen. Given the significant drug interaction between voriconazole and cyclosporine, cyclosporine dose was generally reduced by 50% at the start of the therapy and subsequently titrated based on cyclosporine TDM results [17]. Immunosuppressants were tapered off gradually according to attending physicians’ clinical judgment.

Data on the patients’ characteristics, underlying hematological diseases, disease status at allo-HSCT, conditioning regimens, donor types, stem cell sources, time to engraftment, anticonvulsant use, voriconazole total daily doses, baseline laboratory data, and post-HSCT liver function were retrieved from electronic medical records. For the calculation of voriconazole total daily dose, a 1:1 conversion ratio was applied between oral (PO) and intravenous (IV) formulation, based on the high oral bioavailability reported previously and the lack of patient-specific bioavailability data [18].

Measurement of Voriconazole Concentrations

The trough concentrations of voriconazole were obtained as clinically indicated, including suspected toxicity, lack of efficacy, or to assess steady-state concentrations during the allo-HSCT process until further monitoring was judged unnecessary by the clinicians. Samples were prepared as described in previous work [19]. In short, the final solutions were injected into a high-performance liquid chromatography-ultraviolet system with an analytical Chromolith® C18 column (4.6 mm × 100 mm, Merck, Germany). The limit of detection and limit of quantification were 0.2 and 0.5 mg/L, respectively. Intraday and interday precision and accuracy were within 15%.

Outcome Definition

For voriconazole concentration analysis, all TDM results obtained within 30 days before or after allo-HSCT were included. Day zero (D0) was defined as the day of stem cell infusion. The primary comparison focused on pre-HSCT (ie, D-30 to D-1) versus post-HSCT (ie, D0 to D30) concentrations. To further investigate temporal changes in voriconazole exposure, trough levels were also categorized into 4 sampling windows: (1) D-30 to D-9, (2) D-8 to D-1, (3) D0 to D14, and (4) D15 to D30. Within each period—including pre-HSCT, post-HSCT, and the 4 defined windows—individual concentrations were averaged for analysis. Only patients with measurements available both pre-HSCT and post-HSCT were included in concentration analysis. All included patients were analyzed for breakthrough IFIs, which were classified according to European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) criteria [20, 21]. Post-HSCT and D0–D14 voriconazole concentrations were used to evaluate their association with breakthrough IFI, using a cutoff value of 1 mg/L. Subjects were followed for at least 1 year after allo-HSCT or until death to capture breakthrough IFI events.

Statistical Analysis

The patients’ characteristics were reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Pre-HSCT and post-HSCT voriconazole trough concentrations were compared using a mixed-effects model, which accounts for repeated measurements and missing data. Age and sex were adjusted in the mixed-effects model. For concentrations categorized into 4 sampling windows, comparisons were also performed using a mixed-effects model, with the D-30 to D-9 window serving as the reference category. Both the cumulative incidence function (CIF) and the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model were used to evaluate 4 scenarios combining 2 concentration categories (post-HSCT and D0 to D14 voriconazole trough levels) and 2 follow-up periods (3 and 12 months). The CIF was applied to estimate the probability of breakthrough IFI while accounting for death as a competing risk, and statistical significance between groups was assessed using Gray's test. The Fine–Gray model, which also accounts for death as a competing event, was used to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) comparing the risk of breakthrough IFI between trough concentration categories. All Fine–Gray models were adjusted for age and sex.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings. First, the comparison of pre-HSCT and post-HSCT concentrations was stratified by dosing route to account for differences in bioavailability. Mean concentrations were recalculated within each dosing route. Consequently, some patients were excluded from either the PO or IV subgroup if their pre- and post-HSCT administrations used different routes. Second, for concentrations categorized into 4 sampling windows, an additional analysis was performed, including only patients with available concentrations across all 4 windows. Third, in the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model, only proven and probable breakthrough IFIs were considered as events of interest, whereas possible IFI cases were treated as censored at the time of their occurrence to restrict the analysis to breakthrough IFI with higher diagnostic certainty.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate differences in pre- and post-HSCT voriconazole concentration changes according to the following factors: (1) age (<45 vs ≥45 years); (2) sex (male vs female); (3) hematologic diagnosis (acute myeloid leukemia [AML] vs non-AML); (4) conditioning regimen (myeloablative conditioning [MAC] vs reduced-intensity conditioning [RIC]); (5) donor type (matched vs mismatched; related vs unrelated); and (6) concurrent phenytoin use (with vs without). All analyses were performed using the mixed-effects model adjusted for age and sex, except when these variables were the stratifying factors in the subgroup analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A two-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Boxplots were generated using R software, and CIF plots were generated using SAS.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Among 83 adult patients who underwent first allo-HSCT and received voriconazole for secondary antifungal prophylaxis between 2009 and 2015, 70 (33 men and 37 women) with at least 1 post-HSCT voriconazole trough concentration measured within 30 days after HSCT were included (Supplementary Figure 1). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of all patients. The median age was 49.5 years (IQR: 37–58). The most common underlying hematologic diagnoses were AML (64.3%), followed by acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL; 18.6%) and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS; 7.1%). Most patients (90.0%) received RIC regimens. Donor types were evenly distributed between matched related (30.0%) and mismatched related (28.6%), with fewer matched unrelated (18.6%) and mismatched unrelated (22.9%) donors. The median follow-up duration after allo-HSCT was 9.4 months; during which 17 patients (24.3%) experienced breakthrough IFIs.

Dynamic Change of Voriconazole Concentrations Before and After Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation

A total of 48 patients with paired voriconazole trough concentrations measured before and after HSCT were included in the concentration analysis. The median voriconazole concentrations before and after HSCT were 1.86 (IQR: 1.06–3.17) and 1.20 (IQR: 0.80–1.70) mg/L, respectively. As shown in Figure 1 (left panel), voriconazole trough concentrations declined substantially after HSCT. Mixed-effects model analysis confirmed a significant reduction in post-HSCT concentrations compared with pre-HSCT levels (mean difference: −0.87 mg/L, P = .0004; Table 2). Despite dose adjustment guided by TDM—with an average increase of 9.7% in total daily dose following HSCT—subtherapeutic exposure remained frequent. Specifically, the proportion of patients with trough concentrations below the prespecified lower limit of 1 mg/L increased from 25.0% in the pre-HSCT period to 41.7% post-HSCT. Further stratification by 4 sampling windows revealed that the decline was most pronounced during the D0 to D14 period (Figure 1, right panel). The median voriconazole concentrations for the 4 sampling time windows were (1) 3.31 (IQR: 2.85–5.08), (2) 1.69 (IQR: 0.94–2.64), (3) 0.86 (IQR: 0.46–1.45), and (4) 1.64 (IQR: 0.96–2.10) mg/L, respectively. Compared to the D-30 to D-9 period, mixed-effects model analysis showed mean differences of −1.17, −2.22, and −1.19 mg/L for the D-8 to D-1, D0 to D14, and D15 to D30 periods, respectively (all P < .05; Table 2). These findings collectively indicated that voriconazole exposure markedly decreased immediately after HSCT, even under comparable or slightly escalated dosing.

To evaluate whether the observed post-HSCT decline in voriconazole concentrations could be influenced by administration route—given that the main dose calculations assumed equivalent between PO and IV doses—a sensitivity analysis stratifying concentrations by formulation was performed. As shown in Supplementary Figure 2, both PO and IV groups exhibited a downward trend in voriconazole concentrations after HSCT, with a more pronounced decrease observed in the IV group. However, statistical significance was reached only in the PO group (Supplementary Table 1; mean difference: −0.61 mg/L, P = .0009), whereas the IV group showed a similar but nonsignificant trend (mean difference: −2.24 mg/L, P = .0710), likely reflecting the limited sample size (PO, n = 39; IV, n = 8). These findings indicate that voriconazole trough concentrations decreased after HSCT for both administration routes. While changes in oral absorption during the post-HSCT cannot be excluded, the decrease observed with IV administration suggests that the post-HSCT reduction in exposure is not solely attributable to formulation differences. An additional sensitivity analysis was performed including only the 10 patients with serial voriconazole trough measurements across all 4 sampling windows, ensuring within-patient comparisons over time. The analysis demonstrated that trough concentrations during the D0 to D14 period were significantly lower than those in the D-30 to D-9 period (Supplementary Table 2; mean difference: −2.02 mg/L, P = .0083), whereas no significant differences were observed for the other time windows. These results further support that the early posttransplant period is particularly vulnerable to subtherapeutic voriconazole exposure.

In subgroup analyses of pre- and post-HSCT voriconazole concentrations (Supplementary Table 3), the significant reduction observed in the overall cohort persisted across subgroups defined by age, sex, and diagnosis (AML vs non-AML). In contrast, patients who received MAC as the conditioning regimen, stem cells from unrelated donors, or concurrent phenytoin therapy did not exhibit a statistically significant post-HSCT decrease. Among these, patients receiving concurrent phenytoin were particularly notable, as their mean post-HSCT difference was minimal (−0.14 mg/L) and the 95% confidence interval was centered near zero (−1.29–1.01), corresponding to a large P value (P = .7892).

Suboptimal Voriconazole Trough Concentrations and Risk of Breakthrough Invasive Fungal Infection

During the follow-up period, 17 breakthrough IFIs were identified among 70 patients. The cumulative incidence of breakthrough IFI at 3 months and 1 year after allo-HSCT was 11.4% and 18.6%, respectively. Breakthrough IFI was classified as possible (47.1%), probable (29.4%), and proven (23.5%) based on EORTC/MSG criteria. Among the 4 cases with proven breakthrough IFIs, 2 patients were diagnosed with Candida fungemia, 1 case was identified as invasive Aspergillus pneumonia, and the other one presented as invasive Aspergillus sinusitis.

The cumulative incidence function was used to estimate the probability of breakthrough IFI while accounting for death as a competing risk. Patients were categorized based on average voriconazole trough concentrations post-HSCT using a therapeutic threshold of 1 mg/L (≥1 mg/L vs <1 mg/L). No significant differences in the cumulative incidence of breakthrough IFI were observed between the 2 groups at either 3 or 12 months (Gray's test, both P > .05; Figure 2A and B). Because a marked drop in voriconazole concentrations was frequently observed during the D0 to D14 period, we further evaluated whether concentration levels within this period were associated with the risk of breakthrough IFI (Figure 2C and D). Among the 62 patients with available D0 to D14 concentrations, no significant differences in CIF estimates were observed between the 2 concentration categories, consistent with the post-HSCT findings.

A sensitivity analysis was performed in which only proven and probable breakthrough IFIs were considered as events of interest, while possible IFI cases were treated as censored at the time of occurrence to restrict the analysis to infections with higher diagnostic certainty. Because no events occurred in the ≥1 mg/L groups at 3 months, only the 12-month follow-up period was evaluated (Table 3, models 5–6). For post-HSCT trough concentrations (n = 70), the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model showed a trend toward a lower risk of breakthrough IFI in the ≥1 mg/L group compared with <1 mg/L (sHR = 0.18, 95% CI 0.03–1.27, P = .0852). Similarly, for D0 to D14 trough concentrations (n = 62), the ≥1 mg/L group had a lower, though nonsignificant, risk compared with <1 mg/L (sHR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.06–2.74, P = .3537). Compared with the primary analysis including all IFI events, the point estimates in this sensitivity analysis were generally lower, suggesting a potential association between trough concentrations and the risk of higher-certainty events. Nevertheless, as statistical significance was not reached.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this study may be among the first to report a substantial reduction in voriconazole levels in patients undergoing allo-HSCT during secondary prophylaxis for IFI, as well as a potential association with the risk of posttransplant breakthrough IFI [22–24]. Although the mechanism of this alteration has not yet been clarified, it seems that allo-HSCT may have a negative impact on voriconazole trough levels.

Voriconazole trough concentrations decreased significantly after allo-HSCT and remained in low levels for about 4 weeks after transplantation, underscoring the potential need for intensified early drug concentration monitoring. Voriconazole levels were difficult to maintain within the therapeutic range despite dose adjustments. These fluctuations appeared unrelated to age, sex, diagnosis, conditioning regimens, donor types, and administration route. Interestingly, patients receiving concurrent phenytoin did not show a substantial additional decrease in voriconazole levels after allo-HSCT. A likely explanation is that phenytoin, initiated during the conditioning period before transplantation, had already induced voriconazole metabolism; therefore, only minimal additional decline was observed following allo-HSCT. Taken together, these findings suggest that allo-HSCT introduces complex and dynamic changes in voriconazole exposure. The pronounced decline and sustained low concentrations—despite dose adjustments and in the absence of clear clinical predictors—imply that transplantation-related physiological alterations may substantially affect voriconazole pharmacokinetics.

Given the markedly reduced voriconazole exposure observed in the early posttransplant period, the implications for coadministered immunosuppressants warrant careful consideration. In our cohort, cyclosporine was initiated at a reduced dose, typically 50% of the standard dose as recommended, and the trough levels of cyclosporine were carefully monitored during coadministration with voriconazole [17, 25]. Under usual circumstances, concurrent use of voriconazole and calcineurin inhibitors was considered safe as long as TDM and appropriate dose adjustments are applied to calcineurin inhibitors. However, in light of the consistently low voriconazole concentrations after allo-HSCT, the routine practice of initial calcineurin inhibitor dose reduction may be unnecessary, as the impact of voriconazole-mediated drug–drug interactions is likely minimal during this period. Moreover, letermovir is now increasingly coadministered in contemporary practice and is known to interact with multiple transplant medications. Unfortunately, the impact of letermovir could not be evaluated in the present study because it was not yet available during the study period. This emerging combination therapy may further complicate posttransplant pharmacotherapy, highlighting the need for future research to delineate optimal dosing strategies when voriconazole, calcineurin inhibitors, and letermovir are used together [26, 27].

To further assess the clinical impact of these pharmacokinetic changes, we next examined the incidence of breakthrough IFIs during voriconazole secondary prophylaxis. The cumulative incidence of proven, probable, and possible breakthrough IFIs was 11.4% at 3 months and 18.6% at 1 year. The 1-year incidence is slightly higher than those in a retrospective study conducted by the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (12%) and in a prospective phase 2 clinical trial (6.7%) when voriconazole was prescribed as the secondary prophylaxis in allo-HSCT patients [28, 29]. Notably, in our cohort, voriconazole was continued throughout the entire course of allo-HSCT, whereas in the phase 2 clinical trial, voriconazole was discontinued 3 days before allo-HSCT and restarted at least 48 hours after completion of conditioning chemotherapy [28, 30]. Differences in IFI incidence across studies may reflect regional variation in fungal epidemiology, differences in patient characteristics, and local clinical practices [31]. Importantly, whether the transient reductions in voriconazole trough concentrations observed immediately after allo-HSCT had any impact on breakthrough IFI risk remains unclear. To address this, we next examined the relationship between voriconazole exposure and the incidence of breakthrough IFIs in our cohort.

Unexpectedly, the transient decreases in voriconazole trough concentrations were not associated with an increased risk of breakthrough IFI at either 3 or 12 months. However, in a sensitivity analysis restricted to proven and probable breakthrough IFIs, a trend toward lower risk of breakthrough IFIs was observed among patients with voriconazole trough levels ≥ 1 mg/L. This finding suggests that prolonged periods of subtherapeutic voriconazole exposure may be associated with an increased risk of breakthrough IFIs, although the trend did not reach statistical significance. Several potential explanations for this finding can be proposed. First, the duration of the subtherapeutic voriconazole levels was insufficient to expose patients to a significantly higher risk of IFI, in a context where the patients’ previous IFIs had been proven voriconazole sensitive. Second, voriconazole tissue concentrations may correlate poorly with plasma levels, at least during the allo-HSCT period. Third, during the time in which low levels were observed, the patients were hospitalized in rooms with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, which make the environment as clean as possible. In summary, transient reductions in voriconazole trough concentrations were not associated with a significant increase in overall breakthrough IFIs, although a trend toward lower risk was observed for proven and probable IFIs in patients with trough levels ≥ 1 mg/L. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the study's limited sample size and observational design.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design. First, our transplant unit employs HEPA-filtered protective environments and adheres to standardized protocols, including the conditioning regimens, anticonvulsant prophylaxis, and cyclosporine-based immunosuppressant. These consistent practices may have reduced environmental mold exposure and created a uniform pattern of comedications. Consequently, the observed relationship between early posttransplant reductions in voriconazole exposure and breakthrough IFI risk may not be directly generalizable to centers with different environmental controls or transplant protocols. Second, voriconazole concentrations were not measured at regular intervals for all patients, which may have introduced variability in exposure estimates. Third, the bioavailability of oral voriconazole may change after allo-HSCT, and some patients may briefly need to switch to intravenous voriconazole due to severe anorexia, mucositis, or gastrointestinal graft-versus-host disease. To simplify the analysis, we used a 1:1 ratio as the equivalent dose for oral and intravenous voriconazole and calculated the daily voriconazole dose accordingly, although this assumption is not perfectly accurate [18]. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis suggested that the impact of this assumption is likely small.

In conclusion, when voriconazole was used as a secondary antifungal prophylaxis, there was a noticeable decline in voriconazole trough concentration at D0–D14 after allo-HSCT. Early voriconazole TDM may assist in identifying this transient decrease. Although most subtherapeutic exposures were brief and did not significantly affect breakthrough IFI risk, a potential trend toward increased risk in certain subgroups cannot be excluded. Larger prospective studies are warranted to clarify the clinical impact of these transient voriconazole pharmacokinetic changes in allo-HSCT patients.
