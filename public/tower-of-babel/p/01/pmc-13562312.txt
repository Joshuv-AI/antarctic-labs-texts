Safety and Tolerability of Adjunctive Lumateperone for the Treatment of Major Depressive Disorder: A Pooled Analysis of Two Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trials
Authors: Willie R Earley, Suresh Durgam, Susan G Kozauer, Changzheng Chen, Raffaele Migliore, Christoph U Correll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesLumateperone, a simultaneous modulator of serotonin, dopamine, and glutamate neurotransmission, demonstrated efficacy and safety as adjunctive therapy in two phase III, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials in patients with major depressive disorder with inadequate antidepressant therapy (ADT) response. The objective of this pooled analysis of Studies 501 and 502 was to investigate the safety and tolerability of lumateperone 42 mg + ADT.MethodsData were pooled from two studies that enrolled adults (aged 18–65 years) with Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition-defined major depressive disorder with inadequate response to one to two ADTs in the current depressive episode (Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale Total score ≥ 24; Clinical Global Impression-Severity score ≥ 4). Patients were randomized to 6 weeks of oral lumateperone 42 mg + ADT or placebo + ADT. Safety measures included adverse events, body morphology, cardiometabolic parameters, prolactin levels, extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), and suicidality.ResultsThe pooled population comprised 964 patients (lumateperone + ADT, n = 483; placebo + ADT, n = 481). Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 68.1% and 45.1% of the lumateperone + ADT and placebo + ADT groups, respectively. Treatment discontinuation because of an adverse event occurred in 8.7% of the lumateperone + ADT group and 0.8% of the placebo + ADT group. The most common TEAEs (≥ 5%, greater than twice placebo + ADT) were dizziness, dry mouth, somnolence, nausea, fatigue, and diarrhea. Of patients experiencing TEAEs, the majority (96.3%) had events of mild or moderate severity. Potentially clinically significant weight increase (≥ 7%) occurred in 0.4% and 1.3% of the lumateperone + ADT and placebo + ADT groups, respectively. Changes in cardiometabolic parameters and prolactin levels were minimal and similar to placebo + ADT. No notable changes occurred in EPS-related scales. EPS-related TEAEs occurred in 5.8% and 1.7% of lumateperone + ADT and placebo + ADT groups, respectively. The most common EPS-related TEAE was tremor (lumateperone + ADT, 3.9%; placebo + ADT, 0.2%), which had a mean duration of 11.9 days in the lumateperone + ADT group. Emergence of suicidal ideation was infrequent (lumateperone + ADT, 1.6%; placebo + ADT, 2.5%).ConclusionsLumateperone 42 mg + ADT had a good safety profile and was generally well tolerated in this pooled analysis, with low risks of weight gain, cardiometabolic abnormalities, and EPS with short-term treatment but with higher discontinuation rates because of adverse events (8.7%) versus placebo + ADT, in patients with major depressive disorder with inadequate ADT response.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04985942 (registered 22 July, 2021); NCT05061706 (registered 20 September, 2021).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40263-026-01314-8.

Key Points

In this pooled analysis from two phase III studies, lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to antidepressant therapy (ADT) was generally well tolerated in patients with major depressive disorder with inadequate ADT response.Changes in cardiometabolic parameters, prolactin levels, body morphology, and extrapyramidal symptoms-related scales were minimal with lumateperone 42 mg + ADT; treatment-emergent adverse events and discontinuation because of adverse events were more common in the lumateperone 42 mg + ADT group versus the placebo + ADT group.First onset of treatment-emergent adverse events occurred most often within the first week of treatment, and the duration of the most common treatment-emergent adverse events was generally similar between the lumateperone 42 mg + ADT and placebo + ADT groups.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a burdensome and disabling mental illness with high prevalence rates that are increasing over time [1, 2]. Major depressive disorder is also associated with comorbidities and presents a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, and obesity [3–7]. Patients with MDD are particularly vulnerable to suicidal ideation, which in turn worsens depression severity and can lead to difficulty in identifying feelings, known as alexithymia [8, 9]. Treating MDD is challenging because of factors such as varied clinical presentation, symptomatology, and treatment effects [2, 10].

Current treatment guidelines for MDD include antidepressant therapy (ADT) with a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor [11]. Inadequate response to ADT is common, with approximately 40% of patients achieving response after initial ADT in the real-world Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) study [12]. In addition to inadequate response, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors are associated with tolerability concerns, including gastrointestinal effects (e.g., nausea, diarrhea, dyspepsia), cardiovascular concerns (e.g., change in heart rate, QT interval prolongation), sexual dysfunction (e.g., reduced libido, arousal dysfunction, orgasmic dysfunction), and weight gain [13, 14]. Suboptimal medication adherence is common (71%) in people with MDD [15], and adverse treatment effects are prominent reasons for nonadherence [16, 17].

Augmenting ADT with an atypical antipsychotic is one of several strategies used when patients have an inadequate ADT response [18–22]. Several second-generation antipsychotics are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as adjunctive therapy to ADT in patients with MDD and inadequate response to ADT, including aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, quetiapine extended release, and cariprazine [23–27]. Treatment with atypical antipsychotics adjunctive to ADT has been associated with side effects (e.g., akathisia, parkinsonism, sedation, metabolic abnormalities, weight gain) and a significantly increased risk for discontinuation because of adverse events (AEs) compared with adjunctive placebo in patients with MDD [20–22, 28]. Because treatment nonadherence can lead to increased risk of relapse and recurrence as well as worsened depression severity in patients with MDD [29], an effective treatment option with a favorable safety profile is needed.

Lumateperone is an atypical antipsychotic indicated for: treatment of schizophrenia in adults; treatment of depressive episodes associated with bipolar I or II disorder (bipolar depression) in adults, as monotherapy and as adjunctive therapy with lithium or valproate; and treatment of MDD in adults as adjunct to ADT [30]. Lumateperone is rapidly absorbed, with a mean time to maximum concentration of ~ 3–4 h after oral administration [31]; with once-daily administration, lumateperone steady state is reached in approximately 5 days [30, 32]. Lumateperone is a potent serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonist, a dopamine D2 receptor presynaptic partial agonist and postsynaptic antagonist, a D1 receptor-dependent indirect modulator of glutamatergic AMPA (α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid) and NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) currents, and a serotonin reuptake inhibitor [33, 34]. Lumateperone has negligible binding to H1 histaminergic, 5-HT2C, and muscarinic receptors [33] and a low striatal D2 receptor occupancy (39%) at the effective and approved dose of 42 mg/day compared with other antipsychotics (65–80%) at efficacious doses [35]. These characteristics of lumateperone may be relevant for patients with MDD, although applicability may vary across clinical populations.

The efficacy and safety of lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to ADT were demonstrated in two phase III, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies (Study 501 [NCT04985942]; Study 502 [NCT05061706]) in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response [36, 37]. In both trials, lumateperone 42 mg + ADT met primary and key secondary efficacy endpoints, significantly improving the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) Total score and Clinical Global Impression-Severity score from baseline to day 43 compared with placebo + ADT. Lumateperone 42 mg + ADT had a good safety profile and was generally well tolerated in both trials; additional safety outcomes such as time to first onset of treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs), duration of TEAEs, and shifts in cardiometabolic parameters during the studies will provide a more comprehensive view of the adjunctive lumateperone safety profile.

This pooled analysis of Study 501 and Study 502 evaluated the safety and tolerability of lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to ADT in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response. Compared with the individual study reports [36, 37], the current pooled analysis utilizes the benefits of a larger sample size to provide a more comprehensive safety analysis with additional outcomes that will enhance understanding of the safety profile of adjunctive lumateperone in this population.

Methods

Study Designs and Trial Oversight

Data from two identically designed, phase III, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to ADT (Study 501 [NCT04985942] and Study 502 [NCT05061706]) were pooled for analysis as prospectively specified. Detailed study designs have been previously published [36, 37]. Briefly, both studies included a 2-week screening period, a 6-week double-blind treatment period, and a 1-week safety follow-up period. Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to ADT or placebo adjunctive to ADT for 6 weeks, administered orally once daily.

The studies were approved by the appropriate institutional review board (IRB)/independent ethics committee (Study 501: IRB Protocol #20213126; Study 502: IRB Protocol #20214571). The protocols were initially approved by the central IRB (WCG, Cary, NC, USA) in the USA. In addition, local ethics committees approved them in individual countries. The studies were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before entering the studies.

Patients

Studies 501 and 502, which had identical eligibility criteria, enrolled adults (aged 18–65 years) with Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition-defined MDD [10] who were experiencing a current major depressive episode (MADRS Total score ≥ 24 and Clinical Global Impression-Severity score ≥ 4) and had a Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self Report-16 item score ≥ 14 [38–40]. In the current major depressive episode, patients had inadequate response to one to two courses of ADT, defined as a < 50% improvement with ≥ 6-week ADT monotherapy. Patients were excluded if there was a ≥ 25% decrease in MADRS Total or Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self Report-16 item Total scores between screening and baseline or a lifetime history of treatment resistance.

Safety Assessments

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics, treatment duration, and treatment exposure were reported. Safety in the pooled studies was evaluated by AEs, clinical laboratory parameters, vital signs, extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), Hy’s law criteria, electrocardiogram measures, and suicidality. Extrapyramidal symptoms were assessed with the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale [39], Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale (BARS) [41], and Simpson-Angus Scale (SAS) [42]. Suicidality was evaluated using the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale [43].

Statistical Analyses

The pooled safety population included all randomized patients who received one or more doses of the study drug in Study 501 or Study 502. Descriptive statistics were presented for continuous variables, and frequency distributions were presented for categorical variables. Evaluation of AEs included incidence, severity, relation to the study drug, discontinuation, first onset, and duration. Adverse events were analyzed using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities Version 24.0. An AE was considered a TEAE if it was not present before the first dose of the double-blind study drug or was present before the first dose but increased in severity after the first dose. Adverse event severity was graded as mild (usually transient, may require only minimal treatment or therapeutic intervention), moderate (usually alleviated with additional specific therapeutic intervention, causes discomfort but poses no significant or permanent risk of harm), or severe (interrupts usual activities of daily living, significantly affects clinical status, or may require intensive therapeutic intervention).

Changes from baseline to end of treatment (EOT) in vital signs, laboratory parameters, and EPS-related scales were analyzed descriptively. Potentially clinically significant (PCS) changes in weight, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, glucose, and triglycerides were evaluated. Shifts in cardiometabolic laboratory parameters, measured under fasting conditions, from baseline to EOT were reported descriptively. The incidence rates of EPS-related TEAEs were evaluated by broad and narrow standardized MedDRA query. Treatment-emergent akathisia (baseline BARS ≤ 2 to BARS > 2 during treatment) and parkinsonism (baseline SAS ≤ 3 to SAS > 3 during treatment) were assessed. Missing data in safety assessments were left ‘as-is’ and no missing data imputation was implemented.

Results

Patient Population

Of 964 patients treated in the pooled safety population (lumateperone + ADT, n = 483; placebo + ADT, n = 481), 91.4% completed double-blind treatment (Fig. 1). The most common reasons for treatment discontinuation included AEs in the lumateperone + ADT group and withdrawal of consent in the placebo + ADT group.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were similar between pooled treatment groups (Table 1). The majority of patients were female (652 [67.6%]) and White (829 [86.0%]). The mean age was 45 years in the lumateperone + ADT group and 46 years in the placebo + ADT group. Most patients (854 [88.6%]) had experienced one ADT failure in the current depressive episode. The most common ADT during double-blind treatment was citalopram/escitalopram, taken by 32.7% of the lumateperone + ADT group and 30.6% of the placebo + ADT group. As previously published, patients in both treatment groups had moderate-to-marked depression at baseline based on mean MADRS Total score and Clinical Global Impression-Severity score [36, 37, 44].

Treatment duration was similar between groups, with mean exposure 38.9 days (range, 1–53 days) in the lumateperone + ADT group and 41.3 days (range, 1–54 days) in the placebo + ADT group. Of 964 patients who received one or more doses of treatment, most had ≥ 6 weeks of treatment exposure (lumateperone + ADT, 67.1%; placebo + ADT, 73.6%). The total treatment exposure corresponded to 51.5 and 54.4 patient-years in the lumateperone + ADT and placebo + ADT groups, respectively.

Adverse Events (AEs)

Treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) occurred in 68.1% of patients in the lumateperone + ADT group and 45.1% of those in the placebo + ADT group (Table 2). The most common TEAEs (occurring in ≥ 5% in the lumateperone + ADT group and greater than twice the rate of placebo + ADT) in the pooled population were dizziness, dry mouth, somnolence, nausea, fatigue, and diarrhea. Treatment-emergent AEs occurring in ≥ 2% of patients in the lumateperone + ADT group and at a greater incidence than with placebo + ADT are listed in Table S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Of patients experiencing TEAEs, the majority (526/546 [96.3%]) experienced TEAEs of mild (lumateperone + ADT, 187/329 [56.8%]; placebo + ADT, 149/217 [68.7%]) or moderate (lumateperone + ADT, 126/329 [38.3%]; placebo + ADT, 64/217 [29.5%]) severity. The common TEAEs (reported by grouped terms) in the lumateperone + ADT group were predominantly mild or moderate in severity (100% of patients experiencing dizziness, nausea, fatigue, and diarrhea; 96.7% experiencing somnolence; and 95.1% experiencing dry mouth). The number of patients discontinuing treatment because of an AE was 42 (8.7%) in the lumateperone + ADT group and 4 (0.8%) in the placebo + ADT group (Table S2 of the ESM). Adverse events leading to discontinuation in > 1% of patients in any group, according to individual preferred terms, were dizziness (lumateperone + ADT, n = 9 [1.9%]; placebo + ADT, n = 1 [0.2%]) and fatigue (lumateperone + ADT, n = 6 [1.2%]; placebo + ADT, n = 0).

Serious AEs during treatment occurred in one patient each in the lumateperone + ADT group (polypectomy, moderate) and the placebo + ADT group (joint dislocation, severe); these serious AEs did not lead to treatment discontinuation, were unrelated to the study drug, and resolved. No patients died during the treatment period.

Of TEAEs potentially related to serotonergic effects, no patients experienced treatment-emergent serotonin syndrome, hyponatremia, or angle closure glaucoma. Treatment-emergent AEs of sexual dysfunction occurred in two patients (0.4%) in the lumateperone + ADT group (one event of each: erectile dysfunction, loss of libido, and sexual dysfunction) and one patient (0.2%) in the placebo + ADT group (libido decreased).

First onset of TEAEs occurred most often within the first week of treatment (lumateperone + ADT, 33.7%; placebo + ADT, 7.7%) (Table 3). In the lumateperone + ADT group, rates of first TEAE onset generally decreased over time after week 1. The rate of first onset of the most common TEAEs (dizziness, dry mouth, somnolence, nausea, fatigue, and diarrhea) decreased to <2% by the second week and <1% by the fourth week of treatment.

Duration of the most common TEAEs was generally similar between the lumateperone + ADT and placebo + ADT groups (Table 4). Treatment-emergent AEs of nausea and diarrhea had the shortest mean durations in the lumateperone + ADT group (nausea: 7.2 days vs placebo + ADT, 11.0 days; diarrhea: 7.3 days vs placebo + ADT, 12.2 days), and dry mouth had the longest duration (lumateperone + ADT, 22.8 days vs placebo + ADT, 17.3 days). The mean duration of fatigue was longer in the lumateperone + ADT group compared with placebo + ADT, while the range was similar between groups (mean [range], lumateperone + ADT, 20.7 [2–43] [2-43] days; placebo + ADT, 11.8 [3–40] [3-40] days).

Cardiometabolic Parameters, Body Morphology, and Prolactin Levels

There were no clinically relevant changes in body morphology, including weight, body mass index, and waist circumference at EOT (Table 5). The percentage of patients with a PCS increase (≥ 7%) in weight was 0.4% in the lumateperone + ADT group and 1.3% in the placebo + ADT group. Mean changes in cardiometabolic parameters and prolactin were small and not clinically meaningful across treatment groups (Table 5). The incidence of patients meeting the PCS criterion for total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, glucose, and triglycerides at EOT was lower in the lumateperone + ADT group compared with the placebo + ADT group.

Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

Extrapyramidal symptom-related TEAEs per broad standardized MedDRA query occurred in 5.8% of patients in the lumateperone + ADT group and 1.7% in the placebo + ADT group (Table 6). The most common EPS-related TEAE was tremor, occurring in 19 patients (3.9%) in the lumateperone + ADT group and 1 (0.2%) in the placebo + ADT group. In the lumateperone + ADT group, the mean duration of tremor was 11.9 days. All events of tremor were mild (15/19 [78.9%]) or moderate (4/19 [21.1%]) in the lumateperone + ADT group and mild (1/1 [100%]) in the placebo + ADT group. In the lumateperone + ADT group, the first onset of tremor occurred in the first 2 weeks of treatment for 15 of the 19 patients experiencing tremor (≤ 1 week, n = 5; > 1 week and ≤ 2 weeks, n = 10). Tremor led to treatment discontinuation in one patient in the lumateperone + ADT group. Treatment-emergent AEs of akathisia or restlessness occurred in ≤ 1% of patients in both treatment groups. Per narrow standardized MedDRA query, EPS-related TEAEs occurred in 1.4% of the lumateperone + ADT group and 0.6% of the placebo + ADT group. No notable changes occurred in the EPS-related scales Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale, BARS, or SAS (Table 6). The incidences of akathisia, defined by categorical shifts from baseline in BARS scores, and parkinsonism, defined by shifts in SAS scores, were low during treatment (Table 6).

Additional Safety Outcomes

No patients met criteria for Hy’s law, defined as alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase ≥ 3× the upper limit of normal with total bilirubin ≥ 2× upper limit of normal and alkaline phosphatase < 2× upper limit of normal, and no other cause for liver result abnormalities. No notable changes occurred in alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase levels during treatment. The incidence of patients meeting PCS hepatic laboratory criteria was low.

Incidence of PCS changes in blood pressure and pulse rate was low, with no patient in either treatment group meeting PCS criteria for increased systolic blood pressure, decreased diastolic blood pressure, or increased pulse rate. No patients in either treatment group had PR interval ≥ 250 ms. Reduction of ≥ 20 in systolic blood pressure or reduction of ≥ 10 in diastolic blood pressure while changing from supine to standing position was similar between treatment groups (lumateperone + ADT, n = 30/457 [6.6%]; placebo + ADT, n = 29/470 [6.2%]).

Six patients (1.4%) in the lumateperone + ADT group and 11 (2.4%) in the placebo + ADT group had a QT Fridericia-corrected interval ≥ 450 ms; no patients had an interval ≥ 500 ms. No patients in the lumateperone + ADT group and one patient in the placebo + ADT group had an increase in QT Fridericia-corrected interval > 60 ms.

According to the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale, no suicidal behavior occurred during treatment. Rates of emergent suicidal ideation were low and similar between groups (lumateperone + ADT, 1.6%; placebo + ADT, 2.5%). No emergence of serious suicidal ideation occurred during treatment. One TEAE of suicidal ideation occurred in the placebo + ADT group.

Discussion

In this pooled analysis of two phase III, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, lumateperone 42 mg adjunctive to ADT had a good safety profile and was generally well tolerated in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response. The favorable safety profile for adjunctive lumateperone in this study was consistent with that reported in other lumateperone studies in patients with schizophrenia [45–47], bipolar depression [48–50], and MDD with mixed features [50].

Medication adherence is a key challenge in providing optimal treatment for patients with MDD, and studies report that 42–71% of patients with MDD are nonadherent or prematurely discontinue treatment [15, 17, 29, 51]. For patients with depression, common reasons for nonadherence are adverse effects including weight gain, sexual dysfunction, and fatigue [17]. In pooled analyses of approved antipsychotics in patients with MDD, the most common events leading to discontinuation for quetiapine XR adjunctive to ADT were somnolence (150 mg/day: 2.9%; 300 mg/day: 3.2%) and sedation (150 mg/day: 1.9%; 300 mg/day: 4.8%) [52], and for cariprazine adjunctive to ADT, akathisia (1.5 mg/day: 0.6%; 3.0 mg/day: 2.2%) [53]. The only AEs leading to discontinuation in > 1% of patients treated with adjunctive lumateperone in this study were dizziness (1.9%) and fatigue (1.2%). Overall, in this analysis, a greater proportion of patients treated with lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT discontinued because of an AE (8.7% vs 0.8%). The first onset of most TEAEs occurred early in the course of treatment with lumateperone + ADT, which may have contributed to the imbalance in discontinuation rates observed between treatment arms. The majority of patients (88.0%) completed double-blind adjunctive lumateperone treatment.

While adjunctive antipsychotic treatment is Food and Drug Administration approved for improving depression symptoms in patients with MDD, individual antipsychotics have varying safety profiles [20, 22]. Treatment side effects are an important consideration in patients with MDD, as additional effects may lead to limited tolerability in patients who experience a high disease-related symptom burden [54]. Understanding the safety profile associated with specific treatment options can be informative in selecting a treatment that would be most compatible for each patient. Findings from other studies suggest that the tolerability profile of lumateperone may differ from that of some atypical antipsychotics. A meta-analysis of atypical antipsychotics for adjunctive treatment of MDD identified several AEs linked to approved drugs, reporting that aripiprazole was associated with akathisia, sedation, and weight gain, and quetiapine was associated with sedation, metabolic disturbances, and weight gain [20]. In pooled analyses of adjunctive antipsychotics in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response, the most common TEAE was akathisia with aripiprazole (24.8%), weight increase with brexpiprazole (7.3%), dry mouth with quetiapine XR (150 mg/day: 27.3%; 300 mg/day: 39.7%), and headache with cariprazine (8.4%) [52, 53, 55, 56]. The most common TEAE (via grouped terms; occurring in ≥ 5% and greater than twice the rate of placebo + ADT) with adjunctive lumateperone in this study was dizziness (16.6%), followed by dry mouth (12.6%). The majority of the most common TEAEs (dizziness, dry mouth, somnolence, nausea, fatigue, and diarrhea) were of mild or moderate severity (95–100% of patients experiencing the TEAEs). Overall, lumateperone + ADT was associated with a greater proportion of patients experiencing TEAEs (68.1%) compared with placebo + ADT (45.1%). The onset of TEAEs with adjunctive lumateperone occurred most often in the first week of treatment, with rates of the most common TEAEs decreasing thereafter to <1% by the fourth week. Thus, the most common TEAEs occurred early in the course of treatment, with the shortest mean durations observed for nausea and diarrhea and the longest mean durations observed for dry mouth and fatigue. Because depression is significantly associated with the presence of comorbidities [3], and comorbidities often lead to polypharmacy which can be burdensome to patients [16], a medication for MDD with few adverse effects would be advantageous.

Antidepressants and antipsychotics are commonly associated with metabolic disturbances, including weight gain, increased risk of diabetes, altered glucose and lipid metabolism, and blood pressure changes [14, 57, 58]. In patients with MDD with inadequate response, mean change in weight from baseline to week 6 of adjunctive treatment with other antipsychotics ranged from +0.7 to +1.7 kg, with clinically significant weight gain (≥ 7% increase from baseline) occurring in 1.9–7.2% of patients [52, 53, 55, 56]. Six weeks of adjunctive lumateperone treatment in this study was associated with a minimal change from baseline in weight (-0.1 kg) and a low occurrence of clinically significant weight gain (0.4% of patients). Although 6-week treatment may not be a long enough study duration to fully capture longitudinal effects on metabolic parameters, there were no clinically relevant increases in laboratory parameters during short-term adjunctive lumateperone treatment in this pooled analysis, and the rates of PCS changes in cardiometabolic parameters were low.

Extrapyramidal symptoms are potential serious adverse effects of antipsychotics and can lead to impaired functioning, cognitive decline, and medication nonadherence [59, 60]. One systematic review reported the prevalence rates of parkinsonism, akathisia, and tardive dyskinesia being as high as 20%, 11%, and 7%, respectively, in patients taking antipsychotics [60]. Akathisia was a common TEAE occurring with adjunctive aripiprazole (24.8%) and adjunctive cariprazine (10.8%) in pooled analyses of patients with MDD with inadequate response [53, 55]. Rates of EPS-related AEs with quetiapine XR 150 mg/day and 300 mg/day were 3.8% and 6.4% in a pooled analysis [52]. In this pooled analysis, EPS-related TEAEs occurred in 5.8% of the lumateperone + ADT group and 1.7% of the placebo + ADT group. The most common EPS-related TEAE in this study was tremor, occurring in a greater proportion of patients in the lumateperone + ADT group versus the placebo + ADT group (3.9% vs 0.2%). In the lumateperone + ADT group, tremor TEAEs occurred mostly in the first 2 weeks of treatment, lasted for a mean duration of 11.9 days, and led to treatment discontinuation in 1 of the 19 patients experiencing tremor. The incidence of akathisia or restlessness TEAEs was low with adjunctive lumateperone, occurring in 1.0% of patients. The lack of changes in clinician-rated EPS scales during treatment, and the low incidence of akathisia and parkinsonism defined by shifts in EPS scales, indicates a low risk of EPS with adjunctive lumateperone. Although the occurrence of tremor was greater with lumateperone + ADT versus placebo + ADT, the events occurred early in the course of treatment and with limited duration, and discontinuation because of tremor was not common.

Several limitations need to be considered when evaluating the results of this study. First, the two trials used in the pooled analyses were placebo-controlled trials without an active control arm. Therefore, comparisons with other studies investigating second-generation antipsychotics as adjunctive treatments for patients with MDD are only indirect, and results may be affected by differences in study design, participating sites and countries, and patient population. Secondly, as the studies included in this pooled analysis were randomized controlled trials that had relevant inclusion and exclusion criteria, it limits the generalizability of these results in patients treated in real-world settings. Thus, the results of this analysis are most applicable to a relatively selected clinical trial population excluding patients outside the age range of 18–65 years and patients with imminent suicidal risk, comorbid psychiatric illnesses other than MDD (bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, schizophreniform disorder, or other psychotic disorder), or comedications not allowed in the studies. Thirdly, only patients with inadequate ADT response were included, and results cannot be extrapolated to patients with treatment-resistant depression. Finally, the short-term duration of the studies is an additional limitation; thus, longitudinal changes may not be fully captured for some safety parameters. However, a recent open-label long-term study (NCT05061719) in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response indicated that adjunctive lumateperone was generally well tolerated and did not result in the emergence of new safety signals up to 26 weeks of treatment [61].

The two studies included in this pooled analysis had identical study designs, including identical eligibility criteria, collection of efficacy and safety assessments, schedule of evaluation, and allowance of concomitant ADT, which limits the degree of potential study heterogeneity; a formal heterogeneity assessment was not performed. Differences between the safety results of Study 501 and Study 502 include a greater TEAE incidence and discontinuation rate because of AEs with lumateperone + ADT in Study 502 and slight variations in the most common TEAEs between studies. Geographical site locations were similar between Study 501 and Study 502 (Europe and USA), except that Study 501 included sites in Asia and Study 502 included sites in South America. These differences explain the greater proportion of Asian patients in Study 501 and the greater proportion of Hispanic/Latino patients in Study 502 [36, 37].

Conclusions

This pooled analysis of two phase III trials demonstrated a favorable safety profile of adjunctive lumateperone 42 mg in patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response. Although the TEAE incidence was greater with lumateperone + ADT (68.1%) versus placebo + ADT (45.1%) and the discontinuation rate because of AEs was 8.7% with lumateperone + ADT, most patients experiencing TEAEs had events of mild-to-moderate severity, with the first TEAE onset most often occurring in the first week of treatment. Changes in cardiometabolic parameters, prolactin levels, and body morphology with 6-week adjunctive lumateperone treatment were minimal and similar to those seen with adjunctive placebo. No notable changes were observed in EPS-related scales, and the risk of EPS was low with adjunctive lumateperone. These data suggest that adjunctive lumateperone 42 mg may be a well-tolerated adjunctive treatment option for patients with MDD with inadequate ADT response.
