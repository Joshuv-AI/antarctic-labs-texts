Application of a novel LC–MS-based proteomic workflow for host cell protein monitoring and risk control in biotherapeutics
Authors: Xiaojuan Yu, Jianhui Cheng, Qianchuan Lv, Feiyu Wang, Jianzhong Hu, Xinxin Li, Chuanfei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComprehensive monitoring of host cell proteins (HCPs) is essential for ensuring the quality, safety, and consistency of antibody therapeutics. Conventional enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay–based assays provide only total HCP burden and lack the resolution to identify individual, functionally active impurities that may affect product stability or efficacy. To address this limitation, we developed a versatile, high-sensitivity mass spectrometry–based workflow capable of molecular-level HCP characterization and risk assessment.MethodsWe developed and evaluated a sub-ppm, easy-to-use liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry–based proteomics workflow for the identification and relative quantitation of HCPs across downstream purification and final formulation, which offers molecular-specific, risk-based process characterization and regulatory-aligned quality assurance for antibody therapeutics.ResultsThe workflow demonstrated sub-ppm sensitivity and excellent reproducibility, enabling comprehensive tracking of HCP clearance across the purification process. Scale-up from 100 to 500 L led to higher total HCP levels and moderately increased species diversity, indicating reduced clearance efficiency and altered impurity profiles. Mechanistic interpretation linked transient thioredoxin and thioredoxin reductase presence to disulfide bond reduction and nonreducing capillary electrophoresis–sodium dodecyl sulfate purity fluctuations. In the formulation stage, lipoprotein lipase was identified as a key contributor to polysorbate 80 (PS80) degradation, consistent with accelerated stability results, leading to successful process optimization.ConclusionsThis study establishes a comprehensive, sub-ppm data-independent acquisition (DIA)–mass spectrometry (MS) platform for HCP monitoring in antibody therapeutics, bridging analytical characterization with process and formulation control. The workflow enables molecular-level understanding of impurity behavior, supports proactive risk mitigation, and enhances overall process consistency, representing a significant advancement in HCP management and biotherapeutic quality assurance.Keywords: host cell proteins, bioprocess monitoring, native digestion, nanoLC–DIA-MS, risk control

This study establishes a sensitive nanoLC–DIA-MS workflow for comprehensive HCP monitoring in antibody therapeutics, enabling sub-ppm quantitation and mechanistic linkage between specific impurities and product stability. The approach advances HCP analysis into a proactive tool for process consistency, risk control, and quality assurance in biotherapeutic development.

Introduction

Host cell proteins (HCPs) are process-related impurities derived from host organisms such as Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells or Escherichia coli during recombinant protein manufacturing. Although downstream purification processes eliminate most HCPs, trace amounts may persist in the final drug product (DP), potentially compromising potency, stability, and safety [1]. For example, polysorbates are widely used excipients that maintain the formulation stability of biologics, yet residual HCPs with lipase or esterase activity can catalyze their degradation, leading to the formation of visible or subvisible particles [2–4]. Consequently, regulatory agencies require rigorous monitoring and control of HCP levels throughout biopharmaceutical development and manufacturing [5, 6].

The primary objective of HCP analytics is to evaluate process clearance efficiency and provide actionable insights for purification optimization and risk assessment. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is the industry standard for total HCP quantitation due to its high sensitivity, often reaching sub-ppm levels [7]. However, ELISA measurements depend on the antibody coverage of the specific anti-HCP reagent and do not reveal the identity or concentration of individual proteins [8]. These limitations constrain the mechanistic understanding of process behavior and hinder the development of HCP-specific control strategies. Furthermore, developing process-specific ELISA methods is time- and resource-intensive, making them less practical during early process development when rapid analytical turnaround is required.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)–based proteomics provides an orthogonal and complementary approach, enabling direct identification and quantitation of individual HCPs [8]. Targeted strategies such as multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) and parallel reaction monitoring (PRM), as well as untargeted discovery proteomics, have all been applied to HCP characterization [9–13], with data-independent acquisition (DIA) gaining particular traction due to its ability to reproducibly and comprehensively quantify all detectable peptides across complex bioprocess matrices [14–16]. By systematically fragmenting all ions within defined m/z windows, DIA improves dynamic range, sensitivity, and reproducibility—critical advantages for the detection of low-abundance HCPs in the presence of highly abundant therapeutic proteins.

Despite these advantages, MS-based HCP analysis continues to face challenges related to the broad dynamic range between product and impurity signals. To enhance detection sensitivity, various enrichment techniques—such as Protein A elution [16], immunoprecipitation, [17] filtration [18], surfactant assentation [19], and ProteoMiner strategies [20]—have been explored. However, these approaches often introduce analytical biases, compromise quantitative accuracy, or require large sample volumes. The “native digestion” approach, first introduced by Huang et al. [21], has recently emerged as a promising alternative for HCP profiling due to its simplicity, low sample requirement, and compatibility with direct analysis. Nevertheless, it remains unclear how native digestion affects individual protein quantitation, as HCPs that are not readily digestible under native conditions may exhibit inconsistent quantitative behavior [14].

In this study, we developed and validated a sensitive nanoLC–DIA-MS workflow that integrates native digestion with library-free DIA analysis using DIA-NN [22]. This versatile workflow eliminates the need for spectral library construction while achieving high quantitative accuracy and reproducibility. Using spiked-in standards for method evaluation, we demonstrate that this workflow consistently quantifies individual HCPs across four orders of magnitude, reaching sub-ppm detection sensitivity, even though digestion efficiency may vary substantially among different proteins. Beyond its analytical performance, the workflow has been successfully applied in routine bioprocess monitoring, providing insights into disulfide bond reduction events mediated by HCPs and supporting more informed risk assessment and product quality control.

Materials and methods

Trypsin, dithiothreitol (DTT), formic acid (FA), acetonitrile, and the Pierce™ Intact Protein Standard Mix (Human IGF-I LR3, Human Thioredoxin, Streptococcus dysgalactiae Protein G, Bovine Carbonic Anhydrase II (BCA II), Streptococcus Protein AG (chimeric), and E. coli Exo Klenow) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The iRT kit was purchased from Biognosys (MA, USA). The CHO host cell protein ELISA kit was purchased from Duoning (Shanghai, China). CE-SDS purity analysis kits were purchased from SCIEX (MA, USA).

Sample preparation

For native digestion, 500 μg of the mAb sample was diluted with water and 1 M ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) to achieve a final protein concentration of 2 mg/ml and 50 mM ABC. Samples were digested with trypsin at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:500 (w/w) at 37°C for 14–16 h. Digestion was followed by reduction with 10 mM DTT at 95°C for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, 1% formic acid was added to quench digestion. The resulting precipitate was removed by centrifugation at 12 000 g for 10 min. Supernatants were transferred to low-bind 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes, freeze-dried via SpeedVac, and reconstituted in 50 μl of 0.1% formic acid. Prior to LC–MS/MS analysis, 2 μl of a 10-fold diluted iRT mixture was added, followed by centrifugation at 12 000 g for 10 min. A 2 μl aliquot of the supernatant was injected for analysis.

To assess identification and quantitation performance, the Pierce™ Intact Protein Standard Mix was spiked into a 2 mg/ml mAb1 solution at final total concentrations of 0.38, 0.76, 1.52, 3.8, 7.6, 15.2, 38, 76, 152, 380, 760, 1520, and 3800 ppm. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared in triplicate by spiking the mix at 3.8, 38, and 380 ppm to evaluate accuracy, precision, and consistency. Details of each individual standard and its spiked levels are listed in Table 1.

LC–MS/MS analysis

LC–MS/MS analysis was carried out using an UltiMate™ 3000 RSLCnano system connected to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, 20 μg of peptide mixture (based on the original mAb amount) was loaded onto a 20 cm × 75 μm capillary column packed in-house with 1.9 μm C18 resin. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 80% acetonitrile. The flow rate was 300 n/min, and the column temperature was maintained at room temperature. Peptides were eluted using a 95-min gradient at 300 nl/min and ambient temperature with the following gradient: 0–0:05 min, 3%–5% B, 0:05–40:05 min, 5%–20% B; 40:05–74:55 min, 20%–35% B; 74:55–86:55 min, 35%–50% B; 86:55–87:00 min, 50%–100% B; 87:00–95:00 min, 100%–100% B. The Q Exactive HF-X operated in DIA mode, acquiring 25 × 24 m/z precursor isolation windows (400–1000 m/z) at a resolution of 30 000, an AGC target of 1e6, and a maximum injection time of 60 ms using a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 27. Precursor scans were acquired at a resolution of 60 000 every 25 MS/MS scans.

DIA data processing by DIA-NN

Raw DIA files were processed using DIA-NN (v1.8.1) with library-free search enabled. FASTA files were obtained from UniProt (accessed on 30 May 2023). The following settings were used: “FASTA digest for library-free search” and “deep-learning-based spectra, RTs and IMs prediction” in DIA-NN were enabled, trypsin/P was set as the digestion enzyme, methionine oxidation, and protein N-term acetylation were set as variable modifications, cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a constant modification, and one missed cleavage was allowed. Precursor FDR was set at 1%, heuristic protein inference was disabled, and all other settings were kept at default values.

Skyline visualization and quantitation

The DIA-NN results were imported into Skyline (25.1.0.142) for visualization and quantitation. Briefly, all the result files from DIA-NN were kept in the same directory; Default settings were used, except that precursor charges were set to 2, 3, and 4, and one missed cleavage was allowed. The spectral library was loaded using the report-lib.tsv.speclib file from the DIA-NN results.

Total host cell protein quantitation by ELISA

HCPs in mAb2 samples were quantified using a CHO HCP ELISA kit. Briefly, unknown samples, standards, quality controls, and negative controls were incubated in microtiter strips coated with affinity-purified anti-CHO HCP capture antibody and reacted simultaneously with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–labeled anti-CHO HCP detection antibody, forming a sandwich complex (capture antibody–HCP–HRP antibody). After washing to remove unbound reagents, the tetramethylbenzidine substrate was added to generate a chromogenic signal, which was stopped with the stop solution. Optical density was measured at 450 nm (reference 650 nm) and is directly proportional to CHO HCP concentration. A four-parameter logistic model was used to fit the standard curve, and sample concentrations were interpolated accordingly. HCP levels in ppm were calculated as the ratio of HCP (ng/ml) to total protein concentration (mg/ml).

Non-reduced CE-SDS analysis of mAb2

The CE-SDS experiments were conducted on a Beckman Coulter PA800 plus system equipped with a photodiode array (PDA) detector, using the IgG Purity/Heterogeneity Assay. The bare fused silica (50 μm ID × 30 cm total length) underwent sequential preconditioning with 0.1 M NaOH, 0.1 M HCl, deionized (DI) water, and SDS-MW Gel Buffer. Samples were introduced electrokinetically by applying a voltage of 5 kV for 20 s. Electrophoresis separation was performed at 15 kV, with the capillary temperature maintained at 25°C, using recirculating liquid coolant. As for sample preparation, the original mAb2 sample was initially diluted to a concentration of 4 mg/ml with DI water. Sequentially, 23.8 μl of the diluted sample, 71.2 μl of sample buffer, and 5 μl of 250 mM Iodoacetamide (IAM) were thoroughly mixed in a 1.5 ml tube. The mixture was then subjected to a metal bath at 70 °C for 10 min. Finally, the sample was allowed to cool to room temperature and transferred into a microvial for CE-SDS analysis.

Results and discussions

Method evaluation using spiked-in 6-STD proteins in drug product samples

To qualify the analytical performance of the native digestion–DIA-MS workflow, six standard proteins (6-STD) with molecular weights ranging from 9 to 68 kDa were spiked into an mAb1 DP matrix that contained no detectable endogenous HCPs. The total spiked levels ranged from 0.38 to 3800 ppm. Using DIA-NN, all the individual spiked proteins were confidently identified at sub-ppm concentrations, each supported by at least two unique peptides and their corresponding MS2 chromatograms, as shown in Table S1. For instance, Exo Klenow was detected at 0.36 ppm with 15 unique peptides, demonstrating strong fragment-ion evidence. Extracted product ion chromatograms confirmed the presence of signature peptides, such as VHTSYHQAVTATGR (dotp = 0.93), as visualized by Skyline™ in Fig. S1. Only Protein G posed minor ambiguity due to its sequence homology with Protein AG, though the combined Protein AG/G detection limit remained as low as 0.135 ppm.

Native digestion is widely used for HCP profiling due to its simplicity, low sample requirement, and moderate sensitivity (typically 1–5 ppm) [18, 21, 23, 24]. However, it remains unclear how native digestion affects individual protein quantitation. To assess the consistency of protein quantitation using our workflow, quantitative assessment across a 104-fold concentration range (0.38–3800 ppm) was performed with a spiking experiment. Analytical performance characteristics, including limit of detection (LOD), linearity, accuracy, and precision, were evaluated from sub-ppm to hundreds of ppm levels. As shown in Table 2, the LOD for all spiked-in proteins reached sub-ppm level (with more than or equal to two supporting peptides identified), and across a 103–104-fold concentration range, excellent linearity, acceptable accuracy, and precision across all 6-STD proteins were achieved. The quantitation and visualization of peptide APQTGIVDECCFR2+ from human IgF-I LR3 is illustrated in Fig. 1.

To further evaluate digestion completeness, heavy-labeled peptides corresponding to representative signature sequences of the 6-STD proteins were added at known levels. Comparison of native and labeled peptide ion intensities revealed protein-dependent digestion efficiencies ranging from 7% to 99%, as shown in Table S2 and Fig. S2. For example, IGF-I LR3 exhibited a 26% digestion recovery, whereas Exo Klenow polymerase reached nearly complete digestion (>80%). Despite variations in absolute efficiency, relative quantitation was reproducible across replicates, indicating stable and consistent digestion behavior under native conditions.

Overall, the qualified workflow demonstrated sub-ppm sensitivity, broad dynamic range, high reproducibility, and robust quantitative performance. Notably, for the first time, the digestion efficiency and consistency under native digestion were systematically investigated. The results suggested that HCP digestion efficiency via native digestion is protein-dependent, but the digestion consistency is maintained across a wide concentration range. Collectively, these findings support the workflow’s suitability for high-sensitivity HCP profiling and relative quantitation in complex biopharmaceutical samples.

Consistency study on biologics samples: comprehensive host cell protein tracking

Comprehensive monitoring of HCPs across downstream purification is essential for ensuring product quality and process consistency in biologics manufacturing. Conventional immunoassays such as ELISA quantify the total HCP burden but cannot resolve individual species that may influence product stability, efficacy, or safety. In contrast, MS-based proteomics enables molecular-level characterization, allowing both qualitative identification and quantitative tracking of specific impurities [14, 15, 25]. Using the nanoLC–DIA workflow, this study establishes a sub-ppm platform for relative quantitation of HCPs across the purification process, thereby linking process performance more directly to molecular impurity profiles.

To further demonstrate the applicability of this workflow, the nanoLC–DIA workflow was applied to quantify HCP dynamics across the entire downstream purification process of two early-stage mAb2 batches—batch #1 (100 L scale) and batch #2 (500 L scale). Sampling points included the Protein A affinity pool (AF), intermediate depth filtration pool (IDF), anion exchange chromatography eluate (AEX), cation exchange chromatography eluate (CEX), nanofiltration pool (NF), and final drug substance (DS). At each purification stage, 500 μg of material was analyzed by the nanoLC-DIA workflow. The comprehensive tracking results are summarized in Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 2A–D.

Quantitative comparison revealed consistently higher total HCP levels in the 500 L batch relative to the 100 L process at all purification stages (Fig. 2A; Table 3). The most pronounced increase was observed at the IDF step, where HCP content more than doubled (755 → 1725 ppm), suggesting reduced clearance efficiency or a higher upstream HCP load during scale-up. Despite these quantitative differences, the number of identified HCP species remained comparable in the early purification steps (AF and VIN) but increased moderately in later stages. Venn analysis (Fig. 2B) identified 134 shared HCPs between the two batches in the DS, with 68 unique to the 500 L batch and 8 unique to the 100 L batch (data are provided in Table S3), indicating that process scale-up may alter both the levels and composition of the HCP species. Relative quantitative LC–MS analysis of representative HCPs, including clusterin and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) (Fig. 2C and D), demonstrated that the workflow can monitor individual HCPs across the downstream process, thereby providing improved insights into the clearance behavior of specific HCPs and their potential impact on product stability and immunogenicity.

These results indicate that process scale-up led to a higher overall HCP burden and moderately increased HCP diversity in the final product. The nanoLC–DIA workflow provides a powerful analytical solution for comprehensive HCP monitoring across the downstream purification of biologics. By enabling simultaneous identification and relative quantitation of individual HCP species at sub-ppm levels, this approach overcomes the limitations of conventional ELISA assays and reveals molecular insights into process consistency and product quality. The ability to track HCP dynamics across different purification scales and unit operations supports more informed process characterization, facilitates early detection of scale-dependent variations, and strengthens the overall analytical control strategy. Collectively, this workflow not only enhances the scientific understanding of HCP clearance mechanisms but also establishes a robust foundation for ensuring process comparability and regulatory compliance in biologics development.

Insights into risk control during downstream purification

During the downstream purification of mAb2, an unexpected trend was observed in the nonreducing capillary electrophoresis–sodium dodecyl sulfate (nrCE–SDS) purity profile. As summarized in Fig. 3A, the product purity decreased after Protein A capture and intermediate depth filtration but subsequently recovered following the anion exchange chromatography step. The nrCE–SDS electropherogram further revealed partial reduction of mAb2, suggesting the presence of a reducing agent in the process intermediate.

After 48 h of incubation in the in vitro Trx system, mAb2 underwent reduction and reoxidation, resulting in increased fragment and HMWs in the nrCE–SDS analysis. (D) Schematic illustration of the thioredoxin (Trx) system mechanism. (E) Relative abundance profiles of high-risk HCPs Trx and TrxR, extracted product ion chromatograms of representative signature peptides VGEFSGANK2+ from Trx and LELTPVAIQAGR of TrxR used to support identification and quantitation are embedded.

NanoLC–DIA analysis provided a molecular-level explanation for the observed changes in product purity. Two redox-active host cell proteins, thioredoxin (Trx) and thioredoxin reductase (TrxR), were identified in the early stages of downstream purification, as shown in Fig. 3E (although a correlation between Trx abundance and mAb degradation was observed, we do not claim that Trx is the sole contributor). Thioredoxin contains a highly conserved Cys-Gly-Pro-Cys active site capable of catalyzing disulfide bond reduction under mild conditions. When acting together with TrxR, these enzymes can cause partial reduction of interchain disulfide bonds in mAb2, accounting for the observed decrease in nrCE–SDS purity. As the reducing-active species were gradually depleted, the reduced antibody molecules could spontaneously reoxidize to reform disulfide bonds; however, this reoxidation may lead to incorrect disulfide pairing, increasing the risk of high–molecular–weight (HMW) aggregate formation.

To further verify this hypothesis, an in vitro thioredoxin system was established to simulate the redox environment. In a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube, 200 μg of intact antibody (DS) was incubated with Trx (2 μl, 0.5 mg/ml), TrxR (2 μl, 0.5 mg/ml), and 10× PBS (5 μl) in a total reaction volume of 40 μl. Reduction was initiated by adding 10 μl of freshly prepared NADPH (50 mM), and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for up to 48 h. Aliquots were collected periodically, denatured, and analyzed by nrCE–SDS. As shown in Fig. 3B and C, the resulting purity trend closely paralleled that observed during downstream purification—initial reduction followed by gradual reoxidation—confirming that thioredoxin-mediated redox cycling can transiently affect the structural integrity of antibodies under process-relevant conditions. These findings highlight that even trace levels of redox-active HCPs such as thioredoxin can introduce reversible structural perturbations during downstream purification and should be carefully controlled.

This case highlights the mechanistic value of high-resolution MS-based HCP tracking in interpreting unexpected product quality changes during downstream purification. Conventional process analytics, such as nrCE–SDS can reveal the phenotypic outcome (e.g. partial reduction), whereas DIA-MS analysis provides the molecular-level insight necessary to identify the causative impurity. The detection of thioredoxin and thioredoxin reductase in this context underscores the importance of monitoring redox-active HCPs, which may affect product integrity even at trace levels.

Identification of high-risk host cell proteins in biologics formulation

The high sensitivity and reproducibility of the nanoLC-DIA workflow enabled the confident detection of residual HCPs in the final DS formulation of mAb2 at sub-ppm levels during early-stage process development. This molecular-level resolution provides a detailed perspective for evaluating formulation-related risks that may not be apparent from total HCP measurements alone. Even when total HCP content was within acceptable regulatory limits (typically <100 ppm), several low-abundance yet functionally active proteins were consistently detected, including hydrolases and oxidoreductases that are known to affect product stability through enzymatic or redox-mediated degradation mechanisms.

As summarized in Table S4, one HCP of particular concern—LPL—was identified in the mAb2 DS. This enzyme is capable of hydrolyzing polysorbate surfactants, which are widely used to stabilize monoclonal antibody formulations. The presence of trace enzymes poses a significant risk to long-term product stability, as polysorbate degradation can generate free fatty acids and lead to particle formation, opalescence, or visible aggregates.

Consistent with this mechanism, accelerated stability studies on an early mAb2 batch of DS showed substantial polysorbate 80 (PS80) degradation, decreasing from 0.39 mg/ml to 0.30 mg/ml after 6 months (Fig. 4A). This degradation correlated with the detection of LPL in the same batch (Fig. 4B), suggesting that enzymatic hydrolysis LPL is a potential high-risk and representative causative HCP responsible for PS80 degradation. In contrast, subsequent process-optimized batches, which exhibited improved HCP clearance with no detectable LPL in the final product (Fig. 4C), showed only minimal PS80 loss under identical stress conditions, indicating that enzyme-mediated degradation had been effectively mitigated.

(A) PS80 content monitored under accelerated stability conditions for pre- and post-optimized batches. (B) MS spectra of the LPL-derived peptides DFTDIESK2+ and LVGNDVAR2+ detected in the pre-optimized batch confirm the presence of LPL in the final DS formulation. (C) Extracted ion chromatograms of the same peptides in the post-optimized batch, demonstrating effective removal of LPL after process optimization.

These findings demonstrate how our workflow provides actionable insight for formulation control and process optimization. While total HCP levels alone may suggest acceptable purity, the detection of functionally active enzymes such as LPL emphasizes the importance of molecular-level characterization of impurities. The native digestion–DIA-MS workflow thus serves not only as a sensitive quality control tool but also as a mechanistic platform to link process-related impurities with formulation stability outcomes, supporting proactive risk management in biologics development.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and demonstrated a sub-ppm nanoLC–DIA-MS workflow for comprehensive HCP monitoring and risk assessment throughout biotherapeutic process development. By integrating native digestion with data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry, this approach enables both global profiling of HCPs and relative quantitation with high reproducibility and sub-ppm sensitivity. Unlike conventional ELISA assays, which report only total HCP levels, the DIA-MS workflow provides molecular-level resolution, allowing the identification, quantification, and tracking of individual HCPs that may affect product purity, stability, or immunogenicity.

Application of this workflow across two process scales (100 vs. 500 L) revealed distinct HCP clearance patterns and batch-dependent variations, demonstrating its capability for assessing process consistency and comparability. Furthermore, the detection of redox-active proteins such as thioredoxin and thioredoxin reductase elucidated the mechanistic basis for transient mAb reduction during purification, underscoring the workflow’s utility for root-cause investigations of quality deviations. Finally, the identification of functionally active hydrolases (e.g. LPL) in the final formulation established a direct link between residual enzymatic impurities and formulation instability, enabling targeted process optimization to mitigate PS80 degradation risks.

Collectively, this work establishes a comprehensive and mechanistically informative analytical platform that bridges process analytics, formulation science, and quality risk management. The nanoLC–DIA-MS workflow not only advances the sensitivity and coverage of HCP detection but also provides a molecular framework for proactive impurity control. When applied appropriately—such as serving as a complementary tool to ELISA for process characterization, risk assessment, and root-cause investigations, particularly for identifying high-risk, functionally active impurities—MS-based HCP analysis may progressively support or partially replace ELISA in specific contexts as standardized workflows, reference materials, quantitative strategies, and regulatory experience continue to mature. The integration of this approach into routine biologics development offers a transformative step toward data-driven, risk-based process characterization and regulatory-aligned quality assurance for next-generation biotherapeutics.
