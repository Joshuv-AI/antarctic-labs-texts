Incidence and trends of adverse drug reactions during seasonal malaria chemoprevention among children: evidence from Ghana’s pharmacovigilance database, 2015–2023
Authors: Benjamin Akwasi Forkuo Otoo, Michael Opoku-Mireku, Seth Kwaku Seaneke, Delese Mimi Darko, George Tsey Sabblah, Harriet Affran Bonful
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeasonal Malaria Chemoprevention, which involves giving sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine and amodiaquine (SPAQ) to children aged 3–59 months, is a key strategy for preventing malaria in Ghana. Although SPAQ has been used since 2015, systematic evidence on adverse drug reactions remains limited. This study assessed the patterns and incidence of ADRs associated with SMC to guide safety practices, strengthen public trust, and inform policy.Materials and methodsWe analyzed SPAQ-related ADRs reported to the Ghana Food and Drugs Authority (FDA) database between 2015 and 2023 using STATA IC 16. Incomplete records were excluded. ADRs were classified by age, sex, seriousness, and reporter category, with serious cases further categorized by outcomes such as hospitalization and death. Consistent classification was ensured with standardized MedDRA coding. Annual incidence per 100,000 SPAQ doses was calculated using ADR reports and dispensing data from the National Malaria Elimination Programme.ResultsA total of 2,097 ADRs were analyzed. Slightly more than half (51.2%) involved male children, and 60.3% occurred among those aged 13–59 months. The most frequently reported ADRs were fever (22.7%), vomiting (21.5%), and diarrhoea (16.9%). Serious reactions accounted for 8.6% of cases, most often leading to prolonged hospitalization. The incidence of ADRs declined from 220.63 per 100,000 doses in 2015 to 0.34 in 2023. Caregivers/non-healthcare professionals submitted two-thirds of reports.ConclusionSMC with SPAQ is generally safe in Ghana, though serious ADRs continue to occur. The low reporting rate by healthcare professionals, contrasted with high reporting by caregivers, underscores the need to enhance professional engagement, ensure accurate community reporting, and expand active surveillance during SMC campaigns.Keywords: SPAQ, Seasonal malaria chemoprevention, Adverse drug reactions, Pharmacovigilance, Ghana, Children

Introduction

Malaria remains a major global public health challenge, accounting for substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide. The 2024 World Malaria Report estimates that over 260 million malaria cases and approximately 600,000 deaths occurred globally in 2023, with children under five years of age continuing to account for the majority of malaria-related deaths [1]. The African Region bears a disproportionate share of the burden, contributing approximately 95% of global malaria cases and deaths [2]. Ghana remains among the high-burden countries, reporting approximately 5.2 million malaria cases and 151 deaths in 2022, representing a marked decline from the 1,137 deaths recorded in 2015, reflecting sustained progress in malaria control over the past decade [3, 4].

Several interventions have contributed to this progress. These include seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC), indoor residual spraying (IRS), widespread distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), prompt treatment with artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), use of recommended herbal products [5], and, more recently, the introduction of malaria vaccines such as RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M [6–8]. The World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed SMC in 2012, and Ghana adopted it in 2015, after a successful pilot in the Upper West Region demonstrated its effectiveness. Since then, SMC has become the country’s longest-running child-focused malaria intervention, providing protection during peak transmission seasons and gradually expanding to other regions within the northern belt [9].

SMC is a key preventive intervention for children under five years of age and is primarily implemented during periods of high malaria transmission. It involves the administration of sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine plus amodiaquine (SPAQ) to children aged 3–59 months over three consecutive days each month for up to four cycles, providing protection during peak risk periods [10]. Recent WHO malaria guidelines have introduced greater flexibility in SMC implementation, allowing adaptation of targeting to local transmission patterns and consideration of perennial malaria chemoprevention in settings with extended or year-round transmission [11]. Despite its proven effectiveness, concerns regarding adverse drug reactions (ADRs), particularly vomiting, diarrhoea, and weakness, persist and may undermine caregiver confidence in the programme [12].

In response, Ghana’s National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) has set ambitious targets, including a 90% reduction in malaria mortality and a 50% reduction in case incidence by 2028. Achieving these goals will require not only continued scale-up of SMC but also strengthened pharmacovigilance for SPAQ to ensure safety and acceptability [5]. However, limited resources, gaps in healthcare worker training, and widespread underreporting continue to constrain pharmacovigilance systems [13]. Pharmacists are more likely to report ADRs when adequately trained, whereas reporting by physicians and other frontline health workers remains low, often due to limited awareness of pharmacovigilance requirements [14–17]. Although the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA) has introduced digital reporting tools and guidelines, their effective use depends largely on the knowledge and confidence of reporters [18–20].

Even where data exist, such as for SMC, gaps remain in analysis, dissemination, and response [13]. Understanding the safety of SMC under real-world conditions and identifying populations most vulnerable to ADRs are essential for shaping future malaria interventions. Clear communication about possible side effects is also critical for sustaining public confidence and reducing hesitancy toward malaria prevention strategies.

This study, therefore, reviews spontaneous ADR reports linked to SMC implementation in Ghana. It seeks to estimate the incidence and trends of ADRs among children under five and to examine reporting patterns from both healthcare workers and caregivers.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study analyzed reports of SPAQ-related ADRs recorded in the Ghana FDA’s safety database between 2015 and 2023. The analysis used secondary data collected as part of the SMC programme in Ghana.

Study area

Ghana, a West African country with a population of 30.8 million according to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, has implemented SMC since 2015 across 7 of its 16 administrative regions. The populations of these regions are: Upper West (901,502; 2.92%), Upper East (1,301,226; 4.22%), North East (658,946; 2.14%), Northern (2,310,939; 7.50%), Savannah (653,266; 2.12%), Oti (747,248; 2.42%), and Bono East (1,203,400; 3.90%) [10, 21–23].

Together, the 68 implementing districts account for about 60% of Ghana’s malaria burden, with peak transmission occurring during the July–October rainy season [6]. These districts are predominantly rural, and SMC delivery relies heavily on community health workers and community-based volunteers. Each year, approximately 1.5 million children aged 3–59 months receive four monthly cycles of SPAQ, with each cycle involving a 3-day regimen of sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine and amodiaquine [11].

Data source

The secondary dataset was obtained from the Ghana FDA pharmacovigilance safety database, which compiles spontaneous ADR reports submitted by caregivers and healthcare professionals during SMC campaigns between 2015 and 2023. Reports are received through multiple reporting channels, including paper-based forms, telephone calls, the Med Safety mobile application, and online submissions. All reports are entered into the database upon receipt. Causality assessment, based on World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) criteria, is conducted during routine signal detection activities and targeted follow-up of serious cases, including assessment of symptom resolution where applicable (e.g. following drug withdrawal). As a passive surveillance system, pre-storage verification is limited and focuses primarily on data completeness and standardisation rather than clinical confirmation, which may influence the interpretation of findings. Reports are coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) to ensure consistency and enable national analysis and submission to the WHO global database (VigiBase).

Adverse drug reactions were classified using MedDRA preferred terms, including seriousness criteria such as hospitalization, death, and life-threatening reactions.

Study population

The study population included children aged 3–59 months who experienced ADRs following at least one dose of SPAQ during SMC campaigns in the Upper East, Upper West, Northern, Savannah, Oti, Bono East, and Bono West regions between 2015 and 2023. ADR records unrelated to SMC were excluded during data cleaning to maintain focus on SPAQ safety within the target age group and regions.

Study variables

The primary outcome was the incidence of ADRs following SPAQ administration, expressed as the number of ADRs per 100,000 doses dispensed. An ADR was defined as a noxious and unintended response to a drug that occurs at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy, excluding expected therapeutic effects or disease progression (e.g., pre-existing malaria symptoms) [24].

Secondary outcomes were the incidence of specific ADR types, including vomiting, fever (recorded if new-onset or worsening post-SPAQ dose, per reporter narrative), and diarrhoea. Independent variables included age (3–12 months, 13–59 months), sex (male, female), reporter type (caregivers/non-healthcare professionals, physician, pharmacist, other), ADR seriousness (serious: death, hospitalization, life-threatening; non-serious), categorized according to WHO criteria [25].

Data collection and handling

A total of 2,135 de-identified ADR records were extracted from the FDA database using unique case identification codes. During data cleaning, duplicate entries, reports unrelated to SMC, and records with missing SPAQ dose information were excluded. Missing values for age and sex were handled using listwise deletion. SPAQ dose data were administrative coverage estimates derived from National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) stock reconciliation and drug distribution records, rather than individual-level dosing records, and were cross-validated against available NMEP documentation. After data cleaning, 2,097 records were retained for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Background characteristics of the study population were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The annual cumulative incidence of ADRs was calculated per 100,000 SPAQ doses by dividing the total number of ADRs reported each year by the total number of doses dispensed in that year. Results were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Line graphs illustrated annual ADR incidence and reporting patterns by reporter type over the nine years. Data on annual SPAQ doses dispensed were obtained from the NMEP. Analyses were performed using STATA I/C version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). No inferential analysis was conducted because, although clustering was suspected, data on potential clustering variables were not available [26]. Furthermore, the few available variables were insufficient to be considered conceptually adequate for such an analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC) (Reference: GHS-ERC: 067/05/24). The FDA granted administrative permission to use the data. To protect confidentiality, ADR data were de-identified and stored in encrypted files on a password-protected laptop, in line with the Public Health Act 2012 (Act 851).

Results

Descriptive characteristics of spontaneous reports

A total of 2,097 ADR cases reported among children who received SMC were included in the analysis. Among these ADR cases, 1,024 (48.8%) occurred in female children. The majority of reported ADRs (60.3%; 1,264/2,097) were observed in children aged 13–59 months, reflecting the age distribution of cases with reported reactions rather than the underlying SMC-treated population.

With respect to seriousness, 180 (8.6%) of the reported ADR cases were classified as serious after exclusion of congenital anomalies. Among these serious ADR cases, hospitalization (caused or prolonged by the reaction) was the most frequently reported seriousness criterion (68.9%; 124/180), followed by life-threatening conditions (27.2%; 49/180), death (2.2%; 4/180), and other medically important conditions (1.1%; 2/180), specifically persistent severe anemia requiring transfusion and anaphylaxis without hypotension.

Reporter type was documented for all ADR reports. Of the 2,097 ADR cases, most were reported by caregivers or other non–healthcare professionals (e.g., parents or guardians) (67.9%; 1,424/2,097). Physicians submitted 104 reports (5.0%), pharmacists 34 reports (1.6%), and other healthcare professionals 111 reports (5.3%). Reports from unknown sources accounted for 20.2% (424/2,097).

All findings are descriptive. Inferential statistical testing was not conducted due to the absence of a non-ADR comparison group and limited availability of covariates suitable for multivariable analysis (Table 1).

Annual incidence of ADRs (2015–2023)

The cumulative incidence of ADRs from the SMC campaign over the study period was 7.71 per 100,000 SPAQ doses. The incidence peaked in 2015 at 220.63 per 100,000 doses, before dropping sharply to 23.93 in 2016. It doubled to 48.18 in 2017, halved again to 23.94 in 2018, and then declined steadily from 2019 (10.49) to 2021 (0.11). A modest increase was observed in 2022 (2.49), followed by another decline in 2023 (0.34).

Figure 1 illustrates a significant decline in ADR incidence per 100,000 doses reported by all reporter categories from 2015 to 2023. Reports by caregivers and non-health professionals were highest initially, peaking in 2015, followed by a sharp decrease and stabilizing at low levels from 2017 onwards. Reports from pharmacists, physicians, and other professionals remained consistently low throughout the entire period. The “Unknown” category exhibited a moderate peak in 2015, then rapidly declined to minimal levels. Overall, ADR reporting incidence decreased markedly over the observed decade, especially among non-health professional reporters.

Trend of ADR reports by reporter category

Figure 2 displays the annual distribution of reported ADR symptoms from 2015 to 2024. Fever and vomiting were the most commonly reported symptoms throughout the period, with vomiting peaking around 2021 at over 40%. Diarrhoea consistently ranked third but showed fluctuations over the years. Other symptoms, such as weakness, abdominal pain, loss of appetite, rash, itch, cough, and dizziness, occurred at relatively lower frequencies, each remaining below 15%. Overall, fever and vomiting were the dominant ADRs reported, while the incidence of other symptoms stayed comparatively low and varied over time.

Discussion

This study examined ADRs among children aged 3–59 months who received SMC in northern Ghana between 2015 and 2023, based on Ghana FDA database reports. The most frequently reported ADRs were mild to moderate malaria-like symptoms, including vomiting, fever, and diarrhoea. Incidence was slightly higher among males (51.2%) and older children (13–59 months; 60.3%). The overall ADR reporting rate declined substantially over the study period from 220.6 per 100,000 doses in 2015 to 0.34 in 2023 which, despite increasing doses (Table 2), likely reflects improved community education on SMC adherence, reduced novelty effects post-initial rollout (2015–2016), and reporting fatigue among caregivers (peaking 2015–2017; Fig. 1), alongside persistent underreporting rather than solely enhanced safety or pharmacovigilance.

The 2021 trough (0.11 per 100,000 doses) coincided with COVID-19-related disruptions to healthcare access and reporting systems, despite the administration of 4.76 million SMC doses that year, suggesting substantial under-reporting and highlighting important surveillance gaps.

Demographic distribution

Among 2,097 children with reported ADRs, cases were almost evenly split by sex, though slightly more frequent in males (51.2%). Older children (13–59 months) accounted for most ADRs (60.3%), consistent with the SMC program’s target age group (SMC Alliance, 2023). Similar studies in Nigeria also observed higher ADR rates in males and children aged 12–59 months [27]. While sex does not appear to significantly affect susceptibility to antimalarial ADRs [28, 29], higher ADR frequency in older children may relate to cumulative drug exposure across multiple SMC cycles or age-related pharmacokinetic and immunological changes [11, 30]. In Ghana, concerns about side effects have occasionally reduced caregiver adherence to SMC despite widespread recognition of its benefits [31]. Nevertheless, most reported ADRs remain mild and self-limiting [32–36], with vomiting consistently identified as a common reaction to SPAQ.

Trends in ADR incidence

The incidence of reported SPAQ-related ADRs declined markedly over the study period, from 220.6 per 100,000 doses in 2015 to 0.34 per 100,000 doses in 2023, with the lowest incidence observed in 2021 (0.11 per 100,000 doses). Comparable reductions in spontaneous ADR reporting have been documented in other SMC programmes and are primarily attributed to changes in reporting behavior rather than confirmed improvements in the intrinsic safety of SPAQ [37]. In low- and middle-income settings, passive pharmacovigilance systems are frequently limited by underreporting, reporting fatigue, and inconsistent engagement of reporters [13, 23].

The fluctuations observed between 2016 and 2018 likely reflect variability in surveillance intensity and reporting practices rather than meaningful changes in the safety profile of SPAQ. Notably, the decline in reported ADRs began prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating that pandemic-related disruptions alone do not explain this trend. Although SPAQ administration remained high in 2021, the very low ADR reporting rate observed during this period should be interpreted cautiously and is more plausibly attributable to reduced spontaneous reporting and shifting public health priorities. These findings highlight the need for strengthened and more proactive pharmacovigilance approaches to complement spontaneous reporting during SMC implementation.

Patterns in ADR types

Vomiting, fever, and diarrhoea were the most common ADRs, consistent with findings from Ghana [38], Mali [39], Gambia [40], and Nigeria [41, 42]. While usually mild, these symptoms can contribute to dehydration and malnutrition in at-risk children. Less frequent ADRs such as weakness, cough, and loss of appetite showed occasional spikes, possibly linked to co-occurring infections or environmental factors (requiring confirmation via active surveillance) [43, 44]. Rare events such as rash and dizziness suggest potential allergic or neurological effects requiring monitoring.

Gaps in Ghana’s pharmacovigilance system stemming from passive FDA database reliance (e.g., limited causality verification) remain; recommendations include hybrid active surveillance during SMC to address underreporting and improve data quality [23].

Reporter categories

This study found predominance of caregiver/non-healthcare professional reports [15, 16], reflecting increased FDA engagement and digital tools [13]. While broadening coverage, they raise accuracy concerns (21% unknown) [45]. Professional reporting remains low despite training. While non-professionals are likely to treat even mild symptoms with urgency, professionals, saddled with heavy workload, are less likely to report such events, likely perceiving them as expected and benign. Low professional reporting rates suggests need for targeted training and sensitization of healthcare workers on ADR mandatory reporting during SMC campaigns to improve completeness and quality of safety data.

Severity of ADRs

Only 8.6% of ADRs were serious, but nearly 70% resulted in prolonged hospitalization, straining the healthcare infrastructure. Rare fatalities highlight post-marketing surveillance needs.

Implications for policy and practice

In line with World Health Organization (WHO) guidance on seasonal malaria chemoprevention and pharmacovigilance, ADR monitoring should be systematically integrated into routine child health and SMC delivery platforms to strengthen signal detection and risk management. Increased reporting by trained healthcare professionals, supported by simplified reporting tools, is needed to complement caregiver reports. Programmatic review of SPAQ dosing and administration strategies for high-risk children should be considered as part of continuous safety monitoring. At the implementation level, WHO-recommended training of health workers and structured caregiver counselling are critical, as common reactions such as vomiting, fever, and diarrhoea may compromise caregiver adherence to doses administered on days 2–3 of the three-day SPAQ regimen. Early identification and management of mild ADRs may support regimen completion and sustained protective effectiveness. Longitudinal studies are warranted to inform WHO-aligned risk–benefit assessments and adherence-sensitive SMC policies.

Study strengths and limitations

This study analyzed nine years of national ADR reports among children under five years of age. The large sample size and extended observation period strengthen the descriptive assessment of reporting patterns over time. However, reliance on passive surveillance introduces inherent limitations, including underreporting, incomplete case ascertainment, and potential misclassification. The predominance of caregiver-submitted reports and the substantial proportion with unknown reporter type further constrain data quality and clinical verification.

Limited covariate availability within VigiBase restricted the ability to conduct multivariable or patient-level risk analyses. Interpretation is additionally complicated by the clinical overlap between reported ADR symptoms and manifestations of malaria or other common childhood illnesses, which may contribute to diagnostic uncertainty and reporting bias.

The absence of a comparison group precludes differentiation between events temporally associated with SMC administration and background morbidity expected in this population. Consequently, causal inference is limited, and the true excess risk attributable to SMC exposure cannot be estimated from these data.

Conclusion

Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) with SPAQ in Ghana appears generally safe, with vomiting, fever, and diarrhoea as the most frequently reported ADRs. Serious ADRs were rare but clinically significant when they occurred. Although reported ADRs declined over time despite increasing programmatic doses delivered, this pattern likely reflects program maturation and growing community familiarity with SMC. However, the possibility of underreporting inherent to passive surveillance systems cannot be excluded. Interpretation is further constrained by the absence of active or survey-based comparator data to contextualize reporting trends. Strengthening pharmacovigilance through enhanced engagement of both healthcare providers and caregivers, complemented by targeted active surveillance, will be critical to sustaining confidence in SMC and supporting continued malaria control gains. Future analyses incorporating exploratory time-trend regression may help distinguish true safety signals from shifts in reporting behavior.
