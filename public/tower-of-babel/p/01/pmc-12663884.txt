Generative Artificial Intelligence for Retinal Image Translation to Improve Glaucoma Screening With Deep Learning
Authors: Iyad Majid, Nazlee Zebardast, Mengyu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to improve automated glaucoma detection by utilizing generative adversarial networks (GANs) to translate underutilized scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (SLO) fundus images into synthetic color fundus (CF) photographs.MethodsA Cycle-Consistent GAN model (CycleGAN) framework was used to translate 16,936 SLO fundus photographs into corresponding synthetic CF images. Five deep learning models were trained using real CF, synthetic CF, SLO fundus, and combined datasets to classify glaucoma from a holdout test set of real CF photographs. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the operating characteristic curve (AUC) and sensitivity at 90% and 95% specificities.ResultsThe “GAN+CFP” model, trained on real and synthetic CF images, achieved the highest AUC (0.94, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.93–0.96, P < 0.05) and sensitivity at 90% and 95% specificities (0.83 and 0.77, respectively), outperforming the “CFP” (AUC = 0.89, sensitivities = 0.77 and 0.66), “SLO+CFP” (AUC = 0.88, sensitivities = 0.71 and 0.56), and “GAN” models (AUC = 0.82, sensitivities = 0.51 and 0.33). The “GAN+CFP” and “SLO+CFP” models demonstrated consistent sensitivity across racial and ethnic groups, with “GAN+CFP” yielding superior results across demographics.ConclusionsGANs effectively translate SLO images into synthetic CF photographs, addressing domain shifts and increasing dataset sizes to enhance glaucoma detection.Translational RelevanceGANs may improve glaucoma classification models by improving dataset consistency and mitigating domain shifts. By generating synthetic CF images from SLO data, GANs expand available training data in a clinically relevant imaging modality.

Introduction

Glaucoma is a set of optic neuropathies characterized by damage to the optic nerve, degeneration of the retinal nerve fiber layers, and loss of retinal ganglion cells, leading to a gradual decline in peripheral visual field (VF) function.1,2 As the leading cause of irreversible blindness in the world, careful monitoring and frequent treatment are crucial to preventing significant vision loss.1,3,4 Glaucoma currently accounts for 14% of the total blindness population and is predicted to affect over 110 million individuals by 2040.5

Significant disparities in healthcare access and glaucoma treatment opportunities exist for racial and ethnic minorities. Glaucoma occurs more frequently in Black, Asian, and Hispanic Americans than in non-Hispanic White Americans,6–9 yet these groups often encounter more significant obstacles to receiving timely diagnosis and care.10–14 These challenges are compounded by socioeconomic disparities,15–17 which limit access to quality treatment and result in worse outcomes for these populations.18,19 Addressing these disparities is crucial to improving glaucoma management and ensuring equitable healthcare for all. With the growing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare, there is a promising opportunity for AI-based solutions to assist in the early detection of glaucoma, particularly in underserved populations. Deep learning (DL) models have shown great potential in improving the accuracy and efficiency of glaucoma screening, offering potential benefits for early diagnosis and improved management.20–22

In clinical practice, color fundus (CF) photographs are a primary imaging modality used by ophthalmologists for the diagnosis and monitoring of glaucoma.23,24 Concurrently, optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans produce scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (SLO) fundus images, which are often underutilized.24 From a DL perspective, the availability of large and diverse datasets is crucial for robust model performance.25–27 However, simply combining SLO and CF images for training is problematic due to the issue of domain shift, where a model trained on one imaging modality may not generalize well to another.28,29 An example of this generalizability challenge is exemplified with the first commercial AI device for diabetic retinopathy screening, which was only approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use with a specific camera type due to concerns that performance could degrade if used with different imaging equipment.30,31

To effectively leverage the vast yet underutilized SLO data, we must address the domain shift problem. One promising solution is the use of generative adversarial networks (GANs)32 to perform image-to-image translation, converting SLO images into synthetic CF photographs. This approach creates a larger, more uniform dataset within the target CF photograph domain, which can be used to augment the training of a glaucoma classification model.27,33 The Cycle-Consistent GAN model (CycleGAN)34 is particularly well-suited for this task, as it excels at unsupervised image translation with unpaired data, a common scenario in medical imaging where patients may not undergo multiple imaging tests in a single visit.27,35–46

Whereas GANs have been applied in ophthalmology for tasks such as image enhancement and segmentation,27,40–50 domain transfer between SLO and CF images remains largely unexplored.51–53 Our study aims to bridge this gap by implementing CyleGAN to translate SLO images into corresponding synthetic CF photographs. We then develop and evaluate a series of DL models to determine the most effective strategy or integrating this synthetic data to improve the automated detection of glaucoma.

Methods

Data Cohort and Preparation

The study utilized a data cohort from the Massachusetts Ear and Eye Institute (MEEI) comprising 7271 unique patients. This cohort provided a total of 16,936 SLO images and 16,936 real CF images. Additionally, 16,936 synthetic CF images were generated from SLO images for the same patient encounters.

Only patients with reliable Humphrey 24-2 visual field VF tests performed within 30 days of the OCT and CF imaging tests were included in our data analyses. A reliable VF was defined as fixation loss ≤33%, false-negative rates ≤20%, and false-positive rates ≤20%. Eyes with a VF mean deviation (MD) <−3 decibels (dB), abnormal Glaucoma Hemifield Test (GHT), and abnormal pattern standard deviation (PSD) results were defined as glaucomatous (PSD ≤ 0.05), in accordance with criteria established in previous studies.54–57 Eyes with MD ≥−1 dB, normal GHT, and PSD probability >0.05 were defined as non-glaucomatous. Eyes with an unclear glaucomatous status based on the functional definition were used for a subanalysis of model performance on borderline cases. To ensure consistency in labeling, if multiple VF tests were available within 6 months of an imaging session, we selected the VF test closest in time to the imaging date for classification. If multiple imaging sessions were available for the same eye, each imaging session was labeled independently based on its corresponding VF test. This ensured that each image-VF pair maintained a temporally relevant structural-functional association without introducing conflicting labels for the same eye. In addition, patients’ demographic data and ophthalmic data were collected from the electronic medical records for descriptive statistics. The cohort was randomly split at the patient level into training and testing subsets with 80% (N = 5816) and 20% (N = 1455) proportions, respectively, to prevent data contamination.

Data Partitioning for GAN and Classifier Training

To ensure a clear separation between the generative and classification tasks, we used a rigorous data partitioning strategy, as illustrated in Figure 1. A separate dataset of 94,142 unpaired SLO images and 26,682 unpaired CF images, which did not meet matching criteria for the main cohort, was used exclusively for training the GAN. These data were not used in the training or evaluation of the glaucoma classification models, thereby preventing any circularity or data leakage.

Schematic of the data partitions used for GAN and classifier training, with the final data cohort for the classification model.

SLO Fundus Translation With CycleGAN

We used a CycleGAN34 framework to translate the SLO images from the main cohort into synthetic CF photographs. The CycleGAN architecture, depicted in Figure 2, consists of a generator that performs the image translation and a discriminator that distinguishes between real and synthetic images.

The model was trained for 50 epochs with the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 2e-4, followed by a fine-tuning phase of 10 epochs at a reduced learning rate of 1e-5. Some examples of translations are provided in Figure 3.

Model Architecture and Training

We utilized the VGG19 architecture,58 a convolutional neural network (CNN) widely used in ophthalmic DL studies.

We first investigated three model architectures widely used in ophthalmic DL studies: ResNet101, EfficientNetB0, and VGG19 (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Fig. S2). The base CNNs were connected to an average pooling layer with a 3 × 3 pool size, followed by flattening, batch normalization, and dropout layers to prevent overfitting. The network concluded with a fully connected layer and a sigmoid activation function to produce the final binary classification output.

After selecting the VGG19 architecture for the rest of the study, we developed 5 distinct models to investigate the optimal training strategy:(1)“SLO” model: Trained solely on SLO fundus images.(2)“CFP” model: Trained solely on real CF photographs.(3)“GAN” model: Trained solely on synthetic CF photographs.(4)“SLO+CFP” model: Trained on SLO images and fine-tuned on real CF photographs.(5)“GAN+CFP” model: Trained on synthetic CF photos and fine-tuned on real CF photographs.

Optimal hyperparameters were determined via grid search, resulting in the selection of the RMSprop optimizer (learning rate of 1e-5, momentum of 0.2, weight decay of 1e-3), dropout rate of 0.3, and batch size of 8. For the fine-tuning stages of the “SLO+CFP” and “GAN+CFP” modes, the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1e-5) was used to stabilize convergence. All models were trained for 50 epochs with early stopping and were evaluated on a hold-out test set consisting of only original CF photographs. Any SLO or GAN images paired with the hold-out test set were excluded from the training set to mitigate bias and rote-image memorization.

All models were capped at 50 epochs with early stopping based on validation performance. This strategy was chosen to prevent overfitting and to ensure consistency across all models for fair comparison. Validation sets, derived exclusively from the training partition, were used for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping. The hold-out test set of CFP images was not used at any stage of training, hyperparameter selection, or model optimization. This ensured that test images never indirectly influenced model development, preserving the integrity of reported performance.

Performance and Statistical Analysis

We assessed model performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To evaluate clinical relevance, sensitivities at 90% and 95% specificities were assessed. We also compared model performance among race, sex, and ethnicity groups to assess the generalizability of our findings. To assess statistical significance in model performance, we used bootstrapping and paired t-tests to compare AUC scores. We performed 1000 bootstrap resampling iterations to compute AUC distributions, deriving 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. For direct model comparisons, we conducted paired t-tests on the bootstrapped AUC values. The null hypothesis assumed no significant difference in performance between models, and statistical significance was determined at a threshold of P < 0.05.

Feature Space and Diversity Analysis

To quantitatively assess domain shift and data diversity, we performed two analyses. First, we extracted 2048-dimensional feature embeddings from the penultimate layer of the GAN+CFP classifier for all SLO, real CF, and synthetic CF images. We then applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to visualize the 2D projection of these embeddings. Second, we measured feature-space variance (trace of the covariance matrix) and pixel-level entropy (Shannon entropy of grayscale histograms) for both real and synthetic CF image sets.

Additionally, to further quantify the quality and similarity of the generated images, we calculated three standard metrics. Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) was used to measure the distance between the feature vector distributions of the real and synthetic images, where a lower score indicates a closer perceptual match. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was calculated to assess the average pixel-level difference between image pairs, with lower values signifying greater fidelity. Finally, the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) was used to evaluate perceptual similarity by comparing luminance, contrast, and structure, where a score closer to 1.0 indicates higher structural resemblance.

The AUC scores and sensitivities at 90% and 95% specificities for the 5 developed models in the classification of glaucoma from CF photographs are provided in Figure 4. The “GAN+CFP” model achieved the highest AUC of 0.94 (95% CI = 0.93–0.96, P < 0.05), followed by the “CFP” (AUC = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.87–0.91, P < 0.05), the “SLO+CFP” (AUC = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.86–0.90, P < 0.05), and the “GAN” models (AUC = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.80–0.84, P > 0.05). The “GAN+CFP” model also achieved the highest sensitivities at 90% and 95% specificities (0.83 and 0.77, respectively), followed by the “CFP” (0.77 and 0.66, respectively), “SLO+CFP” (0.71 and 0.56, respectively), and “GAN” models (0.51 and 0.33, respectively). The “SLO” model achieved the worst performance, with an AUC (0.74, 95% CI = 0.72–0.77, P < 0.05) and sensitivities of 0.36 and 0.23 at 90% and 95% specificities, respectively. Of note, the “CFP” model performed significantly better than the “SLO+CFP” model (P = 0.034), demonstrating the domain shift problem when training on two different imaging modalities. Additionally, the “GAN” and “SLO+CFP” models experienced the greatest variability between sensitivities at 90% and 95% specificities (0.18 and 0.15, respectively).

AUC scores and sensitivities at 90% and 95% specificities for each of the 5 developed models.

To further explore model behavior under low-data conditions, we trained both CFP and GAN+CFP models on progressively larger subsets of real CFP data (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%). As expected, models trained on smaller subsets achieved lower training loss and higher apparent training accuracy, but their test performance was inferior. For GAN+CFP, test AUCs ranged from 0.86 to 0.88 with 20% to 40% of the data, steadily improving to 0.94 with 100% (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Fig. S4; Fig. 5). By comparison, CFP models underperformed in the low-data regimes, achieving lower test AUCs (0.80–0.84 at 20%–40%), and converging toward an AUC of 0.89 with 100% data. This pattern indicates mild overfitting in low-data regimes, but also highlights that GAN augmentation enables stronger performance than CFP-only when real data are limited, whereas additional real data progressively improves generalization for both approaches.

ROC curves for (A) CFP and (B) GAN+CFP models on CFP-only hold-out test data trained with varying proportions of real CFP data (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that GANs can be effectively utilized to translate SLO images to synthetic CF photographs, thereby enhancing the performance of automated glaucoma detection models. The model trained on a combination of real and synthetic CF images (GAN+CFP) significantly outperformed models trained on single modalities or a direct combination of SLO and CF images. Notably, the model trained on both synthetic and non-synthetic CF images (GAN+CFP) demonstrated consistent performance across various demographic groups, compared to other models. These findings underscore the importance of addressing domain shift when integrating diverse types in DL pipelines.

The “GAN+CFP” model achieved the highest AUCs and sensitivities out of the five models trained; in contrast, the “SLO+CFP” model, which directly combined SLO and real CF images, underperformed compared to the “GAN+CFP” model. Although DL models require large and diverse datasets to accurately detect diseases and pathologies from medical images,25,26 they often face domain shift issues when combining different imaging modalities.30,31 By using CycleGAN to translate SLO images into synthetic CF photographs, we were able to effectively expand our dataset without collecting additional CF images and improve model performance. Moreover, the “CFP” model outperformed the “SLO+CFP” model, further highlighting that differences in imaging modalities can negatively impact model performance if not properly addressed. Our findings suggest that merely adding more diverse imaging types is insufficient without appropriate adaptation techniques like image-to-image translation.

These findings are consistent with our additional low-data subset experiments, which show that although training curves for 20% and 40% subsets suggested better fit to the training data, test performance was reduced compared with models trained with more real CFPs. This reflects mild overfitting when data are scarce. Importantly, GAN-based augmentation mitigated this effect by sustaining AUCs of 0.86 to 0.88 even with only 20% to 40% of real CFPs, demonstrating practical value in low-data scenarios, such as under-resourced clinics. We therefore clarify that the primary contribution of this work is not broad superiority over CFP-only training, but rather incremental improvement and robustness when real data are limited.

This improvement is particularly important given the challenges in glaucoma screening, where access to imaging data from diverse populations is often limited. Traditional models that rely solely on CF photographs can struggle with domain shift when incorporating other imaging types, such as SLO, which are less commonly used for diagnostic purposes. By leveraging CycleGAN for image-to-image translation, we were able to tap into a vast resource of unused SLO data, thereby expanding the dataset without needing to collect additional CF photographs. Additionally, GANs can expand datasets by increasing the number of images from different races and ethnicities,59–61 addressing data scarcity from different patient populations.

Our results demonstrate that the “GAN+CFP” model consistently outperformed all other models across various demographic groups, including race, sex, and ethnicity. Additionally, the GAN+CFP model exhibited more stable sensitivity scores at 90% and 95% specificities across demographic groups compared with the models trained solely on SLO or CF images. The model's consistent performance suggests that using synthetic CF images translated from SLO data can reduce the performance gaps often observed in models trained on traditional imaging datasets, which tend to underperform for minority groups due to a lack of representation in the training data.62,63

Whereas our diversity analysis indicated that the synthetic images did not introduce new variability beyond the original dataset, the tight alignment in the feature space was crucial for reducing domain shift and improving model robustness. The achieved FID, MAE, and SSIM scores are strong result for a medical imaging task, comparing favorable with scores from similar studies.64–67 This suggests our method produces synthetic images with high quality and fidelity to the target domain. A few publications have applied GANs for translating ophthalmic imaging domains. Costa et al. and Zhao et al. used conditional GANs to translate retinal vessel networks to CF photographs33,52; similarly, Yu et al. used Pix2pix, a supervised GAN, to enlarge CF photograph datasets by translating masked vessel and optic disc images to CF photographs.27 Like our study, these publications sought to use GANs to enlarge small existing CF photograph datasets to facilitate medical image analysis studies; however, they did not compare different models trained on both synthetic and non-synthetic images. Wu et al. utilized a conditional GAN to volumetric OCT scans to fundus autofluorescence photographs to identify regions of geographic atrophy,68 and Ju et al. used CycleGAN to translate CF photographs to ultra-widefield images to improve diabetic retinopathy (DR) grading and lesion detection.53 Like our study, Ju et al. found training their models on synthetic imaging data improves classification performance; however, their publication fails to compare model performances between demographics, as our study does. Other ophthalmic applications using GANs include post-intervention prediction,44,69 denoising and enhancing photographs,46,47,50,70,71 and segmentation.27,40–43,72

We recognize that diffusion models have recently gained attention due to their ability to produce even higher-quality images, especially in complex image-to-image translation tasks.73–75 Although diffusion models may outperform GANs in generating fine details and achieving greater fidelity, they are often more computationally intensive, requiring longer training times and larger datasets, making them less practical for quick, scalable implementations in clinical settings. In contrast, traditional data augmentation techniques, such as rotating, flipping, or cropping existing images, are widely used to artificially increase dataset size and improve model robustness.76,77 Whereas useful, data augmentation alone may not fully address domain shift issues or generate entirely new synthetic images from underutilized modalities like SLO. Thus, GANs are a practical and powerful choice for image translation in medical imaging due to their ability to balance computational efficiency, scalability, and the ability to address domain-specific challenges.

As with all studies, several limitations must be acknowledged. In some cases, poor-quality or blacked-out SLO images led to the generation of unusable synthetic CF photographs, which may have negatively impacted GAN training and DL model performance. Another limitation is the relatively small demographic representation of certain minority groups within our dataset, coming from a single testing center, which may limit generalizing the findings to broader populations. Furthermore, our study did not explore the use of more advanced generative models, such as diffusion models, which may provide higher-quality image translations but require significantly more computational resources and training data. Future studies could address these limitations by improving input image quality, expanding the dataset to include different testing locations, and experimenting with alternative generative models to further enhance model performance, such as StarGAN78 and its variants.79
