Transparent UVA photodetectors based on oxide semiconductors for real-time wearable skin protection monitoring
Authors: Yu Bin Kim, Hyoun Ji Ha, Jung Min Yun, Min Jung Choi, Kyusun Han, Seunghwan Kim, Soohyung Park, Minsu Park, Hongseok Oh, Jae-Young Yoo, Seong Jun Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ultraviolet A (UVA) radiation penetrates deeply into the skin and can potentially damage skin health. Therefore, continuous monitoring of UVA exposure is essential to prevent long-term effects on skin health. For this purpose, UV light sensors should provide selective UVA detection while maintaining high optical transparency in the visible range, enabling integration into wearable and transparent electronic devices. In this study, we fabricated a photodetector optimized for UVA detection based on a fully transparent p-n junction photodiode architecture. A multijunction structure was designed using SnO2/ZnO as n-type oxide semiconductors and CoOx combined with defect-engineered HfOx as p-type semiconductors, with ITO serving as the cathode. The photodiode exhibited excellent photoresponse in the 340 to 350 nm wavelength range, with an average visible transmittance of ~75% and a responsivity of 80.1 mA W−1. Furthermore, the device enables real-time UVA monitoring under sunlight, demonstrating strong potential for applications in wearable UVA sensing and skin protection health care systems.

INTRODUCTION

Solar radiation covers a broad range of electromagnetic wavelengths, with ultraviolet (UV) radiation accounting for ~7% (1). UV radiation is classified into UVC (100 to 280 nm), UVB (280 to 315 nm), and UVA (315 to 400 nm). The ozone layer effectively absorbs UVC, allowing only UVB and UVA to reach Earth’s surface (2–6). Among them, UVA constitutes more than 95% of UV radiation and penetrates deeper into the dermis due to its longer wavelength (6). Prolonged exposure to UVA generates reactive oxygen species, causing oxidative stress that accelerates skin aging and increases the risk of cancer (4–8). Therefore, real-time monitoring of UVA exposure is essential to prevent skin damage and associated health risks.

Effective UV monitoring requires the development of sensors with rapid response, long-term stability, and low power consumption. Among various types of photodetectors, p-n junction photodiodes offer advantages such as fast response, low-voltage operation, and a compact two-terminal structure that facilitates circuit integration (9, 10). Wide-bandgap n-type oxide semiconductors, including zinc oxide (ZnO), titanium oxide (TiO2), tin oxide (SnO2), indium-gallium-zinc-oxide, and gallium oxide (Ga2O3), have been extensively studied for UV photodetectors, with SnO2/ZnO heterostructures and composites exhibiting improved UV selectivity and visible-blind characteristics (11–16). For p-type semiconductors, cobalt oxide (CoOx) has been widely explored in heterojunctions with n-type materials owing to its photochemical stability, tunable bandgaps, and efficient hole transport (17–19). In addition, hafnium oxide (HfOx), commonly used as an insulating layer, has recently been investigated as a hole injection layer or hole transport layer through defect states induced by oxygen vacancies (20, 21).

Previous studies on UV photodiodes have commonly used opaque metal electrodes, which substantially reduced overall device transparency, even when the active layers were transparent (15, 22). Conventional metal electrodes such as aluminum (Al) and gold (Au) exhibit optical transmittance below 40% in the visible range, resulting in a high level of opacity that is noticeable to the naked eye (23, 24). This opacity limits design flexibility and complicates the integration of sensors into electronic devices, including wearables and display systems (25). Visible transparency is crucial for UV sensor design in smart healthcare technology. It enables seamless integration with surroundings and users, particularly in wearable devices such as smart watches, glasses, and patches, where transparent UV detection is required (26–29). This optical imperceptibility allows for hidden sensor integration, which minimizes user awareness of the sensor and contributes to an enhanced user comfort (30). For this purpose, achieving full device transparency requires not only wide bandgap oxide semiconductors for UV detection but also the implementation of transparent electrodes. Indium tin oxide (ITO), a representative transparent electrode material, exhibits high optical transmittance in the visible range, maintaining effective overall transparency without compromising the performance of the UV photodetector (31–33). The high conductivity and chemical stability of ITO additionally ensure stable operation of devices, which is essential for a transparent electrode material (34–36). Owing to these favorable properties, ITO-based transparent devices have been widely adopted in wearable sensors, smart windows, and biomedical devices (37–40).

In this study, a fully transparent oxide semiconductor–based photodiode was developed, which achieves high transmittance in the visible spectrum and selective UV detection with peak responsivity at 340 to 350 nm. A photodiode was constructed by incorporating dual n-type (ZnO and SnO2) and dual p-type (CoOx and HfOx) oxide layers, where the formation of a multijunction—HfOx/SnO2 (p-n), SnO2/ZnO (n-n), and CoOx/HfOx (p-p)—contributed to enhanced device performance by facilitating efficient separation and transport of charge carriers. The device demonstrated an average transmittance of 75%, along with the responsivity of 80.1 mA W−1, highlighting its competitive performance among transparent UV photodetectors. Based on these results, a wearable UV monitoring device capable of continuous operation under natural sunlight was developed, demonstrating feasibility for real-time health care applications.

RESULTS

Device architecture and physical characteristics

The schematic illustration in Fig. 1A shows the multilayer photodiode structure composed of glass/ITO/CoOx/HfOx/SnO2/ZnO/cathode layers. Patterned ITO glass was used as the substrate, where the ITO served as an anode. CoOx and HfOx were used as p-type semiconductor layers (p-layers), while SnO2 and ZnO were used as n-type semiconductor layers (n-layers). All layers were deposited via solution processing. We fabricated the devices using two different cathodes. ITO was deposited by radiofrequency (rf) sputtering to form the transparent cathode, while an opaque Al cathode was deposited on the same multilayer structure by thermal evaporator for comparison with the transparent device. Cross-sectional high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) image in Fig. 1B confirms clear separation of each layer. Figure 1C exhibits the transmittance and absorbance spectra of the CoOx, CoOx/HfOx, CoOx/HfOx/SnO2, and CoOx/HfOx/SnO2/ZnO films. The final multilayer photodiode structure maintained more than 90% transmittance in the 400- to 900-nm range, indicating a highly transparent film. Figure 1D shows scanning transmission electron microscopy high-angle annular dark-field (STEM-HAADF) image. Figure 1E presents the corresponding energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping, revealing the distributions of Co, Hf, Sn, Zn, C, N, In, and O within the film. The CoOx, HfOx, SnO2, and ZnO layers had thicknesses of 5.05, 6.94, 111.1, and 38.1 nm, respectively.

Optoelectronic performance of photodiodes with ITO and Al cathodes

Figure 2A provides a schematic illustration of the device based on the transparent cathode, and the corresponding optical transmittance spectra are shown in Fig. 2B. Despite a slight decrease in transmittance caused by surface roughness and structural defects associated with the ITO cathode thickness, the photodiode maintained a high average transmittance of ~75% in the visible range (380 to 780 nm) (41, 42). The inset of Fig. 2B shows an image of glass/ITO/CoOx/HfOx/SnO2/ZnO with a full-area deposition of the ITO, visually demonstrating its excellent optical transparency. The photodiode with the transparent cathode was evaluated for optoelectronic performance under illumination. Because of the limited power intensity of the available light sources, a 365-nm UV lamp was used to measure the current-voltage (I-V) characteristics under varying light intensities. Figure 2C displays the I-V curves under illumination with 365-nm light, confirming that the photocurrent in the reverse bias region increases as the power intensity varies from 0.354 to 2.45 mW cm−2. Figure 2D shows the I-V characteristics of the device under dark conditions and various UV wavelengths of 300, 340, 350, and 400 nm at a power intensity of 17 μW cm−2. The p-n junction photodiode demonstrated rectifying behavior in the dark, showing a rectification ratio of 1.21 × 104 at ±1.5 V. Among the wavelengths of 300, 340, 350, and 400 nm, the highest photocurrent was observed at 340 and 350 nm, corresponding to the mid-range of the UVA region. Figure 2 (E to I) shows the optoelectronic properties, including responsivity (R), detectivity (D*), external quantum efficiency (EQE), noise equivalent power (NEP), and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). In the I-V curve under dark conditions of Fig. 2D, the dark current reaches a minimum around a bias of −0.7 V. At this bias, the photocurrent-to-dark current ratio under UV illumination is maximized. In addition, higher dark current contributes considerably to sensor noise. Accordingly, the optoelectronic properties were calculated from measurements at −0.7 V. The R was calculated using the following equationR=(Iph)P×A(1)where Iph is the photocurrent, P is the power of the incident light, and A is the illuminated area of the device, which is 0.05 cm2 (43). The highest R value of 80.1 mW A−1 was exhibited at a wavelength of 340 nm, as shown in Fig. 2E. The detectivity was determined using the equationD∗=A·R2qIdark(2)where q is the electronic charge and Idark is the dark current (43). The maximum D* value of 2.55 × 1011 Jones was achieved for the wavelength of 350 nm, as shown in Fig. 2F. The EQE in Fig. 2G was calculated using the following equationEQE=Iph×hc(q×λ×P×A)(3)where h, c, and λ correspond to Planck’s constant, the speed of light, and the wavelength, respectively, and the peak EQE reached 29.3% at 340 nm (43). The lowest NEP shown in Fig. 2H was 9.86 × 10−13 W Hz-1/2 at 350 nm, calculated using NEP = A/D* (44). The SNR was calculated using the equationSNR=I¯ph−I¯dark1N∑iN(Ii−I¯dark)2(4)where I¯ph is the average photocurrent, I¯dark is the average dark current, Ii is the measured photocurrent, and N is the number of measurements (45). The highest SNR value was 74.5 at 340 nm, as shown in Fig. 2I. The superior values of R, D*, EQE, NEP, and SNR were observed at either 340 or 350 nm, near the center of the UVA region. These results demonstrate that the transparent photodiode achieves excellent UVA detection while maintaining high optical transparency.

In transparent electrodes, electrical conductivity enhances with increased electrode thickness, while visible transparency deteriorates owing to intensified optical absorption as described by Beer-Lambert’s law (46, 47). However, compared to previously reported oxide-based fully transparent p-n junction photodiodes, our proposed photodiode achieves competitive responsivity while maintaining high optical transparency, as shown in Fig. 3A (48–54). Table 1 summarizes a detailed comparison of previous studies, including device structure, responsivity, transmittance, and the specific UV wavelength range to which the devices respond. To further validate the performance of the transparent device, we compared it with a photodiode using a conventional Al cathode. Figure 3B shows a schematic illustration of the device with the Al cathode. The I-V characteristics of the Al-cathode device were measured under conditions equivalent to those of the ITO-cathode device, as shown in Fig. 3C. Consistent with the photodiode incorporating the ITO cathode, the photocurrent was relatively low at wavelengths of 300 and 400 nm, whereas it increased at wavelengths corresponding to the mid-UVA region. The R, D*, EQE, and NEP at 350 nm were 94.7 mA W−1, 4.19 × 1011 Jones, 33.6%, and 5.33 × 10−13 W Hz−1/2, respectively. These results demonstrate superior optoelectronic performance, as shown in Fig. 3 (D to G). Consequently, the ITO-based photodiode not only maintains high optical transparency but also shows enhanced responsivity compared to previously reported fully transparent devices, achieving performance comparable to that of devices with metal electrodes.

Response characteristics depending on time and power

To evaluate the time-dependent photoresponse of the photodetectors with ITO and Al cathodes, periodic UV light was applied from 300 to 400 nm at 10-nm intervals. As shown in Fig. 4 (A and B), the devices demonstrate stable and reproducible photocurrent responses under alternating light conditions. The time-dependent photoresponse of the photodetectors with ITO and Al cathodes exhibits similar trends. The ratio of photocurrent to dark current values (Iph/Idark) at each wavelength is summarized (table S1). At 350 nm, both the ITO and Al electrodes exhibited the highest Iph/Idark values of 7.72 and 11.6, respectively. In addition, the photocurrent—and consequently the Iph/Idark ratio—tends to decrease as the wavelength shifts away from 350 nm, either toward 300 or 400 nm. Figure 4 (C and D) depicts the rise time (τr) and decay time (τd) of the ITO-cathode device at wavelengths of 340 and 350 nm, respectively. The τr and τd are defined as the time intervals during which the signal varies from 10 to 90% of its maximum value. At 340 nm, the τr and τd were measured to be 80 and 110 ms, respectively. For 350 nm, the τr of 100 ms and τd of 110 ms were observed. To investigate the correlation between light intensity and the photocurrent, the time-dependent photoresponse was evaluated under various UV intensities. As shown in Fig. 4E, the photocurrent increases as the incident optical intensity at 365 nm varies from 0.353 to 2.19 mW cm−2. The dependence of the photocurrent on light intensity is described by the power law Iph α Pα, where the exponent α denotes how the photocurrent changes with light intensity, as shown in Fig. 4F (55). The nonunity exponent (0.5 < α < 1) reflects the presence of complex effects such as electrode-hole generation, trapping, and recombination in the photoresponse of the semiconductor photodetector (55). For an ideal photodetector, the exponent α is close to 1; thus, a measured α value of 0.909 highlights effective charge carrier generation and collection, with negligible recombination losses (56, 57). Furthermore, the adjusted coefficient of determination (R2) value of 0.998 demonstrates an excellent linear correlation in the graph, with a value approaching unity. As a result, these stable and efficient photoresponse behaviors of the photodiode suggest promising characteristics for high-performance UVA detection applications.

Electronic structure and chemical state analysis of p-layers

To determine the appropriate concentrations for CoOx and HfOx films used as p-layers, XPS analyses were conducted on films prepared with solution concentrations of 0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 M. Figure 5 (A to C) shows the Co 2p XPS spectra for CoOx (0.01 M), CoOx (0.03 M), and CoOx (0.05 M) films. The spectra were deconvoluted into Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 peaks, centered at 780 and 795 to 796 eV, respectively, using Lorentzian-Gaussian fitting. Weak satellite peaks were observed at higher binding energies relative to the main Co 2p peaks. Notably, the asymmetry of the Co 2p3/2 peak suggests the coexistence of Co2+ and Co3+ oxidation states (58). These oxidation states were further deconvoluted into two components, with Co3+ assigned to the lower binding energy. Accordingly, the Co 2p XPS spectra for all solution concentrations were resolved into five peaks, Co3+ 2p3/2, Co2+ 2p3/2, Satellite1 (Sat1), Co 2p1/2 (Co2+ + Co3+), and satellite2 (Sat 2). The areal ratios of these peaks are summarized in table S2. As the molar concentration of solutions increases, the Co3+ ratio rises, while the ratios of both Sat1 and Sat2 decrease. Co3+ gives rise to weak satellite peaks, whereas intense satellite peaks are mainly attributed to Co2+ (59–61). The distinct decrease in the satellite peaks thus confirms the reduction of Co2+. Consequently, the highest Co3+ ratio observed in the CoOx (0.05 M) film increases the hole concentration, resulting in improved p-type conductivity. For this reason, CoOx (0.05 M) was selected as the p-type layer.

The formation of oxygen vacancies in HfOx generates defect states below the Fermi level, which facilitate the hole transport and contribute to the p-type conductivity (20, 62). Figure 5 (D to F) presents the Hf 4f XPS spectra of the HfOx films with concentrations of 0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 M. The spin-orbit splitting of Hf 4f orbitals resulted in two peaks observed at ~19.6 eV (Hf 4f5/2) and ~18.1 eV (Hf 4f7/2) (63, 64). The peaks were deconvoluted into Hf4+ states at higher binding energies (Hf4+ 4f7/2, Hf4+ 4f5/2) and reduced oxidation state HfX+ (x < 4) peaks at lower binding energies (HfX+ 4f7/2, HfX+ 4f5/2) (63, 65). As shown in table S3, the HfX+ ratios obtained from the Hf 4f XPS spectra of HfOx films were 63.9, 80.7, and 68.8% for 0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 M concentrations, respectively, with the HfOx (0.03 M) film exhibiting the highest ratio. Considering that peak area correspond to chemical state changes associated with the number of oxygen bonds, the HfOx (0.03 M) film with the highest HfX+ ratio can be inferred to exhibit the most pronounced oxygen bonding changes (21, 66). To verify the presence of oxygen vacancies essential for p-type properties in HfOx, the O 1s XPS spectra were additionally examined. As shown in Fig. 5 (G to I), the O 1s spectra of the HfOx films were deconvoluted into three peaks corresponding to metal-oxygen (M-O), oxygen vacancy (VO), and hydroxide compounds (−OH), centered at binding energies of 530, 531, and 532 eV, respectively (67). The areal ratios of M-O, VO, and −OH are summarized in table S3, and the HfOx (0.03 M) film indicated the highest amount of VO. This observation is consistent with the previously mentioned Hf 4f analysis, which showed the highest HfX+ ratio. This suggests that the oxygen vacancy–related defect states were the most prominent in the HfOx (0.03 M) film. Therefore, HfOx (0.03 M) was chosen as another p-type layer.

Interfacial energy level alignment and UV response mechanism

UPS measurements were carried out to examine the interfacial electronic band structure of the ITO/CoOx/HfOx/SnO2/ZnO photodetector. Figure 6A shows the work function (WF) from the secondary electron cutoff (SECO) region and the valence band maximum (VBM) from the valence region for the ITO, ITO/CoOx, ITO/CoOx/HfOx, ITO/CoOx/HfOx/SnO2, and ITO/CoOx/HfOx/SnO2/ZnO films. The WF values of ITO, CoOx, HfOx, SnO2, and ZnO were measured to be 4.52, 4.75, 4.57, 3.58, and 3.66 eV, respectively. The energy level between the Fermi level (EF) and the VBM were determined as 0.56, 2.80, 2.59, and 3.33 eV. As shown in the inset of Fig. 6A, the near valence region of the HfOx film exhibits a defect state located 1.22 eV below the EF, which is attributed to oxygen vacancies. The optical bandgap (Eg) was extracted from the Tauc plot obtained via UV visible spectroscopy (see fig. S1). Two optical band gaps were identified in CoOx, Eg1 = 2.17 eV and Eg2 = 1.52 eV, associated with O2− to Co2+ and O2− to Co3+ transitions, respectively, as depicted in fig. S1A (68). Meanwhile, the measured bandgaps of HfOx, SnO2, and ZnO were 5.47, 3.76, and 3.30 eV, respectively, as shown in fig. S1 (B to D). The schematic energy level diagram of the photodetector, constructed based on the EF alignment, is illustrated in Fig. 6B. The defect state of HfOx, the VBM of SnO2, and the conduction band minimum (CBM) of both layers constitute a type II (staggered) band alignment, as seen in typical p-n junctions. The interface between CoOx and HfOx in the p-layers forms a type I (straddling) heterojunction, where the gap states of HfOx contribute to efficient hole transport. On the other hand, the interface between SnO2 and ZnO in the n-layers forms a type II (staggered) heterojunction, which promotes electron transport and suppresses recombination (16).

The formation of a type II heterojunction between SnO2 and ZnO particularly improves the detection in the UV wavelengths (69–72). This observation is supported by Fig. 1C, which demonstrates that incorporating ZnO into the CoOx/HfOx/SnO2 results in decreased transmittance and increased absorbance in the UV range. To further elucidate the mechanism of the photodiode, Fig. 6C presents a schematic illustration of its operation under UV light. The built-in electric field (Epn) in the p-n junction is directed from the n-type SnO2 to the HfOx exhibiting p-type behavior, where gap state exists. Because of the CBM difference (ΔEC = 1.12 eV) between SnO2 and ZnO, an electric field (Enn) is formed between the n-layers, consistent with the direction of the Epn (73). In addition, a valence band offset (ΔEV = 0.66 eV) between the gap state in HfOx and the VBM of CoOx drives hole transport from HfOx to CoOx. This leads to the establishment of an electric field (Epp) between the p-layers, aligned with the direction of Epn. Therefore, the overall electric field—composed of Epn, Enn, and Epp arising from the multijunction formed by these multilayers—enables efficient charge separation and transport.

Real-time UV monitoring enables practical UV health care application

The photodetector has competitive optoelectronic properties, which contribute to detecting UVA radiation in outdoor environments. Moreover, outstanding transparency minimizes visual interference, which is beneficial for health devices used on the skin. Accordingly, the UV sensor was validated under sunlight to ensure detection of natural UV radiation and to evaluate its potential as a wearable device for preventing UV-related health risks. Figure 7 (A and B) schematically illustrates the system overview and practical applications of the UV health care device. Figure 7A indicates that UV light is incident on the photodetector; the generated electrical signal is amplified through an operational amplifier and then wirelessly transmitted to a smartphone application via a Bluetooth module. The UV sensing device responds to variations in UV conditions, converting them into application data that are displayed in real time on the smartphone user interface (UI). Figure 7B depicts a schematic illustration of a user wearing the UV sensing health care device in outdoor sunlight. The enlarged schematic in the top left reveals the integration of the photodetector fabricated in this study with various electronic components within the circuit, forming the complete healthcare device. The corresponding photograph in the bottom left shows the actual device, where its excellent transparency can be clearly observed. The right side of Fig. 7B shows that users can monitor information about the intensity of UV exposure via smartphones. The operational of the complete device is confirmed in fig. S2 through the response of the UV sensor application under 365-nm UV illumination. These observations demonstrate that the UV sensing device operates properly in integration with the application, enabling effective monitoring of UV radiation. UV monitoring was conducted under sunlight as a practical demonstration using the UV sensing device integrated with the ITO/CoOx/HfOx/SnO2/ZnO/ITO photodetector at 2-hour intervals from 10:00 to 18:00 based on UV power intensity (mW cm−2) measured by a UV dosimeter. Figure 7 (C and E) shows the UV power intensities observed on a sunny day (7 February 2025) and a cloudy day (12 February 2025), respectively. As the UV intensity is reduced under cloudy conditions compared to the sunny day, the corresponding application data from the smartphone also decreases, as shown in Fig. 7 (D and F). The developed device demonstrates a sensitive real-time response to changes in sunlight conditions. This result indicates its potential for effective use in detecting UV exposure and protecting user health in outdoor environments.

The minimal erythemal dose (MED) is defined as the threshold UV dose (J cm−2) that induces sunburn according to skin type (74, 75). To evaluate the practical applicability of the developed UV sensing device, we aimed to verify its feasibility for real-time UV monitoring. The device continuously accumulates application data during UV exposure and provides a warning to the user upon reaching 80% of the MED. The application data obtained at different UV intensity levels using a 365-nm UV lamp were analyzed via nonlinear fitting, as shown in Fig. 8 (A and B). A fitting equation was derived to correlate UV power intensity with application data, allowing the estimation of the UV exposure dose from the accumulated application data at specific intensity levels. For this analysis, a user was assumed to have skin type I, for which the MED is ~20 J cm−2 (75). The warning level was determined to be 80% of this value, 16 J cm−2. When the UV intensity was set to 1.91 mW cm−2, the time-dependent accumulation data at 80% of the MED was measured (Fig. 8C). Under this UV condition, the theoretical time required to reach 16 J cm−2 was calculated to be ~8377 s (about 140 min). The application data at this intensity was estimated to be around 347 from the fitting equation. The total exposure dose is calculated as the cumulative sum of the product of UV intensity at each measurement point and the measurement interval, expressed as ∑(Ii × Δt) (76). Therefore, the accumulated data at 80% of the MED was obtained by summing the application data corresponding to each measurement. With the power intensity and the application data fixed at 1.91 mW cm−2 and 347, respectively, the accumulated data were calculated as 2 × 8377 × 347 = 5,813,638, where “2” represents the number of measurements per second and “8377” represents the total duration in seconds. Figure 8D confirms that the accumulated data reached 5,813,638 after ~8533 s, resulting in a relative error of 1.9%. DISCUSSION

In this work, we successfully demonstrated a p-n junction photodiode based on the ITO/CoOx/SnO2/ZnO/ITO structure, achieving enhanced responsivity in the UVA radiation range. A fully transparent photodetector was fabricated using ITO as the cathode, exhibiting a high average transmittance of 75% along with high responsivity. The total electric fields induced at between the p-n, n-n, and p-p junctions were combined, facilitating effective charge transport. Moreover, the fabricated UV photodiode was integrated with a circuit to construct a UV monitoring device capable of real-time sunlight detection. Notably, a UV warning system was developed that accumulates application data in real time and alerts the user upon reaching 80% of the MED, depending on the skin type. This health care device, integrated with a smartphone, demonstrates its potential as a practical approach to prevent risks associated with prolonged UV exposure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication of the device

CoOx solutions were prepared by dissolving cobalt acetate tetrahydrate [(CH3COO)2·4H2O, Daejung] in ethylene glycol monomethyl ether (C3H8O2, Daejung) at concentrations of 0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 M, followed by stirring for 30 min. HfOx solutions at concentrations of 0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 M were prepared by mixing hafnium (IV) chloride (HfCl4, Sigma-Aldrich) in ethylene glycol monomethyl ether, also stirred for 30 min. Tin (IV) oxide colloidal dispersion (15% in H2O, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 2.5 weight % ZnO nanoparticle (NP) solution (N-10, Avantama, NP size of 12 nm) were used as received.

Patterned ITO glass substrates were sequentially ultrasonicated in deionized (DI) water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 15 min, respectively. After cleaning the substrates, UV-ozone treatment was conducted for 15 min to enhance hydrophilicity and eliminate residual contaminants. CoOx and HfOx layers were then deposited by spin coating at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal annealing at 180°C for 1 hour. SnO2 and ZnO layers were sequentially spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 40 s and 2000 rpm for 30 s, respectively, and annealed at 180°C for 30 min. For the transparent cathode, ITO was deposited by rf magnetron sputtering using a 9:1 In2O3:SnO2 target at a rate of 4.5 nm min−1 for 16.5 min. For the opaque Al cathode, 130 nm of Al was deposited by thermal evaporator under 5 × 10−6 torr at a deposition rate of 3 Å s−1.

Characterization of the device

The optoelectronic characteristics of the photodiode were measured using a probe station and a SourceMeter (Keithley 2636A). The light intensity from the tunable light source (Newport) was measured using a photodetector (Thorlabs). The transmittance of the films was analyzed by a UV-visible spectrometer (Cary100, Agilent) and UV-visible Spectroscopy System (Agilent 8453, Agilent Technologies). Using the Nexsa (Thermo Fisher Scientific) system, XPS and UPS analyses were performed using Al Kα (1486.6 eV) and He-I (21.22 eV) source, respectively. A UV meter (GT-510, Giltron) was used to measure the UV intensity of sunlight.
