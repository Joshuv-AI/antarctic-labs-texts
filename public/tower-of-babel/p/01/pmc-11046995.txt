Diffusion of responsibility: a clinical audit
Authors: Sian Harkin
Published: 2024

Diffusion of responsibility (DoR) can be defined as the psychological phenomenon where individuals who are part of a large group feel less responsibility to act within an emergency scenario.1, 2, 3 This DoR can be seen in everyday clinical practice, and research has demonstrated its detrimental impact on patient safety. In 2005, a case study was reported that supports this phenomenon, in which Elaine Bromiley sadly passed away after admission to hospital for a routine day-case procedure. A 'cannot intubate, cannot ventilate’ scenario was encountered by the anaesthetic team during induction to anaesthesia.4, 5 A coroner's inquest6 highlighted the role of DoR in the incident, specifically commenting on the fact that no one took a decision to lead and initiate the standard emergency procedures, despite numerous experienced clinicians being present.

The aim of the current study was to identify whether DoR during morning handover has an impact on patient safety within the acute medical department. During handover, there is a discussion between numerous senior clinicians to decide whether the patients who presented to the department overnight should be handed over to specialties for assessment within 24 hours. Here, it is the responsibility of several providers to discuss patients defined as ‘suspected stroke/TIA’ with the stroke team; however, in the absence of standardised procedures, individual roles and responsibilities are frequently assumed rather than clearly spelled out. The current patient group included 16 individuals who were defined as ‘suspected stroke/TIA’ after being admitted to the medical assessment unit between the hours of 20:00 and 08:00. Quantitative data were collected over an 18-day period using standardised data collection proformas.

The results of this audit reported that this particular handover method resulted in 16% of patients being missed on the post-take ward round by both the stroke and medical teams within 24 hours of admission. In the 2022 stroke National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines update,7 two research recommendations for the management of stroke/TIA were proposed: (i) that aspirin administered within 48 hours of acute ischaemic stroke improves outcome compared with no treatment or early anticoagulation, and (ii) that ‘immediate assessment’ by a stroke clinician had both better health outcomes and lower costs than ‘assessment within a week’ for the entire population of suspected TIA, without the use of a risk stratification tool.8 This evidence has been supported by a more recent study in 2022.9 From this data collection and analysis, it can be suggested that DoR may play a role in patient safety and measures need to be put in place to abolish this phenomenon during morning handover within the acute medical department.
