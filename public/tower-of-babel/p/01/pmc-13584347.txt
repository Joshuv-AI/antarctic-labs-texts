Comparative analysis of complete chloroplast genomes provides insights into phylogenetic relationships and screening of polymorphic markers in Dimocarpus and related species
Authors: Yuan Guo, Chong Sun, Han Liu, Chongyu Liu, Yifei Deng, Xia Liu, Xueqian Fu, Ruxin Xu, Mi Kuang, Lingling Peng, Shanrong Li, Dalan Feng, Xiaohong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLongan (Dimocarpus longan) is a highly nutritious subtropical perennial fruit tree that has been used as a traditional medicine since ancient times for its tonic properties. However, morphological variations and frequent hybridization events complicate cultivar identification and phylogenetic studies of this species.ResultsThis study sequenced and assembled the chloroplast genomes of 12 longan varieties and assembled Dimocarpus confinis chloroplast genome using resequencing data, along with published chloroplast genomes of two Litchi species and three longan species, conducting comprehensive analyses to reveal structural variations in longan chloroplast genomes. A total of 490 long repeat sequences and 1,420 SSR loci were identified, mainly composed of A/T mononucleotide repeats located in the LSC region and intergenic spacers. Comparative analysis revealed low genomic divergence, with six highly variable regions and four polymorphic loci identified, offering potential for molecular marker development. Primers designed from the highly variable trnR (UCU)-atpA region showed preliminary discriminatory potential between D. longan and L. chinensis. IR boundary comparison showed expansion in Litchi species and slight contraction in D. confinis. Selection pressure analysis indicated most genes were under purifying selection, with only ccsA under positive selection. Phylogenetic analysis supported a close relationship between Dimocarpus and Litchi, with D. confinis forming an independent branch positioned between them, indicates that D. confinis might be a hybrid of lychee and longan. Primers based on the highly variable trnR (UCU)-atpA region enabled effective differentiation among longan and Litchi species.ConclusionsThis study provides essential genetic resources for accurate species identification, resolving phylogenetic relationships, and assessing genetic diversity in Dimocarpus. Our findings establish a molecular foundation for germplasm conservation and breeding programs of this economically and medicinally important species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09337-5.Keywords: Dimocarpus, Chloroplast genome, Species identification, Phylogenetic relationship

Introduction

Longan (Dimocarpus longan), also known as dragon’s eyeball and closely related to lychee, is a subtropical evergreen fruit tree, and belongs to the family Sapindaceae [1]. Due to its high nutritional and medicinal value, longan is now broadly cultivated in many countries, such as China, Thailand, Vietnam and Australia [2]. Among these countries, China has the highest production and possesses the largest planted area of longan [3], mainly distributed in Guangxi, Guangdong, Fujian, Yunnan, Taiwan and Hainan [4]. Longan has been cultivated in China for over 2000 years with rich germplasm resources [5]. It has been reported that there are about 400 longan cultivars in China [6]. However, most varieties have a rather ambiguous genetic background due to their reproduction through both crossbreeding and inbreeding in the field. Most longan cultivars are named based on historical, geographical or local traditional customs, which often results in a lack of authoritative and unified nomenclature.

Given the agricultural and medicinal importance of longan, several studies have used morphological traits (leaf size, seed shape and maturity time) [7, 8] and utilized molecular markers to assess the diversity of the many longan cultivars cultivated worldwide [2]. For example, AFLP (Amplified fragment length polymorphism) [9], ISSR (Inter-Simple Sequence Repeat) [10, 11], RAPD (Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA) [12], SNP (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism) [13], and GBS (Genotyping-By-Sequencing) [14]. However, the expression of morphological traits is often influenced by environmental conditions, and relatively short sequences are not powerful enough to distinguish the genetic diversity of longan due to the low resolution of the markers. Furthermore, the phylogeny relationships of longan, litchi and D. confinis remain controversial because of complex interspecific crosses, limited number of species and the lack of effective phylogenetic signals markers. Thus, further studies and clarification based on more data are urgently required [9].

The complete chloroplast (cp) genome offers advantages such as uniparental (maternal or paternal) inheritance, high copy number, and conserved structure compared to using only a few nuclear gene markers [15, 16]. Therefore, the chloroplast genome has become a more practical and powerful molecular tool for resolving phylogenetic relationships among species. With the increasing availability of complete chloroplast genome sequences, a larger number of phylogenetic informative sites are provided, offering higher resolution and more valuable information for taxonomic and phylogenetic studies of various plant groups. Plastome of angiosperms generally has a highly conserved quadripartite structure consisting of a small single copy (SSC) region, a large single copy (LSC) region, and two copies of inverted repeat (IRs) regions [17–19]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has made it simpler and cheaper to obtain cp genome sequences, allowing for comparison of sequences evolution among different individuals [20].

Compared to the previously reported chloroplast genome of D. longan [21]. In this study, 33 (13 newly sequenced and assembled, and the remainder download from the GenBank) representative samples plastome data were obtained by sequencing complete chloroplast genome, and comparative analyses of their structure and function. The purpose of this study is to (i) generate information on the basic genome structure, repetitive structures characteristics, codon usage, and IR expansion of the Dimocarpus chloroplast genome; (ii) reconstruct the relationships of Dimocarpus species and clear the taxonomic status of longan, litchi and D. confinis based on the complete cp sequences. The results of this research will provide insights into the rapid identification and development of longan germplasm resources in the future.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The longan leaf materials used in this study were collected from a longan plantation located in Zhutuo Town, Yongchuan District, Chongqing, China (29.07155° N, 105.824071° E). A total of 12 longan varieties (D. longan) were newly sequenced, and one sample of D. confinis was newly assembled. The newly sequenced samples of this study were preserved at plant herbarium of Chongqing University of Arts and Sciences, and the voucher number information of samples refer to Table S1. Fresh leaves were collected and rapidly stored in liquid nitrogen, upon returning to the laboratory, the samples were stored at −80℃ for DNA extraction.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total DNA from all samples was extracted using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Tiangen Biotech., Beijing, China), and the quality of the DNA products was assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Short-insert libraries (250 bp) were constructed using DNA fragments, and the BGISEQ-500 platform from BGI, Shenzhen, China, allows paired-end sequencing of 150 bp, yielding at least 2 Gb of clean data per sample. NGS QC Toolkit v2.3.33 [22] with Q30 (quality score of ≥ 30) filtered low-quality sequences to obtain high-quality reads.

Chloroplast genomes assembly and annotation

The complete chloroplast genome sequences were assembled with NOVOPlasty v.2.6.2 using clean data with default parameters [23] and annotated with GeSeq [24] and ARAGORN [25] software. The tRNAscan-SE v2.0 tool [26] was also used to detect tRNA genes, and Geneious v9.1.8 [27] was used to manually adjust and confirm the detected tRNA genes. The D. longan cp genome sequence (NCBI accession number NC037447) was used as a reference for annotation. Organellar Genome DRAW (OG-DRAW) v1.3.1 [28] was used to draw the circular genome maps. The annotated whole cp genome sequences have been submitted to GenBank, the accession numbers are listed in Table 1 and Table S1.

Genome structure analyses

The Editseq v7.1.0 [29] software was used to measure the GC content of the longan chloroplast genome sequence, EMBOSS [30] software was used to calculate the GC content of the first, second and third base positions (GC1, GC2 and GC3) of the coding region in cpDNA and the total GC content of the coding region. CodonW tool [31] calculated the relative synonymous codon utilization rate (RSCU) for each codon. Use the online REPuter tool [32] to count the four types of repeated sequences (forward, palindromic, complement and reverse) in the chloroplast genomes of 13 longan varieties. The maximum computed repeats were set to 100 bp, the minimum repeat size was 30 bp, and the Hamming distance was 3; The repeat numbers of the SSRs for mononucleotides, dinucleotides, trinucleotides, tetranucleotides, pentanucleotides, and hexanucleotides were 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3, respectively, and analysis was performed using MISA v1.01 [33]. The minimum distance between two SSRs was set to 100 bp.

Genome comparison and nucleotide variation analyses

Using the mVISTA v2.0 program [34], 18 cp genomes were selected for visual analyses in Shuffle-LAGAN mode. Use IRscope [35] software to detect the contraction and expansion of the boundaries of LSC, SSC and IRs regions. DnaSP v6.12.03 [36] was used to calculate the nucleotide diversity (Pi) of the genome sequence and screen out highly variable regions of the genome. The sliding window was set to 600 and the step size was 200.

Selective pressures analyses

The D. confinis cp genome sequence was used as the reference. The sequences of the 12 longan varieties were used to calculate Ka and Ks values, respectively. The coding and protein sequences of the longan cp genome were extracted using PhyloSuite software. The protein sequences of the 12 longan varieties and D. confinis were extracted using BLASTN v2.10.1 software. The sequences were compared to identify the best match and obtain the homologous protein sequence. MAFFT v7.0 [37] was used to align the homologous protein sequences. A perl script to map the aligned protein sequences to the coding sequence to generate the aligned coding sequences. Finally, KaKs_Calculator v3.0 [38] was used to calculate Ka and Ks values.

Phylogenetic analyses

A total of 33 plastomes were used in this study, including 15 accessions of D. longan (12 of which were newly sequenced), one sample of D. confinis (newly assembled), two accessions of litchi, and 15 other related species downloaded from GenBank. Aesculus assamica (Hippocastanaceae) and Dipteronia sinensis (Aceraceae) were used as outgroups. MAFFT v7.471 [37] was used to align the chloroplast genome sequences of 33 species. A Phylogenetic tree was constructed using MrBayes v3.2.6 [39], with Bayesian Inference (BI) analysis, using the GTR + I + G model. The MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo) algorithm was applied for 200,000,000 generations with four Markov chains, and the trees were sampled every 100 generations. The first 25% of trees were discarded as burn-in. Nucleotide substitution models were determined using jModelTest v2.1.1 [40]. The BI tree tree was visualized using Figtree v1.4.

Screening of candidate chloroplast regions for species discrimination

The DnaSP software was used to determine the nucleotide diversity (Pi) with a 200 bp step size and a 600 bp window length. Candidate primer pairs were designed using Geneious Prime [27] software based on highly variable regions of the chloroplast genomes to evaluate their potential for species discrimination among D. longan, D. confinis, and L. chinensis. These markers were derived from the highly variable regions of their cp genomes. Three pairs of primers were designed for PCR amplification (Table 4). The primers were synthesized in Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). PCR reactions were conducted in a total volume of 25 µL, which contained 12 µL 2 × PCR Mix, 1 µL each of the sense and antisense primers (10 pM), 1 µL genomic DNA (18 ng), and 10 µL ddH2O. The thermal cycling consisted of 94 ℃ for 4 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 ℃ for 30 s, 53–55 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 1 min and a final extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min. PCR products were visualized using 1.5% agarose gels after electrophoresis. Finally, the amplified PCR products were sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for first generation sequencing.

Results

Chloroplast genomes characteristics of Dimocarpus species

This study analyzed and compared the characteristics of 13 samples (12 D. longan cultivars and one accession of D. confinis) in Dimocarpus. The results showed that the cp genomes of the 13 samples have a typical quadripartite structure, with sizes ranging from 160,670 to 160,803 bp. Two inverted repeat (IRs) areas (28,407–28,428 bp) were separated a large single-copy (LSC) region (85,588–85,677 bp) and a small single-copy (SSC) region (18,268–18,271 bp), their total GC content was 37.79%−37.92% (Fig. 1, Table 1). The GC content of the IR region of the chloroplast genome of 13 samples is 42.34%−42.35%, which is higher than the GC content of the LSC (36.04%−36.24%) and SSC (31.81%−31.99%) regions (Fig. 2, Table 1). The cp genomes of the 13 samples encode a total of 136 genes, including 90 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 37 tRNA genes, 8 rRNA genes and 1 pseudogene (Table 2). In addition to the double-copy gene rps12 located in the LSC and IR regions, a total of 21 genes were repeated in the IR regions, including 7 tRNA genes (trnV (GAC), trnR (ACG), trnN (GUU), trnL (CAA), trnI (GAU), trnI (CAU), trnA (UGC)), 4 rRNA genes (rrn23, rrn16, rrn5, rrn4.5) and 10 PCGs (ycf1, ycf15, ycf2, rps7, rpl23, rps19, rpl22, rpl2, rps3, ndhB). Furthermore, the LSC region contains 22 tRNA genes and 57 PCGs, while the SSC region has only 1 tRNA genes and 10 PCGs, ndhF spans the SSC-IRB region (but the ndhF gene of D. confinis located in the SSC region), and the pseudogene infA was located in the LSC region. There are 18 genes containing introns in the longan chloroplast genome, of which 3 contain two introns (rps12, clpP1, pafl), and the remaining 15 have only one intron. The two copies of the rps12 gene share the first exon, the first exon is located in the LSC region, and the other two exons are located in the IR region (Table 3).

Codon usage

The study analyzed the RSCU values of the protein-coding genes regions in the chloroplast genomes of 15 Dimocarpus samples, 1 D. confinis sample, and 2 L. chinensis samples (Fig. 3). The genes in the D. longan, D. confinis, and L. chinensis cp genomes contain 27,353, 27,408, and 27,849 codons (including stop codons), respectively. Among these encoded amino acids, the most abundant are leucine (Leu), isoleucine (Ile), and serine (Ser), accounting for 10.43–10.47%, 8.46–8.50%, and 7.67–7.70% of the total codons, respectively (Table S2). The codon for cysteine (Cys) is the least common in cp genomes, accounting for 1.14% of the total number of codons on average. A comparison of the GC content at positions 1–3 (GC1-GC3) and the total GC content across 18 chloroplast genome samples revealed no considerable differences in GC content among the 15 D. longan species and varieties. However, there were slight differences in the GC composition between D. longan and both D. confinis and L. chinensis (Fig. 4A-D). Additionally, the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values ranged from 0.009 to 1.990, with 32 codons having RSCU values greater than 1. Most codons (87.88%) ended with A/U (A/T), indicating a preference for A/T bases. Notably, arginine (Arg), leucine (Leu), and serine (Ser) exhibited a high degree of codon preference across the 18 species. Only one codon, UGG, had an RSCU value of 1, indicating that tryptophan (Trp) does not exhibit codon bias.

Repeat sequences and SSR loci analysis

A total of 1,420 SSRs were identified in the cp genomes of 18 species using MISA. Among these, each of the five D. longan samples (D. longan ‘Linglong’, D. longan ‘QingkeShixia’, D. longan 1, D. longan 2, and D. longan 3) contained 79 SSRs, while D. longan ‘Wanyou’ had 82 SSRs. The remaining nine D. longan samples each had 81 SSRs detected. In the two L. chinensis species, a total of 75 SSRs were detected in each species. In comparison, the D. confinis species had the fewest SSR loci detected, with a total of 64. A total of five types of nucleotide repeat sequences were identified, comprising 1,006 mononucleotide repeats, 89 dinucleotide repeats, 145 trinucleotide repeats, 106 tetranucleotide repeats, and 74 pentanucleotide repeats, with no hexanucleotide repeats detected. In each species, SSRs are predominantly composed of mononucleotide repeat sequences (ranging from 43 to 59), accounting for 67.19% to 74.67% of the total SSRs. Trinucleotide SSRs are the second most common, while dinucleotide and pentanucleotide SSRs are the least frequent. Among all nucleotide repeat sequences, A/T repeats are the most abundant. Two SSR types not found in the D. longan species were detected in the two L. chinensis species (AGC/CTG and AAAAG/CTTTT). Compared to the other 17 species, the D. confinis species did not exhibit the SSR types AAAT/ATTT and AGAT/ATCT (Fig. 5A-B, Table S3). Furthermore, these SSR loci are concentrated in the LSC region and are primarily distributed in the intergenic regions.

A total of 490 long repeat sequences were identified in the chloroplast genomes of 15 D. longan samples, one D. confinis species, and two L. chinensis species, including 187 forward (F), 30 complement (C), 272 palindromic (P), and 1 reverse (R) repeats (Table S4). A comparison of the number and types of long repeats in the cp genomes of 18 species. The results showed that the detected D. longan species have the same types of long repeat sequences. Except for D. longan 2 (which contains a total of 29 long repeat sequences, including 11 forward (F), 0 reverse (R), 2 complement (C), and 16 palindromic (P) repeats), the other 14 D. longan samples also exhibit the same number of long repeat sequences for each type (with a total of 27 long repeat sequences, including 10 forward (F), 0 reverse (R), 2 complement (C), and 15 palindromic (P) repeats). In D. confinis, a total of 27 long repeat sequences were detected, including 11 forward (F), 1 complement (C), and 16 palindromic (P) repeats, with no reverse (R) repeats detected. In the two species of the Litchi genus, 28 long repeat sequences were detected in each, with the same types and numbers of sequences, including 13 forward (F) and 15 palindromic (P) repeats, and no reverse (R) or complement (C) repeats detected (Fig. 5C).

Comparative analysis of chloroplast genomes

To clarify the degree of difference between the chloroplast genome sequences of 15 D. longan samples and those of D. confinis and L. chinensis, mVISTA was used to compare the 18 sequences with D. confinis as the reference sequence (Fig. 6). The results indicate that the degree of divergence among the 18 sequences is relatively low, demonstrating a high level of similarity. The variation in the IR region is lower than that in the SC region, and the variation in non-coding regions is markedly higher than that in coding regions.

A total of six highly variable regions were identified, trnR (UCU)-atpA, petN-psbM, ndhC-trnV (UAC), ndhF-rpl32, ccsA-ndhD, and ndhD-psaC. The nucleotide diversity (Pi) of the cp genomes of the 18 species was analyzed using the DNAsp software, revealing a total of 798 polymorphic sites, with Pi values ranging from 0 to 0.01739 and an average Pi value of 0.00164 (Table S5). In addition, a total of four sites with relatively high nucleotide variability (Pi > 0.01) were identified, namely petN-psbM, ndhC-trnV (UAC), ndhF-rpl32, and ycf1 (Fig. 7). Two of these sites are located in the LSC region, and two are in the SSC region, with the highest Pi value observed in the intergenic region ndhC-trnV (UAC). The identification of these sites holds potential value for the development of molecular markers in D. longan species.

The expansion and contraction of the IR region boundaries are commonly observed in many species and are considered to be a primary reason for the differences in chloroplast genome size during evolution. Therefore, a comparative analysis of the IR region boundaries of the cp genomes of the 18 samples were conducted (Fig. 8). The results indicate that the cp genomes of the 18 species are highly conserved, but some differences also exist. The IR region of the 15 D. longan species is consistently 28,428 bp, while the IR regions of the two L. chinensis species measure 30,103 bp, they show a notable expansion compared to those of the D. longan species. The IR region of D. confinis measures 28,407 bp, indicating a smaller degree of contraction. Among the 18 samples, the genes located at the IR/SC boundary are the same (rpl16, rps3, ycf1, ndhF, trnH). The ndhF gene in the 15 D. longan samples is 2,243 bp in length, while the ndhF gene in the two L. chinensis species is shorter at 2,237 bp, although both contain the JSB (SSC/IRb boundary) boundary. The ndhF gene of D. confinis is completely located in the SSC region, with a length of 2,235 bp, and is 6 bp away from the IRb region. The JSA (SSC/IRa boundary) boundary of all species is entirely situated within the ycf1 gene, resulting in the formation of a pseudogene ranging from 1,484 to 3,182 bp in the IRb region. The trnH gene of the 14 D. longan species and the 2 L. chinensis species is located in the LSC region and is 3 bp away from the IRa boundary. In D. confinis and D. longan 2, the distances to the IRa boundary are 4 bp and 43 bp, respectively. The rpl16 and rps3 genes of all 18 species show no expansion or contraction at the IR boundary.

Adaptive evolution analysis

An analysis of synonymous (KS) and non-synonymous (KA) substitution rates was conducted using 52 protein-coding genes from the chloroplast genomes of 15 D. longan species and one D. confinis species. The KA/KS ratios for all protein-coding genes range from 0 to 1.7525, with an average of only 0.1896. Among these, the ccsA gene has the highest average KA/KS ratio of 1.7525 and it may be a gene that has undergone recent rapid evolution, and its function requires further investigation (Fig. 9, Table S6).

Phylogenetic analysis

To clarify the phylogenetic relationships among the 15 D. longan species and 1 D. confinis species, sequences of the shared protein-coding genes from the cp genomes of 31 Sapindaceae plants, 1 Aceraceae plant, and 1 Hippocastanaceae plant were extracted. After sequence alignment, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Bayesian method (BI) (Fig. 10, Table S1). The resulting tree showed generally high support values at the species level. Aesculus assamica (Hippocastanaceae) and Dipteronia sinensis (Aceraceae) formed independent branches as outgroups, while all Sapindaceae species clustered into a well-supported clade. Within Sapindaceae, Dimocarpus and Litchi formed a sister relationship with strong support (posterior probability = 1.00), indicating their close phylogenetic affinity.

Phylogenetic tree of 33 species constructed using the Bayesian inference (BI) method. The samples with accession numbers marked in red represent the newly sequenced samples in this study

At the interspecific level, D. confinis was placed between D. longan and L. chinensis with full support (1.00), suggesting a close evolutionary relationship among these taxa. This placement reflects species-level divergence within the Sapindaceae family. Within D. longan, plastome sequences of the 15 cultivars showed shallow differentiation and formed three major plastome-based clusters. Ten cultivars (‘Chuliang’, ‘Shuinan No. 1’, ‘Lufeng No. 1’, ‘Dawuyuan’, ‘Shuguan’, ‘Youtanben’, ‘Zaoshudawuyuan’, ‘Fuyan’, ‘Huangke Shixia’, and ‘Wanyou’) grouped together with high support (0.98). Five cultivars (‘Qingke Shixia’, D. longan1, D. longan2, D. longan3, and ‘Linglong’) formed a separate cluster with moderate support (0.73).The clustering among cultivars reflects shallow plastome variation within D. longan, which is consistent with the conserved and maternally inherited nature of chloroplast genomes.

Selection of optimal primers for species identification

Based on the highly variable region trnR (UCU)-atpA identified in this study, sequence variation was observed among the chloroplast genomes of L. chinensis, D. confinis, and D. longan in this region. Therefore, six primers (Table 4), including three forward and three reverse primers, were designed from conserved flanking regions. These primers were randomly combined to generate nine primer pairs for preliminary evaluation. Due to the unavailability of D. confinis samples, primer screening was using genomic DNA from L. chinensis and D. longan. PCR amplification products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. All nine primer pairs generated size differences between the two species, with amplification products of D. longan consistently larger than those of L. chinensis. For each primer pair, the amplified product in D. longan was consistently larger than that in L. chinensis (Fig. 11). Among them, the primer pair (3F, 2R) yielded a single, bright band with the most pronounced difference, making it the optimal candidate for distinguishing among the two species, L. chinensis and D. longan.

Discussion

Chloroplast genome characteristics and genetic markers

Due to the conserved structure and slow evolution of plant chloroplast genomes, they have been utilized in plant taxonomy and molecular evolution studies. Species identification and phylogenetic research based on cp genomes are a development trend in plant taxonomy and have gained increasing attention and recognition from researchers [41]. This study is the first to sequence the chloroplast genomes of 12 D. longan cultivars and assemble the chloroplast genome of D. confinis based on resequencing data. The sequencing results reveal a genome structure similar to that of most angiosperms. The cpDNA of D. longan and D. confinis both exhibit a typical circular quadripartite structure, with cp genome lengths ranging from 160,670 to 160,803 bp. The length differences are relatively small compared to closely related species such as L. chinensis (162,524 bp) [42], Nephelium lappaceum (161,321 bp) [43], and Pometia tomentosa (160,818 bp) [44], with variations between 148 and 1,854 bp. The GC content is 37.79% and 37.92%, respectively. Both D. longan and D. confinis have annotated a total of 136 genes, including 90 protein-coding genes, 37 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes, which closely aligns with the findings for Pometia tomentosa. Although the genome lengths of certain species vary slightly in the LSC, IR, and SSC regions, the overall gene number and GC content show minimal differences across all genomes. This indicates a high degree of conservation among the chloroplast genomes within the Dimocarpus genus.

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are widely present in chloroplast genomes. Due to their repeatability, codominant inheritance, and high level of transferability, SSRs are frequently used in studies of species’s genetic diversity, cultivar identification, and phylogenetic analysis [45, 46]. This study detected 79–82 SSR loci in the D. longan species, 64 SSR loci in D. confinis, and 75 SSR loci in each of the two L. chinensis species. Among these, mononucleotide repeats range from 43 to 59, showing the greatest variation in number. The Dimocarpus genus has the fewest repeat sequences, with notable differences compared to various D. longan cultivars and Litchi species. The identified SSR loci provide a valuable resource for future molecular marker development in Dimocarpus species. However, subsequent studies should focus on screening additional SSR loci and employing more sensitive genotyping techniques to develop robust markers for species identification in Dimocarpus.

Codons are present in both nuclear and organellar genes of higher plants, and codon usage bias refers to the selective or non-random use of synonymous codons to encode amino acids in the protein-coding genes of organisms [47, 48]. The codon usage bias may also exhibit consistency across different species. This study shows that the codon usage patterns of D. longan, D. confinis, and L. chinensis are quite similar, with leucine being the most frequently used amino acid in their chloroplast genomes. A total of 576 codons with RSCU values greater than 1 were shared across the three species, with 87.88% of these codons ending in A/U (A/T) bases. Therefore, all three species exhibit a strong preference for A/T at the third codon position, which is consistent with the codon usage bias observed in most dicotyledonous plants [49].

Variable regions and selective pressure analysis

The chloroplast genome contains highly variable regions that are informative for phylogenetic and population genetic studies [50, 51]. Regions with elevated evolutionary rates are particularly useful for distinguishing closely related taxa and for DNA barcoding research [52]. Based on comparative analysis of 18 plastomes and nucleotide diversity (Pi) assessment, seven highly variable regions were identified, including six intergenic spacers [trnR(UCU)-atpA, petN-psbM, ndhC-trnV(UAC), ndhF-rpl32, ccsA-ndhD, and ndhD-psaC] and one coding gene (ycf1). All hotspots were located in the single-copy (SC) regions, indicating higher variability in the LSC and SSC regions compared with the relatively conserved IR regions. These regions may provide useful loci for future phylogenetic and population-level investigations within Dimocarpus.

This pattern of identified hotspot regions shows some concordance with variation hotspots reported in other angiosperm chloroplast genomes. For instance, the ndhF-rpl32 intergenic region, identified here as highly variable, has also been highlighted as a potential SSR hotspot and genetic marker in diverse taxa such as Musa [53], Ficus [54] and Cannabis sativa [55]. Similarly, the ccsA-ndhD region has been found to exhibit elevated variability in multiple plant lineages. This suggests that certain intergenic regions may be predisposed to elevated mutation rates across angiosperms, making them valuable resources for comparative evolutionary studies and marker development.

Studies have shown that variations in the cp genome are largely the result of the contraction and expansion of the IR regions [56]. The IR size of the 15 D. longan varieties is identical, with the IR region in D. longan being larger than that in D. confinis, at 28,428 bp and 28,407 bp, respectively. The two Litchi species have the largest IR regions, both at 30,103 bp. The IR sizes of these three species exceed the normal range for most angiosperms (20–27 kb) [57]. The shift at the IR/SSC boundary may be due to the expansion of the IR region and a certain degree of contraction in the SSC region in L. chinensis. The LSC region shows relatively small changes, resulting in a more stable state at the LSC/IRb boundary. The overall conservation of chloroplast genome structure and the concentration of genetic variation in intergenic regions observed in this study are consistent with the typical characteristics of plastid DNA in angiosperms. This conservation is primarily attributed to its non-recombinant, uniparentally inherited nature, high copy number in cells, and the essential functions of most plastid genes in photosynthesis and other metabolic processes, which impose strong purifying selection on coding regions [58]. Consequently, most observed genetic variation, including SSRs and nucleotide polymorphisms, tends to accumulate in non-coding intergenic regions where selective constraints are relaxed [59]. In these regions, mutations are more likely to be neutral or nearly neutral, thus serving as rich sources of polymorphism for population genetics and phylogeographic studies while maintaining the functional integrity of essential genes.

The KA/KS ratio is widely used to evaluate selective pressure and adaptive evolution in genes [60, 61]. Nonsynonymous substitutions (KA) may alter protein structure and function, whereas synonymous substitutions (KS) generally reflect background mutation rates [62]. A KA/KS ratio < 1 indicates purifying selection, while a ratio > 1 suggests potential positive selection [63]. In this study, KS values were generally higher than KA values, indicating overall evolutionary conservation in Dimocarpus. Among the analyzed genes, ycf1 showed a relatively high average KS value, rpl32 exhibited a higher KA value, and ccsA had a relatively high average KA/KS ratio, which may indicate the possibility of positive selection. These results suggest that most plastid genes are likely under purifying selection, consistent with previous reports that photosynthesis-related genes evolve more slowly than other protein-coding genes [17, 64]. Selective pressure analysis provides important insights into the evolutionary dynamics of Dimocarpus species and contributes to understanding their adaptive evolution [65].

Phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic controversies

In a previous study, Mahar et al. [66] conducted phylogenetic analysis using ribosomal ITS sequences, and the results showed that D. confinis and Pometia tomentosa have the closest relationship, while D. longan is more closely related to L. chinensis. Although D. confinis and D. longan are within the same evolutionary clade, their phylogenetic relationship appears to be the most distant. In recent years, many studies have used protein coding regions or cp genome sequences for phylogenetic analysis [67]. This study constructed a phylogenetic tree using the complete cp genome sequences of Dimocarpus species and their close relatives. The results show that Dimocarpus forms an evolutionary clade with Litchi, with the closest phylogenetic relationship and high support values, which is consistent with the findings of Gonçalves et al. [68] and Wang et al. [21]. D. confinis formed a distinct lineage with high support. In the plastome-based phylogenetic tree, D. confinis formed an independent clade, whereas the 15 D. longan cultivars were grouped into two separate clusters (Fig. 10). This pattern suggests a complex evolutionary relationship among these taxa that warrants further investigation. Some researchers have constructed a phylogenetic tree using ITS sequences, showing that D. confinis holds a taxonomic position higher than both L. chinensis and D. longan, supporting the classification of D. confinis as a separate genus. This finding differs substantially from the results of the present study. Further research is required to validate the genetic relationships among D. longan, and L. chinensis based on chloroplast genome data.

Conclusion

The chloroplast genomes of 12 Dimocarpus species were sequenced, and the cp genome of one D. confinis individual was assembled using resequencing data in this study. These genomes were compared with previously published cp genomes of Dimocarpus and Litchi species. The analysis revealed similarities and differences among the chloroplast genomes of Dimocarpus varieties and D. confinis, as well as their phylogenetic relationships. While differences among the cp genomes of Dimocarpus varieties were minimal, the distinctions between D. longan and D. confinis were primarily reflected in genome size and variations in intergenic regions. Additionally, seven highly variable regions, such as trnR (UCU)-atpA, petN-psbM, ndhC-trnV (UAC), ndhF-rpl32, ccsA-ndhD, ndhD-psaC, and ycf1, were identified as potential candidate loci for future DNA barcoding and studies of genetic structure and species diversity. In the adaptive evolution analysis, only ccsA showed a relatively high average KA/KS ratio (1.7525), suggesting possible positive selection. Phylogenetically, D. confinis appears intermediate between D. longan and L. chinensis, which may indicate a complex or reticulate evolutionary relationship; however, further investigation using additional genomic data is needed to confirm this. This observation offers a genetic basis for re-evaluating reticulate evolution within the Sapindaceae family. Overall, this study characterizes the genomic structure, repetitive sequences, SSR loci, and codon usage bias among Dimocarpus varieties and D. confinis at the cp genome level. A candidate primer pair was also preliminarily screened for species discrimination between Dimocarpus and Litchi, contributing to the comparative phylogenetic analysis of closely related species. These findings provide a foundation for the identification of Dimocarpus varieties and their phylogenetic classification.
