Breaking down breakfast: the relationship between morning meals and learning difficulties in adolescents
Authors: Reza Rahmanian, Ali Razeghi Pirbasti, Mohsen Shaygantabar, Fatemeh Javaheri-Tafti, Leyli Zahra Bahreini-Boroujeni, Alireza Hatami, Andisheh Khoshrang, Leila Sajedi, Marjan Ajami, Mina Esmaili, Anahita Houshiarrad, Saeid Doaei

AbstractBackgroundLearning difficulties (LDs) impair academic performance and cognitive function. Background

Learning difficulties (LDs) are recognized as a diverse set of disorders characterized by considerable challenges in the development and utilization of skills related to listening, speaking, reading, writing, or mathematics [1]. These impairments hinder academic performance and lead to reduced self-esteem, feelings of discouragement, poor social skills, a higher risk of dropping out of school, and challenges in both employment and social integration [2]. Moreover, LDs may result in feelings of anxiety, inadequacy, and shame, potentially leading to behavioral issues in school-aged children [3]. The estimated prevalence of LDs varies between 2% and 10%, influenced by the identification methods and definitions employed in different countries [4]. Genetic and environmental factors play significant roles in the development of various neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, such as LDs [5, 6]. Dietary behavior is one of the environmental factors that has been effective on LDs in school-aged children [7]. Breakfast consumption is among the important diet-related behaviors that can potentially affect learning performance [8, 9].

Breakfast is commonly recognized as the key meal of the day [10], typically comprising food groups like low-fat dairy products, grains, and fruits [11, 12]. The breakfast pattern can be associated with different mechanisms. For example, Evidence suggests that dairy products containing β-lactolin can increase monoamine levels in various regions of the brain, which may contribute to improved working memory and attention [13–15]. Additionally, skipping breakfast after overnight fasting may impede memory tasks like story recall and backward counting [16]. Breakfast consumption has consistently been linked to positive nutrient intake and enhanced dietary quality among children and adolescents [17–19]. Breakfast consumption positively impacts cognitive and school performance in children [8]. Ren Y et al. and Nawaja et al. reported a reverse association between simple sugar consumption and academic performance [20, 21]. In contrast, Yeng et al. reported a positive association between moderate simple sugar consumption and cognitive function [22]. Furthermore, Chen et al. and Choi et al. found a positive association between milk intake and school performance [23, 24]. As a result, inconsistencies within the existing body of research regarding the relationship between breakfast intake and LDs, combined with a notable lack of studies specifically examining this association, necessitate a new investigation to resolve these uncertainties.

For this reason, the current study aimed to explore the relationship between the consumption of different food groups at breakfast and the presence of LDs.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the association between the quantity and quality of breakfast and the presence of LDs. Participants were selected using a two-stage cluster sampling method, including boys and girls aged 10–19 years (defined as adolescents by WHO) from four schools in Jahrom, Iran, between March and June 2024 [25]. The study focused on middle and high school adolescents, adhering to specific age guidelines. Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1) for logistic regression, assuming a minimum detectable odds ratio of 1.8, α = 0.05, and statistical power of 80%. To adjust for including food groups and demographic covariates in the models, we accounted for a covariate adjustment of 0.1 (R² = 0.1), ensuring sufficient power after controlling for confounders, which led to a primary sample size estimate of 420. We set an initial target sample size of 501 adolescents.

Based on the inclusion criteria, two girls’ schools and two boys’ schools were randomly selected. These criteria included the administrators’ willingness to participate, the absence of previously conducted sequential nutrition and health programs, socioeconomic similarity, adherence to uniform health regulations, and proximity to one another to minimize adolescent interaction across schools. In the second stage, all adolescents in the selected schools were invited to participate through complete enumeration. Stratification by single-gender school type was employed due to its logistical feasibility, ability to secure an adequate sample size, and potential to minimize selection bias. Parents received written explanations of the study and its benefits, and written consent was obtained from those who agreed to allow their children to participate. The inclusion criteria included voluntary participation of adolescents and written parental consent. Adolescents who reported medical conditions causing dietary alterations, such as malabsorption syndromes, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), were excluded. Additionally, adolescents using medications that affect the absorption of dietary antioxidants or taking digestive enzyme inhibitors (e.g., Orlistat) were excluded. Adolescents who chose to withdraw from participation or for whom the necessary information could not be obtained were also excluded.

Dietary assessment

Two trained dietitians assessed adolescents’ dietary intake using three non-consecutive 24-hour dietary recalls (two weekdays and one weekend) following the USDA five-step multiple-pass method validated for adolescents [26]. Portion sizes were estimated with validated Iranian household measures and a photographic atlas [27–29]. Based on the Iranian Food Composition Table, the dietary intake of participants was computed [30, 31], and data quality was monitored via duplicate recalls in a 10% subsample and random supervisor call-backs. Based on the food pyramid, specific data on the breakfast meal, including the frequency of consumption and type of food groups (meat, dairy, grains, vegetables, and added sugars), were extracted [32]. To assess breakfast eating habits, participants were asked, “On how many days per week do you eat breakfast?” Their answers were grouped into the following categories: less than 1 day per week, 1 to 2 days per week, 3 to 4 days per week, 5 to 6 days per week, and every day of the week.

Assessment of adolescents’ anthropometric indices and socio-demographic information

The weight was measured using a SECA standard scale, calibrated to a scale of 100 g by trained personnel [33]. The scale was positioned on a flat surface, and the adolescents’ weights were recorded without shoes and with minimal additional clothing. The height was measured using a stadiometer while adolescents were standing erect and without shoes, with their heads still and parallel to the ground [34]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by the following equation: [35, 36]. Consequently, age- and sex-specific BMI-for-age z-scores were calculated based on the WHO BMI for age graphs [15]. Socio-demographic information including age, gender, parents’ marital status, monthly income, and tobacco use was collected using general questionnaire. Using, multivitamin, vitamin D and zinc supplements were also assessed.

Assessment of learning difficulties

All parents of the included adolescents were invited to the school. We utilized the Colorado Learning Difficulties Questionnaire (CLDS), a validated parent-report screening tool, to assess the adolescents’ learning difficulties [37]. This questionnaire includes 20 distinct items; each is rated on a scale from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) according to the responses provided. The total score is determined by summing the scores of all the questions answered by the participants [37].

Statistical analysis

Adolescents were classified into four equally sized quartiles based on the CLDS distribution. This approach ensured adequate statistical power while preserving the continuous nature of CLDS as a variable. One-way ANOVA and chi-square test were employed to identify general differences among the CLDS quartiles. At the same time, multinomial logistic regression was utilized in three models to assess the relationship between breakfast frequency, food group intake (dairy, grains, meat, simple sugars, and vegetables), and the quartiles of CLDS. In the first model, age (in years) and sex (male/female) were included as covariates. The second model was additionally adjusted for zinc supplementation (yes/no), vitamin D supplementation (yes/no), tobacco consumption (yes/no), multivitamin supplementation (yes/no), parental marital status (married/divorced), monthly household income (<$100, $100-$214, and >$214), and education level (middle/high). Finally, the last model included age- and sex-specific BMI-for-age z-scores (underweight, normal, overweight, and obese) as an additional adjustment, along with the factors from the previous models. The first category of the exposures including food group intake (< 1/d) and breakfast frequency (< 1/d) were considered as reference groups in the analysis. Furthermore, the first quartile of the outcome (i.e., CLDS), which indicates adolescents with the lowest risk of LDs, was used as a reference in the analysis. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS Corp, version 27, Chicago, IL, USA).

Discussion

Breakfast plays a crucial role in cognitive function and learning, particularly in children and adolescents. Short-term positive effects of breakfast consumption on cognitive domains such as attention, memory, and executive function have been reported [38]. However, the limited number of studies examining the association between breakfast intake patterns and LDs raises significant questions about the roles of nutritional intake patterns and LDs. In the present study, we assessed the association between breakfast pattern and CLDS. Higher scores of CLDS reflect greater LDs.

In the current study, compared to adolescents who consumed less than one serving of dairy, those who consumed more than one serving had lower odds of being in the highest CLDS quartile. Similarly, adolescents consuming more than four servings of dairy had lower odds of being in the third CLDS quartile. Furthermore, adolescents who consumed more than four servings of simple sugar had approximately three times the odds of being in the highest CLDS quartile. Adolescents who consumed one to two servings of vegetables had lower odds of being in the third CLDS quartile. In contrast, consumption of one to two servings of grains was associated with higher odds of being in the third quartile of CLDS.

Chen et al. indicated that higher dairy intake is associated with better executive function [23]. Also, Choi et al. found a positive association between milk intake and school performance [24]. These findings may support our results. However, the absence of observed LDs in their studies prevents us from generalizing their findings to LDs. Furthermore, various limitations, such as neglecting potential confounding factors, including the lack of reporting supplement intake and monthly income, may compromise the validity of their reports. In contrast, Guyot et al. indicated that total dairy product consumption was not associated with cognitive function. However, milk intake was negatively related to verbal memory performance, which may be attributed to several limitations, such as the 13-year gap between dietary recalls and the assessment of cognitive function [39]. Furthermore, Ren Y et al. reported an inverse association between sugar-sweetened beverages and academic performance [20]. Also, Nawaja et al. reported a reverse association between sweet consumption and academic achievement [21]. However, they assessed simple sugar consumption as a continuous variable by utilizing linear regression, which does not allow for comparison of simple sugar intake between different groups. Furthermore, in addition to the variability among observed populations that complicates the generalization of their findings, significant limitations may also distort the results. In Ren Y et al.‘s study, the reporting period for dietary data was not specified, making it difficult to determine whether it coincided with the reporting period for academic achievement [20]. In Nawaja et al.‘s study, sweet consumption was assessed using a single, brief question without any description of portion size, which reduces measurement precision [21]. In contrast, Yang et al. found a positive association between moderate simple sugar intake and cognitive function [22]. Additionally, Huang et al. indicated a positive association between vegetables and cognitive function, which may support our findings [40]. Finally, the absence of research examining the association between breakfast intake and LDs, combined with inconsistencies in previous study results, highlights the need for a new study to resolve these questions.

Several mechanisms may explain the association of breakfast pattern and LDs. Evidence suggests that dairy products with β-lactolin can increase monoamine levels in various regions of the brain that can contribute to improved working memory and attention [13, 14]. Furthermore, milk is a rich source of B-group vitamins, which are associated with short-term memory and learning and ultimately influence the development of lower cognitive scores in schoolchildren [41, 42]. Iron and riboflavin deficiencies can negatively affect motor and psychological functioning. These micronutrients, which are abundant in cow’s milk, may help protect some of the mechanisms involved in cognitive performance [43]. Additionally, dairy products are an excellent source of micronutrients such as calcium, zinc, and selenium, as well as vitamins C, D, and B6, all of which are essential for brain development and function [44–46]. The positive effects of dairy on cognitive performance may also be mediated by improved glucose regulation through whey protein and calcium [7]. It has been demonstrated that micronutrients, the antioxidant potential of vegetables, and their relation to mitochondrial function influence cognitive performance [45, 47]. Simple sugars are a good source of glucose, the brain’s primary energy substrate. Balanced glucose intake can synergistically enhance brain glucose levels and metabolism, thereby improving cognitive performance [22]. In contrast, a high intake of simple sugars triggers chronic low-grade inflammation in the brain, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [48]. Consequently, this induces insulin resistance and reduces levels of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), which adversely affects cognitive function [48] (the proposed pathway is presented in Fig. 1).

When interpreting our findings, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing causal relationships between breakfast consumption patterns and CLDS. It is possible that adolescents altered their responses to 24hDRs to reduce their reported nutrition intake, especially among overweight and obese individuals. Additionally, socioeconomic and cultural factors specific to the Iranian context may influence both breakfast habits and LDs, potentially limiting the generalizability of our results. Additionally, some wide confidence intervals were reported, which suggests that a larger sample size may be necessary. Furthermore, conducting multiple analyses across quartiles of CLDS could increase the risk of type I error. Fruit and ultra-processed food consumption were infrequent in this population, reflecting local dietary patterns. Due to the small number of participants reporting intake of these food groups, statistical analysis was not feasible, and these variables were therefore excluded from the study. We adjusted for the effects of potential confounding factors. However, the impact of residual confounders, such as breakfast calorie intake, physical activity, screen time, sleep quality, and the role of family, could not be fully ruled out. Furthermore, we were unable to assess the association between fruit and ultra-processed food intake at breakfast and CLDS. Although no significant association was found between breakfast quantity, meat intake, and the quartiles of CLDS, significant p-trend indicated meaningful differences between groups. However, we were unable to observe this probable non-strictly dose-response association. Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths, including a comprehensive evaluation of multiple food groups within the breakfast meal. The application of multiple statistical models with progressive adjustment for potential confounders enhances the robustness of our findings.

Further research is essential to deepen our knowledge about the association of dietary patterns and LDs by utilizing valid indices such as the Alternative Healthy Index (AHEI) [49]. Additionally, future randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to investigate the causal effects of simple sugar and dairy intake at breakfast on learning disorders. Moreover, subsequent studies should consider additional confounding factors, including calorie intake, sleep duration, and physical activity levels.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that adolescents who consumed more than one serving of dairy had lower odds of being in the highest CLDS quartile. Additionally, consuming more than four servings of simple sugars was associated with higher odds of being in the highest CLDS quartile.
