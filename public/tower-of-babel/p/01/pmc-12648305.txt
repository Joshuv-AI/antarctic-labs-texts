Electrifying Catalyst Production by Continuous-Flow Slurry Electrolysis
Authors: Jingjing Xiong, Guanwu Lian, Kangshu Li, Chunjing Ran, Yucong Huang, Haotong Cai, Chao Wu, Wenkai Ye, Xiaocang Han, Huaiguang Li, Jiabin Wu, Jiahua Zhu, Shibo Xi, Dongxu Ji, Dan Ren, Xiaoxu Zhao, Zhigang Zou, Zhongxin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The chemical industry is electrifying toward a net-zero emission future. Unfortunately, the catalyst manufacturing process remains almost untouched in the transition to electrification due to challenges in size controllability and powder handling. Herein, we reported a slurry electrolysis strategy for scale-up production of Cu nanocatalysts at the productivity of 15 g per hour with a Cu loading of 2.5 wt % in laboratory flow electrolyzers, while maintaining excellent controllability of particle size down to single atoms by regulating the nucleation and particle growth process via pulsed electrochemistry. Our strategy can be further extended to the synthesis of Ag and CuAg catalysts for diverse electrochemical applications. Further techno-economic analysis shows an extremely low greenhouse gas emission and production cost (0.03 kgGHG and 16.4 USD per kg catalyst) compared to traditional approaches. This effectively addresses the productivity bottleneck in the electrosynthesis of nanocatalysts and paves the way for practical applications.

Introduction

The global shift toward electrification is a response to the pressing need for a sustainable future with net-zero greenhouse gas emissions. , According to the International Energy Agency, the chemical industry is the world’s largest industrial energy consumer and the third-largest source of industrial emissions. Substantial efforts have been made to electrify chemical production via numerous electrochemical pathways (such as hydrogen production by electrochemical water splitting). Unfortunately, the catalyst manufacturing process, as the upstream and an integral part of chemical production, remains almost untouched in the transition to electrification.

The manufacturing of heterogeneous nanoparticle catalysts primarily involves the calcination and activation process at high temperatures after immobilizing the active components on the support by precipitation, impregnation, or ion exchange. Since chemical reactions only occur on the available surface, industrially relevant catalysts are usually synthesized into small nanoparticles with a size of less than 10 nm to maximize the exposure of active sites. , Such subnanometric species have an inherent aggregation tendency to minimize the surface energy during the high-temperature treatments in a reductive atmosphere (such as H2), leading to the emergence of larger particles with inferior catalytic activity and selectivity, as suggested by conventional wisdom. A variety of additives and ligands, together with high-surface-area supports, are therefore necessary to prevent the aggregation process.

Modern electrochemistry provides a sustainable and energy-efficient alternative to produce future nanocatalysts. Driven by the external potential, the electrochemical processes can be operated in an aqueous phase in ambient conditions, thus eliminating heat-induced aggregation in conventional thermal processes. , Meanwhile, the more flexible feature of electrified processes allows producers to shift electricity consumption to off-peak hours and integrate with renewable energy production, thus further reducing manufacturing costs. However, critical challenges remain in size controllability and compatibility with powder processing in traditional potential-dependent electrodeposition techniques. At more negative potentials than the equilibrium potential, metals spontaneously grow into bulk crystals during overpotential deposition (OPD). , By contrast, underpotential deposition (UPD) may allow precise control for monolayer and single-atom deposition owing to favorable metal-substrate interaction. − However, this will sacrifice the deposition rate and substrate scope. The extension of UPD to heterogeneous supports such as carbon particles is particularly challenging as a result of weak metal-substrate interaction and inherent heterogeneity of supported surfaces. , Additionally, current practices of electrodeposition are mainly based on fixed electrode systems with single-phase electrolytes, which are incompatible with the catalyst manufacturing process that handles powders. Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop a novel electrosynthesis approach to address the above challenges.

Herein, we report a continuous-flow electrodeposition approach using carbon slurry as a flow electrode for the mass production of Cu nanocatalyst. By regulating the nucleation and particle growth process by pulsed electrochemistry, high-performance Cu nanocatalysts consisting of single atoms and tiny nanoparticles can be obtained at a productivity of 15 g per hour with a Cu loading of 2.5 wt %. Our strategy is also applicable to Ag and bimetallic CuAg systems. This effectively addresses the productivity bottleneck in the electrosynthesis of nanocatalysts and paves the way for their energy-related applications.

Results and Discussion

Regulation in Nucleation and Particle Growth

It is well-recognized that metal deposition relies on the applied potential in the century-old electrodeposition technology. Unfortunately, a critical challenge remains in particle size controllability as limited by thermodynamic constraints in the potential-dependent processes. The downsizing to single atoms using underpotential deposition (UPD) is only feasible for well-defined electrode surfaces and is kinetically sluggish owing to the small potential window.

Distinct from conventional methods, we adopted a time-dependent pulsed overpotential deposition (OPD) strategy to decouple the correlation between particle size and voltage. As illustrated in Figure A, the catalyst was prepared by periodic oxidation–reduction galvanostatic electrolysis, consisting of a short reductive deposition (∼5 s) to reduce the Cu2+ ions onto the carbon support, and a transient oxidative stripping (∼0.1 s) to dissolve the unwanted large Cu nuclei in every repeating cycle. Preliminary cyclic voltammogram (CV) measurements were performed to determine the suitable voltage window for operation in a slurry system. As shown in Figures B and S1, the reduction and oxidation peaks of Cu begin to appear at a cell voltage of −1.5 V and become prominent at −2.0 V. Further reducing to −2.5 and −3.0 V will lead to severe hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) that slows down the deposition of Cu. This agrees well with the corresponding XRD and ICP results (Figure S2 and Table S1), where nanocrystalline Cu (2.0 wt %) appears in the sample of −2.0 V after periodic galvanostatic electrolysis. Deviation from the optimal voltage will lead to almost no Cu deposition (at −1.5 V) or formation of large crystallites (−2.5 V or below). The regulation of nucleation and particle growth by pulsed electrochemistry was further confirmed by Cu stripping in positive CV scans after depositing at −2.0 V for different periods. The Cu nucleation in the slurry electrolyte is evident by the tiny oxidation peak for the initial 0.1–1 s in Figure C. Extending the deposition time to 15 s will result in a more prominent oxidation peak at higher voltages due to the co-occurrence of nucleation and particle growth. Finally, the inevitable aggregation of Cu species occurs when the deposition time increases to 30 or 60 s, leading to two sets of split stripping peaks for bulk crystals and subnanometric species. Such particle size regulations from Cu nuclei to nanoparticles and bulk crystals are verified by the XRD patterns in Figure D, where a semiquantitative analysis of the particle size by the Scherrer equation is also given in Table S2. Likewise, the optimal stripping conditions on the particle size (i.e., 1/50 of the deposition time) were screened by CV and XRD measurements in Figures S2–S4.

A series of Cu nanocatalysts with Cu loading of 0.7–2.5 wt % were then prepared in optimal conditions via periodic galvanostatic electrolysis at a productivity of 3 g per hour in a 16 cm2 flow electrolyzer for further characterization. We should point out that the particle size controllability is correlated with the maximum availability of binding sites on the substrate. With increasing metal contents, the substrate could be saturated and cannot accommodate extra metal atoms with atomic dispersion. Control samples were synthesized via continuous UPD and OPD processes with much worse size controllability and Cu loading. The substrate effect was carefully studied using pure CB, oxidized CB (using nitric acid), and our nitrogen-rich CB/PANI, showing the necessity of N-functional groups in stabilizing single atoms and minimizing the particle size on pulsed deposition. Detailed XRD and HRTEM results are provided in Figures S5–S7 and Tables S3–S5.

In situ X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) measurements were further conducted to understand the nucleation and growth kinetics in the pulsed deposition. As shown in Figures E and S8, successful Cu deposition was observed by an increase in the absorption edge upon pulsed deposition for 20 min. Extending the deposition time will not lead to significant changes in the Cu K-edge spectra due to a delicate balance between deposition and stripping. This is distinct from the overgrowth of Cu in continuous deposition, where a thick Cu layer will be formed within a couple of scans. The real-time mass changes on the electrode surface during dynamic electrochemical processes were then measured by in situ electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM) in Figures F and S9A. The growth rate of pulsed deposition was roughly 3.5 times slower than that in continuous mode (0.2 vs 0.7 μg cm–2 s–1) due to transient dissolution of the deposited Cu upon stripping. Multicycle CV scans were conducted to verify the process reversibility, where the deposited Cu during both pulsed and continuous deposition can be removed entirely by oxidative stripping at a higher voltage of +0.6 V. The deposition duration in a single pulsed cycle has a prominent influence on the deposition rate. As shown in Figure G, the mass change rate gradually decreased from 0.31 to 0.12 μg cm–2 s–1 with decreasing deposition time from 15 to 1 s in one cycle, indicating the regulation of nucleation and particle growth by pulsed electrochemistry. Given the weight of a Cu monolayer (∼0.12 μg cm–2) on Au (111) by UPD, this corresponds to a deposition rate of one to three monolayers (by weight) per second on the substrate surface. We should note that the actual deposition would probably adopt a 3D island growth mode rather than layer-by-layer epitaxial growth. This is also reflected in the reduction of particle size from bulk crystals to nanoparticles via pulsed deposition for those deposited Cu on the EQCM electrode in the XRD patterns in Figure S9B.

Characterization of the Synthesized Nanocatalysts

To determine the chemical state and local coordination environment of subnanometric Cu species, X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) measurements were conducted. The Cu K-edge XANES spectra of the 0.7 and 2.5 wt % Cu-CB/PANIs with the referencing samples of Cu foil, Cu2O, CuO, and CuCl2 are presented in Figure A. The absorption edge of all catalysts is located between Cu foil and Cu2O, indicating an average valence state of Cu between 0 and +1. As shown in Figure B, the Fourier-transformed (FT) EXAFS spectra in the R-space of the two catalysts exhibit two prominent peaks at around 1.5 and 2.2 Å from the Cu–N and Cu–Cu coordination, confirming the coexistence of Cu single atoms and nanoparticles in both samples. Notably, the intensity ratio of the Cu–Cu peak to the Cu–N peak gradually increases at higher Cu loadings due to prolonged deposition time. The EXAFS wavelet transforms (WT) at the Cu K-edge were performed to further investigate the coordination characteristics of the 0.7 and 2.5 wt % Cu-CB/PANIs. As shown in Figure C, the intensity maxima at ∼4.8 Å–1 in the 0.7 wt % sample are ascribed to the Cu–N path in the Cu K-edge spectra, whereas the 2.5 wt % Cu-CB/PANI displays an intensity maxima at 7.2 Å–1 due to the Cu–Cu path. This is supported by the qualitative EXAFS fitting in Table S6.

The coexistence of Cu single atoms and nanoparticles was visualized by aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) in Figure D. Isolated bright dots and clusters of several atoms can be well-recognized in the 2.5 wt % Cu-CB/PANI sample. Further analysis in Figure E suggests a typical Cu (111) facet for the 1.2 nm-sized nanocrystal, in accordance with the literature using conventional electrodeposition. , Meanwhile, the corresponding line profile in Figure F and the inter-Cu distance distribution in Figure S10 also confirm that these individual bright dots are Cu single atoms rather than Cu dimers or trimers. The atomic distribution of single atoms to nanoparticles is estimated to be 3:2 according to postimage analysis in Figure S11, suggesting the majority of Cu exists as single atoms in our sample. Cu catalyst with only single-atom sites could be obtained despite a lower Cu loading of 0.7 wt % (Figure S12). The energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mappings also reveal a uniform distribution of C, N, and Cu elements across our samples in Figure G. Apart from the EXAFS and STEM analysis, we have also conducted a comprehensive material characterization using XRD, ICP, XPS, and HRTEM in Figures S13 and S14, and Table S2.

Likewise, monometallic Ag and bimetallic CuAg nanocatalysts were produced using our strategy for broader applicability. As shown in Figure S15, similar nucleation and growth kinetics were observed for Ag in the stripping curves, indicating the particle size can be regulated from nuclei to nanoparticles and bulk crystals by pulsed deposition, as confirmed by the XRD patterns. The HAADF-STEM image also validates the coexistence of Ag single atoms on the carbon substrate. Bimetallic CuAg nanocatalysts with various metal contents and particle sizes (from single atoms to nanoclusters) were synthesized via codeposition in the HAADF-STEM image. This is confirmed by the uniform distribution of two elements across the sample in the EDS mapping, as well as the presence of a mixed phase in the XRD pattern in Figure H–J. ICP and XPS measurements were further performed to confirm the metal loading and oxidation state of as-prepared CuAg-CB/PANIs in Figure S16 and Table S7. Beyond these, Pt nanocatalyst with a particle size of 5.0 ± 1.3 nm could be successfully synthesized in Figures S17 and S18, demonstrating the universal applicability of our approach.

Formation of the Electro-Percolation Network

As the working electrode comprises solid particles suspended in the slurry electrolyte rather than a static solid matrix, the ability to form a conductive percolation network is the key to successful Cu deposition. The complex transfer paths involving the interparticle, electrode-particle, and electrolyte-particle electron transfers are presented in Figure A. In particular, the dynamic movement of particles and resulting charge redistribution in the slurry are crucial for effective electron transfer in the slurry electrolysis. , Using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) calculations to simulate the charging process in the half-cell, we have identified the decisive role of solid content in the formation of the electro-percolation network, mainly focusing on the interparticle charge transfer when reaching the percolation threshold. Figure B visualizes the dynamic charge distribution of the particles at a cell voltage of −2.0 V for slurries with 5, 15, and 30 wt % of solid contents. The complete charging behavior over time for each case is provided in Figure S19 and Videos 1–3. At a low solid content of 5 wt %, the electrode-particle charge transfer dominates due to insufficient solid particles to form the percolation network. Only particles near the current collector are charged, while most particles in the slurry are not involved in either charge transfer or metal deposition. This results in a very low Cu utilization efficiency in the electrolyte and unsatisfactory particle growth into large crystallites. The interparticle charge transfer occurs when reaching a percolation threshold of 15 wt %, leading to evenly distributed charged particles between the collector and the membrane. Further increasing the solid content to 30 wt % will result in the dominance of interparticle transfer in a semisolid electrolyte and violent collision of charged particles during dynamic movement. Unfortunately, this will sacrifice the fluid mobility and stability, as shown in Figure S20, where a compromise between the conductivity (percolation network) and fluid properties has to be made in the slurry system.

The formation of the electro-percolation network can be reflected by the cell current change during electrodeposition in Figure C. A sudden increase in the deposition current could be observed when the solid content of the slurry is increased from 5 to 10 to 15 wt %. This indicates reduced solution resistance due to more efficient electron transfer via interparticle interaction. After exceeding the percolation threshold, the percolation network continues to develop in the whole region, leading to a slight increase in the current for the 20 wt % slurry. Likewise, rheological measurements of the fluid properties in Figure D also provide direct evidence of the percolation network. An apparent shear thinning phenomenon, together with a relatively low viscosity of the fluid, could be observed at low solid contents of 5–10 wt % owing to weak interparticle interaction. This is distinct from the case in the 15 wt % slurry, where shear thinning weakens and the static viscosity at 100 s–1 increases by ∼20 times. The linear correlation between static viscosity (100 s–1) and solid content also indicates a stronger bonding in the network due to percolation in Figure S21. Dynamic strain sweeping at a frequency of 1 Hz was performed to obtain the storage (G′) and loss (G″) modulus of the slurry in Figure E. The slurry behaves as a gel under a small strain of 0.1%, where the storage modulus (G′) is greater than the loss modulus (G″). The sol–gel transition, which implies the rupture of metastable hydrogel networks, appears with increasing dynamic strains. Higher solid contents will postpone the sol–gel transition and generate a more stable network in the slurry (roughly 4.5 times greater in G′ at 10% strain for 15 wt % slurry than 5 wt %).

To further understand the formation mechanism of the electro-percolation network during Cu deposition, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out to quantify the charge transfer contributions from the electrode, electrolyte, and particles using a modified Randles fitting model. As shown in Figures F,G and S22, and Tables S8 and S9, the porosity resistance of the electrode (R p ) increases with higher solid contents, regardless of the applied potential. This is attributed to a thickened electric double layer (EDL) caused by the formation of a conductive network. In contrast, the charge transfer resistance (R CT) is an indicator of reaction kinetics and is strongly potential-dependent. The R CT of the solid-free electrolyte (i.e., 0 wt % slurry) significantly decreases from 151.0 Ω at open-circuit voltage (OCV) to 2.1 Ω at −1.5 V due to the occurrence of Cu deposition. This agrees well with the CV and XRD observations in Figures B and S2. With increasing solid contents from 0 to 20 wt %, the R CT values at OCV dramatically reduced by a factor of 10, proving the formation of an electro-percolation network that facilitates the charge transfer via interparticle interaction. The R CT value of the 20 wt % slurry at −1.5 V further reduces to 1.2 ohm, showcasing its promotion effect in electrodeposition. Benefiting from the effective charge transfer in the percolation network, the utilization efficiency of Cu in the electrolyte can be significantly increased from less than 10% in the 5 wt % slurry to 55% in the 15 wt % slurry in Figure F, while also preventing the unsatisfactory growth of large crystallites due to uneven charge distribution. This enables the rapid production of 2.5 wt % Cu nanocatalysts containing tiny nanoparticles and single atoms for high-performance ammonia electrosynthesis.

Catalytic Performance toward NO3RR and CO2RR

We have chosen the electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR) as a model reaction to evaluate the catalytic performance of the as-prepared Cu-CB/PANI catalysts. The NO3RR activity was first evaluated by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in an electrolyte containing 1.0 M KOH and 0.1 M KNO3 in a customized 3-electrode H-cell, showing a positive shift in the onset potential for more than 450 mV in 0.1 M KNO3 than nitrate-free electrolyte for the 2.0 wt % Cu-CB/PANI in Figures A and S23. For comparison, the commercial CuO catalyst was also examined at the same Cu loading, showing a much inferior performance compared to the Cu-CB/PANIs.

The productivities toward ammonium and nitrite were then verified by chronoamperometry (CA) measurements from −0.1 to −0.7 V vs RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode) in 1 M KOH and 0.1 M KNO3. The well-established indophenol blue and diazotization colorimetric methods were used to quantify the produced ammonium and nitrite in the electrolytes for the determination of Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) and yield rates (Figures S24–S26). A volcano shape is observed in the FEs toward ammonium for 2.0 wt % Cu-CB/PANI in Figure B,C, giving a maximum FE of 95% and a corresponding ammonium yield rate of 0.23 mmol cm–2 h–1 at −0.5 V vs RHE. Further applying more negative potentials will lead to slightly decreased FEs and ammonium yield rates due to the competitive HER reaction. Meanwhile, the FEs toward nitrite gradually decrease from 35% at −0.1 V to <10% at −0.3 V and maintain very low levels for more negative potentials. This suggests that the NO3RR reaction follows a well-documented NO3 – → NO2 – → NH4 + pathway over the Cu-CB/PANI catalysts. To confirm the reliability of NO3RR performance, we have also quantified the amount of ammonium by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra in Figure S27. All the results are compatible with those from the indophenol blue method. Isotope labeling experiments using K15NO3 as the reactant were also conducted in Figure D to demonstrate that the origin of ammonium is from the nitrate rather than impurities in the electrolyte and air. After electroreduction at −0.4 V vs RHE, the 1H NMR spectrum of the electrolyte containing K15NO3 indicates typical double peaks of 15NH4 + (7.07 and 6.95 ppm) without the presence of the triplet from 14NH4 + (7.11, 7.02, and 6.93 ppm).

The influence of the synergistic effect on ammonium production was further examined at the optimal condition (−0.5 V vs RHE) for the Cu-CB/PANI catalysts. As shown in Figure E, a volcano-type relationship between the Cu loading and NO3RR performance is observed, where the 2.0 wt % Cu-CB/PANI exhibits the highest FE and yield rate toward ammonium among all the samples. The normalized mass activity indicates the synergistic effect between single atoms and nanoparticles has a more profound influence on the NO3RR performance than the metal loading, where the 2.0 wt % sample outperforms the 0.7 and 2.5 wt % variants due to the optimal proportion of atomic and metallic Cu species (5.2 versus 1.8 and 0.6 mmolammonium gcat –1 h–1, respectively). The turnover frequency (TOF) is an important index to evaluate the intrinsic activity as defined by the number of reactants evolved on each catalytic site per unit of time. 46,47 Benefiting from the synergistic effect, the TOF value of 2.0 wt % Cu-CB/PANI is calculated to be 335 h–1, significantly higher than that of other Cu-CB/PANIs and the reported Cu-based catalysts in Figures F and S28. Apart from standard 3-electrode measurements, we have also assembled a 16 cm2 flow electrolyzer to verify the feasibility of our catalyst on practical NO3RR application. As shown in Figures S29–S31, the flow electrolyzer equipped with 2.0 wt % Cu-CB/PANI catalyst (18 mg cm–2) exhibits a superior ammonium yield rate of 0.3 mmol cm–1 h–1 and a FE of 91% at a full cell voltage of −2.4 V. The flow electrolyzer affords a steady performance with negligible degradation in current density and ammonium yield rate after a continuous operation of 30 h at a full cell voltage of −2.4 V.

The electrochemical CO2 reduction (CO2RR) performance of our catalysts were further assessed using a gas diffusion electrode-based flow cell in a 1.0 M KOH electrolyte. The optimal CuAg-CB/PANI (1.0 wt % Cu and 0.9 wt % Ag) demonstrates a larger cathodic response current in linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) curves compared with other mono- and bimetallic variants in Figure G, suggesting a more efficient CO2 activation owing to the synergetic effect of Cu and Ag. The FEs toward various gaseous and liquid products at different potentials using CuAg-CB/PANI (1.0 wt % Cu and 0.9 wt % Ag) as the catalyst are determined by gas chromatography (GC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) spectroscopy in Figures H, S32, and S33. The current density gradually increases in the tested range of −0.6 to −0.85 V vs RHE, with a product evolution from CO to CH4. Figure I shows the catalyst comparison at −0.85 V vs RHE. As expected, HER reaction dominates in metal-free and monometallic Ag-CB/PANI over a wide potential window. This can be ascribed to the weaker binding energy between Ag and the *CO intermediate that is responsible for the production of CO. In the case of Cu-CB/PANI, more CO and hydrocarbon products, such as CH4 and C2H4 could be formed due to the moderate binding energy between Cu and *CO intermediate. The selectivity toward CH4 further increases from 20% in the Cu sample to 37% in bimetallic CuAg-CB/PANI. This is accompanied by an increased C2 product selectivity from 25 to 28%, suggesting the promising role of Ag in serving as a CO generator for subsequent hydrogenation. Compared to the reported Cu-based SACs, our CuAg-CB/PANI catalysts show a high FECH4 even at a low overpotential of −0.85 V vs RHE in Figure S34 and Table S8.

Techno-Economic Analysis of the Catalyst Production

To assess the economic feasibility of the continuous-flow electrosynthesis of nanocatalysts, we have calculated the greenhouse gas emission (GHG) and production cost of our protocol compared to the traditional calcination process. As shown in Figure A and Table S11, traditional manufacturing of 10 wt % Cu/C catalysts at high temperatures will generate ∼22.5 kg GHG per kg catalyst produced, where more than half of the emissions are attributed to the decomposition of metal precursors and substrate, as well as the balance arising from fossil fuel-powered temperature control units. Our electrosynthesis protocol is much more sustainable, giving an extremely low GHG emission of ∼0.03 kg per kg catalyst, which is 750 times lower than the traditional process. The GHG emission can be further reduced by coupling with green electricity production by solar cells or wind farms, eventually reaching net zero for the future catalyst industry. This is also reflected in a much lower production cost for our protocol than traditional methods (16.4 versus 45.1 USD per kg catalyst) in Figure C. Key influencers, such as the reactor life, Cu loading of the catalyst, metal utilization efficiency, and electricity rate, are considered for cost evaluation under more universal scenarios. As shown in Figure D and Tables S11–S13, the calculation shows a much lower production cost with longer reactor lifespans owing to lower capital and installation (CapEX) costs. A 20-year reactor life is adopted according to the literature. Similarly, the production cost gradually increases with higher metal loadings in the catalyst due to the changes in chemical expenditures. The Cu utilization efficiency has almost no influence on the total cost due to the ease of recovery of unreacted Cu precursor from the slurry by filtration or sedimentation. We should point out that the cost evaluation of our catalyst would be slightly different from the one using CatCost (from the US Department of Energy, DOE) due to its inability to evaluate electrochemical processes. The influence of electricity price on the production cost also remains modest due to a much lower number of electrons required for catalyst production than other electrochemical processes (CO2RR, etc.). This indicates the excellent profitability of our electrosynthesis protocol. As proof of demonstration, we have also verified the scale-up production in a 100 cm2 flow electrolyzer, giving 15 g of Cu nanocatalyst in 1 h without significant performance deviation from the optimal condition in Figure S35.

Conclusions

We have developed a slurry electrolysis protocol for catalyst production in a sustainable and cost-effective manner. By employing pulsed overpotential deposition in a slurry electrolyte, the nucleation and growth process of Cu on the carbon support can be precisely regulated at the atomic level, producing highly active Cu nanocatalysts containing single atoms and tiny nanoparticles. Our protocol can be further extended to Ag and bimetallic CuAg nanocatalysts. This is evidenced by in situ XAS and EQCM measurements on the pulsed deposition. Distinct from conventional electrolysis, the slurry system relies on the formation of the electro-percolation network at high solid contents for effective charge transfer via interparticle interaction. The resulting Cu and CuAg nanocatalysts exhibit superior NO3RR and CO2RR activities. Further techno-economic analysis confirms the profitability and sustainability of our protocol compared to traditional calcination methods. This work opens a new avenue for electrifying the catalyst manufacturing industry toward net-zero emissions.

Experimental Section

Slurry-Based Flow Electrolyzer Setup

The Cu-CB/PANI catalysts were prepared via pulsed overpotential deposition in a 16 cm2 two-electrode flow electrolyzer separated by a Nafion-117 membrane at room temperature using a Correst CS310X workstation with a 10 A current booster (CS2020B). The flow electrolyzer was assembled with two stainless-steel cover plates, two gold-coated copper plates as the current collector, and two monopolar graphite plates with parallel paths for electrolyte distribution. A dimensionally stable anode (RuIr/TiO2 on Ti-mesh, 4 × 4 cm2) and the slurry electrode containing various solid contents (5–20 wt %) of CB/PANI were used for a two-electrode system in an aqueous catholyte containing 1.0 M H2SO4 and 0.1 M CuSO4 (or 0.1 M CuSO4 and 0.05 M AgNO3 for the CuAg sample), and an anolyte of 1.0 M H2SO4. The flow rate (10 mL min–1) was controlled using two peristaltic pumps (DIPump 550), and the actual flow rate was determined using a measuring cylinder.

Electrosynthesis of the Cu Nanocatalysts

The electrodeposition process was carried out using a pulsed overpotential deposition method, in which the deposition voltage and stripping voltage were set according to the CV patterns (See Supporting Information). The total synthesis period was set as 1.5 h, where the deposition time in a single cycle was set as 0.1, 1, 5, 10, or 15 s, and the corresponding stripping time was set to 1/50 of the deposition time. As-obtained samples were washed with deionized water to remove unreacted Cu precursor. Chronoamperometry (CA) was also conducted at a constant cell voltage with various deposition times (0.1, 1, 5, 10, 15, 30, and 60 s). This was followed by a CV scan from 0 to +2.0 V to verify the metal dissolution after deposition. Likewise, the bimetallic CuAg sample was synthesized following a similar procedure.
