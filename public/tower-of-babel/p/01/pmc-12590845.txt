Prolonged dysregulation of gene expression and biomarkers of coagulation, fibrinolysis, and endothelium in pediatric central nervous system malaria
Authors: Arlene E. Dent, Bruce A. Rosa, Katherine R. Dobbs, Sidney Ogolla, David Midem, Paula Embury, Yelenna Skomorovska-Prokvolit, Emily R. Hannon, Emmily Koech, Alvin H. Schmaier, Walter Otieno, Makedonka Mitreva, James W. Kazura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren with central nervous system malaria (CNS-M) have gene expression profiles and plasma biomarkers of malaria pathogenesis that differ from children with uncomplicated malaria (UM, malaria infection with fever and no systemic organ dysfunction) or acute febrile non-malaria illness (AFN). However, there is scant knowledge of the temporal dynamics of gene expression and plasma biomarkers during recovery from CNS-M.MethodsWe compared serial changes of peripheral blood mononuclear cell transcriptomes at 6 weeks after recovery and plasma biomarkers of malaria pathogenesis at 6 weeks and 6 months after recovery in cohorts of Kenyan children who presented with CNS-M (n = 37), UM (n = 20) and AFN (n = 12).ResultsUpregulated genes and Reactome pathways of interleukin/cytokine signaling, neutrophil degranulation and innate immunity and downregulated pathways of protein biosynthesis of children presenting with CNS-M were partially reversed at hospital discharge 2–6 days later and 6 weeks. CNS-M and UM transcriptomes before treatment versus 6 weeks after recovery shared several pathways, but megakaryocyte/platelet production, IL-4/IL-13/IL-10 signaling and overexpression of LAG3, CCR5, PDGFRB and ICMT were specific to CNS-M. The Hemostasis Reactome pathway was uniquely downregulated in CNS-M at 6 weeks. Biomarkers of coagulation, fibrinolysis, neutrophil activation, endothelium integrity and cytokine signaling correlated with high plasma cell-free DNA in acute CNS-M and persisted for 6 weeks, whereas these values normalized at 6 weeks in UM and AFN.ConclusionsRecovery from pediatric CNS-M is associated with prolonged dysregulation of gene expression and biomarkers associated with coagulation, fibrinolysis and platelet activation. Future studies are needed to evaluate whether this dysregulation is associated with neurocognitive sequelae following recovery.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11957-5.

Background

Children living in Plasmodium falciparum (Pf) endemic areas experience a spectrum of clinical manifestations when asexual blood stage infection develops that ranges from subclinical malaria (lack of fever and overt malaria symptoms) to UM and severe malaria [1–3]. Clinical diagnostic criteria of severe malaria include a Pf + blood smear or rapid diagnostic test and severe anemia (hemoglobin ≤ 5 g/dL), respiratory distress with metabolic acidosis and CNS-M manifest by complicated seizures or impaired consciousness [4]. Cerebral malaria is the most severe form of CNS-M as defined by a Blantyre Coma Score (BCS) < 3 [5]. In this study, we enrolled patients with BCS ranging from 0 to 4.

The 2024 WHO World Malaria Report estimated that the number of global deaths due to malaria was 597,000 in 2023. 95% of these deaths occurred in sub-Saharan Africa where CNS-M primarily affects children under 5. Parenteral artesunate is the recommended treatment of CNS-M and other severe malaria syndromes [6]. Nevertheless, 10 to 20% of children with severe malaria have been estimated to die despite appropriate treatment [7].

The underlying mechanisms of pediatric CNS-M are complex and incompletely understood. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggests that brain swelling is due to vasogenic edema [8], a process whereby plasma constituents cross blood-brain barrier endothelial tight junctions damaged by oxidants and inflammation [9]. In contrast, MRI of adults with cerebral malaria outside Africa has a stronger component of cytotoxic edema associated with brain hypoxia [10]. Dysregulated cerebral venous blood flow that presumably is a consequence of cytoadherence of Pf-infected red blood cells (iRBCs) to brain endothelium also contributes to brain swelling [11].

Pathologic examination of brains of fatal CNS-M cases has shown ring hemorrhages, intravascular thrombi with fibrin deposition and cytoadherence of iRBCs to vascular endothelium. These findings were accompanied by local brain infiltration of monocyte/macrophages, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils and platelets [12–14]. Heme released from iRBCs and uninfected RBCs is thought to be a key factor in neutrophil activation [15]. High parasite biomass estimated by Pf Histidine Rich Protein 2 (HRP2) ELISA and elevated plasma levels of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), micro-vesicles, histones and cell-free DNA (cfDNA) correlate with the severity of malaria illness and death [16–18]. Negatively charged plasma constituents such as cfDNA may also lead to activation of the blood coagulation system and vascular endothelium thrombosis propagated by activated neutrophils and platelets [19]. Infected RBCs isolated from cerebral malaria patients have a propensity to express on their surface Pf erythrocyte membrane protein-1 (PfEMP1) head structures that bind to and impair the cytoprotective function of endothelium protein C receptor and thrombomodulin on the luminal surface of brain endothelial cells [20].

In contrast to the present level of knowledge of how gene expression and biomarkers differ in children presenting with CNS-M relative to UM and AFN, there is less understanding of the molecular pathways associated with recovery. This gap in knowledge potentially has high clinical significance since neurologic and behavioral deficits occur in ~ 30% of children who recover from CNS-M and other severe malaria phenotypes [21–23]. A previous study compared sequential changes in plasma biomarkers of systemic inflammation and endothelial activation in children at the time of presentation and recovery from cerebral malaria, UM, AFN, and healthy uninfected children [24]. Levels of intracellular adhesion molecule-1, angiopoietin-2 and C-reactive protein (CRP) were higher in cerebral malaria than UM, AFN and healthy uninfected children at presentation and 7 days after treatment with antimalarial drugs. Twenty-eight days after recovery, plasma CRP, a biomarker of systemic inflammation, was higher in children with cerebral malaria relative to healthy controls. In addition, comparison of children with severe malaria treated with parenteral artemisinin derivatives to those treated with quinine, the previous standard of care, showed that the former was associated with reduced long-term neurologic deficits and lower CRP levels one month after recovery from acute illness [25].

In the present investigation, we conducted an unbiased genome-wide transcriptome analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from Kenyan children with CNS-M before initiation of artesunate treatment, at hospital discharge and clinical recovery 2 to 6 days later, and 6 weeks after recovery. Transcriptomes of these children were compared to those of children with UM or AFN at the time of presentation and 6 weeks after recovery. We also measured changes in plasma biomarkers of malaria pathogenesis at presentation and 6 weeks and 6 months after recovery.

Methods

Study participants and hospital facilities

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) Scientific and Ethical Review Unit and University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center Institutional Review Board. Patients were enrolled from 2017 to 2021 at the Chulaimbo Sub-District Hospital in Kisumu County, Kenya. Parental informed consent was obtained for all study participants.

The research diagnosis of cerebral malaria is defined by a BCS < 3 accompanied by fundoscopic examination that reveals the distinctive features of malaria retinopathy [4]. We use the term “central nervous system malaria” (CNS-M), not “cerebral malaria”, because hospital personnel responsible for malaria diagnosis and treatment did not have the capacity to perform indirect ophthalmoscopic examination to evaluate retinal pathology. Kenyan guidelines for CNS-M diagnosis include: (i) a clinical presentation of unconsciousness and convulsions like the BCS; (ii) Pf + blood smear or rapid diagnostic test; (iii) no other apparent cause of CNS dysfunction. Demographics, clinical laboratory data and plasma biomarkers for all study participants at all time points are presented in Additional file 1: Table S1. Children with clinical findings suggestive of meningitis, e.g. positive Kernig’s sign, were transferred to the tertiary level Kisumu County hospital where lumbar puncture and microscopic examination and culture of cerebrospinal fluid could be performed. These children were not enrolled in the study.

Venous blood for research purposes was obtained from 37 children with CNS-M at study entry (En), before initiation of intravenous artesunate treatment, and the day of hospital discharge (Dc). Criteria for Dc included BCS of 5, negative blood smear, and axillary temperature ≤ 37.5°C. A 3-day course of artemether-lumefantrine was provided to caregivers to administer to children with CNS-M after Dc. Additional venous blood samples were obtained at 6 weeks (6w) and 6 months (6mo) post-recovery. Neurologic examination of children recovered from CNS-M was not performed at 6w or 6mo follow-up unless the Chulaimbo hospital clinical officer observed recent or active febrile illness. Children with UM (n = 20) and AFN (n = 12) were recruited at the hospital’s outpatient clinic. Blood was collected at study En before outpatient treatment of UM patients with artemether-lumefantrine and AFN patients with antibiotics. Venous blood was collected from children with UM and AFN at 6w and 6mo post-recovery.

Clinical laboratory tests

Hematologic parameters measured included complete blood count, differential leukocyte count, hemoglobin, hematocrit and platelet count using an automated cell counting machine (Beckman Coulter). Blood glucose, peripheral capillary oxygen saturation and blood urea nitrogen were respectively measured by point-of-care glucometer, pulse oximeter and ELISA. Blood cultures from CNS-M patients were performed using BD BACTEC™ at the KEMRI Centre for Global Health Research.

Biological sample preparation

Heparin anti-coagulated venous blood was subjected to ficoll-hypaque density gradient centrifugation to separate PBMCs from whole blood within 2 h of blood sampling [26]. PBMCs were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen and shipped to Case Western Reserve University for further processing as previously described [27]. RNA was isolated from frozen PBMC samples for RNA-seq analysis by the Washington University in St. Louis Tissue Procurement Center. Details of RNA-seq methodology and statistics are provided in Additional file 2: Supplementary Methods.

Plasma was separated from whole blood as described for PBMCs. The Pf biomass was estimated by HRP2 ELISA (Celllabs Australia) and cfDNA was measured by QUBIT assay (Thermo Fisher) that included circulating DNA of both host and parasite origin. Plasma biomarkers were measured by multiplexed MagPix assays and plate-based ELISA. Statistical analysis, plasma dilutions, assay sensitivity and range are shown in Additional file 1: Table S2.

PBMC transcriptome profiles distinguished CNS-M from both UM and AFN at study entry

Transcriptional profiles of bulk PBMC were generated using RNA-seq based on an average of 79.5 million reads per sample. Cryopreserved PBMCs selected for RNA-seq were based on those with the highest cell number and viability after thawing of frozen aliquots. For time-series analysis, we first compared transcriptomes that described the temporal changes of 9 children in each of the 3 cohorts (Fig. 1, comparisons A-D). This takes advantage of the pairing of PBMC samples from the same individual to improve statistical resolution for DESeq2 analysis. We also compared transcriptomes across cohort phenotypes collected at the same time point (Fig. 1, comparisons E-J). RNA-seq sample read count processing statistics are provided in Additional file 1: Table S3 and read counts, gene expression data and differential expression statistics for all genes and all comparisons are provided in Additional file 1: Table S4.

The highest number of differentially expressed genes (FDR-adjusted P value ≤ 0.05) was found in CNS-M En vs. CNS-M 6w, followed by CNS-M En vs. CNS-M Dc. Fewer significant differences were found in children with UM En vs. UM 6w. No significant differences were observed in AFN En vs. AFN 6w (zero values, no alphabet letter for comparisons). We believe the latter finding is due to the heterogeneity of clinical diagnoses in the AFN cohort relative to the two malaria cohorts and the inconsistent and often small differences in transcriptional profiles between study En and follow-up for these individuals.

CIBERSORT and flow cytometry analysis of bulk PBMCs

Immune cell subset distributions estimated by CIBERSORT [28] indicated that the proportions of CD4 and CD8 cell subsets, B cell subsets, monocytes, mast cells, NK cells, neutrophils and dendritic cells were similar at study En and 6w for all 3 cohorts (Additional File 3: Fig. S1). CIBERSORT does not include transcripts that estimate megakaryocyte/platelet inclusion in PBMCs or whole blood. However, density gradient centrifugation used to isolate PBMCs includes ~ 3 platelets per nucleated PBMC [26]. We found that many genes of interest were expressed by platelets. With respect to the high expression of neutrophil genes described below, CIBERSORT estimated that only 1 to 11% neutrophils were included in PBMC preparations. We speculate that expression of neutrophil-specific genes such as MPO in CNS-M and UM may be due to RNA contained in circulating NETs that adhere to cells in the PBMC layer following density gradient centrifugation and the presence of low-density “inflammatory” granulocytes that co-localize with PBMCs [29]. Flow cytometry was performed after thawing of cryopreserved PBMCs and placement in Trizol for RNA extraction to evaluate basic cell type frequencies. Monoclonal antibodies to CD3 (clone UCHT1 Alexa Fluor 406 eBioscience), CD4 (clone RPA-T4 APC Biolegend), CD8 (clone RPA-T8 APC-Cy7 Biolegend), CD19 (clone SJ25C1 PE Biolegend) and CD14 (clone 61D3 PE-Cy7 Thermo Fisher) were used to collect data with an LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) analyzed by FlowJo software (Treestar Inc.) Live/Dead cells were determined by SYTOX Green (Thermo Fisher). The following progressive gating strategy was applied to all samples. (1) Time (2) Singles (doublet exclusion); FSC-H vs. FSC-A and SSC-H vs. SSC-A (3) Viability gating: Histogram Count vs. Sytox (Sytox (-) i.e. LIVE cells) (4) Population gating FSC/SSC; P1 gate was drawn to exclude debris (i.e. small with low FSC and SSC values) (5) Lymphocytes: From the Sytox (-) P1 gate FSC vs. CD3 (+) > CD4/CD8; Quad Gate (6) B cells: From the Sytox (-) P1 gate CD3 (-) CD19 (+); Quad Gate (7) Monocytes: From the Sytox (-) CD3 (-) CD14 (+): Quad Gate. There was a good correlation between CIBERSORT and flow cytometry for lymphocytes and monocytes. The correlation with CD4 T cells was relatively poor (Additional File 3: Fig. S2).

Reactome pathways changed in CNS-M at study entry, hospital discharge and 6 weeks post-recovery and differed relative to UM and AFN

Significantly enriched reactome pathways were identified among differentially expressed genes of interest from Fig. 1A-D. Additional file 1: Table S5 contains complete gene lists, significance values and enriched pathway enrichment for all comparisons based on the RNA-seq data analysis. Thirty-three pathways (Table 2A) were enriched among the 740 genes upregulated at CNS-M En vs. CNS-M Dc. These included: increased interleukin and cytokine signaling; neutrophil degranulation; activation of toll-like receptor pathways and MyD88-independent TLR activation; IFNG signaling; interleukin-10 signaling; and activation of coagulation that included the gene tissue factor F3 (P = 0.042). Among the pathways enriched among the genes underexpressed at CNS-M En vs. CNS-M Dc were several concerned with protein biosynthesis and amino acid metabolism that were enriched primarily due to the underexpression of 22 ribosomal protein genes. These results reflect an increase in gene expression at CNS-M En vs. CNS-M Dc, with lower relative expression of constitutively expressed ribosomal genes at CNS-M En. CNS-M gene sets shown in Table 2B were overexpressed at CNS-M Dc vs. CNS-M 6w (Fig. 1 comparison B). These included pathways of erythrocyte oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide release, hemostasis, heme scavenging, megakaryocyte RUNX1, megakaryocyte development, triggering of complement and platelet activation. The downregulation of genes in CNS-M Dc vs. CNS-M 6w did not result in significant pathway changes. However, the most significantly underexpressed genes included Fc fragment of IgE receptor Ia FCER1A, serpin family F member 1 SERPINF1, and C-type lectin domain family 4 member C CLEC4C. We next evaluated CNS-M En vs. CNS-M 6w (Table 2C), identifying 52 pathways significantly overexpressed at CNS-M En. Many of the same pathways were enriched among the genes overexpressed at CNS-M En vs. CNS-M Dc (neutrophil degranulation, interleukin and IL-10 signaling) as well as CNS-M Dc vs. CNS-M 6w (hemostasis, megakaryocyte development, platelet production and degranulation). With respect to underexpression of gene sets at CNS-M En vs. CNS-M 6w (Table 2C), reactome pathways included those related to protein translation and amino acid and selenoamino acid metabolism. This included 61 underexpressed ribosomal protein genes.

Eighteen pathways were significantly enriched among the genes overexpressed in UM En vs. UM 6w (Table 2D). While all 18 pathways overlapped with pathways in CNS-M En vs. CNS-M 6w, 156 genes were specific to CNS-M. Aside from multiple cell cycle, mitotic, DNA synthesis and cell senescence pathways, the latter included pathways related to platelet production and degranulation and IL-10 signaling. Within the pathways shared by CNS-M En and UM En, several genes were overexpressed exclusively in CNS-M. These included coagulation factor II thrombin receptor F2R, syndecan 4 SDC4 and syndecan 1 SDC1 in the hemostasis pathway, and cathepsins A and B CTSA and CTSB and myeloperoxidase MPO in the innate immune system pathway. Unlike the downregulation of pathways that included those relevant to protein biosynthesis at CNS-M at study En (Table 2C), only 18 pathways were downregulated at UM En. None were concerned with protein synthesis but are germane to impairment of immunoregulatory networks and adaptive immunity (Table 2D).

As an alternate approach, we identified pathway enrichment among genes differentially expressed in CNS-M vs. AFN but not in UM vs. AFN. Four genes were significantly overexpressed in CNS-M En vs. both UM En and AFN En (Fig. 1 comparison E) – lymphocyte activating 3 LAG3, C-C motif chemokine receptor 5 CCR5, platelet derived growth factor receptor beta PDGFRB and isoprenylcysteine carboxyl methyltransferase ICMT. With respect to differentially expressed genes at CNS-M En and UM En vs. AFN En (Fig. 1 Overlaps), the 392 genes that were significantly overexpressed in CNS-M En vs. AFN En but not in UM En vs. AFN En, were enriched for neutrophil degranulation, innate immune system, and immune system (Table 3; Fig. 1 comparison H). Among the genes contributing to these pathways were CD180, LAG3, IL21, IL10, IL6 and MPO. The 338 genes that were downregulated in CNS-M En vs. AFN En, but not in UM En vs. AFN En (Table 3; Fig. 1 comparison I), contributed to 7 enriched pathways, including 3 related to interferon signaling and one to cytokine signaling. No genes were differentially expressed in UM 6w vs. AFN 6w. However, 136 genes were downregulated in CNS-M 6w vs. AFN 6w. This gene set was enriched exclusively with genes in the Hemostasis pathway (Fig. 2). Differences in expression of genes contributing to this pathway included platelet factor 4 variant 1 PF4V1, thrombomodulin (TM), THBD, and selectin P SELP. These data infer that hemostatic dysfunction in CNS-M persists for at least 6w.

Validation of transcriptomic results at the protein biomarker level

We selected a panel of biomarkers that reflects biological processes relevant to coagulation/fibrinolysis, neutrophil activation, endothelium homeostasis, and cytokine signaling. We first evaluated changes in biomarker levels in CNS-M children at hospital En vs. Dc. Significant changes were observed in all biomarkers indicative of coagulation/fibrinolysis (Fig. 3a), 2 markers of neutrophil activation (Fig. 3b), all but 1 endothelium marker (Fig. 3c) and all 7 cytokines/chemokines (Fig. 3d). Subsequent changes of biomarker levels for CNS-M Dc vs. 6w included increased UPA and PAI-1, reduced MPO, increased Ang-1 and Synd-4 and decreased Tie-2, VEGFR1, E-selectin, and Synd-1, and decreased levels of all cytokines/chemokines except IL-6. Detailed statistical outputs for all tests are provided in (Additional file 1: Table S6).

Biomarkers Ang-2, PAI-1, VEGF, IFN-γ, TNF, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and IL-27 were measured at 6mo (Additional file 1: Table S1). Paired comparisons of 6w vs. 6mo values for the CNS-M cohort showed no significant differences. Biomarker levels in the 3 cohorts at 6mo were similar (unpaired t-test, two-tailed) with the exceptions of TNF and IL-8.

Parasite biomass in CNS-M correlated with biomarkers at hospital entry and discharge

We determined whether biomarker levels at CNS-M hospital En and Dc correlated with HRP2 (Additional file 1: Table S7). With respect to coagulation/fibrinolysis, HRP2 correlated positively with TM at CNS_M En but not Dc. HRP2 at study En correlated with both ELA and MPO. The continuing positive correlation at hospital Dc may be indicative of release of MPO from myeloid precursor cells [30] and/or the presence of low-density pro-inflammatory granulocytes in peripheral blood [29]. The positive correlation of HRP2 with Ang-2, Tie-2 and VEGFR1 at study En is consistent with the requirement of a high Pf biomass to induce endothelial activation that diminishes in parallel with artesunate mediated reduction of the parasite biomass. Elevated circulating Synd-1 biomarkers of the endothelial glycocalyx (Fig. 4c) suggests that shedding of proteoglycan constituents occurs in both CNS-M and UM at presentation [31, 32].

Biomarker changes in CNS-M differed from those in UM and AFN at study entry and 6 weeks

Eight biomarkers were significantly higher in CNS-M En vs. UM En, and all but 6 were higher in CNS-M En than AFN En (Fig. 4a-c). Six-week recovery values highlighted the persistent elevation of PAI-1, cfDNA and IL-21 in CNS-M relative to both UM and AFN. In contrast, VEGF, an angiogenic growth factor, was lower at CNS-M than UM 6w and AFN 6w. Collectively, these data are evidence of temporally persistent dysregulation of coagulation/fibrinolysis, blood vessel repair, and inflammatory and regulatory interleukins 6w after recovery from CNS-M but not UM or AFN. Median values and paired comparisons of biomarkers at En vs. 6w for individual study participants within each cohort are described in Additional file 1: Table S6. Significant changes between En and 6w values of biomarkers of coagulation/fibrinolysis, neutrophil activation, endothelium markers and cytokines/chemokines were observed for CNS-M and UM, except for no changes in PAI-1, cfDNA, and Cit-H3 in CNS-M. Fewer biomarkers were different in AFN En vs. AFN 6w.

A previous study reported that plasma cfDNA levels were higher in children with cerebral malaria than those with UM and predictive of increased risk of death [33]. We therefore quantified plasma cfDNA and correlated its level with biomarkers at study En and 6w. DNA levels were higher at CNS-M En than UM En and AFN En. Unlike UM 6w values that decreased relative to En values, CNS-M cfDNA showed no significant change at 6w. We therefore determined Spearman’s correlations of cfDNA with biomarkers at study En and 6w in CNS-M and compared the results to those in UM and AFN. Ten biomarkers (in addition to HRP2) correlated with cfDNA in CNS-M En patients (Additional File 1: Table S8). The strongest positive correlations (r > 0.500) included Cit-H3, VEGFR1, and Synd-1. At CNS-M 6w, cfDNA correlated positively with UPA and negatively with TFPI and IL-21. In contrast, in UM En, only four biomarkers (in addition to HRP2) correlated positively with cfDNA. UM 6w cfDNA correlated positively with Synd-1 and negatively with UPA, VWF and IL-6, a pattern distinct from CNS-M 6w. With respect to AFN, only Tie-2 and TNF correlated with cfDNA at study En, and none with AFN 6w. In sum, these data indicate that elevated cfDNA in CNS-M before and 6w after treatment correlated with a variety of malaria pathogenesis biomarkers. Fewer and different biomarker correlations with cfDNA were observed at study En and 6w in children with UM or AFN.

Protein biomarkers correlated with gene expression in children with CNS-M

We examined gene expression of biomarkers shown in Figs. 3 and 4 using PBMCs isolated from 9 CNS-M, 9 μm and 9 AFN patients at study En, Dc (CNS-M only) and 6w. No gene expression changes over time in CNS-M En were seen in the coagulation/fibrinolysis category, whereas there were parallel changes of gene expression and biomarkers of neutrophil activation, endothelium markers and cytokines (Additional File 3: Fig. S3). Principal component analysis suggests that, like the plasma biomarkers, overall gene expression profile changes in CNS-M ameliorated over time with greater heterogeneity in both UM and AFN (Additional File 3: Fig. S4). Relative to children with UM and AFN at En and 6w, children with CNS-M had significantly different levels of genes related to coagulation/fibrinolysis, neutrophil activation, and IL-10, IL-6 and IL-21 production (Additional File 3: Fig. S5).

Discussion

Knowledge of the molecular pathways underlying pediatric CNS-M pathogenesis has in large measure been based on observational studies that compared children with CNS-M to those with UM, AFN and healthy children living in the same community. Herein, we made similar comparisons of Kenyan children with CNS-M, UM and AFN and performed RNA sequencing of PBMCs to track the dynamic changes in gene expression and gene sets in reactome pathways at presentation and 6w after recovery. In parallel, we measured levels of plasma biomarkers related to gene expression changes revealed by RNA-seq. Reactome pathways and circulating biomarkers associated with dysregulated coagulation and fibrinolysis, neutrophil activation, endothelium activation and inflammatory and regulatory cytokines were more pronounced and temporally prolonged in CNS-M relative to both UM and AFN.

Unlike UM, in which plasma cfDNA levels significantly decreased at 6w post-recovery, cfDNA levels in CNS-M were higher at presentation and did not decrease at 6w. Collectively, these data suggest that a thrombotic propensity persists for at least 6w following recovery from CNS-M, but not UM and AFN, that may in part be driven by elevated cfDNA. Mechanistic links between inflammation, innate immunity and thrombosis have been described in pediatric cerebral malaria [34], other severe infections such as bacterial sepsis and COVID-19, autoimmune diseases such as lupus, and inflammatory bowel disease [35–37].

Notably, when CNS-M En vs. 6w and UM En vs. 6w pathways were compared, the results distinguished CNS-M from UM with respect to underexpression of protein biosynthesis and overexpression of pathways of innate immunity, cytokine signaling, platelet degranulation, and VEGF-VEGFR1 interaction. In addition, increased expression of four genes - LAG3, CCR5, PDGFRB, and ICMT - was unique to CNS-M En. CCR5 contributes to the pathogenesis of experimental cerebral malaria in the C57BL/6 mouse - P. berghei ANKA infection model [38, 39], and PDGFRB expression was reported to be higher in whole blood of children with retinopathy-negative than retinopathy-positive cerebral malaria [40]. Cross-cohort comparisons of gene set expression of CNS-M, UM and AFN cohorts at 6w indicated that underexpression of the Hemostasis reactome pathway was exclusive to CNS-M. Functional studies of zymogens and enzymes that regulate hemostasis and fibrinolysis are needed to confirm the in vivo significance of these observations. This was not possible in the current study since heparin, not acid citrate dextrose, was used to prevent coagulation of whole blood for subsequent isolation of PBMCs.

Other investigators have analyzed the whole blood transcriptome in acute CNS-M by comparison to other severe malaria syndromes or healthy children living in the same community. Lee et al. [41] performed dual RNA-sequencing on whole blood for Pf and human genes in Gambian children with cerebral malaria, cerebral malaria with hyperlactemia and hyperlactemia alone, and evaluated differential gene expression relative to that of children with UM. 770 human genes were differentially expressed in the combined cerebral malaria and hyperlactatemia groups compared to UM. Upregulated genes in these children included those encoding neutrophil granule proteins and gene ontology analysis showed enrichment of co-translational protein targeting, cell motility, and immune response functions. Using microarray gene chips, Nallandhighal et al. [42]. compared the whole blood transcriptomes of Ugandan children with cerebral malaria or severe malaria anemia and evaluated differential gene expression relative to healthy children. 603 and 210 genes respectively were differentially expressed in children with cerebral malaria and severe malaria anemia relative to healthy children. Blood transcriptional modules enriched in cerebral malaria included upregulation of mitochondria and neutrophil genes. Data from these and other whole blood microarray studies of cerebral malaria overlap, in part, with results of RNA sequencing of PBMC described here.

A recent study reported the impact of TNF and Pf iRBC lysates on in vitro human brain microvascular endothelial cell monolayer integrity that simulates breakdown of the blood-brain barrier. RNA-seq data from this study [43] (GSE211439) showed upregulation of many of same genes we identified to be differentially expressed by PBMC of children with CNS-M, e.g. ICAM1, VEGFA, F3, FLT1, IL6, SDC4, HIF1A, IL18R1. Future transcriptomic studies of pediatric CNS-M should consider performing single-cell RNA sequencing that allows for more in-depth knowledge of the heterogeneity of gene expression of low frequency peripheral blood cells such as innate immune lymphoid cells, platelets, and neutrophils. This will be challenging in the case of neutrophils and platelets since these blood constituents must be processed rapidly to limit ex vivo activation [44].

Pathogenesis biomarkers used in our study were similar to those previously reported to indicate coagulation and fibrinolysis, neutrophil activation and NET formation, vascular endothelium dysfunction, and cytokine and chemokine responses in pediatric severe malaria [45–47]. Biomarker levels in CNS-M En before artesunate treatment compared to 2 to 6 days later at hospital Dc showed that dysregulated pathways were only partially reversed. Biomarker levels in CNS-M at hospital Dc continued to change relative to 6w values. A similar analysis of data from UM and AFN children showed that 6w biomarker levels were not significantly different from each other, but that both differed from CNS-M at 6w. Six-month biomarker levels were similar in the three cohorts. What drives this persistent dysregulation of biomarkers and gene expression in CNS-M but not UM? One possibility is that elevated plasma cfDNA and other polyanions activated coagulation and impaired fibrinolysis and endothelial homeostasis. Plasma levels of cfDNA in CNS-M have previously been reported to be higher in CNS-M than UM [33]. In this study, we also found that levels in CNS-M patients were higher than in UM patients at study En. However, unlike UM, cfDNA levels did not decrease at 6w in CNS-M. Thus, the positive correlation with cfDNA at 6w suggests that fibrin deposition is ongoing. In addition, the negative correlation with TFPI, an enzyme that functions to inhibit TF activation of the extrinsic pathway of coagulation [48], and positive correlation with PAI-1, which inhibits plasmin-mediated fibrinolysis [49], is consistent with a thrombotic propensity in CNS-M that persists for at least 6w. Future studies are required to identify the host and parasite sources of plasma cfDNA before and after recovery from CNS-M and perform functional studies of coagulation and hemostasis [50]. If cfDNA is found to mediate functional activation of coagulation and impair fibrinolysis, pharmacologic approaches used to mitigate inflammation in COVID-19 and immune thrombotic diseases such as lupus erythematosus might be considered as adjuvant treatments for CNS-M [36, 51, 52]. Adjunct treatments to parenteral artesunate that target circulating cfDNA and other charged plasma constituents [53, 54] also might be considered for use in the weeks to months after resolution of CNS-M when coagulation and inflammatory pathways of malaria pathogenesis resolve.

Our study has several limitations. We were unable to perform ocular examination for the presence of malaria retinopathy that allows for comparability to prior studies of retinopathy positive and retinopathy negative CNS-M. The overall small sample size and heterogeneity of the AFN cohort reduced power to detect differences among the groups. The CNS-M cohort was younger than the UM and AFN cohorts, raising the possibility that age, independent of malaria illness, could confound biomarker responses. The use of heparin as an anti-coagulant, opposed to acid citrate dextrose, precluded functional analysis of hemostasis. Children with UM were not evaluated for infection with non-malaria pathogens, which could result in biomarker differences relative to both CNS-M and AFN. Finally, field studies that prospectively monitor neurocognitive and behavioral metrics of a larger number of children who survive CNS-M are required to address whether prolonged thrombotic propensity is associated with this post-treatment clinical phenotype.

Conclusion

Recovery from pediatric CNS-M is associated with prolonged dysregulation of gene expression and biomarkers associated with coagulation, fibrinolysis, and platelet activation. Future studies are needed to determine whether this is associated with post-recovery clinical sequalae.
