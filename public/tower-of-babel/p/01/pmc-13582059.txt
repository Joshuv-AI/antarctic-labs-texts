Associations of Polypharmacy, Diuretics and Antipsychotics with Injurious Falls: A Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Tewodros Yosef, Julie A. Pasco, Monica C. Tembo, Kara B. Anderson, Lana J. Williams, Kara L. Holloway-Kew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePopulation ageing increases multimorbidity, which in turn contributes to polypharmacy, elevating fall risk through drug interactions and adverse effects on cognition or balance. Although polypharmacy is recognised as a risk factor, specific fall-risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs) may pose greater hazards. This study aimed to examine the associations of polypharmacy, diuretic and antipsychotic use with injurious falls among older adults.MethodsData from 952 participants (590 men and 362 women) aged ≥ 65 years in the Geelong Osteoporosis Study were linked with the Victorian Emergency Minimum Dataset. The outcome variable was time to first fall-related emergency presentation or injurious falls, with polypharmacy, diuretics and antipsychotics as exposure variables. The associations were examined using competing risk regression, with results presented as adjusted subdistribution hazard ratios (aSHR) and 95% confidence intervals. The population attributable fraction (PAF) was calculated to assess the population-level burden of polypharmacy, diuretics and antipsychotics on injurious falls. PAF indicates the proportion of cases in a population attributable to a specific risk factor.ResultsThe median time to the first injurious fall was 8.0 years (IQR: 4.0–12.2). The overall incidence rate of injurious falls was 19.3 per 1000 person-years (95% CI: 16.9–22.0). Polypharmacy (aSHR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.23–5.59), diuretic use (aSHR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.81–4.92) and antipsychotic use (aSHR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.23–4.07) increased the risk of injurious falls, with PAF of 17.1%, 23.0% and 4.6%, respectively.ConclusionsPolypharmacy, diuretic and antipsychotic use were associated with an increased risk of injurious falls, with diuretics accounting for the greatest population-level burden. These findings highlight the potential importance of routine medication review in identifying medications that may contribute to injurious falls among older adults. However, given the observational nature of this study, interventional studies are needed to determine whether medication review and deprescribing strategies can effectively reduce injurious falls and improve safety outcomes.

Introduction

Worldwide population ageing [1] poses substantial public health challenges [2], particularly owing to the increasing burden of multimorbidity with advancing age [3]. Consistent with this, approximately 80% of older adults live with at least one chronic condition, and more than half have two or more [4]. The management of these conditions often requires multiple medications [5], with 43.3% of adults aged ≥ 50 years and more than two thirds of those aged ≥ 75 years taking five or more medications daily [6].

Medications are essential for managing chronic conditions [5, 7, 8]. However, polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—can lead to adverse outcomes, including drug interactions, side effects and an increased risk of falls [5]. Definitions of polypharmacy vary across studies, with different thresholds used, including ≥ 2 medications [9, 10], ≥ 4 medications [11, 12], ≥ 5 medications [13–17], and ≥ 6 medications daily [18–20], while the World Health Organization defines polypharmacy as the use of five or more medications daily [5].

Falls, defined as an unexpected event in which an individual comes to rest on the ground, floor, or a lower level, affect around one in three older adults annually [21] and can substantially reduce the quality of life [22]. Polypharmacy may contribute to falls in older adults through several mechanisms. Some medications can cause dizziness, drowsiness, blurred vision, and postural blood pressure changes, impairing balance and coordination [23, 24]. The use of multiple medications increases the risk of adverse interactions and cumulative side effects that may further reduce stability [25]. Certain medications may also impair cognition or confuse, affecting function and increasing fall risk [26]. In addition, medications acting on the nervous and musculoskeletal systems may reduce muscle strength and physical performance, increasing susceptibility to falls [27].

Previous studies have reported an association between polypharmacy and increased fall risk among older adults, although the magnitude of the association varies across study designs and definitions of polypharmacy. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that adults aged ≥ 65 years using four or more prescription medications daily had a 1.5–2-fold higher likelihood of recurrent falls [28]. A longitudinal study of adults aged ≥ 60 years found that polypharmacy (≥ 5 medications/day) was associated with a 21% higher rate of falls over 2 years [12]. Supporting this, a cross-sectional study of 6,928 adults aged ≥ 55 years found that polypharmacy (≥ 4 medications/day) was significantly associated with increased fall risk [29].

However, evidence from prior studies indicates that certain medications, notably psychotropics, may confer a higher risk of falls than polypharmacy alone [7, 30, 31]. Ie et al. [32] reported that adults aged ≥ 65 years using ≥ 2 fall-risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs) had a 1.7-fold higher risk of falls. This risk is particularly pronounced with psychotropic medications such as antidepressants, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, antiparkinsonian drugs, anticonvulsants and sedatives [33]. Ju and Sinn [34] highlighted that diuretics are also classified as fall-risk-increasing drugs owing to their potential to cause volume depletion, orthostatic hypotension, electrolyte disturbances, and urinary urgency, thereby impairing balance and increasing fall risk. With population ageing and increasing multimorbidity, the use of multiple medications has risen [35]. While polypharmacy contributes to fall risk, certain high-risk medications can independently and substantially increase the likelihood of falling. This study aimed to examine the associations of polypharmacy, diuretic and antipsychotic use with injurious falls among older adults.

Methods

Study Design and Population

Participants (n = 952; 590 men and 362 women) aged ≥ 65 years were drawn from the Geelong Osteoporosis Study (GOS) [36], a population-based cohort study conducted in the Barwon Statistical Division in southeastern Australia. Participants were randomly selected from the electoral roll to provide an age-stratified representative sample of the general population. Although the study was originally established to investigate osteoporosis and bone health, recruitment was not limited to individuals with osteoporosis. Participants were eligible if they had resided in the region for at least 6 months and provided informed consent. Women were recruited between 1993 and 1997, with follow-up assessments at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 15 years post recruitment. Men were recruited about a decade later (2001–2006), with follow-ups at 5 and 15 years. For the current analysis, data were taken from men’s baseline appointment (2001–2006) and from women’s 6-year follow-up appointment (2001–2003).

Study Variables

Outcome Variable

The outcome was the time to first fall-related emergency presentation. As all fall-related emergency presentations in this study involved injuries, the terms ‘injurious falls’ and ‘fall-related emergency presentations’ are used interchangeably throughout the manuscript. These events were identified through linkage with the Victorian Emergency Minimum Dataset (VEMD), which collects emergency department data from major Victorian public hospitals in Australia, including Warrnambool and District Base Hospital, Hamilton Base Hospital and University Hospital Geelong [37]. Participants were followed from men’s baseline assessment (2001–2006) and women’s 6-year follow-up assessment wave (2001–2003) until the first fall-related emergency presentation, death or the end of follow-up (31 December 2022). As assessments occurred at different times, each participant’s baseline visit served as their individual index date and the start of follow-up. Only fall-related emergency presentations occurring after the index date were included in the analysis. Mortality information was obtained via linkage with the National Death Index, maintained by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare.

Exposure Variable

Medication use was recorded at the baseline assessment. Participants were asked to bring all current medications or a medication list to confirm their medication information. During study visits, trained research staff collected medication data directly from medication containers and/or medication lists where available. This process was conducted by trained personnel to ensure accuracy and minimise recall bias. Medication names, therapeutic classes, dosages and frequency of use were systematically documented. Medication exposure was classified according to baseline use and considered fixed over the follow-up period, as data on subsequent changes in medication use, such as initiation, cessation, switching or dose adjustment, were unavailable. Therefore, exposure intensity and temporal patterns of medication use could not be evaluated. Polypharmacy was defined as the use of five or more medications daily [8], including prescription drugs, over-the-counter medications and complementary medicines [38]. Fall-risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs) elevate fall risk even when used in isolation [39], and we therefore also analyzed them as individual risk factors. FRID use was defined as the use of at least one medication from the following classes: cardiovascular medications (antihypertensives, beta-blockers, diuretics, antiarrhythmics and cardiac inotropes), psychotropic medications (sedatives/hypnotics, anxiolytics, antipsychotics, antidepressants and anticonvulsants), analgesics (opioids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents), and antihyperglycemic agents (insulin and oral hypoglycemics). For the analysis, each medication category was modelled separately.

Covariates

Covariate data were collected at baseline using both self-reported information and objective measurements. Smoking status was defined as the regular use of tobacco in the form of cigarettes, cigars or pipes [40]. High alcohol intake was defined as consumption of more than two standard drinks per day for women and more than three for men, with one standard drink equivalent to 10 g of pure alcohol. Balance confidence (BC) was assessed using the 14-item Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES) [41], which measures an individual’s confidence in performing daily activities without falling; scores range from 0 to 140, with lower scores indicating lower balance confidence. The reliability of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with item–test correlations (correlation with the total scale including the item), item–rest correlations (correlation with the total scale excluding the item) and the change in alpha if items were deleted, to evaluate each item’s contribution. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.953), with all items–rest correlations above 0.70 and removing any item did not improve reliability.

Mobility was self-reported using a seven-point scale as previously described [36], with categories of very active, active, sedentary, limited, inactive, chair/bedridden and bedfast. For analysis, mobility was grouped as high (very active, active) or low (sedentary, limited, inactive, chair/bedridden and bedfast). Participants’ use of mobility assistive devices (e.g. walkers or canes) was recorded (yes/no) [21, 40]. A fall was defined as an event in which an individual comes to rest on the ground unintentionally, from a lying, sitting or standing position [36]. Self-reported falls in the past 12 months were collected at baseline and classified as a binary variable (yes = experienced ≥ 1 fall; no = no falls) [42]. The Timed-Up-and-Go (TUG) test assesses mobility and balance by timing how long a person takes to stand from a chair, walk three metres, turn, return and sit down; a duration ≥ 12 s indicates slow performance [43].

Lean mass was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Lunar), which provides a precise estimate of lean soft tissue mass. Leg lean mass, calculated as the sum of lean mass from both legs, was used as an indicator of lower limb muscle mass. Relative leg lean mass (kg/m2) was calculated by dividing leg lean mass by height squared. Eccentric hip muscle force was assessed using a break test with the Nicholas manual muscle tester (model 01160, Lafayette Instrument Company). Hip flexion and abduction forces were measured three times per leg without rest between trials, and the highest force values were recorded [44]. The maximal force values for hip flexion and abduction were converted to Newtons (N) by multiplying by 9.81. These force values were then divided by leg lean mass (in kilograms), as measured by DXA scans, to calculate hip muscle strength as a force-to-mass ratio (N/kg) [45]. Weight was measured to ±0.1 kg using electronic scales and height to ±0.1 cm using a wall-mounted Harpenden stadiometer.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize participant characteristics and covariates related to falls. Normality was assessed using visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots, supported by skewness and kurtosis statistics. These assessments guided the presentation of continuous variables, with means and standard deviations reported for normally distributed data, and medians with interquartile ranges for skewed distributions. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables.

Because more than 10% of participants died during the period of follow-up—potentially preventing emergency presentations for falls—we applied Fine and Gray’s competing risk regression analysis. The proportional subdistribution hazard assumption was evaluated using covariate–time interactions and Schoenfeld residuals, with no violations detected. Potential covariates were identified on the basis of theoretical reasoning informed by previous research. Each covariate’s relationship with the outcome was initially examined using crude (bivariable) analyses to identify candidate variables for the multivariable model. Variables with p < 0.25 [46] were considered for inclusion, a threshold chosen to retain potentially important predictors while reducing the risk of overfitting [47].

Polypharmacy was retained in the multivariable model despite not being significant in bivariable analysis, given its established clinical importance as a recognised risk factor for falls. The number of FRIDs used by each participant was counted and categorised as ≥ 1 FRID (yes/no) and ≥ 2 FRIDs (yes/no) for analysis; however, neither met the prespecified significance threshold for inclusion in multivariable models. All individual FRID classes were first examined in bivariable analyses, and those associated with the outcome at p < 0.25 were considered for multivariable analysis on the basis of both statistical and clinical relevance. Only diuretics and antipsychotics met these criteria and were therefore retained as separate exposure variables in the multivariable models. To avoid overlap between exposure definitions, these two FRID classes were excluded from the polypharmacy count. All other FRID classes were not included as independent exposures in the multivariable analysis and were therefore incorporated into the polypharmacy measure. This approach ensured a clear separation between individually modelled FRID exposures and overall medication burden, while preventing double-counting and structural multicollinearity in the multivariable analysis. Medications were analysed at the class level to maintain statistical power, as individual drugs were infrequently used and produced unstable estimates. Within diuretics, 91% were potassium-sparing; however, subtype analyses were limited by small sample sizes and yielded attenuated associations. Therefore, class-level modelling was adopted as the most robust analytical approach [48]. Polypharmacy, diuretics and antipsychotics exposure were evaluated as separate medication-related risk factors. The analyses were designed to assess their individual associations with fall-related emergency presentations rather than to directly compare their relative predictive strength.

The multivariable model was adjusted for age, sex, smoking, high alcohol intake, prior self-reported falls, mobility, use of mobility assistive devices, Timed-Up-and-Go test, hip muscle strength and balance confidence (MFES score). Adjusted subdistribution hazard ratios (aSHR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Multicollinearity was minimal (variance inflation factors < 2). Potential interaction effects between polypharmacy and selected covariates, including diuretic and antipsychotic use, were assessed by including interaction terms in the multivariable models. No statistically significant interaction effects were observed. However, a comprehensive assessment of all possible drug–drug interactions among medications contributing to polypharmacy is not statistically feasible or interpretable owing to multiple testing issues. Therefore, we focused on selected clinically relevant pairwise interactions, none of which demonstrated statistically significant evidence of interaction.

Population attributable fractions (PAFs) indicate the proportion of cases in a population attributable to a specific risk factor. We estimated the population-level impact of polypharmacy and fall-risk-increasing drugs (diuretics and antipsychotics) on falls by calculating PAFs using exposure prevalence and adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio (aSHR) from multivariable Fine–Gray competing risk models. This metric represents the percentage reduction in injurious falls that would occur in the study population if the risk factor were eliminated. It is calculated as PAF = Pe (aSHR − 1)/[1 + Pe (aSHR − 1)], where Pe is the prevalence of the risk factor and aSHR is the subdistribution hazard ratio for falls associated with that risk factor [49]. The data were analyzed using Stata version 18 (Stata Corp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: Stata Corp LLC).

Incidence of Injurious Falls

The median time to the first fall-related emergency presentation was 8.0 years (IQR: 4.0–12.2). Of the 952 participants, 219 (23%) experienced at least one injurious fall during follow-up; 125 were men (13.1%) and 94 were women (9.9%). The incidence of injurious falls was 20.5 per 1000 person-years in men (95% CI: 17.2–24.5) and 17.8 per 1000 person-years in women (95% CI: 14.5–21.8), with an overall incidence of 19.3 per 1000 person-years (95% CI: 16.9–22.0). Polypharmacy and FRIDs are Predictors of Injurious Falls

Polypharmacy was not associated with injurious falls in the crude model but showed a significant association after adjustment (aSHR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.23–5.59; p = 0.013). Diuretic use (aSHR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.81–4.92; p < 0.001) and antipsychotic use (aSHR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.23– 4.07; p = 0.009) were associated with a higher risk of injurious falls (Table 3). Other FRID classes were not associated with injurious falls in crude models (Table 2). The PAFs were 23.0% for diuretic use, 17.1% for polypharmacy and 4.6% for antipsychotic use. Discussion

This study aimed to examine the associations of polypharmacy, diuretic and antipsychotic use with injurious falls among older adults. All three exposures were independently associated with an increased risk of injurious falls. Diuretic use demonstrated the highest PAF (23.0%), followed by polypharmacy (17.1%) and antipsychotic use (4.6%), reflecting their relative contributions to the observed population burden of injurious falls.

Polypharmacy was associated with more than a twofold higher risk of injurious falls among older adults, consistent with previous studies [12, 50] identifying it as a key predictor of falls risk. The absence of a significant association in the unadjusted analysis likely reflects confounding and suppression effects, whereby factors such as age, comorbidity burden and mobility limitation—each related to both medication burden and fall risk—obscured the underlying relationship. After adjustment, the independent effect of polypharmacy became apparent, demonstrating a strong positive association with injurious falls. Such suppression patterns are well documented in pharmacoepidemiologic research, particularly where medication burden is closely tied to underlying health status.

Several mechanisms may explain the observed association. Exposure to multiple medications increases the likelihood of adverse effects such as dizziness, sedation, hypotension and delirium [51], all of which contribute to falls. Polypharmacy also heightens the risk of drug–drug and drug–disease interactions that can impair balance, cognition [52] and cardiovascular regulation, particularly in the context of multimorbidity. The concurrent use of central nervous system–active medications may further compound cognitive and functional decline [53], leading to slower reaction times and impaired postural control. In addition, age-related reductions in renal and hepatic function can alter drug metabolism and clearance, increasing susceptibility to adverse effects and physiological instability [54]. Complex medication regimens may also contribute to non-adherence and suboptimal disease control, indirectly increasing fall risk.

The PAF for polypharmacy was 17.1%, indicating that a substantial proportion of injurious falls was attributable to this exposure. This is plausible given both the strength of the association and the high prevalence of polypharmacy in older populations. In Australia, approximately 36–40% of older adults take five or more medications [42, 56, 57], indicating that even modest effect sizes may translate into a substantial population-level burden. When defined more leniently as ≥ 4 medications, the PAF decreased to 5.2%, likely reflecting a weaker association despite a higher prevalence of exposure [58]. These estimates should be interpreted cautiously, as they do not imply that eliminating polypharmacy would directly prevent injurious falls, and the causal effects of deprescribing cannot be inferred from this observational study.

While polypharmacy is associated with injurious falls, the risk appears to be more strongly influenced by specific medication classes, particularly those known to increase susceptibility to falls [7]. In this study, diuretic use was associated with a threefold higher likelihood of injurious falls compared with non-use, consistent with previous evidence identifying diuretics as high-risk medications in older populations [55–57]. This association may be explained by several mechanisms. Diuretics can cause volume depletion, leading to reduced blood pressure and cerebral perfusion, thereby increasing the likelihood of orthostatic hypotension, dizziness and impaired balance [34].

They may also induce electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia, which can adversely affect muscle function, coordination and cognitive performance [58]. Furthermore, increased urine output may result in nocturia, which disrupts sleep and increases exposure to nighttime falls, particularly among individuals with mobility or visual impairment [59]. Residual confounding may also contribute to the observed association, as diuretics are commonly prescribed for cardiovascular and renal conditions that themselves are associated with increased fall risk [57]. In this study, diuretics accounted for a PAF of 23.0%, suggesting that a substantial proportion of injurious falls occurred among individuals exposed to diuretics. This reflects both the strength of the association and the high prevalence of diuretic use in older adults [60]. The observed relationship is biologically plausible, with effects mediated through orthostatic hypotension, electrolyte imbalance and reduced muscle function [61]. In clinical contexts such as heart failure, diuretics remain an essential component of therapy, and any consideration of deprescribing requires a careful evaluation of potential benefits and harms [57]. Given their widespread use in older populations, even modest associations may translate into important implications at the population level. These findings support the relevance of regular medication review, monitoring for adverse effects and targeted fall-risk assessment among older adults prescribed diuretics. The results do not suggest that diuretics are inappropriate when clinically indicated but rather highlight the importance of individualised risk–benefit assessment and proactive mitigation of fall-related risks in vulnerable populations.

Antipsychotic use was associated with more than twofold higher risk of injurious falls, consistent with previous studies [62–64]. Fraser et al. [62] reported that initiation of atypical antipsychotics in older adults increased the risk of falls by 52% and nonvertebral osteoporotic fractures by 50%, while Landi et al. [63], in a large community-dwelling cohort, observed a 45% higher risk of falls among users. Collectively, these findings reinforce the substantial fall burden associated with antipsychotic medications in older populations. The increased risk is likely multifactorial, reflecting both pharmacological effects and clinical context. Reported mechanisms include sedation and reduced alertness, orthostatic hypotension and extrapyramidal symptoms such as rigidity and tremor, all of which can impair gait and balance [62, 63, 65]. In addition, antipsychotics may contribute to cognitive slowing, confusion and disorientation, and their effects can be amplified through drug–drug interactions that enhance sedation or hypotension [66]. Their use may also indicate underlying psychiatric or cognitive disorders, which themselves are associated with elevated fall risk.

In the present study, antipsychotics contributed a PAF of 4.6% for injurious falls, which was modest compared with diuretics (24.1%). This may reflect the combined influence of the above mechanisms alongside their comparatively lower prevalence of use in the cohort, rather than a smaller individual-level effect. Falls are multifactorial in nature and likely reflect a combination of medication use, underlying health conditions, and functional impairments. Therefore, the observed associations between specific medication classes (e.g. diuretics and antipsychotics) and fall-related emergency presentations should not be interpreted as evidence of direct causal effects or as standalone targets for deprescribing. Rather, these findings likely reflect broader underlying clinical risk states, including frailty and comorbidity burden, for which these medications may serve as markers. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as population-level risk associations that may inform clinical awareness and risk stratification rather than as direct causal effects or prescribing recommendations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of more than 20 years of reliable follow-up data and the objective identification of fall-related emergency presentation rather than self-reports. The analyses accounted for key covariates, used advanced competing risk methods to account for deaths occurring before a fall event and estimated the proportion of injurious falls at the population level that may be attributable to major risk factors. However, the study also had several limitations. For example, falls were recorded only if participants presented to the hospital; consequently, minor falls that did not lead to hospital visits were not recorded. Although functional measures such as TUG, MFES and muscle strength were included only as adjustment variables, they strengthened internal validity by improving control for key confounders. However, as these measures are not routinely available in many clinical datasets, this may limit comparability with studies that rely on routinely collected variables and could affect the transferability of the adjustment strategy across different settings. Medication exposure was assessed only at baseline and treated as fixed throughout follow-up, with no information on subsequent changes in use. This may have introduced nondifferential exposure misclassification and potential survivorship bias over the extended follow-up period. In addition, the lack of data on dose changes, duration and timing of initiation or discontinuation meant that it was not possible to distinguish between acute and chronic effects, assess dose–response relationships or evaluate temporal patterns of risk. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as associations based on baseline medication use rather than time-varying or detailed exposure–response relationships. All possible drug–drug interactions between individual medications included in the polypharmacy count were not assessed. Consequently, potential synergistic or cumulative effects of specific medication combinations could not be evaluated, and the findings should be interpreted as reflecting independent associations rather than the combined effects of multiple medications. Potential confounding by indication and residual confounding may have been present, particularly for diuretics and antipsychotics, because their use may have reflected underlying conditions such as cardiovascular disease, neuropsychiatric disorders and frailty. Although several chronic diseases were assessed at the bivariate level, none met the threshold for inclusion in the multivariable models. However, important factors such as disease severity, symptom burden, orthostatic hypotension and cognitive impairment were not fully captured. Therefore, residual confounding could not be excluded. The observational nature of the study limits causal interpretation of the findings. While extensive adjustments were made for demographic, clinical and behavioural covariates, unmeasured confounding may have influenced the observed relationships.

Conclusion

Polypharmacy, diuretic and antipsychotic use were associated with an increased risk of injurious falls, with diuretics accounting for the greatest population-level burden. These findings highlight the potential importance of routine medication review in identifying medications that may contribute to injurious falls among older adults. However, given the observational nature of this study, interventional studies are needed to determine whether medication review and deprescribing strategies can effectively reduce injurious falls and improve safety outcomes.
