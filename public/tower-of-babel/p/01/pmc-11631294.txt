Cardiac biomarkers for screening and prognostication of cardiac dysfunction in critically ill patients
Authors: Oscar Cavefors, Freyr Einarsson, Jakob Holmqvist, Odd Bech‐Hanssen, Sven‐Erik Ricksten, Björn Redfors, Jonatan Oras
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to assess the use of high‐sensitivity troponin T (hsTNT) and N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) in screening for cardiac dysfunction [left ventricular (LV) systolic or diastolic dysfunction or right ventricular (RV) dysfunction] in mixed intensive care unit (ICU) patients and establish whether these biomarkers are independently associated with an increased risk of death.MethodsWe performed a secondary analysis of a single‐centre prospective observational study in which consecutive ICU patients were examined with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and cardiac biomarkers. Patients with systolic or diastolic LV dysfunction, RV dysfunction or a combination of these were compared with patients with normal cardiac function. Sensitivity and specificity for different cut‐off levels were calculated using receiver operating characteristic curves. Regression models were used to evaluate the associations between cardiac biomarkers, sepsis, renal failure and mortality.ResultsA total of 276 patients were included. Most of the patients had cardiac dysfunction on TTE (64%). Combined cardiac dysfunction was most prevalent (71 patients, 26%), followed by isolated diastolic LV dysfunction (40 patients, 15%). Levels of hsTNT and NT‐proBNP were higher in all types of cardiac dysfunction versus patients with normal cardiac function. The area under the curve (AUC) for hsTNT to detect any cardiac dysfunction was 0.75. An optimal cut‐off at 30.5 ng/L rendered a positive predictive value (PPV) of 80% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 58%. The AUC for NT‐proBNP to detect any cardiac dysfunction was 0.788. Using an optimal cut‐off at 1145 ng/L rendered a PPV of 86% and an NPV of 58%. Using a clinically relevant 90% sensitivity for detecting cardiac dysfunction put the cut‐offs at 14.1 ng/L for hsTNT and 247 ng/L for NT‐proBNP, resulting in a specificity of 48% and 46%, respectively. Levels of NT‐proBNP were associated with sepsis and renal failure (P < 0.001), while levels of hsTNT were associated with renal failure only (P < 0.001) after adjustment for cardiac dysfunction. Levels of biomarkers were associated with an increased risk of 90 day mortality after adjustments for age, Simplified Acute Physiology Score 3, cardiac dysfunction and factors independently associated with biomarker increase (sepsis and renal failure) (P = 0.048 for hsTNT and P < 0.006 for NT‐proBNP).ConclusionCardiac biomarkers, hsTNT and NT‐proBNP, are strongly correlated to cardiac dysfunction in ICU patients and have a robust association with increased mortality. However, the relatively low NPV and the low specificity at relevant sensitivity levels of the biomarkers make them unsuitable for use in screening for cardiac dysfunction.Keywords: cardiac dysfunction, echocardiography, ICU, NT‐proBNP, troponin

Introduction

The cardiac biomarkers N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) and troponin are extensively used in cardiology and primary care to evaluate cardiac dysfunction. NT‐proBNP is associated with systolic as well as diastolic left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, and low levels in such patients can rule out LV failure. 1 Troponins have a clearly defined role in cardiac injury and the diagnosis of myocardial infarction. 2 Echocardiography is often used to evaluate cardiac function in the intensive care unit (ICU). However, measuring cardiac biomarkers could be beneficial for screening and risk stratification in ICU patients, as echocardiography is user dependent, time‐consuming and not always feasible. 3 , 4

Nonetheless, using cardiac biomarkers in ICU patients is challenging as these can be elevated for reasons other than cardiac dysfunction, including sepsis and renal failure. 5 , 6 , 7 Septic patients, for example, have been shown to have levels of NT‐proBNP similar to those of heart failure patients. 8 , 9 A few studies have evaluated the use of cardiac biomarkers to identify cardiac dysfunction in ICU patients, with mixed results. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Increased levels of troponins and NT‐proBNP have also been linked to mortality, but most of these studies have not adjusted for cardiac function or other important confounders, such as sepsis and renal failure. 15 , 16 , 17 Hence, the usefulness of cardiac biomarkers in screening for cardiac dysfunction and prognostication in general ICU patients has been inadequately assessed.

The present study aims to (1) evaluate the use of cardiac biomarkers in screening for cardiac dysfunction [LV systolic, LV diastolic or right ventricular (RV) dysfunction] in mixed ICU patients and (2) establish whether cardiac biomarkers are independently associated with increased mortality after adjusting for cardiac dysfunction and other risk factors.

Methods

Study design

The following analysis of cardiac biomarkers is based on a single‐centre prospective observational study in which critically ill patients were examined with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (Clinical Trials ID: NCT03787810). The original study was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee in Gothenburg, Sweden (Registration Number 036‐18). Patients were included for 151 days between 28 May 2018 and 20 January 2019. All adult patients who could undergo echocardiography within 24 h of admission to the ICU were included consecutively in the cohort. 18 The systematic measurement of biomarkers was introduced into the study protocol after 3 months. The inclusion criteria for the current study were (1) NT‐proBNP or high‐sensitivity troponin T (hsTNT) acquired at the time of TTE and (2) a complete TTE examination in which all parameters specified in the study protocol were available.

Patient inclusion

Patients were recruited from the general and neuro ICUs at Sahlgrenska University Hospital (Gothenburg, Sweden). The general ICU admits surgical patients (trauma, vascular, upper abdominal surgery, spinal surgery and liver and kidney transplantations) and medical patients from all disciplines. The neuro ICU admits patients with neurosurgical and neurological diseases, including traumatic or haemorrhagic brain injuries, strokes and patients after major neurosurgery. Patients referred for specialist care at this tertiary centre are treated alongside local patients, both in the neuro and general ICUs. Patients with acute cardiac disorders are treated in the coronary care unit unless they require invasive ventilation. If so, they are admitted to the general ICU. Post‐operative cardiothoracic ICU patients or patients in need of mechanical circulatory support are treated in the dedicated cardiothoracic ICU and were not included in this study.

Clinical data

Age, sex and medical history were obtained from patient charts. In addition, sepsis (suspected or verified), according to guidelines, and renal failure (defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2) were registered separately. 19 , 20 Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) 3, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and 90 day mortality were obtained from the local ICU registry. 21 , 22

Definitions and echocardiography

Within 24 h of admission to the ICU, patients underwent a standardized TTE conducted by an experienced clinician licensed to perform TTE. 23 LV systolic and diastolic functions in this cohort have been reported earlier. 18 , 24

Cardiac dysfunction was defined as follows:

‐isolated LV systolic dysfunction: having an LV ejection fraction (EF) < 50% or regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMAs) in two or more adjacent segments with no isolated RV systolic function or isolated LV diastolic dysfunction

‐isolated LV systolic dysfunction: having an LV ejection fraction (EF) < 50% or regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMAs) in two or more adjacent segments with no isolated RV systolic function or isolated LV diastolic dysfunction

‐isolated LV diastolic dysfunction: fulfilling criteria for diastolic dysfunction or indeterminate diastolic function according to the guidelines of the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) 25 and exhibiting no isolated LV systolic dysfunction or isolated RV systolic dysfunction. The following parameters and cut‐offs are used in the guideline: (1) septal e′ (e′sep) < 7 cm/s or lateral e′ (e′lat) < 10 cm/s; (2) E/e′ average > 14 (E/e′lat > 13 or E/e′sep > 15, if only one was available); (3) tricuspid regurgitation max velocity > 2.8 m/s; and (4) left atrial volume index > 34 mL/m2. Patients fulfilling <50% of the criteria are considered to have a normal diastolic function; those fulfilling 50% are regarded as having an indeterminate diastolic function; and patients fulfilling >50% are assessed as having diastolic dysfunction

‐isolated LV diastolic dysfunction: fulfilling criteria for diastolic dysfunction or indeterminate diastolic function according to the guidelines of the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) 25 and exhibiting no isolated LV systolic dysfunction or isolated RV systolic dysfunction. The following parameters and cut‐offs are used in the guideline: (1) septal e′ (e′sep) < 7 cm/s or lateral e′ (e′lat) < 10 cm/s; (2) E/e′ average > 14 (E/e′lat > 13 or E/e′sep > 15, if only one was available); (3) tricuspid regurgitation max velocity > 2.8 m/s; and (4) left atrial volume index > 34 mL/m2. Patients fulfilling <50% of the criteria are considered to have a normal diastolic function; those fulfilling 50% are regarded as having an indeterminate diastolic function; and patients fulfilling >50% are assessed as having diastolic dysfunction

‐isolated RV systolic dysfunction: fulfilling any of the following: (1) tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) < 17 mm; (2) RV tissue Doppler‐derived tricuspid lateral annular systolic velocity wave (S′) < 11 cm/s; or (3) fractional area change (FAC) < 35% with no isolated LV systolic dysfunction or isolated LV diastolic dysfunction

‐isolated RV systolic dysfunction: fulfilling any of the following: (1) tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) < 17 mm; (2) RV tissue Doppler‐derived tricuspid lateral annular systolic velocity wave (S′) < 11 cm/s; or (3) fractional area change (FAC) < 35% with no isolated LV systolic dysfunction or isolated LV diastolic dysfunction

‐combined cardiac dysfunction: fulfilling any criteria of LV systolic or LV diastolic dysfunction and isolated RV systolic dysfunction

‐combined cardiac dysfunction: fulfilling any criteria of LV systolic or LV diastolic dysfunction and isolated RV systolic dysfunction

‐any cardiac dysfunction: fulfilling any criteria of cardiac dysfunction

‐any cardiac dysfunction: fulfilling any criteria of cardiac dysfunction

‐normal cardiac function: fulfilling no criteria of cardiac dysfunction.

‐normal cardiac function: fulfilling no criteria of cardiac dysfunction.

Biomarkers

Blood samples for biomarkers were obtained at the time of the TTE and analysed by our hospital laboratory using immunoassay techniques. NT‐proBNP was analysed with the Elecsys assay (Roche) and hsTNT with the Roche hsTNT assay.

Statistics

We present normally distributed variables as the mean ± standard deviation and non‐normally distributed variables as the median (interquartile range). ANOVA or t‐test was used for the comparison of means for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U test was used for the comparison of distributions of non‐normally distributed variables. The χ 2 test was used for the comparison of nominal outcomes between groups. Levels of biomarkers were compared between patients with normal cardiac function and those with cardiac dysfunction, as defined earlier. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to analyse cut‐off values for biomarkers, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Youden's test was used to identify the optimal relationship between sensitivity and specificity. The negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV) were calculated. This analysis was performed for the whole cohort as well as in a subgroup of patients without a history of or an acute ongoing cardiac disease. A quantile regression model of the median was used to analyse if sepsis and renal failure were associated with increased biomarkers independent of cardiac function. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association of levels of hsTNT and NT‐proBNP on mortality in four different models, adding variables to each model as follows: hsTNT and NT‐proBNP only (Model 0); adjusting for any cardiac dysfunction (Model 1); adjusting for SAPS 3 score and age (Model 2); and adjusting for independently associated variables (renal failure for hsTNT and sepsis and renal failure for NT‐proBNP) (Model 3). A P‐value < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Version 24.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

The analysis included 276 of the 411 patients from the original study. The TTE was incomplete in 46 patients, and an additional 89 patients were excluded due to the absence of cardiac biomarkers (Figure 1). Levels of hsTNT were available in 269 patients (98%) and NT‐proBNP in 237 patients (86%). Sensitivity analysis showed that the 276 patients included in the study were a representative sample of the entire original study population with regard to SAPS 3 score, age, 90 day mortality and the prevalence of any cardiac dysfunction. 21 The majority of the patients had cardiac dysfunction on TTE (64%). A combined cardiac dysfunction was the most prevalent type (71 patients, 26%), followed by isolated diastolic LV dysfunction (40 patients, 15%). Most patients were non‐surgical (57%), and most were male (59%). The cohort had a median SAPS 3 score of 59 and a median age of 65. Verified or suspected sepsis was seen in 29% of patients; 21% had renal failure. 19 , 21 Patients with any cardiac dysfunction were older, had higher SAPS 3 scores and more often had a history of cardiac disease. According to SAPS 3, they more frequently had a cardiovascular, respiratory, renal or metabolic reason for admission and more frequently suffered from sepsis or renal failure (Table 1). A complete list of admission data for all types of cardiac dysfunction is presented in Table S1. Haemodynamic and echocardiographic data are presented in Table 2. Patients with any cardiac dysfunction had lower systolic blood pressure, higher levels of central venous pressure (CVP), higher doses of administered noradrenaline and higher lactate levels. They also had a lower PaO2/FiO2 ratio and higher oxygen requirements. All echocardiographic variables exhibited a greater degree of abnormality in patients with any cardiac dysfunction.

Levels of cardiac biomarkers in cardiac dysfunction

Levels of NT‐proBNP were significantly higher in patients with all specified types of cardiac dysfunction compared with patients with normal heart function. Similarly significant results were found for hsTNT, except in patients with isolated RV dysfunction (P = 0.08). The highest levels of hsTNT were seen in patients with isolated systolic LV dysfunction, while the highest levels of NT‐proBNP were seen in patients with combined cardiac dysfunction (Figure 2). Cut‐off values for biomarkers

ROC curves for any cardiac dysfunction rendered an AUC of 0.788 for NT‐proBNP and 0.757 for hsTNT (Figure 3). According to Youden's test, the optimal cut‐off levels for detecting any cardiac dysfunction were an NT‐proBNP of 1145 ng/L and an hsTNT of 30 ng/L. For hsTNT, this cut‐off resulted in a sensitivity of 71% and a specificity of 69%, generating a PPV of 80% and an NPV of 58%. Using an NT‐proBNP cut‐off of 1145 ng/L rendered a sensitivity of 67% and a specificity of 80%, generating a PPV of 86% and an NPV of 58%. To establish a 90% sensitivity for detecting any cardiac dysfunction, the cut‐off value of hsTNT was 14.1 ng/L, resulting in a specificity of 48%, a PPV of 75% and an NPV of 73%. The cut‐off value for 90% sensitivity of NT‐proBNP was 247 ng/L, rendering a specificity of 46%, a PPV of 72% and an NPV of 74%. A cut‐off at over 90% specificity for any cardiac dysfunction was seen at an hsTNT level of 80 ng/L and an NT‐proBNP level of 3265 ng/L. These cut‐offs generated a sensitivity of 39%, a specificity of 91%, an NPV of 46% and a PPV of 88% for both biomarkers (Table 3).

Conditions associated with cardiac biomarkers

Sepsis and renal failure were associated with higher NT‐proBNP levels in a crude analysis and after adjustment for cardiac function (P < 0.001). Levels of hsTNT were independently associated with renal failure in non‐adjusted and adjusted analyses (P < 0.001), but no associations were found with sepsis (P = 0.160 and P = 0.212, respectively; Table 4).

Mortality analysis

Higher levels of hsTNT and NT‐proBNP were associated with increased 90 day mortality in a crude analysis (P < 0.001) (Model 0). After adjusting for any cardiac dysfunction (Model 1), both hsTNT and NT‐proBNP were associated with an increased risk of death (P = 0.009 for hsTNT and P < 0.002 for NT‐proBNP). 21 Adding an adjustment for SAPS 3 score and age to the model (Model 2) did not alter these results (P = 0.027 for hsTNT and P = 0.009 for NT‐proBNP). Finally, adding adjustments for independently associated variables (renal failure for hsTNT and sepsis and renal failure for NT‐proBNP) (Model 3) did not change the results (P = 0.048 for hsTNT and P < 0.006 for NT‐proBNP) (Table 5).

Discussion

The main findings of the present study were as follows: (1) Cardiac biomarker levels are higher in ICU patients with cardiac dysfunction compared with patients with normal cardiac function; (2) using cardiac biomarkers to rule out significant cardiac disease in general ICU patients is not feasible; and (3) NT‐proBNP and hsTNT are independently associated with increased mortality after adjustment for cardiac dysfunction and other risk factors.

To evaluate the use of cardiac biomarkers, we identified clinically relevant cardiac dysfunction using echocardiography. LV systolic dysfunction was defined as LVEF < 50% with or without RWMAs, which has been associated with increased mortality in general ICU patients. 18 LV diastolic dysfunction was defined according to the 2016 EACVI guidelines, ‘Recommendations for the Evaluation of Left Ventricular Diastolic Function by Echocardiography’. 25 Patients with diastolic dysfunction and indeterminate diastolic function were included, as these patients have increased mortality. 24 , 26 , 27 Patients were classified as having RV systolic dysfunction if they fulfilled any of three established RV dysfunction markers because previous studies have associated these markers with increased mortality. 28 , 29

Levels of biomarkers were higher in critically ill patients with cardiac dysfunction as compared with patients with normal cardiac function, thereby underlining the associations between cardiac biomarkers and cardiac dysfunction. Nevertheless, the overlap in biomarker levels between patients with and without cardiac dysfunction was significant, and patients with normal cardiac function had median levels of biomarkers above typical laboratory cut‐off values.

In specific ICU populations, such as subarachnoid haemorrhage, cardiac biomarkers can be used to systematically screen patients for cardiac dysfunction, decreasing the need for routine TTE. 12 , 30 Similarly, a smaller study in unselected patients showed that a BNP < 144 pg/mL had an NPV of 96% when used in screening for cardiac dysfunction in a broader cohort of ICU patients. 31 We explored the use of hsTNT and NT‐proBNP in screening for relevant clinical cardiac dysfunction in mixed ICU patients. The group any cardiac dysfunction was used to define cardiac dysfunction as it encompasses the most clinically relevant diagnoses sought by echocardiography. ROC analysis demonstrated moderate accuracy in detecting cardiac dysfunction in the cohort (Figure 3). The best cut‐offs had a PPV range of 80%–86% and an NPV of 58%, making biomarkers relatively specific but not sensitive. Using a clinically relevant level for a screening marker (i.e., 90% sensitivity) as applied to any cardiac dysfunction resulted in cut‐offs lower than the median values of patients with normal cardiac function: 247 ng/L for NT‐proBNP and 14 ng/L for hsTNT, which led to specificities below 50%. In a subgroup analysis, excluding patients with a primary cardiac disorder, the sensitivity and specificity were not better than for the whole cohort. Hence, NT‐proBNP and hsTNT cannot be recommended for screening for cardiac dysfunction in unselected ICU patients. Instead, the high prevalence of cardiac dysfunction in unselected patients (64%) makes TTE reasonable for all patients, regardless of cardiac biomarker levels.

Earlier research has shown that several non‐cardiac conditions, such as sepsis and renal failure, are associated with increased levels of cardiac biomarkers. 5 , 6 , 7 However, those studies were not adjusted for an increased prevalence of cardiac dysfunction in such groups, for example, septic cardiomyopathy in sepsis, possibly contributing to higher levels of cardiac biomarkers. Our analysis showed that sepsis and renal failure were associated with higher levels of NT‐proBNP, independent of cardiac function. In comparison, hsTNT levels were associated with renal failure, independent of cardiac function. The high prevalence of sepsis (29%) and renal failure (21%) in the present study may have lowered the specificity of identifying cardiac disease and might partially explain why cardiac biomarkers are not useful in screening for cardiac dysfunction in a mixed ICU population.

Associations between increased biomarker levels and mortality in ICU patients have been demonstrated in earlier research, particularly in septic patients. 15 , 16 , 17 In these studies, no adjustments have been performed for cardiac dysfunction or conditions independently associated with increasing biomarker levels. Consequently, it is unknown if increased cardiac biomarkers alone add to the prognostication of ICU patients. To decrease the effect of confounding factors, we used a three‐step model adjusted for cardiac function, SAPS 3, age and conditions independently linked to biomarker levels. Even with these adjustments, biomarkers were significantly associated with a 90 day mortality increase. This study does not explore the reason for the increased mortality in patients with higher levels of biomarkers. Nevertheless, our results show that NT‐proBNP and hsTNT are independently associated with increased mortality in mixed ICU patients. Such elevated levels should alert the clinician to search for a reversible cause of the biomarker increase.

The study evaluates the clinical usefulness of biomarkers in undifferentiated ICU patients. Although a multicentre study would have been useful, we find the study population diverse enough to represent a mixed ICU population. The extensive TTE examination, the identification of clinically relevant cardiac dysfunctions and corrections for other causes of biomarker increase add to the robustness of this study's evaluation of biomarkers for screening and prognostication. The study has several limitations. One limitation is the 3 month delay in systematic screening with biomarkers, excluding 89 patients. In these patients, it is possible that the prevalence of cardiac dysfunction could have been lower. This would probably not have affected the associations between cardiac dysfunction and biomarkers or the mortality association. However, if the true prevalence of cardiac dysfunction were lower, then it could have led to a higher NPV and a lower PPV. Nonetheless, it is unlikely that a larger cohort would have yielded different results because the sample size was relatively large and sensitivity analysis showed similar characteristics in excluded patients.

Another limitation is the lack of previous echocardiographic information before ICU, such as known heart failure. It is possible that biomarkers could have been higher in patients with acute cardiac disease and performed better as screening markers. However, excluding patients with previous cardiac dysfunction would limit the use of biomarkers, as a complete medical history is often not known upon ICU admission. The study could have been strengthened if TTE and biomarkers had been repeated during the ICU stay, making it possible to evaluate dynamic changes in biomarkers.

In cardiac biomarkers, hsTNT and NT‐proBNP, are strongly correlated to cardiac dysfunction in ICU patients and have a robust association with increased mortality. Despite this, the biomarkers are not specific enough to be used in screening for cardiac dysfunction. Instead, screening ICU patients with echocardiography seems reasonable, given the high prevalence of clinically relevant cardiac dysfunction.
