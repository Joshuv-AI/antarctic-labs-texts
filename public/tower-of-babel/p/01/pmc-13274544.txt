Left and Right Heart Remodelling at 1 Year After Transcatheter Versus Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement: A Speckle-Tracking Echocardiography Study
Authors: Olga N Kislitsina, S Chris Malaisrie, James D Thomas, Seokyung An, Beth Whippo, Jane Kruse, Ranya N Sweis, Anita W Asgar, Charles J Davidson, James D Flaherty, Patrick M McCarthy, Christopher K Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSpeckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) identifies subclinical myocardial dysfunction after aortic valve replacement. We compared strain-based remodelling and prognostic values after transcatheter (TAVR) vs surgical (SAVR) aortic valve replacement.MethodsWe analysed preoperative (preop) and postoperative (postop) left atrial volume index (LAVI), left ventricular (LV) global longitudinal strain (LV-GLS), right ventricular (RV) free wall strain (RV-FWS), RV global longitudinal strain (RV-GLS), and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) in 373 patients (TAVR n = 195; SAVR n = 178). Multivariable logistic regression assessed echocardiographic predictors of 1-year clinical outcomes.ResultsLeft ventricular ejection fraction and LV-GLS remained stable in both groups. Right ventricular function deteriorated post-SAVR compared to TAVR (P < .001), with marked declines in TAPSE and RV-GLS (both P < .001). Preop LAVI predicted mortality (adjusted OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.02-1.05; P = .001) and atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence (OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02-1.06; P = .001). Stroke was predicted by preop RV-GLS (OR, 0.91; P = .031) and TAPSE (OR, 1.12; P = .013). Postop LAVI (OR, 1.06; P = .001), RV-FWS (OR, 1.06; P = .016), RV-GLS (OR, 1.10; P = .006), and TAPSE (OR, 0.93; P = .035) also remained significant predictors. Furthermore, postop LV-GLS was associated with mortality (OR, 1.08; P = .013) and AF recurrence (OR, 1.16; P = .001).ConclusionsPreop and postop strain parameters, particularly LAVI, LV-GLS, and RV longitudinal metrics, provide substantial prognostic value. Divergent RV remodelling patterns support integrating routine echocardiographic strain assessment for longitudinal risk stratification.Keywords: aortic stenosis, transcatheter aortic valve replacement, surgical aortic valve replacement, left atrial volume index, LV global longitudinal strain, right ventricular function

INTRODUCTION

Following the introduction of the catheter-based transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) procedure in 2002,1 its use has grown rapidly and has replaced surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) in many patients. The earliest trials comparing TAVR to SAVR included patients considered to be either inoperable2 or at very high surgical risk,3 as defined by the Society of Thoracic Surgery as exceeding 8%.4 The indications for TAVR were subsequently expanded to include patients at intermediate surgical risk and eventually, to patients in the low-risk groups.5–9 However, legitimate concerns remain regarding the long-term durability, safety, and overall clinical effectiveness of TAVR valves, especially considering their potential broader application in younger, low-risk populations. Consequently, the Food and Drug Administration has mandated ongoing annual patient follow-up for up to 10 years as a condition for TAVR device approval.8,10

While conventional echocardiography remains the gold standard imaging modality for evaluating aortic stenosis (AS) severity, prosthetic valve function, and patient follow-up, it may fail to detect subtle, subclinical myocardial dysfunction following TAVR or SAVR procedures.9 To address the limitations of conventional echocardiography, we employed speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) for strain analysis to detect and compare changes in left ventricular (LV) and right ventricular (RV) regional and global function 1 year after either TAVR or SAVR procedures.

METHODS

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from our institution’s research database, obtained with written informed patient consent and approved by our Institutional Review Board (STU00012288). We calculated left atrial (LA), LV, and RV function using both standard echocardiography and advanced STE in 373 patients before and 1 year after undergoing either TAVR (n = 195) or SAVR (n = 178) between June 1, 2008 and December 31, 2018. All patients were in sinus rhythm at the time of their preoperative and postoperative strain calculations. In the original cohort, exclusion criteria included frame rates <40 frames per second, poor image quality unsuitable for STE, prior TAVR or SAVR, or patient refusal to participate, leaving 373 patients eligible for analysis (Figure S1). Because the primary end-point required paired studies, patients who died before follow-up or lacked usable follow-up imaging were excluded from the strain dataset.

Severe AS was defined using guideline criteria: aortic valve area (AVA) ≤1.0 cm2 or indexed AVA ≤0.6 cm2/m2, or haemodynamic severity (peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s or mean gradient ≥40 mmHg). Discordant low-flow, low-gradient cases were included if adjudicated as severe by the heart team. Valve model and size were categorized as balloon-expandable or self-expanding (Tables S1 and S2).

Speckle-tracking echocardiography was performed retrospectively using TomTec software by 2 experienced analysts, with manual region of interest (ROI) adjustment only when needed to optimize tracking. For LV-GLS, 2D greyscale images were obtained from parasternal long-axis and apical 4-, 3-, and 2-chamber views at ≥40 fps. Left ventricular function was measured by Simpson’s biplane method (LVEF and LVEDV). Left atrial size was assessed using LA volume and LAVI. Right ventricular function was evaluated using strain analysis and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) (Figure 1).

Echocardiographic right ventricular strain analysis showing an apical chamber image with right ventricular tracking, segmental time-to-peak mapping, and longitudinal strain curves used to assess regional contraction timing and dyssynchrony.

The primary end-point of this study was echocardiographic remodelling of the left and right cardiac chambers, assessed from preoperative baseline to 1-year follow-up using conventional echocardiography and speckle-tracking strain analysis. Secondary end-points included adverse clinical events (mortality, atrial fibrillation [AF] recurrence, and stroke), evaluated using multivariable logistic regression to identify relevant echocardiographic predictors.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using 2-sample t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages and compared with chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Paired comparisons of echocardiographic parameters before and after intervention were performed using paired t-tests. All P-values are reported to 3 decimals, with statistical significance set at a 2-sided P-value < .05.

Missing data were handled using an available-case (complete-case) approach with no imputation. The exact N for each variable is reported in the tables. Sample size reflected all eligible patients during June 1, 2008-December 31, 2018, with no a priori power calculation.

Logistic regression assessed echocardiographic predictors of postoperative clinical outcomes (mortality, AF recurrence, and stroke). Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, CHADS2VASC, baseline AF, prior myocardial infarction, and procedure type (TAVR vs SAVR). Models for AF recurrence included these covariates plus prior cardiac intervention. Sensitivity analyses further adjusted for baseline aortic mean gradient using a parsimonious covariate set. Propensity score matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were explored, but limited overlap and inadequate covariate balance (especially age) led to primary reliance on multivariable adjustment (Figure S2). Analyses were performed in SPSS (IBM Corp).

RESULTS

Preoperative comparisons between TAVR and SAVR patients

Preoperatively, TAVR patients were significantly older, had higher CHADS2 and CHADS2VASC scores, and greater burden of comorbidities (including diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, infectious endocarditis, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, prior myocardial infarction, and congestive heart failure) (Table 1). Most TAVR devices were balloon-expandable (Edwards Sapien), with fewer self-expanding valves (Medtronic CoreValve, Evolut) (Tables S1 and S2).

Standard echocardiography showed that preoperatively, TAVR patients had smaller LV end-diastolic diameters and end-diastolic dimensions, higher right ventricular fractional area changes, and bigger left atria with increased LA volume index (LAVI). Conversely, TAVR patients demonstrated lower preoperative TAPSE. The SAVR group had significantly higher incidences of severe aortic insufficiency and severe mitral insufficiency preoperatively.

Despite these baseline differences, preoperative mean aortic valve peak gradients, LV ejection fractions (LVEFG), and strain measures (LV-GLS, RV-GLS, right ventricular free wall strain [RV-FWS]) were similar between groups (Table 1).

Perioperative course

All TAVR procedures were elective, whereas the SAVR cohort included 9.5% urgent and 0.6% emergent cases (P < .001). There were no operative deaths in either group. The incidence of postoperative stroke or transient ischaemic attack was similar between groups (1.5% vs 1.1%, P = .723). Hospitalization averaged 2 days shorter in the TAVR group, and SAVR patients had more prolonged (>24 hours) ventilation and were readmitted to the intensive care unit more often than TAVR patients (1.5% vs 6.7%, P = .011). At discharge, TAVR patients were more often prescribed angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and ADP receptor inhibitors, but less often beta-blockers and warfarin (all P < .001). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated no meaningful between-group differences at 1 year for stroke-free survival (TAVR 97.8% vs SAVR 98.3%). However, by 5 years, SAVR was associated with higher freedom from stroke (95.8% vs 90.9%; log-rank P = .016) (Figure 2A). Similarly, AF-free survival was comparable at 1 year (TAVR 87.7% vs SAVR 87.5%) but favoured SAVR at 5 years (78.6% vs 65.0%; log-rank P < .001) (Figure 2B). Given the older age and differing baseline risk profile in the TAVR cohort, these long-term differences should be interpreted in the context of potential residual confounding.

From baseline to 1-year follow-up, both TAVR and SAVR showed favourable LA remodelling (Table 2). Baseline AS haemodynamics differed modestly between groups (Table 1), with a higher mean transvalvular gradient in SAVR (Table S3). Left ventricular systolic function (LVEF and LV-GLS) was overall stable, with LV-GLS improving only in SAVR (−15.6 ± 7.1% to −16.7 ± 3.7%, P = .032). Right ventricular responses differed by procedure: TAVR largely preserved TAPSE and RV-FWLS, whereas SAVR was associated with significant declines, consistent with greater RV deterioration (Table 2). Right ventricular global longitudinal strain worsened in both groups, with smaller absolute changes favouring TAVR in the mini-SAVR comparison (Table S4). Mean gradient reductions were greater after SAVR, although final gradients were slightly lower after TAVR (Table S3).

Associations between echocardiographic parameters and clinical outcomes

Multivariable analyses identified LAVI and LV-GLS as powerful predictors of adverse clinical outcomes. Each incremental increase in LAVI significantly elevated the risk of mortality (adjusted OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.02-1.05; P < .001), AF recurrence (adjusted OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02-1.06; P = .001), and stroke (unadjusted OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.002-1.05; P = .038, but did not persist adjusted P = .120) both pre- and postoperatively (Table 3, Table S5). Preoperative TAPSE emerged as an independent predictor of stroke, with each 1 mm decrease associated with a 12% increased risk (adjusted OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.02-1.23; P = .013) (Table S6). Absolute and relative decreases in LAVI post-procedure correlated with improved clinical outcomes, underscoring its prognostic value for atrial remodelling and stroke prevention. While preoperative LV-GLS (unadjusted OR, 1.07,; 95% CI, 1.02-1.13; P = .010) and TAPSE (unadjusted OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.91-1.0; P = .047) were associated with mortality in unadjusted analyses, they did not retain statistical significance after multivariable adjustment (adjusted P = .047 for left ventricular global longitudinal strain [LVGLS]; P = .260 for TAPSE). In contrast, postoperative LV-GLS remained associated with mortality (adjusted OR, 1.08; 95% CI, 1.02-1.15; P = .013). Postoperative RV-FWS (adjusted OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.01-1.08; P = .018) and TAPSE, whether assessed preoperatively or postoperatively, were not significant predictors of mortality in the adjusted models. These results underscore the value of LAVI and LV-GLS as non-invasive prognostic imaging markers for longitudinal risk stratification in patients undergoing aortic valve replacement. Preop and postoperative LAVI were independently associated with recurrent AF (preop: adjusted OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02-1.06; post: adjusted OR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.04-1.09; both P = .001). Worse postoperative LV-GLS also predicted AF recurrence (adjusted OR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.07-1.26; P = .001), and LV-GLS change from baseline remained predictive, supporting LV strain progression as a dynamic marker of arrhythmogenic remodelling. In contrast, postoperative TAPSE, RV-FWLS, RV-GLS, and changes in RV strain were not associated with AF recurrence (Table S5). Findings for baseline LAVI and follow-up LV-GLS were unchanged after additional adjustment for baseline aortic mean gradient (Tables S9 and S10). In tertile analyses, higher baseline LAVI was strongly associated with mortality (highest vs lowest tertile adjusted OR, 3.15; 95% CI, 1.77-5.61; P < .001), while worse follow-up LV-GLS showed a borderline association (adjusted OR, 1.77; 95% CI, 0.98-3.19; P = .057), both were associated with incident AF (P = .001).

For stroke, LAVI was significant unadjusted (OR, 1.03; P = .011) but borderline after adjustment (adjusted OR, 1.02; P = .050). Preoperative LV-GLS was not predictive of stroke. Postoperative RV-FWLS and RV-GLS were associated with higher stroke risk, while higher TAPSE was protective (all adjusted P ≤ .035). Importantly, greater reductions in LAVI were strongly protective: each unit absolute decrease (adjusted OR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.88-0.96; P = .001) and each percentage relative decrease (adjusted OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.93-0.98; P = .001) were associated with lower stroke risk (Table S6). Other adjusted RV change metrics were not significant.

In supplemental multivariable regression analyses, baseline RV strain was the strongest predictor of postoperative RV-FWS improvement, with worse preoperative strain associated with greater recovery. Surgical aortic valve replacement patients experienced more favourable RV strain improvement than TAVR, whereas diabetes and NYHA Class III or IV heart failure predicted less postoperative RV improvement. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion changes were driven mainly by baseline values. Surgical aortic valve replacement was associated with smaller TAPSE decline than TAVR, while female sex independently predicted greater TAPSE deterioration. Inverse probability of treatment weighting and propensity score matching did not achieve adequate covariate balance due to limited overlap between TAVR and SAVR populations. Therefore, primary analyses relied on multivariable adjustment (Figure S2).

DISCUSSION

This study offers a comprehensive echocardiographic analysis of the different impacts that TAVR and SAVR have on postoperative atrial and ventricular function in patients with severe AS. While global LV function remained preserved following both TAVR and SAVR, LV-GLS was shown to be an independent predictor of both late atrial AF recurrence and mortality. Lower preoperative LV-GLS is associated with adverse post-procedure outcomes, confirming that LV-GLS is a more sensitive indicator of subclinical myocardial dysfunction than LV ejection fraction alone and that it is more valuable as a risk marker for outcomes following aortic valve replacement. Our updated tertile analyses further demonstrate that patients in the worst tertile of postoperative LV-GLS face a 4.43-fold increased risk of AF recurrence, underscoring its utility for long-term risk stratification.

Both preoperative and postoperative LAVI also are robust, independent predictor of clinical outcomes, including mortality, AF recurrence, and stroke. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have identified LA enlargement as a surrogate marker of chronic diastolic burden, atrial myopathy, and elevated systemic risk. In TAVR-specific populations, LAVI has been particularly well established as a long-term prognostic biomarker. For instance, one large-scale study demonstrated that patients with a LAVI exceeding 48 mL/m2 had significantly higher mortality at 4 years post-TAVR, suggesting its utility in long-term risk stratification.11

LAVI appears to function as a non-invasive marker of cumulative left heart stress and atrial remodelling in both TAVR and SAVR patients. In SAVR patients, this prognostic relevance may reflect shared pathophysiologic pathways, including chronic pressure overload, LA fibrosis, and impaired chamber compliance.12 These findings support the inclusion of serial LAVI measurement into routine echocardiographic follow-up in patients undergoing either TAVR or SAVR. Future prospective studies should aim to refine clinical thresholds and timing for LAVI-based risk stratification, particularly in SAVR populations where current data are limited.

It should be acknowledged that although statistically significant improvements in LV-GLS post-SAVR and modest differences in LAVI between groups were observed, their clinical relevance is uncertain given the minimal magnitude and potential measurement variability. Nonetheless, their consistent predictive associations with mortality, AF recurrence, and stroke underscore their value as sensitive markers for cardiac remodelling and risk stratification.

The present study documents the important predictive capability of right heart and atrial remodelling for cerebrovascular events following TAVR and SAVR. Improvement in LAVI postintervention suggests that atrial remodelling after TAVR or SAVR may significantly reduce stroke vulnerability. Furthermore, we confirmed that right heart functional parameters can be critical independent predictors of stroke risk. Specifically, reduced preop RV-GLS and TAPSE were significant predictors of long-term outcomes. Previous meta-analyses have shown that RV systolic dysfunction is a potent independent predictor of 1-year mortality after TAVR, while other studies have found that postop RV failure is associated with a 3.6-fold increased risk of adverse outcomes, including stroke.13,14

The impact of TAVR vs SAVR on postoperative RV function was significantly different. While patients had relatively well-preserved TAPSE and RV strain parameters following TAVR, patients showed a greater deterioration of RV function following SAVR. These findings are consistent with earlier literature attributing post-SAVR RV dysfunction to loss of pericardial constraint, cardiopulmonary bypass, myocardial ischaemia, and atriotomy with cannulation injury. Retrograde cardioplegia during SAVR, although protective for the LV, is less protective of the RV free-wall, further compounding the risk of depressed postoperative RV function following SAVR.14–16 Postoperative RV dysfunction after SAVR may reflect surgical and pericardial effects rather than the valve intervention itself. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to minimally invasive SAVR, the direction of RV remodelling findings was similar (Table S9).

In contrast to many prior studies reporting uniformly favourable RV outcomes post-TAVR, our data revealed measurable RV strain decline in TAVR patients as well, albeit less severe than in SAVR. This subtle decline may reflect the increasingly recognized burden of post-TAVR permanent pacemaker implantation, especially in patients with pre-existing conduction disease or borderline RV performance.17–19

High-burden right ventricular pacing following permanent pacemaker implantation has been linked to mechanical RV and LV dyssynchrony, RV dilation, and reduced tricuspid annular excursion, culminating in pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.18,20–22 In addition, mechanical interference of pacemaker leads with tricuspid valve leaflets can contribute to or worsen tricuspid regurgitation, further undermining RV functional recovery.23 Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), coronary sinus, and leadless (Micra) systems were absent in our cohort, limiting device-type comparisons. In the paced RV dysfunction subgroup, observed QRS widening in a subset of patients is consistent with electrical dyssynchrony and may reflect standard RV pacing or underlying conduction disease. However, the small sample size precludes definitive inference. Our findings suggest that RV dysfunction post-TAVR may not be as benign as previously thought, particularly in patients who require permanent pacemaker implantation.

Despite its structural vulnerability, RV strain parameters (RV-FWLS, RVGLS) and TAPSE were not independently associated with mortality or AF recurrence in our multivariable models. Preoperative TAPSE, however, was significantly associated with stroke, suggesting that RV performance may still reflect cerebrovascular vulnerability, potentially through its effect on systemic forward flow or thrombotic propensity. Supplementary regression analyses revealed that SAVR was independently associated with greater TAPSE decline while TAVR was not. Notably, female sex and diabetes were associated with poorer RV functional recovery regardless of procedural type, in line with known sex- and metabolic-related vulnerability in RV remodelling.

Both procedures effectively reduced aortic valve gradients and preserved LV ejection fraction, but their impact on myocardial recovery differed. Surgical aortic valve replacement was associated with greater LV functional improvement but more pronounced RV deterioration, whereas TAVR maintained RV function better but had less LV remodelling and a higher incidence of pacemaker-related complications. Recognizing these differences can guide personalized procedural decision-making and postoperative management strategies to optimize clinical outcomes.

This retrospective, single-centre observational study limits causal inference, and residual confounding may remain despite multivariable adjustment. Use of 2D imaging and vendor-specific strain software may introduce measurement variability and limit generalizability. Limited sample size and approximately 1 year follow-up constrained subgroup and longer-term analyses. Early post-procedural echocardiography and effective orifice area (EOA) were not consistently available, preventing EOAi-based prosthesis-patient mismatch assessment. Excluding patients without paired analysable baseline and follow-up echocardiograms may have introduced selection bias. Left ventricular reservoir strain was not evaluated due to inconsistent availability of non-foreshortened apical 4- and 2-chamber views with adequate tracking at both time points. Larger multicentre prospective studies with standardized imaging and longer follow-up are needed.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, while statistically significant improvements in LV-GLS post-SAVR and modest LAVI differences between TAVR and SAVR groups were observed, the clinical significance of these subtle differences remains uncertain due to their minimal magnitude and potential measurement variability. Nonetheless, the consistent predictive associations of LV-GLS and LAVI with mortality, AF recurrence, and stroke highlight their utility as sensitive markers for cardiac remodelling and risk stratification following TAVR or SAVR.
