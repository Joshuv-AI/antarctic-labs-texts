Nanoparticles-enhanced CAR-T cell therapy: current advances and future directions
Authors: Zhan Wang, Guangyang Cheng, Wancheng Guo, Huixiang Chen, Zhaokai Zhou, Run Shi, Hua Gao, Huabing Li, Jiaqi Tu, Wenchong Chen, Shuai Yang, Hongzhuo Qin, Lulu Zuo, Qiong Lu, Ran Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy represents a revolutionary advancement in adoptive cellular immunotherapy, demonstrating remarkable efficacy against hematologic malignancies. However, its clinical application faces significant challenges: complex and costly production processes, limited therapeutic outcomes for solid tumors, and severe adverse reactions. Nanoparticles (NPs), with their tunable physicochemical properties, precise delivery capabilities, and multifunctional design, offer innovative solutions throughout the CAR-T therapy workflow. NPs enable more efficient and safer T-cell genetic engineering, enhance CAR-T cell homing and infiltration into solid tumors, actively remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and overcome antigen heterogeneity. Furthermore, NPs enable real-time monitoring of CAR-T cell spatiotemporal activity in vivo, opening novel avenues for therapeutic safety management. The review also explores the synergistic potential of artificial intelligence in optimizing NPs design, predicting therapeutic efficacy, and anticipating toxicity responses, laying the groundwork for developing more personalized NPs-based CAR-T therapy. Finally, we discuss the multifunctionality of NPs strategies in the other immune cell platforms, such as CAR-NK and CAR-macrophage cells.Keywords: Nanoparticles, Chimeric antigen receptor t cell, Tumor immunotherapy

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy is a cutting-edge adoptive cellular immunotherapy combining the high specificity of antibodies against antigens with the potent cytotoxic effects of T cells on target cells [1]. Through genetic engineering, T cells expressing CAR are generated, purified, activated, and expanded in vitro prior to reinfusion into patients, which endows them with the capacity to specifically target and eradicate tumor cells [2]. CAR-T therapy involves multiple critical steps, including baseline patient assessment, single-nucleated cell collection, bridging therapy, lymphodepleting chemotherapy, CAR-T reinfusion, adverse reaction management, and subsequent efficacy follow-up [3, 4]. CAR molecules are usually composed of four functional modules: the extracellular domain containing a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) for binding to antigens without relying on the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), the transmembrane domain for structural anchoring, a hinge domain, and the intracellular signal transduction domain [5, 6] (Fig. 1). Currently, the CAR-T cell therapy has advanced to the fifth generation (Fig. 1). The first-generation CAR-T cells only connected the scFv with the intracellular signaling domain of CD3ζ to achieve the basic T-cell activation, but they possessed limitations, including short-term efficacy and insufficient durability [7]. The second-generation significantly enhanced the T-cell expansion ability and survival time by introducing co-stimulatory domains such as CD28 or CD137 (4-1BB) [8]. The third-generation integrated multiple co-stimulatory molecules, significantly enhancing the anti-tumor activity, proliferation potential, and the persistence of T cells in vivo [9]. The fourth-generation, referred to as the T-cells redirected for generalized cytokine-mediated cytotoxicity, was characterized by the ability to express specific cytokines or immunomodulatory molecules, thereby actively remodeling the tumor microenvironment (TME) and recruiting endogenous immune cells [9, 10]. The fifth-generation further achieved dynamic response and more precise regulation of microenvironment signals [11, 12]. The latest fifth-generation CAR-T cell has two primary research directions: (1) The first strategy involves structural optimization based on second-generation CAR-T cells, which is achieved by enhancing the signaling capacity of cytokine receptors like IL-2Rβ and transcription factors such as STAT3 and STAT5 within intracellular domains, further improving the self-sustaining capacity, proliferation efficiency, and activation intensity of CAR-T cells [13, 14]. This design aims to leverage the cytokine-transcription factor axis to sustain T-cell functional expansion, thereby achieving more durable and potent anti-tumor immune responses; (2) The second strategy focuses on developing off-the-shelf CAR-T cells, with the core objective of engineering “universal” immune cells capable of recognizing multiple antigens and exhibiting broad-spectrum anti-tumor activity [15]. This approach achieves flexible expansion of antigen recognition through decoupling extracellular antigen recognition modules from intracellular signaling units, utilizing “third-party” systems such as biotin-binding immune receptor (BBIR) CAR or split, universal, and programmable (SUPRA) CAR [15–17]. Simultaneously, universal CAR-T cells often integrate gene editing technologies like CRISPR/Cas9 to knockout TCR/MHC, or related signaling pathway genes in allogeneic T cells, thereby reducing host rejection risks [18, 19]. Although this strategy shows significant potential for large-scale production and clinical application, the high technical complexity and stringent safety requirements mean it remains in the exploratory phase.

CAR structure and its evolution. The CAR consists of three domains: an extracellular domain, a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular domain. Second-generation CARs, built upon the first-generation design, incorporate one additional costimulatory molecule in the cytoplasmic domain. Up to now, seven CAR-T therapies have been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of hematological malignancies (Fig. 2) [20, 21]. Nevertheless, CAR-T cells still face numerous challenges in solid tumors, such as physical barriers like dense extracellular matrix (ECM) and abnormal vascular networks, severe adverse reactions, and immunosuppressive TME, all of which hinder CAR-T cells’ infiltration and function [22]. More disappointingly, CAR-T cells lack precise targeting, monitoring, and activation capabilities, further limiting their efficacy. The nanoparticles (NPs) system, composed of lipids and polymers, has the advantages of low toxicity, controllable cost, and easy functionalization design, offering innovative solutions to these challenges [23–26]. Through fine design of the material composition and surface properties, and regulation of the physicochemical properties for NPs, their biocompatibility and functional efficiency can be optimized. This provides innovative ideas for solving the current bottlenecks of CAR-T therapy: using NPs to improve gene delivery efficiency and reduce genetic toxicity to optimize the CAR-T cells preparation process, designing artificial antigen-presenting cells (APCs) mimics to promote T cell expansion, and even achieving in situ engineering modification of T cells in vivo [27–29]. For the treatment of solid tumors, NPs can deliver chemokines to improve immune cell infiltration, regulate the TME, and achieve multi-antigen co-targeting, thereby enhancing the enrichment ability and persistence of CAR-T cells. Moreover, NPs also help to monitor and spatially and temporally regulate the distribution and functional activity of CAR-T cells in vivo, providing new management strategies for reducing risks such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [30, 31] (Fig. 3).

Another CAR-T cell product targeting BCMA, Ciltacabtagene Autoleucel (Carvykti®), was approved in February 2022. Numerous studies indicate that over the past decade, NPs have demonstrated significant potential in the field of cancer immunotherapy through vaccine development and delivery of immunomodulators, suggesting that NPs hold promise as an effective strategy to address the various challenges faced by CAR-T cell therapy [26]. Herein, this review focuses on the prominent bottlenecks currently existing in the CAR-T cells manufacturing process, efficacy against solid tumors, and safety of the therapy. Currently, the rapid development of nanotechnology has provided a compelling approach to addressing these challenges. Various functionalized NPs with their controllable physicochemical properties, precise delivery capabilities, and multimodal synergy, demonstrate significant advantages in optimizing the preparation of CAR-T cells, enhancing tumor-targeted killing, and enabling visual control of the treatment process [32]. Additionally, we further explore how artificial intelligence (AI) technology can be combined with nanotechnology strategies to promote the construction of a personalized and precise treatment system, and provide an outlook on the technical challenges and development prospects in clinical translation.

Hurdles in CAR-T cell therapy

Although CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable anti-tumor effects, it still faces multiple challenges, such as inefficient manufacturing processes, limited efficiency of gene delivery, poor effectiveness against solid tumors, short persistence of CAR-T cells, and the potential for severe adverse reactions like CRS and ICANS [33, 34] (Fig. 4).

Hurdles of CAR-T cell therapy in treating solid tumors. A. Challenges of CAR-T cell manufacturing. Currently, the two primary methods for introducing CAR genes into T cells include: viral vector-mediated transduction techniques using γ retroviruses or lentiviruses, and non-viral transduction techniques like electroporation. Viral vector-mediated transduction techniques present challenges, including high immunogenicity, potential carcinogenic risks from random genomic integration, complex production processes, and limited payload capacity. Electroporation technology induces the formation of temporary pores in the cell membrane, potentially leading to membrane damage, cytoplasmic leakage, and cytotoxicity, subsequently affecting cell survival and function. B. Efficacy and persistence of CAR-T cells in solid tumors. Multidimensional challenges faced by CAR-T cells targeting solid tumors: (i) Physical and vascular barriers: the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) and dysregulated vascular system jointly impede T-cell infiltration and tumor homing; (ii) Host tumor microenvironment: metabolic features such as hypoxia, acidosis, and nutrient depletion, combined with the presence of numerous immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells, myeloid suppressor cells, and factors (e.g., TGF-β, adenosine), lead to CAR-T cell functional exhaustion and impaired proliferation; (iii) Tumor antigen heterogeneity: variation or loss of target antigen expression on tumor cells enables immune escape and diminishes the efficacy of single-target CAR-T cell strategies. C. Safety and monitoring concerns. CAR-T therapy faces numerous adverse events, including cytokine release syndrome, immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and on-target/off-target toxicity. Concurrently, current real-time in vivo monitoring capabilities are insufficient, making it difficult to track CAR-T cell distribution, persistence, and activity. This poses challenges for toxicity management and efficacy optimization

The current manufacturing procedure of CAR-T cells typically involves three core stages: T cell sorting, genetic engineering modification to achieve CAR expression, and in vitro expansion of CAR-T cells [35, 36]. This process requires strict quality management and corresponding infrastructure support as well as high capital investment. Introducing the CAR gene into T cells through viral or non-viral methods is the core of achieving engineered modification. Currently, viral vectors such as γ-reverse transcriptase virus and lentivirus are widely used due to their high gene transduction efficiency and stable genomic integration ability [37, 38]. However, their clinical application remains burdened by potential carcinogenic risks such as insertion mutations and immunogenicity, complex manufacturing processes, high production costs, limited loading capacity, and susceptibility to vector activity decline during storage and transportation [34]. Non-viral delivery technologies like electroporation form reversible pores on the cell membrane through instantaneous electrical pulses, thereby mediating the introduction of exogenous genes [39]. It has advantages, including better safety, flexible loading capacity, and suitability for mRNA transient expression. Nevertheless, the effect of this technology is affected by factors such as cell types and electric field parameters, which may cause membrane damage, cytoplasm leakage, and cytotoxicity, thereby interfering with the normal physiological state and gene expression profile of the cells [39, 40] (Fig. 4A). Therefore, developing new, efficient, and low-toxic delivery systems is of great significance for optimizing the CAR-T cells preparation process and promoting the clinical application.

Solid tumors severely limit the overall efficacy and persistence of CAR-T cells due to their unique biological characteristics [41] (Fig. 4B). Firstly, solid tumors often down-regulate chemokine expression, and the corresponding receptors on the T cell surface are insufficient, resulting in CAR-T cells being unable to effectively locate [42]. Secondly, solid tumors not only impede CAR-T cells’ infiltration and migration through physical barriers, but also their immunosuppressive TME characterized by hypoxia, acidity, nutrient deprivation, and the presence of abundant immunosuppressive factors, regulatory T cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, further significantly suppress CAR-T cells’ proliferation and accelerate functional exhaustion [43]. Finally, the heterogeneous expression or loss of tumor cells’ surface antigens may prevent single-target CAR-T cells from recognizing them, while designing multi-target strategies may increase the risk of off-target toxicity to normal tissues [44, 45].

CAR-T cell therapy also comes with a series of safety issues, including CRS, ICANS, and immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome (IEC-HS) [46] (Figs. 3 and 4C). Among these, CRS is mainly triggered by the massive release of inflammatory factors such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) by activated CAR-T cells, and can manifest as persistent fever, hypotension, and even multi-organ dysfunction [20, 47]. Currently, CRS can be treated with IL-6 receptor antagonists and glucocorticoids [48]. ICANS is related to the non-specific recognition of central nervous system antigens by CAR-T cells, which commonly show neurological abnormalities such as consciousness disorders and tremors [49, 50]. In addition to supportive treatment, new strategies such as interleukin-18 blockade and surface polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification of CAR-T cells, show regulatory potential [51–53]. IEC-HS mostly occurs in severe CRS patients, characterized by abnormal activation of macrophages and uncontrolled cytokine storm, which can be managed with glucocorticoids combined with etanercept [54, 55]. Moreover, CAR-T cell therapy may also cause graft-versus-host disease, off-target toxicity, allergic reactions, and potential insertional mutation carcinogenic risks [56]. Meanwhile, it lacks effective real-time monitoring methods. Therefore, how to balance efficacy and safety remains a key issue that needs to be addressed urgently in this field.

Integration of NPs and CAR-T cell therapy

NPs-cell interactions and design principles for gene delivery

The effective interaction between NPs and CAR-T cells is the foundation of successful genetic engineering, which is a precise delivery process driven by multiple chemical mechanisms [57]. This NPs system achieves precise targeting through ligand-receptor specific recognition, where the density and spatial configuration of ligands for key T-cell receptors such as CD3 and CD28 need to be finely regulated to achieve optimal binding efficiency and avoid non-specific activation [58, 59]. Before targeted contact, moderate cationic surface charges can promote cell membrane adsorption through electrostatic interactions, while PEGylation can balance the circulation time and targeting ability [60]. After CAR-T cells uptake NPs, unique intracellular microenvironment signals can achieve precise release of NPs through multiple pathways. pH-sensitive materials utilize the acidity gradient of endosomes to achieve the “proton sponge effect”, promoting NPs escape [61]; redox-sensitive disulfide bonds release NPs in the intracellular high glutathione environment [62, 63]; enzyme-sensitive peptide chains can respond to specific intracellular enzymes to achieve spatiotemporal controllable release of NPs [64, 65]. For mRNA that need to directly exert their functions in the cytoplasm, modification with membrane fusion lipids or cell-penetrating peptides can simulate the viral fusion mechanism, promoting direct cytoplasmic delivery and avoiding lysosomal degradation of NPs [66, 67]. The integration of these responsive mechanisms constitutes the chemical basis for achieving efficient endosomal escape and cytoplasmic release of NPs, providing a guarantee for the stable and efficient expression of CAR molecules.

The selection of NPs materials is the chemical basis for regulating delivery performance and reducing cytotoxicity. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are characterized by the design of protonable cationic lipids, such as derivatives of DLin-MC3-DMA and C12-200 [68]. These lipids are neutral at physiological pH, which become protonated and carry positive charges upon entering the acidic endosome, thereby disrupting the endosomal membrane through the “proton sponge effect” and efficiently releasing genetic material [68]. By precisely regulating the length and unsaturation of the lipid chain, the transfection efficiency of Jurkat T cells (Human-derived T-cell leukemia cell line) can be increased by an order of magnitude, and the long-term stable expression of CAR molecules can be achieved [69]. For polymer NPs, in order to balance transfection efficiency and cytotoxicity, cationic polymers such as polyethyleneimine and polylysine are usually modified with PEG or acetylation to reduce their charge density, which can reduce T-cell toxicity by more than 40% [70]. Degradable polymers like poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) and poly (beta amino esters) achieve controlled release and reduce cell accumulation toxicity through hydrolysis of ester or amide bonds [68, 70]. Inorganic NPs like Au NPs and silica NPs, have become emerging carriers due to their precise size control (10–500 nm) [71, 72]. The PEG modification can significantly improve their distribution stability. Lipka et al. found that compared to plain Au NPs, a prolonged blood circulation time was determined for Au NPs with 10 kDa PEG chains after intravenous injection [73].

NPs-enabled optimization of in vitro CAR-T cell manufacturing

Among the manufacturing procedures of CAR-T cells, T cell sorting and expansion stages are supported by relatively mature, commercially viable automated technologies, whereas gene transfection lags behind, representing the primary bottleneck in the preparation workflows [74]. The core task of gene transfection is to efficiently and stably introduce gene sequences encoding CARs into T cells, endowing them with the ability to specifically recognize and eliminate tumor cells. Transfection efficiency and stability directly determine the success or failure of CAR-T cell therapy. The traditional CAR gene delivery tools include viral vectors [75], electroporation technology [76, 77], and transposon systems [78]. Nanotechnology offers promising alternative or complementary strategies for CAR gene delivery. Compared to other gene delivery tools, NPs delivery systems demonstrate significant advantages. As proof: (1) enhanced gene transfection efficiency [79]; (2) reduced off-target gene expression risk [80, 81]; (3) overcoming vector limitations on gene fragment size [67]; (4) relatively simple production processes that facilitate standardization and scaling, thereby contributing to overall cost reduction [82]. Consequently, with the growing demand for CAR-T cell preparation, developing nanotechnology-based gene transfection strategies has become a critical research direction in CAR-T cell therapy.

NPs, with their material diversity, high stability, and payload flexibility, provide an efficient alternative for the in vitro or in vivo CAR-T cell engineering preparation, significantly enhancing production efficiency and clinical applicability [31, 83]. For in vitro transfection, ionizable LNPs have been applied for mRNA delivery to T cells [84]. LNPs exhibit multiple systemic advantages compared to traditional viral vectors (Table 1). In terms of manufacturing and safety, the chemical synthesis process of LNPs fundamentally resolves the complexity, high cost, and scale-up challenges associated with viral vectors’ reliance on biological packaging, and completely avoids the risk of insertional mutations caused by lentivirus random integration [85]. Furthermore, the mRNA delivery efficiency can be a 3-fold increase by optimizing the formulation, like the B10 formulation [86]. The CAR-T cells generated based on LNPs exhibit comparable anti-tumor efficacy to those produced by viral methods. The transient expression characteristic of mRNA provides a natural safety window and the possibility of repeated administration for treatment [86]. As emphasized by Li et al., the LNPs platform, which combines the simplicity of manufacturing, excellent safety, functional equivalence, and targeting flexibility, is driving the transformation of CAR therapy from complex “living cell drugs” to the scalable “genetic preparations” paradigm [87].

Comparison between viral vector and lipid nanoparticle in CAR-T therapy

The activation and expansion process of CAR-T cells in vitro still faces the problems of time-consuming and labor-intensive nature, which is an urgent need to establish a more efficient preparation system [88]. Recently, NPs have been developed as a new generation of artificial APCs platforms thanks to their good modifiability, which can significantly promote the in vitro activation and proliferation of CAR-T cells, thereby effectively shortening the preparation cycle [89] (Fig. 5A). Wu et al. developed a non-classical LNPs delivering reformative transposon system, with long-lasting CAR expression and potent cytotoxicity, achieving antitumor efficacy comparable to that by lentivirus [90]. This study provides a safe, facile, and effective approach for in vitro engineering CAR-T cells, which can avoid the safety concerns of viral vectors, lower the price, and shorten the manufacturing period of CAR-T cells. Moreover, in the cell sorting stage, the sorting technology based on the magnetic amplification effect of NPs can increase the purity of T cells to over 85% while maintaining over 90% cell viability [91, 92]. The CAR-T cells sorted and enriched by this method showed significant improvement in the anti-tumor ability, with significantly increased expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and cytokines like IL-6 and IL-10 [93]. Thus, the activation and sorting strategy guided by NPs provides a feasible technical path for optimizing the CAR-T production process and enhancing therapeutic effects.

NPs-mediated in vivo expansion of CAR-T cells

To circumvent the high costs and complex personalized preparation faced within in vitro production of CAR-T cells, T-cell engineering in vivo has emerged as a highly promising alternative strategy [94–96] (Fig. 5B). Among them, polymer nanoparticles (PNPs) and LNPs are two main non-viral delivery carriers [97]. Studies have shown that PNPs loaded with CAR-coding DNA can successfully transfect circulating T cells and induce anti-tumor effects comparable to those of in vitro prepared products in leukemia model mice [98]. Recently, it has achieved the generation and efficacy verification of in vivo CAR-T cells through the injection of a supramolecular hydrogel system based on cationic PNPs in xenograft mouse models [99]. LNPs are more commonly used for delivering mRNA, but their efficiency in directly transfecting T cells in vivo still needs to be improved [100]. By surface modification with targeting T cells, this process can be significantly improved. For example, LNPs modified with CD3 antibodies co-delivered with CAR mRNA and IL-6 shRNA can generate IL-6-deficient CAR-T cells in vivo, which can effectively kill tumor cells and reduce the risk of CRS [100, 101].

Compared with traditional PNPs or LNPs gene delivery methods, CAR-T-cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (CAR-T-MNPs) have shown enhanced accumulation in the tumor area and anti-tumor effects in both in vitro and in vivo experiments, marking the emergence of a new biomimetic therapeutic platform [102, 103] (Fig. 5C). CAR-T-MNPs possess multiple unique advantages due to their biomimetic design, whose membrane structure retains the CAR and natural adhesion molecules, achieving higher targeting affinity and specificity through multivalent and multi-targeted actions [104]. Meanwhile, the “self” markers in the membrane proteins, such as the widely studied CD47 protein, can effectively evade the clearance by the mononuclear phagocytic system, significantly prolong the circulation time in the body, and promote the accumulation of CAR-T cells within the tumor [105, 106]. Zhao et al. designed virus-like mimetic fusible nanovesicles that can efficiently fuse with circulating T-cell membranes, directly introducing anti-CD19 CAR gene into T cells, instantaneously generating functional CAR-T cells in the body [107]. At the same time, CAR-T cells themselves can also serve as a source of functionalized NPs materials. For example, Ma et al. prepared a novel nanomaterial by coating biomimetic nanoparticles with CAR-T cells membrane, which could recognize GPC3 expressed on the cell surface of hepatocellular carcinoma, providing high specificity for its photothermal therapy [102]. Yaman and colleagues developed a HER2-based CAR-T cell membrane-coated PNPs that can localize preferentially at tumor areas and significantly inhibit tumor growth in a subcutaneous lung cancer model in nude mice [103]. Moreover, the membrane itself of CAR-T-MNPs can serve as a multifunctional platform, with its lipid layer capable of loading drugs, and the membrane proteins like chemokine receptors, can be engineered to confer additional immunomodulatory functions, achieving synergistic treatment [108]. Despite the complex challenges in industrialization, CAR-T-MNPs have opened up new windows for developing high-precision and long-circulation targeted delivery systems.

Boosting the anti‑tumor potency and safety of CAR‑T cell therapy

Challenges in cell homing, immunosuppressive TME, and tumor antigen heterogeneity continue to impede the advancement of CAR-T tumor immunotherapy. To overcome these bottlenecks, researchers are actively developing multiple innovative strategies. Among these, NPs-based systems demonstrate significant potential through synergistic multi-mechanism approaches [109, 110]. Specific mechanisms include: (1) enhancing CAR-T cell targeting and infiltration capabilities within tumor tissues; (2) modulating the immunosuppressive TME; (3) limiting or overcoming tumor antigen heterogeneity; (4) mitigating or circumventing CAR-T therapy-related toxicities. These strategies could significantly enhance the efficacy and accuracy of CAR-T cells to treat solid tumors, delivering superior clinical benefits to patients (Table 2).

Three core strategies for enhancing the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy

Enhancing the directed aggregation of CAR-T cells at tumor sites

In clinical practice, intravenous infusion is the primary systemic administration route for CAR-T cell therapy [95]. Nevertheless, this poses a critical challenge of low cell delivery efficiency in solid tumor treatment. Following infusion, CAR-T cells must migrate through the systemic circulation to the tumor site and penetrate the vascular endothelium and dense ECM barrier. It has been indicated that only a small fraction of cells successfully infiltrate solid tumors, severely compromising therapeutic efficacy [111, 112]. To enhance the targeted accumulation and infiltration of CAR-T cells within tumor tissues, diverse local delivery strategies have been explored [113], such as intratumoral injection [114, 115] and local intraventricular infusion [116, 117], aiming to increase local cellular concentration and potency. Frustratingly, these strategies suffer from limitations imposed by tumor anatomical location and accessibility, making them difficult to generalize across all solid tumor types [95]. Consequently, significantly improving CAR-T cells’ infiltration efficiency within solid tumors and achieving precise targeting remain critical challenges. To address these issues, NPs were used to promote the targeted enrichment of CAR-T cells to the tumor site (Fig. 6A), demonstrating significant feasibility. A type of oxidoreduction-responsive human serum albumin NPs was designed to load IL-12, which was called IL-12 nano-stimulators (INSs) [118]. INSs can bind to CAR-T cells through a bioorthogonal reaction to construct an INS-CAR-T biological hybrid. In the TME, the reductive activity of CAR-T cells increases and triggers the responsive release of IL-12, thereby inducing the production of chemokines such as CCL2, CCL5, and CXCL10 in the tumor area, selectively recruiting and expanding CAR-T cells [118]. Furthermore, NPs can also reshape the TME through photothermal effects to promote tumor infiltration and antitumor activity of CAR-T cells [119] (Fig. 6B). Tang et al. developed a nanosystem that co-encoded the photosensitizer indocyanine green (ICG) and the TGF-β receptor inhibitor LY2157299 [120]. The photothermal effect of ICG can destroy the ECM of tumor cells, expand blood vessels, and promote the release of LY2157299, ultimately upregulating the expression of local chemokines CXCL9/10/11 and their receptor CXCR3 to facilitate the infiltration of CAR-T cells and achieve long-term tumor suppression [120, 121]. Additionally, researchers can also utilize magneto-acoustic manipulation technology to enhance the transportation efficiency of NPs-based CAR-T cells to the tumor site [122, 123] (Fig. 6C). Through combining magneto-acoustic manipulation techniques, immune magnetic particles carrying anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies can be combined with CAR-T cells to construct CAR-T-cell-based live microrobot (M-CAR-Ts) [122]. Under the guidance of the magnetic field, M-CAR-Ts can reversibly migrate towards the tumor periphery along the blood flow. Then, through the acoustic field drive, they can further penetrate into the deep part of the tumor. At the same time, the stimulation of anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies continuously promotes the activation and proliferation of CAR-T cells, significantly enhancing the tumor suppression effectiveness [122].

Through the local photothermal effect, the nanoparticles carrying LY2157299 not only can directly ablate the extracellular matrix of tumor cells, but also can trigger the responsive release of the LY2157299. LY2157299, as an effective inhibitor of the TGF-β signaling pathway, can upregulate the expression of key chemokines and the dilation of tumor blood vessels. Ultimately, the enhanced chemokine gradient and the improved vascular permeability work together to promote the directed migration and deep infiltration of CAR-T cells towards the tumor core. (C) Magneto-acoustic manipulation for spatially-targeted CAR-T cell delivery and activation. The magnetic-responsive nanoparticles are guided by the external magnetic field to be directionally enriched and retained in the tumor area. Meanwhile, focused sound waves are applied to the target site, exerting multiple functions: (1) enhancing the penetration and distribution of the nanoparticles-mediated CAR-T cells in the tumor stroma; (2) providing controllable mechanical or thermal stimulation to trigger the release of the loaded drugs; (3) regulating the tumor microenvironment to improve the accessibility of immune cells. This magnetic-acoustic cooperative manipulation can achieve spatially localized recruitment, expansion and activation of CAR-T cells at the tumor site, thereby enhancing their infiltration ability and reducing off-target effects and systemic toxicity

Remodeling the immunosuppressive TME

When CAR-T cells infiltrate the immunosuppressive TME, their proliferative activity and effector functions are often significantly constrained [117, 124, 125]. Currently, researchers have explored numerous NP-based strategies to modulate the immunosuppressive TME, including chemotherapy [126, 127], radiotherapy [128, 129], immune checkpoint blockade [130], photothermal therapy [131, 132], and ultrasound therapy [133]. These approaches could enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy by improving the immunosuppressive microecology, further demonstrating the significant potential of NPs in modulating the TME to boost CAR-T cell therapy. Dong et al. used CaCO3 NPs loaded with catalase to construct colloidosomal microreactors, which can simultaneously neutralize acidity and decompose hydrogen peroxide to alleviate hypoxia, reversing immunosuppression and enhancing the anti-tumor activity of CAR-T cells in breast cancer models [134]. In addition, Au/polydopamine NPs integrated with sonodynamic therapy and enzymatic catalytic functions can not only induce immunogenic cell death and alleviate hypoxia, but also inhibit tumor glycolysis by mimicking glucose oxidase and reduce lactate accumulation, thereby improving the metabolic environment and promoting the survival and function of CAR-T cells [135]. In addition to directly adjusting physical and chemical conditions, NPs can also deliver immunomodulatory drugs to block inhibitory signals. For example, PNPs co-delivering Toll-like receptor 7 and 8 (TLR7/8) agonists and phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase p110δ (PI3Kδ) inhibitors can promote M1-type macrophage polarization, reduce myeloid suppressor cells, and eliminate regulatory T cells, thereby synergistically enhancing the effect of CAR-T cells [136]. Similarly, NPs expressing an anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody and encapsulating IFN gene stimulator agonist cGAMP can accumulate in the lungs and deliver cGAMP through the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway to tumor tissues, promoting CAR-T cells infiltration and reversing T cells exhaustion [137]. Other immunomodulatory drugs, such as TGF-β inhibitors and A2a adenosine receptor antagonists, also demonstrate regulatory potential [57, 138–140]. To overcome the toxicity of systemic administration of some immunomodulatory agents like cytokines, the “cellular backpack” strategy based on NPs enables local and precise delivery of cytokines [141, 142]. Tang et al. developed the nanogels (NGs) that responded to the reduction potential and could specifically release the IL-15 agonist when CAR-T cells recognized tumor cells [143]. This method can significantly enhance T cell expansion within the tumor and avoid systemic toxicity. Additionally, Liu et al. directly couple anti-tumor cytokines to pre-marked T cell surfaces by using click chemistry [144]. The in vivo function of these T cells can be further enhanced, and the endogenous immune response can be activated. Another local strategy is to encapsulate CAR-T cells and auxiliary NPs together in a degradable hydrogel, achieving continuous local release of IL-15 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies, inhibiting tumor recurrence and preventing CAR-T cell exhaustion [145]. In summary, nanotechnology demonstrates tremendous potential and promise in modulating the immunosuppressive effects within TME. By precisely shaping the TME, nanotechnology can alter the interactions between tumor cells and immune cells, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor immune response.

Limiting tumor antigen heterogeneity

Tumor antigen heterogeneity is a key factor hindering the effectiveness of CAR-T cell therapy [146]. To address this challenge, NPs-based delivery systems have shown significant potential. By fusing the nanobodies (Nbs) targeting the tumor-specific antigen FGFR4 with granzyme B and connecting the complex to human ferritin (h-HFn), dual-targeting NPs can be constructed [147]. This complex not only uses the Nbs to specifically recognize FGFR4, but also combines with the CD71 receptor widely expressed on tumor cells through h-HFn, thereby achieving extensive coverage and efficient killing of tumor cells with different FGFR4 expression levels. Studies have shown that even in gastric cancer cells with low FGFR4 expression, the dual-targeting NPs can achieve effective targeting through CD71-mediated binding, significantly overcoming the treatment limitations caused by antigen heterogeneity [147]. Similar strategies have also been verified in liver cell carcinoma models [148]. By screening for high-affinity FGFR4-targeting Nbs and constructing corresponding CAR-T cells, these engineered cells still exhibit strong anti-tumor activity in vitro and in vivo experiments under inhibitory microenvironments and antigen downregulation conditions [148]. To further expand the application scope of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors, researchers are exploring strategies for in situ modification of tumors using exogenous antigens to overcome the limitations of target dependency [149, 150]. By utilizing fusogenic antigen-loaded NPs (F-AgNPs), the cationic lipid components can effectively fuse with the negatively charged and more fluidic tumor cell membranes, and promote the diffusion of antigens within the tumor through tumor-derived extracellular vesicles [149]. It is possible to achieve specific targeting reorientation of CAR-T cells and maintain the expression of exogenous antigens through dose regulation and repeated administration, finally establishing a universal CAR-T therapy that is not dependent on the tumor’s inherent antigens, and effectively addressing the challenge of antigen heterogeneity [150].

Meanwhile, integrating NPs with vaccine strategies has also provided new ideas for overcoming tumor heterogeneity by activating endogenous immune responses in CAR-T cell therapy [151–153]. Vaccines constructed with amphiphilic ligands and adjuvants can not only expands CAR-T cells but also promotes antigen diffusion by activating the endogenous immune system, enabling the recognition of more tumor epitopes [151]. Mechanistically, IFN-γ secreted by CAR-T cells can recruit dendritic cells (DCs) and promote their antigen uptake, while IL-12 produced by DCs in response to IFN-γ stimulation can further amplify the effector function of CAR-T cells, forming a positive feedback loop [151, 154]. Another study developed a nanovaccine that co-loaded TLR-9 agonist CpG ODN with tumor cell new antigens, which can efficiently promote dendritic cell maturation and pro-inflammatory factor secretion, synergistically inhibiting tumor growth through antibody-dependent cytotoxicity [155]. These combined strategies provide multi-modal solutions for enhancing the efficacy of CAR-T cells and overcoming antigen heterogeneity.

NPs can also synergistically enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T cells through various pathways. Firstly, NPs can serve as carriers to achieve the combination of CAR-T therapy with other immunotherapy strategies. Though co-delivering CAR mRNA and PD-1 siRNA through LNPs, the expression of PD-1 can be temporarily inhibited in T cells, reducing the risk of systemic immunosuppression while enhancing cell activity [156–159]. Moreover, NPs can also promote the synergy between CAR-T therapy, targeted therapy, and radiotherapy. Polymer NPs co-loaded with PI3Kδ inhibitors and TLR7/8 agonists can enhance the anti-tumor effect of local radiotherapy and promote adaptive immune responses [160]. Additionally, delivering the Fas gene to tumor cells through NPs can change the susceptibility of tumor cells, thereby enhancing the killing ability of CAR-T cells against tumors [161]. These multi-mechanism combined strategies provide new possibilities for expanding the application of CAR-T therapy and improving its efficacy.

Overcoming the side effects of CAR-T cell therapy

CRS, the common and major adverse reactions in CAR-T cell therapy, originates from the excessive activation of engineered T cells after recognizing tumor surface antigens via CAR, leading to the massive release of multiple inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, which triggers a systemic inflammatory response, clinically manifesting as high fever, chills, hypotension, dyspnea, and even multiple organ dysfunction [162–164]. Beyond CRS, on-target/off-tumor toxicity also poses significant safety challenges for CAR-T therapy in solid tumor treatment [165]. Several targets have been explored for solid tumors, including mesothelin, phospholipid-containing glycoprotein 3, Claudin 18.2, HER2, GD2, and EGFRvIII [166, 167]. These tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) are also expressed at low levels in normal tissues, potentially causing CAR-T cells to mistakenly attack normal tissues expressing the same antigens and thereby limiting their therapeutic window [167].

To overcome these safety concerns, nanotechnology offers multi-level regulatory strategies [168–170]. On one hand, NPs can be employed to intelligently regulate cytokine release, thereby mitigating CRS. Li and colleagues demonstrated that conjugating temperature-sensitive hydrogels with antibodies targeting the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 and administering them subcutaneously prior to CAR-T cells infusion significantly reduced IL-6 levels during CRS while preserving the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T therapy [48], enhancing the convenience of CAR-T cells and contributes to further advancing its clinical application. Moreover, Pfister et al. demonstrated that CAR-T cells loaded with magnetic nanoparticles achieve controllable targeting, simultaneously suppressing inflammatory cytokine release and altering tumor cell death mechanisms, which provided a foundation for developing site-specific immunotherapies targeting solid tumors while reducing systemic adverse reactions [169]. Similarly, other researchers have engineered the LNPs system surface-modified with CD3 antibodies, loaded with a plasmid containing a combination gene of interleukin-6 short hairpin RNA (IL-6shRNA) and a CD19-CAR (AntiCD3-LNP/CAR19 + shIL6). This system targets T cells via CD3 antibody-mediated binding, stably transfects them to generate IL-6 gene-knockdown CAR-T cells, thereby eliminating CD19-high leukemia tumor cells and mitigating IL-6-induced CRS [100]. On the other hand, NPs can be employed to achieve spatiotemporal control of CAR-T cells activity, thereby reducing off-target risks [171]. Nguyen et al. developed light-switchable CAR-T cells (LiCAR-T) incorporating imaging-guided, surgically removable upconversion nanoplates [172]. LiCAR-T cell enables real-time photoactivation of therapeutic T cells, achieving spatial and temporal control over T cell-mediated anti-tumor activity in vivo, allowing precise tumor elimination while significantly reducing side effects. Meanwhile, Wang et al. proposed the “NanoSwitch” strategy based on gelatinase-responsive NPs that could specifically release heterodimeric switch molecules within TME to activate CAR-T cells, demonstrating effective T-cell activation, tumor targeting, and growth inhibition in animal models, while also showing potential for reducing systemic toxicity [173]. Taken together, NPs offer multiple innovative approaches to enhance the safety of CAR-T therapy, promising to improve antitumor efficacy while reducing CRS and off-target toxicity, thereby advancing the clinical translation of CAR-T for solid tumor treatment.

Strengthen the monitoring and control of NPs-based CAR-T cell therapy

Real-time monitoring of CAR-T cells distribution and activity within the body could not only reduce the occurrence of severe adverse reactions like CRS, but also provide insights into the functional status of CAR-T cells [174]. Therefore, developing technologies capable of precisely tracking CAR-T cells is crucial for optimizing treatment safety and efficacy assessment. NPs provide an important opportunity for using clinical imaging techniques to monitor the location, distribution, and activity of CAR-T cells (Fig. 7). For instance, a physical method based on microfluidics can efficiently introduce ferrite NPs into CAR-T cells without the need for transfection agents to achieve cell labeling [175]. This innovative method can use photoacoustic imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and magnetic particle imaging (MPI) to non-invasively detect labeled T cells, thereby providing a new mean for monitoring CAR-T cells in solid tumors [175]. Similarly, dextran-coated iron oxide NPs can achieve a labeling efficiency of over 99% through a mild cell activation process, which has been used to trace the infiltration of CAR-T cells in intracranial gliomas [176]. Compared to traditional iron oxide NPs with certain risks of clinical adverse reactions, new tracers such as MegaPro-NPs have shown better safety in preclinical studies and can integrate MRI, MPI, and bioluminescence imaging to monitor tumor growth [177]. Additionally, by a heparin-protamine mediated labeling strategy, radioactive nuclide ⁸⁹Zr-labeled dual-modal positron emission tomography (PET)/near-infrared fluorescence silica NPs can be attached to the surface of CAR-T cells to achieve long-term and whole-body-scale dynamic tracking of the cell migration and distribution [178]. These NPs-based tracing techniques provide key support for in-depth understanding of the in vivo behavior of CAR-T cells, real-time visualization of the treatment process, and precise regulation.

Nanoparticles designed to track CAR-T cells in combination with various imaging techniques. Functionalized nanoparticles can serve as “contrast agents or tracers” for non-invasive, multi-modal imaging to monitor the distribution, migration, and activity of in vivo CAR-T cells: (1) Iron oxide nanoparticles enabling combined photoacoustic tomography, MRI, and MPI; (2) MegaPro-NPs, an advanced tracer supporting integrated MRI, MPI, and bioluminescence imaging; (3) Dual-modal silica nanoparticles labeled with the positron-emitting radionuclide zirconium-89 (⁸⁹Zr) and NIR fluorophores, enabling simultaneous positron emission tomography and NIR fluorescence imaging. These engineered nanoparticles can be internalized by CAR-T cells prior to infusion or conjugated in vitro. Upon injection into mouse models, the nanoparticles circulate with CAR-T cells, enabling longitudinal and systemic visualization of their migration to tumor sites. These nanoparticle-assisted imaging platform provides a critical tool for real-time spatiotemporal monitoring of CAR-T cell therapy. Frustratingly, integrating the NPs system into CAR-T manufacturing and bringing it to wide clinical application still faces multiple challenges. In terms of regulation and standardization, NPs are “combination products” and their complex physicochemical properties, such as size, encapsulation rate, and release kinetics, make the definition of qualified product quality and the establishment of standardized testing methods for NPs particularly difficult [179]. From the regulatory practices of various countries, India’s talicabtagene autoleucel program built a relatively efficient production and regulatory system; Brazil coupled a mature regulatory pathway with accredited centers and an emerging national platform for vectors and plasmids to form a collaborative management system; Mexico demonstrated the feasibility of hospital based closed system production under national oversight [180]. However, there is an urgent need to promote the collaboration between the industry and regulatory agencies to establish unified evaluation standards. Regarding the targeting delivery efficiency, T cells, as non-specialized phagocytic cells, possess low uptake efficiency, and NPs are easily cleared by the mononuclear phagocytic system within in vivo applications [181]. Therefore, the cutting-edge strategies should focus on developing intelligent carriers that integrate “invisible” long circulation design and conditional targeting functions, such as enzyme-responsive or pH-responsive release of T cell ligands, or exploring local administration to lymph nodes or tumor sites to enhance specificity [89, 182, 183]. Of note, traditional efficient transfection materials (some cationic lipids or polymers) may cause cell damage or inflammatory responses [184]. Thus, developing new bio-compatible materials is a key direction. Zhang et al. developed a class of lipids that mimic phospholipids, which achieved efficient T cell transfection while showing better biocompatibility [185]. More importantly, at the scale production level, the production of NPs involves a complex multi-component assembly process, and their key quality attributes, such as particle size, encapsulation rate, mRNA integrity, and target ligand density, must be strictly controlled and maintained consistency between batches [186]. Compared with traditional biological products, it requires higher standards for aseptic assurance, stability (especially for LNPs-mRNA preparations in long-term freezing and transportation), and rapid aseptic testing [184]. Therefore, a full-chain quality control system from raw materials for NPs to finished products must be established. Engineered production of NPs-based CAR-T will rely on the innovation of production technologies, such as adopting continuous flow production processes to improve efficiency and control accuracy, developing automated, closed production modules to reduce contamination risks and human operational errors, and applying process analytical techniques to monitor key parameters in real time [187]. Taken together, establishing industry consensus and quality standards for the application of NPs in CAR-T cell therapy will form the foundation for ensuring CAR-T cell quality and facilitating regulatory acceptance.

Artificial intelligence-powered NPs-based CAR-T cell therapy

AI is an important branch of computer science, aiming to enable computers or their controlled devices to possess the complex task-execution capabilities that simulate human intelligence [186, 188, 189]. The deep integration of AI with NPs is driving the transformation of CAR-T cell therapy into a precise medical system integrating multiple technologies [190]. The AI-nanotechnology aims to optimize multiple aspects of CAR-T cell therapy, including manufacturing, in vivo efficacy, and dynamic monitoring, breaking through stumbling blocks for the application of NPs-based CAR-T in solid tumors.

Optimization of CAR-T cells and NPs manufacturing driven by AI

NPs are the key delivery vehicles for mRNA-mediated T-cell CAR expression. Frustratingly, their large-scale production faces challenges in maintaining size, charge, and component stability [191]. Moreover, batch variations can affect cell uptake and gene transfection efficiency [192]. Real-time monitoring combined with ML technology can optimize synthesis parameters, reduce production variations, and enhance the scalability of NPs formulation and CAR-T cells manufacturing [193].

As a gradient boosting algorithm based on decision trees, XGBoost iteratively builds models to correct errors while balancing accuracy and complexity through regularization, achieving remarkable results for optimizing NPs production and delivery efficiency [194]. Maharjan et al. developed different mRNA-LNPs formulations while XGBoost/Bayesian optimization and self-validated ensemble were used to optimize the process and predict lipid mix ratio, enhancing the efficiency of NPs manufacturing [195]. Recently, a study developed an ultrafast flow-dialysis module, which was autonomously controlled by leveraging a multi-objective Bayesian optimization algorithm [196]. This AI-assisted rapid screening of multi-step parameters accomplished the swift manufacturing of both Doxorubicin-loaded LNPs and PNPs. Additionally, Ma and colleagues constructed an interpretable XGBoost-SHAP model based on tumor genomic mutations and NPs properties, which could predict the delivery efficiency of NPs reaching 54%- 75% [194].

The production of CAR-T cells is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly, severely limiting their wide clinical application. By combining AI with automation technology to build an intelligent CAR-T cell manufacturing platform, the cost-effectiveness and stability of production can be enhanced [197]. Moreover, AI-revolution could also extend to CAR-T cells manufacturing with smart bioreactors equipped with nanosensors, enabling precise, real-time monitoring and adaptive control of culture conditions [197, 198]. To address the challenge of medium optimization caused by donor T cell heterogeneity, Grzesik et al. innovatively combined the ML pipeline with experimental design, constructed and trained a highly accurate prediction model by using the survival rate and expansion efficiency data from the T cell culture process of multiple donors [199]. On this prediction model, they screened out the optimal medium formulations suitable for different T cell donors, successfully achieving a one-time optimization of the T cell culture medium, and significantly improving the quality and stability of T cell culture in heterogeneous biological material processing systems.

The application of AI models has optimized the design and management of CAR-T cells and NPs. Platforms like TumorScopePredict can accurately predict and simulate anti-tumor behavior and treatment responses of CAR-T cell therapy by generating dynamic three-dimensional tumor models and combining pre-treatment imaging data processed by convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [200–202]. The adaptability of AI ensures that these models can continuously iterate with new data and remain at the forefront of cancer research.

AI-nanotechnology-enabled CAR-T cell therapy to remodel TME

The major challenge in CAR-T therapy for solid tumors is to overcome the physical barriers and immunosuppressive TME [203]. Nanotechnology can address this issue by reshaping the TME, degrading the ECM, and reprogramming immune suppressive cells like regulatory T cells to cope with it. Meanwhile, AI-driven strategies can optimize the physicochemical properties of NPs, enhance tumor targeting, and reduce systemic distribution [204]. Mi et al. constructed a deep neural network model based on the dataset from Nano-Tumor Database, which accurately predicted the delivery efficiency of NPs in tumors and key organs, thereby promoting the optimization of NPs [205]. Using the collection of large data sets of 403 data points incorporating both numerical and categorical features, Yousfan and colleagues developed a linear mixed-effect model to predict the biological distribution of NPs in specific target tissues of the brain and the accumulation in non-target organs [206]. The model was validated using two different drug delivery routes, such as nasal and intravenous, and ultimately demonstrated excellent predictive accuracy. Interestingly, a study has designed an mRNA nano-vaccine by intelligent design, which can achieve efficient targeted delivery of mRNA antigen and cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate to targets [207]. It activates interferon gene stimulator, promotes the presentation of mRNA-encoded antigens, and enhances anti-tumor immunity in vivo, thus inhibiting tumor growth and ensuring long-term survival of tumor-bearing mice. These studies further highlight the potential of AI-driven nanotechnology in CAR-T cell therapy.

AI optimizes the properties of NPs, enhances their targeted delivery, and combines strategies such as magnetic guidance to overcome the obstacles in CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors, achieving stable and precise therapeutic effects [190]. Akhtar et al. demonstrated that ML-enhanced magnetic targeting could significantly enhance the precision and effectiveness of NPs-mediated drug delivery, offering a novel paradigm in cancer treatment strategies [208]. Furthermore, innovative strategies such as M-CAR-Ts by modifying CAR-T cells with immunomagnetic beads, can achieve precise targeting and deep tumor infiltration through sequential magnetic-sonic drive, promote the proliferation and activation of CAR-T cells, and significantly enhance anti-tumor efficacy [122]. Similarly, functionalized CAR-T cells with superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs can achieve the enrichment of CAR-T cells in the tumor area through magnetic control technology, while also inhibiting the release of inflammatory cytokines and effectively reducing systemic adverse reactions [169]. Taken together, the combination of AI-driven predictive modeling and advanced NPs engineering can unlock the potential of CAR-T cells in treating solid tumors.

AI-enhanced real-time monitoring and toxicity detection of NPs-based CAR-T cells

In the field of CAR-T cell therapy, by integrating clinical data and imaging techniques such as MRI, computed tomography, and PET, an AI-driven multi-modal network model can successfully predict treatment outcomes [209–211]. The real-time tracking of CAR-T cell distribution in the body using contrast agents not only enhances tumor targeting of CAR-T cells but also makes it possible to conduct real-time assessment of treatment responses and precise analysis of tumor features [169]. CNNs trained on NPs-enhanced imaging data are expected to further optimize non-invasive CAR-T cell tracking strategies, such as ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI for iron oxide- or gold-labeled CAR-T cells, which can provide strong support for real-time clinical decision-making and promote the development of cancer monitoring towards automation and precision [175, 212, 213]. The dynamic tracking of CAR-T cells is crucial for evaluating treatment efficacy and ensuring patient safety [174, 214–216]. The RCMNet model, integrating the CNNs and multilayer perceptron trained with peripheral blood cell datasets, realizes real-time automated analysis of CAR-T cells, significantly improving the ability to identify CAR-T cells [217].

Timely identification of NPs-related adverse reactions can further ensure the efficacy and safety of NPs-based CAR-T cell therapy. Nanoelectronic biosensors like silicon nanowire field-effect transistors can precisely monitor the expression levels of cytokines and toxicity markers, providing a solid basis for timely clinical intervention [218]. The high-precision data generated by these biosensors can further optimize AI-driven prediction models, facilitating the early prediction of adverse reactions and the formulation of individualized treatment protocols. Moreover, ML algorithms can integrate the physicochemical properties and exposure conditions like dosage, duration, and target tissue type of NPs, to accurately predict NPs-related toxicity [219, 220]. The construction and application of these prediction models can significantly enhance the safety assessment of NPs, providing support for proactive risk management and the implementation of individualized treatment adjustments [220]. Mitigating the adverse reactions of CAR-T cells while reducing the side effects of the NPs themselves to achieve improved therapeutic efficacy remains an urgent challenge. Promisingly, the integration of AI may hold the fundamental solution to this problem.

The versatility and engineering capabilities of NPs enable their applications to extend far beyond CAR-T cells. Within diverse cell platforms such as CAR natural killer (CAR-NK) cells, CAR macrophages (CAR-M), and CAR natural killer T (CAR-NKT) cells, the NPs strategy demonstrates unique value due to the adaptable flexibility [88, 221] (Table 3).

NK cells, a type of innate cytotoxic lymphocyte, can rapidly kill non-self cells and directly recognize target cells without MHC [222]. Unlike CAR-T cell therapy, NK cells have a unique cytokine secretion profile and are less likely to induce adverse reactions such as CRS and ICANS. Moreover, NK cells can be activated to kill tumor cells through engagement or disengagement of the activating and inhibitory killer Ig-like receptors on the cell surface in addition to the CAR pathway [223]. Therefore, CAR-NK cell therapy is currently being widely explored as an ideal alternative to adoptive cell therapy. However, the transfection effect of viral vectors in NK cells is much lower than that in T cells. In recent years, some studies have attempted to enhance the transfection efficacy of CAR-NK cells through NPs [224]. McKinlay et al. developed the charge-altering releasable transporters (CARTs) as an effective mRNA delivery system [225]. Wilk et al. confirmed that compared with electroporation, CARTs are more efficient in transfecting NK cells, better at maintaining cell viability, and possess the least impact on the reconfiguration of NK cells’ phenotype and function [226]. Multifunctional NPs (MF-NPs) have also been engineered for CAR-NK therapy, demonstrating superior stability and transfection efficiency compared to viral vectors or electroporation [227]. Furthermore, in the mouse model, the NK cells labeled with MF-NPs could be successfully traced using fluorescence imaging and MRI, enabling real-time monitoring of the intracellular distribution of CAR-NK cells [227]. In addition to improving delivery performance, NPs can significantly enhance the anti-tumor ability of CAR-NK cells by constructing cell composite systems. Zhang et al. demonstrated that the mesenchymal stem cell-NK cell complex mediated by LNPs, formed a cell composite delivery system with tumor-tending properties, which not only achieved long-term local supply of IL-15 but also significantly enhanced the infiltration and sustained functional activation of NK cells in solid tumors [228].

CAR-M therapy refers to the delivery of specific CAR genes to macrophages, with the ability to bind to the tumor cell surface through specific antigen identification and subsequently activate macrophage activity against tumor cells [229]. Compared with CAR-T cell therapy, CAR-M cells have significant and unique advantages in terms of in vivo transport efficiency and the ability to penetrate and infiltrate the immunosuppressive TME [230]. Macrophages have extremely strong chemotactic migration ability, which can specifically respond to the chemotactic factors secreted by tumors to complete directional recruitment. At the same time, macrophages can penetrate the ECM barrier of solid tumors and efficiently infiltrate into the deep part of the tumor [230]. In addition, CAR-M can restructure the local TME by phagocytosing inhibitory cells and secreting pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-12, while it is not easily inactivated by inhibitory signals and can maintain a stable functional state in the TME [221, 230]. Due to the complex and time-consuming nature of the traditional CAR-M preparation process, along with the high cost and the potential carcinogenic risk from the viral vectors, the NP-based CAR-M strategy demonstrates significant advantages [88]. Kang et al. reported a strategy for in vivo programming of CAR-M1 macrophages by using nanocomplexes for CAR gene delivery [231]. In vivo injected nanocomplexes of macrophage-targeting nanocarriers and CAR-interferon-γ-encoding plasmid DNA induce CAR-M1 macrophages that are capable of CAR-mediated cancer phagocytosis, anti-tumor immunomodulation, and inhibition of solid tumor growth. Similarly, Ye et al. engineered CAR-M by using LNPs and CAR mRNA [232]. CAR-M exhibited significant cytotoxic effects on B lymphoma in vitro, underscoring the great potential of mRNA-engineered CAR-M therapy based on NPs.

NKT cells possess both the antigen-specificity of adaptive immunity and the rapid response capability of innate immunity, which can generate an efficient response to glycolipids or lipid antigens [233]. After ligand recognition, NKT cells can rapidly secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, as well as anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13. They can directly target and kill tumor cells, or indirectly regulate the anti-tumor immune response by remodeling TME [234]. Based on the natural immune advantages of NKT cells, the potential of the CAR engineering modification strategy in the treatment of solid tumors has also been gradually explored. Xu et al. developed NKT cells coexpressing a GD2-Specific CAR and IL15, showing enhanced in vivo persistence and anti-tumor activity against neuroblastoma [235]. Research conducted by Heczey and colleagues also confirmed that GD2-CAR NKT cells could precisely target and accumulate at the tumor site, effectively inducing anti-tumor activity without graft-versus-host disease [236]. It is worth noting that the IL-6 secretion level of NKT cells was significantly lower than that of CD8+ T cells after CAR transfection [237]. This characteristic provides a possibility for reducing the risk of CRS in the CAR-NKT therapy. To further enhance the preparation efficiency and functional activity of CAR-NKT cells, the intervention of NPs technology provides a promising optimization path for them. By modifying the CD1d ligand or the antibody against NKT cell receptors, NPs can specifically recognize and accumulate on the surface of NKT cells, thereby delivering the mRNA of the CAR gene, and generating or enhancing CAR-NKT cells in vitro [238, 239]. Moreover, as an ideal carrier for lipid antigens, nanoparticles can jointly present the CD1d molecule, strongly activating the cytotoxicity and immune regulatory functions of endogenous NKT cells, forming a synergistic effect with the CAR strategy [238]. Taken together, NPs are not limited to the engineering production of CAR-T cells. It can also be applied to other immune cells through means like gene delivery and functional regulation, developing novel anti-tumor therapies, and providing promising ideas for the diversified development of tumor immunotherapy.

Conclusion

NPs achieve efficient, targeted delivery of CAR genes through material diversity and intelligent response design, while significantly reducing safety risks and production costs. In vitro preparation demonstrates that NPs not only enhance gene delivery efficiency as non-viral transfection tools but also accelerate T-cell activation and expansion by mimicking artificial APCs or integrating activation signals. Magnetic sorting technology further improves cell purity and quality, optimizing overall preparation efficiency and controllability. Furthermore, NPs support in vivo CAR-T cell generation and functional regulation. Through targeted modification or membrane fusion strategies, NPs enable in vivo delivery of CAR genes or proteins, directly modifying T cells within patients and circumventing the complexity and high costs of traditional ex vivo preparation. To enhance antitumor efficacy, NPs synergistically amplify effects through multiple mechanisms: (1) Facilitating targeted migration and deep infiltration of CAR-T cells into tumor sites via chemokine delivery, photothermal effects, and magnetoacoustic drive; (2) Reversing immunosuppression by modulating the TME to enhance CAR-T cell survival and function; (3) Address tumor antigen heterogeneity through multi-targeted design, antigen epitope expansion, or exogenous antigen delivery, broadening therapeutic scope of CAR-T cell; (4) Integrating controlled-release systems to achieve spatiotemporal regulation of CAR-T activity, enhancing targeted killing while reducing off-target toxicity. Regarding safety management, NPs offer novel pathways for monitoring and intervening in CAR-T cell-related adverse events. As imaging probes, NPs enable real-time, long-range, multi-modal tracking of CAR-T cells in vivo, providing evidence for treatment evaluation and dose optimization. Moreover, the integration of AI propels NP-based CAR-T cells toward intelligent and personalized development. AI can optimize NPs composition and performance through big data modeling, predict in vivo delivery efficiency and distribution, support automated production to enhance preparation stability and economies of scale, and enable early warning and dynamic regulation of treatment response and toxic side effects via multi-modal data analysis. Promisingly, NPs strategies have successfully expanded to other immune cell platforms, including CAR-NK, CAR-M, and CAR-NKT cells, further enhancing the potential of these cells in tumor infiltration, immune regulation, and sustained anti-tumor activity, which demonstrated the broad applicability of NPs in cellular immunotherapy. Looking ahead, the ongoing convergence of nanotechnology, genetic engineering, and AI positions NPs to play an increasingly pivotal role in enhancing CAR-T efficacy, expanding indications, mitigating toxicity, and enabling tailored medicine. This AI-driven NPs-based immune cell therapy will ultimately propel tumor immunotherapy into a new personalized medicine era characterized by greater precision, safety, and accessibility.
