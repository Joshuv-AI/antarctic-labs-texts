Associations between the triglyceride‒glucose index and the odds of low muscle mass and sarcopenia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ming Xu, Ziang Liu, Jing Zhou, Jianing Lv, Qin An, Yi Zhang, Yunfeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass and function, is a public health concern linked to insulin resistance (IR). The triglyceride‒glucose (TyG) index, a marker of IR, may reflect a decrease in muscle health.MethodsThis meta-analysis, which adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, reviewed studies from electronic databases investigating the association between the TyG index and low muscle mass or sarcopenia. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated via a random effects model.ResultsTwenty cross-sectional studies involving 521,778 participants were included. A higher TyG index was associated with higher odds of low muscle mass (OR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.10–1.50) and sarcopenia (OR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.29–2.07). Dose‒response analysis revealed that for every 1-unit increase in the TyG index, the odds of low muscle mass were 1.79 times higher (OR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.60–2.00).ConclusionThe TyG index is linked to higher odds of low muscle mass and sarcopenia, highlighting its potential as a screening tool. However, due to the cross-sectional design of the included studies, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02250-w.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Muscle mass, Triglyceride–glucose index, Meta-analysis, Dose–response meta-analysis

Introduction

Sarcopenia, characterized by the progressive and generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function, is a critical public health concern, particularly among older adults [1, 2]. This age-related condition significantly impacts an individual’s quality of life by reducing mobility, increasing the risk of falls and fractures, and leading to a decline in functional independence [2–4]. In addition to its physical manifestations, sarcopenia is also associated with a higher incidence of metabolic and cardiovascular disorders, cognitive impairment, and increased mortality [3–5]. The global prevalence of sarcopenia is considerable and increasing, underscoring the urgent need for effective prevention and intervention strategies [6].

Emerging evidence suggests a strong bidirectional relationship between sarcopenia and metabolic dysregulation, particularly insulin resistance (IR) [7]. IR, a pathological state characterized by the reduced responsiveness of target tissues (such as skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue) to insulin, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and other cardiometabolic diseases [8–10]. Skeletal muscle is a primary site of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal, accounting for a significant portion of whole-body glucose uptake [10, 11]. Impaired insulin signaling in muscle cells can lead to reduced glucose utilization, contributing to hyperglycemia and a vicious cycle of worsening IR and muscle dysfunction [8, 9, 12, 13].

The triglyceride‒glucose (TyG) index, which is derived from fasting triglyceride and fasting glucose levels, has emerged as a simple, reliable, and cost-effective surrogate marker forIR [14–17]. Unlike more complex methods such as the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (the gold standard for assessing IR) or the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), the TyG index is readily calculated from routinely available laboratory parameters, making it highly accessible for large-scale epidemiological studies and clinical screening, especially in resource-constrained settings [15–19].

Given the established link between IR and sarcopenia and the utility of the TyG index as a reliable marker of IR, investigating the comprehensive association between the TyG index and sarcopenia or low muscle mass is of significant clinical and public health importance. While individual studies have explored this relationship, a robust and comprehensive meta-analysis is required to synthesize the existing evidence, provide a quantitative estimate of the association, explore sources of heterogeneity, and delineate the potential clinical implications. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate the associations between the TyG index and low muscle mass and sarcopenia, contributing to a deeper understanding of the interplay between metabolic health and muscle integrity.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

This meta-analysis was designed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251115407), as detailed in Table S1. We conducted a literature search in electronic databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, covering articles from the initiation of the project until July 3rd, 2025. To ensure comprehensiveness, we employed specific keyword combinations tailored to each database’s syntax, focusing on terms for the exposure (e.g., variations of “triglyceride-glucose index”) and outcomes (e.g., “sarcopenia” OR “low muscle mass”). The full search strategy is outlined in Table S2-5. In addition, it should be noted that Google Scholar was used as a supplementary tool to identify potential grey literature or articles not captured by the primary databases, however, the CRIS literature database was not searched.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: (1) studies calculated the TyG index; (2) clear diagnosis of low muscle mass or sarcopenia; (3) studies reporting the impact of the TyG index on low muscle mass or sarcopenia using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); (4) articles in English; (5) observational studies; (6) studies involving human participants; and (7) exclusion of certain types of studies, including duplicate publications, reviews, and conference abstracts. Two independent reviewers assessed relevant studies, extracted eligible data, and resolved discrepancies through discussion. The 29 full-text articles that were excluded after eligibility assessment and specific reason were provided in Table S6.

Data extraction

Relevant data were systematically extracted from the studies, including the first author, publication year, country, study design, participant characteristics, sample size, mean age, sex, diagnosis of low muscle mass or sarcopenia, adjusted confounding factors, TyG index, adjusted overall association estimates, and subgroup association sizes (e.g., region, TyG index analysis, sex, age, body mass index, diabetes status, low muscle mass or sarcopenia diagnosis), along with their corresponding 95% CIs.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias in each study was assessed by two reviewers via the Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS) [20]. A study was considered to have a low risk of bias if its score was above 6. The NOS consists of three components: selection, comparability, and exposure. A maximum of one star can be awarded for selection and exposure, whereas the comparability component can earn up to two stars [21]. Additionally, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system was employed to appraise the quality of the evidence in the articles [22]. The articles were rated with four quality levels: high, moderate, low and very low. Any discrepancies in the quality assessment scores between the two independent reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. A third reviewer was available for arbitration if an agreement could not be reached.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via Stata 15.1 and RStudio 4.5.0. Multivariable-adjusted ORs were used to estimate the association size along with 95% CIs. A random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2 statistic. Heterogeneity was considered significant if p < 0.05. The degree of heterogeneity was classified as low, moderate, or high based on I2 values of 25, 50, and 75%, respectively [23]. Sensitivity analysis, meta-regression, and subgroup analysis were performed to identify sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression and subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of the following factors: region (Asia vs. US & UK), TyG index (continuous vs. categorical), sex (male vs. female), age (<60–65 years vs. ≥60–65 years, <40 years vs. ≥40 years), body mass index (BMI, non-overweight vs. overweight and obese), diabetes status (yes vs. no), and methods of diagnosing low muscle mass or sarcopenia. For the subgroup analysis by age, we were constrained by the data stratification reported in the included primary studies. Therefore, instead of using a single pre-specified cut-off, we grouped studies based on the age thresholds they provided (e.g., <40 vs. ≥40 years; <60–65 vs. ≥60–65 years) to maximize the inclusion of available evidence. This approach, while not uniform, was the most rigorous and comprehensive method possible given the nature of the available data. In response to substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%) observed in the initial analysis of the low muscle mass (LMM) and sarcopenia diagnostic criteria, we conducted a secondary nested subgroup analysis. Specifically, studies utilizing the ASM/height2 or AWGS 2019 criterion were further stratified based on the inclusion or exclusion of BMI or blood lipids as an adjustment variable in their statistical models to investigate their role as a potential effect modifier.

Funnel plots, Egger’s test, and the trim-and-fill method were used to assess potential publication bias [24, 25]. Furthermore, a dose‒response analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the TyG index and low muscle mass or sarcopenia. We employed the generalized least squares for trend (GLST) method described by Greenland & Longnecker to perform the dose-response analysis [26]. In this analysis, the mean or median values provided in the studies were used as estimates for the corresponding range. For studies reporting only ranges, the average of the high and low ends of the range was used as the estimate. In cases where the range was open, TyG index values were divided into four equal intervals, and the estimates for the first or fourth interval were calculated by subtracting or adding the difference between the second and third interval estimates. Importantly, all estimates were contained within their respective intervals.

Results

A total of 2,251 relevant articles were identified through the search strategy across electronic databases, categorized as follows: 325 from PubMed, 320 from the Cochrane Library, 400 from Web of Science, and 1,206 from Google Scholar. Duplicates were removed using EndNote software, eliminating 699 records and leaving 1,552 unique articles. Following a review of titles and abstracts, and excluding irrelevant articles, as well as studies on animals, cell research, reviews, letters, case reports, and conference papers, 49 full-text articles were retained for eligibility assessment. By full-text review, 29 studies were eliminated for the following reasons: no results of interest (n = 27) and unsatisfactory data (n = 2). Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process. After evaluation, 20 studies (11 of LMM, 7 of sarcopenia, 2 of both, involving 521,778 participants) met the inclusion criteria, all of which were cross-sectional studies, as shown in Table 1. Two cohort studies were not included due to the insufficient number [47, 48].

The risk of bias for these studies was assessed via the NOS, and the results are presented in Table 1 and Table S7. The average NOS score was 7.45, indicating a low risk of bias. The quality of evidence was rated by the GRADE, and it showed that the quality of evidence was rated moderate for the study of LMM (Table S8, start at “Low” for observational studies and upgrade by one level for the significant dose-response gradient), and very low for the study of sarcopenia (start at “Low” for observational studies and downgrade by one level due to the potential risk of publication bias, Table S9).

This study included 20 cross-sectional studies involving 521,778 participants, aiming to investigate the relationship between the TyG index and LMM or sarcopenia. The meta-analysis revealed that individuals with a higher TyG index were more likely to have low muscle mass than those with a lower TyG index were (OR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.10, 1.50; p = 0.002; Fig. 2A). Similarly, individuals with a higher TyG index were more likely to have sarcopenia than those with a lower TyG index were (OR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.29, 2.07; p < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

Forest plot of the odds of low muscle mass (A) and sarcopenia (B) in the high TyG index group versus the control group

Furthermore, Egger’s test and funnel plots were used to assess publication bias in the studies on low muscle mass, with no significant publication bias detected (p = 0.392, Fig. 3A). This finding supports the accuracy and reliability of the findings, ensuring that the results are not skewed owing to selective publication. However, in the sarcopenia studies, both Egger’s test and funnel plots suggested potential publication bias (p = 0.054, Fig. 3B). Therefore, we conducted a trim-and-fill analysis for the sarcopenia studies, which suggested the presence of one missing study (Figure S1). The overall association estimates based on this analysis was an OR of 1.547 (95% CI, 1.225–1.953), which was slightly lower than the initially reported overall association.

Funnel plot of TyG index association estimates. (A) Low muscle mass. (B) Sarcopenia

No significant findings were observed in the sensitivity analysis (Figure S2). The results from the subgroup analysis indicated a positive correlation between the TyG index and LMM (Table 2). Additionally, in the subgroup analysis based on age (<40 and ≥40 years), although both groups showed an association between LMM and the TyG index, the correlation was stronger in those under 40 years of age (p = 0.046). Similarly, based on the method of diagnosing LMM, the correlation was found to be greater when using ASM/weight than when using ASM/BMI or ASM/height2 (p < 0.05). Meta-regression analysis revealed that only the classification of the LMM diagnostic method had an impact on the overall association (p < 0.05, Table 2).

While all diagnostic methods were examined, a particularly high degree of heterogeneity was identified in the subgroup of studies employing the ASM/height2 criterion (I2 = 98.5%). To investigate this significant source of heterogeneity, a further nested subgroup analysis was conducted on these four studies, stratifying them by whether their statistical models adjusted for BMI. This stratification revealed a significant interaction (Pinteraction = 0.006), indicating that BMI adjustment acts as a substantial association modifier. Specifically, in the two studies that did not adjust for BMI, a higher TyG index was associated with lower odds of LMM (OR = 0.655; 95% CI: 0.530, 0.781), with no residual heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Conversely, in the two studies that did adjust for BMI, a higher TyG index was associated with higher odds of LMM (OR = 1.594; 95% CI: 1.414, 1.773), and heterogeneity was markedly reduced to a moderate level (I2 = 40.7%). The meta-regression analysis confirmed that the adjustment for BMI within the ASM/height2 subgroup was a significant factor influencing the overall association estimate (p = 0.007). This indicates that the inclusion or exclusion of BMI as a covariate is a primary source of the observed heterogeneity in this specific subgroup.

In the sarcopenia-related studies, no significant findings were observed in the sensitivity analysis (Figure S3). Meta-regression and subgroup analyses were performed based on region, TyG index analysis, gender, sex, age, BMI, diabetes status, and sarcopenia diagnosis. Subgroup analysis revealed a positive correlation between the TyG index and sarcopenia, except in the nondiabetic subgroup (Table 3). In the subgroup analysis based on diabetes status, the TyG index was negatively correlated with sarcopenia in the nondiabetic subgroup (OR 0.845, 95% CI 0.409, 1.280), which was significantly different from that in the diabetic subgroup (p < 0.001). Meta-regression analysis did not reveal any categories influencing the overall association.

In the primary subgroup analysis for sarcopenia, significant heterogeneity was noted among the studies utilizing the AWGS 2019 criteria (I2 > 75%). To explore potential sources of this heterogeneity, a further subgroup analysis was conducted based on whether the primary studies included blood lipids as a statistical covariate. This analysis revealed that adjustment for blood lipids was a significant source of heterogeneity (Pinetacrion = 0.005; Pmeta-regression = 0.014). Specifically, the subgroup of studies that did not adjust for blood lipids demonstrated a strong, positive association between a high TyG index and sarcopenia (OR = 2.579; 95% CI: 2.446–2.712; I2 = 53.3%). In contrast, studies that did adjust for blood lipids showed an inverse association (OR = 0.640; 95% CI: 0.502–0.777), with heterogeneity fully resolved (I2 = 0%). Dose‒response analysis revealed a linear relationship between the TyG index and LMM (p < 0.001), while P value for non-linearity was assessed with restricted cubic splines (p = 0.27). For per 1-unit increase in the TyG index, the odds of having LMM were 1.79 times higher (OR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.50, 2.14) (Fig. 4). For sarcopenia, a dose‒response analysis was not conducted because of insufficient data.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, encompassing 20 cross-sectional studies and a substantial cohort of 521,778 participants, provides evidence for a significant positive association between a higher TyG index and higher odds of both LMM and sarcopenia. Therefore, we propose that the TyG index, a simple and cost-effective marker derived from routine laboratory tests, holds potential for being used in primary care settings to screen for individuals at higher risk of muscle health decline. A high TyG index could trigger a recommendation for more definitive assessments or for early, targeted lifestyle interventions.

These results align with the literature suggesting that IR, which the TyG index reliably surrogates, is a key pathophysiological factor in the development and progression of sarcopenia. In the context of IR, impaired insulin signaling in muscle cells leads to reduced glucose uptake and utilization, diminished protein synthesis, and proteolytic pathway activation, ultimately contributing to muscle loss and dysfunction [12, 49, 50]. Chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, often hallmarks of IR, also contribute to muscle atrophy by damaging muscle cells and impairing their regenerative capacity [51–54]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, which is frequently observed in insulin-resistant states, further exacerbates muscle loss by altering the cellular energy status and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to oxidative stress and impaired insulin signaling [55]. Additionally, altered adipokine profiles associated with IR and excess adiposity can negatively impact muscle metabolism [53, 54, 56]. The observed associations therefore align well with the known biological mechanisms linking metabolic health to muscle integrity. Our subgroup analyses provided further notable insights. For LMM, the association with the TyG index was stronger in individuals aged under 40 years. This finding suggests that metabolic dysregulation, as indicated by the TyG index, may exert a more pronounced association with muscle mass at earlier stages of life, potentially before age-related mechanisms predominate [57]. Furthermore, the diagnostic method for LMM significantly impacted the association size, with ASM/weight showing a stronger correlation than ASM/BMI or ASM/height2 (Fig. 5A). This suggests that the relative measure of muscle mass adjusted for total body weight might be more sensitive to changes associated with the TyG index, possibly reflecting the interplay with adiposity, which is inherently linked to IR [58, 59].

Another finding from our subgroup analyses was the substantial heterogeneity observed within studies that used ASM/height2 to diagnose LMM. To elucidate the source of this variance, a nested subgroup analysis was performed based on whether individual studies adjusted for BMI as a covariate. In studies that did not adjust for BMI, a higher TyG index was associated with a lower odd of LMM (Fig. 5A). This is likely attributable to the confounding effect of total body mass. This is likely because higher body mass often requires greater absolute muscle mass for support. Conversely, in studies that did adjust for BMI, the TyG index was positively associated with higher odds of LMM, and heterogeneity was substantially reduced. The result suggests that for a given BMI, a higher TyG index is indicative of a less favorable body composition, characterized by lower muscle mass relative to fat mass. This finding underscores that the choice of covariates, particularly the adjustment for BMI when using the ASM/height2 index, is not a trivial decision and can lead to opposing conclusions.

For sarcopenia, the subgroup analysis revealed a positive correlation between the TyG index and sarcopenia across most subgroups, confirming the robustness of the overall findings. However, a notable exception was the nondiabetic subgroup, where an inverse association was observed (Fig. 5B). A subgroup study by Li et al. reported that higher TyG and TyG-BMI indices protected against sarcopenia in nondiabetic middle-aged and older females, suggesting that in the absence of overt diabetes, the TyG index might reflect nutritional status or other protective factors [34]. Another study in this subgroup revealed that a higher TyG index was associated with higher odds of sarcopenia in nondiabetic patients on maintenance hemodialysis, indicating that the context of “nondiabetic patients” may vary significantly across different clinical populations and disease states [42]. The conflicting conclusions drawn from various independent studies of nondiabetic populations may be attributed to the fact that in malnourished groups, there is a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and diseases, as observed in a cross-sectional study of cardiovascular disease [60]. In vulnerable populations, particularly older adults, a very low TyG index might not reflect metabolic health but rather serve as a surrogate for poor nutritional status or frailty. Low circulating levels of triglycerides and glucose can indicate a state of protein-energy malnutrition, a primary and potent driver of muscle catabolism [61]. Sarcopenia, malnutrition, and frailty are recognized components of a vicious cycle, where a decline in one domain exacerbates the others [62, 63]. In this context, a low TyG index could be a biomarker for a catabolic, frail state where inadequate nutrient intake directly contributes to muscle wasting. For example, in the study by Li et al., it is plausible that the sarcopenic group had a lower average TyG index because they were more likely to be malnourished or frail compared to the healthier, non-sarcopenic group [34]. However, there is no definitive conclusion regarding the values of the TyG index that reflect nutritional deficiency and metabolic abnormalities. A value of ≥8.31 has been proposed to identify IR in women with polycystic ovary syndrome, while studies suggest that cutoff values for metabolic syndrome in the adult population typically cluster at approximately 8.8 [56, 64]. However, values below 7.9 to 8.3 may indicate malnutrition [60]. Further research, particularly longitudinal studies or studies specifically recruiting and categorizing nondiabetic populations based on IR severity and nutritional status, is warranted to fully elucidate this complex interaction.

Furthermore, an important finding of our analysis was the identification of adjustment for blood lipids as a major source of heterogeneity within studies employing the AWGS 2019 diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia (Fig. 5B). The TyG index is, by definition, calculated from fasting triglyceride levels. Therefore, including blood lipids (hyperlipidemia or cholesterols specifically) as covariates in a model where the TyG index is the primary exposure may introduce a significant risk of over-adjustment. Moreover, studies that adjusted for blood lipids may partial out the critical mediating effect of dyslipidemia. Consequently, we recommend that future observational studies investigating the TyG index be cautious on adjustment for blood lipids.

This study has several strengths. First, it includes a large sample size of over 500,000 participants, which enhances the statistical power and reliability of the findings. Second, strictly adhering to the PRISMA guidelines and employing the GRADE system for quality assessment ensures the methodological rigor of this meta-analysis. Third, the dose-response analysis and comprehensive subgroup analysis provided valuable information regarding the relationship between the TyG index and the risk of LMM and sarcopenia.

This study has several limitations. First, all included studies were cross-sectional in design. While our meta-analysis revealed a significant association, it could not establish a causal relationship between the TyG index and the development of LMM or sarcopenia. Longitudinal cohort studies and interventional trials targeting insulin resistance to test whether TyG reduction preserves muscle mass are needed to confirm the temporal relationship and causal inference. Second, despite extensive subgroup and meta-regression analyses, considerable degree of heterogeneity remained in some analyses, which might be attributed to variations in diagnosis methods and confounding factors that are not fully captured. For example, hand grip strength offers a practical measure of muscle function but is prone to biases from equipment and protocol variations [65]. Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DEXA) provides a good estimate of lean mass but does not differentiate muscle from other soft tissues and can be influenced by hydration and certain medical conditions [66]. Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA) is a convenient and portable option, but its accuracy is heavily dependent on hydration status and the appropriateness of the prediction equations used [67]. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) stands as the most accurate method for quantifying muscle mass and quality but is hampered by cost, accessibility, and a lack of standardized protocols [68]. Thus, lack of standardized diagnostic methods is a major challenge in this field and a key limitation of our meta-analysis. Third, our dose-response analysis for low muscle mass relied on estimating midpoints for the TyG index from categorized data, particularly for open-ended intervals. While we employed a standard and widely used methodology, this approach involves inherent assumptions and may introduce approximation errors. Fourth, we restricted our search to studies written in English and did not perform a systematic search of dissertation databases due to resource limitations, which may introduce bias in the results of the study. Fifth, although the overall quality of evidence for low muscle mass was rated moderate to high, confidence in the sarcopenia estimate is reduced by borderline evidence of publication bias. Trim-and-fill imputation of one potentially missing study yielded an adjusted OR, indicating that the true association may be slightly weaker than originally estimated. Finally, the meta-analysis relied on aggregated data from studies; individual participant data meta-analysis could provide more granular insights and allow for more consistent adjustment of confounding factors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provide strong evidence that a higher TyG index is significantly associated with higher odds of both low muscle mass and sarcopenia. The TyG index, an easily accessible and cost-effective marker of insulin resistance, has potential as a valuable tool for early screening and risk assessment of muscle health decline. However, it is important to emphasize that the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to determine the directionality of these associations. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies and interventional trials to clarify causality and explore targeted interventions. Further investigation into the observed inverse association in nondiabetic sarcopenia subgroups is also warranted to unravel the complex pathophysiology underlying these conditions. Finally, the standardization of diagnostic criteria and careful covariate selection will be critical for future research in this field.
