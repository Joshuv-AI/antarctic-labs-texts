A study on the dynamic mechanical properties of coral concrete reinforced with polypropylene and basalt fibers
Authors: Zhongqing Li, Hao Ge, Taiping Kang, Wen Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To clarify the strain-rate-dependent behavior of hybrid fiber-reinforced coral aggregate concrete, dynamic splitting tensile tests were conducted on specimens incorporating polypropylene fibers(PPF) and basalt fibers (BF) with varying lengths and volume fractions. The results demonstrate that strain rate and fiber length jointly govern the evolution of peak stress, peak strain, and dynamic increase factor (DIF). While all specimens exhibited pronounced strain-rate strengthening, fiber length primarily controlled deformation coordination at the peak stage. Specimens reinforced with 12 mm BF showed higher peak strain and stronger strain-rate sensitivity compared with 6 mm and 18 mm systems, indicating a more effective stress-transfer and crack-bridging mechanism under dynamic loading. In contrast, excessive fiber length or dosage reduced strengthening efficiency due to interfacial instability and non-uniform distribution. Hybrid fiber systems consistently outperformed single-fiber specimens in terms of dynamic tensile strength, energy dissipation capacity, and crack resistance. The findings reveal a clear coupling effect between fiber length and strain rate and identify 12 mm BF at moderate dosage as the optimal configuration for improving dynamic splitting performance. This study provides mechanistic insight into fiber-length-dependent dynamic reinforcement and offers guidance for coral concrete applications in marine structures subjected to impact and wave loading.

Introduction

With the rapid development of coastal cities and island construction projects, concrete has become an indispensable material for infrastructure development. Transporting conventional construction materials from the mainland to remote islands not only increases project costs but also prolongs construction periods1. To address resource scarcity while minimizing ecological disturbance, the utilization of locally available coral as construction material has been proposed. This approach alleviates the pressure on material supply, shortens construction time, and reduces overall costs, thereby offering significant engineering value2.

Coral aggregate concrete (CAC) has thus been introduced as a new construction material system in which coral aggregates serve as the primary aggregate source. Coral debris exhibits physical characteristics similar to those of lightweight aggregates, including a porous internal structure and relatively low density, along with high water absorption but limited water retention capacity. Previous studies have shown that its strength development, curing characteristics, and failure mechanisms are comparable in certain aspects to those of lightweight aggregate concrete3. Owing to the abundance and accessibility of coral resources in coastal regions, CAC has attracted increasing attention. Compared with ordinary concrete, CAC generally exhibits higher early-age strength, a higher tensile-to-compressive strength ratio, lower bulk density, and reduced stiffness4–6. In addition, concrete prepared with sea sand has been reported to show a faster early-age strength development rate than conventional concrete7.

However, the relatively low stiffness and high porosity of CAC may reduce its crack resistance stability under high strain rate loading, making crack propagation more susceptible to rapid development under dynamic conditions. Therefore, the incorporation of fiber reinforcement into such porous and lightweight matrices, particularly hybrid fiber systems capable of providing crack-bridging effects at multiple scales, is of considerable importance for improving dynamic crack resistance.

Although CAC can satisfy general strength requirements in practical engineering applications, its ductility, flexural performance, impact resistance, and crack resistance remain limited. Accordingly, effective modification strategies are necessary to enhance its mechanical performance. Previous studies have reported that the incorporation of macro-sized fibers can significantly improve the strength and toughness of concrete8,9.

Basalt fiber (BF) is a typical silicate fiber characterized by excellent high-temperature resistance, good compatibility with cementitious matrices, high mechanical strength, and superior chemical stability10–12. Polypropylene fiber (PPF), synthesized from isotactic polypropylene, possesses relatively high tensile strength and favorable ductility, enabling effective control of crack propagation and enhancement of concrete deformability13–15.

However, the improvement imparted by a single type of fiber is often limited. The combined incorporation of BF and PPF fiber with different dimensions and mechanical characteristics has been shown to significantly enhance the ductility, toughness, and crack resistance of concrete16–18. Li et al19. conducted four-point bending, shear, and other mechanical tests on concrete reinforced with basalt fiber, polypropylene fiber, and steel fiber, demonstrating that both BF and PPF fiber effectively improved concrete toughness. Liu20 reported that the introduction of BF can markedly increase the splitting tensile strength and flexural strength of concrete. Wu et al21,22. found that optimal enhancement in impact resistance was achieved when the BF volume fraction was 0.25%, and further observed that the dynamic peak stress initially increased and then decreased with increasing fiber content, with the most favorable range between 0.1% and 0.3%.

Under identical loading conditions, Li et al.23 indicated that increasing the PPF content led to higher elastic modulus and peak strain, although the variation in peak strain exhibited certain dispersion. PPF has been shown to effectively control plastic shrinkage cracking, contributing to improved tensile strength and deformation capacity, with finer fibers demonstrating greater effectiveness than coarser ones14. The addition of these small-scale fibers may significantly influence compressive strength, and hybrid fiber systems tend to exhibit synergistic effects13.

Fu et al.24 investigated the mechanical properties of hybrid basalt–polypropylene fiber-reinforced concrete with different matrix strengths, revealing that the synergistic interaction between BF and PPF effectively restrained crack propagation and enhanced overall mechanical performance, particularly in flexural and splitting behavior. Zhang et al.25 experimentally studied hybrid basalt–macro synthetic polypropylene fiber-reinforced concrete under impact loading with strain rates ranging from 101 to 102 s−1, and reported that both fibers contributed significantly to improvements in impact strength.

According to composite material theory, fibers of different types and sizes can act synergistically at various loading stages, providing complementary reinforcing mechanisms and thereby enhancing the overall performance of concrete26. Consequently, increasing attention has been directed toward the dynamic response of hybrid fiber-reinforced concrete systems. Fu et al.27 reported that the crack-bridging effects of BF and PPF enhance the strain rate sensitivity of concrete. P. L. Meyyappan et al.28 found that BF effectively reduced the compressive-to-tensile strength ratio while significantly improving impact resistance and ductility. Liu et al.29 demonstrated that, under appropriate hybridization conditions, BF increased the splitting tensile strength and elastic modulus of concrete by 25% and 8.5%, respectively. Overall, hybrid fiber incorporation not only improves impermeability and crack resistance but also enhances compressive, tensile, and flexural properties, resulting in superior performance compared with ordinary concrete. Notably, Deng et al.30 reported that when the volume ratio of BF to PPF was 1:2 with a total fiber content of 6 kg/m3, a pronounced synergistic enhancement in both compressive and tensile performance was achieved, demonstrating complementary advantages between the two fiber types.

The physical and mechanical properties of CAC differ markedly from those of conventional concrete, while exhibiting certain similarities to lightweight aggregate concrete in specific aspects31. The discrepancies in mechanical behavior between CAC and conventional concrete are primarily attributed to the intrinsic characteristics of coral aggregates, including pronounced intra-particle porosity, highly irregular geometry, high compressibility, and low crushing strength32.

Although previous investigations have explored the mechanical behavior of fiber-reinforced concrete under impact or quasi-static loading, most studies have focused on ordinary concrete or lightweight aggregate concrete systems. For CAC, a material characterized by high porosity and distinctive interfacial features, research on its dynamic splitting tensile behavior under controlled high strain rate conditions remains limited. In particular, the mechanical response and crack evolution mechanisms of polypropylene–basalt hybrid fiber-reinforced CAC at varying strain rates have not yet been systematically elucidated, and conclusions derived from conventional systems cannot be directly extrapolated to this material.

To address this issue, the present study employs the Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) technique to systematically investigate the dynamic splitting tensile performance and strain rate sensitivity of hybrid fiber-reinforced CAC. The findings are expected to provide a more reliable basis for the design and optimization of CAC structures used in marine and coastal infrastructure subjected to impact or high-rate loading conditions. Nevertheless, practical engineering applications must also account for factors such as structural size effects, complex stress states, and long-term environmental exposure. Therefore, the applicability of the present conclusions to real structures requires further validation in conjunction with specific structural conditions.

Experimental overview

Materials and mix proportions

The coral reef limestone used as coarse aggregate and the coral sand used as fine aggregate were collected from a tropical marine coral reef environment. The coarse aggregate was manually crushed into reef limestone particles with a size range of 5–20 mm. The fine aggregate consisted primarily of coral sand, accompanied by a small amount of shell and sea snail debris.

The cementitious system comprised P·O 52.5 ordinary Portland cement, Class I fly ash, and S95-grade ground granulated blast-furnace slag (specific surface area: 430 m2/kg). The incorporation of mineral admixtures was intended to enhance the reactivity of the binder system and to refine the pore structure of the concrete matrix.

To simulate the marine hydration environment, artificial seawater prepared in accordance with ASTM D1141-20 was adopted. The ionic concentrations were controlled within ± 5% of measured data from tropical coral reef environments. A polycarboxylate-based high-range water-reducing admixture (solid content: 38%) was used to improve workability and reduce water demand, with a water-reduction ratio exceeding 25%, in compliance with the Chinese standard Concrete Admixtures (GB/T 8076-2008).

With respect to fiber selection, fibers with different elastic moduli exhibit distinct reinforcing mechanisms in concrete. High-modulus BF enhances matrix stiffness primarily through a crack-bridging effect, whereas low-modulus PPF dissipates fracture energy through plastic deformation.

In general, when the fiber volume fraction is 0.1%, the mechanical performance of CAC can be improved regardless of whether BF are used alone or in combination with PPF24. Short fibers provide limited crack-bridging capacity, whereas excessively long fibers increase the risk of entanglement and non-uniform distribution. Therefore, the PPF length was fixed at 12 mm—commonly adopted in cementitious composites—with a volume fraction of 0.1%, in order to ensure effective microcrack bridging while maintaining good dispersion during mixing.

BF was introduced with three length parameters (6, 12, and 18 mm) and three volume fractions (0.05%, 0.1%, and 0.15%), forming a two-variable experimental matrix. Accordingly, nine groups of fiber-reinforced CAC were subjected to dynamic splitting tests. In addition, a control group without fiber and a group reinforced solely with PPF were tested for comparison.

In fiber-reinforced concrete, the ratio between fiber length and specimen dimensions may influence local fiber distribution and induce potential boundary effects33. In the present study, PP fiber with a length of 12 mm was incorporated into specimens with a minimum diameter of 25 mm. Although the fiber length was approximately half of the specimen diameter, mechanical mixing was employed to ensure random fiber dispersion. During specimen preparation and post-test observation, no evident fiber agglomeration or preferential orientation was detected near the boundaries. Considering the relatively low fiber volume fraction (0.1%), the selected specimen dimensions were considered sufficient to represent the macroscopic dynamic tensile response of the material. No abnormal size-related behavior was observed in the stress–strain curves.

The selected fiber volume fractions were determined based on existing literature and preliminary mixing trials (Fig. 1). Moderate fiber contents were adopted to enhance tensile performance under dynamic loading while avoiding fiber agglomeration and deterioration of workability. The chosen ranges therefore ensured a balance between mechanical enhancement and uniform dispersion. The detailed material properties are summarized in Table 1. Specimen preparation

CAC was prepared using coral reef limestone as coarse aggregate and coral sand as fine aggregate. One half of the artificial seawater was first mixed with the pre-washed and air-dried coral reef limestone and coral sand in a forced mixer at a liquid-to-aggregate ratio of 1:1, followed by thorough mixing. Subsequently, PPF and BF were added sequentially to the mixture and blended for approximately 3 min to promote uniform dispersion. The total mixing time was kept constant for all batches. Thereafter, cement, fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, the polycarboxylate-based high-range water-reducing admixture, and the remaining artificial seawater were introduced and mixed for an additional 2 min. Upon completion of mixing, the fresh concrete was cast into molds with dimensions of 50 mm × 25 mm.

To facilitate demolding, a thin layer of machine oil was applied to the inner surfaces of the specimen molds. After casting, all freshly mixed concrete specimens were subjected to mechanical vibration for 30 s under identical conditions to ensure adequate compaction and proper shaping. Following vibration, the specimens were covered and allowed to undergo an initial curing period of 48 h at room temperature prior to demolding. The vibration duration and the initial curing time were strictly maintained constant for all concrete batches to ensure consistency in specimen preparation.

After completion of the initial curing period, the specimens were demolded. The demolded specimens were subsequently cured at 20 °C and covered with plastic film or moist paper towels. Artificial seawater was periodically applied to maintain surface moisture and to prevent drying-induced cracking. Experimental equipment and loading procedure

The dynamic mechanical response of concrete materials is influenced by the testing method, loading configuration, and boundary conditions. Previous studies have indicated that instrumented drop-weight tests may introduce complex contact conditions and non-uniform stress states, thereby affecting the accurate characterization of dynamic performance34,35. Therefore, the SHPB technique was adopted in this study. This method enables controlled high strain rate loading and allows verification of dynamic stress equilibrium within the specimen. Compared with the drop-weight approach, SHPB testing provides more stable stress–strain data and clearer strain rate characterization.

The experiments were conducted using the SHPB apparatus located in the Impact Dynamics Laboratory of the School of Safety Science and Engineering at Anhui University of Science and Technology. The SHPB system consists of three primary components: the bar system, the measurement system, and the data acquisition and processing system. A schematic diagram of the SHPB setup is shown in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Schematic diagram of SHPB device and pressure bar and air pressure device.

Schematic diagram of SHPB device and pressure bar and air pressure device.

In the bar system, the striker bar, incident bar, transmission bar, and absorption bar were all fabricated from high-strength alloy steel, with an elastic modulus of 195 GPa and a density of 7.5 g/cm3. All bars had a diameter of 75 mm. The lengths of the striker bar, incident bar, and transmission bar were 0.4 m, 4 m, and 2.5 m, respectively. The tested specimen was positioned between the incident bar and the transmission bar. The striker bar, propelled by high-pressure nitrogen gas, impacted the incident bar at a controlled velocity, thereby generating a compressive stress pulse referred to as the incident wave. This wave propagated along the incident bar toward the specimen. Upon reaching the bar–specimen interface (Interface 1–1 in Fig. 4), a portion of the incident wave was transmitted through the specimen into the transmission bar, forming the transmitted wave, while the remaining portion was reflected back into the incident bar as the reflected wave. Strain gauges mounted on the incident and transmission bars recorded the histories of the incident, reflected, and transmitted waves. Based on one-dimensional stress wave theory, the dynamic stress, strain, and strain rate of the specimen were determined from these recorded wave signals.Fig. 4Propagation and reflection of stress waves inside steel rods and specimens.

Propagation and reflection of stress waves inside steel rods and specimens.

Dynamic splitting tests were performed on eleven specimen groups (P0B0, P-2, PB1-1, PB1-2, PB1-3, PB2-1, PB2-2, PB2-3, PB3-1, PB3-2, and PB3-3). For each group, tests were conducted under three different gas pressures, with three parallel specimens prepared for each pressure level. The incident wave was regulated by adjusting the striker velocity and employing a pulse shaper to achieve different strain rate loadings. At a gas pressure of 0.2 MPa, the striker velocity was approximately 9.5 m/s, corresponding to a strain rate fluctuating around 150 s−1. At 0.4 MPa, the striker velocity was approximately 15 m/s, with a strain rate around 300 s−1. At 0.6 MPa, the striker velocity reached approximately 19 m/s, producing a strain rate near 500 s−1. Under different gas pressure levels, variations in pressure result in corresponding changes in the stress state of the specimens. The three loading pressures therefore produce distinct strain rates. In this study, the dynamic splitting tensile strength of the specimens was analyzed as a function of strain rate under these three loading conditions.

The SHPB technique is based on two fundamental assumptions: (i) stress waves propagating in the pressure bars are one-dimensional elastic waves, and (ii) stress and strain within the specimen are uniformly distributed along its length once dynamic equilibrium is achieved. Specifically, the pressure bars are assumed to deform elastically under axial loading, with uniform stress across the cross-section and purely longitudinal tension or compression. Moreover, within a certain strain rate range, the stress–strain relationship of the specimen subjected to impact loading is assumed to be unique.

Experimental results and analysis

Stress–strain curves

Based on the original waveform data processed using the two-wave method, the dynamic splitting stress–strain curves of all specimens were obtained, as shown in Fig. 7. Under varying BF lengths and strain rate levels, the overall curve profiles of each group exhibit similar characteristics; however, noticeable differences are observed in peak stress, peak strain, and post-peak response. These variations indicate that both BF length and loading rate exert significant influences on the dynamic mechanical behavior of CAC.Fig. 7Stress–strain curves of hybrid fiber coral concrete specimens.

Stress–strain curves of hybrid fiber coral concrete specimens.

From the overall trend, the peak stress of most specimens increases progressively with increasing strain rate, demonstrating a pronounced strain rate sensitivity. Meanwhile, the initial slope of the stress–strain curves generally becomes steeper at higher strain rates, suggesting that CAC exhibits a higher apparent stiffness under dynamic loading conditions.

As the applied load increases, the stress–strain curves gradually approach a linear trend. It can be observed that, for most groups, the ascending slope under high strain rate conditions is steeper than that under low strain rate conditions. However, when the BF length is 18 mm, a different tendency is occasionally observed, where the slope at low strain rate exceeds that at high strain rate. This phenomenon may be associated with the spatial distribution of long fibers within the matrix and their interfacial response. Compared with shorter fibers, 18 mm BF is more prone to localized alignment or overlapping during mixing. Under relatively low strain rate loading, interfacial stresses can be more effectively transferred; whereas at high strain rates, premature interfacial slip may occur, thereby reducing the contribution of long fibers to stiffness enhancement.

With respect to peak strain, the specimens exhibited a pronounced dependence on fiber length. The PB2 series incorporating 12 mm BF maintained relatively high peak strain values under all strain rate conditions, and the peak point shifted significantly toward larger strain at higher strain rates, indicating evident strain rate sensitivity. In contrast, the PB3 series reinforced with 18 mm BF exhibited overall lower peak strain values, with only minor variation as the strain rate increased. The PB1 series containing 6 mm BF showed intermediate peak strain values, approaching those of the PB2 series under high strain rate loading. These observations indicate that a fiber length of 12 mm is more conducive to maintaining deformation compatibility during the peak formation stage under dynamic splitting conditions, whereas the 18 mm fibers, affected by orientation effects and stress concentration, contribute less effectively to peak strain enhancement and display limited sensitivity to strain rate variation. As a result, different fiber lengths lead to distinct peak positions in the stress–strain curves.

The analysis indicates that the hybrid fiber system exhibits a synergistic strengthening mechanism. The high-modulus BF restrains the propagation of macrocracks by bridging crack surfaces, whereas the low-modulus PPF promotes energy dissipation through plastic deformation. The coupling effect of the two fibers effectively enhances the crack resistance and toughness of the specimens under dynamic splitting loading. Accordingly, the combined incorporation of PPF fiber and BF contributes to improvements in peak stress, peak strain, ductility, and elastic modulus of the CAC37.

Although the overall morphology of the stress–strain curves for the PB1–PB3 series is similar, variations in fiber configuration and strain rate lead to distinct differences in peak stress, post-peak softening characteristics, and ductility. The increase in peak stress with increasing strain rate is primarily attributed to the inherent strain rate sensitivity of the CAC matrix, as well as the enhanced load transfer capacity at the fiber–matrix interface under rapid loading.

Specimens incorporating hybrid fibers, particularly those containing 6 mm and 12 mm BF, exhibit superior performance in terms of peak strength and post-peak response. This improvement is mainly associated with effective crack-bridging and crack propagation inhibition, which delay crack coalescence and enhance post-peak deformation capacity. In contrast, excessive fiber length or volume fraction may reduce fiber dispersion and induce local stress concentration, thereby weakening the reinforcing effect and diminishing ductility.

Overall, the coupled influence of fiber geometric parameters and strain rate governs the dynamic stress–strain response of the material. An optimized hybrid fiber configuration is therefore beneficial for achieving a balance between strength enhancement and deformation capacity.

The experimental results indicate that as the strain rate increases from approximately 151 s−1 to 504 s−1, the dynamic splitting tensile strength of CAC reinforced with the hybrid fiber system exhibits a progressive upward trend. At 151.28 s−1, the dynamic splitting tensile strength increases from 10.28 MPa to 15.66 MPa; at 314.54 s−1, it rises from 14.48 MPa to 20.71 MPa; and at 503.83 s−1, it further increases to 23.52 MPa. A comparative analysis shows that the strength enhancement of plain CAC is approximately 50.5%, while that of CAC reinforced solely with PPF is about 52.5%. Under the optimal hybrid fiber configuration (BF length of 12 mm and volume fraction of 0.1%), the strength enhancement reaches the highest value, approximately 56.1%.

The increase in strength induced by higher strain rates is fundamentally associated with the restriction of crack propagation and the enhanced inertial confinement under rapid loading. High loading rates shorten the time available for crack development, preventing microcracks from coalescing in a timely manner and improving interfacial stress transfer efficiency, thereby resulting in a more pronounced dynamic strengthening effect. Although all specimen types exhibit an increasing strength trend with rising strain rate, the degree of strain rate sensitivity varies significantly among different fiber configurations.

From a mechanistic perspective, the strength enhancement of plain CAC primarily arises from the intrinsic strain rate sensitivity of the matrix and the inertial confinement effect under dynamic loading. In the system reinforced solely with PPF, energy dissipation occurs through fiber pull-out and interfacial slip; however, due to the relatively low elastic modulus of PPF, its ability to restrain crack initiation is limited.

In contrast, within the hybrid fiber system, high-modulus BF provides more effective crack-bridging restraint during crack initiation and early-stage propagation, thereby enhancing stress transfer efficiency under dynamic conditions. Meanwhile, PPF dissipates energy through pull-out and plastic deformation during crack opening, delaying unstable crack growth. The two fiber types function synergistically at different stages of crack evolution, forming a coordinated crack-bridging mechanism that increases resistance to crack propagation. As a result, the hybrid fiber-reinforced CAC demonstrates a more pronounced strength enhancement and greater strain rate sensitivity.

It should be emphasized that the superior strengthening effect of the hybrid fiber system compared with the single PPF system originates from the functional differentiation and synergistic interaction of the two fibers at different stages of crack development. PPF primarily contributes to energy dissipation through pull-out and plastic deformation, whereas high-modulus BF provides more effective crack-bridging restraint and interfacial stress transfer during crack initiation. The complementary characteristics in scale and mechanical properties enable the hybrid fiber-reinforced CAC to exhibit greater strength enhancement and improved strain rate sensitivity under dynamic loading.

For the systems reinforced with 6 mm and 12 mm BF, the DIF exhibits a trend of initially increasing and then decreasing with increasing fiber volume fraction, and the overall DIF level is markedly higher than that of the 18 mm group. However, when the fiber content becomes excessive, local fiber overlapping and interfacial defects reduce the bridging efficiency, resulting in a diminished increase in DIF.

In contrast, for the 18 mm BF system, the DIF decreases with increasing fiber volume fraction, and the overall strengthening effect remains limited. This can be primarily attributed to the relatively high porosity and comparatively weak interfacial transition zones in CAC. Under dynamic loading, longer fibers are more prone to interfacial slip or premature pull-out, making it difficult to fully mobilize their effective anchorage length. Moreover, in specimens of limited dimensions, longer fibers are more susceptible to orientation deviation, reducing the degree of random distribution and weakening the synergistic multi-scale crack-bridging effect. Consequently, the incorporation of 18 mm BF does not significantly enhance the strain rate sensitivity of the material.

Overall, the variation in the DIF is not determined solely by the presence of fibers, but rather by the degree of compatibility between fiber length and the interfacial characteristics of the matrix. The 6–12 mm length range achieves a more favorable balance among interfacial anchorage efficiency, bridging stability, and dispersion uniformity, thereby exhibiting a more pronounced strengthening effect under dynamic loading conditions.

In the present research, the DIF values ranged approximately from 2.05 to 3.35. The strain rate sensitivity of DIF varied to some extent among hybrid fiber-reinforced CAC specimens with different fiber lengths and volume fractions. For fiber lengths of 6 mm and 12 mm at volume fractions of 0.05% and 0.1%, the DIF demonstrated the highest sensitivity to strain rate. In contrast, within the 18 mm series, the PB3-1 group with a fiber volume fraction of 0.05% exhibited the most pronounced strain rate effect. Similar phenomena were observed in certain specimens; for example, at high strain rates, the DIF value of the PB2-3 group was very close to that of the P-2 group, which may be attributed to experimental scatter. Based on these observations, it can be concluded that when the BF length is 6 mm or 12 mm and the volume fraction is 0.05% or 0.1%, the hybrid fiber-reinforced CAC exhibits relatively high strain rate sensitivity in terms of DIF.
