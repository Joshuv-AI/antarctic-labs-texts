The Interplay Between Schistosomiasis and Hepatitis C Virus: Battling on Two Fronts
Authors: Nahed A Makhlouf, Ahmed Abu-Elfatth, Tasneem Khaled, Mohamed El-Kassas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchistosomiasis is a prevalent health issue in numerous countries in Africa, Asia, and South America. Data regarding the coinfection of schistosomiasis with hepatitis C virus (HCV) is limited, yet this coinfection is prevalent in regions where schistosomiasis is endemic. The extent of the coinfection issue is evident in countries with a high prevalence of both diseases, such as Egypt. Coinfections with schistosomiasis result in more pronounced liver damage compared with an HCV infection alone. Schistosomiasis has been found to disrupt HCV-specific T-cell responses, resulting in high viral load, increased likelihood of HCV chronicity, and accelerated development of comorbidities in individuals with coinfection. Introducing new, directly acting antivirals for HCV treatment resulted in a marked shift in the disease landscape. This shift may have an impact on the incidence of coinfection with schistosomiasis. This review emphasizes the notable influence of schistosomiasis on the vulnerability to HCV coinfection, the gravity of the consequent liver pathology, and the effectiveness of HCV antiviral therapy.

1. Epidemiology of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection and schistosomiasis

Globally, 58 million people are infected with HCV, with about 290,000 fatalities per year, with a global prevalence of 2.5%.[1] HCV infection can cause mild acute hepatitis or chronic hepatitis up to cirrhosis (15%-20%), hepatocellular failure (5%-30%), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (20%).[2] HCV treatment has been changed in the recent decade by direct-acting antiviral (DAA) medications, which have great effects on the virological response of those patients who are chronically infected with HCV. Those agents also have a cure rate of 95%.[3]

The World Health Organization (WHO) established lofty goals in 2016 for HCV elimination. Worldwide goals are to reduce the HCV incidence by 80% in 2030 compared with 2015 data.[3] Every year, 1.5 million cases report an HCV infection for the first time, and 58 million cases have a chronic HCV infection, with an incidence rate of 0.8% in the general population. The Eastern Mediterranean region has the highest frequency, at 1.6%.[4]

Schistosomiasis (bilharziasis) is a neglected disease in tropical areas caused by the trematode worms of the Schistosoma genus. Although endemic regions of Africa have many people at risk, Schistosoma species are also present in the Middle East, the Caribbean, South America, and Southeast Asia.[5] Three major species of Schistosoma infect humans: Schistosoma mansoni, Schistosoma haematobium, and Schistosoma japonicum.[6]

In Egypt, diseases caused by S. mansoni and S. haematobium have been endemic since ancient times.[7] After construction of the Aswan High Dam, S. mansoni has replaced S. haematobium, particularly in the Nile Delta and several regions of Egypt.[8]

In 2019, about 24,000 mortalities due to schistosomiasis were recorded, and about 236 million people required mass drug administration. WHO targeted the interruption of transmission and the elimination of the disease by 2030, but the increasing consequences of climate change and flooding may foster a challenge in controlling the disease.[9]

The crucial initial clinical state of Schistosoma species infection is acute schistosomiasis. People from non-endemic areas (initial infection) who go to and are exposed to freshwater in endemic areas typically exhibit the acute phase. The clinical presentation varies according to the severity of the condition. The degree of the illness is determined by the patient’s immune system reaction to the parasite’s eggs, particularly from the quantity of immune complexes circulating in the patient’s blood.[10]

The 3 most prevalent hepatosplenic morbidities are uneven liver surface, portal hypertension, and hepatosplenomegaly. The increasing periportal fibrosis ultimately leads to portal hypertension and its sequelae, which is hematemesis from bleeding esophageal varices.[11]

Egypt is the third country in Africa and the 15th worldwide reporting cases of HCC. There is an increase in the incidence rate of HCC, which constitutes 70% of liver malignancies.[12] A higher proportion of HCC patients showed concomitant HCV and S. mansoni infection (92.6%) than the S. mansoni negative group. Among cases of schistosomiasis with positive HCC, the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer class D was considerably higher. Concurrent S. mansoni with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and HCV accelerates the development of HCC.[13]

This review provides an overview on the global prevalence of hepatitis C before and after the era of DAAs, the impact of chronic HCV alone on HCC development, the role of DAAs on HCC occurrence and recurrence, the global prevalence of schistosomiasis, the effect of schistosomiasis on the liver, the role of schistosomiasis in HCC, and the complex relationship between schistosomiasis and HCV coinfection, including its implications for liver disease and HCC development.

2. Prevalence of HCV infection in the era of DAAs

2.1. Globally

Understanding the molecular structure of HCV helped identify targets for new drug discovery. An important structure is the HCV polymerase, which is essential for virus replication. The approval of the initial protease inhibitors by the United States Food and Drug Administration was in 2011.[1] New anti-HCV drugs developed were ombitasvir, ledipasvir, daclatasvir, elbasvir, and velpatasvir, which blocked the HCV NS5A protein, whereas sofosbuvir and dasabuvir targeted the HCV NS5B protein. In 2015, available clinical trials reported high results with the combination of sofosbuvir and velpatasvir with 99% sustained virologic response (SVR).[14]

However, estimates of HCV prevalence have long been based on interferon (IFN) era studies that targeted specific high-risk subgroups rather than the general population. Official data from national registries are mostly lacking, with the exception of only a few countries.[15] Even recently, most data on HCV prevalence come from high-risk populations, which have been extensively studied in the context of specific public health policies, even before the WHO set their goal and the advent of the DAA era.[15]

The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control report indicated that HCV prevalence ranged from 1% (Belgium, Ireland, and the Netherlands) to 5.9% (Italy), with estimates representative of the general population available for only 13 countries.[16] To further complicate the scenario, epidemiological data should be sought as an underestimation of the real HCV burden because less than 15% of those chronically infected with HCV are thought to be aware of their condition.[17,18]

HCV prevalence reduced from 170 million chronically infected patients worldwide in 1999 to 71 million in 2013. This decrease is attributable to blood donor screening programs, antiviral treatments, and mortality of infected patients.[19,20] Recently, WHO reported that 58 million people have HCV worldwide, with 1.5 million new infections occurring every year. Most developed countries have an infection rate of less than 1%.[21] Regarding the African continent, high prevalence rates are reported in Egypt (6.6%) and Gabon (7%). Some areas of South and East Asia also have a high HCV burden.[22]

2.2. In Egypt

In 2016, WHO had committed to eliminating viral hepatitis by 2030, focusing on HBV and HCV infections. The Ministry of Health and Population in Egypt established the National Committee for Control of Viral Hepatitis in 2006.[23] By April 2008, this committee developed a national program for viral hepatitis, which included effective surveillance, enforcement of preventive measures to reduce the incidence of HBV and HCV infections, and expanding access to treat patients with chronic infection. In 2018, Egypt formulated the largest mass screening and treatment campaign for HCV, screening 50 million people.[24]

Between 2014 and 2020, Egypt succeeded in conducting a unique program for HCV treatment that treated ≥4 million cases and screened 57 million, with the target of eradicating HCV as a public health burden.[25,26] Recently, Waked et al estimated that the prevalence rate for HCV antibody positivity was 4.6% and HCV viremia was 3.5% among the screened cohort of 50 million Egyptians.[24]

Recently, Kandeel et al stated that of 20,881 subjects interviewed in 27 Egyptian governorates, 48.8% were male, 20.2% were children <15 years of age, and 53.7% were residents of rural areas. Of all subjects, 92 (0.4%) were HCV-infected, 1577 (7.6%) were anti-HCV positive, and 177 (0.8%) were chronically HBV-infected, including 1 patient who had an active mixed HBV and HCV infection. The prevalence of current HCV and HBV chronic infections decreased by 93% and 20%, respectively, compared with 2015.[27]

3. HCC and HCV with the role of DAAs in HCC occurrence or recurrence

The presence of end-stage liver disease of any etiology is the leading risk for tumor development and progression. Nearly 90% of the cases of HCC are linked to other risk factors.[28] The most important risk factor for the development of HCC is liver cirrhosis of any etiology, which is considered a premalignant lesion and is present in over 70% of cases. All etiologies of cirrhosis may develop HCC, but patients with chronic HBV and HCV have the highest risk.[29,30] HCC surveillance is recommended for patients with cirrhosis and F3 fibrosis.[31]

HCV eradication has a favorable effect on the risk of HCC occurrence, observed in the IFN era, where SVR reduces HCC risk, liver failure, and death.[32] An Italian study reported a 3.17% incidence rate of HCC after 24 weeks of follow-up after DAAs, raising alarming signs about early HCC occurrence risk.[33] In a recent Egyptian study, HCC occurred in 9% of patients after DAAs.[34] Many studies discussed such issues after DAA therapy.[35,36,37 and 38] The risk for HCC occurrence was higher among non-responders (3.45%) compared with those who achieved SVR (0.90%), suggesting low residual risk for HCC even after viral eradication by DAAs.[39] However, these results have been calculated as a crude rate without a control group. In addition, patients treated with IFNs are different from patients treated with DAAs regarding the severity of cirrhosis and portal hypertension.[40]

DAA treatment is safe and effective in advanced stages of cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Therefore, HCC occurrence after DAA therapy may be related to treating patients at higher risk of cancer, whereas IFN treatment was used only in patients with well-compensated cirrhosis who had a low risk of HCC.[41,42 and 43] HCC risk is reduced but not eliminated after viral eradication, and cases with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis need surveillance for HCC indefinitely.[44]

The recurrence rate of HCC in cases with previous HCC radically ablated by any technique was 7.4% at 6 months and 47% at 24 months after DAAs, with a wider range from 32% to 100%.[44,45] The assessment of the control group’s recurrence rate is needed to estimate the impact of DAAs on HCC recurrence, and these results could be considered as a benchmark for this purpose. After the first report of HCC recurrence rate of 27.6% (follow-up of 5.7 months),[46] many prospective clinical studies could not find any link between DAAs and HCC recurrence.[47,48 and 49]

4. Effect of schistosomiasis on the liver

After a person gets infected, the cercariae develop into schistosomulae, which travel in the bloodstream to the liver and mature into adult worms. Male and female schistosomes pair as couples, then migrate to either the urogenital veins (S. haematobium) or the mesenteric veins of the gut (S. mansoni). After the maturation of the female, egg production starts.[50,51] Most of the eggs go to the liver and evoke a granulomatous host immune response with chronic inflammation, which causes sequelae of schistosomiasis (portal, pulmonary hypertension and other organ-specific manifestations). Schistosomiasis infection can also initiate or stimulate carcinogenesis through a chronic inflammatory process from the persistence of an inducing agent that can cause damage to the DNA, proteins, and cell structures, as well as changes in enzyme activities. S. mansoni promotes cancer development of hepatic and colorectal cancers based on multiple case reports and data from animal models and cell culture experiments.[52]

5. Role of schistosomiasis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

According to research conducted on animals and in clinical settings, schistosomiasis may potentially increase the risk of colorectal, prostate, and giant follicular lymphomas in addition to HCC.[53,54] Moreover, when coexisting with HBV and HCV infections, schistosomiasis probably exacerbates liver damage. In a mouse model of HCC, S. mansoni infection induced hepatic dysplastic alterations and encouraged the early and more severe initiation of cancer formation.[55]

Blood and liver flukes have been linked to various carcinogenic mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, metabolic oxidative stress triggered by parasite-derived products, and damage to host tissue during parasite development. Additionally, active wound healing, immunosuppression, and a decreased immune surveillance may also play a role in the development of hepatocellular carcinogenesis.[56,57] However, certain biomolecular risk factors that connect S. mansoni infection to HCC remain unidentified.

There is evidence that the initial step in cancer development is mediated by the host’s specific immune cells.[58]S. mansoni infection induces the host’s specific immune response (early Th1 response), directed at worm antigens with induction of interleukin (IL)-12 and IFN.[5]

Switching to Th2 response after egg production results in different cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, and IL-13) from the egg-evoked granulomatous reaction in the host.[59]

S. mansoni antigen–induced cytokine production,[59,60] T-cell proliferation, and in vitro granuloma formation involve the activation of protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs) and protein kinase C. Immunomodulation might either influence PTK activity or be the result of altered PTK regulation. Regulatory T cells and activated macrophages reduced the immune responses and led to the initiation of the tumor environment, therefore promoting cancer progression.[61]

Figure 1 illustrates S. mansoni’s impact on HCC development. Darce et al followed up on 126 patients with schistosomiasis for 16 years. Of those patients, 73 (57.9%) presented portal vein thrombosis. About 8.1% of the patients with schistosomiasis and chronic portal vein thrombosis developed HCC. The authors highlighted the importance of regular follow-up screening for HCC in such cases.[62]

Filgueira et al in Brazil reported 6 HCC cases (with negative HCV and HBV serology) subjected to liver transplantation, where explant pathology revealed schistosomal liver fibrosis. These results suggest that S. mansoni infection might promote HCC development regardless of other risk factors.[63]

The development of liver cancer in patients infected with S. mansoni may be significantly influenced by the irreversible activation of proto-oncogenes associated with HCC, such as c-Jun, and associated transcription factors, such as the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), by substances released from tissue-trapped schistosome eggs. Therefore, identification and therapeutic targeting of the underlying mechanisms is a useful strategy to prevent cancer linked to schistosomiasis.[64]

Hepatocytes from both acute and chronic liver invaded by S. mansoni eggs exhibit permanent activation of the HCC-associated transcription factors c-Jun and STAT3. Also, in c-Jun-positive hepatocytes surrounding liver-trapped eggs, downstream mechanisms like proliferation and DNA double-strand breaks are constantly triggered. This raises the possibility that intrahepatically trapped S. mansoni eggs could directly affect the development of hepatocarcinogenesis.[64]

6. Combined schistosomiasis and HCV infection

Concomitant human helminthic and viral infections are frequent in many geographical areas, particularly in tropical and subtropical countries. Several examples exist, such as human coinfection with S. mansoni and hepatitis B and C viruses, which are associated with increased hepatic viral burdens and severe liver pathology.[65]

Schistosomiasis is a chronic helminthic disease infecting more than 200 million people worldwide. Concomitant schistosomiasis and HCV infection is common in many developing countries; it exhibits a unique clinical, virological, and histological pattern manifested by virus persistence with high HCV RNA titers, higher necroinflammatory and fibrosis scores in liver biopsies, and poor response to therapy. Patients with hepatitis C and S. mansoni coinfection show markedly accelerated hepatic fibrosis.[65]

The increased HCV prevalence in Egypt was due to mass schistosomiasis treatment campaigns in the 1950s and the 1960s.[66] The prevalence of coinfection was nearly 50% in Egypt, and others stated nearly 27.3%.[67,68] Combined infection by S. mansoni and HCV predominate in areas where schistosomiasis occurs endemically.[69] Combined infection reduces the spontaneous viral resolution, promotes fibrosis, and increases mortality.[70]

When schistosomiasis and HBV/HCV co-exist, the result is worsened and advanced liver disease is caused. This is especially true when viral load titers are higher, which raises the death rate by increasing the risk of HCC and liver cirrhosis.[69]Figure 2 demonstrates the effect of combined schistosomiasis and HCV infection.

Compared to patients with chronic hepatitis C but not schistosomiasis, patients with both had a 2.5-fold higher exposure risk for HBV. Lastly, coinfection with HBV and HCV and persistent schistosomiasis have serious consequences to liver pathology. Coinfection accelerates the progression of liver disease and leads to severe liver diseases and liver failure.[69] Coinfection of HCV with schistosoma often results in reducing the virus’s ability to resolve spontaneously and increasing fibrosis and the risk of mortality. These were explained by the synergistic interacting link that exists between schistosoma-HCV with liver fibrosis and mortality. Patients with coinfection showed increased HCV RNA titers, histological activity, cirrhosis/HCC incidence, and higher mortality rates.[70] Numerous researchers have looked into the severity of liver damage in individuals who have both a single viral infection and multiple viral coinfections. Patients with a viral coinfection have a high rate of liver injury and cirrhosis.[71,72]

In Egypt, people with coinfections of HCV and schistosomiasis have a higher frequency of occult hepatitis B (12.8%) than patients without schistosomiasis.

The study demonstrated that schistosomiasis was a risk factor for the greater incidence of occult hepatitis B in patients with chronic hepatitis C because it impacted HBV replication by being able to conceal the virus inside hepatocytes.[68]

Furthermore, individuals with long-term schistosomiasis are at a heightened risk of contracting coinfections with HBV and HCV due to contact with non-sterile parenteral syringes used for schistosomiasis treatment during mass campaigns, blood transfusion for anemia, and surgical and endoscopic procedures.[73]

Regarding the impact of S. mansoni infection on the response to DAA therapy in cases of HCV infection, Shousha et al reported that patients with a history of schistosomiasis infection showed a lower SVR to hepatitis C DAAs than patients without a history of schistosomiasis infection (60% vs. 84.3%, respectively; P = 0.027).[74]

We noticed that all previous studies and reports so far have dealt with combined infection in general or have discussed the effect of schistosomiasis infection on an HCV infection and response to DAAs. However, we have not seen any reports about the effects of HCV on schistosomiasis. Also, no studies have been reported on the change in prevalence of schistosomiasis infection before and after the era of DAAs. This may be explained by the already low frequency of schistosomiasis infection following the strict preventive measures used.

7. Incidence and criteria of HCC in combined infection

El-Tonsy et al reported that a higher proportion of patients with HCC in their study had concomitant HCV and schistosomiasis (61.3 %) compared to HCC in patients with only HCV (38.7 %); they concluded that the coinfection had increased the incidence of HCC. Additionally, a study of the sex distribution within the groups under investigation revealed that, in both groups, males were more likely than females to have HCC. When it came to HCC aggression, patients with concurrent infection had multifocal and larger tumor size than those with HCV alone.[75]

HCC is the cause of mortality in end-stage liver diseases, in any etiology, and the exact association between HCC and S. mansoni is still a debate in human cases.[76] Schistosoma-associated HCC cases develop in end-stage liver diseases on top of chronic HBV, HCV, or alcohol abuse.[56] Combined infection of schistosomiasis with HBV or HCV causes liver cirrhosis and HCC with poor outcomes, especially with higher viral load titers.[69]

In cases of an HCV and schistosomiasis combined infection, HCC was multifocal and discovered at an advanced stage.[75] An experimental study concluded that S. mansoni infection enhances hepatic dysplasia and, together with other risk factors, promotes the early appearance of cancer with aggressive behavior.[54]

An observational study in Egypt included HCC cases with and without previous S. mansoni infection (PMI), which revealed that PMI was associated with HCC, and the response rate to HCC therapy was pronounced in patients without PMI (75% vs. 68%, respectively). The mean survival time was lower with PMI.[74]

Hussein discussed repositioning the antimalarial drug artemisinin as a prophylactic therapy against HCC occurrence in coinfected patients due to the antiviral and antitumor properties of the drug besides its anti-schistosomal properties.[77] According to data from another study, there is a correlation between a previous S. mansoni infection and HCC (adjusted odds ratio = 1.589; 95% CI, 1.187–2.127), and suggests that there is an increased risk of HCC. Furthermore, it has a major impact on the performance status, laboratory features, response to DAAs, and overall survival.[74]

8. Large reservoir of chronic hepatitis C infection

HCV prevalence in Egypt is directly associated with the amount of intravenous tartar emetic used to control schistosomiasis in the geographic areas. The lowest rates are in Cairo and Alexandria (8%), the highest are in rural areas of the Nile Delta (lower Egypt; 15%), and intermediate (8%-16%) are in rural areas along the Nile south of Cairo (middle and upper Egypt).[78]

Prevalence increases with age and is higher in males. This “cohort effect” provides further evidence that inhabitants of rural communities with endemic schistosomiasis had exposures leading to HCV infections more than 20 years ago.

The huge reservoir of HCV among older Egyptians makes exposure to human blood and blood products much more dangerous than in the United States.[24]

Blood transfusions–before screening for HCV, which was started in the early 1990s–infected many Egyptians, and accidental needle sticks of health care workers and the reuse of needles and syringes are much more likely to transmit HCV in Egypt.[24]

9. Preventive measures

It is vitally crucial to avoid HCV and HBV infections in countries where schistosomiasis is endemic. Because there is now no HCV vaccination, numerous steps must be taken to reduce the spread of the virus in this vulnerable group. The HBV vaccine must be administered to them on a regular basis.[69] Interestingly, in patients who had a schistosomal infection, HBV vaccines can enhance the immune response while, on the other hand, it was reported to reduce the responses to the vaccination.[79]

Therefore, more extensive clinical research is needed to determine the precise relationship between coinfection with HBV and schistosomiasis. Lastly, coinfection with HBV and HCV and persistent schistosomiasis have serious consequences on liver pathology. Coinfection causes severe liver disease and liver failure by hastening the course of liver disease.[69]

10. Conclusion

In conclusion, despite the availability of highly effective antiviral treatment for HCV, the risk of HCC is not entirely eliminated even after viral eradication. The incidence of HCV-related HCC remains high, and various risk factors promote carcinogenesis in HCV-related liver cirrhosis, one of which is schistosomiasis. This risk factor for both HCV and schistosomiasis is often neglected in endemic regions. As a result, there is a need for strategies that prevent the transmission of schistosomiasis and HCV, as well as methods for their treatment. Additionally, patients who have advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, particularly in endemic regions, require indefinite surveillance for HCC.
