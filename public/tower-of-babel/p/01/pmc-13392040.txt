Genome analysis of Channel millet reveals a wild dodecaploid shaped by environmental variability
Authors: Rahul Chandora, Agnelo Furtado, Lena Constantin, Jennifer Silcock, Michael C Westaway, Robert J Henry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyploidy is a major evolutionary force generating genetic novelty over macroevolutionary scales, shaping the diversification and success of flowering plants. Channel millet, a wild C4 millet, is from semi-arid and arid inland Australia. Here we show that Channel millet is a dodecaploid by generating a high-quality, de novo, haplotype-resolved genome assembly (2.66 Gb). Subgenome-specific repetitive sequences resolve the evolutionary relationships of this allopolyploid to six ancestral progenitors. The haplotypes of each subgenome show low heterozygosity yet substantial diversity exists between subgenomes, likely underpinning the species’ ecological success and vigour. This extreme polyploidy could be an adaptation to the high environmental variation. Archaeological and ethnohistoric evidence suggests that this species has a long history of use as food, which, along with the highly variable environment, may have contributed to the evolution of this unique genome structure. The genomic complexity within the Echinochloa complex provides critical insights into wild extreme polyploid evolution.Subject terms: Plant breeding, Plant domestication, Plant evolution, Agricultural genetics

Introduction

Polyploidy, involving the combination of the genomes of related species (allopolyploidy) or the duplication of the chromosomes (autopolyploidy), is a fundamental evolutionary process across angiosperms and other eukaryotic lineages, driving genome complexity, phenotypic innovation, and enhanced adaptability1–6. Polyploidisation events have underpinned the evolutionary success of many major crops, including wheat (Triticum aestivum), cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), oilseed rape (Brassica napus), and sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum)7–11. Our understanding of polyploid genome evolution has been shaped largely by these domesticated species9,12–14, leaving naturally occurring polyploid systems, that are critical reservoirs of genetic diversity, comparatively underexplored15. Wild polyploids play a crucial role in speciation10,16. Unlike their domesticated counterparts, wild polyploids have escaped artificial selection bottlenecks and may retain extensive genetic variation17, and often exhibit broad ecological amplitude and phenotypic plasticity16. The features that make wild polyploids evolutionarily flexible, dynamic subgenome interactions, structural rearrangements, gene redundancy, and adaptive introgression10,18–20, operating without the constraints of conscious selection, allow natural polyploidisation outcomes to unfold across ecologically diverse and often stressful environments. Although natural systems, such as Spartina and Tragopogon, have served as model taxa for studying recent polyploidy and post-Whole Genome Duplication dynamics15,21–25, genomic resources for the vast majority of wild species and high-quality chromosome level genome sequences are not available. Investigating their genome architecture is key to understanding natural polyploid outcomes and accessing novel genetics that may prove valuable in future crop improvement efforts17,26,27.

Among wild grasses, millets of the genus Echinochloa (Poaceae) represent a vital but underutilised component of global agrobiodiversity28. Comprising approximately 50 species, the Echinochloa genus is widely distributed across tropical and temperate regions and includes both cultivated and wild taxa29,30. The genus is distinguished by complex patterns of ploidy and hybridisation, positioning it as a valuable model for investigating polyploid genome evolution, divergence, and environmental adaptaion31,32. Within this complex, the diploid E. haploclada (2n = 2x = 18) inhabits moist tropical habitats, while the tetraploid E. oryzicola (2n = 4x = 36) is specialized for flooded temperate paddies30,33. The hexaploids (2n = 6x = 54) include E. crus-galli, a global weed of wet fields, and E. colona, which is adapted to drier upland environments and serves as the wild progenitor of Indian barnyard millet (E. colona var. frumentacea), a resilient millet widely cultivated across the Indian subcontinent31,32.

Channel millet (Echinochloa turneriana), native to floodplains and grasslands of semi-arid and arid inland north-eastern Australia, exemplifies an extraordinary millet Crop Wild Relative (CWR) due to its tolerance to heat, prolonged drought, and saline conditions, traits increasingly essential as climate volatility escalates34–36. Native grass seeds were central to food systems across the distribution of E. turneriana until disrupted by European colonisation. With records of historical use by indigenous communities in Australia’s Channel country37–40, and given its large seeds and abundance across extensive areas in certain seasons, it was an important post-flooding food41–47.

Advances in high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing, chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) scaffolding, and improved assembly algorithms now enable polyploid genomes to be used to test mechanistic models of subgenome divergence and genome remodelling48,49. These technologies are unlocking the full potential of translational genomics50, providing new opportunities to investigate wild systems and their contributions to ecological expansion and stress resilience51. Channel millet provides an opportunity to examine these processes in an extreme wild polyploid adapted to arid, boom–bust environments.

Here, we provide a phased reference genome for Channel millet (E. turneriana) with comparative analyses across the Echinochloa complex to determine chromosomal organisation and aneuploidy, resolve the number and divergence of subgenomes, infer subgenome affinities to extant relatives, and quantify asymmetric evolution across subgenomes, thus revealing the genomic complexity that exists within the Echinochloa complex.

Results

Chromosome-scale genome assembly

Flow cytometry estimated the haploid DNA content at 2.75 ± 0.19 Gb (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), and a k-mer survey (k = 113) from 119.58 Gb of Illumina reads yielded ~2650.21 Mb (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with ploidy level expectations for Echinochloa species. PacBio HiFi contigs (contig N50 = 42.75 Mb) were scaffolded using 429.07 Gb of Hi-C to a chromosome-scale assembly of ~2.77 Gb across 1995 scaffolds (scaffold N50 = 53.25 Mb). Subsequent manual curation anchored 127 contigs and produced clean contact maps (Fig. 1a), resulting in a final high-contiguity assembly of ~2.653 Gb represented by 53 pseudomolecules with a scaffold N50 of 53.17 Mb, GC content of 46.25%, and an L50 of 23 (Supplementary Table 1). Telomeric repeats (CCCTAAA/TTTAGGG) were detected at 88 chromosome ends (Supplementary Data 1). Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO; Viridiplantae dataset) analysis recovered 99.8% complete genes, with 99.1% duplicated, consistent with the extreme polyploidy of the genome. Extended metrics are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 as well as Supplementary Table 1.

HiFi reads covered 99.03% of the assembly at 58.4× with high base and mapping quality. Short reads (119 Gb; ~45×) (Supplementary Data 2) aligned at 99.5% and showed very low heterozygosity (0.0040%), with an exceptionally homozygous genome despite the species’ allopolyploid nature. Assembly accuracy and completeness were supported by consensus quality (QV) = 68.22, 99.19% k-mer completeness, and Clipping information for Revealing Assembly Quality (CRAQ) estimates of 99.5% regional and 100% structural correctness (Supplementary Data 3). Hi-C contact maps were well ordered (Fig. 1a), and RNA-seq mapped at 90.6%, consistent with a comprehensive representation of the gene space (Supplementary Table 1). This near-complete assembly provides the resolution required to resolve homoeologous chromosome groups, define subgenomes and quantify divergence within and among lineages.

Channel millet genome architecture and relationship to other species

The exact ploidy level and genomic structure of E. turneriana had remained unclear prior to this study. To elucidate its polyploidisation history, internal genomic synteny was analysed, revealing that the genome is organised into nine distinct syntenic groups. The total length of this near-complete genome is 2653 Mb (Supplementary Table 1). Across the Echinochloa species examined, chromosome counts span diploid E. haploclada (2n = 2x = 18), tetraploid E. oryzicola (2n = 4x = 36) and allohexaploid weeds such as E. crus-galli and E. colona (2n = 6x = 54)32. To position, E. turneriana in this series, we aligned its chromosomes to those of diploid E. haploclada and Setaria italica (foxtail millet). Our analysis revealed that six copies of E. turneriana aligned with the chromosomes of diploid E. haploclada (Fig. 1b)32 and S. italica (foxtail millet)52 (Supplementary Fig. 3), reflecting half of the expected 12× ploidy and matching the expected copy number in the collapsed primary (consensus) assembly (Fig. 2d). However, in the fourth chromosome of E. haploclada, only five copies were observed, indicating a missing copy and resulting in an aneuploid state (2n-2 = 12x = 106). This likely reflects a chromosome loss event in this specific lineage. Taken together, the nine-group internal synteny and sixfold chromosome representation support a dodecaploid genome organisation for E. turneriana (2n = 12x = 106). Collinearity was strong across most homeologous groups, whereas discrete rearrangement hotspots were evident in specific groups, providing a structural context for subsequent subgenome assignment and divergence analyses.

In delineating the subgenomic architecture of Channel millet, repetitive k-mers served as differential signatures, assuming that these repetitive sequences expanded independently across chromosomes during the evolutionary divergence of its progenitor species. In total, 209,309 differential k-mers (k = 15, frequency ≥ 50) were assigned to the 53 chromosomes into six distinct subgenomes (SG1–SG6) (Fig. 3a). Principal-component analysis supported this classification (PC1, 49.0%; PC2, 32.1%; Fig. 3b). Nomenclature was standardised: ADo, BDo, CDo, GDo, HDo, and IDo (Supplementary Fig. 2, refer to Supplementary Figs. 4–9), with “Do” denoting their dodecaploid nature. Chromosome orientation was determined based on E. haploclada (Supplementary Figs. 4–9). The E. turneriana genome exhibits significant variation in chromosome sizes across its six subgenomes suggesting possible structural differences (Supplementary Table 2).

Cross-species read-mapping was used to assess lineage affinity of chromosome groups and provide orthogonal support for subgenome partitioning. Raw reads from E. crusgalli showed the highest coverage across ADo/BDo/CDo (72.1–90.4%; mean depth 50.9–82.5×). E. oryzicola preferentially mapped to ADo/BDo (64.6–84.4%; 23.7–33.8×). Haplotype resolution and subgenome divergence

Haplotype-resolved assemblies, crucial for accurately representing allelic variation, were constructed leveraging Hi-C technology. Hi-C phasing produced two chromosome-scale haplotypes (hap1/hap2) with scaffold N50s of 52.34/50.20 Mb while BUSCO completeness remained at 99.8% with high base-level accuracy and CRAQ support (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). Haplotype-to-haplotype comparison detected ~217k SNPs and limited structural variations (35 inversions, 113 translocations), as shown in Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 4, and Supplementary Data 5, indicating low allelic divergence within chromosomes. In contrast, inter-subgenome comparisons showed ~0.8–1.1 M SNPs per pair and thousands of SVs (121–169 inversions; 1645–2587 translocations), with 197–266 Mb subgenome-specific sequence and pronounced duplication asymmetry (peak 3904 in IDo), (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. 12). These patterns indicate that the haplotypes capture allelic polymorphism, whereas the subgenomes represent deeply diverged lineages, consistent with an allopolyploid origin. The six subgenomes came from distinct ancestral lineages, not from within-species genome duplication. This distinction matters enormously for understanding its adaptive potential, each subgenome carries a distinct evolutionary history and potentially distinct adaptive alleles.

LTR retrotransposon dynamics and subgenome evolution

Transposable elements (TEs) play critical roles in shaping genome architecture, influencing genome size, structural complexity, and evolutionary adaptability. TEs make up 52.22% (1393 Mb) across 1642 families of the E. turneriana genome, with long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons dominant (22.02%; Ty3/Gypsy 17.70%, Ty1/Copia 4.21%) and DNA transposons comprising 2.74% (Supplementary Table 6). Their abundance, phylogenetic relationships and insertion histories were therefore examined to assess subgenome origins and determine how repeat accumulation contributed to genome evolution. Comparative analyses reveal species-level contrasts, ranging from lower repeat content in E. haploclada (41.09%) to E. oryzicola (~51.5%), which is comparable to that of E. turneriana (Supplementary Table 7). Within E. turneriana, repeat fractions differ among subgenomes: ADo is the most repeat-rich (57.1%; LTRs 24.1%; DNA TEs 3.26%), whereas the least-repetitive subgenome carries 50.7% repeats (Supplementary Data 6 and 7). TE loads are similar across the haplotypes (~51–52%; Supplementary Tables 8 and 9) of the Channel millet genome, but vary among subgenomes, indicating uneven TE accumulation.

TE phylogenies (Copia: Ale/Angela/Ivana; Gypsy: Athila/Ogre/Reina) mirror ancestral subgenome histories while short terminal branches and clades spanning multiple subgenomes indicate mobilisation after polyploidisation (Supplementary Fig. 13). Insertion-age distributions indicate older activity in SG5 and SG6 (2.623 and 2.185 Ma), whereas SG3 experienced a recent burst (0.602 Ma), consistent with stepwise polyploid formation, SG2/SG4 contributions (~1.5 Ma), an intermediate lineage, and SG3-driven recent expansion (Fig. 3c). LTR-retrotransposon chronologies reveal that Echinochloa genus is a mosaic of inherited ancient TE waves and lineage-specific Pleistocene pulses, with subgenome-dependent removal and reactivation shaping present-day repeat landscapes (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15 and Supplementary Table 10). Across 15 subgenomes, Gypsy content varies widely (e.g., E. oryzicola AT: 125.97 Mb vs E. turneriana counterpart: 91.40 Mb), and Copia spans 6.60–28.26 Mb (peak in IDo) (Fig. 6a). Genome size correlates with TE burden across subgenomes (Copia r = 0.72; DMuDR r = 0.47; Gypsy r = 0.86) (Supplementary Fig. 16), with strong associations within E. turneriana (Gypsy R = 0.70; DNA Trns R = 0.66; Copia R = 0.23; Supplementary Fig. 17). Collectively, Gypsy and Copia retrotransposon dynamics emerge as key drivers shaping the genomic landscape and evolutionary trajectory of Echinochloa polyploids. Integration of LTR-retrotransposon dating, whole-genome mapping, and phylogenetic analysis reveals Echinochloa polyploid evolution as a network involving shared diploid gene pools, multiple independent polyploidisation events (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 18).

Genome annotation

High-resolution gene annotation of the allododecaploid Channel millet genome utilised 247.3 Gb of short-read RNA-seq data from four tissues (leaves, spikes at anthesis, spike at 14 days, roots) (Supplementary Data 2), achieving 90.6% alignment to the collapsed assembly. Integrating Viridiplantae protein homology evidence yielded 261,211 genes, including 163,194 high-confidence genes with 99.5% BUSCO completeness (Supplementary Table 1). The final gene set comprises 163,194 genes, encoding 179,722 mRNAs (with an average of 1.1 transcripts per gene), indicating the presence of alternative splicing. Mean gene length is 2526 bp with 1149 bp CDS. The genome contains 848,621 exons and 668,899 introns (4.7 exons, 3.7 introns per transcript). Monoexonic: multiexonic transcript ratio is 0.39 (48,818:123,630) (Supplementary Table 11) and functionally annotated (Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. 19). Alternative splicing was detected in 15,267 genes, defined as loci encoding more than one transcript isoform. Alternatively spliced genes were distributed across all six subgenomes, representing ~9–9.5% of genes in each subgenome, with no significant difference in AS frequency among subgenomes (χ² test, P = 0.65). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that AS-associated genes were significantly enriched in functions related to transcriptional regulation, RNA metabolic processes, protein kinase signalling, chromatin organisation, and ribosome-associated function (FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 20).

Genome-wide densities of genes, LTR retrotransposons, exon/intron and other features across the 53 chromosomes are shown (Supplementary Fig. 21). Comparative analysis with other Echinochloa species revealed 68,544–104,780 genes in polyploids versus 36,946 in diploid E. haploclada, indicating post-polyploidisation gene fractionation. Gene content showed modest subgenome bias: BDo and CDo subgenomes (424.92–451.67 Mb) contained more genes (27,879–28,766) than the larger Ado subgenome (469.96 Mb, 26,125 genes), suggesting density-independent enrichment. Haplotype-specific assemblies contained fewer genes (haplotype 1: 154,285; haplotype 2: 150,082) than the primary assembly, while both maintained 99.5% BUSCO completeness (Supplementary Table 12). The comprehensive gene annotation reveals patterns of gene organisation across the six subgenomes of E. turneriana that may reflect processes associated with polyploid genome evolution. The similar frequency of alternative splicing among subgenomes suggests that transcript diversification might occur genome-wide rather than being restricted to specific subgenomic compartments. Enrichment of alternatively spliced genes in regulatory and RNA-processing pathways further indicates that post-transcriptional regulatory processes could represent key components of gene regulation in this dodecaploid genome. These patterns suggest that alternative splicing might contribute to coordinating duplicated gene copies and maintaining functional balance following polyploidisation in Channel millet.

Detection of homoeologous exchange events

Homoeologous exchanges (HEs) are a recognised mechanism of genomic restructuring in polyploid species, arising from recombination between partially homologous chromosomes derived from distinct ancestral genomes. In E. turneriana, 68 putative HE events were detected across 38 of 53 chromosomes (72%), consistent with the large genome size and complex subgenome architecture of this dodecaploid species (Supplementary Data 8). Subgenome asymmetry was observed in HE composition: SG3 (CDo) and SG1 (ADo) were most frequently identified as the donor k-mer subgenome (27/68 events; 40% and 19/68 events, 28%, respectively), while SG4 (GDo) and SG6 (IDo) were most frequently identified as recipient chromosomes (17 events each, 25%) (Supplementary Data 8). To assess whether HE regions overlap with specific functional gene categories, GO enrichment analysis was performed on 3471 genes within high confidence HE regions. Significantly enriched terms included genes associated with DNA repair and genome stability, resolution of meiotic recombination intermediates (GO:0000712; adj. p = 1.34 × 10−5), nucleotide-excision repair (GO:1901255; adj. p = 9.77 × 10−5), meiotic chromosome separation (GO:0051307; adj. p = 1.30 × 10−3), and mitotic spindle disassembly (GO:0051228; adj. p = 6.02 × 10−5), alongside plant-type cell wall loosening (GO:0009828; adj. p = 1.71 × 10−8), transmembrane sulfate transport (GO:1902358; adj. p = 2.05 × 10−6), and jasmonic acid biosynthesis (GO:0009695; adj. p = 1.97 × 10−4). The enrichment of meiotic recombination-associated genes in HE regions may be consistent with the mechanistic basis of HE events in polyploid species, though further experimental validation would be required to confirm this relationship. A genome-wide integration of subgenome-specific k-mer profiles, LTR-RT densities, and homoeologous block distributions is visualised in the circos plot (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 8).

Gene family evolution analysis

Orthologous clustering across 11 grass species identified 55,889 orthogroups, capturing the majority of protein-coding genes (Supplementary Data 9). Among these, 91% of E. turneriana genes (148,504 of 163,194) were assigned to orthogroups, and 1628 species-specific orthogroups accounted for 4277 genes (2.6%), reflecting modest lineage-specific innovations. Comparable orthogroup assignment rates were observed in E. crusgalli (91.8%), E. colona (89.4%), and E. haploclada (93.8%), indicating conserved core gene repertoires across Echinochloa species. Phylogenomic analysis placed E. turneriana as a basal lineage within a well-supported Echinochloa complex, tracing diversification across the Poaceae ~30 million years ago. The earliest divergence (~29.5 Mya, Oligocene) separates Sorghum bicolor from the Paniceae. Around ~20.2 Mya, Paniceae bifurcated into a Panicum–Pennisetum clade and a Setaria–Echinochloa lineage. Further divergence occurred at ~18.4 Mya, separating Setaria and Echinochloa. Setaria diversification led to the split between S. italica and S. viridis at ~1.67 Mya, while Panicum miliaceum and Pennisetum glaucum diverged earlier at ~11.5 Mya (Fig. 6b). Within Echinochloa, diversification began ~7.7 million years ago (Mya), with E. turneriana emerging as the earliest diverging lineage.

Gene family evolution revealed marked lineage-specific turnover. E. turneriana exhibited the largest net expansion (+13,949/−133), consistent with broad-scale duplication events. A subgenome-resolved analysis further revealed expansion-biased gene family dynamics across all six E. turneriana subgenomes. CDo exhibited the highest net expansion (+748/−339), followed by HDo (+664/−33), BDo (+650/−424), ADo (+627/−418), GDo (+588/−408), and IDo (+598/−579) (Supplementary Figs. 22–24 and Supplementary Table 13). Hdo showed substantial gene gain with minimal loss, suggesting subgenome-specific retention or duplication. Substantial expansions were also observed in E. crusgalli (+4062/−525) and E. colona (+3376/−194), while E. haploclada (+30/−13,337) and S. bicolor (+1622/−8366) exhibited strong contractions, indicative of genome streamlining or loss of redundancy (Fig. 6b).

Functional enrichment analysis of the expanded gene families in E. turneriana revealed significant overrepresentation of gene ontology (GO) terms associated with protein biosynthesis and ribosomal structure (Supplementary Data 10). Highly enriched terms included translation (GO:0006412; adjusted p = 1.37 × 10⁻²16), ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis (GO:0022613; adjusted p = 2.30 × 10−99), macromolecule biosynthetic process (GO:0009059; adjusted p = 3.22 × 10−91), and structural constituent of ribosome (GO:0003735; adjusted P < 10⁻³⁰⁰), suggesting there might be enhancement of translational capacity for rapid stress response (Supplementary Data 10 and Supplementary Fig. 25). Stress-responsive pathways in Channel millet showed substantial gene content as compared to E. crus-galli and S. italica (Supplementary Data 11): plant hormone signal transduction (2591 genes), mediating ABA-dependent drought responses; starch and sucrose metabolism (1917 genes), producing osmoprotectants for osmotic adjustment; MAPK signaling (1264 genes), transducing cold, drought, and salt signals; protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (1493 genes), maintaining proteostasis under stress; and glycerophospholipid metabolism (1183 genes), (Supplementary Data 12), enabling membrane fluidity adjustments for cold acclimation.

Stress-responsive transcription factor families exhibited substantial expansion compared to E. crus-galli and S. italica (Supplementary Data 13), including, APETALA2/ethylene-responsive factor (AP2/ERF;1017 genes), Myb domain (MYB;1577 genes), basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH; 993 genes), and C2H2 zinc finger (827 genes), which regulate drought, osmotic, and temperature stress responses. The ubiquitin–proteasome system displayed exceptional diversification, with RING finger E3 ligases (2542 genes encoding 2823 transcripts) and F-box proteins (3008 genes encoding 3192 transcripts) (Supplementary Data 13 and 14), mediating targeted protein degradation, a pattern consistent with regulatory roles in environmental stress responses.

Subgenome-level analyses revealed asymmetric patterns of gene retention and expression. Across subgenomes, gene counts ranged from 24,907 (HDo) to 28,766 (CDo), with gene densities varying from 55.6 genes/Mb in ADo to 66.0 genes/Mb in GDo. Gene distribution differed significantly among subgenomes (χ² = 341.7, df = 5, p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶), and this deviation remained significant after accounting for subgenome size (χ² = 699.9, df = 5, p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶), indicating an uneven distribution of gene content among the six subgenomes. Duplicate gene classification showed differential fractionation across subgenomes, with subgenome CDo showing the highest singleton proportion (3.2%) and subgenome BDo the lowest (2.3%). Expression analysis of 7084 orthogroups, restricted to 1:1:1:1:1:1 single-copy orthologous gene sets (each containing one homeolog from the six E. turneriana subgenomes), with ≥twofold biased expression revealed non-random subgenome dominance (χ² = 20.75, P = 9.0 × 10⁻⁴). Subgenome B was significantly overrepresented (standardised residual = +3.55), while H and I were underrepresented (−2.14, −1.89). This pattern was primarily driven by leaf tissue (χ² = 22.90, P = 3.52 × 10⁻⁴), where SubgBDo showed strongest enrichment (+3.27) and SubgCDo depletion (−3.40) (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Table 14). Root and reproductive tissues exhibited balanced subgenome contributions (P > 0.05). Subgenome Bdo showed the highest gene retention and strongest inter-subgenome synteny across pairwise comparisons, suggesting it may represent the more dominant subgenome in Channel millet. These findings reveal a multilevel genomic architecture combining pathway-level functional redundancy with lineage-specific transcription factor content and tissue-dependent expression dominance, which are very likely to confer both robustness and adaptive flexibility for stress tolerance.

Evolutionary dynamics of disease resistance genes

To assess whether polyploidisation drives proportional retention or selective restructuring of immune gene families, we annotated immune-receptor repertoires were annotated using RGAugury, encompassing nucleotide-binding site–leucine-rich repeat (NLR), receptor-like kinase (RLK), receptor-like protein (RLP), transmembrane coiled-coil (TM-CC) and Resistance to Powdery Mildew 8-like (RPW8) gene families across seven grasses genomes spanning diploid to dodecaploid Echinochloa. Total RGA load scales with ploidy, rising from 1055 genes in diploid E. haploclada to 4303 in allododecaploid E. turneriana, driven chiefly by RLK expansion. Within Echinochloa, copy number correlated strongly with ploidy for RLKs, RLPs, NLRs and TM-CCs (r = 0.83–0.99; all P ≤ 0.041), with a weaker trend for RPW8 (r = 0.76; P = 0.077). Although NLRs increased in absolute terms (270 to 905), their proportional share declined (~25.6% to ~21%), consistent with preferential retention of cell-surface receptors (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). Lineage effects were evident; domesticated diploid S. italica harboured 368 NLRs, substantially more than the diploid E. haploclada (270), indicating lineage-specific expansion rather than a pattern explained by ploidy alone. Coiled-coil NLRs (CNLs) dominant and TIR-NLRs (TNLs) were absent across all species. E. phyllopogon and E. turneriana showed enrichment for CN and NBS-only classes and depletion of CNLs (odds ratio (OR) up to 3.50 and 1.82). Within cytotypes, E. phyllopogon exceeded E. oryzicola (420 vs. 296 NLRs), and E. crus-galli exceeded E. colona (584 vs. 504), suggesting recent duplication or divergent selection (Supplementary Table 15). In E. turneriana, immune genes were unevenly distributed: NLRs concentrated on chromosome 4 (across the subgenomes) (OR ≤ 7.4; FDR < 1 × 10⁻⁶), with disproportionate contribution from subgenome Ido and depletion in Hdo; TM-CCs and RLPs were enriched on chromosome 7 (OR = 2.36–2.44; FDR ≤ 10⁻⁵), whereas RLKs were markedly depleted on chromosome 4 (OR = 0.26; FDR < 10⁻³⁶). These results indicate that the dodecaploid genome of E. turneriana exhibits biased loss and rebalancing of intracellular resistance genes rather than uniform amplification following genome duplication.

Macrosyntenic conservation and chromosomal rearrangement hotspots

Elucidation of the evolutionary relationships and genomic architecture across the Echinochloa genus, encompassing 17 subgenome types (AT, BT, AH, BH, CH, Ehap, ADo, BDo, CDo, DH, EH, FH, GDo, HDo and IDo) derived from seven species spanning diploid to dodecaploid ploidy levels, revealed a largely conserved chromosomal framework along with extensive lineage-specific rearrangements (Fig. 7). Chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 7 and 8 retained high gene collinearity across subgenomes whereas chromosomes 4 and 6 emerged as evolutionary hotspots, exhibiting frequent megabase-scale inversions, translocations, and lineage-specific deletions. Chromosome 6 showed extensive restructuring, including near-complete inversions between 6DH-6AT (E. colona vs E. phyllopogon) and 6AH–6IDo (E. crus-galli vs E. turneriana), and a large, centrally anchored inversion between 6FH and 6EH within E. colona, accompanied by segmental reshuffling. Widespread translocations were observed between chromosomes 4 and 5, including a megabase-scale segment translocated from 5Ehap (E. haploclada) to 4ADo in E. turneriana, alongside reciprocal swaps among other chromosome-4/5 homologues. Significantly, chromosome 4 was completely absent from the HDo subgenome, representing a major chromosomal loss event.

Discussion

Polyploidy is a key evolutionary driver in plants, promoting diversification, ecological success, adaptability under environmental stress and domestication1,53,54. However, resolving the structure and origin of high-order polyploid genomes remains a major challenge in plant genomics. Here, we report a chromosome-scale, haplotype-resolved assembly of E. turneriana (Channel millet), a naturally evolved allo-dodecaploid and one of the most complex plant genomes assembled. This resource offers unique insights into genome evolution, subgenome organisation, and the adaptive potential of this underutilised millet species.

Despite its compact genome size (~2.6 Gb), assembling E. turneriana was expected to be technically challenging due to extensive sequence similarity among subgenomes, HEs, and structural rearrangements. Such complexities are common in polyploids and reflect the dynamic interactions that occur during subgenome merger and stabilisation55,56. Advances in HiFi long-read sequencing, Hi-C scaffolding, and assembly algorithms such as Hifiasm57 have enabled chromosome-scale assemblies of domesticated polyploid crops like hexaploid wheat58 octoploid strawberry13, and hybrid sugarcane8,59. However, applying these technologies to naturally evolving wild polyploids remains rare. Here, through the integration of PacBio HiFi sequencing, Hi-C data, and allele-aware scaffolding, a chromosome-scale, haplotype-resolved contiguous assembly of E. turneriana was achieved, providing a valuable window into genome architecture shaped by natural evolutionary forces at extreme ploidy. Using a k-mer-based approach, we leveraged subgenome-specific repetitive content to improve subgenome classification beyond conventional synteny-based methods, revealing asymmetric evolution and extensive inter-subgenomic variation.

The observed patterns of orthogroup composition, gene duplication, and collinearity across the six subgenomes of E. turneriana are consistent with asymmetric subgenome evolution and resemble those expected under models of subfunctionalisation, however, transcriptomic data across contrasting environmental conditions will be required to functionally validate these structural patterns. These subgenome dynamics, layered on top of interspecific lineage expansions, illustrate how E. turneriana’s genomic architecture has been shaped by both ancient polyploidy and lineage-specific diversification, contributing to its environmental resilience. The distinct evolutionary pressures acting on individual subgenomes, coupled with coordinated genomic flexibility, is likely to impart the species with phenotypic plasticity. Through integrative genomic and evolutionary analyses, we identified six distinct subgenomes within a single nucleus, suggesting a complex allopolyploid origin involving multiple diploid progenitors. Three subgenomes exhibited strong collinearity with E. crus-galli, while two aligned with E. oryzicola, suggesting repeated contributions from shared progenitor lineages. This stepwise, reticulate mode of genome formation resembles other complex polyploids such as Fragaria × ananassa and Camelina sativa, where polyploidisation occurred via successive hybridisations involving diploid and polyploid progenitors13,60. HEs, common in polyploids such as Brassica and Tragopogon9,61, may facilitate adaptation by creating novel gene combinations and regulatory flexibility62,63. The presence of six subgenomes likely confers genic redundancy, that could enable relaxed purifying selection and functional divergence53.

Although polyploidy often buffers genetic variation, E. turneriana shows very low heterozygosity. This pattern fits a founder selfing bottleneck model operating in a harsh and pulsed environment. The species likely began with a very small number of founding lineages, which restricted the ancestral gene pool. Predominant self-fertilisation then accelerated allele fixation within each of the six homoeologous subgenome pairs. The Channel Country is driven by highly variable cycles of floods and droughts (“boom-bust” ecology, driven by the El Niño Southern Oscillation)64, repeatedly collapsing population size and recovery was probably driven by a few survivors. Restricted floodplain habitat and short windows of favourable conditions kept demes small and isolated, which limited gene flow. Drift together with purifying or background selection in a chronically small effective population size, removed segregating variation. Over successive cycles, lineage sorting within subgenomes drove near-homozygosity, yielding a highly inbred dodecaploid with low diversity. Human use as a food will only have exacerbated the collapse of the populations in long periods of drought. Beyond evolutionary insights, the assembly of E. turneriana holds significant breeding potential. As a native Australian CWR, it possesses inherent tolerance to abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity, which are traits of immense value for climate-resilient agriculture. Unlike sugarcane, a synthetically developed polyploid, E. turneriana represents a naturally stabilised allododecaploid system, offering rare insights into the long-term consequences of wild polyploid genome evolution. The high-quality genome reported here provides foundational resources for identifying genes associated with stress tolerance, flowering time, and domestication-related traits.

This study elucidates the genomic architecture and evolutionary history of a naturally occurring dodecaploid grass. By resolving subgenome complexity and uncovering the reticulate origins of E. turneriana, we expand the repertoire of wild high-order polyploid systems available for studying genome evolution and adaptive genomics. These findings not only enrich our understanding of plant genome biology but also unlock new opportunities for the genetic improvement of underutilised millets and orphan crops crucial for future food and climate security.

Methods

Plant material and DNA extraction

Seed material was sourced from the Australian Grains Genebank in Victoria, Australia. Channel millet (E. turneriana (Domin) J.M. Black) accession, Q24727, was selected for genome sequencing (Fig. 2a, b). Plants were grown in pots containing the University of Queensland’s (UQ) specialised sorghum soil mixture, maintained in glasshouse conditions at UQ, Brisbane, Australia (latitude: −27.495859, longitude: 153.010139). When Channel millet reached three-leaf stage, the fresh young leaf tissues were collected from a single, representative individual and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction. Leaf tissues were pulverised, and high-molecular-weight gDNA was isolated using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide extraction protocol65. DNA purity and concentration were assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometry, agarose gel electrophoresis, and SYBR Safe fluorescence quantification, with λ-DNA serving as a reference standard. gDNA integrity and size distribution were further evaluated using a Qubit fluorometer and the Femto Pulse system, ensuring fragments averaging 15–20 kb was suitable for high-quality sequencing.

Flow cytometry

Nuclear DNA content and ploidy level of E. turneriana were measured using a BD FACSymphony A5 SE flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Young leaves were freshly harvested, chopped into ice-cold 0.5 mL modified protocol buffer66, and co-chopped with E. frumentacea (internal standard, 1.30 pg/1 C)67 within a 5 cm polystyrene petri dish (Advantec, Cat. No. PD-47A). An alternative analysis also carried out by using another standard Pisum sativum cv. Torstag, (2 C = 7.22 pg ± 0.03), the same genotype of Pisum sativum was used in the Soni et al. study68. Samples were analysed on the flow cytometer to determine the nuclei size with a minimum of 10,000 nuclei analysed per sample. Output from the flow cytometer was processed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). Three biological replicates conducted on different days were analysed, and genome size was calculated from fluorescence ratios relative to the E. frumentacea and Pisum sativum cv. Torstag standard. Coefficients of variation were used to assess measurement precision.

Genome sequencing

Approximately 15 µg of high-quality gDNA was utilised to construct PacBio HiFi SMRTbell libraries using the Revio HiFi prep kit (Pacific Biosciences). Size selection was performed using the BluePippin size-selection system, enriching the library for fragments optimal for HiFi sequencing and incorporating single nick repair. Final quality checks preceded sequencing on two SMRT cells using the PacBio Revio platform at the Australian Genome Research Facility (AGRF), the University of Queensland. Consensus HiFi reads were generated using CCS software. Additionally, Illumina short-read sequencing was performed on the same DNA samples using the NovaSeq 6000 platform at the AGRF in Brisbane, producing 150-bp paired-end reads.

Hi-C library construction and sequencing

Chromatin interaction (Hi-C) libraries were generated from fresh young leaves of E. turneriana using the Arima High Coverage Hi-C Kit (https://arimagenomics.com/), which utilises a four-cutter restriction enzyme mix (recognition sites: ^GATC, G^ANTC, C^TNAG, and T^TAA), to achieve comprehensive genome coverage. Leaf tissues from a single individual were crosslinked with formaldehyde to capture native chromatin interactions, followed by restriction enzyme digestion, end repair, and proximity ligation incorporating biotinylated nucleotides at junction sites. After ligation, crosslinks were reversed, and DNA was sheared into appropriate sizes for sequencing. Libraries were then size-selected and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on the NovaSeq 6000 platform at the Biomolecular Resource Facility, Australian National University, Canberra, yielding high-resolution chromatin interaction data.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from four distinct tissues: leaf and spike at anthesis, spike at 14 days post-anthesis, and root, using established Trizol and Qiagen kit protocols69. RNA quality was initially assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer, confirming purity with absorbance ratios of approximately 2.0 for A260/280 and between 2.0 and 2.2 for A260/230. RNA integrity and concentration were further confirmed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing involved constructing high-quality, full-length complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries, which underwent quality control checks prior to sequencing. Libraries were sequenced at the AGRF, on the NovaSeq 6000 platform using 151-bp paired-end reads, achieving approximately 400 million reads per tissue and totalling around 1.6 billion reads across all tissues.

Genome assembly and scaffolding

PacBio HiFi reads were assessed for quality using SMRT Link v11.0. Initial de novo assembly was performed with HiFiasm (v0.19.7-r598), a haplotype-resolved assembler57, using the flag (-l0) to disable purge duplication for homozygous genome assembly. The initial genome assembly totalled 2737.09 Mb with only HiFi data. In the second mode, integrated assembly by using both PacBio HiFi and paired-end Hi-C reads and employed HiFiasm (v0.19.9-r616), to enhance the resolution of haplotypes using the flag “–n-hap 2”57. In the second mode, assembly was generated by specifying the telomer motif using the flag –telo-m TTTAGGG to improve telomere preservation. All assemblies were run on the University of Queensland’s high-performance computing facility. Following the integrated assembly, a scaffolding step was performed.

Hi-C reads were preprocessed by trimming five bp from the 5′ ends to remove overrepresented three base pairs UMI sequences and two dark bases. Contigs from HiFiasm were scaffolded using Hi-C data aligned with two aligners, Chromap70 and BWA71. Considering the complex genome architecture in high-order polyploids, the assembly was scaffolded using three scaffolding tools, SALSA72, YaHS73, and HapHiC (v1.0.3)48. HapHiC was selected due to superior scaffold contiguity and telomere retention. HapHiC performed allele-aware scaffolding without a reference genome. Hi-C reads were aligned, PCR duplicates removed, and reads filtered (MAPQ ≥ 1, NM < 3). BAM files were created using the 5SP flag. Restriction enzyme cutting sites were defined as –RE (^GATC, G^ANTC, C^TNAG and T^TAA) to guide assignment of 54 chromosomes (nchrs = 54). Manual curation ensured assembly accuracy, and errors in contig order and orientation were verified and corrected using Hi-C contact maps visualised with Juicebox74. We utilised the HapHiC pipeline to generate initial haplotype-specific scaffolds (hap1 and hap2). Errors in contig orientation and order were identified and corrected using Hi-C contact maps visualised with Juicebox (v2.17.00), complemented by sequence homology information derived from RagTag (v2.1.0, default parameters)75. Chromosome naming was based on the primary assembly, and the unanchored sequences were designated as unplaced scaffolds. The completeness of the genome assembly and annotation was assessed using BUSCO v5.8.0 with the Viridiplantae lineage dataset (odb10; n = 425)76. Assembly contiguity metrics were generated using QUAST v5.3.077 through the Galaxy Australia platform. The assembled scaffolds were aligned to previously published Echinochloa species genomes using D-Genies v1.478. Raw Illumina paired-end reads were quality trimmed in CLC Genomic Workbench (CLC-GWB) using a 0.01 quality limit. K-mer analysis was conducted using Jellyfish v2.2.1079, and the resulting data were further analysed using KmerGenie80 to estimate genome size and determine the optimal k-mer length.

Telomeric regions within pseudochromosomes were identified both manually and computationally using the Telomere Identification Toolkit (tidk; https://github.com/tolkit/telomeric-identifier). The accuracy of the assembly was further validated with Merqury v1.181, with default parameters. Additionally, structural and regional assembly errors were identified using CRAQ v1.0.982. Raw short paired-end reads were quality trimmed in CLC-GWB using a 0.01 quality limit. Heterozygosity of the genome was estimated using CLC-GWB following read-mapping titration. After establishing optimal mapping at 20× coverage, heterozygosity was calculated based on aligned read statistics.

Detection of structural variation

Large structural rearrangements, including inversions, translocations, and inverted translocations, as well as smaller-scale variations such as insertions and deletions, were identified by aligning chromosome-level sequences from haplotype assemblies (hap1 and hap2) against each other and the primary assembly using minimap2 (v.2.20-r1061)83 with the parameters “-ax asm5 -eqx”. For the comparison among the subgenomes, parameters used were “asm20, –eqx”. Since HDo assembly lacks chromosome 4, we performed Hdo alignment against others by excluding its counterpart. Structural variants were subsequently detected with SyRI (v.1.6)84, and the annotation files (GFF3) were utilised to annotate affected genes. This comprehensive comparative analysis provided insights into the genomic structural variation between haplotypes and subgenomes, contributing significantly to our understanding of genomic complexity and evolution.

Subgenome differentiation and phasing

To differentiate the chromosomes of the dodecaploid Channel millet assembly into six subgenomes, we employed a k-mer-based approach. SubPhaser85 identified 15-mers (frequency ≥ 50) among homoeologous chromosome sets. Syntenic analysis using JCVI (v.1.1.19) and MCScanX identified homoeologous genes, which were then assigned to subgenomes based on k-mer enrichment. A k-means clustering algorithm grouped homeologous chromosomes, with bootstrap resampling estimating classification confidence. To validate this approach, analyses were repeated with varying k-mer lengths (k = 13–25) and frequency thresholds (50–200), further optimising the result. Jellyfish v.2.2.10 counted k-mers across all chromosomes, identifying 209,309 differential k-mers after filtering. Subgenome phasing was refined using k-means clustering, PCA, and Fisher’s exact test (P < 0.05). Differential k-mers were selected based on their total genome-wide count (>200) and relative abundance (≥2×) in one homoeolog. Subgenome-specific k-mers were mapped back using substring matching, and Fisher’s exact test (P < 0.05) assessed enrichment in 1 Mb genomic windows. Minimap2 v.2.2283 identified homoeologous blocks, further refining subgenome phasing. In addition, subgenome assignments were manually curated using the -sg_assigned option. To validate and characterise the subgenomes of the Channel millet genome assembly, we mapped subgenome-specific reads from various assemblies, including E. crus-galli, E. haploclada, E. colona and E. oryzicola, using Minimap283. First, we downloaded the raw sequencing data for these species and conducted quality control using Fastplong86 and aligned the processed reads to the E. turneriana genome assembly. High precision was achieved with Minimap2’s83. PacBio HiFi long-read mode (-ax map-hifi). Alignments with mapping quality scores below 45 were filtered out using Samtools (view -q 45)87 to ensure robust identification of the distinct subgenomes within E. turneriana.

Structural annotation and gene prediction

A de novo repeat library was constructed for the primary and haplotype genome assemblies using RepeatModeler (v2.0.4)88. This library was utilised to annotate and soft-mask repetitive elements with an option (-xsmall) in the genome assemblies with RepeatMasker (v4.1.5)89, softmasking, that is, changing the repeat regions into lowercase, rather than N, with the rmblastn version 2.13.0+, ensuring minimal interference with downstream gene prediction while retaining sequence information.

For gene prediction, high-quality, adapter-trimmed RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) reads from leaf and spike at anthesis, spike at 14 days post-anthesis and root tissues were aligned to the soft-masked genome assemblies using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)90. Transcriptomic alignments were processed into sorted BAM files using SAMtools (v1.13)87. We employed the BRAKER pipeline (v3.0.3)91 for gene prediction, which integrates spliced RNA-Seq alignment and protein homology evidence from the Viridiplantae protein database using option (–prot_seq). Finally, TSEBRA reconciles the predictions from AUGUSTUS and GeneMark-ETP by evaluating the transcript scores, generating a comprehensive set of gene models that accurately capture the genomic landscape across different tissue types. We have done primary and haplotypes assemblies and also ran for individual subgenomes to assess the repetitive elements in individual subgenomes. The completeness of the annotations was assessed using BUSCO against the Viridiplantae_odb10 database, providing a quantitative measure of gene space coverage. A detailed statistical summary of the annotations was generated from the GFF file using the AGAT toolkit (v1.0.0)92. Alternative splicing was inferred from the structural annotation, where genes encoding multiple transcript isoforms were classified as alternatively spliced. GO enrichment analysis of these genes was conducted using Fisher’s exact test in OmicsBox (v3.4.5).

Functional annotation

The functional annotation of E. turneriana was carried out in two sequential phases using OmicsBox v3.4.5. In the first phase, the coding sequences (CDS) were aligned using BLASTx (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool)93 against a non-redundant protein sequence database, focusing on the Viridiplantae taxonomy and an e-value threshold of 1e−10, extracting the top 20 hits for each sequence. Gene Ontology (GO) terms were assigned through the Blast2GO pipeline94–96, which integrates multiple database sources and validates the results using the True-Path-Rule. To identify conserved domains and motifs, InterProScan (v5.72-103.0)97 was employed, querying databases such as ProDom, PRINTS, Pfam, SMART, PANTHER and PROSITE. These GO annotations were then merged with the results from Blast2GO. To minimise paralog-based errors, functional predictions from EggNOG-Mapper (v.5.0.2)98 were incorporated. In the second phase, sequences that returned no blast hits were re-examined using BLASTx against the complete non-redundant protein database with the same parameters. Those that still did not yield hits were assessed for coding potential using the Coding Potential Assessment Tool99, in OmicsBox v3.4.5, utilising both prebuilt Arabidopsis thaliana and Echinochloa-specific models. Finally, KEGG pathway mapping was performed to assign genes to metabolic and biosynthetic pathways, with domain annotations refined using Pfam HMM profiles to enhance functional accuracy. For comparative analyses, KEGG pathway for E. crus-galli and S. italica were retrieved from the milletdb100.

Gene family analysis

We conducted gene family evolution analysis using the predicted protein-coding sequences of E. turneriana and ten additional Poaceae species, E. oryzicola101, E. haploclada, E. phyllopogon102, E. crusgalli101, E. colona101, S. italica52, S. viridis103, Pennisetum glaucum104, Panicum miliaceum105, and Sorghum bicolor106, which served as the outgroup. To avoid redundancy due to alternative splicing, the longest isoform per gene was retained for all species. Orthologous gene families were identified using OrthoFinder (v2.5.5)107, and orthogroups with aberrant or implausible copy numbers were filtered prior to downstream analysis. Gene family expansion and contraction were assessed using CAFE (v4.2.1), with input from a time-calibrated ultrametric species tree(https://hahnlab.github.io/CAFE/src_docs/html/index.html). Divergence times were estimated using penalised likelihood in the chronos function of the R package APE (v5.8), employing S. bicolor as the outgroup. Two secondary fossil calibrations were applied based on the TimeTree database (http://www.timetree.org/; accessed 10 August 2025): (i) the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of E. oryzicola and S. bicolor, constrained to 17.5–29.5 Mya; and (ii) the MRCA of P. miliaceum and E. haploclada, constrained to 14.3–36.7 Mya. A smoothing parameter (λ) sensitivity analysis indicated minimal variation in the phylogenetic information criterion (PHIIC ~ 66.33) across tested values; λ = 1 was selected to maintain calibration bounds and minimise rate heterogeneity. Significantly expanded and contracted gene families (P < 0.05) were identified across all species. Functional enrichment analysis of significantly expanded families was performed using OmicsBox v3.4.5 (https://www.biobam.com/omicsbox/), integrating Gene Ontology (GO) terms via Blast2GO with annotations derived from NCBI’s non-redundant protein database. Transcription factor families were identified using PlantTFDB (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/prediction.php) and InterPro domain signatures (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/).

Comparative genome analysis

Comparative genomic analysis of 17 subgenomes from six Echinochloa species (E. turneriana, E. crus-galli, E. oryzicola, E. colona, E. phyllopogon, and E. haploclada) was conducted using GeneSpace (v1.3.1) with default parameters108. Protein homology was computed via DIAMOND (v2.111)93, all- versus- all searches, and orthogroups were inferred using OrthoFinder (v2.55)107. Collinearity was detected with MCScanX_h, from which syntenic anchors and blocks were extracted109, and quality filtering removed over- dispersed orthogroups and small chromosomes to ensure synteny accuracy. Pan-genome orthogroups were categorised into four functional groups based on presence across the 17 subgenomes: core (present in all 17), softcore (present in ≥80% of subgenomes, 14–16), dispensable (present in 2–13), and private (subgenome-specific). The nomenclature for the tetraploid genome of E. phyllopogon was based on that of E oryzicola. Syntenic relationships and structural variants (inversions, translocations, deletions) were visualised using dot plots and riparian plots, while pairwise collinearity metrics (anchors, blocks, breakpoints) quantified conservation and divergence within the genus. The phylogeny of the 17 subgenomes (ADo, AH, AT, BT, BH, BDo, CDo, CH, DH, EH, FH, GDo, HDo, IDo, AT_py, BT_py, Ehap) was constructed using 3437 single-copy orthologs (SCOs) from Orthofinder (v2.55)107. Proteins for each gene were aligned with MAFFT (v7.490) (–auto)110 and trimmed using trimAl v1.5.0 (-automated 1)111. A concatenated, partitioned maximum-likelihood tree was inferred in IQ-TREE (v2.2.2.3)112 with Modelfinder + MERGE (LG/WAG/JTT; -rclusterf 10), with node support assessed via Ufboot2 (1000 replicates; BNNI)113. To account for among-site heterogeneity, we applied posterior mean site frequency (PMSF) in IQ-TREE on the under LG + C60 + F + G 4 model, reusing the merged partition scheme; Ufboot 2 (1000 replicates) was computed under PMSF113,114 and visualised in Dendroscope (version 3.8.10)115.

Tissue-specific expression

To assess preferential gene expression among homeologs in E. turneriana (Channel millet), the analysis focused on 1:1:1:1:1:1 single-copy orthologs, each comprising six homeologous genes representing the six subgenomes (SubgA–SubgI). RNA-Seq data from four distinct tissue types, leaf, root, spike at anthesis (SpAn), and spike at 14 days post-anthesis (Sp14), were utilised. RNA-Seq reads were mapped to the E. turneriana reference genome using the RNA-Seq analysis tool in CLC Genomics Workbench v25 (Qiagen) under default parameters. Transcript abundance was quantified as transcripts per million (TPM)116. Analysis followed established approaches to identify subgenome expression bias in polyploids117,118. For each orthogroup, tissue combination, the subgenome contributing the highest TPM was recorded. Expression bias was calculated as the fold-change between the highest TPM and the lowest non-zero TPM. Orthogroups were subsequently classified as biased (fold change ≥ 2) or balanced (fold change <2); those lacking sufficient expression were excluded. Biased orthogroups were retained for analysis of subgenome dominance. Chi-squared goodness-of-fit tests were employed to evaluate whether dominant subgenomes deviated from an expected equal representation (1/6). The null hypothesis for these tests was that each subgenome contributed equally to the pool of dominant homeologs. Standardised residuals were computed to assess subgenome-level enrichment or depletion, with an absolute residual value (∣residual∣) ≥2 considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.3) using the following packages: dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2, scales, RColorBrewer and stats.

Resistance gene analogues

Resistance gene analogues (RGAs) were identified using the RGAugury pipeline119 with default parameters. Predicted protein sequences from seven species—six Echinochloa species (E. turneriana, E. crus-galli, E. oryzicola, E. colona, E. phyllopogon, and E. haploclada) and S. italica were analysed. The pipeline integrates BLAST120, nCoils121, Pfam_scan122, Phobius123 and InterProScan124 to detect domain architectures characteristic of nucleotide-binding site leucine-rich repeat (NLR) proteins, RLKs, and RLPs, which play central roles in plant immune responses. Analysis was performed in R (v4.4.0).

Genome-wide variant calling and heterozygosity estimation

Genome-wide heterozygosity was assessed through single-nucleotide variant (SNV) identification in 42 Echinochloa accessions spanning four species: E. colona (11), E. crus-galli (11), E. oryzicola (10), and E. turneriana (10). For each species, chromosome-level reference assemblies corresponding to their respective ploidy states (tetraploid, hexaploid, and dodecaploid) were utilised, excluding unanchored scaffolds. Paired-end sequencing reads underwent quality trimming at a threshold of 0.01, and the processed reads were subsequently mapped to their corresponding references using CLC Genomics Workbench v25.0.1 with both length fraction and similarity fraction set to 0.9. Alignment parameters included: no masking, match score of 1, costs of 2 for mismatches, 3 for insertions, and deletions. Ambiguous alignments were randomly assigned, standard execution mode was employed, and the minimum seed length was set to 15 base pairs. SNV detection utilised the variant detection module with stringent quality filters determined through allele-frequency titration, fixed at a minimum mapped read coverage of 10×, at least 3 supporting alternate reads, and an allele frequency of 20%. Additional parameters included a 90% variant probability threshold, coverage ceiling of 100,000×, exclusion of discordant read pairs and non-specific alignments, and base quality filtering (5 bp neighbourhood radius, central base quality ≥20, neighbourhood quality ≥15). Directional filters were applied with a 5% frequency threshold for read direction and a 1% significance threshold for both relative read direction and read position filters. Identified variants were categorised as either homozygous SNPs or heterozygous SNPs. Homozygous SNPs were defined as SNVs with homozygous genotypes, ≥20× coverage, and 100% allele frequency, excluding reference alleles. Het-SNPs were designated as heterozygous SNVs without reference alleles. Heterozygosity was calculated as the proportion of heterozygous SNV sites relative to the total genome size:

This normalised metric allowed for direct comparison of genetic diversity across species with varying genome sizes. Differences in heterozygosity among species and ploidy levels were tested using Welch’s ANOVA, which accounts for unequal variances between groups. Pairwise comparisons were then performed using Welch’s t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction to control for multiple testing. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (v 4.4.0).
