Penile superficial dorsal vein thrombophlebitis following prolonged sexual activity
Authors: Ahmed Adam Osman, Hanan HassanHirei, Abdulkadir Isse Mohamed, Shuayb Moallim Ali, Amal Naleye Ali, Abdikarim Hussein Mohamed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenile Mondor disease (MD) is a palpable, painful, subcutaneous induration resulting from thrombosis of the superficial dorsal penile vein. In this report, we present a 53-year-old case of Mondor disease following prolonged, vigorous sexual activity. He had engaged in sexual activity the previous night after taking sildenafil. Physical examination revealed penile swelling and tenderness over the protruding dorsal penile vein with no evidence of penile fracture. An in-color Doppler ultrasound was promptly performed, revealing hyperechoic thrombosis in the dilated superficial dorsal penile vein accompanied by soft tissue swelling and the absence of a detectable flow signal within the thrombotic lesion. The patient was diagnosed with penile Mondor disease. The patient received conservative treatment. This case highlights the association between prolonged vigorous sexual activity, oral sildenafil uses and the development of penile Mondor disease.Keywords: Penile mondor disease, Superficial dorsal penile vein thrombosis, Prolonged sexual activity, Sildenafil, Urology, Doppler ultrasound

Introduction

Mondor disease (MD) is a palpable, painful, subcutaneous induration caused by superficial venous thrombosis [1]. It was first reported in 1958 [2]. Penile swelling and pain due to dorsal vein thrombophlebitis are commonly associated with prolonged intercourse [3]. Sildenafil, known as Viagra, is widely used for male erectile dysfunction and is approved for pulmonary hypertension treatment [4]. As Mondor disease is an uncommon occurrence, its aetiology remains unclear. However, there is a strong suspicion of a connection to Virchow's triad followed by prolonged Intercourse and Sildenafil [5].

Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosing Mondor's disease, primarily to identify intravascular thrombus. In diagnostic uncertainty, grayscale and Doppler ultrasound are valuable tools for detecting thrombosis extent, showcasing echogenic material within venous lumens, vessel incompressibility, and absence of flow. Although using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains controversial, it is not routinely employed [6]. Here, we present a case of penile Mondor disease in a 53-year-old man suspected to be related to prolonged sexual intercourse following oral sildenafil use.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old man presented to the urology department with persistent penile pain and swelling. It began a day after sexual intercourse, followed by the use of oral sildenafil for erectile dysfunction. He noted swelling on the dorsum of his penis without priapism or dysuria. A review of the systems was negative for abdominal pain, scrotal pain, or systemic symptoms. His initial vitals were stable, with a blood pressure of 130/80 mmHg, a heart rate of 110 beats/min, and a respiratory rate of 19 breaths/min; his body temperature was within normal range.

On physical examination, the penis appeared swollen with an increase in skin temperature. A palpable cord and tenderness were noted on the dorsum of the penis and pubic symphysis, along with tenderness over the dorsal penile vein area and a mild protrusion (Fig. 1).

Laboratory investigations were unremarkable, including complete blood count (CBC) and biochemical tests at presentation. Penile ultrasonography revealed no abnormal disruption in the tunica albuginea of the penile shaft. However, dilation of the superficial dorsal vein of the penis with hyperechoic thrombosis and soft tissue oedema was observed. Doppler ultrasound imaging confirmed the absence of flow signals in the thrombotic areas (Fig. 2). The patient was diagnosed with penile Mondor disease.

Given the findings, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not pursued. Conservative management consisting of sexual abstinence, oral anticoagulants, and oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) was initiated. The patient was then discharged home. A follow-up appointment at the Urology outpatient clinic revealed improvement in the patient's symptoms within the first few weeks of outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

Mondor disease is a rare and painful superficial venous thrombosis that primarily affects the penis or chest wall, as reported in the literature [7]. Diagnosis typically relies on a comprehensive approach involving history-taking, physical examination and color Doppler ultrasound, which facilitate accurate diagnosis [8].

Vein thrombosis, including penile Mondor disease, has been associated with Virchow's triad, which comprises vascular wall damage, venous stasis, and hypercoagulation [9]. Vascular wall injury, one of the 3 components of Virchow's triad, likely underlies the pathogenesis of penile Mondor disease. Such injury commonly occurs during prolonged intense sexual activity, leading to vessel elongation or rupture, typically within 12 to 48 hours [10].

Mondor disease (MD) is generally a benign, self-limiting condition that typically resolves within 4 to 8 weeks without medical intervention. However, some instances of MD may be associated with underlying illnesses such as hypercoagulation, vasculitis, or malignancy [9,10].

Clinicians should exercise vigilance in identifying patients with Mondor disease, considering the possibility of underlying diseases [8]. A thorough clinical evaluation, including history-taking, physical examination, and appropriate diagnostic tests, is essential for accurate diagnosis and management.

Conservative management, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug administration and counselling against sexual activity, represents the primary treatment approach for penile Mondor disease. Thrombectomy may be considered in rare cases of chronic or therapy-resistant forms. Antibiotic therapy is reserved for cases with evidence of infection, such as superficial cellulitis or sexually transmitted diseases.

Due to its vasodilatory effects, sildenafil is known to reduce systolic blood pressure by 8 to 10 mmHg, potentially contributing to vascular insufficiency [11]. Prolonged oral administration of sildenafil may increase the risk of aberrant venous thrombosis of the superficial dorsal penile vein, thereby exacerbating the development of penile Mondor disease. It is conceivable that individuals with additional risk factors, such as penile vascular wall injury from intense sexual activity, may be predisposed to penile Mondor disease when using sildenafil [11]. Further research is needed to better understand the pathophysiology and optimal management strategies for Mondor disease, particularly in cases where underlying conditions or medication use may play a role in its development [5,9]. By enhancing our understanding of this condition, healthcare providers can ensure timely and effective management, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

While penile Mondor disease is not typically life-threatening, it can significantly impact a patient's quality of life. Therefore, clinicians should remain vigilant about the potential risks of prolonged sildenafil use. They should consider additional predisposing factors, such as vascular injury from sexual activity, in the management and prevention of this condition.

Conclusion

Mondor disease (MD) is a rare, benign, self-limiting condition. This case highlights the association between prolonged vigorous sexual activity, oral sildenafil uses and the development of penile Mondor disease. Sildenafil may contribute to penile vascular thrombosis, warranting further research to clarify this association.
