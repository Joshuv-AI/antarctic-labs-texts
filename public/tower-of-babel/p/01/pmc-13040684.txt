First report of an iridophoroma in a Tegu (Salvator merianae)
Authors: Sorana Catoi, Romelia Pop, Lucia-Victoria Bel, Iulia Melega, Alexandru-Flaviu Tabaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis case report describes the clinical and histological findings of a rare periocular iridophoroma in a tegu.Case presentationIridophoroma is a rare type of chromatophoroma, a tumor that arises from iridophores, specialized pigment cells responsible for reflecting light. This case report describes a 6-year-old male Argentine black and white tegu (Salvator merianae) presented with a large, irregular, proliferative mass in the periocular region. The mass originated from the upper left eyelid, partially covering the ocular surface, and measured 2.5 cm in diameter. The patient was referred to the department of Surgery and Intensive care for mass removal and underwent two separate surgical resections. Fine needle aspiration from the structure revealed the diagnosis of iridophoroma, which was later confirmed through histology. This case aims to broaden current knowledge in reptile medicine by presenting an iridophoroma in Salvator merianae, highlighting its clinical presentation, cytological features, and histopathological aspects.ConclusionsThis case represents the first documented occurrence of an iridophoroma in a tegu, highlighting the need for increased awareness and precision in diagnosing tumors in reptiles. The findings demonsrate the importance of paraclinical investigations, such as histopathology, not only in confirming rare conditions but also in differentiating iridophoromas from other potential diagnoses, such as squamous cell carcinoma, papilloma, or melanophoroma. Background

Cromatophoromas are rare neoplasms composed of pigment-producing and light-reflecting cells that affect the skin of reptiles, fish, and amphibians [1, 2] (Table 1). These neoplasms are categorized based on the type of pigment they contain. Typically, chromatophoromas originate in the skin of the head and body, with reported sizes ranging from 0.2 to 2.0 cm in diameter [15]. Chromatophoromas are neuroectodermal tumors that typically appear as raised, pigmented masses and are most commonly reported in snakes, followed by lizards, chelonians, and crocodilians [15–17].

Reptilian skin contains three primary types of pigment cells, collectively known as chromatophores. Tumors arising from these cells are classified into distinct subtypes depending on their pigment composition: melanophoromas, also referred to as melanomas, which involve melanin-producing cells, xanthophoromas, derived from cells responsible for producing carotenoid or pteridine pigments, and iridophoromas, which develop from crystalline purine-containing cells that house reflective granules of guanine, adenine, hypoxanthine, or uric acid [15]. Environmental factors have occasionally been discussed in relation to chromatophoroma development, but evidence remains limited and inconsistent. A retrospective analysis suggested that some lizards with pigment-cell tumors had been exposed to artificial UV light, although chromatophoromas also occurred in animals without artificial UV exposure, indicating that husbandry alone is unlikely to be a primary etiologic factor. Genetic susceptibility has also been proposed in certain species, particularly in the “Lemon Frost” color morph of the leopard gecko, where cutaneous and oral iridophoromas have been linked to heritable mutations. Overall, current data do not support a direct causal relationship between humidity, enclosure type, or general captive conditions and the development of chromatophoromas [2, 15].

Case presentation

Clinical examination

A 6-year-old male tegu (5 kg) was presented with a progressive periocular mass on the left upper eyelid that had been enlarging for approximately one year. At first clinical evaluation, the lesion measured ~ 2.5 cm in diameter, was firm and non-mobile on palpation, and extended toward the medial canthus. The animal was unable to fully open the affected eye due to the mass effect, which likely contributed to the observed lethargy and reduced appetite. Differential diagnoses based on gross appearance included neoplasia, abscess, foreign body reaction, and blepharitis of various etiologies.

A complete blood count and a reptile biochemistry panel (Abaxis VetScan) were performed using blood collected from the coccygeal vein, with all parameters within normal limits. The owner declined diagnostic imaging and pre-surgical cytology and opted instead for excisional biopsy. Consequently, the first surgical removal was performed without wide margins.

At follow-up, prior to the second surgery, the mass had recurred and now infiltrated approximately 50% of the remaining upper eyelid, despite the initial removal of the lower eyelid to gain access. No evidence of local recurrence at the initial surgical site was identified at that time. However, before euthanasia (second recurrence), the tumor had become markedly invasive, extending into the surrounding periocular tissues and infiltrating the orbital cavity, affecting the ocular adnexa.

Cytology performed on imprint smears after excision of the initial mass was used to correlate the cytologic findings with the subsequent histopathological diagnosis.

The anesthetic protocol included premedication with medetomidine (0.1 mg/kg) and ketamine (10 mg/kg). Analgesia was provided using morphine at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Anesthesia was induced and maintained with isoflurane, and mechanical ventilation was provided throughout the procedure. Additionally, local infiltration with 2% lidocaine (2 mg/kg) was administered for regional anesthesia.

The lizard was positioned in sternal recumbency, and the ocular and periocular regions were surgically prepared using diluted povidone-iodine and a transparent surgical drape. The mass was excised using a curvilinear incision extending from the medial canthus along the upper eyelid to the lateral eyelid junction. Sharp dissection was used to separate the mass from adjacent tissues. Bipolar cautery was applied for hemostasis, and the surgical site was irrigated with sterile saline. Both the subcutaneous tissue and the skin were closed using 4 − 0 monofilament absorbable suture in an everted pattern (Fig. 1b).

The patient’s recovery from surgery and anesthesia was uneventful. Postoperative pain management included morphine administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg every 12 h for five days, and meloxicam at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg once daily for ten days. Local antibiotic therapy with kanamycin was used, along with systemic ceftazidime at 20 mg/kg every 48 h, for a total of ten doses.

Following the first surgery, the mass recurred rapidly, increasing in size to approximately 5 cm within 1.5 months (Fig. 1c). Given the rapid regrowth of the lesion, the owner consented to a second surgical procedure. This second surgery involved enucleation of the affected eye and was performed using the same anesthetic protocol as the initial operation (Fig. 1d). Postoperative treatment remained unchanged.

Two months after the second surgery, the mass recurred, this time invading the surrounding tissues so extensively that another surgical procedure was no longer possible. Due to the poor prognosis, rapid time of recurrence and the fact that the quality of life of the animal was affected (was not eating properly, was very lethargic and long-term management with nonsteroidal anti- inflammatory medication was not effective), the patient, under general anesthesia with 0.2 mg/kg medetomidine and 20 mg/kg ketamine, was euthanized using intracardiac administration of pentobarbital 1 mg/kg. Once lack of corneal reflex was confirmed, pithing was performed. Following euthanasia, the body was submitted to the Pathology Department at the University of Agricultural Sciences and Veterinary Medicine in Cluj-Napoca, Romania, for further investigation.

The lizard was submitted for necropsy due to a progressively enlarging ocular mass. A well-demarcated, irregular, multilobulated mass measuring up to 2.5 cm in diameter was identified at the medial canthus of the left eye. The mass was firm yet slightly compressible, with a predominantly white to pale pink coloration. Its surface was irregular and finely granular in appearance. The mass exerted pressure on the ocular globe, causing visible indentation and mild displacement of the eye within the orbit.

The surrounding eyelid was thickened and distorted, exhibiting an irregular contour due to the mass effect. Additionally, mild conjunctival hyperemia and a serous to mucoid ocular discharge were noted, likely secondary to chronic irritation and impaired tear drainage. No evidence of metastasis or other significant pathological findings was observed in the remaining organs during necropsy.

For cytology, several imprint smears were prepared and routinely stained using a Diff-Quik staining kit. The prepared slides were then analyzed using an Olympus BX51 microscope, and bright-field images were captured with an Olympus SP350 digital camera. Image processing was performed using the Olympus cellSens software. The slides were well-cellularized, with a well-preserved cellular population. The cells were round to oval in shape, with indistinct borders and abundant, finely granular cytoplasm. The neoplastic cells exhibited a single large nucleus with 1–2 prominent nucleoli (Fig. 2a, b). The background was moderately proteinaceous. Anisocytosis and anisokaryosis were moderate. Polarized light was used to enhance the visualization of cytoplasmic granules (Fig. 2c).

For histological examination, the ocular mass was preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) and subsequently embedded in paraffin, following a standard tissue processing protocol. Thin sections, measuring 2 micrometers, were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The histopathological exam showed a well-demarcated mass composed of a dense proliferation of pigmented round to oval cells arranged in sheets and nests within a well-vascularized stroma. The neoplastic cells exhibit abundant cytoplasm containing iridescent, reflective pigment granules, characteristic of iridophores. The nuclei are round to oval with finely dispersed chromatin and occasional prominent nucleoli. Mild to moderate anisocytosis and anisokaryosis may be observed, with rare mitotic figures. The mitotic count was 23 mitoses per 10 high-power fields (HPF) at ×400 magnification (objective ×40). (Fig. 2d, e). The tumoral mass focally infiltrates the adjacent tissue. Under polarized light, the neoplastic iridophores display birefringent cytoplasmic granules, (Fig. 2f).

Discussion

Chromatophoromas remain relatively uncommon neoplasms in reptiles, and understanding their behavior requires familiarity with the unique biology of reptilian chromatophore cells. These specialized pigment and light-reflecting cells, melanophores, iridophores, and xanthophores, collectively shape the diverse coloration seen in reptiles and amphibians [18, 19]. Because most published information arises from isolated case reports, each new description provides valuable context for recognizing the clinical and pathological variability of these tumors [15, 16, 20]. Although chromatophoromas most frequently originate in the skin of the head and body [2, 21] documented cases show that they may also appear in a wider range of anatomical sites across reptile and fish species [17].

Clinically, many reptile skin tumors can appear similar. Raised, firm, pale, or variably pigmented masses may suggest a wide spectrum of potential diagnoses, ranging from epithelial lesions to pigment-cell tumors. However, the microscopic architecture of these neoplasms differs. Epithelial tumors such as squamous cell carcinoma or papilloma arise from keratinocytes and exhibit characteristic keratinization patterns, cellular dysplasia, and architectural distortion [22]. Papillomas in reptiles may be associated with papillomavirus infection in some cases; however, many occur spontaneously without identifiable viral involvement, and their histological features remain consistent regardless of etiology [23, 24].

Chromatophoromas, by contrast, are neuroectodermal tumors originating from dermal pigment cells and lack the cytologic and structural features of epithelial neoplasia. For this reason, the primary diagnostic challenge is not distinguishing chromatophoromas from epithelial tumors, but rather differentiating between the chromatophoroma subtypes themselves iridophoroma, melanophoroma, and xanthophoroma [15, 25].

These pigment-cell tumors share overlapping gross features but are fundamentally different under the microscope. Melanophoromas contain melanin granules that may vary from subtle to heavy pigmentation and typically stain positively with Fontana–Masson [15, 21]. Xanthophoromas exhibit yellow-orange cytoplasm due to pterinosomes or carotenoid vesicles and do not display birefringence. Iridophoromas, in contrast, contain stacked crystalline guanine platelets that appear as bright, refractile structures under polarized light a hallmark feature that reliably identifies iridophore origin [15]. Although chromatophoromas can mimic one another macroscopically, these distinct microscopic and optical characteristics allow clear separation of the three tumor types [25]. Although cytology and biopsy are valuable first-line diagnostic tools in reptiles, their interpretation can be challenging because of the diversity of pigment-producing chromatophores and the similar gross appearance these tumors often share. Cytology may show pigmented cells, but separating iridophores from melanophores or xanthophores frequently depends on additional features such as birefringence under polarized light or detailed histologic evaluation. Moreover, small biopsies may not always capture the most informative regions of the lesion, as reflective pigment granules can be unevenly distributed throughout the tumor. When routine histology leaves uncertainty, additional diagnostic tools can strengthen interpretation. Immunohistochemistry assists particularly in excluding melanophoroma, as markers such as Melan-A, PNL2, HMB-45, and SOX10 are typically expressed by melanocytic tumors but not by iridophoromas. Xanthophoromas remain more challenging due to a lack of specific IHC markers, and their identification often relies on pigment morphology. Transmission electron microscopy is considered the gold standard when differentiation is difficult, as it directly visualizes the ultrastructural characteristics of chromatophore types—melanosomes in melanophores, pterinosomes in xanthophores, and reflective crystalline platelets in iridophores [15, 25, 26].

In the tegu described here, the combined findings strongly supported a diagnosis of iridophoroma. The neoplastic cells contained abundant refractile, non-melanin, non-carotenoid granules that demonstrated marked birefringence under polarized light features entirely consistent with iridophore morphology. The absence of melanin granules excluded melanophoroma, while the lack of carotenoid-rich cytoplasm ruled out xanthophoroma.

This case also highlights the potentially aggressive local behavior of chromatophoromas in reptiles. Despite surgical excision, the mass recurred rapidly, progressively infiltrated surrounding tissues, and ultimately required enucleation. A second recurrence developed within two months, mirroring the invasive growth and high recurrence rates reported in malignant or incompletely excised chromatophoromas in other species [4–6]. These observations emphasize the importance of early recognition, achieving wide surgical margins when anatomically feasible, and maintaining close postoperative monitoring. Similar observations were described in a green iguana with cutaneous iridophoroma, where local recurrence and infiltrative growth were noted despite intervention [3].

Ultimately, this represents the first documented iridophoroma in Salvator merianae, expanding the known spectrum of chromatophore-derived tumors in lizards. Recognizing the distinct microscopic and optical features of these neoplasms is essential for accurate diagnosis, guiding treatment, and improving clinical outcomes in reptile medicine. As additional cases are documented, our understanding of their biological behavior and optimal management strategies will continue to evolve.

While chromatophoromas typically originate in the skin of the head and body [6, 21], they have also been reported in other anatomical locations, indicating a wider potential for distribution (Table 1). The 12 reports in Table 1 were selected based on a literature search conducted on Google Scholar using terms including “chromatophoroma”,“chromatophoroma neoplasms in reptiles and fish”, “cutaneous chromatophoromas in reptiles and aquatic species”, “pigment cell tumors in reptiles, amphibians, and fish”, “reptile and fish chromatophoroma case reports”, and “chromatophoroma, iridophoroma, melanophoroma in reptiles and fish”. Reports were chosen for their clinical relevance, histopathological detail, and representation across reptile and fish species.

Conclusions

This is the first documented case of iridophoroma in a tegu (Salvator merianae), emphasizing the need for greater awareness of the diversity in reptile oncology. The findings highlight the importance of paraclinical investigations in confirming rare conditions and distinguishing iridophoromas from squamous cell carcinoma, papilloma, or melanophoroma. Accurate diagnosis is essential for guiding treatment and improving outcomes in reptile medicine and one can only assume based on mammalian surgical oncology that early surgical intervention with wide margins could be curative and limit recurrence.
