Familial hypercholesterolemia: a history of loss but a future of hope
Authors: Allison Jamison
Published: 2025

Familial hypercholesterolemia: the lived experience

I’ve never known a life without heart disease. Generations of my family have died too soon, lost to heart attacks in their forties. My paternal grandfather died of a heart attack at 42; my own father had his first heart attack at 28, and had at least three more before he was forty. After years of damage to his heart, he underwent a heart transplant at 49, and lived another twenty-one years before a final heart attack took him from us at 69. While my mother’s family had a less remarkable history – my maternal grandfather had a heart attack in his early fifties, which was attributed to environmental factors – we all knew that heart disease “ran in the family.”

As early as the 1970s, my parents were told that the heart disease in my dad’s family was tied to genetic high cholesterol, but preventing cardiac events proved tougher. I developed “bumps” on my knees and Achilles’ at 3, which were dismissed as dermatological. A few years later, my parents had cholesterol levels checked for me and my two siblings. My siblings both had very high cholesterol, but my own was over 850 mg/dl. We learned that those bumps were actually xanthomas. My siblings and I were all given the same diagnosis of familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), despite my much higher levels. Given the apparent lack of evidence for FH on my mother’s side, I was diagnosed with “severe FH.”

All three of us were quickly started on bile acid sequestrants, and statins were added when I was ten. We had regular appointments with cardiologists, and at age thirteen, I was also diagnosed with aortic stenosis. My brother and sister had good results on the statins, but nothing got my LDL under 400 mg/dl. Still, I was hopeful that because I started medication so early, I would be able to avoid the recurrent heart attacks that had plagued my dad. I developed this idea that if I could get past 28 years old without a cardiac event – past that age when my dad had his first heart attack – that meant the medications were doing enough to save me.

At 26, I finally lived close enough to an LDL apheresis center to add that treatment to my regimen. I would leave apheresis with an LDL under 100, but after two weeks it would be back above 300. However, despite everything I was doing, in January of 2004, just six months shy of my 29th birthday, I underwent my first open-heart surgery, a bypass of my LAD and aortic valve replacement. I hadn’t made it past that 28-year-old milestone, and now lived with the fear that the next decade would be filled with more surgeries, if I even lived.

Though recovery from surgery was long, eventually I was almost back to normal. We added two children to our family – one by birth and one by adoption – and I kept up with my medications and apheresis for the next six years. I had renewed hope that the addition of the apheresis would prevent any further cardiac events.

In March of 2011, we moved to a new city and my new cardiologist conducted a stress echo. I had no prior symptoms, but as I stepped off the treadmill, I suffered a heart attack and sudden cardiac arrest. We discovered I had a 99 % blockage in my right coronary artery, and I had my second bypass surgery three days later.

The second recovery was harder – I now had two young children who needed their mom to be okay. I knew I didn’t want to wait around for this to happen again, so I asked my cardiologist for other treatment options. He found a clinical trial that was starting nearby for a new FH treatment. Genetic testing was required for the trial, and only then, at age 35 and after two open heart surgeries, was I told that I had homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH). Although the diagnosis fit perfectly with my experience, it was shocking to my entire family that we had not received it much earlier in my life.

Looking back, I seemed to display every indication of HoFH, only my mom’s family history didn’t seem to fit. I feel fortunate that despite the missing diagnosis, I still was treated for FH. But I lost years of potential aggressive treatment, and missed opportunities for other trials. I have no doubt that for HoFH, early diagnosis matters.

After my diagnosis, I started researching HoFH. Initially, the only articles I could find were significantly outdated. These were articles from the 1980s saying those with HoFH didn’t live past their second decade, and other more dire predictions. But I kept looking, and that’s when I came upon a discussion group for the Family Heart Foundation. Finally, I had found an organization dedicated to helping people like me and my family.

I reached out, and soon enough I met dozens of people like me, who had similar family histories, stories of early heart disease, loss, and fears. But more importantly, we all had stories of hope. Now that we had found each other, we had a louder voice. I became a trained Ambassador for Awareness, and soon joined the Board of Directors, representing the patient voice. Together, the Family Heart Foundation has made significant strides in raising awareness of FH and HoFH, and now elevated Lp(a) as well. We still have a way to go, but the progress we make every day is inspiring.

With new medications, my levels are finally within target range today, but I do live with damage to my heart muscle and a lingering fear of what may come. I am also aware of another looming milestone: this year I turn 50, which will be one year older than my dad was when he had a heart transplant. I am more excited to turn 50 than most people, I’d guess.

And despite my fear, I have hope for the future. I am hopeful that new treatment options that are less cumbersome and time-consuming are more effective, and that one day we may be able to reverse cardiovascular damage. I am hopeful that Family Heart will continue to help drive change, and eventually be able to say that everyone living with FH and HoFH are being identified and treated. I believe that we can change the future for the newest generations of our family, and that FH will become something we all live with but that we manage, instead of waiting for the next milestone to arrive.
