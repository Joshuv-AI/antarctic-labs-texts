A multicenter COVID-19 database from four waves in the south metropolitan area of Barcelona, Catalonia
Authors: N. Pallarès, J. Carmezim, A. Rodríguez-Molinero, A. Coloma, E. Izquierdo, V. Díaz-Brito, S. Videla, J. Carratalà, G. Gómez-Melis, C. Tebé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic has been characterized by a series of epidemiological waves, each defined by distinct viral variants and shifting transmission dynamics. Examining these waves is essential for understanding temporal changes in case incidence, hospitalization rates, mortality patterns, and healthcare system capacity. In this work, we present a database from a multicenter COVID-19 study including data of 5813 patients from four waves of the pandemic in the south metropolitan area of Barcelona. The database contains information collected at hospital admission and during patient follow-up while in hospital. Data were prospectively recorded from the patients’ medical records. The database includes information on demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and clinical signs and symptoms at admission, as well as detailed data on clinical management and treatments administered during hospitalisation across four pandemic waves. The database has been made publicly available to support reproducible research and to enable its reuse in secondary analyses and predictive model validation, and for use as a teaching resource.

Background & Summary

The first confirmed case of COVID-19 was in Wuhan, China, in December 2019. Due to its contagious nature, the lack of knowledge about how to treat the disease, and dense global travel and mobility networks, it spread rapidly around the world1. By the end of February 2020 the virus had spread worldwide and on 11 March 2020 the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the outbreak a pandemic2. In Europe, the first cases were detected in France and the first outbreak occurred in Italy at the end of February 20203. The first case in Spain was detected on 31st January 2020 in La Gomera, Canary Islands4 and subsequent cases were detected in Mallorca and Tenerife. The first case in Catalonia was reported on 25th February, with the first deaths occurring in early March.

In this context, the DIVINE cohort5 contributes to the literature on the COVID-19 pandemic by providing a curated collection of data on hospitalized patients during four waves of the pandemic. Data comes from a prospective cohort of consecutive adult patients admitted to five centres in the south metropolitan area of Barcelona. The south metropolitan area of Barcelona6 is a health administrative region in Catalonia, Spain, with a population of 1.2 million. It covers a mix of urban and rural areas with a mainly low- and middle-income population. All subjects included in the DIVINE cohort were consecutively recruited upon hospital admission, with a confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, as evidenced by a positive PCR or antigen test result. All patients presenting to the participating centers during the study period were enrolled, reflecting routine clinical practice during the pandemic. A total of 6653 subjects were included in the DIVINE cohort. Subjects who were admitted to hospital within less than 24 h (N = 499), subjects who died within the first 24 h (N = 17), subjects who had incomplete data in a pool of essential variables (age, sex, Charlson score, ceiling of care, and circumstances at discharge) (N = 274), underage subjects (N = 2), or subjects admitted firstly in one hospital but transferred to another and treated in the latter (N = 48) were excluded from the cohort. Patients were hospitalized during the following periods: the first wave, from 1st March to 15th April 2020; the second wave, from 1st October to 31st November 2020; the third wave, from 1st January to 28th February 2021; and the fourth wave, from 1st July to 31st August 2021. The database includes information on baseline characteristics (e.g. demographics, comorbidities, and concomitant medications, and symptoms at hospital admission) and follow-up during hospitalisation (e.g. intensive care unit (ICU) admission, ventilation, and treatments received).

A general limitation to consider is the external validity of the DIVINE cohort, as the data were collected from a single, specific region. Regional factors such as demographic composition, socioeconomic conditions, and local policies could have influenced the data collected, potentially limiting the extent to which the findings can be generalized to other contexts.

Methods

The following lines summarise the steps taken to create the database. In March 2020, the Infectious Diseases Department at Bellvitge University Hospital and the Biostatistics Unit statisticians at the Bellvitge Biomedical Research Institute designed an electronic case report form (eCRF) to collect information on all consecutive patients hospitalized with SARS-CoV-2 infection. REDCap, a secure web-based software platform designed to build and manage online databases7,8, was used to programme the eCRF. The initial eCRF was designed to collect study data from the first wave (1 March to 15 April 2020). However, as the pandemic evolved, data collection was extended to include all four waves. Data were collected during hospital admissions and entered into REDCap from patients’ medical records. During the first and second waves of the pandemic, medical doctors and research assistants completed the eCRF using information extracted directly from patients’ medical records. After the second wave, given the substantial volume of data already recorded and the likelihood of subsequent waves of the virus, four data entry staff, partially funded by the Secretariat for Universities and Research of the Department of Enterprise and Knowledge of the Government of Catalonia (2020PANDE00148), were assigned to the project. To ensure consistency in variable recording, these staff members were trained by the medical doctors who had previously entered data into the database. As the data from the first and second waves had been entered by different clinicians across multiple hospitals, the initial task of the data entry team was to review these records. When the third and subsequent fourth waves of the pandemic occurred in Catalonia, they also entered data from these waves into the eCRF. The data entry staff were granted access to patients’ medical records across all participating hospitals. Any inconsistencies identified were reported to the responsible medical doctors, who were asked to review the information and make corrections in the eCRF when necessary.

As the understanding of the virus grew, the eCRF was modified, with new variables added and others removed. Importantly, variable definitions remained consistent across all waves. Each variable was standardized across participating hospitals at the time of its inclusion in the eCRF, and these definitions were applied uniformly throughout the entire data collection period.

Due to the increased burden of care during the pandemic, not all five centers were able to enrol participants in every wave. Hospitals A and B contributed data across all waves; Hospitals C and D contributed data during Waves 1 and 2; and Hospital E contributed data only during Wave 1. A series of data protection measures were adopted to ensure the confidentiality of subjects within the clinical database. To mitigate the risk of re-identification, directly identifiable details were modified as follows: hospital names were replaced with pseudonyms (Hospital A to Hospital E), and the date of birth was substituted with the subject’s age at admission. Furthermore, the absolute date of hospital admission was removed from the database. Instead, key clinical event dates—including those for ICU admission, intubation, death, and hospital discharge—were instead calculated and recorded on a relative timeline, expressed as the number of days since the admission event. As a result, although exact calendar dates are not available, the structure necessary for most time-dependent analyses is preserved. We acknowledge, however, that certain analyses relying on absolute calendar time (e.g., week-based or seasonal patterns) are constrained by this approach. This limitation is explicitly noted in the manuscript as a point of caution.

Quality checks were performed monthly using the quality checks available in REDCap itself and R code. Any missing or inconsistent values were manually revised by data entries, who checked the information against the medical records. If changes were required, the data were updated in REDCap.

The study, with application number HUB-INF-COHORT·HUB·COVID, was approved on 15th April 2020 by the Bellvitge Hospital Research Ethics Committee (IRB00005523) with medicines (CREm) in accordance with Spanish legislation and was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 with application number HUB-INF-COHORT·HUB·COVID. Due to the exceptional circumstances of the pandemic, the Committee waived the requirement for individual informed consent. Bellvitge’s CREm decision was the basis for the approval of the remaining hospital centers. The principal investigator at each hospital was subsequently contacted to obtain approval for the publication of the study data in an open format. As previously stated, measures to mitigate the risk of re-identification were agreed upon with each hospital as a condition for data sharing.

Data Records

The DIVINE database is freely available in the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN) and also on GitHub and on Zenodo. CRAN is a platform for hosting R’s source code, executable files, documentation and user-created packages. GitHub is a cloud-based platform where you can store, share and collaborate with others on code. Zenodo is a free, open-access online repository where researchers can upload their research outputs.

Via CRAN you can download the DIVINE R package in R. It contains 14 datasets in ‘.Rda’ format and functions that can be used to summarize and visualize data. Further information on the main characteristics of the datasets and on the functions of the package can be found in the package vignette9.

Via GitHUB, the 14 datasets can be downloaded in CSV format (https://github.com/bruigtp/DIVINE-datasets). Full descriptions of the available datasets and their variables can be found in a PDF document (Datasets-Full-Description.pdf).

Via Zenodo, the 14 datasets are permanently archived and openly accessible10 in CSV format. This repository provides a stable and citable version of the data and software, ensuring long-term reproducibility and accessibility for future research.

All datasets have the following common variables: record_id (participant identification); covid_wave (wave of the pandemic that the patient belongs to); and center (centre of admission). Datasets contain baseline data (analytics, comorbidities, concomitant_medication, demographic, scores, symptoms, vital_signs, and vaccine) and data on patient follow-up (complications, icu, inhosp_antivirals, inhosp_other_treatments, inhosp_antibiotics, and end_followup) for the 5813 patients. Technical Validation

Medical doctors and research assistants complete the eCRF study using information form patients’ medical records. Given the multicentre nature of the study and the involvement of clinicians with heterogeneous backgrounds, a structured data management plan was implemented to ensure consistency and data quality across sites.

Automated validation rules were programmed in REDCap to minimize data entry errors. These included range checks (e.g., biologically plausible values for laboratory parameters), format constraints (e.g., date and categorical fields), and mandatory field requirements for key variables. In addition, logical consistency checks were applied to detect discrepancies between related variables (e.g., admission and discharge dates, ICU admission status and procedures performed in ICU). These validation rules were uniformly implemented across all participating centres.

To further ensure data integrity, periodic data quality assessments were conducted by the central biostatistics team responsible for curating the study data. These included consistency checks, as well as identification of outliers and implausible combinations of clinical variables. Queries were generated and communicated to the research team in each hospital for resolution. Missing or inconsistent data were addressed through manual review. Data entries and research assistants revised the original medical records to verify and correct values in the eCRF. All modifications were tracked within the REDCap system to maintain an audit trail.

As the study evolved across different pandemic waves, updates to the eCRF were required to incorporate new variables and clinical knowledge. Version control procedures were implemented to ensure harmonization across sites. When variables were added, clear definitions were distributed among all the data entry and medical doctors involved in the data inclusion.
