Higher Burden of Cerebral Small Vessel Disease Is Associated With Risk of Incident Stroke in Community‐Dwelling Individuals
Authors: Adlin Pinheiro, Hugo Aparicio, Vasileios‐Arsenios Lioutas, Alexa Beiser, Oluchi Ekenze, Charles DeCarli, Sudha Seshadri, Serkalem Demissie, Jose R. Romero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMild manifestations of individual cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) markers are common and may not denote increased risk, but high CSVD burden identifies individuals at increased risk of stroke and dementia. Scores incorporating multiple individual CSVD markers may better identify a person’s risk. We related a multimarker CSVD score to risk of incident stroke and compared it with the Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP) in community‐dwelling individuals.MethodsFramingham Heart Study participants aged ≥55 years, free of stroke and dementia, and with brain magnetic resonance imaging ratings of CSVD markers were included. A multimarker CSVD score reflecting increasing CSVD burden was used, assigning 1 point each for presence of cerebral microbleeds, severe perivascular spaces, extensive white matter hyperintensities, covert brain infarcts, and cortical superficial siderosis. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to relate CSVD score to incident stroke.ResultsAmong 1154 participants (46% men, mean age 70.9±8.7 years), 92 (8%) developed stroke over a median follow‐up of 8.6 years (Q1–Q3: 5.1–12.5). In models adjusting for age, sex, time interval between clinic exam and magnetic resonance imaging, Framingham cohort, and FSRP, those with ≥3 markers had increased risk of stroke (hazard ratio [HR], 2.62 [95% CI, 1.17–5.88]). In comparison, a 5% increase in FSRP was also associated with increased risk (adjusted HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.04–1.29]). The FSRP and CSVD score had similar model discrimination metrics.ConclusionsHigher CSVD burden is associated with increased risk of stroke, beyond the effect explained by risk factors in the FSRP. These findings support consideration of CSVD burden to identify risk of stroke in community‐dwelling individuals for early implementation of preventive strategies.

Can multimarker scores reflecting the burden of cerebral small vessel disease improve stroke risk prediction?

Can multimarker scores reflecting the burden of cerebral small vessel disease improve stroke risk prediction?

Stroke incidence remains high in the United States, with 795,000 annual cases, 1 and is much higher in developing countries around the world. 2 Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is recognized as one of the major contributors to stroke and its adverse consequences. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has advanced the study of CSVD in subclinical stages, allowing the identification of disease years to decades before stroke events occur, thus offering an opportunity to evaluate individuals at increased risk. Among CSVD markers readily identifiable on MRI are white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), covert brain infarcts (CBIs), cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), visible (enlarged) perivascular spaces (PVSs), and cortical superficial siderosis.

Although these markers frequently coexist, their presence is heterogenous, with prominent interindividual differences. Recently, research efforts have led to the study of total CSVD scores which aim to incorporate the presence of multiple markers in an individual to better assess their global contribution to cerebrovascular disease and events. However, most studies have been conducted in clinical samples 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 or small samples, 11 , 12 , 13 with a paucity of data in large, long‐term studies of community‐dwelling individuals. Such data are needed to further improve public health interventions aiming to ameliorate the consequences of CSVD, including clinical stroke events.

Thus, we studied the relation of a multimarker CSVD score incorporating all CSVD measures evaluated in the population‐based Framingham Heart Study (FHS) in relation to incident stroke. We also compared its model performance against clinical risk factors alone summarized by the Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP). Our hypothesis was that higher multimarker CSVD scores would be related to increased stroke risk and capture an individual’s risk as well as or better than clinical risk factors alone, which may help identify individuals for targeted preventive therapies.

METHODS

Data

Requests to access data from the Framingham Heart Study can be made at

https://www.framinghamheartstudy.org/fhs‐for‐researchers.

Sample

The FHS is the longest running prospective community‐based cohort study in the United States, now in its 76th year. Participants from multiple generations were enrolled beginning with the Original cohort (N=5209) since 1948, followed by the Offspring cohort (N=5124) since 1971. Participants have been followed prospectively every 2 to 4 years for 32 examinations in the Original cohort and every 4 to 7 years for 10 examinations in the Offspring cohort.

Original and Offspring participants were eligible for the present study if they had at least 1 available brain MRI with ratings of CSVD markers, had follow‐up data for incident stroke, and were over the age of 55 years at the time of the MRI. If multiple MRIs were available, the earliest MRI was selected. Participants with prevalent stroke, dementia, missing exposure or outcome data, or neurological conditions affecting MRI measurements (e.g., tumor, multiple sclerosis, and head trauma) were excluded. A total of 1154 participants were included in the final sample (Figure 1).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consent

The institutional review board of Boston University Medical Center approved the study protocol and informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Exposures

Brain MRI

Brain MRI acquisition measures and image processing methods have been described in detail. 9 , 10 Brain MRIs were acquired using 1T Siemens Magnetom (Siemens Medical, Erlangen, Germany) or 1.5T Siemens Symphony, Siemens Avanto, or GE Signa (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI) scanners. All ratings and analyses were performed blinded to the subject’s demographic and clinical characteristics and outcome ascertainment. All CSVD markers were defined and assessed following the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging (STRIVE) criteria and published guidelines for individual markers. 14 , 15

Cerebral Small Vessel Disease Markers

CBIs were determined based on the size (≥3 mm), location, hyperintensity on T2‐weighted images, and imaging characteristics of the lesion (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid signal intensity on subtraction images) using previously described methods. 16

CMBs were defined and rated using standard criteria 14 as round or ovoid hypointense lesions on blood sensitive sequences with a diameter up to 10 mm and surrounded by brain parenchyma over at least half the circumference of the lesion. CMB mimics were excluded. 15

WMHs were measured with previously published quantitative methods using T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery MRI sequences to estimate the volume of WMH. 11 Presence of extensive WMH was defined as having a log‐transformed WMH volume as a percentage of total brain volume that is >1 SD above an age group‐specific mean.

MRI‐visible PVSs were defined as round, ovoid, or linear following the course of penetrating cerebral vessels, with signal intensity resembling cerebrospinal fluid on all sequences, and having a diameter <3 mm. PVSs were separately assessed in the centrum semiovale and basal ganglia and categorized based on counts: grade I (1–10), grade II (11–20), grade III (21–40), and grade IV (>40). 17 Due to low prevalence of grade IV cases in FHS participants, we further classified PVSs as low burden (grades I and II) or high burden (grades III and IV) and defined severe PVSs as grade III–IV in either the basal ganglia or centrum semiovale.

Cortical superficial siderosis was classified as either focal (≤3 sulci) or disseminated (≥4 sulci) per previously described methods. 18 , 19 Intra‐ and interrater reliability for all CSVD markers above have been previously reported and considered to range from good to excellent. 16 , 20 , 21

Multimarker CSVD Score

We calculated the multimarker CSVD score using a practical ordinal scale designed for clinical application. The score ranges from 0 to 5, with 1 point assigned for the presence of each of the following markers: CBI, CMB, extensive WMH, severe PVS, and cortical superficial siderosis.

Outcome

Ongoing surveillance for incident stroke cases includes systematic daily screening of participants admitted to the local hospital in Framingham or other area hospitals, regular FHS examinations, annual heath status updates, ongoing review of Medicare claims data, or report by participants, their family members, or primary care physicians via a 24/7 stroke pager (1‐800‐FHSTROK). All medical records for any emergency room visit, hospitalization, or death are reviewed to supplement rigorous screening.

Stroke case adjudication is performed by a panel of 2 vascular neurologists evaluating all data, including medical records, imaging studies (reports and images), and diagnostic testing performed to evaluate stroke cause. Additional data include acute stroke treatment (thrombolytic, mechanical thrombectomy), clinical angiography (computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, conventional angiography), evaluation for cardioembolism (electrocardiogram, transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac event monitor, implantable loop recorder), carotid ultrasound, surgical procedures, and autopsy. Head computed tomography/MRI was available in >90% of cases.

The follow‐up period extended from the date of the MRI (1999–2015) to December 31, 2021. Censoring for participants who did not experience an incident stroke occurred at the last date known to be stroke free, end of study period, or death from other causes.

Vascular Risk Factors

Vascular risk factors were evaluated at the closest examination cycle to MRI, occurring within 5 years before or up to 1 year after the scan. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure >140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure >90 mm Hg, or use of antihypertensive medication. Smoking status was self‐reported and defined as any smoking within 1 year of the examination. Diabetes was defined as a random blood glucose >200 mg/dL for the Original cohort, fasting blood glucose >126 mg/dL for the Offspring cohort, or use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medications. Prevalent cardiovascular disease included coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, and peripheral arterial disease. Use of antihypertensive, lipid‐lowering, or diabetes medications were also determined by self‐report.

Framingham Stroke Risk Profile

The revised FSRP is a widely used score to predict cardiovascular risk and has been validated in various samples. 22 The sex‐specific score includes the following vascular risk factors to describe a 10‐year probability of incident stroke: age, systolic blood pressure, use of antihypertensive medications, prevalent cardiovascular disease, current smoking status, current or previous atrial fibrillation, and diabetes.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive characteristics for baseline demographic, clinical, and imaging variables included frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and mean±SD for continuous variables. Due to few participants having <3 CSVD markers, the multimarker CSVD score was recategorized into the following: 0 (no CSVD biomarker, reference group), 1 (1 biomarker), 2 (2 biomarkers), and 3+ (≥3 biomarkers) for multivariable analyses. We also evaluated a second score collapsing ≥2 markers into 1 category to determine whether results depend on choice of cut point.

Incidence rates (per 1000 person‐years) were calculated for incident stroke by dividing the total number of new stroke events by the total follow‐up time and multiplying by 1000. Three multivariable Cox regression analyses were then used to relate the multimarker CSVD score to incident stroke: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, FHS cohort, time interval between MRI and clinic exam; Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking, systolic blood pressure, prevalent cardiovascular disease, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and antihypertensive medication use; and Model 3 adjusted for the covariates in Model 1 and FSRP. Effect modification by age and sex was also evaluated in stratified analyses. Additionally, we related FSRP to incident stroke adjusting for the covariates in Model 1. Hazard ratios (HRs) for the FSRP were expressed as a 5% increase in the 10‐year probability of stroke.

The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Kolmogorov‐type supremum tests, and nonlinearity in the predictors was assessed using plots of the Martingale residuals.

We compared models with the multimarker CSVD score and the FSRP using the Harrell C statistic, a measure analogous to the area under the curve of a receiver operating characteristic curve, allowing assessment of discrimination of participants with longer versus shorter event‐free survival. 23

Exploratory models assessed effect modification by MRI scanner strength, whereas additional models evaluated the individual contributions of MRI markers to incident stroke. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and a P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Supremum tests were not significant, and proportional hazards were assumed. Additionally, plots of martingale residuals did not suggest nonlinearity in the continuous covariates. Participants with CSVD score ≥3 had significantly higher risk of stroke after adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors (HR, 2.81 [95% CI, 1.24–6.36]) and FSRP (HR, 2.62 [95% CI, 1.17–5.88]) compared with participants without any CSVD markers (Table 3). Similarly, using the 3‐category CSVD score, participants with ≥2 markers had a significantly higher risk of stroke across all models.

Effect Modification

In stratified analyses evaluating the role of age, we observed higher stroke risk in individuals aged ≥65 years across all multimarker CSVD scores (Table 4). Although there were no participants younger than 65 years with 2 or more CSVD markers who developed stroke, those with a multimarker score of 1 had significantly higher risk of stroke compared with those without any CSVD markers (HR, 3.21 [95% CI, 1.24–8.31]). Some heterogeneity in stroke risk was seen across multimarker CSVD scores, but overall higher risk was observed in women.

Model Comparisons

Model performance comparing CSVD score, CSVD score adjusted for individual components of the FSRP, and CSVD score adjusted for the FSRP were similar (C=0.69, 0.71, and 0.70, respectively). Additionally, CSVD score and FSRP had similar C statistics using the covariates in Model 1 (C=0.69 and 0.68, respectively).

Exploratory Models

After stratifying by scanner strength, HRs were higher for CSVD scores of 2 or 3 or greater in 1.5T scanners compared with 1.0; meanwhile, HRs were higher for CSVD score of 1 in 1.0T scanners (Table S5). Additionally, in the 3 covariate adjusted models, CMB was the only MRI marker of CSVD significantly associated with increased incident stroke risk (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

In this large cohort study of asymptomatic community‐dwelling individuals, we observed participants with higher multimarker CSVD scores were at increased risk of stroke, with greater risk as the number of single CSVD measures increased. The CSVD score signaled increased risk even after adjustment for vascular risk factors as individual covariates or summarized in the FSRP, indicating that CSVD score denotes risk independent of the FSRP.

Both CSVD score and FSRP, which were specifically developed for stroke risk prediction, had similar model performance metrics when comparing C statistics. This suggests that the CSVD score reflects aspects of stroke risk that are similar or complementary to the FSRP and are equally informative in identifying groups of high‐risk individuals. Despite acceptable model discrimination metrics, the multimarker CSVD score may still be useful for risk stratification but requires further evaluation to determine its clinical relevance and potential to enhance stroke risk prediction.

Our stratified analyses showed higher risk of stroke in women with high CSVD scores compared with men. In addition, risk of stroke in participants with 1 CSVD marker was higher in those aged <65 years, although no stroke events were observed in those aged <65 years with scores >2. Comparison of vascular risk factors between men and women showed that women had higher total cholesterol (199 versus 174 mg/dL), higher low‐density lipoprotein (110 versus 100 mg/dL), and lower use of lipid‐lowering treatments (39% versus 49%); similar findings were noted in the younger age group, where individuals aged <65 years had higher mean levels of total cholesterol (196 versus 184 mg/dL), low‐density lipoprotein (114 versus 102 mg/dL), and lower use of lipid‐lowering therapy (30% versus 50%). Although it remains a speculation, it is possible that treatment effects and level of control of some vascular risk factors over time may explain this difference in stroke risk. However, this requires further prospective studies to assess differences in CSVD burden and outcomes in men and women, and young and older individuals.

In exploratory analyses assessing the influence of scanner strength on the results, we found that the direction of effects remained unchanged. However, the strength of associations varied in some subgroups, suggesting that scanner strength should be considered when interpreting results of multimarker CSVD scores that incorporate variable MRI scanner strength. In addition, CMB was the only individual marker of CSVD associated with increased risk of incident stroke, with similar findings observed by Akoudad et al. 24 However, in a previous study, covert brain infarcts and extensive WMHs were found to be significantly associated with incident stroke in the FHS. 25 Differences in findings may be explained by variations in sample composition, because data availability for CSVD markers differed between studies.

Prior studies have shown that total CSVD scores are associated with higher risk of recurrent stroke, 26 incident stroke, dementia, and mortality. 27 , 28 Our results corroborate that previous findings reported in clinical samples also apply to asymptomatic individuals dwelling in the community, bringing attention to the subclinical stage and helping to set the stage for consideration of screening for CSVD for the clinical prevention of stroke. Additionally, our score differed from previous ones by including PVS in either the centrum semiovale or basal ganglia, CBI, cortical superficial siderosis, and CMB in any region. These markers represent both hypertensive arteriopathy and cerebral amyloid angiopathy, allowing for a broader characterization of small vessel disease pathology.

With this broader definition, our multimarker CSVD score represents a pragmatic approach to enable early risk stratification and intervention in clinical practice by summarizing routinely available MRI markers before symptom onset. Although there is no specific treatment for subclinical CSVD, its strong association with vascular risk factors, particularly hypertension, highlights the need for an objective measure of cardiovascular risk, because CSVD burden may also capture the cumulative impact of long‐term vascular risk exposure that is not easily assessed through intermittent clinical evaluations.

Clinical trials now incorporate subclinical CSVD in their outcomes, and it is likely that additional data will become available to guide clinical decisions in the near future. For instance, the LACI‐2 (Lacunar Intervention Trial‐2) 29 randomized clinical trial assessed treatments that target endothelial dysfunction in patients with CSVD‐related stroke, showing promising results for risk reduction for recurrent stroke and cognitive impairment.

It is important to note that we labeled our scores as multimarker CSVD scores, because the term "total" suggests the inclusion of all possible CSVD markers. Although this comprehensive approach is desirable, evolving knowledge and detection methods for CSVD may lead to new measures that must be integrated with traditional ones. Therefore, "total" scores today may differ significantly from "total" scores in the near future.

Our study has several strengths including the inclusion of a large sample, blinded assessments of exposure and outcomes, comprehensive longitudinal follow‐up for incident stroke with high retention, and accurate and reliable ascertainment of exposures, confounders, and outcomes. Limitations include a small number of participants with the highest CSVD scores and the predominantly White racial composition of FHS participants, thus restricting generalization to other racial groups. In addition, selection of participants based on availability of CSVD markers may have resulted in the inclusion of generally healthier participants, which may underestimate risk in the included sample.

CONCLUSIONS

Multimarker CSVD scores capture the heterogeneity of CSVD manifestations and signal increased risk of incident stroke when detected in subclinical stages, even in community‐dwelling individuals. These scores are independent of the FSRP, capturing different aspects of stroke risk not accounted for by the FSRP, and had similar model discrimination performance to the FSRP. Further studies are needed to assess implementation of preventive measures in patients with high burden of subclinical CSVD.
