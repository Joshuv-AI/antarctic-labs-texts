An Ex Vivo Pharmacodynamic Study of KN057, a Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor Neutralizing Antibody, in Plasma Samples From Patients With Haemophilia or VWD3
Authors: Mankai Ju, Feng Xue, Wei Sun, Fang Li, Ting Xu, Renchi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionTissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), a key regulator of tissue factor‐initiated coagulation through FXa‐dependent inhibition of the tissue factor‐FVIIa complex, has emerged as a promising target for restoring thrombin generation.AimThis ex vivo pharmacodynamic study aimed to evaluate the KN057, a novel humanized TFPI‐neutralizing antibody, in plasma samples from participants with haemophilia or type 3 von Willebrand disease (VWD3).MethodsIn this ex vivo spiking study, plasmas obtained from haemophilia or VWD3 participants were supplemented with KN057 at the concentration of 0–140.00 nM. The thrombin generation assay (TGA) and the dilute prothrombin time (dPT) assay were performed to assess coagulation responses.ResultsBetween 26, May 2021, and 3, September 2021, a total of 29 participants were enrolled in this study, including 10 haemophilia A (HA) without inhibitors, 6 haemophilia B (HB) without inhibitors, 6 HA with inhibitors, 3 HB with inhibitors, and 4 VWD3. In participant plasmas supplemented with KN057, peak thrombin and endogenous thrombin potential (ETP) increased notably between 1.12–5.60 nM, and reached a maximal level at 28.00 nM. A dose‐dependent decrease in dPT was observed in all participants with haemophilia or VWD3.ConclusionsKN057 exhibited a consistent ex vivo pharmacodynamic profile, including both the response trend and effective concentration range, across plasma samples from patients with HA or HB, irrespective of inhibitor status. Furthermore, its pharmacodynamic activity in plasma from patients with VWD3 was comparable to that observed in haemophilia plasma.Keywords: dilute prothrombin time, haemophilia, KN057, thrombin generation assay, tissue factor pathway inhibitor

Introduction

Haemophilia A and B (HA and HB) are rare congenital, recessive X‐linked disorders caused by lack or deficiency of clotting FVIII or FIX, respectively [1, 2]. The hallmark clinical characteristic, especially in untreated severe forms, is bleeding (spontaneous or after trauma) into major joints such as ankles, knees, and elbows, which could result in the development of arthropathy [2]. For patients with severe HA or HB, prophylaxis with clotting factor concentrates (CFC) is recommended by the World Federation of Haemophilia (WFH) guidelines to avert life‐threatening hemorrhagic events [3]. Nevertheless, the standard CFC replacement therapy is limited by the requirement for intravenous administration, a short half‐life, and the risk of inhibitor formation [3], thereby necessitating the advancement of novel therapeutic approaches to address these unmet clinical needs.

There are unmet clinical needs for prophylaxis of von Willebrand Disease (VWD) similar to those for haemophilia. VWD is a bleeding disorder caused by inherited defects in the concentration, structure, or function of von Willebrand factor (VWF), which acts as a stabilizer of FVIII in the circulation. This is obtained by the formation of a non‐covalently bound VWF‐FVIII complex that protects FVIII from degradation and localises FVIII to sites of platelet plug and subsequent clot formation [4]. Of all the VWD types, bleeding events in patients with type 3 von Willebrand disease (VWD3), which is virtually complete deficiency of VWF, tends to be the most severe and frequent [5].

There is increasing interest in the development of novel therapies with alternative mechanisms of action and routes of administration that may improve patient adherence. Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), a key physiological regulator of coagulation initiation, has emerged as a potential target for alternative therapeutic strategies [6]. By binding to the tissue factor‐FVIIa‐FXa complex, TFPI inhibits FVIIa activity and suppresses thrombin generation, thereby reducing blood coagulation [6, 7, 8, 9]. Currently, two anti‐TFPI agents‐marstacimab and concizumab‐have been approved for haemophilia by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [10], representing an important advance in non‐factor replacement therapy. However, concerns remain regarding thromboembolic risk in patients with underlying prothrombotic conditions, particularly when these agents are administered alongside other haemostatic therapies [11]. Therefore, the development of additional anti‐TFPI antibodies remain warranted to broaden therapeutic options and better accommodate individual patient needs.

KN057 is a novel recombinant, humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody that selectively targets TFPI. By binding to TFPI, KN057 blocks its interaction with activated coagulation factor X (FXa), thereby preventing the FXa‐dependent binding of TFPI to the factor VIIa‐tissue factor (FVIIa‐TF) complex, thus preserving the procoagulant activities of these factors. This mechanism enhances thrombin generation, which is critical for effective haemostasis and the prevention of bleeding events associated with impaired coagulation. Importantly, the activity of KN057 is not influenced by the inhibitor status in patients with haemophilia. KN057 exhibits nanomolar affinity for human TFPI (Kd = 1.37 nM), comparable to marstacimab (2.63 nM) [12] and weaker than concizumab (0.03 nM) [13], suggesting a more reversible TFPI binding profile that may support less frequent dosing strategies and thereby potentially improve patient adherence. For example, concizumab is administered once daily, while KN057 and marstacimab are administered once weekly. Here, the present study aimed to establish the effective plasma concentration range of KN057 in plasma from participants with HA or HB (with or without inhibitors) or VWD3.

Participants and Methods

Study Populations

This ex vivo pharmacodynamic study assessed the procoagulant activity of KN057 in plasma from participants with haemophilia or VWD3. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Approved No. XY2020034‐EC‐3), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ICH Good Clinical Practice. Written informed consent was obtained for all participants.

Participants were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older and had severe HA or HB (FVIII or IX activity < 1 IU/dL, or historical documentation of < 1 IU/dL if current level was ≥ 1 IU/dL). Those without inhibitors required a negative result at screening or within the past month, whereas those with inhibitors required a positive result (≥ 0.60 BU/mL) within the past 6 months. Participants with VWD3 were eligible if plasma VWF antigen and factor VIII activity levels were obtained. Exclusion criteria included receipt factor replacement within 96 h prior to study entry; a history, clinical signs, or family history of thrombosis; being considered at high thrombotic risk, or participation in another clinical trial involving investigational drugs within the past 3 months.

Collection and Preparation of Blood Samples

A total of 20.00 mL fasting whole blood was collected from each participant using 4 vacuum blood collection tubes (5.00 mL of 3.20% sodium citrate anticoagulant). Following collection, samples were subjected to centrifugation for plasma separation. If immediate processing was precluded by specific circumstances, samples were maintained at room temperature for no longer than 1 h to minimize pre‐analytical variability and preserve sample integrity.

Plasma was separated by centrifuging the whole blood at 1500 g for 15 min at room temperature (18°C–25°C) to obtain platelet‐poor plasma (PPP). Using sterile pipettes or micro pipettors, the upper plasma layer was carefully aspirated‐avoiding contamination with blood cells or residual platelets‐and transferred into cryovials. Aliquoted plasma samples were subsequently stored at −60°C to −80°C until further analysis.

Thrombin Generation Assay (TGA)

KN057 was tested at final plasma concentrations of 0 nM, 1.12 nM, 5.60 nM, 28.00 nM, and 140.00 nM, with the KN057 placebo as a negative control. Plasmas mixed with KN057 were incubated at room temperature for 30 min prior to the analysis. 80 µL of plasma was added to reagents consisting of thrombin calibrator (20 µL, Stago) in one set of wells and a trigger (PPP‐Reagent LOW: 20 µL, Stago) in another set of wells to activate coagulation. Fluorescent intensity was detected using a Fluoroskan Ascent fluorometer type 374 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) and thrombin generation curves were calculated with Thrombinoscope software. TGA parameters monitored included lag time, peak thrombin, and endogenous thrombin potential (ETP).

Dilute Prothrombin Time (dPT) Assay

The dPT assay was performed on a SF‐8100 automated blood coagulation analyser (Succeeder, Beijing, China) according to validated magnetic bead method (SOP: SDGC‐GC‐16‐01). Activator was prepared by diluting PT reagent 1:2000 in dilution buffer (STA‐Neoplastine CI plus reagent 2). Plasma samples were thawed at 37°C and supplemented with KN057 (final concentration: 0 nM, 0.009 nM, 0.045 nM, 0.224 nM, 1.12 nM, 5.60 nM, 28.00 nM, 140.00 nM), with KN057 placebo as a negative control. Plasmas mixed with KN057 were incubated at room temperature for 30 min prior to the analysis. 50 µL of plasma was incubated at 37°C for 3 min in a STA cuvette followed by activation with 100 µL of activator prepared above. Plasma clotting times were reported in seconds.

To reduce inter‐batch variability, two normalization ratios were applied. Ratio 1 (Timesample/Timestandard) was calculated as the clotting time of the plasma sample divided by that of a standard plasma within the same assay batch. Ratio 2 (Timesample/TimeKN057 buffer) was defined as the ratio of the clotting time of the same participant's plasma sample after drug treatment to that of the sample treated with KN057 buffer, both measured within the same batch.

Statistical Analysis

No formal statistical hypothesis was pre‐specified for this study. A total of 31 fasting blood samples from eligible participants with haemophilia was collected, consisting of 10 with HA without inhibitors, 6 HB without inhibitors, 6 HA with inhibitors, 3 HB with inhibitors, and 6 VWD3. Given the distinct clinical characteristics of VWD, the sample size for this subgroup may be adjusted in accordance with actual patient availability.

The continuous variables were presented as the numbers, mean, and standard deviation. For dPT assay and TGA, mean values‐concentration curves were generated to characterize the pharmacodynamic profile. Intergroup comparisons were conducted to evaluate differences in ex vivo pharmacodynamic responses across haemophilia subtypes. EC50 was calculated using GraphPad Prism 6.01 software, and the other statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., USA).

Results

Thrombin Generation

Thrombin generation increased in a concentration‐dependent manner in supplemented with KN057 plasma from participants with haemophilia, based on peak thrombin and ETP parameters. The effect increased notably between 1.12 and 5.60 nM, and reached a maximum at 28.00 nM. The mean peak values of peak thrombin and ETP in plasma from participants with haemophilia ranged from 85.00–120.00 nM and 1400.00–1650.00 nM*min, respectively, while lag time remained unchanged (Figure 2). Similar dose‐response pattern and effective concentration range was observed across haemophilia subtypes, including HA without inhibitors, HB without inhibitors, HA with inhibitors, and HB with inhibitors (Figure 2, Supplementary Materials Figure S1–S3).

For participants with VWD3, the trend of supplemented ex vivo with KN057 was similar to that of haemophilia. The activity of coagulation factor measured on Day 1 (D1; the day of blood sample collection) in VWD3 participants was higher than that in haemophilia participants, resulting in higher peak thrombin and ETP at KN057 concentration of 0 nM and 1.12 nM compared with haemophilia samples. As the KN057 concentration increased to ≥ 5.60 nM, the difference in peak thrombin progressively narrowed, and ETP levels became comparable between the VWD3 and haemophilia (Figure 2).

KN057 Reduces the dPT

A dose‐dependent decrease in dPT clotting time was observed in all participants with haemophilia. Similar dose‐response pattern and effective concentration range was observed across haemophilia subtypes (including HA without inhibitors, HB without inhibitors, HA with inhibitors, and HB with inhibitors) as well as VWD3 (Figure 3, Supplementary Materials Figure S4–S6). Baseline dPT values were comparable among participants with HA without inhibitors, HB without inhibitors, and HA with inhibitors, with mean values of 90.54 s, 98.60 s, and 85.77 s, respectively. After supplemented with KN057, the dPT values were decreased to 74.15 s, 79.00 s, and 68.53 s, respectively. Participants with HB with inhibitors exhibited the longest baseline dPT (111.40 s), which was reduced by 27.13 s after supplemented with KN057, reaching 84.27 s. In addition, the shortest baseline dPT was observed in VWD3 (80.10 s), which decreased by 13.07 s after supplemented with KN057, with a minimum value of 67.03 s (Figure 3). The mean EC50 values across all samples was 0.57 nM.

At baseline, mean dPT ratio 1 values in plasma from participants with different haemophilia subtypes were greater than 1, indicating pathological prolongation of dPT compared with standard plasma. After supplemented with KN057, dPT ratio 1 gradually decreased with increasing KN057 concentrations, showing a trend consistent with that of absolute dPT values (Figure 3). The dPT ratio 2 was 1 across all samples at baseline. DPT ratio 2 progressively decreased after supplemented with KN057, exhibiting a trend consistent with that of absolute dPT values. These results suggest a consistent dPT‐shortening effect of KN057 across haemophilia subtypes compared with placebo (Figure 3). A similar response of KN057 on dPT ratio was observed in plasma from participants with VWD3 (Figure 3).

Ex Vivo Pharmacodynamic Comparison of KN057 in Hemophilic Plasma With and Without Inhibitors

To evaluate the pharmacodynamic differences in hemophilic plasma with and without inhibitors, two KN057 concentrations (5.60 nM and 28.00 nM) were selected based on ex vivo pharmacodynamic profiles, using dPT, peak thrombin, and ETP as assessment parameters. No significant differences in pharmacodynamic activity were observed between samples from participants with or without inhibitors (Figure 4). In addition, the ex vivo pharmacodynamic profiles of KN057 were consistent between participants with HA without inhibitors and HB without inhibitors, with similar trends also observed in plasma from HA with inhibitors and HB with inhibitors (Figures 2 and 3).

Discussion

TFPI regulates tissue factor‐mediated coagulation through a FXa‐dependent feedback inhibition of the tissue factor‐FVIIa complex and has emerged as a therapeutic target for restoring thrombin generation [6]. Inhibition of TFPI reverts the coagulation process to a more evolutionarily primitive state, offering a mechanistic rationale for TFPI‐targeted approaches in haemophilia. The present study aimed to evaluate the ex vivo pharmacodynamics of a new anti‐TFPI antibody‐KN057 in blood samples from participants with haemophilia or VWD3. As VWD3 samples were scarce, this discussion mainly focused on haemophilia. In this study, KN057 was shown to induce thrombin generation in hemophilic plasma in a concentration‐dependent manner.

Thrombin plays an essential role in achieving and maintaining effective haemostasis and stable clot formation [14]. TGA provides an integrated assessment of the haemostatic balance, capturing inter‐individual variability in bleeding phenotype and treatment response driven by differences in pro‐ and anticoagulant factor levels [14, 15]. Moreover, TGAs have proven valuable in monitoring the pharmacodynamic effects of non‐factor replacement therapies, such as emicizumab [16] and concizumab [17]. Previously published data with concizumab (23.7 nM) and marstacimab (100 nM) showed that the generated thrombin Cmax and ETP values were approximately 90 nM and 1000 nM*min, and 125 nM and 1250 nM*min, respectively [17, 18]. In the present study, TGA using PPP revealed a concentration‐dependent increase in thrombin generation across haemophilia types, as evidenced by increased peak thrombin levels and ETP. Notably, a marked increase was detected at 5.60 nM of KN057, with thrombin generation approaching near‐maximal levels at 28.00 nM. The mean peak values of peak thrombin and ETP in plasma from participants with haemophilia ranged from 85.00–120.00 nM and 1400.00–1650.00 nM*min, respectively. Additionally, only subtle changes in lag time have been reported following the addition of anti‐TFPI agents to PPP‐based TGAs [17]. Consistently, our study also showed no obvious alteration in lag time after being supplemented with KN057, supporting the reproducibility of this observation across TFPI‐targeting agents under ex vivo conditions. This finding might be explained by the characteristics of the PPP system, in which exogenously added phospholipids primarily serve as a catalytic surface for coagulation reactions [18]. Under these conditions, circulating free TFPI had limited interaction with the artificial phospholipid membranes, resulting in a minimal inhibitory effect on coagulation initiation. Therefore, TFPI inhibition was not expected to significantly affect lag time in the PPP system. Taken together, the observed concentration‐dependent enhancement of thrombin generation by KN057 supported its potential as a TFPI‐targeting agent for restoring haemostatic balance in hemophilic plasma.

The dPT assay measures the activity of the extrinsic pathway following activation of coagulation with tissue factor. In our study, we observed a dose‐dependent decrease in dPT when KN057 was added to plasma samples within the range of 1.12 to 5.60 nM, with a highly consistent dose‐response across all participant samples. This pattern aligns with findings reported for other TFPI‐targeting agents, including marstacimab [18] and befovacimab [19], although cross‐study interpretation should be undertaken cautiously due to potential variability arising from differences in assay design, plasma handling, and experimental conditions. These findings support the role of KN057 as an anti‐TFPI antibody capable of enhancing tissue factor‐initiated coagulation in hemophilic plasma.

The present study included 4 participants with VWD3 as an exploratory cohort, extending the evaluation of KN057 beyond haemophilia to a broader spectrum of severe congenital bleeding disorders characterized by impaired thrombin generation. In this study, in PPP from patients with type 3 VWD, the addition of KN057 increased thrombin generation peak (Peak) and ETP compared with control, suggesting an improvement of the thrombin generation defect in vitro. Mechanistically, type 3 VWD is characterized by near‐complete VWF deficiency, resulting in defective platelet adhesion and aggregation as a primary haemostatic defect. In addition, the lack of VWF reduces FVIII stability and markedly lowers FVIII activity, which impairs thrombin generation and further exacerbates bleeding tendency [20]. As a critical negative regulator of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, TFPI directly inhibits activated factor X (FXa) at first. Subsequently, it suppresses the TF‐FVIIa complex in an FXa‐dependent manner, resulting in the assembly of a stable tetrameric TF‐FVIIa‐FXa‐TFPI complex, thereby limiting thrombin generation [21]. Neutralization of TFPI by KN057 relieves this inhibitory effect and partially compensates for impaired coagulation cascade amplification due to FVIII deficiency, ultimately resulting in increased thrombin generation. Accordingly, the observed pharmacodynamic responses in VWD3 plasma provide preliminary evidence supporting the biological consistency of KN057 activity, although these findings remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size.

Several limitations of our study should be taken into account. Firstly, this was a single‐centre study with a relatively small sample size, particularly in subgroup analyses, which might have limited the statistical power and restricted the generalizability of the findings to broader patient populations. Larger cohorts involving various haemophilia subtypes, both with and without inhibitors, are needed to further characterize ex vivo responses of KN057 in haemophilia. Nonetheless, under controlled conditions, our study demonstrated consistent trends across individual patient‐level data, regardless of haemophilia type. Secondly, platelet‐rich plasma and whole‐blood assays were not performed due to strict pre‐analytical requirements, including the need for testing within 2 h of sample collection, which could not be consistently ensured under current study conditions. Thirdly, EC50 could not be determined in the TGA assay due to limited concentration points and the absence of a clear response plateau. Finally, differences in assay conditions might influence both the experimental outcomes.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study demonstrated that KN057 induced pro‐coagulant responses in plasma from participants with haemophilia. The pharmacodynamic profile and effective concentration range of KN057 were broadly consistent across different haemophilia subtypes, including HA and HB, with or without inhibitors. Based on this ex vivo data, the clinical trial (KN057‐A‐201, NCT05421429) will evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of KN057 in patients with haemophilia.
