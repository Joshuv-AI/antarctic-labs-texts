Virtual brain endocasts of the palaeanodont Metacheiromys marshi and the neurosensory evolution of early Pholidotamorpha
Authors: Eduard Cabasés Bru, Raquel Moya‐Costa, John R Wible, Stephen L Brusatte, Ornella C Bertrand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPalaeanodonts are extinct placental mammals exhibiting myrmecophagous and fossorial adaptations. They have been viewed as the sister group to Pholidota (pangolins), which is the sister‐clade to Carnivora within Ferae. Palaeanodonts display morphological features that are similar to those in pangolins and xenarthrans, which are likely related to their specialized ecological niche. Therefore, it has been challenging to decipher whether shared features are synapomorphies or convergences. Here, we describe in detail the brain virtual endocast anatomy of the middle Eocene palaeanodont Metacheiromys marshi and make morphological and quantitative comparisons with two extant African pangolins, Smutsia gigantea and Phataginus tricuspis. We found several differences between the fossil and extant taxa including in the olfactory bulbs, the cerebellum, and the venous and arterial systems. However, we propose an anatomical feature as a possible synapomorphy uniting palaeanodonts and pangolins in Pholidotamorpha: the presence of a well‐developed orbital gyrus and a presylvian sulcus. These correlated features are present in some other groups but are likely derived traits acquired independently, for example in xenarthrans. In terms of the behavioural ecology of M. marshi and pangolins, we suggest that reduced olfaction and eye movement control is likely linked to fossoriality or slow arboreality as previously proposed for rodents. The expansion of the orbital gyrus of the neocortex is proposed to be related to the evolution of myrmecophagy and the emergence of a protrusile tongue in early Pholidotamorpha, acquired convergently in giant anteaters. Overall, this work provides a novel perspective on the evolution of palaeanodonts and pangolins and presents new hypotheses related to the impact of ecological adaptations on the brain evolution of mammals.Keywords: myrmecophagy, neocortex, olfactory bulbs, orbital gyrus, petrosal lobules, Pholidota, presylvian sulcus, slow locomotion, tentorium

INTRODUCTION

Palaeanodonta is an extinct clade of placental mammals with fossorial adaptations mainly known from the early Paleocene (Torrejonian) to the late Eocene (Chadronian) of North America and to a lesser degree from Eurasia (Rose, 2006, Kondrashov & Agadjanian, 2012). This group is composed of three families: Escavadodontidae, Epoicotheriidae, and Metacheiromyidae. ‘Escavadodontidae’ is likely basal and paraphyletic with the other two families that show more derived postcranial and dental features (Rose, 2008). Metacheiromyids display robust postcrania adapted to a fossorial lifestyle, although they are less derived in this regard than the Epoicotheriidae. A progressive reduction of the dentition is also present and was greater in Metacheiromyidae than in Epoicotheriidae (Rose, 2008). In metacheiromyids specifically, a grooved and rugose edge on the palate posterior to the cheek teeth suggests the presence of a protrusile tongue used for eating small invertebrates (Rose, 1978).

The phylogenetic position of Palaeanodonta has been a topic of debate for decades, with two main hypotheses. They have either been considered closely related to Xenarthra (anteaters, tree sloths, and armadillos) from the Americas (e.g., Gaudin, 2004; Patterson et al., 1992; Simpson, 1945; Szalay, 1977) or to Pholidota (pangolins) from Asia and sub‐Saharan Africa today and from North America and Europe in the past (e.g., Alba et al., 2018; Emry, 1970; Gaudin et al., 2009, 2016, 2020; McKenna & Bell, 1997; Rose et al., 2005). More recently, the clade Palaeanodonta + Pholidota, referred as Pholidotamorpha (Gaudin et al., 2009), has received additional support from combined morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses of placental mammals (O'Leary et al., 2013). Additionally, molecular phylogenies have recovered Pholidota as sister‐clade to Carnivora (e.g., Delsuc et al., 2002; Foley et al., 2023; Meredith et al., 2011; Murphy et al., 2021; Murphy, Eizirik, Johnson, et al., 2001; Murphy, Eizirik, O'Brien, et al., 2001; O'Leary et al., 2013; Shoshani & McKenna, 1998). Past studies have found some potential morphological synapomorphies between Pholidota and Carnivora, including features in the cranium (ossified tentorium cerebelli) and in the wrist (fusion of the scaphoid and lunate bones). However, those characteristics are absent or reduced in the early members belonging to each group (Gaudin et al., 2020; Rose et al., 2005). To date, no molecular phylogenies have found Xenarthra + Pholidota as sister‐clades, and at this point in the maturity of molecular analyses such a relationship would be highly unexpected (Gaudin et al., 2020).

The reason that a link between Xenarthra and Pholidota persisted in the literature is because of the presence of shared morphological features related to a convergent lifestyle including diet (e.g., reduction of the dentition) and fossoriality (e.g., sacro‐innominate fusion in the vertebral column), but also cranial similarities (e.g., shallow subarcuate fossa, palatine confined to the ventral orbital wall, mandibular symphyseal spout, squamosal epitympanic sinus) reported by several studies (Gaudin, 2004; Novacek, 1986, 1992; Novacek & Wyss, 1986). It is worth noting that the morphological analysis of O'Leary et al. (2013) recovered the group ‘Edentata’ including Pholidota + Xenarthra + Tubulidentata (the aardvarks from Africa), which was first proposed by Cuvier (1798). Later, Weber (1904) proposed to classify pangolins and aardvarks in their own orders as Pholidota and Tubulidentata. Regarding the phylogenetic position of Palaeanodonta, this group displays derived features that are present in both xenarthrans and pangolins, including fossorial adaptations and reduced dentition suggesting a myrmecophagous diet (Gaudin et al., 2020; Rose, 2006). The grouping of Palaeanodonta with pangolins and xenarthrans was originally proposed by Matthew (1918) and supported by Novacek (1986), but later was abandoned (Gaudin et al., 1996; Rose et al., 2005; Rose & Emry, 1993). Additionally, Palaeanodonta, and specifically the taxon Metacheiromys, share morphological features of the auditory region with both Xenarthra and Pholidota (Gaudin et al., 2019), most of which are likely derived characters (O'Leary et al., 2013).

The fossil record of Pholidota is relatively incomplete, which may in part explain the difficulties of finding shared morphological features between Pholidota and Carnivora. Today, there are eight recognized species of pangolins belonging to three genera, including Manis from Asia and Phataginus and Smutsia from Africa (Gaubert et al., 2011, 2020). However, it is likely that the number of species is underestimated based on recent molecular data (Gu et al., 2023; Heighton et al., 2023; Wangmo et al., 2025). There is also a significant ghost lineage for Pholidota as the oldest fossil is dated to the middle Eocene, while the oldest Carnivoramorpha and Palaeanodonta are Paleocene in age (Gaudin et al., 2016, 2020; O'Leary et al., 2013). Eocene pangolins are already relatively derived, sharing morphological features with Palaeanodonta, including postcranial fossorial adaptations in the scapula, humerus, metapodials, and unguals (see Gaudin et al., 2009, 2020; Rose et al., 2005; Storch, 2003). More specifically, the Eocene pangolin Eomanis was already clearly adapted to a fossorial lifestyle and likely had a myrmecophagous diet (e.g., it was edentulous; Gaudin et al., 2020). Another taxon, Patriomanis is a late Eocene pangolin from North America that is also edentulous, but shows a mix of fossorial and arboreal adaptations with a prehensile tail (Gaudin et al., 2016, 2020).

Internal cranial anatomy has had an important role supporting the close relationship between Pholidota and Carnivora because the presence of an ossified tentorium has been one of the very few derived morphological characters linking both clades. Most mammals have a membranous tentorium that separates the cerebrum and cerebellum, but Pholidota and Carnivora have a bony lamina within the membranous tentorium (Gaudin et al., 2016). In Pholidota, the ossified tentorium extends from the anteroventral border of the petrosal to the dorsal part of the cranial cavity and includes components from the parietal and squamosal (Gaudin et al., 2016). Carnivora also has a tentorial process of the parietal but not of the squamosal (Nojima, 1990). To study endocranial anatomy in fossil pangolins, the sediment inside the holotype cranium of the latest Eocene taxon Patriomanis americana (AMNH F:AM 78999) was removed. Its endocranial anatomy was briefly described by Emry (1970), who found that this specimen had a less developed bony tentorium in comparison to extant pangolins. More recently, the use of micro‐computed tomography (micro‐CT) scanning has revolutionized our understanding of the internal anatomy of extinct species by serving as a non‐invasive technique that reveals endocranial anatomy while preventing the destruction of the fossils themselves (Bertrand et al., 2025; Dozo et al., 2023 and references therein). For example, a study of the auditory region of Metacheiromys using CT data provided substantial new information regarding potential shared morphological features among Palaeanodonta, Xenarthra, and Pholidota (Gaudin et al., 2019). An ossified tentorium was found to be absent in Metacheiromys, with only a sharp crista petrosa present forming the ventral anchor of the membranous tentorium as in most other mammals (Gaudin et al., 2019).

One aspect of the biology of both Pholidota and Palaeanodonta that has been rarely studied relates to the evolution of their neurosensory system, but here are some exceptions. A latex endocast of the Pholidota Patriomanis americana was produced by Emry (1970). A restoration of an endocranial cast based on the cranium of the Palaeanodonta Palaeanodon ignavus (AMNH 15086) was published by Matthew (1918) and reproduced by Simpson (1931). Additionally, two natural endocasts of the epoicotheriid palaeanodont Epoicotherium sp. (FMNH UM483; AMNH 9245) were published by Patterson et al. (1992). Finally, a brain virtual endocast of M. marshi (USNM 452349) was generated for a study by Bertrand et al. (2022), but only quantitative data were produced and no anatomical description of the virtual endocast was published.

We here describe the brain virtual endocasts of M. marshi USNM 452349 and AMNH 131777. We also provide quantifications on the size of the brain and of its components for AMNH 131777 and compare these values to those obtained for USNM 452349 by Bertrand et al. (2022). We acknowledge that the relationships of palaeandonts to extant placentals are not fully settled. Because the few appropriate phylogenetic analyses (e.g., Gaudin et al., 2009; O'Leary et al., 2013) recovered palaeanodont relationships with Pholidota, we also report on the brain virtual endocasts of two extant pangolins, Smutsia gigantea and Phataginus tricuspis. Because of the scarcity of information on the endocasts of the extinct pangolins Pat. americana and the palaeanodont Pal. ignavus and Epoicotherium sp., we only make references to these taxa when relevant information is available. We also propose potential morphological characters based on endocranial anatomy that could improve our understanding of the phylogenetic relationships of Palaeanodonta, Pholidota, and Xenarthra. Finally, we provide some hypotheses related to the impact of myrmecophagy and slow locomotion on the brain evolution of Pholidotamorpha and other mammals with similar adaptations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimens studied

The two relatively complete crania of M. marshi (AMNH 131777; USNM 452349) used in the current study were recovered from the Bridger Formation of Wyoming (middle Eocene; Bridgerian NALMA). Metacheiromys marshi is considered one of the most dentally derived palaeanodonts (Rose, 2008) as this taxon includes the most extreme tooth reduction among palaeanodonts, with an almost completely edentulous jaw (Schoch, 1984). Regarding the dental formula, the upper teeth include a canine and one post‐canine, while the lower teeth correspond to one incisor, one canine, and two post‐canine teeth (Rose, 2008). Two extant pangolins were also included for comparison. Smutsia gigantea (giant pangolin) is a large nocturnal terrestrial pangolin (30–40 kg; 112.5–180 cm long) that can be found in African savannas and rainforests and in areas with large termite populations (Hoffmann et al., 2020). Phataginus tricuspis (white‐bellied pangolin) is one of the smallest pangolins (up to 3 kg; 100 cm long), with a tail longer than its head and body combined. This semi‐arboreal and nocturnal species is found in lowland tropical moist forests (both primary and secondary), as well as in savanna/forest mosaics of Africa (Jansen et al., 2020). Other comparisons were made with a latex endocast of Pat. americana (AMNH F:AM 78999; Emry, 1970), a reconstruction of a natural endocast of Pal. ignavus (AMNH 15086; Matthew, 1918; Simpson, 1931), and natural endocasts of Epoicotherium sp. (FMNH UM483; AMNH 9245; Patterson et al., 1992).

Virtual endocast acquisition

To generate the virtual endocasts of the different specimens, CT scans of the crania were processed using Avizo Lite 2019.4 (Visualization Sciences Group, 1995–2019). Metacheiromys marshi (AMNH 131777) was CT‐scanned with a 2010 GE phoenix nano‐focus v|tome|x s240 at the American Museum of Natural History's Microscopy and Imaging Facility (MIF). The other specimen of M. marshi (USNM 452349) was scanned at the High‐Resolution X‐ray Computed Tomography Facility at UChicago PaleoCT (University of Chicago, Department of Organismal Biology and Anatomy, Chicago, IL, USA) by T. Gaudin. Both specimens were provided by JRW. In terms of the scanning settings: M. marshi (AMNH 131777) has a voxel size of 0.04240788 mm and was scanned with a voltage of 225 kV, and an amperage of 160 μA. Regarding M. marshi (USNM 452349), the voxel size is 0.026012 and the specimen was scanned with a voltage of 190 kV and an amperage of 136 μA. The two modern specimens: S. gigantea (AMNH 53855; Media ID: 000106417) and P. tricuspis (AMNH 53874; Media ID: 000084693) were downloaded from Morphosource. Both specimens were uploaded by A. Harrington. The specimens are curated at the AMNH Mammalogy Department and were CT‐scanned with a Nikon Metrology XT H 225 ST at the Duke Shared Materials Instrumentation Facility (SMiF, Duke University, NC, USA). Smutsia gigantea has a voxel size of 0.079287 mm and was scanned with a voltage of 193 kV and an amperage of 82 μA, while P. tricuspis has a voxel size of 0.046592 mm and was scanned with a voltage of 178 kV and an amperage of 82 μA.

For this work, AMNH 131777 was segmented by ECB following the segmentation procedure for USNM 452349, segmented by OCB (Bertrand et al., 2022). The pen tool was used to isolate the endocranial cavity of the cranium because the inside was filled with sediment, and in some areas, the matrix was of similar density to the bone. Then interpolation was performed every three slices. In the extant S. gigantea and P. tricuspis, the absence of sediment made the segmentation simpler. Interpolation was performed every five slices for the modern dataset instead of three for the fossils. Then, Biomedisa (Lösel et al., 2020) was used for semi‐automatic interpolation using the CT data and the label field file of the segmentation that had been generated by hand for the extant specimens. This resulted in a completely segmented version of the virtual endocast that was cleaned in Avizo to generate the final versions of the endocasts. In the fossils, when the bone was not preserved or was detached from the matrix due to a fracture, a straight line was used to link the two nearest pieces of bone and to be conservative with the reconstruction of the endocast. In the three virtual endocasts made for this work, the post‐processing protocol for USNM 452349 (Bertrand et al., 2022) was followed. The endocranial volume and the size of different brain components were quantified. To determine the endocranial volume, we generated a ‘surface rendering’ of the endocast in Avizo. The olfactory bulbs were isolated from the rest of the endocast using the ‘volume edit’ module and subsequently their volume was measured with the ‘Surface Area Volume’ option in Avizo (Figure S1). The petrosal lobules (= paraflocculi) were first re‐segmented in Avizo and their volume was calculated using the ‘Surface Area Volume’ for USNM 452349 in Bertrand et al. (2022). Because of the poorer preservation of AMNH 131777 in this area and the great reduction of the subarcuate fossa (Jollie, 1968; where the petrosal lobules are located) on the endocast of S. gigantea and P. tricuspis, the volume of the petrosal lobules was not calculated in these specimens. Finally, to calculate the neocortical surface area, the ‘generate surface’ function was used, and then this structure was isolated from the rest of the endocast through the ‘extract surface’ tool. The boundaries of the neocortical surface are defined by the rhinal fissure on the lateral aspect of the endocast, but this structure was challenging to identify with certainty on the lateral aspect along the entire length of the cerebrum in the two modern specimens of pangolins. Imam et al. (2018) illustrated the brain and specifically the boundaries of the paleocortex of P. tricuspis (= Manis tricuspis), which helped us in our segmentation of the neocortex in the extant taxa (Figure S1). However, the value for neocortical surface area of S. gigantea should be taken with caution as the brain of this species has not been published to guide our extraction and only P. tricuspis was used. Linear measurements were taken following the procedure from Bertrand and Silcox (2016) for rodents that were also applied to other mammals (e.g., Bertrand, Jiménez Lao, et al., 2024). Rendering of the virtual endocasts inside the translucent crania were also produced in Avizo (Figure 1). The colours used for the endocasts correspond to what is calculated for the brain endocast (blue) and the petrosal lobule (green) volumes in Avizo. The remaining part in yellow corresponds to vessels in bony canals that are not counted as part of the endocranial volume because they would lead to an overestimation of the endocranial volume.

Body mass and encephalization quotient estimations

The most common proxy used to estimate the body mass of a fossil mammal is the area of the first lower molar (Hopkins, 2018), but the taxa studied here either have reduced or no teeth, which means we had to use another proxy. We could have used limb‐based mass estimations, with either the lengths of long bones or the midshaft cross‐sectional dimensions of the humerus, femur, or both. However, there are no postcrania elements at our disposal to estimate the body mass of AMNH 131777. Another option is to estimate body mass using the occipital condyle width (OCW). This dimension has been found to be a strong predictor of body mass in therian mammals (Engelman, 2022). Therefore, we used the following equation: ln(body mass) = 7.70727 × ln(OCW)2/3–8.21527 (only therians equation). We used the same equation to estimate the body mass of USNM 452349, S. gigantea, and P. tricuspis for consistency.

A standard practice in palaeoneurobiology is to calculate the Encephalization Quotient (EQ; Jerison, 1973). The EQ corresponds to the ratio between the actual brain size (Ei) of a species ‘i’ and the brain size that is expected for a hypothetical mammal of the same body mass (E c; Martin, 1990). The EQ was calculated following the equation from Jerison (1973) (EQ = brain size/(0.12 × (body mass)0.67)) and the equation from Eisenberg (1981) (EQ = brain size/(0.0553 × (body mass)0.74)). We used both because Jerison (1973)'s equation may underestimate and Eisenberg (1981)'s equation may overestimate the EQ of a particular specimen because of differences between the samples that were used to produce these equations (see Bertrand et al., 2017).

Cranial vascular system

Little has been published on the cranial vascular system of extant pangolins. Cranial arteries have been described for P. tetradactyla (= Manis macrura) by Hyrtl (1854) and Manis javanica by Bugge (1979). Radiographs of injected cranial arteries are illustrated for P. tricuspis (= Manis tricuspis) and Manis pentadactyla in Du Boulay and Verity (1973). Aspects of the intracranial venous system have been reported recently for extant pangolins as well as for Pat. americana (AMNH 78999) and M. marshi (USNM 452349) by Billet et al. (2026).

DESCRIPTIONS AND COMPARISONS

Overall brain endocast and encephalization quotient

The cranium of the two specimens of M. marshi does not show any deformation and only presents a few fractures that minimally alter the final endocast models with the exception of the poor preservation of the subarcuate fossa in AMNH 131777. Metacheiromys marshi USNM 452349 has a larger brain endocast based on endocranial length and width (TL: total endocast length; CRMW: Cerebrum maximal width; Table 1) compared with AMNH 131777. Regarding P. tricuspis, it has similar dimensions to USNM 452349, while the endocast of S. gigantea displays the largest dimensions of the sample (Table 1). Based on body mass and endocranial volume, AMNH 131777 has an EQ of 0.49 and 0.66 based on Jerison's equation (1973) and Eisenberg's equation (1981), respectively (Table 1), while USNM 452349 has an EQ of 0.57 (Jerison equation) and 0.76 (Eisenberg equation; Table 1). For both S. gigantea and P. tricuspis, the EQ is 0.48 using Jerison's equation, which is very similar to the values for M. marshi using the same equation. With Eisenberg's equation, the values are again higher, at 0.52 and 0.60 for P. tricuspis and S. gigantea, respectively, but still lower than the values obtained for M. marshi (Table 1).

Olfactory bulbs

In dorsal view, the mid‐region of the olfactory bulbs in both specimens of M. marshi is wide (although wider in AMNH 131777) and then narrows anteriorly towards the tip and posteriorly at the level of the circular fissure (Figure 2a,b). In lateral view, the olfactory bulbs have a triangular form, with the narrowest point located anterodorsally (Figure 3a–d). In both S. gigantea and P. tricuspis, the olfactory bulbs are rounded in dorsal view and separated by an anteroposterior gap (Figure 2c,d), which is absent in M. marshi where instead we can observe the cast of the superior olfactory sinus (Figure 2a,b). The superior olfactory sinus is also visible in Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). In lateral view, the olfactory bulbs are shorter anteroposteriorly in both S. gigantea and P. tricuspis (Figure 3e–h) compared with M. marshi (Figure 3a,b); however, the narrowest point of the olfactory bulbs is also the anterodorsal region as in M. marshi (Figure 2a,b). Emry (1970) described Pat. americana as having short olfactory bulbs anteroposteriorly, which is more similar to extant pangolins than to M. marshi. In contrast, the olfactory bulbs in Pal. ignavus have similar proportions to those of M. marshi, its close relative (Simpson, 1931). The olfactory bulbs of Epoicotherium sp. are anteroposteriorly shorter and mediolaterally wider (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19) compared with M. marshi and the two extant pangolins. The circular fissure, which separates the olfactory bulbs from the rest of the endocast, appears expanded in M. marshi (Figure 2a,b) compared with the two modern species. It is narrower in S. gigantea (Figure 2d) and even more compressed anteroposteriorly in P. tricuspis (Figure 2c). The anterior aspect of the olfactory bulbs is oriented more ventrally in M. marshi compared with S. gigantea and P. tricuspis in which it is oriented more anteriorly (Figure 1). One of the reasons might be due to the fact that the olfactory bulbs are more expanded anterodorsally in M. marshi than in both extant pangolins (Figure 1).

The olfactory bulbs of AMNH 131777 represent a total of 7.10% of the total volume of the endocast and 0.04% compared with body mass (Table 1). They account for a higher volume in USNM 452349 (Table 1), but represent a lower percentage in comparison to endocranial volume (6.42%), but higher compared with body mass (0.05%). In terms of the linear measurements of the olfactory bulbs, they are slightly higher in USNM 452349 than in AMNH 131777 (Table 1). In S. gigantea, the olfactory bulbs represent a lower percentage in relation to both endocranial volume (5.58%) and body mass (0.01%; Table 1) compared with M. marshi. In P. tricuspis, the volume of the olfactory bulbs represents a similar ratio to S. gigantea in comparison to both endocranial volume (5.42%) and body mass (0.02%; Table 1). Using the ratios OL/OH, OL/OW, OL/TL (OL: olfactory bulb length, OH: olfactory bulb height, OW: olfactory bulb width), we observe that M. marshi has more elongated olfactory bulbs than the extant species, in which the bulbs appear to be compressed anteroposteriorly (Table 1, Figure 3).

Cerebrum and midbrain

The cerebrum, as in all other mammals, is composed of two different parts, the neocortex and the paleocortex (= piriform lobe; Martin, 1990). In dorsal view, the cerebrum of M. marshi displays an elongated ovoid shape with a maximum width in the mid‐region to posterior third that slightly narrows towards the olfactory bulbs, which means that the cerebrum is mediolaterally narrower anteriorly (Figure 2a,b). In the extant pangolins, the cerebrum is more rectangular in dorsal view, with the posterior region being wider than the anterior part (Figure 2c,d). Patriomanis americana has a smaller cerebrum compared with the extant pangolins (Emry, 1970). Both Palaeanodon ignavus and Epoicotherium sp., have a cerebrum that is mediolaterally narrower anteriorly compared with its posterior region than M. marshi and extant pangolins (Simpson, 1931; Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). The cerebrum is separated from the olfactory bulbs by the circular fissure in all taxa including Epoicotherium sp. and it was likely the case as well in Pal. ignavus and Pat. americana, although it was not noted in the original descriptions of Simpson (1931) and Emry (1970). In dorsal view, the medial region of the cerebrum is more anteriorly positioned than the lateral edges in M. marshi and S. gigantea (Figure 2a,b,d), while in P. tricuspis (Figure 2c) and Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19), it is mediolaterally straight. In both specimens of M. marshi, the neocortical surface is delimited by the rhinal fissure laterally (Figures 2a,b and 3a,b), the transverse sinus posteriorly, and the circular fissure anteriorly (Figure 2a,b). In dorsal view, the neocortex does not expand laterally (Figure 2a,b) and in lateral view, the neocortex does not expand ventrally either, and is overall mainly confined to the dorsal aspect of the endocast (Figure 3a,b). In dorsal and lateral views, the neocortex expands laterally, ventrally, and posteriorly more in both S. gigantea and P. tricuspis compared with M. marshi (Figures 2c,d and 3e–h). In lateral view, the neocortex of the extant taxa is also delimited by the rhinal fissure from the paleocortex (Figure 3e–h). The neocortex of M. marshi (AMNH 131777) accounts for 10.54% of the total surface of the endocast (Table 1). In M. marshi (USNM 452349), it represents 11.65% of the endocast (Table 1). Compared with the fossils, the neocortex represents a much larger proportion of the brain in both S. gigantea (25.96%) and P. tricuspis (32.25%). In relation to body mass, the neocortex is smaller in S. gigantea (0.01%) and higher in P. tricuspis (0.04%) in comparison to M. marshi (0.03%). The rhinal fissure would have had a more dorsal position in Pat. americana compared with Manis (Emry, 1970). The same structure was also annotated with a question mark in Pal. ignavus but its position was not discussed in Simpson (1931: figure 9). The rhinal fissure in Pal. ignavus appears to be in a similar position as in M. marshi, also suggesting a neocortical expansion limited to the dorsal aspect of the endocast. Similarly in Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19), the neocortex appeared to have been limited to the dorsal surface of the cerebrum delimited by the rhinal fissure laterally, although not labelled.

As in M. marshi, there is no contact of the neocortex with the olfactory bulbs in the extant taxa because of the presence of the circular fissure anteriorly (Figure 3e–h). In dorsal view, the circular fissure is in contact with the neocortex medially and the paleocortex laterally in M. marshi (Figure 2a,b). In the extant species, the posterior region of the neocortex is separated from the cerebellum by the ossified tentorium, which acts as a physical barrier between these structures (Figure 3e–h; S2m‐p). The orbital gyrus can be differentiated in the most anterior region of the neocortex in M. marshi, S. gigantea, and P. tricuspis (see fresh brain of a P. tricuspis in Imam et al., 2017: Figure 1a,b). This gyrus is delimited anteriorly by the circular fissure and posteriorly by the presylvian sulcus (Figure 2; as observed in the carnivoran Canis in Lyras et al., 2023: figure 17.4e), medially by the superior sagittal sinus (or by the medial border of the hemispheres in extant pangolins as their superior sagittal sinus if they have one does not leave a trace on the endocast) and laterally by the rhinal fissure (Figures 2 and 3). In dorsal view, it is difficult to identify the presence or absence of other sulci in both specimens of M. marshi (Figure 2a,b). We note the possible presence of a lateral sulcus on the right hemisphere of M. marshi AMNH 131777 (Figure 2a) and Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). Simpson (1931) also mentioned that one or more longitudinal sulci may have been present in Pal. ignavus, but Patterson et al. (1992) noted that a presylvian sulcus (=transverse cerebral fissure) was not present in Palaeanodon. In Epoicotherium sp., a presylvian sulcus and an orbital gyrus are both present and well developed (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19).

In dorsal view, the gyri and sulci on the surface of the endocast of P. tricuspis are relatively well demarcated and some of them are visible in S. gigantea (Figure 2c,d). The orbital gyrus, presylvian, and lateral sulci are all visible in P. tricuspis and S. gigantea. The lateral sulcus holds a similar position in the extant taxa as in M. marshi (Figure 2). Additionally, both extant taxa have a clearly demarcated lateral gyrus extending anteroposteriorly bordered by the cerebral hemisphere midline medially and the lateral sulcus laterally (Figure 2c,d). Lateral to the latter sulcus, there are three gyri running in an anterior to posterior direction: sigmoid, coronal, and suprasylvian gyri (Figure 2c,d). They could not be distinguished with ease, so we used Imam et al. (2017) as a guide to identify them in both P. tricuspis and S. gigantea. Lateral to these gyri, a suprasylvian sulcus is present and more clearly visible in P. tricuspis than in S. gigantea (Figure 2c,d). The most lateral region next to this sulcus corresponds to the ectosylvian gyrus (Figure 2c,d). In dorsal view, anterior to the orbital gyrus in S. gigantea, a sulcus and likely the paleocortex are visible (Figures 2d and 3g,h), suggesting that the neocortex only contacts the circular fissure medially as in M. marshi, but contrasts with P. tricuspis in which the neocortex is in contact with the circular fissure mediolaterally (Figure 2c). Those different gyri and sulci can also be seen in lateral view with the ectosylvian gyrus as the most ventrally located neocortical gyrus (Figure 3e–h). We were able to identify the anterior and posterior ectosylvian gyri, which are separated by the sylvian sulcus (see Imam et al., 2017). The sylvian sulcus is very faint, is not visible in lateral view, and only visible when observed at specific angles. In lateral view, the paleocortex is more ventrally expanded in P. tricuspis and S. gigantea in comparison to M. marshi (Figure 3). In ventral view, the paleocortex appears more developed anteriorly in M. marshi and S. gigantea, forming bulbous lobes also known as olfactory peduncles (Igado et al., 2021), in comparison to P. tricuspis, which exhibits less pronounced lobes (Figure 4c). Emry (1970) described Pat. americana as displaying a pattern of sulci very similar to that of Manis without providing further details.

In dorsal and lateral views, M. marshi has a groove located posterior to the cerebrum and anterior to the cerebellum corresponding to the exposure of the midbrain (Figures 2a,b and 3a,b). In P. tricuspis and in S. gigantea, the midbrain is not visible. It is covered mainly by the neocortex and also slightly by the cerebellum (see Imam et al., 2017: Figure 6a). Patriomanis americana and Pal. ignavus were also described as not displaying an exposed midbrain by Simpson (1931) and Emry (1970) but it is challenging to verify this claim as this regions is not well preserved on the latex endocast of Pat. americanus. On the reconstruction of the brain endocast of Pal. ignavus, the midbrain is not annotated, only the confluence of sinuses is labelled and there appears to be little space between the sinuses and the vermis (Simpson, 1931: figure 9). In Epoicotherium sp., the midbrain is not visible and likely ventral to the cerebrum and cerebellum as these two structures are in contact with each other (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). An extensive dorsolateral gap is present between the cerebrum and the cerebellum in P. tricuspis in contrast to S. gigantea, in which the gap is shallower (Figures 2c,d and 3e–h). This space for the ossified tentorium is hidden dorsally by the large canal containing the transverse sinus in S. gigantea, which is relatively thinner in P. tricuspis (Figures 2c,d and 3e–h). There is a clear development of the ossified tentorium in P. tricuspis in contrast to M. marshi in which this structure is absent (Figure 5). In P. tricuspis, the tentorium includes processes from the parietal, squamosal, and alisphenoid, while in S. gigantea, it is made of the same bones minus the alisphenoid. An ossified tentorium was present in the early pangolin Pat. americana but was less developed than in Manis. It is comprised of parietal and squamosal (Gaudin et al., 2016). According to Emry (1970), an ossified tentorium was present in the palaeanodont Pal. ignavus, although less prominent than in Manis; however, he did not specify the bones comprising the tentorium. Similarly, Epoicotherium sp. was described as having an ossified tentorium by Patterson et al. (1992).

Cerebellum

The cerebellum is slightly narrower than the maximum mediolateral dimension of the endocast in all specimens, although to a higher degree in S. gigantea and potentially in Pal. ignavus (Figure 2; Simpson, 1931: figure 9). Interestingly, the cerebellum appears to be the narrowest mediolaterally in Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). The ratio between the cerebellum maximal width (CLMW) and the cerebrum maximal width (CRMW) shows that in M. marshi and P. tricuspis, the cerebellum accounts for 83–84% of the width of the cerebrum, while in S. gigantea, the cerebellum accounts for only 78% of the cerebrum (Table 1). In dorsal view, each lateral lobe of the cerebellum is medially separated from the vermis by the paramedian fissure. A dorsal cerebellar vein left a trace lateral to the paramedian fissure in M. marshi (AMNH 131777; Figure 2a). The vermis and lateral lobes are of similar proportions in all taxa described here as well as in Pat. americana (Emry, 1970). The cerebellar lobes are slightly broader than the vermis in Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). These cerebellar structures were not compared in terms of their proportions in Pal. ignavus but appear to be of similar proportions (Simpson, 1931: figure 9). The cast of the vermis does not have any trace of the transverse fissures in the described specimens including in Pal. ignavus (Simpson, 1931). However, this is likely due to the transverse fissures not leaving a mark on the endocranial cavity as they are present on the brain of P. tricuspis (Imam et al., 2017: figure 1). No description of cerebellum anatomy for Pat. americana was provided in Emry (1970) and the natural endocast does not provide sufficient details for formulating a fair description of the cerebellar anatomy. The paramedian fissure is well marked on the endocranial surface of M. marshi and P. tricuspis in comparison to S. gigantea in which this structure is harder to distinguish, which could be due to the dura mater being thicker in that region (Figure 2). In lateral view, the vermis is more dorsally expanded in M. marshi compared with P. tricuspis and S. gigantea (Figure 3). In lateral view, the petrosal lobules (only fully segmented in USNM 452349) are located anteroventral to the rest of the cerebellum (Figure 3c,d). Their combined volume is 10.23 mm3. The petrosal lobules represent 0.15% and 0.001% of the brain endocast and body mass, respectively. In M. marshi (AMNH 131777), the volume of the petrosal lobules was not measured because only part of these structures could be reconstructed (Figure 3b). In S. gigantea and P. tricuspis, the subarcuate fossae are very reduced and therefore the petrosal lobules could not be quantified in these two specimens (Figure 3e–h). Simpson (1931) mentioned that the floccular regions of the cerebellum (= petrosal lobules) are not visible in Pal. ignavus. CT scanning of a specimen of Pal. ignavus will be necessary to verify the presence or absence of these structures.

Cranial nerves and blood vessels

In lateral view, the ethmoidal nerve and vessels are located ventral to each olfactory bulb in all specimens (Figure 3). In S. gigantea, the canal containing these nerves and vessels divides into two branches that exit the cranium separately (Figure 3g,h). This contrasts with M. marshi and P. tricuspis, in which this canal is restricted to one foramen (Figure 3a–f). In ventral view, M. marshi has a canal for the optic nerve (II) located posterior to the circular fissure connected to the optic chiasm, which is located posteromedial to the cast of each optic nerve (Figure 4a,b). This configuration is different in the extant taxa, with P. tricuspis and S. gigantea having the optic canals more posteriorly located, the chiasm less prominent, and shorter canals for the optic nerves leading to the optic foramina (especially in P. tricuspis) compared with M. marshi (Figure 4c,d). These canals and the optic foramina are also much closer to the median sagittal plane in extant pangolins compared with M. marshi, in which they open far from each other mediolaterally (Figure 4). Using the CT data, we observe that in P. tricuspis, the optic nerves remain in the chiasma until they exit the cranium bilaterally through the optic foramen (Figure 4c). In ventral view, posterior to nerve II (optic), the sphenorbital fissure is present and likely carried the ophthalmic vein and cranial nerves III (oculomotor), IV (trochlear), V1 (ophthalmic), V2 (maxillary), and VI (abducens) in all specimens (Figure 4). Indeed, this configuration is likely the ancestral condition for eutherians and is present in many placental mammals, including dermopterans, chiropterans, and carnivorans (Christensen & Evans, 1979; Novacek, 1986; O'Leary et al., 2013).

Posteromedial to the sphenorbital fissures, all specimens preserve the hypophyseal fossa that would have contained the hypophysis (pituitary gland) (Figure 4). This structure has a more rectangular shape and appears larger in M. marshi compared with P. tricuspis and S. gigantea in which it is smaller and more rounded (Figure 4). In ventral view, slightly anterolateral to the hypophyseal fossa, cranial nerve V3 (i.e., mandibular branch of the trigeminal nerve) is present in all specimens (Figure 4). This branch exits the endocranial cavity through the foramen ovale. Cranial nerves VII (facial nerve) and VIII (vestibulocochlear nerve) can be identified and are located ventral to the boundary between the cerebrum and the cerebellum in M. marshi (Figure 3a,b) and anterior to this boundary (ventral to the cerebrum) in P. tricuspis and S. gigantea (Figure 3c,d). The separation between these two nerves is only visible in M. marshi (USNM 452349) because of the poor preservation in AMNH 131777 (Figure 4a,b). The two extant specimens, P. tricuspis and S. gigantea, have a longer common canal before splitting into separate canals (not traced beyond the common canal in Figure 4c,d) compared with the condition in M. marshi.

The exit of the internal jugular vein and cranial nerves IX (glossopharyngeal nerve), X (vagus nerve), and XI (accessory nerve) can be identified in M. marshi. In contrast to other mammals, such as rodents (e.g., Bertrand et al., 2019), there are two foramina instead of one jugular foramen in M. marshi (Figure 4a,b), as noted by Gaudin et al. (2019). The nerves would have exited more medially through the medial jugular foramen and the jugular vein through the lateral jugular foramen (see Gaudin et al., 2019). The cast for cranial nerve XII (hypoglossal) located posterior to the jugular foramina leaves the endocranial cavity through the hypoglossal foramen in M. marshi (Figure 4a,b). The extant P. tricuspis has a more typical arrangement with only one jugular foramen for the internal jugular vein and nerves IX, X, XI (Figure 4c), which is also the case in Palaeanodon (Matthew, 1918; Patterson et al., 1992) and Pat. americana (Gaudin et al., 2016). As in M. marshi, nerve XII exited through the hypoglossal foramen in P. tricuspis (Figure 4c). In contrast, in S. gigantea, only one large foramen is visible, suggesting that all nerves and vessels (i.e., IX, X, XI, XII and the internal jugular vein) would have exited through this opening (Figure 4d). A separate hypoglossal foramen is absent in S. gigantea (Gaudin & Wible, 1999).

In lateral view, a large longitudinal channel containing arteries and veins runs between the occiput and orbit in both specimens of M. marshi (Figure 3a–d). The posterior part of this channel is enclosed in a canal, whereas the anterior part is open on the virtual endocast. Although a continuous structure, the bone enclosed portion is actually two named canals: the posterior half opposite the cerebellum is the post‐temporal canal and the anterior half opposite the cerebrum is the orbitotemporal canal (see Wible, 1987). The primary occupants of the orbitotemporal canal were the ramus supraorbitalis and accompanying vein and of the post‐temporal canal are the arteria and vena diploëtica magna (Wible, 1987). The vessels on the virtual endocast anterior to the orbitotemporal canal are the continuation of the ramus supraorbitalis and accompanying vein, which we refer to as orbitotemporal vessels. The orbitotemporal vessels sent arteries and veins to the temporalis muscle (rami temporales) through foramina in the braincase wall. Gaudin et al. (2019) reported five foramina on the right and four on the left for AMNH 131777, two in the squamosal bilaterally and the remainder in the parietal. The orbitotemporal vessels do not leave a trace on the endocranial surface and the post‐temporal canal is absent in P. tricuspis and in S. gigantea (Figure 3e–h), and in Pat. americana (Billet et al., 2026). The orbitotemporal vessels appear to leave a trace on the endocast of Epoicotherium sp. and the orbitotemporal canal might also be present (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). Bugge (1979) reported the arteria diploëtica magna as absent in Manis javanica, but a small ramus supraorbitalis runs open in the endocranium. It is uncertain whether or not the African pangolins had a similar ramus supraorbitalis. Foramina for rami temporales are absent in Pat. americana (Gaudin et al., 2019) and extant pangolins (Jollie, 1968).

In dorsal view of the endocast of M. marshi, the superior sagittal sinus running medially between the cerebral hemispheres connects to the transverse sinus, which in turn is connected to the confluence of sinuses, forming the posterior margin of the neocortex (Figure 2a,b). The same configuration was observed in Pal. ignavus by Simpson (1931) and in Epoicotherium sp. (Patterson et al., 1992: figure 19). In extant pangolins and Pat. americana, the presence of the superior sagittal sinus cannot be determined with certainty as it does not leave a trace on the surface of the endocranial cavity (Figure 2c,d); instead, there is a vast network of diploic canals in the frontal and parietal (Billet et al., 2026). The transverse sinus, which connects to the confluence of sinuses in P. tricuspis and S. gigantea, has branches directed posteriorly, but these are not present on either specimen of M. marshi (Figure 2c,d). After inspecting the CT data carefully, we confirm that this branch leads to a multitude of small canals in the interparietal and supraoccipital in P. tricuspis, while these canals are relatively larger in S. gigantea. Their content likely corresponds to supraoccipital emissary veins exiting caudally through multiple supraoccipital foramina in both specimens, although they are more numerous and larger in diameter in S. gigantea (Figure 6a,b).

In placentals, the transverse sinus divides into three branches: the capsuloparietal emissary vein (CEV) or temporal sinus, the sigmoid sinus (SS), and the superior petrosal sinus (SPS; e.g., Wible, 1984, Novacek, 1986; Muizon et al., 2015). The CEV exits the cranium as the postglenoid vein via the postglenoid foramen, the SS as the internal jugular vein via the jugular foramen, and the SPS runs anteriorly on the endocranial surface of the petrosal. The postglenoid foramen is absent in M. marshi (Gaudin et al., 2019), and there is no indication of the CEV or of the SPS (Figure 2a,b). However, a postglenoid foramen is present in Palaeanodon sp. USGS 21876 (Patterson et al., 1992). In P. tricuspis and S. gigantea, the CEV is continuous with the transverse sinus, but they are both encased in canals (Figure 3e–h). In lateral view, the CEV starts posterior to the tentorium at the level of the lateral lobe of the cerebellum and runs anteroventrally, reaching the level of the ventral aspect of the paleocortex and leaving the cranium through the postglenoid foramen as the postglenoid vein (Figure 3e–h). The whole canal (i.e., transverse sinus, CEV, and postglenoid vein) is much larger in S. gigantea than in P. tricuspis (Figure 3e–h). Billet et al. (2026) found a similar canal for these veins in Pat. americana and extant pangolins. Neither P. tricuspis nor S. gigantea show evidence of the SPS.

A large SS is seen in M. marshi (Figure 3a,b). Its most dorsal segment is in a short canal that communicates with the post‐temporal canal. It then lies against the cerebellum and its path has a slightly posteroventral orientation that becomes completely ventral on the brainstem, exiting at the jugular foramen. Additionally, the SS is connected to the condyloid vein, which is encased in a canal in M. marshi (Figure 3a,b). The content of the condyloid canal would have exited via the foramen magnum in this taxon (Figure 4a,b). The condyloid canal is also connected to the hypoglossal foramen. One difference between the two specimens of M. marshi is that in AMNH 131777 the anterior end of the condyloid canal splits into two canals, while in USNM 452349, there are three canals including two leading to the SS and one to the hypoglossal foramen (Figure 4a,b). The SS is tiny in the extant pangolins. In S. gigantea, the SS on the left side is first in a short canal oriented dorsoventrally and then connects to the endocranial cavity as in M. marshi; a similar canal is absent on the right side (Figure 3g,h). In P. tricuspis, the SS is also within a short canal, but it is oriented anteroposteriorly before it too joins the endocranial cavity. This specimen also exhibits asymmetry, as one side presents two canals while the other only one (Figure 3e–f). The path of the SS is challenging to identify on the surface of the endocast of P. tricuspis and S. gigantea once within the endocranial cavity, but it appears to exit via the jugular foramen as in M. marshi (Figure 3a–f). The condyloid canal is absent in both P. tricuspis and S. gigantea (Figure 4c,d). In lateral view in M. marshi, the tract for the occipital emissary vein (not preserved on the left side of USNM 452349) is connected to the SS and to the post‐temporal canal – and exited the cranium posteriorly through the mastoid foramen (Figure 3a–d) as in the dog (Evans & de Lahunta, 2012; Wible, 2008). There is also a separate posterior exit out of the cranium for the content of the post‐temporal canal through the post‐temporal foramen in M. marshi (Gaudin et al., 2019). The occipital emissary vein canal is absent in P. tricuspis and S. gigantea (Figure 3e–h). The inferior petrosal sinus is hard to trace in M. marshi (Figure 4a,b), as noted by Gaudin et al. (2019). It is present in both P. tricuspis and S. gigantea (Figure 4c,d). This sinus runs open on the endocast in P. tricuspis but is encased inside a canal in S. gigantea.

Metacheiromys marshi, Pat. americana (Gaudin et al., 2016), P. tricuspis, and S. gigantea have a frontal diploic vein foramen (Figures 2, 3, 4 and 6). However, in M. marshi this opening is more ventrally positioned in the orbit, whereas it is near the dorsal orbital rim in the others (Figure 6; Billet et al., 2026). Additionally, we observe intraspecific variation in M. marshi, with USNM 452349 displaying two foramina (on the right side only), while AMNH131777 only has one as in extant pangolins (Figure 6a,b). The frontal diploic vein has an extensive area of drainage in the pangolins, but this is more limited in M. marshi. In the pangolins, it drains veins that occupy a canal running along the circular fissure (Figure 3e–h), but a similar canal and vein are not present in M. marshi (Figure 3a,d). Additionally, the extant pangolins have a large posterior frontal branch of the frontal diploic vein within a canal (Figure 3e–h), but these are absent in M. marshi (Figure 3a,d). We observe some variation between P. tricuspis, and S. gigantea in terms of the configuration of the frontal diploic vein. Indeed, in lateral view, the posterior frontal diploic vein of P. tricuspis turns ventrally at the level of the coronal gyrus and then connects to an anterior branch of the frontal diploic vein that drains through the postglenoid foramen (Figures 3e,f and 6c,g). Based on a large intraspecific sample (>10 specimens), Billet et al. (2026) found that P. tricuspis has a unique pattern of diploic veins within extant pangolins. The classical path that links the CEV and the transverse sinus is very reduced to absent and is replaced by a more extensive diploic network in the frontal area in P. tricuspis (also see Figure 3e–h). In S. gigantea, the posterior frontal diploic vein continues on the dorsal aspect of the cerebrum until reaching the level of the circular fissure where it turns ventrally and exits through the frontal diploic foramen (Figure 6d,h,l). We also note the presence of anterior branches of the frontal diploic vein in S. gigantea, which connect to the capsuloparietal emissary vein (Figures 3g,h and 6d,h). This connection is absent in P. tricuspis (Figure 3e,f). Finally, a ventral branch of the frontal diploic vein is present only in P. tricuspis (Figure 4c); it occupies a canal in the frontal, orbitosphenoid, and presphenoid and the left and right vessels communicate on the midline.

Narrow grooves for the internal carotid artery and one of its branches, the stapedial artery, are present on the promontorium of the petrosal in M. marshi (Gaudin et al., 2019). However, these arteries appear to leave no trace on the endocranial cavity. It is uncertain if the small stapedial artery is connected via a ramus superior to the ramus supraorbitalis anteriorly and into multiple rami temporales posteriorly as in other fossil laurasiatherians (see Bertrand, Jiménez Lao, et al., 2024). In extant pangolins, the internal carotid is small and the stapedial artery is absent at least in adult pangolins (Bugge, 1979; Gaudin et al., 2019; Guth, 1961; Wible, 1984, 1987). A small canal for the internal carotid artery, posterior to the mandibular nerve (V3), is visible in ventral view in P. tricuspis and S. gigantea (Figure 4c,d).

DISCUSSION

Endocranial anatomy and potential phylogenetic signals

Morphological features that might represent synapomorphies uniting Palaeanodonta and Pholidota have been challenging to establish because many morphological features are also adaptations to myrmecophagy and fossoriality, and thus potentially prone to convergence (Rose, 2006; Gaudin et al., 2019, 2020). As noted in the Introduction, Metacheiromys shares many features of the auditory region with xenarthrans, but also has a decent number of features in common with pangolins (Gaudin et al., 2019). In the Descriptions and comparisons section above, we only made comparisons with extant pangolins as we have followed the view that Palaeanodonta are likely stem pangolins (Gaudin et al., 2009; O'Leary et al., 2013). In the following, however, we include xenarthrans in our discussions.

Metacheiromys and extant pangolins have many endocranial dissimilarities including features associated with the olfactory bulbs and the cerebellum. Noticeable differences include the presence of the following features in Metacheiromys that are absent in pangolins: (1) superior olfactory sinus, (2) trace of the orbitotemporal vessels on the endocranial surface, (3) post‐temporal canal (present in xenarthrans; Le Verger et al., 2021), (4) trace of the superior sagittal sinus on the endocranial surface, (5) trace of the transverse sinus on the endocranial surface, (6) well‐developed SS, (7) condyloid canal, (8) occipital emissary vein, and (9) separate foramina for the exit of the internal jugular vein and for cranial nerves IX, X, and XI. We note that the subarcuate fossa is not absent in pangolins but is reduced, while it is more expanded in Metacheiromys. It is worth noting that the absence of venous grooves on the endocranial surface of extant pangolins was coded in the phylogenetic analyses of Gaudin and Wible (1999) and Gaudin et al. (2009). Other features present in the extant pangolins but absent in Metacheiromys include: (1) the network of diploic veins in the frontal and parietal (Billet et al., 2026), (2) supraoccipital veins, (3) the ossified tentorium, and (4) the capsuloparietal emissary vein and postglenoid foramen (Table 2). Additionally, the inferior petrosal sinus in Metacheiromys is hard to distinguish in comparison to extant pangolins. Other notable differences include the presence of a small neocortex, low number of sulci and gyri, and an exposed midbrain in Metacheiromys. Those last anatomical characteristics are likely related to the expansion of the neocortex in extant pangolins (see below).

Nevertheless, Metacheiromys and pangolins also share endocranial features that might not be plesiomorphic for all mammals and could potentially be synapomorphies. Among these features, we note two morphological characters: the presence of an orbital gyrus and a presylvian sulcus (Table 2). The orbital gyrus and presylvian sulcus should be considered as co‐occurring structures because the orbital gyrus is delimitated from the rest of the neocortex by the presylvian sulcus (Imam et al., 2017). Therefore, if the presylvian sulcus is absent, the orbital gyrus will be also absent. We checked the literature for the presence of this sulcus in Pan‐Carnivora and Xenarthra to deduce whether or not this sulcus could be a synapomorphy for Pholidotamorpha. The presylvian sulcus is not present in stem Carnivora, specifically in the Eocene Carnivoraformes ‘Miacidae’ Vulpavus palustris (Flink et al., 2021; Lyras et al., 2023; Radinsky, 1977). This sulcus is present in the Eocene stem Carnivoramorpha ‘Creodonta’ Hyaenodontoidea Cynohyaenodon cayluxi and Proviverra typica; however, these taxa are nested well within ‘Creodonta’ and the more basal Thinocyon velox does not have a presylvian sulcus (Dubied et al., 2019; Flink et al., 2021; Radinsky, 1977). The presence of a presylvian sulcus and the orbital gyrus in ‘Creodonta’ and in extant Carnivora for which these structures are well developed (Lyras & Van der Geer, 2003) might be derived, although more early diverging members of these groups would be needed to evaluate which condition is more likely to be plesiomorphic. Similarly, it is possible that the common ancestor of Carnivora and Pholidota did not have this pattern (i.e., orbital gyrus and presylvian sulcus), and instead that it evolved independently in both groups, but more basal taxa are required to test this hypothesis.

The extant xenarthran Cingulata (armadillos) Zaedyus pichiy, Chaetophractus villosus, and Dasypus novemcinctus also have a presylvian sulcus, but the orbital gyrus is only well developed in Z. pichiy based on the figures published by Tambusso and Fariña (2015) and Troyelli et al. (2023). However, the brain natural endocast of the Eocene xenarthran Cingulata Utaetus buccatus presents lateral sulci, but does not have a presylvian sulcus (Dozo, 1998). Regarding Folivora (sloths), the extant Choloepus and Bradypus and the late Pleistocene Glossotherium robustum and Catonyx tarijensis also present an orbital gyrus and presylvian sulcus, although the orbital gyrus is not very developed (Boscaini, Iurino, Mamani Quispe, et al., 2020; Boscaini, Iurino, Sardella, et al., 2020). Because of the lack of data for Eocene Folivora, the ancestral condition for Xenarthra cannot be resolved. It is possible that the presence of an orbital gyrus and presylvian sulcus also appeared convergently in Xenarthra, but more basal representatives will be required to determine the ancestral condition for the group.

As mentioned in the Results section, the Eocene pangolin Pat. americana was described as having a cerebral sulcus pattern similar to that of extant pangolins but no images or detailed comparisons were provided by Emry (1970) and the AMHN latex endocast of that specimen does not have sufficient details to make a conclusion. Based on this current understanding of the evolution of the presylvian sulcus, it is likely that early pangolins already had this sulcus and this would support the hypothesis that Metacheiromys, Epoicotherium, and Palaeanodonta more generally are the sister‐clade to Pholidota. It is worth noting that the presence of the orbital gyrus and presylvian sulcus represents very labile structures, with a high level of homoplasy as described above for xenarthrans. However, this is not surprising because brain structures might increase in size when used for a specific behaviour (Bertrand & Krubitzer, 2025). Therefore, we acknowledge the challenge in teasing apart phylogenetic signal from convergence and that the presence of these structures might have also been acquired independently in palaeanodonts and pangolins. Ultimately, more basal members of each group will be crucial to determine the ancestral condition of the orbital gyrus and presylvian sulcus in these various clades.

In the scenario in which palaeanodonts and pangolins are sister‐clades, it is possible that the tentorial process of the parietal either arose convergently in Carnivora and in Pholidota or that it was lost in Metacheiromys. The next step will be to CT scan the specimen of Pal. ignavus (AMNH 15086) to verify the presence and composition of a tentorial process. Another feature shared by both Carnivora and Pholidota is the presence of the dense network of diploic veins enclosed in canals, which is not present in Metacheiromys (Billet et al., 2026). This pattern could have also been acquired convergently in both clades as a course within a canal or within the endocranial cavity appears to also be fairly homoplastic as a course in a canal is present in humans (Rangel de Lázaro et al., 2016). Additionally, a large network of veins in canals is present in tillodonts (Bertrand, Jiménez Lao, et al., 2024). Alternatively, if these features (ossified tentorium and dense network of diploic veins within canals) are truly absent in palaeanodonts and can be considered synapomorphies of Carnivora and Pholidota, this would suggest that palaeanodonts might not be so closely related to pangolins.

Neurosensory ecology of pholidotamorpha

The auditory region and the postcrania have shown that Metacheiromys was likely fossorial and a myrmecophagous mammal like extant pangolins (Gaudin et al., 2019). The size of three brain regions was measured (i.e., olfactory bulbs, neocortex, and petrosal lobules) to study the behavioural ecology of Metacheiromys. We used previous research showing that the size of a brain structure is generally correlated with its function (Jerison, 1973). To study that relationship, we looked at the size of a structure in relation to both endocranial volume and body mass (for a review see Bertrand & Krubitzer, 2025).

Concerning the olfactory bulbs, they have a relatively larger size in comparison to both brain and body mass in Metacheiromys compared with extant pangolins. Therefore, Metacheiromys relied more heavily on its sense of smell than extant pangolins. Molecular studies have found that pangolins have a large number of olfactory receptor genes compared with other mammals, indicating that pangolins have a very good sense of smell (Choo et al., 2016; Li et al., 2024). However, what is important is the ratio between the number of active (functional) vs. inactive (pseudogenes) receptor genes (Imam et al., 2018). The number of functional or intact chemoreceptor genes has been quantified for Manis (Martinez et al., 2025). From this study, Manis has an average number of intact chemoreceptor genes and therefore average olfaction within mammals. Behavioural and anatomical studies have shown contrasting results. In terms of cortical regions, no specialization of the olfactory system in the brain of pangolins was found in comparison to laboratory rats (Imam et al., 2018). Based on behavioural observations, olfaction might be used as sniffing behaviour when foraging for food as in other mammals (Mohapatra & Panda, 2014), but pangolins do not perform well at ‘olfactory distance’ and appear to only use olfaction when following scent trails (DiPaola et al., 2020). Additionally, nasal turbinates are not necessarily better developed in myrmecophagous mammals compared with their close relatives (Wright et al., 2024). This mismatch between molecular and morphological data is not uncommon and the link between molecular data and function such as olfaction is rarely tested (except in model organisms) and therefore the results of such studies should be interpreted with caution (Yohe & Krell, 2023). For example, in Yinpterochiroptera, some bats show very specialized olfactory systems, but most of the olfactory genes are lost (Yohe & Krell, 2023). This demonstrates the need to more systematically combine behavioural, anatomical, and genetic data to more fully understand the evolution of senses such as olfaction. It is possible that lower selective pressures in olfaction in the evolution of pangolins led to an expansion of the number of pseudogenes, as has occurred in primates (Niimura et al., 2018).

The orbital gyrus of the neocortex is relatively well developed in Metacheiromys and extant pangolins (Imam et al., 2017). This gyrus is part of the orbital cortex, which is located in the frontal lobe, also known as the orbitofrontal cortex in primates and rodents (Duvernoy, 2005; Hoover & Vertes, 2011; Price, 2007). The orbital cortex is also presumably present in pangolins (Imam et al., 2017) and lipotyphlans (Igado et al., 2021). Because different cortical areas are connected to achieve specific functions, the use of networks rather than cortex as a whole has been preferred to determine brain functions. Therefore, the orbital cortex is not the same as the orbital network and indeed the orbital network does not include the entire orbital cortex (Carmichael & Price, 1996; Hoover & Vertes, 2011; Price, 2007). Regarding the associated functions of the orbital network, it may be involved in assessing sensory objects such as food items and especially flavour, appearance, and texture (Price, 2007; Rolls, 2005). Based on behavioural studies and stomach content analyses, pangolins are myrmecophagous mammals that are highly selective in terms of preferred prey (e.g., Karawita et al., 2020; Pietersen et al., 2016; Tamang et al., 2022). Based on the previous section, the senses used for this selection might not heavily favour olfaction. Myrmecophagous mammals have a specialized tongue and pangolins specifically have two relatively well innervated tactile organs on the tip of the tongue (Cheng, 1986; Doran & Allbrook, 1973; Kubota et al., 1962; Reiss, 2000). Pangolins also appear to have some taste buds but they are restricted to the lateral epithelium of each papilla (e.g., Casali et al., 2017; Doran & Allbrook, 1973; Kubota et al., 1962).

No stimulation studies appear to have been conducted on pangolins (Imam et al., 2018). Stimulations in the primary motor and somatosensory cortices located in the parietal lobe lead to tongue movements in bats, primates (Halley et al., 2022, 2025), and rodents (Pineda et al., 2025). This information is then sent to the orbitofrontal cortex to be processed, which then guides feeding behaviour through a reward system (Rolls, 2021). Therefore, a potential interpretation of the presence of a larger orbital gyrus could be that it is related to a specific food selection behaviour via the use of a specialized tongue that is needed for survival. This particular behaviour might have already been present in Metacheiromys more than 50 million years ago. This behaviour would coincide with the emergence of other morphological adaptations to myrmecophagy that are present in Metacheiromys as this species likely had a protrusile tongue also present in extant pangolins based on osteological correlates (Rose, 1978). The giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla) also has a protrusile tongue (Gaudin et al., 2018) and its brain shows a relatively well‐developed orbital gyrus (see the Comparative Mammalian Brain Collections; Welker et al., 2001). Two other myrmecophagous mammals that also have a protrusile tongue (Reiss, 2000), the armadillos Z. pichiy and D. novemcinctus, have an orbital gyrus, but it is less developed (see Tambusso & Fariña, 2015; Troyelli et al., 2023).

The petrosal lobules have a role in controlling and maintaining eye movements during locomotion (Rambold et al., 2002) and they occupy the subarcuate fossa in mammals (e.g., Bertrand et al., 2020; Lang et al., 2022). These structures are relatively small in Metacheiromys and extremely reduced in pangolins, which might be related to their fossorial adaptations or more precisely to their slower mode of locomotion in comparison to more active mammals (Bertrand et al., 2021; Bertrand, Lang, et al., 2024; Goyens et al., 2022; Lang et al., 2022). The subarcuate fossa is also weakly developed in extant xenarthrans (Troyelli et al., 2023; Wible, 2010), which might be associated with this convergent lifestyle of fossoriality and myrmecophagy, suggesting a low reliance on this function to navigate their surroundings. However, there is an exception with the anteater Tamandua tetradactyla (UCMP 117518; Morphosource Media ID: 000766298), which shows relatively developed petrosal lobules. However, its close living relatives Cyclopes didactylus (PSU ANTH301.1; Morphosource Media ID: 000066622) and Myrmecophaga tridactyla (AMNH 133489; Morphosource Media ID: 000085043) have less developed subarcuate fossae. Additionally, the fossil anteater Protamandua was scored as having a deep and large subarcuate fossa (Gaudin & Branham, 1998), but no CT data or images are available for this specimen. It is important to note that the reduction to absence of a subarcuate fossa could also be related to a packing‐problem of fitting the brain into the cranium. Therefore, the function of the petrosal lobule might still be present in the non‐lobule part of the cerebellum (see Bertrand et al., 2021; Troyelli et al., 2023). Additionally, the inner ear semicircular canals might influence the size of the subarcuate fossa (Billet et al., 2015). However, we note that in squirrels, the petrosal lobules (=paraflocculus in Bertrand & Silcox, 2016: figure 7) grow beyond the canals as the latter leave marks on the surface of the subarcuate fossa.

Finally, the neocortex in Metacheiromys is relatively smaller than in pangolins and is very close in size to another potential fossorial mammal from the late Eocene: Anagale (López‐Torres et al., 2023). An increase in the neocortical surface and in the number of sulci has occurred through time in mammals (e.g., Bertrand et al., 2022; Jerison, 2012), which is also likely what occurred in the lineage leading to pangolins. However, the neocortex looks relatively smaller when compared with body mass in S. gigantea but larger in P. tricupsis in comparison to Metacheiromys. This result could suggest that increase in complexity of the neocortex occurred before the increase in body mass in S. gigantea. The EQ is slightly lower in extant pangolins than in Metacheiromys. In fossorial rodents, Aplodontiidae, there is a general decrease through time in all of the structures (i.e., petrosal lobules, olfactory bulbs, neocortex) and in the EQ in comparison to squirrels (Bertrand et al., 2018; Bertrand et al., 2021). In fossorial Euarchontoglires, the petrosal lobules are also significantly smaller compared with other locomotor categories of the same clade (Lang et al., 2022). Other arboreal rodents that move carefully through branches also appear to have relatively smaller petrosal lobules, olfactory bulbs, and neocortex (Bertrand, Lang, et al., 2024; Fernández Villoldo et al., 2023; Vincent et al., 2025). The fact that extant pangolins have a smaller relative brain size in comparison to their purported sister group, Palaeanodonta, and more specifically Metacheiromys is especially interesting, because usually an increase in neocortical size leads to an increase in relative brain size, even if we consider that Metacheiromys has a derived condition in comparison to more basal palaeanodonts. One possibility that will need to be tested with additional fossils is that a second increase in body mass might have occurred after the increase of the neocortex. Indeed, there is some evidence showing that Pleistocene fossils belonging to S. gigantea tend to be smaller than extant forms (Botha & Gaudin, 2007; Terhune et al., 2021). Therefore, possible interpretation of this result would be that specialization to fossoriality and/or slow arboreal locomotion (Gaubert et al., 2020; Gaudin et al., 2016) led to a decrease in several brain structures (olfaction and eye movement control). The reason for the increase in the neocortex in extant species (before the increase in body mass in S. gigantea) might be related to ecological circumstances during the Eocene when crown placental clades such as pangolins emerged. All crown clades taxa consistently show relatively larger neocortices than their respective stem relatives (Bertrand et al., 2022) and pangolins are no exception. The neocortex likely plays a major role in the survival of pangolins today. In the future, it will be important to evaluate the brain endocasts of fossil pangolins to investigate when the increase of the neocortex and the potential decrease of both the olfactory bulb and the petrosal lobule size started in Pholidota, and how this related to body mass.

CONCLUSION

We described the first brain virtual endocasts of palaeanodonts, which have been considered the sister group to Pholidota (Gaudin et al., 2009). Because of shared morphological similarities related to myrmecophagy and fossoriality with both Pholidota and Xenarthra, it has been challenging to tease apart synapomorphies from convergent traits when evaluating palaeanodont relationships (Gaudin et al., 2019, 2020; Rose, 2006). Metacheiromys and pangolins exhibit many differences in their endocranial morphology including features related to the olfactory bulbs, the cerebellum, the venous and arterial systems, which suggest some phylogenetic distance between Metacheiromys and pholidotans, as palaeanodonts lack features that are present in extinct and extant pangolins. However, they display a similarity that could be viewed as a synapomorphy of Pholidotamorpha, with the presence of an orbital gyrus and a presylvian sulcus. Based on the fossil record, the evolution of these features in derived xenarthrans may be convergent.

We find that the size of the olfactory bulbs (olfaction) and the petrosal lobules (eye movement control) is smaller in pangolins compared with Metacheiromys, which aligns with trends recognized in aplodontiid rodents (Bertrand et al., 2018, 2021). The lack of petrosal lobules in most xenarthrans and pangolins is likely convergent as Metacheiromys had petrosal lobules. Overall, this suggests a lower need to maintain head and eye movement control during locomotion in animals engaging with a relatively slow‐moving lifestyle such as fossoriality or carefully navigating from one tree branch to another. Regarding olfaction, despite some genetic data claiming that pangolins have an exceptional sense of smell (Choo et al., 2016; Li et al., 2024), our results support behavioural, anatomical, and quantitative and new genetic data that pangolins might not be relying on olfaction as much as previously thought (DiPaola et al., 2020; Imam et al., 2018; Martinez et al., 2025; Wright et al., 2024). The possible mismatch between these types of data could be the result of an expansion of the number of pseudogenes related to olfaction, as found for primates (Niimura et al., 2018). Probably the most interesting finding is the presence of the orbital gyrus in Metacheiromys, which is also well developed in pangolins and in the giant anteater. We hypothesize that the orbital gyrus might have played a major role in specialization for myrmecophagy and might be linked to the use of a protrusile tongue, as all of these taxa have this specialized tongue (Gaudin et al., 2018; Rose, 1978).

The relatively smaller brain of pangolins compared with Metacheiromys might be related to the smaller size of the olfactory bulbs and of the petrosal lobules. However, the neocortex takes up a larger proportion of the brain in pangolins compared with Metacheiromys. Therefore, we hypothesize that after the increase in the neocortex in the evolution of pangolins, an increase in body mass occurred, which led to a decrease in relative brain size. Brain endocasts of fossil pangolins will be needed to verify whether this was the case. Overall, the study of the brain virtual endocast of Metacheiromys provides significant new information in relation to its phylogenetic position and to the evolution of behavioural adaptations of pangolins and other myrmecophagous mammals.
