Malignant middle cerebral artery infarction revealing atrial fibrillation: A Multimodal imaging case of M1 occlusion treated with decompressive craniectomy
Authors: Hafsa Erregui, Mehdi Moujahid, Hamza Madouim, Abdelhafid Houba, Nawfal Doghmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarction represents a severe form of ischemic stroke characterized by extensive cerebral ischemia, rapid development of cerebral edema, and a high risk of fatal brain herniation. Atrial fibrillation is a major cause of cardioembolic stroke and is frequently associated with large vessel occlusion and poor outcomes. We report the case of a 70-year-old woman with poorly controlled hypertension who presented with acute left-sided hemiplegia and altered consciousness with a delay of approximately 48 hours from symptom onset. Electrocardiography revealed previously undiagnosed atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. Initial non-contrast brain computed tomography demonstrated early ischemic changes involving the right MCA territory with signs of mass effect. Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a large cortico-subcortical infarction involving more than 50% of the right MCA territory, with marked diffusion restriction and no diffusion–FLAIR mismatch, indicating a completed infarction. MR angiography demonstrated occlusion of the M1 segment of the right MCA. Imaging also revealed significant cerebral edema with a midline shift of 12 mm and early signs of subfalcine herniation. Despite intensive care management and urgent decompressive hemicraniectomy, the patient showed no neurological improvement and had a fatal outcome. This case highlights the crucial role of multimodal imaging in the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of malignant MCA infarction. Large vessel occlusion, extensive infarct volume, and significant mass effect are key imaging features associated with poor outcome. Early detection and appropriate anticoagulation of atrial fibrillation remain essential to prevent severe cardioembolic stroke.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Ischemic stroke, Malignant MCA infarction, MRI, CT, MR angiography

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia and a well-established risk factor for ischemic stroke, increasing the risk by approximately 5-fold [1]. Cardioembolic strokes related to AF are typically more severe, frequently associated with large vessel occlusion, particularly involving the middle cerebral artery (MCA), and result in larger infarct volumes and worse clinical outcomes [2].

Malignant MCA infarction represents the most severe form of ischemic stroke, characterized by infarction involving more than 50% of the MCA territory, rapid development of cerebral edema, and a high risk of increased intracranial pressure and brain herniation [3]. Early identification of these cases is crucial, as prognosis is closely related to infarct size, presence of large vessel occlusion, and the extent of mass effect.

Multimodal neuroimaging, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and MR angiography, plays a central role in the diagnosis, assessment of infarct extent, and identification of vascular occlusion, providing essential information for both therapeutic decision-making and prognostic evaluation.

In this report, we present a case of malignant MCA infarction revealing previously undiagnosed atrial fibrillation, highlighting the key imaging features and their prognostic implications.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old woman with a history of poorly controlled hypertension and no previously documented atrial fibrillation presented to the emergency department with acute onset of left-sided hemiplegia, dysarthria, and altered level of consciousness, with a prolonged delay of approximately 48 hours from symptom onset, likely related to living alone and delayed presentation to medical care.

On admission, the patient was initially conscious but rapidly deteriorated. Vital signs showed a blood pressure of 160/90 mmHg and an irregular tachycardia with a heart rate of approximately 140 beats per minute. Neurological examination revealed dense left hemiplegia (Medical Research Council score 0/5), left central facial palsy, and a positive Babinski sign, with an initial National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 14. Within a few hours, the patient’s neurological status worsened significantly, with a decline in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score from 15/15 to 8/15, indicating severe neurological impairment.

Electrocardiography demonstrated atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, characterized by an irregularly irregular rhythm and absence of distinct P waves (Fig. 1). No prior history of anticoagulation was reported.

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating atrial fibrillation (∼ 140 bpm), characterized by irregularly irregular RR intervals and absence of distinct P waves, with secondary ST–T changes.

Bedside transthoracic echocardiography was performed during hospitalization and demonstrated no major structural cardiac abnormalities apart from moderate left atrial dilatation. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved, and no visible intracardiac thrombus was identified on transthoracic imaging. However, transesophageal echocardiography, which is more sensitive for the detection of left atrial appendage thrombus in cardioembolic stroke, could not be performed because of the patient’s rapidly deteriorating neurological condition, severe intracranial hypertension, and the need for urgent neurosurgical management and mechanical ventilation.

Laboratory investigations were unremarkable, including normal coagulation parameters. A mild normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 11.9 g/dL) was noted.

Initial non-contrast brain computed tomography, performed approximately 30 minutes after admission, demonstrated early ischemic changes involving the right middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory, including loss of gray–white matter differentiation, cortical hypoattenuation, and involvement of deep structures (basal ganglia and insular region). These findings were associated with early mass effect, including effacement of cortical sulci and compression of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle (Fig. 2).

Axial non-contrast brain CT demonstrating early signs of acute ischemia involving the right cerebral hemisphere in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory. Red arrow: indicates loss of gray–white matter differentiation at the cortical level, consistent with early cytotoxic edema. Yellow arrow: indicates hypoattenuation of deep structures (basal ganglia and insular region), suggesting early infarction in the deep MCA territory.

Given the severity of the clinical presentation, multimodal magnetic resonance imaging was performed approximately 1 hour after admission. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) revealed a large cortico-subcortical area of marked hyperintensity involving more than 50% of the right MCA territory, consistent with extensive acute ischemia. The corresponding hypointensity on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map confirmed true diffusion restriction. Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences demonstrated hyperintense signal abnormalities without diffusion–FLAIR mismatch, indicating a completed infarction with no salvageable penumbra (Fig. 3).

Yellow arrows: indicate deep structure involvement (likely basal ganglia/internal capsule region), suggesting extension to perforating artery territories.

Given the prolonged delay from symptom onset (approximately 48 hours), the absence of diffusion–FLAIR mismatch, and the presence of a completed infarction with no salvageable penumbra, the patient was considered ineligible for reperfusion therapy, including intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, according to current stroke management guidelines.

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) demonstrated asymmetry of the middle cerebral arteries, with markedly reduced visualization of the right MCA compared to the contralateral side, consistent with a large vessel occlusion at the M1 segment of the right middle cerebral artery (Fig. 4).

Further imaging findings revealed severe cerebral edema with significant mass effect, including compression of the right lateral ventricle, effacement of cortical sulci, and a midline shift of approximately 12 mm, associated with early signs of subfalcine herniation (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Based on the clinical presentation and imaging findings, the diagnosis of malignant right MCA infarction secondary to cardioembolism from atrial fibrillation was established.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit approximately 1 hour after presentation for continuous monitoring and advanced supportive management. Initial treatment focused on hemodynamic stabilization and pharmacological rate control of atrial fibrillation with amiodarone, while electrolyte levels remained within normal limits. Despite these measures, neurological deterioration persisted, with the development of signs of intracranial hypertension.

In view of progressive clinical and radiological deterioration, an urgent decompressive hemicraniectomy was performed approximately 8 hours after admission. The surgical procedure lasted approximately 2 hours. The perioperative course was complicated by persistent atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (up to 160 bpm), despite ongoing medical management, requiring continued rate control with amiodarone.

Postoperatively, the patient remained comatose and required mechanical ventilation. Post-operative brain imaging was not available for inclusion because of the rapidly unfavorable clinical evolution and the patient’s critical condition. Despite maximal supportive care, including ventilatory and hemodynamic support, no neurological recovery was achieved. The clinical course was characterized by sustained intracranial hypertension and progressive neurological deterioration.

Discussion

Malignant middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarction represents one of the most severe forms of acute ischemic stroke and is frequently associated with large vessel occlusion and poor neurological outcomes. In the present case, previously undiagnosed atrial fibrillation led to a cardioembolic occlusion of the M1 segment of the right MCA, resulting in extensive territorial infarction, malignant cerebral edema, and fatal neurological deterioration despite aggressive management.

Atrial fibrillation is a major cause of cardioembolic stroke and is strongly associated with proximal large vessel occlusion, particularly involving the MCA territory. Cardioembolic strokes are often characterized by larger infarct volumes, rapid neurological deterioration, and increased mortality compared with other ischemic stroke subtypes. Recent European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines emphasize the importance of early detection and anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation to reduce the risk of disabling cardioembolic stroke [1,2].

Prior to the index event, the patient had a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 3, based on age between 65 and 74 years (+1), hypertension (+1), and female sex (+1), indicating a clear indication for long-term anticoagulation. The absence of anticoagulation likely contributed to the occurrence of this severe cardioembolic event.

From a radiological perspective, early recognition of malignant infarction is essential because prognosis is closely related to infarct size, vascular occlusion, and the severity of cerebral edema. Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) remains the first-line imaging modality in acute stroke and may demonstrate early ischemic changes, including loss of gray–white matter differentiation, cortical hypoattenuation, and involvement of deep gray structures such as the basal ganglia and insular cortex [3]. Although sometimes subtle, these findings may predict malignant evolution and poor neurological outcome.

Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides a more accurate assessment of infarct extent and tissue viability. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) allows early detection of cytotoxic edema, while corresponding hypointensity on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map confirms true diffusion restriction. In the present case, the absence of diffusion–FLAIR mismatch suggested a completed infarction with no salvageable penumbra, reflecting delayed presentation and advanced ischemic injury [4]. A positive diffusion–FLAIR mismatch could have suggested the presence of viable ischemic tissue and potential eligibility for imaging-guided reperfusion therapy in a wake-up or delayed-presenting stroke.

Mechanical thrombectomy was not performed because the patient presented approximately 48 hours after symptom onset, far beyond the currently recommended therapeutic window for endovascular reperfusion therapy. In addition, multimodal MRI demonstrated a completed infarction with no evidence of salvageable penumbra. According to current AHA/ASA recommendations, the absence of viable ischemic tissue markedly limits the expected benefit of reperfusion strategies in large vessel occlusion stroke [5].

MR angiography (MRA) plays a key role in the identification of intracranial large vessel occlusion and may demonstrate abrupt arterial cutoff or markedly reduced visualization of the affected vessel. In this patient, MRA demonstrated severe asymmetry between the middle cerebral arteries, with markedly reduced visualization of the right MCA, consistent with M1 segment occlusion explaining the extensive territorial infarction [6].

Malignant MCA infarction is generally defined by involvement of more than 50% of the MCA territory and is associated with rapid progression of cerebral edema, intracranial hypertension, and brain herniation [3]. Imaging findings such as ventricular compression, sulcal effacement, and midline shift greater than 10 mm are strongly associated with poor prognosis and high mortality [7]. In the present case, the presence of a 12 mm midline shift associated with early subfalcine herniation reflected advanced malignant evolution and severe intracranial hypertension.

Although decompressive hemicraniectomy has been shown to reduce mortality in selected patients with malignant MCA infarction, functional outcome remains highly dependent on factors such as age, timing of intervention, infarct volume, and preoperative neurological status [8]. In this patient, delayed presentation and extensive completed infarction were associated with an unfavorable outcome despite urgent surgical decompression and intensive care management.

Overall, this case highlights the crucial role of multimodal imaging in confirming diagnosis, identifying large vessel occlusion, evaluating infarct extent, and assessing prognosis in malignant MCA infarction. Beyond diagnosis, multimodal imaging is essential for therapeutic decision-making, particularly in determining eligibility for reperfusion therapy. Finally, this report underscores the importance of early detection and appropriate anticoagulation of atrial fibrillation to prevent severe cardioembolic stroke and catastrophic neurological complications.

Conclusion

Malignant middle cerebral artery infarction represents a life-threatening neurological emergency characterized by rapid progression, extensive cerebral injury, and poor prognosis despite aggressive management. This case highlights the central role of multimodal imaging in establishing the diagnosis, identifying large vessel occlusion, and assessing disease severity.

Key imaging features, including extensive infarction, significant mass effect, and midline shift, are strong predictors of unfavorable outcome. In this context, delayed presentation and absence of early reperfusion therapy markedly limit therapeutic options and contribute to poor prognosis.

Ultimately, this case emphasizes that early detection and appropriate management of atrial fibrillation remain essential to prevent severe cardioembolic stroke. Multimodal imaging not only guides diagnosis but also plays a crucial role in prognostic evaluation and clinical decision-making.

This case underscores the importance of early recognition, prompt imaging evaluation, and timely therapeutic intervention to improve outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke.
