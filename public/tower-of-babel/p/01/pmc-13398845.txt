Production of Functional Trastuzumab in Phaeodactylum tricornutum: Advancing Microalgae as Next‐Generation Sustainable Antibody Biofactories
Authors: Charlotte Toustou, Manon Salvetti, Magalie Bénard, Christelle Fécourt, Sébastien Calbo, Kylian Jouie, France Blanchard, Camille N'Zué, Chloé Placines, Thomas Badou, Nicolas Piton, Patrice Lerouge, Ludovic Galas, Muriel Bardor
Published: 2026

The global demand for monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) is expanding at an unprecedented pace driven by their central role for the treatment of cancers, infectious and autoimmune diseases as well as their use in diagnostics. Access to these mAbs remains drastically uneven worldwide. Fewer than 5% of people can globally benefit from mAb‐based therapies, and nearly half of the world's population lacks access to these diagnostic tools (Fleming et al. 2021). A major factor underlying this disparity is the high production cost of mAbs, which are predominantly manufactured in mammalian cells using Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells. The Gates Foundation has underscored that alleviating this global health inequity will require dramatic reductions in production costs. Therefore, identifying alternative low‐cost expression systems represents a major challenge.

Unicellular eukaryotic microalgae recently emerged as a promising platform for mAbs affordable biomanufacturing (Hempel et al. 2011, 2017; Hempel and Maier 2012; Vanier et al. 2015, 2018). Microalgae offer a unique combination of advantages: they grow on inexpensive media, are photoautotrophic and do not contain human pathogens (Banerjee and Ward 2022). They naturally secrete very low levels of proteins into the culture medium (Chuberre et al. 2022), simplifying mAbs downstream processing and quality control. Importantly, while reaching growth rates comparable to bacteria, microalgae possess the eukaryotic machinery required for protein N‐glycosylation, essential for proper antibody folding, assembly and functionality (Vanier et al. 2015). At industrial scale for the same productivity, the mAb production in microalgae will be 10 times cheaper than in CHO cells (personal data).

Trastuzumab is a clinically validated CHO‐produced mAb, approved for the treatment of HER2‐positive breast cancer. It binds the Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2), which is overexpressed in aggressive breast and stomach tumours. The global market for HER2‐targeted diagnostic tests was valued at USD 166 million in 2025 and is projected to exceed USD 387 million by 2037 (CAGR + 7%). These trends reflect a growing demand for HER2‐directed antibodies. Thus, developing a cost‐effective production platform for Trastuzumab could meet the demands.

Here, we report the production of functional Trastuzumab in the diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum and demonstrate that microalgae‐derived Trastuzumab exhibits functional bioequivalence to the commercially available CHO cells‐produced antibody, supporting its use for research and diagnostic applications.

Trastuzumab was expressed in P. tricornutum and accumulated in the culture medium, from which it was purified by protein A affinity chromatography allowing recovery of several mg/L of purified antibody. SDS‐PAGE analysis, under reducing (+) and non‐reducing (−) conditions, confirmed that P. tricornutum secretes a fully assembled H2L2 Trastuzumab (Figure 1A).

The selective specificity of the microalgae‐derived Trastuzumab toward HER2 was first confirmed by Western blot analysis performed on a recombinant HER2 protein and proteins extracted from the HER2‐overexpressing human breast cancer cell line SK‐BR3 (Figure 1B), similarly to its CHO‐derived counterpart (Figure S1). Binding affinity was then evaluated by ELISA (Figure 1C) and by Fluorescence‐Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) (Figure 1D). In both assays, the microalgae‐derived Trastuzumab displayed binding profiles comparable to those of CHO‐produced Trastuzumab. Serial dilution curves revealed identical dose–response patterns in FACS for both antibodies (Figure S2). Finally, the microalgae‐based Trastuzumab was able to recognise specifically HER2 3‐positive breast and stomach cancer tissues of human donors in immunohistochemical experiments (Figure 1E,F; Figure S3).

In conclusion, the microalgae‐derived Trastuzumab displays a specificity and binding affinity fully comparable to commercial CHO‐derived Trastuzumab. While Trastuzumab was used here as a case study to assess both activity for diagnostic and research applications, our findings support the broad potential of microalgae as a versatile, sustainable, scalable and low‐cost biofactories for next‐generation biologics.

If microalgae‐derived Trastuzumab is to be developed as Biologics for clinical applications, future studies will need to assess its in vivo bioequivalence relative to CHO‐derived Trastuzumab as well as its safety and tolerability. In parallel, further efforts will be required to increase production yields and scale up the manufacturing process to industrial levels. The successful large‐scale production of mAbs in P. tricornutum will also depend on a better understanding of its interactions with associated bacterial communities. Indeed, P. tricornutum naturally establishes associations with marine bacteria that contribute to nutrient recycling, iron acquisition (Amin et al. 2012) and positively influence culture stability and metabolic performance (Moejes et al. 2017). Such interactions may represent an advantage for large‐scale cultivation by alleviating some of the constraints associated with maintaining axenic cultures. However, in the context of biologics production, associated bacterial populations may also introduce process variability. Therefore, future research should manage the microbial environment of P. tricornutum cultures to ensure product consistency, process robustness and regulatory compliance.
