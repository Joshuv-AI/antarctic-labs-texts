Lock-Line Entrapment in Mitral TEER
Authors: Masataka Arakawa, Akihisa Kataoka, Yusuke Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: complication, mitral valve, MR sequences, postoperative

Mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair is now an established therapy for mitral regurgitation, supported by guideline recommendations and randomized clinical trials.1,2 Periprocedural complications are infrequent, and device embolization is rare, reported at approximately 0.1% to 0.3% of cases.3, 4, 5 Although uncommon, embolization is a clinically consequential event and may necessitate urgent surgical or percutaneous rescue. Even with newer-generation devices, mechanical complications can still occur, and lock-line failure requires prompt and careful management when encountered.6

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Kumi et al5 describe an unusual sequence culminating in MitraClip embolization into the right lower pulmonary vein after a lock-line problem during the release process. After placement of a second XTW MitraClip (Abbott Vascular) that was locked securely, the lock line became knotted deep within the delivery system while they were being pulled out and could not be removed. The team proceeded with clip release and then applied gentle traction to unknot the threads. This maneuver unlocked the MitraClip, leaving it loosely attached via the grippers. Carried by the regurgitant jet, the clip briefly migrated into the steerable guide catheter before embolizing into the right lower pulmonary vein. The authors successfully retrieved the embolized clip percutaneously by advancing a gooseneck snare through a long multipurpose catheter inside the steerable guide catheter to achieve stable engagement at the clip neck.5

Rare but Consequential Complications in Mitral Transcatheter Edge-To-Edge Repair

Lock-line removal is usually uneventful. This case illustrates that resistance during lock-line removal should never be ignored. When unexpected resistance is encountered, operators should pause and reassess rather than applying further traction, because excessive force may destabilize the clip by releasing the locking mechanism.

Lock-Line Entrapment: Mechanism, Prevention, and Bailout

The mechanism is illustrated in Figure 1. In the locked state (Figure 1A), the lock lines maintain clip stability. Transition to an unlocked state (Figure 1B) occurs when the lock lines are pulled proximally, allowing the harness to shift outward and the lock to release. In this context, traction applied to a stuck lock line can unintentionally place the clip in an unlocked configuration and predispose to clip opening and loss of secure leaflet attachment, consistent with the sequence described in the present case report.5

Lock-Line Entrapment During Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair: Mechanism and Stepwise Bailout Strategy

(A) Locked configuration. The clip is in the locked state, with the lock line maintaining the locking mechanism. (B) Unlock transition. Pulling the lock line proximally (toward the operator) shifts the harness outward (yellow arrow), resulting in clip unlocking. (C) Prewithdrawal inspection. After clip placement, a knot is identified on the lock line (yellow arrow). Before any withdrawal, confirm the absence of knotting and fraying; if present, address the abnormality before proceeding. (D) Bailout using the contralateral side. When entrapment is suspected and the contralateral lock-line limb remains external/accessible, gentle traction from the opposite side may resolve the issue (yellow arrow). (E) State of contralateral lock-line retraction. When the contralateral limb retracts into the CDS, bailout becomes more complex; proceed with the standard release sequence, including removal of the release pin. (F) State after clip release. After release, the clip and CDS may remain connected only by the entrapped lock line, creating a nonstandard and potentially unstable configuration. G) M-knob release. Releasing the ‘M’ knob improves CDSSGC coaxiality, but may increase lock-line tension and traction on the clip; proceed with caution (yellow arrow). (H) Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)–guided control of coaxiality and lock-line tension. Under continuous TEE guidance, use steering adjustments to restore and maintain coaxial alignment and minimize lock-line tension while advancing the delivery system toward the clip. (I) CDS extraction. Slow CDS extraction can allow complete disengagement of the lock line from the clip. (J) Final withdrawal and air management. After CDS removal, withdraw the remaining lock line carefully, with attention to avoiding air entrainment. AML = anterior mitral leaflet; CDS = clip delivery system; PML = posterior mitral leaflet; SGC = steerable guide catheter.

A common pathway to entrapment is knot formation during the transition from clip implantation to the release process (Figure 1C). Therefore, prevention begins with careful inspection before withdrawing any lock line. Operators should confirm that no knot formation or thread fraying is present. If a knot is identified at this stage, operators may consider withdrawing the lock line from the knotted side or cutting the knotted segment instead of pulling from the opposite side.

If a knot is not recognized and the nonknotted side is pulled, the knot may become stuck within the clip delivery system (CDS). At the moment lock-line entrapment is recognized, the most important action is to avoid forceful traction. If the contralateral lock-line end remains outside the CDS, withdrawing the contralateral side may resolve the problem (Figure 1D). In contrast, if the contralateral end retracts into the CDS and both ends become inaccessible (Figure 1E), management becomes more challenging.

A step-by-step bailout strategy for knot-related lock-line entrapment has been reported.7 At this stage, direct lock-line removal is no longer feasible. The operator should proceed with the standard clip release sequence: the release pin is removed (Figure 1E), releasing the clip from the CDS. After release, the clip and CDS are connected only by the entrapped lock lines (Figure 1F), creating an unusual and potentially unstable configuration.

Disengagement is achieved by carefully withdrawing the clip delivery system under real-time transesophageal echocardiographic guidance while maintaining coaxial alignment (Figure 1H). The system should be withdrawn slowly until the lock lines disengage from the clip (Figure 1I). The remaining lock lines are then removed carefully, with attention to avoiding air entry (Figure 1J).

Operators should be familiar with the mechanism of lock-line entrapment and prepared to manage it appropriately.
