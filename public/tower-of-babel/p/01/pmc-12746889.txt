Immunotherapy and ovarian function: bridging reproductive health in oncology clinical trials
Authors: Sarah C. Cromack, Kara N. Goldman
Published: 2025

Immunomodulatory therapeutics, which engage the power of one’s natural immune response to destroy malignant neoplastic cells, represent a significant innovation in the treatment of many cancers. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as particularly attractive agents in oncology care. Immune checkpoint inhibitors act by blocking cellular checkpoint proteins expressed by T cells, which are normally held in the inactive state by binding to complementary proteins within the tumor. Immune checkpoint inhibitors disrupt this binding, thus removing the blockade on activation and permitting T-cell destruction of malignant cells. In the last 15 years, the US Food and Drug Administration has approved multiple ICIs to treat a number of cancers affecting reproductive-aged women, including melanoma, breast cancer, and Hodgkin lymphoma (1).

Both the American Society for Reproductive Medicine and the American Society of Clinical Oncology advise physicians to counsel reproductive-aged patients on the possible reproductive risks of cancer therapeutics before treatment. However, this is difficult in the setting of immunotherapy, wherein only a mere handful of human studies focus on their possible gonadotoxic effects (2, 3). Immune checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy broadly enhances immune activation, which can lead to immune-related adverse reproductive events involving the pituitary, hypothalamus, ovary, and uterus, and ovarian dysfunction and adverse pregnancy outcomes have, in fact, been reported in animal studies (3).

Rhodes et al. (4) sought to address this critical lack of knowledge surrounding reproductive outcomes after ICI therapy, reporting on reproductive outcomes of 49 women aged 18–40 years using systemic ICI as the first and only therapy for advanced-stage melanoma. The study assessed reproductive outcomes through menstrual cycle, ovarian reserve, and pregnancy data after exposure to ICI therapy (either ipilimumab, nivolumab, or pembrolizumab). Among the women not using hormonal contraception (n = 24), only 1 had amenorrhea because of pituitary dysfunction. Although antimüllerian hormone was infrequently checked in relation to treatment (n = 5; mean, 3.23 mIU; range, 0.5–9.86 mIU), more patients had their follicle-stimulating hormone assessed, (n = 22; mean, 4.53 IU; range, 0.30–7.90 IU). None of the ovarian reserve values were concerning for primary ovarian insufficiency. These data, although limited, were reassuring for this particular patient population but warrant wider investigation. Eight patients had a total of 9 pregnancies during (n = 3) and after (n = 6) ICI therapy: 3 spontaneous abortions; 1 elective abortion (because of need to restart ICI); and 5 term deliveries.

Despite the American Society of Clinical Oncology and American Society for Reproductive Medicine recommendations advising fertility preservation counseling, only 5 patients (11%) in this ICI cohort underwent a formal consultation with a reproductive endocrinologist to discuss possible effects of ICI exposure. The results do not detail if any patients underwent oocyte or embryo cryopreservation before immunotherapy. It is relevant to note that the study population represents only a small subset of women treated at the center for melanoma (only 10% of all patients exposed to ICI therapy). More than half of patients receiving an ICI were removed from the study because of exposure to additional treatments; however, it would be important in future studies to review those treated with both chemotherapy and immunotherapy to evaluate for potential synergistic gonadotoxic effects. Moreover, as the investigators point out, patients with melanoma specifically are older at the time of diagnosis than other cancers treated with immunotherapy, such as Hodgkin lymphoma. Future studies focusing on this cancer population specifically are critical because they will likely offer more robust data on reproductive effects of immunotherapy agents.

All retrospective studies in the realm of oncofertility are subject to the limitation of pregnancy intent, for example, whether patients were interested in conceiving at the time of or after cancer treatment. Most patients in the study were actively using contraception (96%), likely at the recommendation of their oncology team, with typical counseling for patients to avoid pregnancy for a minimum of 2 years after treatment. Moreover, many women in the study may have already completed childbearing (47% had children at the time of their cancer diagnosis, with a median of 2.3 children total). It is difficult to fully ascertain the possible effects of ICI therapy on fecundability when the denominator, e.g., the number of women actually interested in/attempting conception, is not known. This emphasizes the need for prospective studies on immunotherapy in reproductive-aged women who have not yet completed childbearing. Perhaps more importantly, it highlights the imperative for clinical trials of immunotherapy agents to include reproductive impacts as secondary treatment endpoints. This is an imperative that has been called on in the fields of reproductive medicine and oncology for years and that we strongly echo (2, 3).

To date, only a small minority of clinical oncology trials include reproductive biomarkers. A recent systematic review of breast cancer therapeutics evaluated whether reproductive function was assessed among phase III clinical trials for multiple investigational treatments, including chemotherapy, endocrine, immunotherapy, and poly-ADP-ribose polymerase or cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (5). Less than 10% of evaluated trials included ovarian function assessment as a prespecified endpoint, and none of those trials were specifically for immunotherapy or poly-ADP-ribose polymerase or cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors. Only a handful (3%) specifically assessed posttreatment ovarian reserve, and the majority sought to only collect menstrual data, which are a poor surrogate marker for fertility.

As life expectancy for individuals with cancer continues to improve in part because of these therapies and as therapies such as ICIs become more ubiquitous in cancer management, the inclusion of their longer-term reproductive effects in both phase III clinical trials and postmarket surveillance trials is long overdue. To best assess reproductive impacts of immunotherapy, drug trials should include baseline questionnaires at study enrollment regarding family-building goals, pretreatment and posttreatment ovarian reserve evaluation, and follow-up on outcomes of pregnancies during and after treatment. The latter should consider whether patients were referred for and/or underwent fertility preservation, if they required assisted reproductive technology to facilitate pregnancy, or if posttreatment pregnancies were unassisted. Through the collection of this reproductive data, we will better equip hematologic oncologists and reproductive endocrinologists with real data to counsel patients hoping to build their families after cancer treatment. It is past time to add these “checks” of reproductive function to clinical trials.
