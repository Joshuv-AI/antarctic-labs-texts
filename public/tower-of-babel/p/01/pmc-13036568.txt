HERV-R (ERV3-1) Env gene knockout reduces invasion, migration, and tumor growth in DLD1 colon cancer cells
Authors: Min-Hye Kim, Eun-Ji Ko, Hyojin An, Seongsoo Choi, Heui-Soo Kim, Sun-Hee Leem, Hee-Jae Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human genome contains sequences derived from endogenous retroviruses (HERVs), which constitute approximately 8% of chromosomal DNA. Most HERVs are currently inactive and noninfectious due to recombination, deletion, and mutation after integration into the host genome. However, recent studies have implicated HERVs as mutagens of intracellular genes, contributing to autoimmune diseases and tumors. Several studies have shown a significant association between HERVs and certain cancers. We focused on knocking out the HERV-R (ERV3-1) env gene in the DLD1 colon cancer cell line. A 208-bp deletion was confirmed by genomic PCR and DNA sequencing. As a result, HERV-R env gene expression was significantly lower in DLD1 HERV-R knockout (HERV-R KO) cells compared to control cells at both RNA and protein levels. Additionally, the invasion and migration abilities of HERV-R KO cells were significantly reduced. In vivo experiments on mice injected with HERV-R KO cells showed smaller tumor sizes compared to mice injected with control cells, suggesting that HERV-R env plays an important role in tumor growth. Further mRNA-seq analysis identified genes associated with cell invasion and migration. The STRING tool, which analyzes gene correlations, confirmed that HERV-R is linked to genes involved in cancer proliferation, migration, and invasion in colon cancer. This study suggests that the expression of the HERV-R env gene influences the tumorigenic properties of colon cancer, providing valuable evidence for potential clinical studies in colon cancer patients.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, HERV-R, Knockout, mRNA-seq, Tumorigenic characteristics

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a malignant tumor that occurs in the mucosa of the colon and rectum and is one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers worldwide (1, 2). CRC detected early can be cured with surgery and follow-up medication. However, it has a high recurrence rate and can develop resistance to anticancer drugs, which increases the risk of treatment failure. Therefore, it is important to detect CRC early, as well as to conduct regular checkups and remove any detected polyps. Despite this, current diagnostic methods are limited, with only 40% of CRC cases being detected early (3). Hence, there is a need for the development of new diagnostic techniques.

Retroviruses have the ability to insert their genes into the DNA of the host during replication. Over millions of years, some viruses have become part of the human genome and exist as inactivated endogenous retroviruses that can no longer replicate due to mutations, deletions, and other factors (4, 5). Endogenous retroviruses constitute approximately 8% of human chromosomal DNA and can form viral particles that have lost their ability to replicate and infect (6). However, recent studies have shown that human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs) can be expressed in certain diseases. There is a growing body of evidence suggesting a correlation between HERV expression and cancer. Cancers linked to HERV expression include lymphoma (7), breast cancer (4, 8), prostate cancer (9, 10), melanoma (11, 12), pancreatic cancer (13), ovarian cancer (14), leukemia (15), and germ cell-derived cancers (16). Notably, the HERV env gene, which encodes envelope proteins responsible for fusion and receptor recognition, has been implicated in carcinogenesis in some malignant tumors (17, 18). It has been suggested that HERV env promotes cancer progression through immunosuppression. Previously, we reported that the HERV-R (ERV3-1) env protein is expressed in both adult human organs and tumors, using tissue microarrays. To further investigate the relationship between HERV-R and tumor formation, we compared HERV-R expression in normal and tumor tissues. We confirmed that HERV-R is highly expressed in certain types of tumors, including lung adenocarcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, papillary carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma (16).

In this study, we prepared colorectal cancer cells and knocked out HERV-R in these cells. We then compared the proliferation, mobility, and invasiveness of the control and HERV-R knockout (KO) cells, and evaluated the effects of HERV-R KO in vivo. Additionally, we investigated the mechanisms and factors involved through transcriptome analysis of both control and HERV-R KO cells.

RESULTS

HERV-R env KO in DLD1 cells

We aimed to KO the HERV-R env gene in the DLD1 cell line (colon adenocarcinoma cells) to construct a new cell line. To achieve this, we used the CRISPR-Cas9 system to obtain cell candidates with HERV-R env KO and confirmed the results through genomic PCR and electrophoresis. Compared to the control group, the TF4, TF5, and TF17 candidates showed bands of a similar size, approximately 955 bp. However, for the TF1 candidate, two bands were observed: an upper band of 955 bp, which matched that of the control group, and a smaller lower band (Fig. 1A). Next, we evaluated the RNA levels of these candidates using RT-PCR and found that HERV-R env expression in TF1 was the lowest compared to the control group (Fig. 1B). This suggests that a portion of the HERV-R env region was knocked out, resulting in reduced gene expression. To pinpoint the exact deleted region, we conducted a sequencing analysis. The sequencing results revealed a 208-bp deletion in the PCR product, reducing the size from 955 bp (Fig. 1C). Based on these findings, we designated the TF1 candidate as HERV-R KO with HERV-R env KO and proceeded with further experiments.

(B) RNA levels in DLD1 control and DLD1 HERV-R KO candidates were confirmed and compared using RT-PCR. (C) The gDNA extracted from DLD1 and HERV-R KO cells was subjected to TA cloning and sequence analysis. A 208-bp deletion was confirmed in the HERV-R gene.

Confirmation of cell growth, mobility, and invasiveness in HERV-R env KO colon cancer cell lines

We performed RT-PCR and western blot analysis to assess the RNA and protein levels of HERV-R in both DLD1 and HERV-R KO cells. The results showed that HERV-R expression was lower in HERV-R KO compared to DLD1 at both the RNA and protein levels (Fig. 2A). Next, we seeded cells to monitor the growth of each cell line over 96 hours. The data revealed that HERV-R KO cells grew significantly slower than DLD1 cells (Fig. 2B). This indicates that HERV-R KO slows the growth of colon cancer cells. We then performed a wound-healing assay to assess cell mobility. In HERV-R KO cells, the wound gap closed more slowly than in DLD1 cells (Fig. 2C). When the images were analyzed quantitatively, the gap in HERV-R KO cells remained wider than in DLD1 cells. To further evaluate cell mobility, we conducted a Transwell assay. Fewer HERV-R KO cells migrated through the membrane compared to DLD1 cells (Fig. 2D). Using the same method, we observed a similar trend in cell invasiveness; HERV-R KO cells exhibited reduced invasiveness compared to DLD1 cells (Fig. 2D). In conclusion, knocking out HERV-R in colon cancer cells results in reduced growth, mobility, and invasiveness.

In vivo effects of HERV-R

To assess the effects of HERV-R reduction in vivo, DLD1 and HERV-R KO cells were injected subcutaneously into 6-week-old BALB/c nude mice. After 5 weeks, the tumor volume in mice injected with HERV-R KO cells increased more slowly compared to those injected with DLD1 cells (Fig. 3A). Additionally, when the actual tumor sizes were measured, HERV-R KO tumors were significantly smaller than DLD1 tumors (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that HERV-R plays a role in tumor proliferation in vivo, and that knocking out HERV-R leads to a reduction in tumor growth.

Gene expression profiling using next-generation transcriptome sequencing (NGS)

We performed mRNA-Seq analysis to examine the cellular characteristics of the HERV-R KO in greater detail. Through categorical analysis of the HERV-R KO cell line, we first confirmed that the expression of HERV-R (ERV3-1) was reduced in HERV-R KO cells (Fig. 4C). And through correlation analysis, we confirmed that the correlation between DLD1 and HERV-R KO (=DLD1-R) was high because the ratio was close to 1, and through scatter plot analysis, we were able to identify the expression patterns of several genes including HERV-R (ERV3-1) (Fig. 4A). Next, we identified 10 upregulated genes and 13 downregulated genes related to cell proliferation (data not shown). In the cell cycle, which is linked to cell proliferation, three genes were upregulated and one was downregulated, accounting for 0.31% of the total differential gene expression. For cell migration, 1 gene was upregulated and 9 were downregulated, making up 1.12% of the total differential gene expression (Fig. 4B).

To investigate gene correlations associated with the HERV-R knockout, we used the STRING tool, which analyzes protein-protein interactions (PPIs). In terms of cell mobility, ITGA3, TGFB1, SDC4, PDLM1, and CD24 were linked to HERV-R. For cell proliferation, several genes, including ACE2, GHRL, GRPR, and AGT, were found to be associated (Fig. 4D).

CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION

In our previous study, we confirmed that HERV-R env protein expression was elevated in colon cancer tissues compared to various other cancer tissues (16). This suggests that HERV-R may play an important role in colon cancer. However, the specific role of HERV-R in colon cancer has not been clearly investigated. Therefore, we generated a HERV-R KO cell line using CRISPR-Cas9 in DLD1, a colon adenocarcinoma cell line, to elucidate the function of HERV-R. Through this study, we confirmed that HERV-R plays a significant role in carcinogenesis by promoting cell proliferation, mobility, and invasiveness in colon cancer cell lines. Additionally, we demonstrated that HERV-R is involved in tumorigenesis in vivo through mouse experiments.

Furthermore, we performed mRNA-Seq analysis on the original DLD1 and HERV-R KO cell lines to identify the mechanisms and factors associated with HERV-R in the processes of carcinogenesis and tumorigenesis in colon cancer. Many of the genes affected by the decrease in HERV-R expression were predominantly downregulated. These downregulated genes were particularly involved in angiogenesis, cell death, immune response, and cell migration. In this study, we also confirmed the relationship between HERV-R and genes related to cell proliferation, the cell cycle, and cell migration, as characterized in previous experiments. As a result, we identified a total of 5 genes associated with cell mobility and 15 genes related to cell proliferation. Integrin subunit α3 (ITGA3) belongs to the integrin family and is known to interact with many ECM proteins, mediating both cell-to-cell and cell-to-matrix adhesion. Its dysregulated expression when influenced by other factors, can contribute to cancer development (19). Transforming growth factor β1 (TGFB1) is a potent cytokine that regulates various cellular processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, wound healing, and immune responses. In normal epithelial tissues, TGFB1 generally inhibits tumor progression by negatively regulating proliferation and growth. However, in cancer, TGFB1 can rewire signaling pathways to evade apoptosis and suppress immune responses, thereby promoting tumor progression (20). Syndecan 4 (SDC4) is an important member of the syndecan family, interacting with extracellular matrix proteins through heparin sulfate chains and playing a direct role in PPIs. The function of SDC4 has been closely associated with tumor development and metastasis (21). PDZ and LIM domain protein 1 (PDLM1), also known as CLP36, is a cytoskeletal protein containing two essential structural domains: an N-terminal PDZ domain and a C-terminal LIM domain. PDLM1 has been shown to regulate disease progression through various signaling pathways in several studies (22). CD24 is a small GPI-anchored cell surface glycoprotein that plays a variety of roles in several malignancies. Recent studies have demonstrated that CD24 serves as a stem cell surface marker in many cancers and plays an important role in tumor cell immune evasion. It has also been associated with increased metastasis formation in cancer (23). Angiotensin I-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) is highly expressed in colorectal cancer patients and has been widely reported in the clinical characteristics of coronavirus disease. A high expression of ACE2 in colorectal cancer tumors has been associated with better survival rates (24). The ghrelin prepropeptide gene (GHRL) encodes ghrelin, which acts on the growth hormone secretagogue receptor to promote the release of growth hormone. Recently, abnormal GHRL expression has been detected in several cancers, where it is known to influence cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through various signaling pathways (25). Gastrin-releasing peptide receptor (GRPR) is a member of the bombesin G protein-coupled receptor family and is known to be overexpressed in various malignant tumors across different anatomical sites. It plays a role in regulating human physiological mechanisms, including gastrointestinal motility, gastric emptying, and smooth muscle contraction (26). Angiotensinogen (AGT) is the sole precursor of angiotensin peptides and serves as a substrate for the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Additionally, AGT has been shown to play an important role in tumorigenesis, as well as in cell migration and invasion (27). In this context, we confirmed that several genes related to cell proliferation, migration, and invasiveness are influenced by the expression of HERV-R in colon cancer.

Through this study, we confirmed the effect of HERV-R on the characteristics of colon cancer and found that several genes involved in these characteristics are correlated with HERV-R expression. However, further research is needed to determine how these genes and HERV-R function in colon cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and transfection

The human colon adenocarcinoma cell line DLD1 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). DLD1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 1% penicillin, and L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). The cell lines were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 90% humidity. Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were trypsinized and passaged the day before transfection and plated to reach approximately 80% confluency on the day of transfection. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by RT-PCR.

Generation of the KO cell line with CRISPR-Cas9

Guide RNA (gRNA) sequences for CRISPR-Cas9 were designed using the CRISPR design tool provided by Toolgen (Seoul, Rep. Korea), a company specializing in CRISPR-Cas9 technology. The HERV-R gRNAs used were #1 (5’-AATGACATTTCTCATTTTTGTGG-3’), #2 (5’-TGTCATTTATCAAAATAGACTGG-3’), and #3 (5’-GCCCAGGAAGAGGGAGTATGCGG-3’), targeting the HERV-R locus at chr7: 63,451,590-64,453,404 (hg19). Complementary oligonucleotides for the gRNAs were annealed and cloned into the CRISPR-Cas9-Puro vector and pRGEN_Human-Herv-R_US (Toolgen). DLD1 cells were transfected with CRISPR-Cas9 along with gRNA #1, #2, and #3. Eighteen hours after transfection, cells were treated with 100 μg/ml hygromycin for 2 days. After 10 days, colonies were isolated using cloning cylinders, and the KO was confirmed by RT-PCR, genomic analysis, and western blotting.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

RNA was isolated from cells using Tri-RNA Reagent (Favorgen Biotech, FATRR 001, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was synthesized using PCR Mix (Bioneer, Seoul, Rep. Korea), and HERV-R gene expression was confirmed. The HERV-R primers were as follows: HERV-R forward: 5’-CATGGGAAGCAAGGGAACT-3’, reverse: 5’-CTTTCCCCA GCGAGCAATAC-3’. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a control with the following primers: GAPDH forward: 5’-CAATGACCCCTTCATTGACC-3’, reverse: 5’-GACAAGCTTCCCGTTCTCAG-3’. The RT-PCR conditions for HERV-R included activation of DNA polymerase at 94°C for 5 minutes, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 1 minute, 58°C for 1 minute, and 72°C for 1 minute, and a final extension step at 72°C for 5 minutes. For GAPDH, the conditions were similar, except the annealing temperature was 55°C for 30 seconds, with a total of 34 cycles. PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel.

Genomic PCR and sequencing

DNA was extracted from harvested cells using E-prep reagent (FIAT, USA) following the provided protocol. The extracted DNA was subjected to HERV-R genomic PCR using AccuPower HotStart PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Seoul, Rep. Korea). The HERV-R primers for genomic PCR were as follows: HERV-R gPCR forward: 5’-TCCACCGATTCCCCAGTAAC-3’, gPCR reverse: 5’-TTTCTCATCCCTGCTCCTGG-3’. The genomic PCR conditions included a pre-denaturation step at 94°C for 5 minutes, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 1 minute, 60°C for 1 minute, and 72°C for 1 minute, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 minutes. PCR products were confirmed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel, and the desired DNA band was extracted using a gel extraction procedure with a Fragment DNA purification kit-MEGAquick-Spin Plus (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea). The extracted DNA was ligated into a T-vector using the pGEM-T Easy Vector System (Promega, USA). The cloned vector was transformed into HIT Competent Cells-DH5 alpha Value 108 (Real Biotech Corporation, Taiwan). The cells were plated on LB＋Amp solid medium and incubated for 16 hours. Confirmed colonies were inoculated into LB＋Amp liquid medium and cultured for 16 hours. Plasmid DNA was isolated using the LaboPass Plasmid DNA Purification Kit Mini (Cosmogenetech, Seoul, Korea). The isolated plasmid DNA was confirmed through sequencing analysis.

Western blot analysis

Briefly, 100 μg of protein was extracted using PRO-PREP Protein Extraction Solution (Intron Biotechnology, Kyunggi, Korea), and the protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay system (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). The proteins were then separated by electrophoresis on a Novex 4%-12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen). The gel was incubated for 30 minutes in transfer buffer containing NuPAGE Bis-Tris transfer buffer (Invitrogen) and 20% methanol. Proteins were transferred electrophoretically to a PVDF membrane (LC2002, Invitrogen), and equal protein loading was confirmed using Ponceau S Solution (P9100-010, GenDEPOT, USA). Membranes were preincubated for 1 hour in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 5% skim milk and 0.05% Tween 20 (TBS-T). Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C in TBS-T solution containing primary antibodies. The antibody dilutions and sources were as follows: anti-ERV31/HERV-R (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab116723) and GAPDH (1:5000 dilution, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The membranes were washed three times with TBS-T and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour. The secondary antibodies were diluted as follows: HERV-R (1:10,000, Goat anti-rabbit IgG, Enzo, ADI-SAB-300-J) and GAPDH (1:5000, Goat anti-mouse IgG, Enzo, ADI-SAB-100-J). After three more washes with TBS-T, immunoreactive proteins were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell growth and wound-healing assay

For the cell growth assay, cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured in a complete medium for 96 hours. Cells were harvested every 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours, and cell numbers were measured using the trypan blue exclusion method. For the wound-healing assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured in complete medium for 24 hours. The following day, when cells were 80% confluent, a straight wound was made in the center of the well using a 200 μl micropipette tip. After replacing the medium with fresh medium, the wells were cultured for 24 and 48 hours, and the wound closure was observed under a microscope. The distance of cell migration to close the wound was measured using the ImageJ program.

Migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed as described previously (5). Briefly, Transwell chambers (Invitrogen) with 8-μm pore-size membranes were used for both migration and invasion assays. For the cell migration assay, cells were cultured in DMEM with serum for 18 hours, and 1 × 104 cells were suspended in 100 μl of serum-free DMEM and placed in the upper chamber of each well. In the lower chamber, 600 μl of DMEM with serum was added. After culturing for 18 hours at 37°C, cells that had migrated to the lower chamber were stained with Diff-Quik (Sysmex Corporation, 38721) and counted. For the cell invasion assay, a similar procedure was followed, but the Transwell membrane surface was first coated with 20 μl of 0.5 mg/ml Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA) before adding the cells to the upper chamber. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and each data point was measured in triplicate. The mean and 95% confidence interval were calculated.

Tumor xenograft assays

Six-week-old male immunodeficient athymic nude mice (BALB/c nude) (NCI, Frederick, MD, USA) were used in groups of five. DLD1 and HERV-R KO cells were prepared and injected subcutaneously into each group at 1 × 105 cells/100 μl. Tumor sizes were measured using digital calipers for 5 weeks after injections. Mice were euthanized before the tumor diameter exceeded 2 cm. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kosin University College of Medicine (KUCMIACUC, KMAP-22-09).

Next-generation mRNA sequencing (NGS)

To analyze the transcriptome expression pattern in HERV-R KO cells, mRNA-Seq analysis was performed on DLD1 and HERV-R KO cell lines provided by eBiogen. Total RNA was isolated from each cell line, and NGS analysis was conducted. Cluster analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using software provided by eBiogen, and related genes were identified using Venn diagram, Correlation, Scatter plot, DAVID, and PPI analysis tools. The dataset is available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public database under data series accession number GSE285815.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed at least three times, and statistical significance was assessed using the Student’s t-test provided by Graph Pad Prism 5 software. The P-values are expressed as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.
