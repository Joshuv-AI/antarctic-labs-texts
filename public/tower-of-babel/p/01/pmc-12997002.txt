Dataset of groundwater bacterial communities at varying distances from public cemeteries in surakarta, Indonesia
Authors: Triastuti Rahayu, Yasir Sidiq, Nur Miladan, Arief Budiono, Noor Alis Setiyadi, Bambang Budhi Santosa, Erma Musbita Tyastuti, Listy Hasti Mandiri, Alden Ganendra Madhava Priya Hardianto, Banuwati Kartika Sari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a dataset describing the bacterial communities of groundwater collected from inside and around public cemeteries in Surakarta, Central Java, Indonesia. Water samples were obtained from wells located inside cemeteries (S1), at 150 m (S2) and 300 m (S3) away from cemetery boundaries across six urban subdistricts. From each site, 5 L of well water was filtered through 0.45 µm membrane filters to concentrate microbial cells. DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS™ Soil DNA Extraction Kit (Zymo Research, USA). Bacterial community profiling was conducted using PacBio Sequel II full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Circular consensus sequences (CCS) were processed using SMRT Link and QIIME2 for quality control, chimera removal, and taxonomic classification against the SILVA 138 database. The dataset includes raw sequence reads, taxonomic abundance tables, and diversity index representing bacterial communities in groundwater at varying distances from cemeteries. Groundwater samples from cemeteries in Surakarta showed clear spatial differences in bacterial composition. Samples collected inside cemeteries exhibited the highest richness and proportion of low-rank taxa, with notable dominance of Verrucomicrobiota. At 150 m and 300 m distances, microbial diversity decreased. Proteobacteria, Planctomycetota, and Bacteroidota were also dominant phyla, showing differential abundance across sampling zones. Beta diversity analysis revealed distinct clustering by distance, confirming spatial structuring of microbial communities.The raw sequence data have been submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under project ID PRJNA1345172 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1345172). These data provide a valuable reference for understanding burial-related microbial dynamics and groundwater quality in tropical urban environments.Keywords: Groundwater, Bacteriomics, Contamination, Decomposition, Burial ground

Background

Cemeteries play important ecological, social, and cultural roles in urban environments. In addition to serving as burial grounds, they function as green open spaces that contribute to urban landscape and ecological balance. In Indonesia, burial depths typically range from 1.5 to 1.8 m. During decomposition by microorganisms, the human body releases gases and soluble compounds that can migrate into surrounding soil and groundwater. Studies in South Africa, Poland, and the United States have reported that cemetery leachate may contain opportunistic or pathogenic bacteria, posing potential contamination risks to nearby groundwater. However, limited data exist on groundwater microbial communities near cemeteries in Indonesia, where well water remains a major source of domestic water.

Groundwater ecosystems harbor diverse bacterial communities that play critical roles in organic matter degradation, nutrient cycling, and overall subsurface ecological functioning. Changes in microbial composition and diversity may indicate environmental stress or contamination, including those influenced by burial-related processes. Understanding the bacterial community structure in groundwater near cemeteries is essential for assessing potential health and ecological risks in urban settings.

Regulations regarding safe distances between cemeteries and residential zones vary internationally. In Surakarta, local regulations state that cemeteries should not be located too close to densely populated areas, but no specific minimum distance is defined. By contrast, Ecuador recommends a 200-meter buffer, and the World Health Organization (WHO) suggests a 250-meter separation.

This dataset presents PacBio Sequel II full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing data describing bacterial communities in groundwater from wells located inside cemeteries and at 150 m and 300 m distances in Surakarta City, Indonesia. The dataset provides a baseline for understanding groundwater microbial diversity, potential pathogen presence, and the environmental safety of urban cemetery zones.

Data Description

This data article presents a comprehensive metabarcoding analysis of groundwater bacterial communities from wells at varying distances from urban cemeteries in Surakarta City, Indonesia. The dataset encompasses both raw sequencing files and processed data outputs. In total, 18 groundwater samples were collected from six cemetery sites (each site contributing one well inside the cemetery, one at 150 m outside, and one at 300 m outside). This spatial design yields six replicates for each distance group (inside vs. 150 m vs. 300 m). All samples were labeled to identify the site and position (e.g., SK-S1 for Site SK, inside cemetery at Sangkrah sub-district). The PacBio circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads were high quality (mean Phred score >35 across reads) (Fig. 1). FastQC reports confirmed high-quality full-length reads (1500 bp, spanning V1–V9 regions) [3]. The dataset exhibited minimal read errors, no GC anomalies, and tight read-length distribution—confirming suitability for high-resolution taxonomic analysis.

Mean phred quality scores across read positions generated by fastqc.

Taxonomic profiles showed stratified structure inside-cemetery samples had the highest proportion of genus and species-level assignments; distant samples had more phylum- and family-level classifications (Fig. 2). Thus, groundwater collected inside cemetery zones contained the highest proportion of lower-rank taxa (species and genus), suggesting a more complex and diverse microbial community directly influenced by organic matter released from burial sites. In contrast, samples from 150 m and 300 m exhibited fewer low-level taxa but higher representation at the family and phylum levels, consistent with an ecological filtering effect that reduces diversity with distance from the contamination source. This stratification reflects ecological filtering of taxa by distance, nutrient input, and burial site proximity [4,5].

Group stacked taxonomy levels across cemetery zones.

Expanding on this trend, (Fig. 3) displays the relative abundance of dominant bacterial phyla across the three zones. Several major phyla Proteobacteria, Planctomycetota, and Bacteroidota were consistently dominant across all samples, related to the patterns observed in contaminated groundwater ecosystems [2,6]. Notably, Proteobacteria became increasingly prevalent at 300 m, indicating the dominance of metabolically versatile taxa in less burial-affected environments. Contrary, Planctomycetota and Bacteroidota were more abundant closer to the cemetery, likely linked to the decomposition of organic matter and nutrient cycling under mildly anaerob conditions.

Relative abundance of dominant bacterial phyla across cemetery distances.

Venn analysis were used to identify shared and site-specific microbial components. A total of 382 ASVs were identified shared across all groups—representing a regional core microbiome [7,8]. Unique ASVs were highest in S1 (648), followed by S3 (202) and S2 (150), supporting environmental selection by distance. ASV presence/absence tables are provided (Fig. 4). The diagram shows 382 shared ASVs, representing a core microbiome that persists across spatial zones, a phenomenon also reported in soil and groundwater metabarcoding surveys [7,8]. The presence of unique ASVs—648 (inside), 150 (150 m), and 202 (300 m)—highlights the existence of localized microbial populations adapted to distinct geochemical niches and burial-induced stressors.

Venn diagram showing shared and unique ASVs among sample groups.

Integrating these taxonomic and diversity insights, (Fig. 5) uses Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) to visualize beta diversity patterns among groundwater samples. The ordination plot reveals clear spatial clustering by proximity to cemeteries, with inside, 150 m, and 300 m samples forming distinct groups. This clustering pattern supports the hypothesis that burial proximity drives measurable microbial differentiation [[9], [10], [11]]. The first two principal coordinate axes (explaining 12 % and 10 % of variation, respectively) capture the ecological gradient shaping groundwater microbiome composition, with higher heterogeneity observed closer to the burial zones.

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) of bacterial community structure.

Collectively, these results establish a coherent ecological narrative: microbial richness and diversity are highest near burial grounds and gradually decline with distance, accompanied by compositional turnover and ecological specialization. The integration of taxonomic composition (Fig. 2 and 3), shared diversity structure (Fig. 4), and community ordination (Fig. 5) underscores the spatial structuring of the cemetery groundwater microbiome. These findings align with global observations linking anthropogenic influence to groundwater microbiology [8,9], and provide a foundational dataset for future research on subterranean microbial ecology, biogeochemical resilience, and potential environmental health implications of cemetery sites.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

In this dataset, groundwater samples were collected from public cemeteries located in Surakarta City, Central Java, Indonesia, (Lat −7.576938, Long 110.837003) in July 2025. Sampling was performed at six distinct locations (SK, NS, PJ, BJ, PS, and JG), each representing a different district of the city. At every location, groundwater was sampled from wells situated inside the cemetery area, and at 150 m and 300 m distances from the cemetery boundaries. This spatial sampling design was intended to capture potential variation in bacterial community composition associated with burial activities and surrounding environmental gradients.

•DNA preparation

DNA preparation

For each sampling point, 5 liters of well water were collected using sterile polyethylene containers and maintained under cold conditions during transport to the laboratory. Microbial cells were concentrated by filtration through 0.5 µm membrane filters and was placed into the preservation buffer. The filters were then aseptically transferred into sterile tubes in longmire solution and stored at 4 °C until further processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS™ Soil DNA Extraction Kit (Zymo Research, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purity and concentration were evaluated using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer prior to sequencing.

•PacBio sequencing

PacBio sequencing

Bacterial community profiling was conducted by full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing on the PacBio Sequel IIe platform. Full-length bacterial 16S rRNA genes were amplified using universal primers 27F: AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG and 1492R: RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT

PCR reactions (25 µL) contained 2 × KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Kapa Biosystems, USA), 2.5 µM of each primer, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. The amplification protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The PCR products were verified on 0.8 % agarose gels and quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All high-quality PCR products were pooled in equimolar concentrations and purified using AMPure PB magnetic beads (PacBio, USA) to remove primer dimers and residual impurities.

•Library construction and PacBio sequencing

Library construction and PacBio sequencing

Approximately 600 ng of pooled amplicons were used to prepare the SMRTbell library using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 3.0 (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The process included DNA damage repair, end-repair, and adapter ligation steps to generate sequencing-ready SMRTbell templates. Sequencing was performed on the PacBio Sequel IIe platform using SMRT Cell 8 M with Chemistry v2. Primary base-calling and quality filtering were conducted on-board the Sequel IIe system, and secondary analysis was carried out using the SMRT Link software (v11.0.0).

•Bioinformatics workflow

Bioinformatics workflow

Raw subreads were demultiplexed and processed using the SMRT Link pipeline, followed by downstream analysis in QIIME2 (Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology, version 2023.2). Reads were filtered for quality (Phred score ≥15), trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.38), and denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 with default parameters.

Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using a trained SILVA 16S rRNA reference database (Release 138). The ASV abundance table was rarefied to an even depth across all samples prior to calculating alpha- and beta-diversity indices.
