Retroperitoneal extrarenal Wilms tumor: A diagnostic challenge and kidney-sparing approach
Authors: Julia G Debertin, Nathan C Hull, D Dean Potter, Wendy A Allen-Rhoades, Nadia N Laack, Stephanie F Polites
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis is a report of a 2-year-old female who presented with a large right retroperitoneal mass that was abutting the right kidney but appeared distinct with absence of the “claw sign.” Biopsy showed Wilms tumor. Following neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the tumor remained associated with the kidney, though without clear renal origin. Intraoperatively, an extrarenal Wilms tumor was confirmed, and tumor resection was performed without nephrectomy. Postoperatively, the patient underwent kidney-sparing proton beam flank radiation and completed chemotherapy. Imaging remains negative for recurrence, and there is no postradiation atrophy of the right kidney 18 months following completion of therapy. A high index of suspicion is needed for extrarenal, retroperitoneal Wilms tumor to allow for kidney-sparing treatment.Keywords: Wilms tumor, Nephroblastoma, Extrarenal Wilms tumor, Proton beam radiation

Introduction

Wilms tumor, or nephroblastoma, is one of the most common extracranial malignant solid tumors in children, representing over 90% of renal tumors in this population [1]. Extra-renal Wilms tumors (ERWT) are rare and comprise between 0.5% and 1% of Wilms tumor diagnoses [2]. Previous case studies have identified ERWT in locations including the retroperitoneum [[3], [4], [5], [6]], spine [2], urinary bladder [7], undescended testis [6], duplicate sigmoid colon [6], and uterine cavity [8]. Diagnosis is challenging due to the rarity of this histology outside of the kidney. It is important to distinguish retroperitoneal ERWT from exophytic renal Wilms tumor, neuroblastoma, and hepatoblastoma, due to the differences in management. Here, we report the workup, treatment, and outcome of a patient with retroperitoneal ERWT Wilms tumor with initial equivocal imaging.

Case presentation

A 2-year-old female presented with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Physical exam and abdominal x-ray were concerning for an abdominal mass which was confirmed on computed tomography as a heterogeneous, enhancing, 8.1 × 11.2 × 9.6 cm mass in the right upper quadrant with regional mass effect, including on the right kidney, liver, and pancreas, without clear origin (Fig. 1). There was no renal claw sign or calcifications, and the right adrenal gland appeared distinct. The mass effaced the inferior vena cava and bilateral renal veins. There was no suspicious lymphadenopathy or pulmonary nodules. Urine vanillylmandelic acid and homovanillic acid were within normal limits. An ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy was performed, which demonstrated Wilms tumor with loss of 1p, gain of 1q, loss of 11p, and without loss of heterozygosity of 16q or anaplasia. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed to better characterize the tumor with respect to the kidney, given the histology (Fig. 2). MRI favored exophytic renal Wilms tumor with origin from the anterior right kidney, based on focal discontinuity of the renal cortex, though also was concerning for invasion into the liver and right adrenal gland. Repeat imaging following neoadjuvant therapy was recommended to better delineate tumor origin.

The patient was started on three-drug chemotherapy (vincristine, dactinomycin, and doxorubicin) per Children’s Oncology Group protocol for higher-risk favorable histology Wilms tumor [9]. Repeat MRI obtained following 6 weeks of chemotherapy demonstrated an excellent response. The tumor measured 2.7 × 3.7 × 4.5 cm with evidence of necrosis (Fig. 3). There was persistent but improved mass effect on the right kidney, liver, pancreas, IVC, and renal vessels without invasion. The right kidney was otherwise normal in appearance. The patient then underwent open renal-sparing tumor resection with lymph node sampling. Intraoperatively, the tumor was densely adherent to the duodenum, head of pancreas, adrenal gland, IVC, portal vein, and right renal vessels with obvious treatment effect. The right renal capsule was intact, consistent with ERWT. There was no intraoperative spill. Pathology showed Wilms tumor with 10% viability and no anaplasia. Biopsies taken from the tumor bed were negative for tumor, as were seven lymph nodes that were sampled. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4. Due to the preoperative biopsy, flank radiation was indicated. This begun on postoperative day 10 and utilized a proton, kidney-sparing approach (Fig. 4A). Chemotherapy was resumed on postoperative day 14 to complete 25 weeks. At 18 months off therapy, imaging remains negative for recurrence, and there is no postradiation atrophy of the right kidney (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Retroperitoneal ERWT is important to distinguish from other histologies more common outside of the kidney, such as neuroblastoma, hepatoblastoma, and pancreatoblastoma. It is also important to distinguish from exophytic renal Wilms tumor, as most patients with unilateral Wilms tumor are recommended to have upfront nephrectomy of the involved kidney. Here we report the case of a young patient with a large retroperitoneal tumor with equivocal initial imaging for which biopsy and neoadjuvant therapy facilitated visceral-sparing complete resection.

The most common location of ERWT is retroperitoneal, which was the location of our patient’s tumor [10]. Several previous cases of retroperitoneal ERWT reported similar challenges in identifying the origin of the retroperitoneal ERWT on diagnostic or preoperative imaging [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. For example, Wakely et al. [11] describe a case series of four ERWT patients, three of which had adherence to the adjacent kidney either to the upper pole or the midportion. The computed tomography at diagnosis for two of those patients identified the tumor as arising from the kidney. Another case study reported by Gordetsky et al. [12] also described an inability to confirm renal involvement of a retroperitoneal Wilms tumor based on preoperative image. Houben et al. [13] described two patients with tumors displacing the kidney on imaging and found to be adherent to the kidney during resection.

Several case studies also present retroperitoneal ERWT that were determined to be tumors uninvolved with the adjacent kidney based on preoperative imaging [3,14]. For example, Rojas et al. [14] presented a patient with a retroperitoneal tumor ventral to the lumbosacral region with no evidence of renal involvement, and Taguchi et al. [3] discussed an encapsulated mass separate from the left kidney. Despite the same classification as retroperitoneal ERWT, there is variance in adherence or involvement of adjacent kidneys, which determines patient morbidity, as many patients with tumor adherent to an adjacent kidney had a complete or partial nephrectomy at the time of tumor resection [[11], [12], [13]].

While the etiology of ERWT remains somewhat elusive, previous studies have supported a heterotrophic metanephric blastema origin, similar to renal Wilms tumor [13,15,16]. Genetic predisposition and genetic variants, specifically in regard to the tumor suppressor gene WT1, explain the malignant transformation of this ectopic tissue [13,17]. Clinically, however, the treatment of ERWT parallels renal Wilms tumor as the National Wilms’ Tumor Study system is applied for ERWT staging [18]. However, staging ERWT can be challenging. For example, in the case of our patient, biopsy was able to confirm favorable histology; however, prior to surgery, the origin of the tumor was unclear, and therefore her disease could not be staged completely. Due to the rarity and heterogeneity of ERWT, developing separate staging criteria proves challenging and previous assessment of patients with ERWT who were treated according to the National Wilms’ Tumor Study demonstrated comparable prognosis relative to patients with intrarenal Wilms tumor [2,16].

Conclusion

Extrarenal Wilms tumor is a rare oncologic diagnosis for which a high level of suspicion is needed at diagnosis, especially for large tumors in the retroperitoneum. Local staging can be challenging. While ERWT may be immediately adjacent to the kidney, it can be successfully managed without nephrectomy.
