Assessment of cardiac and pulmonary pressure for transatrial-to-left-ventricular cannulation: implications for surgical safety
Authors: Atsutaka Aratame, Masanori Sakaguchi, Toshio Baba, Yosuke Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal cannulation strategy in surgery for acute type A aortic dissection (ATAA) remains controversial. A recently introduced transatrial-to-left ventricle (TA-LV) perfusion method allows antegrade perfusion without exposing peripheral vessels. However, concerns regarding the risks of pulmonary oedema and cardiac overdistension during this approach exist. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the hemodynamic safety of this technique and identify perfusion parameters that could mitigate associated risks.MethodsEighteen patients undergoing emergency surgery for ATAA using TA-LV perfusion were included in this study. A pulmonary artery catheter was placed in each patient to obtain objective hemodynamic measurements. Systemic arterial pressure and pulmonary arterial pressure were monitored throughout the procedure, particularly during the period of ventricular fibrillation (VF).ResultsAmong 18 cases, 13 patients experienced VF during cooling. During this period, the mean systemic arterial pressure and pulmonary arterial pressure were comparable at approximately 40 mmHg. The median duration of VF was 7 min. No cases developed clinically significant pulmonary oedema or evidence of cardiac overdistension.ConclusionsMaintaining the lowest acceptable perfusion pressure during VF in this perfusion technique may help reduce the risk of cardiac overdistension and pulmonary congestion.

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAA) is a life-threatening condition that requires emergency surgical intervention (1). Over the years, various advancements have been made in surgical techniques and perioperative management; however, surgical treatment continues to be associated with a high perioperative mortality rate (1,2). Unlike other cardiac procedures, safe cannulation of the ascending aorta is feasible in only a limited number of cases. In this regard, rapid establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass and safe arterial cannulation play a crucial role in improving surgical outcomes (2).

Arterial cannulation techniques can be broadly classified into antegrade and retrograde perfusion, but each method has its own disadvantages, such as the time required for cannulation, risk of vascular injury at the cannulation site, thromboembolic complications, and inadequate organ perfusion (3). Some reports state that axillary cannulation was associated with a reduced risk of stroke (4,5). However, if axillary arteries are dissected, the surgeon should reconsider another cannulation strategy (2). Klotz et al. (6) reported equivalent outcomes between femoral and central cannulation approaches. Currently, there is no universally accepted perfusion method, and the choice largely depends on institutional experience and the attending surgeon’s preference.

To address these challenges, Schoeneich et al. (7) first reported a technique known as transatrial-to-left ventricle (TA-LV) cannulation. In this method, a perfusion cannula is inserted through the right upper pulmonary vein and left atrium into the left ventricle, similar to the placement of a left ventricular vent. This approach offers the advantage of establishing antegrade flow without the need for exposing additional vessels and is relatively simple to perform (7).

Midterm follow-up studies that evaluated this technique found no significant difference in perioperative mortality compared with conventional methods; however, they did report reductions in operative time, incidence of postoperative stroke, and acute kidney injury (8,9).

However, several issues have been reported to be associated with the TA-LV cannulation technique. Nakahara et al. (10) reported that TA-LV cannulation caused pulmonary oedema resulting from severe mitral regurgitation in cases with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Additionally, Ak and Çalışan (11) raised concerns about a risk of left ventricular distension. Therefore, it is essential to confirm proper placement of the perfusion cannula in the left ventricle using transoesophageal echocardiography and manually assess the left ventricular tension (8,9). However, objective parameters to guide this perfusion strategy remain unclear. In particular, objective indicators that may help prevent pulmonary oedema and cardiac overdistension are clinically important.

Therefore, in this study, we assessed these concerns by preoperatively inserting a Swan-Ganz catheter to obtain objective hemodynamic data and explored strategies for safe and effective implementation of this perfusion technique. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0915/rc).

Methods

Surgical procedure

A Swan-Ganz catheter was inserted preoperatively to record the pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) during the surgery. Following median sternotomy and systemic heparinization, CPB was established using a 21-Fr arterial cannula (EZ Glide; Edwards Lifesciences) described in the TA-LV cannulation technique via the left atrium (7). Although the insertion of the perfusion cannula follows a technique similar to that used for left ventricular vent placement via the right upper pulmonary vein and left atrium, careful manipulation is essential. In our institution, we routinely use a curved plastic introducer attached to the Williams vent catheter (Mera, Japan), which allows for safe and reliable cannula placement. The superior and inferior vena cava were cannulated with a common venous cannula, with a pump flow of 2.4 L/min/m2 maintained throughout the procedure in all cases. We continually monitored the LA-LV cannula position using Swan-Ganz catheter data and TEE. If the cannula becomes dislodged into the left atrium during surgery, a sudden rise in PAP can be monitored. This dislocation occurs particularly during cardiac manipulation for retrograde cardioplegia catheter placement.

The pharyngeal temperature was monitored as the core body temperature. During cooling of the body, progressive bradycardia developed, and epicardial pacing was applied to sustain cardiac output. Circulatory arrest was induced once moderate hypothermia (28 ℃) was achieved. Myocardial protection was achieved through retrograde cardioplegia and selective cerebral perfusion was initiated. After completion of the distal anastomosis, antegrade perfusion was resumed through the side branch of the prothesis graft, while the previously placed TA-LV cannula was repurposed as a left ventricular vent by connecting it to the venting suction line. Following central anastomosis, the graft clamp was released to restore cardiac flow. In cases requiring total arch replacement or hemiarch replacement, reconstruction of the arch branch vessels was subsequently performed. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Bell-land General Hospital (No. CR2025-03). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for participation in this study.

Statistical analysis

The normality of all datasets was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. For variables with a normal distribution, the results are presented as the mean and standard deviation. For variables with an abnormal distribution, the results are presented as the median and interquartile range. All statistical analyses were performed with EZR (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). More precisely, it is a modified version of R commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics (12).

Table 3 shows the postoperative variables and complications. No significant postoperative elevation was observed in markers suggestive of organ malperfusion compared with preoperative values. Although creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme levels were slightly elevated, the degree of elevation appeared to be within an acceptable range for conventional cardiac and aortic procedures involving cardiac arrest. Lactate levels increased to 8.0 mmol/L, which was considered likely attributable to the surgical insult; in nearly all patients, lactate levels peaked out by the following day. One patient developed a major stroke, precluding discharge home, whereas the remaining three patients with minor stroke were ultimately discharged home. Of the four patients requiring prolonged pulmonary ventilation, one developed a major stroke, and another, who presented with preoperative shock vital after cardiopulmonary resuscitation, showed evidence of hypoxic brain injury postoperatively. The remaining two patients were aged ≥75 years and were frail, requiring prolonged ventilatory support. No postoperative findings suggestive of pulmonary oedema, including a butterfly shadow on chest radiography or prolonged mechanical ventilation due to impaired oxygenation, were observed in any patient. There were no cases of paraplegia or paraparesis, renal failure requiring long renal replacement therapy, or heart failure requiring long inotropic support. Postoperative echocardiography showed a mean left ventricular ejection fraction of 53.6%, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter of 40.8 mm, left ventricular end-systolic diameter of 25.6 mm, and right ventricular fractional area change of 46.4%. No moderate or greater mitral or aortic regurgitation was detected. Collectively, these findings suggested no clinically apparent left or right ventricular functional injury.

Discussion

Intraoperative findings

In this study, we retrospectively evaluated the safety of the TA-LV cannulation technique using Swan-Ganz catheter measurements. Our results showed that during VF, the mean SAP and mean PAP were compatible, regardless of the mitral regurgitation grade, as assessed by intraoperative transoesophageal echocardiography after TA-LV cannulation. In selected cases, pulmonary artery wedge pressure was also measured by inflating the balloon of the Swan-Ganz catheter during VF, and the wedge pressure was comparable to SAP and PAP. Because pulmonary artery wedge pressure is generally considered to reflect left atrial pressure, these findings suggest that pressure comparable to SAP may be transmitted to the pulmonary artery, left atrium, and left ventricle during VF. In addition, because the aortic valve is generally open during VF, SAP may also be transmitted to the left ventricle.

We hypothesize that our findings may be attributable to the ventricular congestion due to VF, which resulted in loss of both ventricular diastolic and systolic function and even the atrial kick. Furthermore, this led to blood stagnation within the left atrium, equalization of left atrial and ventricular pressures, and subsequent elevation in PAP. Scholz et al. (13) inferred that mitral valve incompetence might elevate the PAP to a level close to the SAP in a non-beating heart. Conversely, as long as spontaneous cardiac rhythm was preserved, the mean PAP remained within an acceptable range (21.6±10.1 mmHg). These observations may be relevant for perfusion pressure management during cardiopulmonary bypass, particularly during VF. However, left atrial pressure and left ventricular pressure were not directly measured in this study. Therefore, PAP and pulmonary artery wedge pressure should be interpreted as indirect haemodynamic indicators rather than definitive measurements of left-sided chamber pressure.

Strategies for preventing potential complications

Based on these findings, we propose two key strategies to prevent cardiac overdistension and pulmonary oedema. First, intentional reduction of SAP during VF may help prevent pulmonary oedema and cardiac distention. Pressure management during VF is not clearly addressed in the TA-LV cannulation technique, while a targeted perfusion pressure of 50–70 mmHg is provided during CPB (8). We intentionally controlled the mean SAP at around 40 mmHg during VF as long as an adequate CPB flow could be sustained, aiming to prevent pressure-related myocardial and pulmonary injury. In particular, as the right ventricle is part of a low-pressure system, pressure control during VF may be of greater importance for the right ventricle than for the left.

Second, maintaining spontaneous cardiac activity as long as possible appears to be effective because, compared with during VF, the PAP was clearly lower during spontaneous cardiac activity. In this study, we tried to maintain cardiac output by applying epicardial pacing when bradycardia was observed as the core body temperature was decreasing. Although pacing does not always prevent VF, early epicardial pacing support may reduce the duration of VF and mitigate its associated risks. Additionally, considering that VF occurred at around 31 ℃, the cooling protocol was adjusted to reduce the cooling rate below 32 ℃. Cooling to 32 ℃ was achieved over approximately 10 min, after which temperature reduction was performed gradually at a rate of 2 ℃ per 10 min. Adjustment of the cooling rate may also represent an important strategy for shortening the duration of VF. However, once VF occurs, a faster cooling rate may be preferable to minimise the duration of VF.

However, there are some concerns about our cases, including what the safe duration limit is for VF. Moreover, VF is associated with two major concerns: one is myocardial ischaemia resulting from reduced coronary perfusion due to the loss of pulsatile pressure, and the other is myocardial injury caused by an increase in left ventricular pressure. Hippeläinen et al. (14) investigated the VF time during coronary artery bypass surgery in 61 cases and reported that a VF time of up to 10 min was not harmful when left ventricular decompression was ensured. Recent studies have suggested that in minimally invasive cardiac surgeries procedures performed under VF may achieve outcomes comparable with those obtained with conventional aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegic arrest techniques (15,16). In addition, reportedly, the combination of VF and mild hypothermia (30–31 ℃) permits prolonged VFs without apparent adverse effects (15,16). Notably, these favourable reports on VF are based on the fundamental premise of adequate left ventricular venting.

In ATAA surgery, cooling the body is necessary for circulatory arrest, implying that the safe VF time duration may be longer than that reported in the aforementioned studies. However, left ventricular decompression could be challenging in the TA-LV cannulation technique. Therefore, to mitigate overdistension of the left ventricle, we controlled the mean SAP during VF at around 40 mmHg.

In the patients in this study, the median duration of VF was 7 min. The distribution was non-normal due to an outlier with a maximum duration of 24 min. However, based on our experience and data, when VF occurs at about 31.2 ℃ on average, it may be possible to achieve cooling to 28 ℃ within approximately 8 min. These findings may provide useful insights for centres targeting lower levels of deep hypothermia.

Another question worthy of consideration is whether a perfusion pressure of 40 mmHg during VF is safe. Vedel et al. (17) compared patients assigned to higher (70–80 mmHg) versus lower (40–50 mmHg) mean arterial pressure targets during cardiopulmonary bypass. They concluded that no significant difference was observed in frequency of severe adverse events. Turner et al. (18) reported that in coronary artery bypass grafting and/or valvular surgery, low perfusion flow (<1.6 L/min/m2) and pressure (< 50 mmHg) were not associated with postoperative acute kidney injury, stroke, or death in multicentre registry data analysis. However, Ono et al. (19) reported that both the duration for which and degree by which the mean arterial pressure is below the cerebral autoregulation threshold may predict organ dysfunction, and the threshold varies among individuals. In addition, hypothermia-associated VF appears to differ from that in conventional valve or coronary artery bypass grafting procedures.

Therefore, SAP of approximately 40 mmHg should not be regarded as a validated safety threshold. Rather, it represents an observational finding in our cohort and may serve as a practical intraoperative reference. When TA-LV cannulation is used, the left ventricle cannot be vented during VF. Excessive perfusion pressure may increase pressure loading on the left-sided chambers and may contribute to cardiac overdistension or pulmonary congestion. Considering these haemodynamic characteristics, maintaining the lowest acceptable perfusion pressure during VF, according to each institution’s perfusion strategy and the patient’s haemodynamic condition, may be useful for reducing the risk of cardiac overdistension and pulmonary congestion. Additionally, lowering systolic arterial pressure should be limited to periods of VF.

Patient selection and technical considerations

TA-LV cannulation has been used as our routine cannulation strategy for all emergency surgeries of ATAA after introduction of this technique. Regarding postoperative outcomes, no heart failure attributable to myocardial injury or pulmonary oedema—potential concerns of this technique—were observed. The postoperative left ventricular ejection fraction was generally preserved, and no complications such as mitral valve injury were identified. Taken together, our findings suggest that this strategy may be useful in preventing complications specific to this technique.

In patient selection, previous reports have suggested that this technique may be applicable to ATAA, except in patients with mitral stenosis, as described in the original study evaluating long-term outcomes (9). Another report also suggested that the technique may be useful in patients with aortic arch aneurysm associated with a shaggy ascending aorta (20).

However, this technique should not be considered universally applicable to all patients. The major concerns associated with this technique are cardiac overdistension and pulmonary congestion during VF. Therefore, patients with significant myocardial ischaemia, severe mitral regurgitation, mitral stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, or marked left ventricular dilatation may require particular caution. These conditions may increase left ventricular pressure loading, potentially causing earlier onset of VF and prolongation of the VF period. Redo surgery may also be a cautionary condition because adhesions may make exposure of the right superior pulmonary vein difficult and may increase the risk of injury before cannulation. The exposure of the right superior pulmonary vein can be technically challenging in patients with ATAA, particularly when the ascending aorta is markedly enlarged. In such cases, the operative field around the right superior pulmonary vein may be limited. In our practice, the pericardium is opened longitudinally in the midline and the incision is extended toward the superior and inferior vena cava to enlarge the intrapericardial working space. Gentle retraction of the enlarged ascending aorta toward the assistant’s side then allows exposure of the right superior pulmonary vein. If the operative field remains insufficient, temporary cessation of pulmonary ventilation may be helpful by allowing the lung to collapse and providing additional space for cannulation. In patients with a high risk of rupture or haemodynamic collapse after pericardiotomy, we consider that the femoral artery should be exposed or prepared in advance so that rapid arterial cannulation can be established if necessary. Specifically, when preoperative computed tomography demonstrates pericardial effusion, the pericardium should first be opened by approximately 1 cm to drain the pericardial fluid. In most cases, the effusion is serosanguineous, which may reflect blood that has seeped into the surrounding tissues at the onset of aortic dissection, and continuous drainage is rarely observed. If persistent drainage is encountered, rupture should be suspected. In such cases, the pericardial window should be closed, and conversion to an alternative arterial perfusion strategy should be considered.

Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography is important to confirm cannula position and to detect newly developed mitral regurgitation, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, or ventricular distension. Continuous PAP monitoring using a Swan-Ganz catheter may also help detect abnormal haemodynamic changes during VF. In our technique, the introducer for the left ventricular vent is pre-shaped into a J-configuration before insertion, which facilitates safer and more controlled cannula placement.

Limitations

First, this was a single-centre retrospective study including a small number of patients. Therefore, the findings are vulnerable to chance, selection bias, and the influence of institutional practice. In addition, this study did not include a control group or comparison with other cannulation strategies, such as axillary or femoral cannulation. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory observational findings during VF under TA-LV cannulation, rather than definitive evidence supporting a standard pressure management target. Larger comparative studies are required to validate the optimal haemodynamic management strategy during this technique.

Second, detailed preoperative echocardiographic data, including objective measurements of left ventricular size, wall thickness, and right ventricular function, were not systematically available. Because ATAA is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate surgical intervention, most patients proceeded to surgery within a few hours after diagnosis, which limited the opportunity to obtain comprehensive and standardised preoperative echocardiographic measurements. Therefore, the assessment of baseline cardiac function and potential pre-existing cardiac dysfunction was limited.

However, the consistent results observed in this series support the feasibility of the technique. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted.

Conclusions

In this single-centre retrospective series, continuous PAP monitoring using a Swan-Ganz catheter provided practical haemodynamic information. Considering these haemodynamic characteristics, maintaining the lowest acceptable perfusion pressure during VF may be useful for reducing the risk of cardiac overdistension and pulmonary congestion. Although the present findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size and lack of a control group, these data may be useful for guiding cardiopulmonary bypass management during VF under TA-LV cannulation.
