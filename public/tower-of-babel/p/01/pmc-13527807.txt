Prominent Self-Injurious Behavior Masking the Diagnosis of Rett Syndrome: A Case Report from a Resource-Limited Setting.
Authors: Franca Ogechi Iheonu, Beatrice Nkolika Ezenwa, Elizabeth Eberechi Oyenusi, Foluso Ebun Afolabi Lesi, Lilian Obianuju Ndukwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rett syndrome (RTT) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by developmental regression, loss of purposeful hand use, and stereotypic movements after apparently normal early development. We report a 26-month-old girl who presented with severe self-injurious behavior (SIB), including repetitive biting of the left forearm and head banging, associated with restlessness, frequent yelling, and poor sleep. Further evaluation revealed regression of motor, language, and hand skills with stereotypic hand wringing. Examination showed impaired social interaction, ataxic gait, and hypertrophic scars on the left forearm, while neurologic and systemic examinations were otherwise non-focal. Brain magnetic resonance imaging and electroencephalography were normal. In the absence of genetic testing, the diagnosis of RTT was established based on internationally accepted clinical diagnostic criteria. Parents were counseled for multidisciplinary care and followed up over a six-month period. This case highlights that prominent SIB may obscure the diagnosis of RTT and delay recognition, particularly in resource-limited settings where genetic testing is unavailable.

Introduction

Rett syndrome (RTT) is a rare X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder that occurs almost exclusively in females and is most commonly caused by pathogenic variants in the Methyl-CpG-Binding Protein 2 (MECP2) gene. Affected individuals typically exhibit apparently normal early development followed by a period of developmental regression between 12 and 18 months of age,[1] characterized by loss of purposeful hand skills, impaired communication, deterioration in motor abilities, and the emergence of characteristic stereotypic hand movements such as hand wringing.

The diagnosis of RTT remains primarily clinical and is based on the revised international diagnostic criteria proposed by Neul et al. in 2010 [2]. Although molecular genetic testing can provide confirmatory evidence in most affected individuals, access to such testing remains limited in many low- and middle-income countries. In addition to the core diagnostic features, affected individuals may exhibit a range of supportive manifestations, including sleep disturbances, autonomic dysfunction, breathing abnormalities, growth impairment, and behavioral difficulties.

Self-injurious behavior (SIB), defined as repetitive self-directed behavior resulting in actual or potential tissue damage,[3] is well recognized in neurodevelopmental disorders and several genetic syndromes, and may constitute a significant management challenge.[4,5] Although SIB has been reported in individuals with RTT, it is not generally considered a dominant presenting feature and may obscure the underlying diagnosis, particularly when developmental regression is not immediately recognized, and access to genetic testing is limited.

We report a child with classic RTT in whom severe SIB was the dominant presenting complaint, resulting in diagnostic uncertainty. This case highlights how prominent SIB may mask the diagnosis of RTT and emphasizes the importance of careful clinical assessment in young girls presenting with developmental regression and behavioral disturbances, particularly in resource-constrained settings where access to molecular diagnostic testing is limited.

Case Description

A 26-month-old girl presented to the pediatric neurology clinic with a 13-month history of repeated biting of her left forearm, frequent head banging, persistent yelling, and marked restlessness. Her mother also reported the development of repetitive hand-wringing movements.

Further history revealed regression in acquired language and gross and fine motor skills. She was no longer able to use previously acquired words, walk steadily, or grasp objects such as a spoon during feeding. Additional symptoms included poor sleep, social withdrawal, aggression, such as biting her siblings, and intermittent episodes of rapid breathing.

She was born at 38 weeks gestation via spontaneous vaginal delivery following an uncomplicated pregnancy. Her birth weight was 3.2kg, while her birth head circumference was not available. The neonatal period was uneventful, and she was discharged 48 hours after birth. Development during the first year of life was reportedly normal. She achieved head control at 3 months, sat without support at 7 months, and walked independently at 12 months. She was able to feed herself with a spoon and had acquired several meaningful words before the onset of regression. At approximately 13 months of age, progressive loss of these acquired skills became apparent and was accompanied by the onset of stereotypic hand-wringing movements and SIB. She was the third child of non-consanguineous parents, both of whom had tertiary education. There was no family history of neurological, psychiatric, or genetic disorders.

On examination, she demonstrated poor social interaction, impaired purposeful hand use, and repetitive hand-wringing movements. Her gait was broad-based and ataxic. Repeated biting of the left forearm was observed during the consultation (Figure 1). A prominent hypertrophic scar with surrounding skin thickening was present on the left forearm and dorsum (Figure 2), consistent with recurrent self-inflicted biting.

There were no dysmorphic features. Her weight was 13 kg (between the 50th and 75th centiles), height was 82 cm (between the 3rd and 5th centiles), and occipitofrontal circumference was 47.2 cm (between the 25th and 50th centiles). As birth head circumference was unavailable, postnatal deceleration of head growth could not be assessed. The remainder of the neurological and systemic examination was unremarkable.

Electroencephalography and brain magnetic resonance imaging were normal. Molecular genetic testing for MECP2 mutation could not be performed because of its unavailability. Based on the revised diagnostic criteria proposed by Neul et al. in 2010, the patient fulfilled all four core criteria for classic RTT, and a diagnosis of RTT with SIB was made.

The parents were counseled regarding the nature of the disorder, limitations in diagnostic testing, and the need for multidisciplinary management. No psychotropic medications were prescribed. Although they initially defaulted from follow-up, subsequent engagement and psychological support facilitated re-enrolment into care. During six months of follow-up, attendance at behavioral therapy, occupational therapy, and physiotherapy sessions remained inconsistent because of poor clinic adherence. There was no recovery of the lost developmental skills. SIB and hand stereotypies persisted, although the child demonstrated improved sleep duration and a reduction in episodes of yelling and aggression.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge posed by an unusual presentation of RTT, in which SIB was the predominant presenting feature. Although pathogenic variants in MECP2 are identified in most individuals with classic RTT, the diagnosis remains primarily clinical. The revised diagnostic criteria proposed by Neul et al.[2] Require a period of developmental regression following apparently normal early development, with partial or complete loss of acquired purposeful hand skills and spoken language, gait abnormalities, and the emergence of stereotypic hand movements. In the present case, all four core criteria for classic RTT were fulfilled (Table 1). In addition, the patient exhibited several supportive features, including sleep disturbance, breathing abnormalities, growth impairment, and frequent episodes of unexplained screaming, all of which have been reported in natural-history studies and large clinical cohorts of individuals with RTT.[6]

Several neurodevelopmental and neurogenetic disorders may mimic RTT and should be considered in the differential diagnosis. These include autism spectrum disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder, Angelman syndrome, FOXG1-related disorder, Pitt–Hopkins syndrome, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, mitochondrial disorders, inherited metabolic diseases, and epileptic encephalopathies. In the present case, the history of apparently normal early development followed by developmental regression, loss of purposeful hand use and expressive language, gait abnormalities, and the emergence of characteristic hand-wringing stereotypies strongly supported a diagnosis of classic RTT. Other conditions associated with SIB, such as Lesch–Nyhan syndrome, Smith–Magenis syndrome, and Cornelia de Lange syndrome, were also considered. However, the absence of dysmorphic features, congenital anomalies, hyperuricemia, dystonia, systemic manifestations suggestive of an underlying metabolic disorder, and clinical or electroencephalographic evidence of epilepsy made these alternative diagnoses less likely. A notable feature in this patient was the early and severe presentation of SIB, which dominated the clinical picture and contributed to diagnostic uncertainty. Although SIB is a recognized behavioral manifestation in RTT, it is not included among the diagnostic criteria and rarely constitutes the principal presenting complaint. Consequently, clinicians may initially focus on behavioral or psychiatric explanations, resulting in delayed recognition of the underlying neurodevelopmental disorder. Previous studies have reported SIB in approximately 28–49% of individuals with RTT,[6] with hand biting being the most commonly reported form. However, large cohort studies suggest that internalizing behaviors, including anxiety, social withdrawal, and mood disturbances, are generally more prominent than externalizing behaviors such as aggression and SIB. [8] Cianfaglione et al.[7] reported lower rates of SIB in individuals with RTT compared with those with other causes of severe intellectual disability and found that factors associated with SIB were broadly similar across groups. Likewise, Buchanan et al.[8] observed that behavioral manifestations in RTT are heterogeneous and may vary according to clinical severity. Compared with these reports, the severe forearm biting and head banging observed in our patient represent a relatively uncommon behavioral phenotype and were sufficiently prominent to overshadow the characteristic features of RTT during the early stages of illness.

The coexistence of SIB, aggression, sleep disturbance, unexplained screaming, abnormal breathing patterns, and social withdrawal in this patient is consistent with the complex behavioral and autonomic phenotype described in previous RTT cohorts.[6] However, unlike many reported cases in which developmental regression and stereotypic hand movements are the dominant presenting features, behavioral disturbance was the principal reason for medical consultation in our patient. This unusual presentation underscores the phenotypic variability of RTT and highlights the need for clinicians to consider RTT in young girls presenting with persistent SIB, particularly when accompanied by developmental regression or loss of previously acquired skills.

The pathophysiology of SIB in RTT is multifactorial and incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include autonomic dysregulation, altered nociceptive processing, sensory-seeking behaviors, emotional dysregulation, and abnormalities in neurotransmitter systems resulting from MECP2 dysfunction. These mechanisms may interact with environmental factors to initiate and maintain SIB. In the present case, regression of language and developmental skills may have impaired communication and contributed to frustration and behavioral dysregulation. The associated sleep disturbance and autonomic abnormalities further support the concept of widespread neural network dysfunction underlying the behavioral manifestations of RTT.

Management of RTT with coexisting SIB is challenging and requires a multidisciplinary approach. Family counseling and psychological support are essential, particularly because behavioral symptoms often cause significant caregiver distress and may influence healthcare-seeking behavior. This was evident in the present case, where the parents discontinued follow-up in pursuit of alternative therapies, resulting in interruption of planned interventions. Behavioral interventions based on functional behavioral assessment remain central to management and aim to identify and modify factors that reinforce SIB. Pharmacological therapies may be useful for associated symptoms such as aggression, impulsivity, sleep disturbance, anxiety, or pain [6], while physiotherapy and occupational therapy play important roles in maintaining mobility and functional abilities. Although recent advances have expanded therapeutic options for RTT [9], treatment remains largely supportive and directed at symptom control.

Limitations

Molecular confirmation of an MECP2 pathogenic variant could not be obtained because genetic testing was unavailable.

Birth head circumference was not documented, precluding assessment of postnatal deceleration of head growth. Nevertheless, the patient fulfilled all four core diagnostic criteria for classic RTT and exhibited several supportive clinical features, making the diagnosis highly likely.

A formal behavioral assessment tool was not used to quantify the frequency or severity of SIB during follow-up. Parental reports and clinical observation were used.

Conclusion

This case expands the recognized clinical spectrum of RTT by demonstrating that severe SIB may dominate the initial presentation and obscure recognition of the underlying neurodevelopmental disorder. In resource-limited settings where access to molecular diagnostic testing is limited, careful attention to developmental history, developmental regression, and characteristic clinical features remains essential for timely diagnosis and appropriate multidisciplinary management.
