A Bibliometric Analysis of Publications on the Prevalence of Chronic Pain in Children and Adolescents From 2009 to 2023
Authors: Justine Dol, Christine T. Chambers, Jennifer A. Parker, Perri R. Tutelman, Brittany Cormier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBibliometric reviews explore patterns in publications in a given research area by exploring trends over time and the contributions by citations, such as relationships between authors and publications. Despite “chronic pain” being the second most common keyword in pain research, no bibliometric reviews have focused on publication trends related to the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents. A bibliometric analysis was conducted with articles included in a systematic review and meta‐analysis on the prevalence of pediatric chronic pain to identify the recent trajectory of the field and guide future directions. Publication bibliometrics data from the articles were extracted and analyzed (e.g., gender of authors, citation counts, and countries) and was visualized in VOSViewer. Among 119 studies, the number of publications per year ranged from 4 (2023) to 11 (2014, 2021) with an average of 8/year. Articles were cited on average 36.6 times (SD = 51.7, range 0–380) with 5058 unique citations. There were 74 different journals represented, with most publishing only 1 article (n = 52, 70%). Seventy countries were represented in prevalence data, 78% from high‐income countries; fifteen (21.4%) had only one data point, primarily from low‐ and lower‐middle income countries. There were 109 different corresponding authors, with only 1 corresponding author who had more than 2 published articles. There was relative gender equity in terms of first and corresponding author. There was little to no collaboration between author groups identified. Despite a steady number of articles published over the 14‐year period, the literature on the prevalence of pediatric chronic pain appears fragmented with articles published in a wide variety of journals. Prevalence data from low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries were under‐represented. Future work should focus on expanding evidence in underrepresented areas and greater collaboration among research groups to collect prevalence data in geographical areas where data gaps exist.

Introduction

Bibliometric analyses are used to explore patterns in publications in a given research area by exploring trends over time and the contributions by citations, such as relationships between authors and publications [1, 2]. Bibliometric analyses are critical literature review tools to measure the scientific outputs of papers, authors, journals, keywords, and countries, within a specific research field using numerical analysis and statistics on bibliometric indicators (e.g., number of publications, top authors and journals of the field, keyword analysis, and geographical representation) [3]. Unlike systematic reviews, bibliometric analyses use a macro‐level approach to reveal the organization and dynamics of a research field to provide groundwork to advance the field in a meaningful way [3].

Between 1975 and 2020, over 60 000 pain‐related articles were published, with a steady increase in the number of publications since the 1980's [4]. The field of pediatric pain consists of a subset of these studies, with 4256 pediatric pain publications identified between 1975 and 2010 [5]. Nearly 190 bibliometric analyses have been conducted on a variety of pain research topics, including a meta‐synthesis of bibliometrics on pain literature [6]. Many of these bibliometric reviews have focused on pain broadly [4], outputs in specific journals (e.g., Journal of Pain, PAIN) [4, 7], specific pain conditions [8, 9], or specific geographical locations [10, 11]. Only a few bibliometric reviews have focused on pediatric pain [5, 8, 12]. To date, none have focused on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents. This is despite a bibliometric analysis of keywords in pain research between 2017 and 2021 identifying “chronic pain” as the second most used word, after “pain” [13].

Chronic pain has significant impacts on children, parents, and society, with a recent meta‐analysis finding that one in five (20.8%) children experience chronic pain [14]. By conducting a bibliometric analysis on studies identified in Chambers et al.'s systematic review and meta‐analysis [14], it will advance our understanding of where the field has been and identify areas of research needed in the future. The objective was to conduct a bibliometric review on publications between 2009 and 2023 that reported on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents as a parallel analysis of an updated systematic review and meta‐analysis [14]. This analysis aimed to identify productivity in the research field of the prevalence of pediatric chronic pain and conduct a mapping of the scientific relationships between authors, journals, and countries. Specifically, this paper will explore: (1) publication bibliometrics (e.g., gender of authors, citation counts, and countries); and (2) visualization bibliometrics (e.g., co‐citation, keyword analysis) for studies that were identified as having reported on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents.

Methods

This study is a bibliometric review of articles identified as part of an updated systematic review on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents [14]. A modified bibliometric review approach was taken, only including articles identified in the Chambers et al. [14] review, rather than a broad look at the pain literature. This was done deliberately to map the patterns in publications in the research area of prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents by exploring trends over time and the contributions by citations. The associated systematic review identified 119 articles that were observational studies providing population‐based prevalence estimates for chronic pain in adolescents and children (aged ≤ 19 years) published in English between January 2009 and June 2023 [14]. The definition of chronic pain used in this review and meta‐analysis, consistent with the IASP definition and ICD‐11 [15], was: (1) pain with a minimum duration of at least 3 months; or (2) pain that is described as chronic, persistent, or recurrent with no timeframe reported. Studies reporting the prevalence of pediatric chronic pain were categorized into the following pain types: headache, abdominal pain, back pain, musculoskeletal pain, multisite/general pain, and other pain (e.g., fibromyalgia). Additional details relative to the systematic review and meta‐analysis, including data related to the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents, is available in Chambers et al. [14]. Consistent with bibliometric research methods, the present bibliometric review analysis was conducted on two aspects: (1) publication bibliometrics and (2) visualization bibliometrics.

Publication Bibliometrics

Data for the publication bibliometric analysis were extracted from the full text of included studies in the systematic review update (i.e., country of data collection, country of corresponding author, first author name, corresponding author name, year of publication, journal, and number of authors). Publication bibliometrics were extracted separately using a data extraction sheet and verified by another reviewer. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Income categories of countries was determined using the 2022 World Bank classification [16]. Low‐income economies are defined as those with a Gross National Income (GNI) per capita of $1135 or less in 2022; lower middle‐income economies are those with a GNI per capita between $1136 and $4465; upper middle‐income economies are those with a GNI per capita between $4466 and $13 845; high‐income economies are those with a GNI per capita of $13 846 or more [16].

Gender, defined as socially constructed roles, behaviors, expressions, and identities of girls, women, boys, men, and gender diverse people [17], of first author and corresponding author was identified through GenderAPI (https://gender‐api.com/). While there is no consistent approach to identify the gender of individuals by first name alone, GenderAPI is considered the most accurate tool available [18]. If an author's first name was missing on a publication, institutional websites, social media accounts, and/or internet search engines were searched to identify their full name prior to analysis. Only first names that had a 90% or above accuracy assessment from GenderAPI or were able to be confirmed manually were included in the analysis. Names that did not meet the 90% threshold were manually confirmed through image visualizations on institutional websites, social media accounts, and/or internet search engines.

To determine the number of citations per article, all included articles were identified and saved to a common list in Scopus (www.scopus.com). Four articles were not indexed in Scopus and were manually searched on Google Scholar (scholar.google.com) to collect citation numbers. Using Scopus, citations per article and overall were extracted on March 26, 2024, as was the CiteScore and SCImago Journal Rank (SJR) of each journal identified. According to Scopus, CiteScore measures the average citations received per document published in the journal while the SJR measures the weighted citations, dependent on subject field and prestige of the citing journal [19].

Visualization Bibliometrics

Data for the bibliometric visualization were conducted using VOSViewer [20]. Within the Scopus list, there were four articles that were not indexed and thus were not able to be included in this analysis component, leaving 115 studies included. Data for the visualization bibliometric analysis included networks of co‐authorship, co‐occurrence, and co‐citation, as well as co‐occurrence between keywords (excluding non‐relevant or generic terms). Co‐authorship analysis reveals the network between categories, while co‐citation analysis highlights similarities and networks in citations of papers [3]. Analysis of keywords was conducted based on text data using title and abstract as well as co‐occurrence of author‐provided keywords. In the output, the size of a node, or relationship, is indicative of the frequency of output, with larger nodes representing a higher number of outputs. Lines between nodes indicate connections between items. Nodes of the same color belong to a cluster of similar items as determined by VOSViewer.

Results

A total of 119 studies comprising 1 043 878 children (52% female, mean age 13.4 years [SD 2.4]) with chronic pain were included in the current bibliometric analysis. For additional details on the individual study characteristics, see Chambers et al. [14].

Publication Bibliometrics

Across the 14 years (2009–2023), the number of publications per year ranged from 4 (2023) to 11 (2014, 2021) with an average of 8 publications per year (see Figure 1). Of note, 2023 only included studies published prior to June 30, 2023, thus reflecting only half a year of publications. Studies were published in 74 unique journals, with most journals publishing 1 (n = 52, 70%) or 2 articles (n = 13, 18%). Cephalalgia published the greatest number of articles (n = 9, 12%). Among the 119 included studies, there were 109 different corresponding authors, and only one corresponding author had > 2 published articles (1.7%, M. Saps). All other corresponding authors had one or two articles. On average, there were 5.5 authors on each article (SD: 2.6, range 1–21). In 80 cases (67.2%), the first author and corresponding author were the same. Corresponding authors were from 41 different countries, with the most from the United States (n = 14), Brazil (n = 10), and Germany (n = 9). Two‐thirds of corresponding authors were from high‐income countries (n = 81, 68.1%), followed by upper‐middle income (n = 24, 20.2%), and the rest were from lower‐middle income countries (n = 14, 11.7%). No corresponding authors were from low‐income countries. Almost all articles had corresponding authors with affiliations in the country where the data were collected, except for 10 studies (8.4%). These 10 articles had corresponding authors in high‐income countries (e.g., United States, Netherlands) or upper‐middle income countries (e.g., Malaysia), with data collection mostly occurring in a lower income category (e.g., lower‐middle, or upper‐middle income country). Three mismatched studies had corresponding authors in high‐income countries, and data collection occurred in another high‐income country.

In the associated systematic review [14], there were a total of 448 prevalence data points due to several studies reporting prevalence data from multiple countries. Data originated from 70 different countries, with the highest number of data points coming from Finland and Germany (n = 19 each, 4.3%). Fifteen countries (3.3%) had only one data point. There were 350 data points from 45 high‐income countries (78.1%), 72 data points from 16 upper‐middle‐income countries (16.1%), and 26 data points from nine lower‐middle‐income countries (5.8%). No data points were available from low‐income countries. Using the region classification by the World Bank [16], 355 data points were from 41 countries in Europe and Central Asia (79.2%), 23 data points were from 6 countries in the Middle East and North Africa (5.1%), 20 data points were from 2 North American countries (4.4%), 20 data points were from 7 countries in Latin America & the Caribbean (4.4%), 17 data points were from 8 countries in East Asia & the Pacific (3.8%), 8 data points were from 3 South Asian countries (1.9%), and 5 data points were from 3 Sub‐Saharan African countries (1.2%). Gender Analysis

Among 127 unique first names of first and corresponding authors, 18 (14.2%) names had an accuracy estimated under 90% and 5 (3.9%) were unable to be categorized through GenderAPI. Of those 23 studies, 13 were verified by hand searching as accurate, while 5 were inaccurate/unknown and updated, and 5 were unable to be confirmed. As a result, there were 116 corresponding authors and 115 first authors that were analyzed. Among corresponding authors, there were 57 men (49.1%) and 59 women (50.9%) while among first authors, there were 54 men (47.0%) and 61 women (53.0%).

Citation Analysis

As of March 26, 2024, articles included in this review were cited on average 36.6 times (SD = 51.7, range 0–380) with a total of 4058 unique citations. This is an average citation count of 289.9 per year. The articles with the greatest number of citations are shown in Table 2. According to CiteScores (average citations received per document published in the journal, providing evidence of a journal's citation impact [19]), the three journals with the highest CiteScores were Clinical Gastroenterology & Hepatology (CiteScore = 14.9), PAIN (CiteScore = 12.5), and The Journal of Headache and Pain (CiteScore = 11.6). In terms of SJR weighted ranking, the top three journals were Clinical Gastroenterology & Hepatology (2.93), PAIN (2.45), and Journal of Adolescent Health (2.18).

In addition, a keyword term analysis was conducted that used all the words in the title and abstract to identify the most frequently used terms using a binary approach, where the presence or absence was counted, regardless of frequency, excluding structured abstract labels and copyright statements. Discussion

This bibliometric review provided evidence on the publication and visualization of bibliometric trends in a subset of studies published between 2009 and 2023 on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents as identified through a meta‐analysis published by Chambers et al. [14]. Among the 119 articles included, findings suggest that the field is fragmented, with limited overlap between authors and countries. Considering the wider pain literature, bibliometric analysis has emerged as a growing trend; nearly 190 publications related to bibliometrics and pain research were conducted, with most appearing after 2000 [6]. As the first bibliometric analysis in the field of chronic pediatric pain prevalence, this review provides significant insight into where the field has been and where it needs to go in the future.

For studies included in the current review, there were 109 different corresponding authors, yet only one author had more than two published articles, highlighting Miguel Saps as a leader in the field of pediatric chronic pain prevalence. Saps was also the highest co‐cited author and the author with the most citations, further solidifying their leadership in this field. When analyzing the overarching network of co‐authorship, most author groups did not appear to collaborate with others who published on this topic, indicating that there is disjointedness in authorship, with little collaboration across author groups in relation to pediatric chronic pain prevalence. This is not unique to the field of pediatric chronic pain prevalence research. Du et al. [33] found that in relation to fascia and pain, there was also no collaboration between authors, countries, or institutions. Collaboration between author groups is recommended to produce enhanced high‐quality research output. In relation to pediatric chronic pain prevalence, this is especially relevant to developing collaborative registries so that data can be examined on a larger, more meaningful scale.

In the current bibliometric analysis, most data points came from high‐income countries as well as those located in Europe and Central Asia. Furthermore, two‐thirds of corresponding authors were from high‐income countries; no corresponding authors were from low‐income countries. Other bibliometric analyses on pediatric pain [5] and pain broadly [34] have found that authorship tends to originate in high‐income countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada. The lack of representation in terms of corresponding authors from low‐income countries is not unexpected, given that no data points were available from low‐income countries, and only three countries had data from Sub‐Saharan Africa or South Asia, respectively. Thus, the evidence available on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents does not necessarily reflect the worldwide prevalence, but rather the prevalence in high‐income countries. Future work is needed to expand the knowledge as it relates to pediatric chronic pain prevalence in low, lower‐middle, and upper‐middle income countries. This is not the only bibliometric review on pain that has identified this gap, as others have also noted that the available data is skewed towards high‐income countries [34]. This may be due to factors such as lower research productivity or funding; yet, the evidence is necessary to provide a fuller picture of the global prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents. Given the significant number of children and adolescents in low and lower‐middle income countries, there is a need to understand the prevalence in these countries to advocate for better pain services and pain management [35]. To reach The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health Commission's four transformative goals in pediatric pain—to make pain matter, understood, visible, and better [36]—understanding the prevalence of pediatric chronic pain in all areas of the world is required to develop appropriate pain management strategies and interventions to improve outcomes for children and adolescents around the world.

The 119 articles were cited on average 36.6 times with a total of 4058 unique citations, which is an average citation count of 289.9 per year. Similar to the review by Caes et al. [5], which reviewed the bibliometrics of the pediatric pain literature more broadly, the top journals were not pediatric pain specific, but rather pain‐focused journals. In the current analysis, the top journals varied depending on the analysis approach taken; however, in all analyses, similar to Caes et al. [5], PAIN was in the top three journals. Of particular interest is that the journals with the greatest number of publications (i.e., Cephalalgia, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders, and Headache) were not the journals with the highest CiteScores (i.e., Clinical Gastroenterology & Hepatology, PAIN, and The Journal of Headache and Pain). This suggests that articles are likely being published in journals that reflect their chronic pain area (e.g., headache, musculoskeletal disorders) rather than a focus on impact score. This also may be related to the dominance of adult pain in comparison to pediatric pain, which are highly cited in journals such as PAIN [7].

In this bibliometric review, the gender split for first and corresponding authors was relatively equal. This differs from Zhong et al.'s bibliometric analysis on women's authorship in pain research broadly, which found that women authors were significantly more likely to be first (46.7%) than senior authors (30.5%). They noted that authorship was moving towards more gender parity between 2002 and 2021. Thus, the difference in findings could be related to three factors: the definition used of senior/corresponding author, the time of data collection, and focus on pain broadly versus pediatric chronic pain. First, for the current bibliometric analysis, corresponding author was defined as the senior author as publication norms vary across disciplines, and the corresponding author is the one expected to maintain all ownership of the work. However, other bibliometric reviews, including Zhong et al. [37], defined senior author as last author, which is consistent with the medical field. Thus, differences may have arisen due to these classifications. Secondly, Zhong et al. [37] noted that gender parity improved between 2002 and 2021. Given that this bibliometric analysis only included articles published after 2009, this may have captured the trend towards more gender parity in authorship. Finally, there may have been differences in gender based on the specific focus on pediatric chronic pain, compared to overall pain publications. Overall, the field of publications as it relates to the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents reflects gender parity in terms of both first and corresponding authorship.

Strengths and Limitations

While this study has several strengths, including using standardized tools (e.g., Scopus, Gender API) and a rigorous review methodology that was used to ensure only publications relevant to the topic were analyzed, it was a secondary analysis of a recently updated systematic review, and as a result, only studies published between 2009 and 2023 included in that meta‐analysis were analyzed. This resulted in only 119 studies included in this bibliometric review. This differs from other bibliometric methods [2, 3] which may have a broader scope, and therefore more publications. As a result, it may not fully reflect historical trends in the field of prevalence research as it relates to chronic pain in children and adolescents. Nevertheless, this analysis does provide insight into the current publication trends as they relate to the field of research around the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents, which was its purpose. For the determination of gender, authors were not contacted to confirm self‐reported gender, nor were other genders included in the analysis, limiting the ability to provide a full commentary on the gender diversity of authorship in this field.

Future Directions and Recommendations

Based on the findings from this bibliometric review, two key recommendations can be made for future research in the field of prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents. First, there is a need for more evidence from low‐income countries as well as authorship from countries other than high‐income countries. Future work should expand the exploration of the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents in these countries to further the field in relation to equity, diversity, and inclusion. Second, there is a need for more collaboration between research groups working on collecting data on the prevalence of chronic pain in children and adolescents. This will strengthen the field and enhance output through greater collaboration of research groups. It is also recommended that research groups working in high‐income countries should collaborate with research groups in low‐income countries to collect prevalence data in areas where data gaps exist.

Conclusions

Despite a steady number of publications over a period of 14 years with good citation evidence, the literature on the prevalence of pediatric chronic pain is fragmented. Articles were published in a wide variety of journals with little to no collaboration between author groups identified. Many countries were under‐ or not represented, particularly in low and lower‐middle‐income countries in both prevalence data and representation from corresponding authors. Future work should focus on bringing together this important topic in pediatric pain and expanding evidence in underrepresented areas.
