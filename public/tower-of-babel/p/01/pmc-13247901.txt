Immune-mediated tumor regression and subsequent escape driven by antigen loss and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I downregulation following GPC3 DNA vaccination in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Hang Li, Xudong Wang, Zhentao Yan, Siyuan Weng, Wendi Kang, Junqing Xi, Zhengqiang Yang
Published: 2026

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide (1). Glypican-3 (GPC3) has emerged as an attractive immunotherapy target due to its high expression in HCC and minimal expression in normal tissues (2). Previous therapeutic strategies, such as GPC3-targeted peptide vaccines, have shown acceptable safety but limited clinical efficacy (3). GPC3 plasmid DNA vaccines offer a novel approach to circumvent these limitations through sustained antigen expression and enhanced immunogenicity (4).

Our preclinical data validated the immunogenicity and efficacy of an XCL1/GPC3 fusion DNA vaccine (Figure S1 and Table S1) (5). Building upon this foundation, we now delineate the first-in-human clinical application of this GPC3-targeted plasmid DNA vaccine. This report details the clinical course of a patient with advanced, treatment-refractory HCC. Our objective is to document the vaccine’s therapeutic potential while also using histopathological analysis to investigate the adaptive mechanisms of immune escape that evolved under its selective pressure. A patient in the 50s was diagnosed with a 4-cm hepatic mass during routine screening in March 2021. Laparoscopic right hepatectomy with cholecystectomy was performed in April 2021, revealing a 6.5-cm HCC with focal necrosis, hemorrhage, and microvascular invasion (MVI-M1), but no capsular involvement or satellite nodules. The tumor approached but did not involve the resection margin (Figure 1A). Despite prophylactic transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) in June 2021, multifocal intrahepatic recurrence emerged by March 2022. The patient underwent sequential systemic therapies including: (I) six cycles of hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab and sintilimab (April 2022–August 2023), achieving initial response (1.2–0.4 cm) followed by progression (1.0 cm); (II) tislelizumab with regorafenib plus TACE, resulting in continued progression with the dominant lesion reaching 1.8 cm by March 2024.

Following failure of conventional treatments, the patient received compassionate-use XCL1/GPC3 plasmid DNA vaccine (NWRD06) between March and August 2024 (Appendix 1). The treatment protocol comprised six intramuscular injections (4 mg each) delivered. Treatment was well-tolerated with only mild injection-site discomfort (visual analog scale 2–4) and no systemic adverse events. Post-vaccination, the patient continued lenvatinib plus tislelizumab maintenance through April 2025.

A profound radiological effect was observed via dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Serial arterial-phase imaging, which delineates viable tumor tissue, documented a progressive decrease in arterial hyperenhancement after vaccination. The target lesion, initially characterized by avid enhancement, transitioned to a peripheral rim-enhancing pattern. Ultimately, all arterial enhancement completely resolved from August 2024 onward (Figure 1B). This radiological evolution signified a complete response (CR) according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST). In contrast, evaluation based on RECIST 1.1, which relies on dimensional measurements, categorized the response as stable disease (SD) due to insufficient volumetric reduction (Figure 1C,1D).

We conducted longitudinal monitoring of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using an enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assay to quantify the systemic, antigen-specific cellular immune response (Appendix 1). It increased from a baseline of 800 spot-forming units (SFU) per 106 PBMCs to a peak exceeding 8,000 SFU/106 PBMCs at month 20 (Figure 2A,2B).

Comparative immunohistochemical analysis between primary [2021] and post-vaccination recurrent [2024] tumors revealed two critical escape mechanisms (Appendix 1). First, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I expression significantly diminished, impairing T-cell recognition capacity (Figure 2C,2D). Second, GPC3 expression decreased from strong (3+) to weak (1+), suggesting antigen loss under immune selection pressure.

In this case, a notable finding is the discrepancy observed in radiological assessments following GPC3 DNA vaccine therapy. According to mRECIST criteria, the patient achieved CR, as evidenced by the disappearance of arterial enhancement within the tumor. Conversely, evaluation based on RECIST 1.1 criteria, which quantifies tumor dimensions, indicated SD due to the absence of significant volumetric reduction. This divergence suggests a critical biological phenomenon: the vaccine-induced immune response demonstrated sufficient potency to eliminate viable, vascularized tumor components, yet failed to achieve complete volumetric clearance. This outcome substantiates the central hypothesis that while the vaccine successfully elicited a robust systemic immune response, as evidenced by elevated interferon gamma (IFN-γ) levels, its ultimate local efficacy was constrained by immune escape mechanisms that evolved within the tumor microenvironment.

The pathological findings elucidate two key immune escape mechanisms that explain this incomplete tumor clearance. First, antigen loss under immune selective pressure: GPC3 expression declined substantially from 3+ in the primary tumor to 1+ in the post-treatment lesion, effectively rendering GPC3-specific T cells unable to recognize and eliminate surviving tumor cells. This exemplifies classic cancer immunoediting, wherein therapeutic pressure eliminates antigen-positive cells while selecting for survival and proliferation of antigen-negative or low-expressing resistant clones (6). This dynamic evolution of antigen expression represents a major challenge for targeted immunotherapies, including cancer vaccines (7). Second, downregulation of MHC class I molecules significantly impaired T-cell recognition and cytotoxic activity against tumor antigens. This well-documented immune evasion strategy may result from genetic deletions, mutations, epigenetic modifications, or transcriptional alterations (8). From an evolutionary perspective, MHC class I downregulation represents a consequence of T cell-mediated clonal selection, favoring tumor clones that evade T cell-mediated immunity (9). While neoantigen-specific CD4+ T cells retain some antitumor efficacy, the therapeutic potential of T cell-based vaccines is significantly compromised in MHC class I-deficient tumors While neoantigen-specific CD4+ T cells retain some antitumor efficacy, the therapeutic potential of T cell-based vaccines is significantly compromised in MHC class I-deficient tumors (10). Many malignancies escape immune surveillance through MHC class I downregulation, making the restoration of MHC class I expression a crucial strategy for enhancing vaccine efficacy. Studies have demonstrated that approaches to upregulate MHC class I expression may involve TLR agonists, STING agonists, and cytokines including TNF, IL-1, and type I and II interferons (10). Therefore, combining vaccine therapy with treatments capable of upregulating MHC class I expression could represent a promising therapeutic approach.

Several limitations of this single-case report must be acknowledged. A principal limitation is the potential for confounding effects from the concurrent administration of lenvatinib and tislelizumab after the vaccination series. However, the patient’s history of relentless disease progression on prior immunotherapies, followed by a rapid clinical response immediately after vaccination, strongly supports our interpretation. We hypothesize that the DNA vaccine functioned as a potent immunomodulatory priming agent. It likely generated a robust repertoire of tumor-specific T cells that synergized with the subsequent checkpoint blockade and anti-angiogenic therapy. Additional limitations include the single-patient nature of observation, which precludes definitive conclusions about causality, and the potential for sampling bias in the histopathological analysis. Therefore, prospective, multi-center clinical trials with robust translational endpoints are warranted to corroborate these preliminary findings. Future studies should be designed to systematically evaluate the vaccine’s efficacy, both as a monotherapy and in combination, and to longitudinally profile the dynamic co-evolution of the tumor and the immune response.

In conclusion, this study presents the first clinical success of GPC3-targeted plasmid DNA vaccination in HCC, demonstrating robust systemic immune activation and effective elimination of viable tumor tissue. These findings establish a promising foundation for advancing DNA vaccine-based immunotherapy strategies in HCC.
