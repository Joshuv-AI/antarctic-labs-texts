Inquisitive Neighborhood Logic
Authors: Ivano Ciardelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explore an inquisitive modal logic designed to reason about neighborhood models. This logic is based on an inquisitive strict conditional operator ⇛, which quantifies over neighborhoods, and which can be applied to both statements and questions. In terms of this operator we also define two unary modalities ⊞ and , which act respectively as a universal and existential quantifier over neighborhoods. We prove that the expressive power of this logic matches the natural notion of bisimilarity in neighborhood models. We show that certain fragments of the language are invariant under certain modifications of the set of neighborhoods, and use this to show that ⇛ is not definable from ⊞ and , and that questions embedded under ⇛ are indispensable. We provide a sound and complete axiomatization of our logic, both in general and in restriction to some salient frame classes, establish decidability via the finite model property, and discuss the relations between our logic and other modal logics interpreted over neighborhood models.Keywords: Inquisitive logic, Modal logic, Neighborhood semantics, Bisimulation, Strict conditional

Introduction

A neighborhood model is a modal structure where each world w is associated with a set Σ(w) of neighborhoods, where each neighborhood is a set of worlds. As an illustration, Fig. 1 shows the neighborhoods of three worlds, w1,w2,w3: the neighborhoods of w1 are the four singletons, {wpq},{wpq¯},{wp¯q},{wpq¯}, where the subscript on a world represents the corresponding propositional valuation; the neighborhoods of w2 are the same four singletons as well as their union, {wpq,wpq¯,wp¯q,wpq¯}; finally, the neighborhoods of w3 include, in addition, also the sets {wpq,wpq¯} and {wp¯q,wpq¯}.

Sets of neighborhoods associated with three worlds

Like Kripke models, neighborhood models can be given many concrete interpretations. Among others, they have been used to interpret logics of evidence and evidence-based belief (van Benthem et al., 2014), and to define an inquisitive generalization of epistemic logic in which agents are curious, i.e., they do not just possess certain information, but also entertain certain questions (Ciardelli & Roelofsen, 2015). A special class of neighborhood models, sphere models, provides the most popular semantics for counterfactual conditionals (Lewis, 1973) and is standardly employed to model rational belief revision (Board, 2004; Baltag & Smets, 2006). Another special class, topological models, provides a natural semantics for spatial reasoning (Aiello et al., 2003). Furthermore, neighborhood models emerge naturally in the context of reasoning about the abilities of individuals and groups in a strategic setting (Brown, 1988; Pauly, 2002).

In light of these many interpretations, it is not surprising that there is a long tradition of work using neighborhood models to provide semantics for modal logic (see Pacuit, 2017, for an overview). In particular, two different neighborhood-based semantics for standard modal logic are well-established in the literature. According to the so-called Scott-Montague semantics (Scott, 1970; Montague, 1970), □α is true at a world w if the truth-set |α|M, the set of worlds in the model M where α is true, is a neighborhood of w.

M,w⊧□α⟺|α|M∈Σ(w).

According to a different approach, rooted in the topological semantics of Tarski (1938) and McKinsey and Tarski (1944), (see Chellas, 1980; Brown, 1988; Aiello et al., 2003; Hansen, 2003, for modern formulations), □α is true at w if α is true everywhere in some neighborhood of w. We refer to this as the ∃∀-semantics.

M,w⊧□α⟺∃s∈Σ(w)s.t.∀v∈s:M,v⊧α.

The two clauses coincide in the context of upward-monotone neighborhood models, i.e., models in which every superset of a neighborhood is itself a neighborhood, but they come apart in the general case.

As it happens, however, the standard modal language with either of the above clauses does not allow us to express much about the configurations that arise in the neighborhoods of a given world. For instance, the situation at worlds w1,w2, and w3 above is clearly different in some salient respects: for instance, every neighborhood of w1 settles whether p is true or false (i.e., the truth value of p is constant within each neighborhood) while this is not the case for w2 and w3; moreover, every neighborhood of w2 that settles whether p also settles whether q, whereas this is not the case for w3. Surprisingly, these simple facts cannot be expressed in the standard modal language with either of the above semantics.

A way to bring the problem more sharply into focus is to think about the notion of behavioral equivalence in neighborhood models. Intuitively, it seems natural to regard two worlds as behaviorally equivalent if they validate the same atomic formulas and every neighborhood of the one is behaviorally equivalent to some neighborhood of the other; in turn, it seems natural to regard two neighborhoods as behaviorally equivalent when each world in the one is behaviorally equivalent to some world in the other. This leads naturally to a notion of bisimilarity for neighborhood models, analogous to the standard notion of bisimilarity that plays a key role in the setting of relational semantics.

Given the usefulness of standard modal logic in describing bisimulation-invariant properties of relational models, it seems worthwhile to develop a modal logic to describe the bisimulation-invariant properties of neighborhood models. The expressive power of this logic should match the notion of neighborhood bisimilarity in the same sense in which the expressive power of standard modal logic matches the standard notion of bisimilarity. In particular, bisimilarity should guarantee modal equivalence, and the opposite should hold as well in the context of finite models over a finite set of atoms.

Neither of the above interpretations of the standard modal language satisfies this desideratum. The Scott-Montague semantics is not bisimulation-invariant, since it is inherently non-local: the truth of □α at a world w depends on the truth value of the argument α across the entire model, and not only locally in the neighborhoods of w; as a consequence, the truth of □α may fail to be preserved even under the simple operation of taking the disjoint union of two models. The ∃∀-semantics, by contrast, is bisimulation-invariant, but it fails to distinguish non-bisimilar worlds even in a finite setting. For instance, the three worlds in Fig. 1 are not bisimilar to one another, and the difference shows up already after a single modal transition: for instance, w3 has a neighborhood, {wpq,wpq¯}, which is not behaviorally equivalent to any neighborhood of w1 or w2; nevertheless, it is easy to see (cf. also Proposition 4.3 below) that these three worlds satisfy exactly the same modal formulas under the ∃∀-semantics.

Recently, a modal logic that satisfies the above desiderata has been proposed by van Benthem et al. (2017): their approach, called instantial neighborhood logic, is based on modal formulas of the form □(β1,⋯,βn;α), where n≥0, which are true at a world w if there is a neighborhood of w where α is true everywhere, and each βi is true somewhere.

In this paper, we explore a very different way to set up a modal logic with the desired level of expressive power. Our approach is based on the framework of inquisitive logic (Ciardelli et al., 2018; Ciardelli, 2023), which means that our logical language contains not only formulas regimenting statements, as usual, but also formulas regimenting questions. For instance, our language includes, next to an atom p formalizing the statement “that p”, also the formula ?p, formalizing the question “whether p”. To the standard language of inquisitive propositional logic, our logic adds a binary modality ⇛, read ‘yields’. This modality is essentially a strict conditional operator, except for the fact that it quantifies not over worlds, but over neighborhoods; this is possible since in inquisitive logic formulas can be evaluated directly relative to a set of worlds, in terms of a relation called support.1 Using the same symbol ⊧ both for truth at a world (on the left) and for support at a set of worlds (on the right), the semantics of our modality can be stated as follows:

M,w⊧φ⇛ψ⟺∀s∈Σ(w):M,s⊧φimpliesM,s⊧ψ.

Crucially, the modality ⇛ may be applied not only to statements, but also to questions. For instance, the claim “every neighborhood settles whether p” (true at w1 but not at w2,w3 in Fig. 1) can be expressed by the formula ⊤⇛?p, whereas the claim “every neighborhood that settles whether p also settles whether q” (true at w1,w2 but not at w3) can be expressed as ?p⇛?q. Thus, by means of modal formulas that involve embedded questions, the worlds w1,w2, and w3 from Fig. 1 can be distinguished.

In terms of the modality ⇛, we may define two unary modalities, ⊞ (‘window’) and (‘kite’), which function respectively as universal and existential quantifier over neighborhoods. Previous work on inquisitive modal logic (see, a.o., Ciardelli and Roelofsen, 2015; Ciardelli, 2016; Ciardelli and Otto, 2021; Meißner and Otto, 2022; Punčochář and Sedlár, 2021; Marić and Perkov, 2024) has focused on a language based on the modality ⊞. As Ciardelli and Otto (2021) have shown, this language is expressively adequate for bisimulation-invariant properties in the setting of downward-monotone neighborhood models, i.e., models where every subset of a neighborhood is itself a neighborhood. The focus on downward-monotone models in this literature was motivated by the particular interpretation of neighborhood models that first drove the development of inquisitive modal logic, namely, the inquisitive-epistemic interpretation (Ciardelli & Roelofsen, 2015). However, as discussed above, neighborhood models admit many interpretations; if one wants to view inquisitive modal logic as a tool to reason about neighborhood models in general, a language based on ⊞ is no longer expressively adequate; for instance, this language would not be able distinguish worlds w2 and w3 in Fig. 1 (in fact, as we shall see, even a language with both ⊞ and cannot distinguish these worlds, cf. Proposition 4.3). The logic explored in this paper can thus be seen as a generalization of standard inquisitive modal logic which is suitable for reasoning about neighborhood models which are not necessarily downward-monotone. This makes it possible to explore a much broader spectrum of concrete interpretations for inquisitive modal logic.

In this paper, we explore in detail two aspects of the inquisitive modal logic InqML⇛: its expressive power, and its axiomatization.

With respect to expressive power, we will show that our logic matches the natural notion of bisimulation for neighborhood models in exactly the same sense in which standard modal logic matches the notion of bisimulation relative to Kripke models. In a slogan:

In particular, over a finite set of atoms, a property of worlds is definable in InqML⇛ just in case it is invariant under some finite degree of bisimilarity. Moreover, two worlds in a finite model are bisimilar if and only if they agree on all modal formulas.

With respect to axiomatization, we will show that the logic of ⇛ is characterized by four principles: (i) if φ entails ψ, then φ⇛ψ is a logical validity; (ii) ⇛ is transitive; (iii) if an antecedent φ yields two consequents, it yields their conjunction; (iv) if a consequent ψ is yielded by two antecedents, it is yielded by their inquisitive disjunction. Moreover, we will show that some salient conditions on neighborhood frames are captured by natural modal axioms (in other cases, the problem of finding a suitable axiom remains open).

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 covers some preliminaries on inquisitive propositional and modal logic; Section 3 introduces the logic InqML⇛ and illustrates it with some examples; Section 4 shows that certain fragments of InqML⇛ are invariant under certain modifications of the set of neighborhoods; this is used to prove that ⇛ is not definable from ⊞ and and that questions embedded on the right of ⇛ are indispensable; Sect. 5 characterizes the expressive power of InqML⇛ in terms of bisimilarity; Sect. 6 provides an axiomatization of InqML⇛ and establishes its strong completeness by constructing a canonical model; through a finitary adaptation of this canonical model construction, Sect. 7 shows that InqML⇛ has the finite model property, and thereby establishes its decidability; Sect. 8 extends the completeness result by giving canonical axioms for some salient frame conditions; Sect. 9 discusses the treatment of the empty neighborhood; Sect. 10 relates InqML⇛ to the instantial neighborhood logic of van Benthem et al. (2017); Section 11 concludes by outlining directions for future work.

Definition 2.5

(Declarative operators) A n-ary operator M is said to be declarative if the formula M(φ1,⋯,φn) is truth-conditional for any φ1,⋯,φn∈LM.

Note that in order to define the semantics of a declarative modality M, it suffices to give the truth conditions for formulas M(φ1,⋯,φn); the corresponding support conditions will then simply say that the formula is supported at s if it is true at each world w∈s.

From now on, when we speak of an inquisitive modal logic InqMLM we will assume that M is a set of declarative modalities. Such a logic is guaranteed to preserve the key properties of support, namely, persistence and the empty state property. Monotonicity Properties of Fragments

A couple of questions may arise naturally at this point. Is it really necessary to base our language on the binary modality ⇛? Could we, instead, take the unary modalities ⊞ and as primitives? Also, is it crucial that the modality ⇛ is added to an underlying inquisitive logic? Could we, instead, just add ⇛ to standard propositional logic?

Bisimilarity and Expressive Power

In this section, we introduce the natural notion of bisimilarity for neighborhood models (cf. also van Benthem et al., 2017; Ciardelli and Otto, 2021), and characterize the expressive power of InqML⇛ in terms of it.

Bisimilarity. The notion of bisimilarity captures the idea of behavioral equivalence. In the setting of neighborhood models, it is natural to regard two worlds as behaviorally equivalent when they agree on atomic propositions and every neighborhood of the one is behaviorally equivalent to a neighborhood of the other. In turn, it is natural to regard two neighborhoods as behaviorally equivalent when each world in the one is behaviorally equivalent to a world in the other. This leads naturally to a notion of bisimilarity, which may be formalized equivalently in two ways: by defining a notion of bisimulation relation, or in terms of a bisimulation game. We start with the former approach.

Bisimilarity can also be given an alternative characterization in terms of a bisimulation game with two players: Spoiler and Duplicator. The game alternates between world-positions ⟨w,w′⟩∈W×W′ and state-positions ⟨s,s′⟩∈℘(W)×℘(W′). Playing from a world-position, Spoiler picks a neighborhood of either world and Duplicator responds with a neighborhood of the other world, leading to a state-position. Playing from a state-position, Spoiler picks a world in either state and Duplicator responds with a world in the other state. A move in which a state is selected is called a state move; a move in which a world is selected is called a world move. The game ends if one of the players is unable to make a move, in which case the other player wins, or if a world-position is reached where the worlds disagree on the truth of some atomic sentence, in which case Spoiler wins; infinite runs of the game are won by Duplicator. In addition to the unbounded version of the game, in which the match is allowed to go on indefinitely, we also consider its n-round versions for n∈N, in which Spoiler is allowed to make at most n state moves.

Now we can say that two world-pointed models ⟨M,w⟩ and ⟨M′,w′⟩ are bisimilar (respectively, n-bisimilar) if Duplicator has a winning strategy in the unbounded game (respectively, n-round game) starting from the position ⟨w,w′⟩. Two state-pointed models ⟨M,s⟩ and ⟨M′,s′⟩ are bisimilar (n-bisimilar) if Duplicator has a winning strategy in the unbounded (n-round) game starting from ⟨s,s′⟩. We leave it to the reader to verify that this characterization of (n-)bisimilarity coincides with the one given earlier in terms of (n-)bisimulation relations.

As in the case of relational semantics, bisimilarity implies n-bisimilarity for each n∈N, but not conversely: two worlds can be n-bisimilar for each n∈N, and yet not bisimilar. A straightforward adaptation of the standard example from Kripke semantics suffices to make the point.

(Modal equivalence) Two world-pointed models ⟨M,w⟩ and ⟨M′,w′⟩ are modally equivalent (notation: M,w≡M′,w′) if they make true the same formulas of InqML⇛, and n-modally equivalent (M,w≡nM′,w′) if they make true the same formulas of modal depth up to n. Similarly for state-pointed models ⟨M,s⟩ and ⟨M′,s′⟩, where “make true” is replaced by “support”.

The first result we will show is that, provided the set P of atoms is finite, n-bisimilarity coincides precisely with n-modal equivalence—that is, the worlds (or states) that can be distinguished by means of formulas of modal depth up to n are precisely those that exhibit a behavioral difference within n steps.

One direction of the result, which does not require the finiteness of P, can be proved by a simple induction; we omit the straightforward proof.

Proposition 5.6

(n-bisimilarity implies n-modal equivalence)

M,w∼nM′,w′ implies M,w≡nM′,w′

M,w∼nM′,w′ implies M,w≡nM′,w′

M,s∼nM′,s′ implies M,s≡nM′,s′.

M,s∼nM′,s′ implies M,s≡nM′,s′.

Since full bisimilarity implies n-bisimilarity for each n, as an immediate corollary we get that InqML⇛ as a whole is bisimulation invariant.

Corollary 5.7

(Bisimulation invariance)

M,w∼M′,w′ implies M,w≡M′,w′

M,w∼M′,w′ implies M,w≡M′,w′

M,s∼M′,s′ implies M,s≡M′,s′

M,s∼M′,s′ implies M,s≡M′,s′

By using the characteristic formulas χM,wn, we can show that modal equivalence on formulas of modal depth n implies n-bisimilarity, both at the level of worlds and at the level of information states. In other words, any behavioral difference that shows up within n modal transition steps can be detected by a formula of modal depth n.

Theorem 5.11

(Over finite languages, n-modal equivalence implies n-bisimilarity) If the set of atoms P is finite, we have:

M,w≡nM′,w′ implies M,w∼nM′,w′

M,w≡nM′,w′ implies M,w∼nM′,w′

M,s≡nM′s′ implies M,s∼nM′,s′.

M,s≡nM′s′ implies M,s∼nM′,s′.

Proof

Suppose M,w≡nM′,w′. Since M′,w′⊧χM′,w′n and χM′,w′n has modal depth n, we have M,w⊧χM′,w′n, which by the previous lemma implies M,w∼nM′,w′.

Next, consider the claim for information states. Suppose M,s≁nM′,s′. This means that some w∈s is not n-bisimilar to any w′∈s′, or vice versa. Without loss of generality, suppose the former is the case. Then by the previous lemma, for every w′∈s′ we have M′,w′⊧¬χM,wn. Since ¬χM,wn is a declarative, and thus truth-conditional, it follows that M′,s′⊧¬χM,wn. However, M,w⊭¬χM,wn, and since w∈s, by persistence M,s⊭¬χM,wn. Since ¬χM,wn is a formula of modal depth n, this shows that M,s≢nM′,s′. □

It is worth noting that the situation for worlds and states is not fully analogous: in the case of worlds, M,w≁nM′,w′ implies that there is a formula of modal depth n that is true at w but not at w′ and one that is true at w′ but not at w. In the case of states, M,s≁nM′,s′ only implies that there is a formula of modal depth n supported by s but not s′, or one supported by s′ but not by s. Indeed, even if M,s≁nM′,s′ it may be that every formula supported by s is also supported by s′; in particular, this happens by persistence when M=M′ and s′⊂s.

As in standard modal logic, modal equivalence does not in general guarantee full bisimilarity: by Theorem 5.11, modal equivalence amounts to n-bisimilarity for each n, which is strictly weaker than full bisimilarity, as shown by Example 5.4. However, what we can prove is an analogue of the classical Hennessy-Milner theorem. To state this analogue, we first need to adapt the notion of image-finiteness to neighborhood models.

Definition 5.12

(Image-finiteness) A neighborhood model M=⟨W,Σ,V⟩ is image-finite if for every world w, the union ⋃Σ(w) of its neighborhoods is finite. Equivalently, M is image-finite if the underlying Kripke model MK is image-finite.

Note that image-finiteness is equivalent to the conjunction of the following two conditions: (i) each world has finitely many neighborhoods and (ii) each neighborhood is finite.

The counterpart of the Hennessy-Milner theorem can now be stated as follows.

Corollary 5.17

Proof

Suppose P is finite and fix n∈N. By Observation 5.9, there are only finitely many formulas of the form χM,wn. Thus, there are only finitely many inquisitive disjunctions of such formulas without repetitions. By the proof of Theorem 5.16, any formula in L⇛n is equivalent to such a disjunction, so the number of equivalence classes of formulas in L⇛n is finite. □

Axiomatization

Having explored the expressive power of InqML⇛, we now turn to its axiomatization. In this section, we provide a sound and complete Hilbert-style proof system for the logic InqML⇛, as interpreted over the class of all in-models. In Sect. 8 we then turn to the logic of some specific classes of models. Allowing Empty Neighborhoods

We have so far been assuming that neighborhoods are nonempty. What happens if we drop this restriction, allowing the empty set to count as a neighborhood? A couple of remarks on this translation. First, note that given a formula σ∈LINL, the size of σ∗ grows linearly on the size of σ. By contrast, since the number of resolutions of a formula φ∈L grows exponentially in the length of φ due to the clause for implication, the size of the translation α⋆ of a formula α∈L⇛⊙! may grow exponentially relative to the size of α. It seems natural to conjecture that this is inevitable for such a translation, and thus that InqML⇛⊙ is exponentially more succinct than INL. However, we will not try to provide a proof of this conjecture here. It is also worth noting that this translation strategy, that relies crucially on resolutions, would not be viable in the setting of inquisitive predicate logic, where no analogue of resolutions is available. It is natural to conjecture that a first-order version of InqML⇛⊙ would be strictly more expressive than a first-order version of INL. For instance, one challenge would be to translate the inquisitive modal formula ⊞(∀x?Px→∀x?Qx), which says that in every neighborhood, the extension of Q is functionally determined by the extension of P.

Further Work

The work described in this paper can be taken further in various directions.

First, the mathematical theory of InqML⇛ should be developed further. In particular, it would be interesting to compare the expressive power of InqML⇛ with that of first-order predicate logic over neighborhood frames, when the latter are encoded as two-sorted structures with distinct domains for worlds and neighborhoods: do we have an analogue of van Benthem’s theorem, stating that every bisimulation-invariant first-order formula is equivalent to a modal formula? In previous work (Ciardelli & Otto, 2021) this question was answered positively for InqML⊞ over downward-monotone models, and it seems that the proof can be generalized smoothly to InqML⇛ over arbitrary models. Alternatively, one could get this result from the analogous result established by de Groot (2022) for instantial neighborhood logic, via the translations established in Sect. 10.

As mentioned at the end of Sect. 8, the modal logic of many interesting frame classes remains to be worked out. Relatedly, it seems interesting to consider modal formulas at the level of frames, developing a modal correspondence theory for InqML⇛. It would be interesting, for instance, to determine what classes of neighborhood frames are definable by modal formulas (in the spirit of the Goldblatt-Thomason theorem for Kripke frames), and to what extent from a modal formula we can cook up a corresponding (two-sorted) first-order formula expressing the same frame condition (in the spirit of Sahlqvist theory).

In a different direction, it would be relevant to explore concrete interpretations of InqML⇛, and extensions motivated by such interpretations. For instance, it seems interesting to study a multi-agent version of InqML⇛ interpreted over concurrent game models (see Bulling et al., 2016, for an overview). In a concurrent game model, worlds represent stages of a dynamic process unfolding over time; at each world w, each agent may choose between multiple actions available to them, and the choices of all agents jointly determine the world that will be realized next. A concurrent game model is naturally associated with a multi-agent neighborhood model in the following way: for each world w and each action x available to an agent a at w, we may collect the set outa(x,w) of outcomes that may be realized if a performs x at w. We then define a neighborhood structure where Σa(w) contains as neighborhoods the outcome sets outa(x,w) for each action x available to a at w.
