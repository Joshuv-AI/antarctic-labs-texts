Non-enzymatic glucose detection via ordered 2D arrays of nickel and nickel chitosan nanowires
Authors: Vicente Gonzales, Chris Rightsell, Alejandro Morales Betancourt, Kelly L Nash
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucose sensors play a vital role in the everyday healthcare needs of diabetic patients. However, glucose sensor cost and reliability, particularly regarding their standard functionalization with expensive and environmentally sensitive enzymes, remains a challenge. A method for the fabrication of nickel nanowire arrays (NWAs) coated with a thin layer of chitosan for the non-enzymatic detection of glucose is herein reported. The method is based on the electrodeposition of nickel into anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) templates, followed by a novel chitosan coating procedure. The nickel and nickel chitosan NWAs were characterized via electron microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and electrochemical techniques. Electrochemical testing using cyclic voltammetry and chronoamperometry demonstrated that the chitosan coating enhanced the selectivity and stability of the electrochemical sensor for glucose detection, even in the presence of interfering species. Additionally, the coating improved the sensor’s sensitivity by 46.39 % and expanded its linear detection range from 3.85 mM to 4.37 mM. The chitosan coating also helped to retain these characteristics even after exposure to physiologically accurate samples and prevented biofouling after exposure to proteins. This simple and robust glucose sensor paves the way for the fabrication of glucose sensors with a high linear range without the need for functionalization with traditional glucose detecting agents such as glucose oxidase.Keywords: Glucose detection, Chitosan, Nickel nanowires, Non-enzymatic

Introduction

Diabetes ranks among the top ten causes of death in the United States, as reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). This is primarily due to the significant medical costs incurred by individuals with diabetes, estimated at approximately $17,000 annually [1,2]. A substantial portion of these costs is attributed to glucose monitoring supplies, specifically the glucose testing strips, which can vary in price from a few cents to around a dollar per strip. This pricing becomes burdensome for patients that require multiple daily tests. In addition to the cost of these glucose strips, it was reported in 2012 that only 27 out of 34 different brands of glucose test strips met standards set by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) [3]. These drawbacks are in part due to the glucose oxidase (GOx) that current standard glucose strips are functionalized with to detect glucose. Although GOx has long been considered the benchmark for glucose sensing and is still used in modern in vivo glucose monitors available today, GOx sensors face several challenges including high cost, instability due to humidity, surface poisoning from intermediates during glucose detection, and low sensitivity in neutral pH environments [4–6]. Furthermore, some GOx in vivo continuous glucose monitors require calibration via single use test strips. Therefore, the limitations of GOx sensors must be investigated to ensure accurate and consistent glucose readings are achieved. One approach to overcome the limitations of the glucose test strip is to utilize non-enzymatic electrochemical glucose sensors. There are two main techniques in which non-enzymatic glucose detection has been studied: electrochemical and photoelectrochemical [7–11]. This study focuses on the electrochemical detection method, which has commonly been studied utilizing two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) arrays of nanomaterials [10]. These sensors leverage increased catalytic properties granted by the material (generally metallic nanomaterials) in conjunction with the geometric increase in surface area. Such materials have demonstrated significant potential for the non-enzymatic detection of glucose, offering promising avenues for the development of more reliable and cost-effective glucose monitoring technologies [12,13].

Noble metal nanomaterials and their alloys are commonly utilized in non-enzymatic glucose sensors owing to their superior catalytic properties, heightened sensitivity, and specificity towards glucose detection [14]. Despite these advantages, noble metals such as platinum, palladium, and gold are susceptible to interference from compounds like uric and ascorbic acids, which can compromise the integrity of electrochemical detection processes. Moreover, noble metals demonstrate lower catalytic capabilities on their own compared to alloys and transition metals alongside poor selectivity to glucose attributed to overlapping oxidation potentials of glucose and other sugars [14]. To address these limitations, ordered nickel NWAs are proposed for the non-enzymatic detection of glucose. Nickel nanowires have been explored as electrochemical sensors due to their excellent sensitivity and selectivity to glucose, which can be attributed to the electrocatalytic activity of the Ni (III) surface state that forms during detection in alkaline environment [10,15–18]. These nanowires offer a cost-effective and readily available alternative to noble-metal-based sensors, featuring excellent electrical conductivity and the capability for facile functionalization with other metals or organic substances to enhance electrochemical oxidation of glucose [17,19]. Despite these attributes, nickel eventually succumbs to biofouling due to the adsorption of glucose intermediates on the active sites of the sensor [20]. Specifically, nickel tends to oxidize many other interfering species due to easily accessible catalytic sites present during the electrochemical breakdown of glucose (for example, NiOOH), leading to a lack of selectivity [17]. This biofouling ultimately limits nickel-based glucose sensors being used as a potential alternative to traditional glucose monitoring strips [20]. To mitigate these issues and to increase both the linear range and selectivity of the sensor, a novel coating method was employed to envelope the ordered nickel nanowires in a thin film of chitosan. Chitosan is a polysaccharide derived via the deacetylation of chitin and is a well-established polymer in the field of biosensing due to its hydrophilicity, antibacterial properties, high water permeability, lack of toxicity, ease of preparation, and its ability to be easily functionalized with a variety of materials, including nickel [21,22]. The unique capability of chitosan to swell or contract in response to environmental pH variations facilitates its coating on the nanowires, effectively enveloping them in a nanometer thin layer [23,24]. This dynamic behavior of the polymer provides a route for the attachment of the chitosan around the nickel nanowires to form a thin coating. Moreover, its noted ability to prevent biofouling [25] coupled with its ample proton-binding sites conducive to the electrochemical oxidation of glucose [26] underscores its suitability for biosensing applications. Further, the abundant and environmentally sustainable nature of chitosan further supports its use in biosensing applications. While chitosan has been determined as an excellent material for use in developing electrochemical glucose sensors, they are generally not used as a coating, but rather as scaffolding for the functionalization of the electrode of other materials, most commonly GOx or noble metal nanomaterials [20]. Although functionalization with these materials generally results in increased detection capabilities, these materials can be expensive, require the use of enzymes, and overall add much more complexity to the fabrication process of these sensors. Furthermore, little work has been done elucidating the effect a chitosan coating has on the electrochemical properties of these kinds of non-enzymatic glucose sensors. Here, we report a chitosan wrapped nickel NWA grown via a simple electrochemical process. The nickel NWA chitosan hybrid sensor combines the excellent glucose detection capabilities of nickel with the anti-biofouling properties of chitosan, yielding a sensor with remarkable sensitivity, selectivity, and improved glucose detection range. These improvements can be attributed to the chitosan’s antibiofouling and electrochemical affinity for the breakdown of glucose.

Methodology

Materials

Nickel Sulfamate RTU (25.6 % Nickel Sulfamate, Technic Inc.) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Corning) were used as received. All solutions were prepared using 18.2 MΩ water (Thermo Scientific Barnstead MicroPure Ultrapure water system) unless otherwise specified. Medium molecular weight chitosan was purchased and used without further purification. Solutions of chitosan were made in 1 % (v/v) acetic acid. Electrochemical measurements were performed in a 0.1 M NaOH solution (pH 13) to promote the optimal anomer (β-d-Glucopyranose) for glucose detection [27]. All standard solutions of d-glucose, ascorbic acid (AA), uric acid (UA), dopamine (DA), diclofenac sodium salt (DSS) and d-fructose were prepared freshly in 0.1 M NaOH and used immediately.

Fabrication

First, a rectangular piece silicon substrate (2.75 mm x 30 mm) was degassed in acetone and methanol, and then subsequently coated using thermal evaporation with a 50 nm thin film of copper to act as an adhesion layer followed by a 100 nm thin film of gold evaporated onto the copper surface to act as the electrode. The growth of the ordered nickel NWAs was achieved using AAO template-assisted electrodeposition utilized in conjunction with a two-polymer transfer process [28]. Briefly, high purity aluminum foil (99.999 % Goodfellow) was degassed via sonication in acetone and methanol, followed by electropolishing at 0°C in a 3:1 ethanol (C2H6O)-perchloric (HClO4) solution (Fisher Chemical) at 12 V for 8 min. After the electropolishing step, a two-step anodization process was carried out in a 0.3 M oxalic acid solution at 2°C with an applied potential of 40 V. Finally, the template was submerged in a phosphoric acid solution (5 wt. % H3PO4) (Fisher Chemical) at 45°C for 4 min to achieve the desired pore diameter.

To place the template onto the gold coated silicon substrate, the template was removed from the bulk aluminum via the two-polymer transfer process. A layer of S1813 (Shipley Microposit) and poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA A11 MicroChem) are spin coated and soft-baked onto the top of the template to provide chemical resistance and mechanical strength. Then, the removal of the template from the bulk aluminum base layer was etched away using a copper chloride – hydrochloric acid solution, leaving the template floating in solution. The template is then carefully moved to a dish using a microscope slide, rinsed with DI water, and placed into a 5 % phosphoric acid solution at room temperature for 1 h to remove the alumina barrier layer at the bottom of the pores. The template is then placed onto the electrode surface, then chloroform and acetone are used to remove the polymers inside the template pores as well as the DI water in between the template and the substrate. This process creates a van der Waals bond between the AAO template and the substrate, preventing the substrate from peeling off during the following electrochemical processes [29,30].

This technique not only allows for the fabrication of NWAs made from a variety of different nanomaterials (such as gold, silver, palladium, copper, or nickel), but also allows for the direct electrodeposition of nanowires onto any substrate of choice, making it ideal for electrochemical sensor fabrication. The electrodeposition of nanowires into the AAO template took place on the gold coated silicon substrate in a nickel sulfamate solution at a potential of −0.935 V. The chronoamperometric electrodeposition process allows for the facile monitoring of the nanowire morphology, ensuring uniformity of each fabricated sensor. The AAO template was then removed by submerging the sensor in a 1.5 M NaOH solution for 1 h.

Chitosan coating procedure

To coat the nanowires in a thin film of chitosan, first a 0.01 wt.% chitosan (medium molecular weight) solution was prepared in a 1 % (v/v) acetic acid solution and adjusted to a pH of 4.0 using a 0.1 M NaOH solution. Then, the NWA was submerged in the solution under constant stirring for 5 h to thoroughly coat the wires. Next, the sensors were removed from the pH 4.0 chitosan solution submerged in 1 % (v/v) acetic acid solutions of increasing pH (4, 4.5, 5, and 6) under constant stirring for 30 min each to remove excess chitosan and allow for the wrapping of the chitosan around the nanowires [23], resulting in a thin coating over the NWA. Lastly, the sensor was submerged under constant stir in DI water for 30 min. The samples were then removed from the water and dried gently with air.

SEM and Raman surface morphology analysis

To analyze the morphology of the nickel NWAs with the chitosan coating (Nickel Chitosan NWAs), electron microscopy was performed using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi STEM 5500) equipped with a Bruker solid-state EDS detector. To further confirm the presence of the chitosan coating on the nickel NWAs, Raman Spectroscopy was performed using a Horiba/Jobin Yvon iHR320 Raman Imaging Spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm laser.

Protein exposure procedure

To demonstrate the antibiofouling properties of the chitosan coating, the sensitivity of the nickel and nickel chitosan NWAs was obtained after submerging the sensors in a solution of FBS to expose the sensors to proteins. Both the nickel and nickel chitosan NWAs were submerged in FBS for 20 min. This is ample time for the proteins to form a hard or dense protein corona capable of inhibiting glucose detection.

Electrochemical measurements

Utilizing the CHI-660E potentiostat, cyclic voltammetry (CV) and chronoamperometry measurements were performed to determine the glucose sensing characteristics of the fabricated NWA sensors. All electrochemical experiments were performed in a standard three-electrode electrochemical cell (depicted in Fig. 1) with an Ag/AgCl counter electrode, platinum mesh counter electrode, nickel and nickel chitosan NWAs as the working electrode, and a support electrolyte solution of 0.1 M NaOH (pH 13) for optimal glucose detection. Chronoamperometric measurements were made with successive additions of 0.1 M glucose prepared in 0.1 M NaOH to maintain a pH of 13. Additionally, CV experiments were performed with a scan rate of 0.05 V/s between +0.2 V and +0.7 V with successive additions of 0.1 M glucose. All experiments were performed in triplicate to demonstrate reproducibility.

Results and discussion

Structural characterization of sensors

The morphology of the nickel NWA sensors was determined using electron microscopy, while statistical analysis of the nanowire height and diameter was performed using ImageJ software [31]. As shown in Fig. 2A, nickel nanowires with a diameter of ~67 nm and a height of ~312 nm were fabricated. This height was targeted to ensure all nanowires were self-supporting to prevent collapsing (which occurs at a nanowire height of ~400 nm), which limits surface area exposed to glucose and compromises the sample’s homogeneity.

A Nickel nanowires inside the AAO template and B with the template removed. C Nickel nanowires coated with a thin layer of chitosan. D Line profile and elemental analysis of nanowires showing composition, successful elimination of AAO template, and confirms the presence of a thin layer of chitosan.

Upon liberation from the AAO template (Fig. 2B), the nickel NWAs are self-supporting and uniform in height. Furthermore, the nickel NWA exhibits a near 100 percent filling factor, allowing for an increased surface area compared to bulk nickel. This increase in surface area comes from the cylindrical morphology of the nickel nanowires in the array. The nanowire height and diameter remained constant for all sensors used in this study. The homogeneity across samples granted by this method allows for remarkable consistency during glucose detection. Fig. 2C shows the uniform coating of chitosan, which is subsequently applied to the nanowires. Lastly, EDS analysis of the nickel NWA (Fig. 2D) confirms the nickel composition, successful elimination of the AAO template, as well as a small carbon signal indicating the presence of the thin chitosan coating.

To further confirm the presence of the thin chitosan coating, Raman Spectroscopy was performed on both the nickel and nickel chitosan NWAs using a 532 nm (green laser) as shown in Fig. 3. As can be seen from the graph, the spectrum with chitosan shows numerous overlapping peaks between 1000 cm−1 and 2000 cm−1 corresponding to the stretching and bending vibrations of the CHn, NH2, and OH groups on the chitosan molecule. The graph also shows the inclusion of a peak at 2920 cm−1, which corresponds to the stretching vibration of the (CHn) groups bonded to the chitosan pyranoid ring [32]. Therefore, the presence of these peaks on the nickel chitosan sensor and absence on the bare nickel sensor confirms the presence of chitosan.

Raman spectra of nickel nanowire arrays with (red) and without (black line) chitosan. An insert with the chitosan structure is depicted in the graph with the relevant species circled for reference.

Glucose oxidation potential determination

To determine the optimal amperometric potential for glucose detection, the current response as a function of potential was studied under constant stirring utilizing cyclic voltammetry. This technique sweeps across a broad range of potentials in the presence of glucose to determine the optimal voltage for glucose detection. This analysis was performed to independently assess the optimal current for each sensor and ascertained from the peaks found during CV and are not normalized by surface area as this will not affect the peak locations. First, a baseline CV with no glucose was measured within a potential range of +0.2 V and +0.7 V. Then, subsequent measurements were performed with increasing amounts of glucose present in the cell, ranging from 0 µL to 1260 µL (from 0 to 3.5 mM). As shown in Fig. 4, for the nickel NWAs, glucose oxidation peaks formed at approximately +0.505 V with slight variations in the peaks. Previous studies have shown that during CV experiments, nickel electrodes show minimal fouling due to adsorbed intermediates or species from the breakdown of glucose into gluconic acid, which results in a small shift of the glucose oxidation peaks [33]. The small shift exhibited by the nickel nanowires follows this trend. The relatively large peak shift exhibited by the nickel chitosan NWAs is due to the rapid breakdown of glucose into gluconolactone, which is an irreversible electrochemical reaction due to the complex and dynamic processes occurring during this reaction. These occurrences coupled with the plentiful hydroxyl (-OH) and primary amine (-NH2) groups which support electrostatic interactions with glucose explain the increased peak shift as compared to bare nickel NWAs [34]. It can be seen for both sensors that an increase in glucose concentration resulted in anodic peaks between +0.4 V and +0.55 V, demonstrating the sensitivity of the sensors to glucose. After analysis of the peaks, it was determined that for both bare nickel NWAs and nickel NWAs coated in chitosan, a glucose detection potential of +0.505 V (versus Ag/AgCl) was selected for both sensors to provide a balance of sensitivity as well as selectivity. This potential was used to carry out the remaining electrochemical experiments.

A Cyclic voltammograms of bare nickel nanowire arrays and B nickel nanowire arrays coated in a thin layer of chitosan. Volume amounts correspond to the total amount of glucose in the cell during each measurement ranging from 0 to 1260 µL (0 to 3.5 M).

Chronoamperometric sensitivity measurements

To demonstrate the glucose detecting capabilities of the fabricated glucose sensors, the current response of the sensors to successive additions of glucose up to 10.256 mM concentration were measured utilizing the +0.505 V glucose oxidation potential found during cyclic voltammetry. A comparison of the current response to glucose of the two sensors is shown in Fig. 5A. To determine the linear sensitivity of the sensors, the slope of the current density vs. concentration was calculated while the current response was linear. For this work, a one stage linearity was utilized with a high correlation coefficient (R2=0.950 or greater) to indicate the region of sensitivity for the glucose sensors. The region in the sensitivity curve after the region where the correlation coefficient is <0.950 is considered non-linear and considered the start of the saturation of the sensor. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated using the formula LOD= σ/S where σ is the standard deviation of the response and S is the slope. For the bare nickel NWAs, a linear sensitivity value of 590.26 µA /mM⋅cm2 (R2 = 0.952) was obtained up to a concentration of 3.85 mM with a LOD of 0.329 mM. For the nickel NWAs coated in chitosan, a linear sensitivity value of 864.88 µA /mM⋅cm2 (R2 = 0.956) was obtained up to a concentration of 4.37 mM with a LOD of 0.288 mM. After these concentrations, non-linear increases in the current density are obtained due to biofouling on the active surface sites of the working electrode as evidenced by the decreased change in current response as time passes during the experiment and is typical of nickel glucose sensors.

A Chronoamperometric graph depicting the current density response of bare nickel nanowires (black points) and nickel NWAs coated in chitosan (red points), demonstrating bot the linearity and consistency of the biosensors. Insert: Linear fit line representing linear region of current response. B Graphic Depicting Chitosan coated NWAs and C bare nickel NWAs electrochemically breaking down glucose.

Overall, the sensor’s consistent sensitivity to glucose (as visualized by the error bars present on the sensitivity curves in Fig. 5A) is owed to the uniform ordered nickel NWA on the sensor. Additionally, by functionalizing the nickel NWAs with a thin layer of chitosan, the sensitivity of the sensor increased by 46.53 % and the linear range of the sensor increased by 13.68 %. These improvements are the result of the hydrophilicity of the chitosan coating inhibiting the attachment of glucose intermediates and other impurities from the glucose solution, which in turn slows the biofouling of the active sites on the electrode during the chronoamperometric measurements [40]. This preserves the electrode’s ability to detect glucose at a sensitivity comparable to other systems found in previous reports. Table 1 compares these fabricated nickel NWAs to other nickel based non-enzymatic glucose detection systems. Regarding the sensitivity, the fabricated nanowire array sensors are equivalent to the other systems shown in Table 1 and utilize an applied potential in the same range. Additionally, the linear range is better compared to the other nickel-based systems. In addition to the increased linear range, the fabricated glucose sensors offer superior control over the order of the nanowire array morphology, granting superior reproducibility without the need for complex fabrication methods. With further optimization, the limit of detection and linear range of the sensors can be improved to increase the applicability of the fabricated sensors. Overall, the above demonstrates the antibiofouling and electrocatalytic properties the chitosan imbues on the nickel NWA. This result suggests that functionalization with chitosan improved the nickel biosensor’s glucose detecting abilities, highlighting a promising route for the improvement of the linear detection range and electrocatalytic properties of glucose biosensor systems.

Chronoamperometric selectivity measurements

Selectivity is a critical property to evaluate the overall glucose sensing performance. To demonstrate selectivity to glucose at +0.505 V, the current responses of the nickel and nickel chitosan NWAs were measured in the presence of known concentrations of glucose as well as common interfering species found in blood. The interfering species being investigated were ascorbic acid (AA), uric acid (UA), d-fructose, sucrose, dopamine (DA), and diclofenac sodium salt (DSS). To mimic the approximate concentrations of these interfering species present in blood, 0.05 mM of each interfering agent was added during sensing. To show that the sensor is selective to only glucose, a 1 mM addition of glucose was added before the interfering agents, followed by two 1 mM additions and a 0.5 mM addition after the interfering agents. As shown in Fig. 6(A), the nickel chitosan nanowire array demonstrated remarkable selectivity towards glucose, whereas the bare nickel NWA sensor never settled into a stable current. This result demonstrates that functionalizing the nickel nanowire array with a thin layer of chitosan increases the sensor’s stability and selectivity. This is due to the hydrophilicity of the chitosan coating inhibiting the breakdown of the interfering species and the attachment of glucose intermediates, preventing biofouling, and allowing the sensor to achieve a stable current. This result further implies that this novel glucose biosensor possesses ideal glucose sensing characteristics due to a simple fabrication process that yields a selective and sensitive glucose biosensor without the need for enzymes, noble metals, or complicated fabrication techniques employed by similar systems.

A Chronoamperometric graph depicting the current density response of the bare nickel (black) and nickel chitosan (red) nanowire arrays to individual additions of glucose and interfering species. B Chronoamperometric graph depicting the current density response of the bare nickel and nickel chitosan nanowire arrays after a single addition containing glucose and all interfering species at the same time.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the biosensor in the presence of a simulated human serum test, 1 mM addition of glucose as well as 0.05 mM of each interfering agent (all present in a 0.1 M NaOH solution) was added at the same time to show that the sensor would detect the 1 mM of glucose as normal with minimal effect from the interfering species. As seen in Fig. 6B, when the glucose and interfering species were added at the same time, the nickel NWAs with chitosan settled into a stable current quickly (within ~16 s), whereas the un-coated nickel nanowire array exhibited a constant decrease in current and did not reach stability during the total duration of measurements. This result shows that adding a thin chitosan coating reduces the time needed to reach a stable current on the sensor and increases selectivity towards glucose. Furthermore, large aggregates such as melanin that could potentially form during the electrochemical breakdown of dopamine do not affect the chitosan coated nanowire array, demonstrating the effectiveness of chitosan as a protective coating against biofouling [41]. This effect can be attributed to the hydrophilicity and protonation of the chitosan surface preventing the attachment of biofouling aggregates during the electrochemical breakdown of glucose in the presence of interfering species, This increases the selective properties of the electrochemical sensor even in complex sensing environments, demonstrating the potential for this glucose biosensor to be used for the detection of glucose in real-life samples.

Chronoamperometric selectivity measurements

Lastly, to mimic a physiological detection environment containing complex biological species, the sensitivity of the nickel and nickel chitosan sensors was obtained after submerging the sensors in a solution of FBS. Both the nickel and nickel chitosan NWA sensors were submerged in FBS for 20 min. This is ample time for the proteins to form a hard or dense protein corona capable of inhibiting glucose detection [42]. After exposure to FBS for 20 min, the samples were rinsed with DI water and dried lightly with air. The effect of the protein corona was then determined by measuring the sensor’s sensitivity to glucose. This was done using the previously described electrochemical setup in ambient conditions, constant stirring, 0.1 M NaOH (pH 13) as the support electrolyte, and additions of 0.1 M glucose prepared in 0.1 M NaOH. The results can be seen in Fig. 7.

Chronoamperometric graph depicting the current density response of bare nickel nanowires (black triangles) and nickel chitosan nanowire arrays (red squares) after exposure to proteins. Lines represent the linear region of each data set.

After exposure to proteins, the bare nickel NWAs yielded a linear sensitivity value of 409.08 µA /mM⋅cm2 (R2 = 0.963) up to a concentration of 3.85 mM with a LOD of 0.291 mM. For the nickel NWAs coated in chitosan, a linear sensitivity value of 408.79 µA /mM⋅cm2 (R2 = 0.956) was obtained up to a concentration of 10.26 mM with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.160 mM. Compared to the sensitivity experiment shown in Fig. 5, this resulted in a percent decrease in sensitivity of 30.69 % for the bare nickel NWAs and 52.73 % for the nickel chitosan NWAs. Additionally, the linear range of the bare nickel NWAs remained the same, whereas the linear range of the nickel chitosan NWAs increased from 4.37 mM to 10.26 mM. This is a percent increase of 134.58 %. Despite a significant loss in sensitivity, the chitosan coating improved the linear range to the maximum tested. This is believed to be the effect of the chitosan encapsulating the proteins during FBS exposure [43]. Then, upon submersion into the pH 13 cell environment, the chitosan begins to deprotonate due to the alkaline environment. This not only causes the polymer to undergo deswelling, but also allows for the electrostatic attachment of the proteins to the chitosan polymer matrix [44]. This results in the formation of a thin chitosan/protein layer wrapped around the nickel NWAs that increases the linear detection range due to the increased protection from saturation of the surface sites of nickel during the electrochemical breakdown of glucose. Although the sensitivity value was decreased as a result of the chitosan/protien layer, the sensitivity value is still acceptable for measuring glucose concentration. While further characterization of this protein/chitosan hybrid system needs to be done, these results demonstrate that the chitosan coating surrounding the nickel NWA helps minimize the effect of biofouling on the sensor after to exposure to proteins, increasing the linear range of the functionalized sensor and further demonstrating the potential use as a commercial glucose sensor. Overall, the above results suggest that this simple biosensor ensemble is capable of detecting glucose with a high degree of sensitivity, selectivity, and reproducibly, even in the presence of biological species such as other sugars and proteins without using complicated procedures or glucose detecting enzymes.

Conclusions

In summary, an enzyme-free chitosan wrapped nickel NWA glucose biosensor was fabricated utilizing a facile electrochemical procedure. Utilizing a combination of electrochemical techniques, it was demonstrated that the fabricated glucose biosensor was capable of accurately and selectively detecting glucose at sensitivities comparable to similar systems in literature. It was shown that chitosan improved the linear range of glucose detection, maintained the sensor’s selectivity to glucose, and decreased the time needed to reach stability. Further, the chitosan demonstrated remarkable glucose detection capabilities after exposure to proteins, which resulted in an improved linear detection range and excellent sensitivity to glucose. This impressive sensing behavior can be attributed to the antibiofouling and polymeric structure of chitosan, which provides ample proton-binding sites for the electrochemical breakdown of glucose. Overall, the properties imbued on the nickel nanowire array glucose biosensor by simple functionalization with chitosan combined with the rapid and scalable electrochemical fabrication methods for producing the AAO template and Nickel NWAs and yield a promising route for the development of potentially mass produced commercial non-enzymatic glucose sensors.
