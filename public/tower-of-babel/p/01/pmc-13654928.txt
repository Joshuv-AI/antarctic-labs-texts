Observation and modeling of fertility loss attributable to natural disasters from 1990-2021
Authors: Manman Chen, Wanzhou Wang, Din Son Tan, Zichen Ye, Yuankai Zhao, Ze Liang, Xijie Wang, Juan Juan, Chao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNatural disasters have increasingly threatened population health, but their global impacts on fertility remain uncertain. This study aims to quantify global fertility loss attributable to natural disasters and its socioeconomic disparities.MethodsWe conducted a two-stage analysis using disaster records from the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT) and total fertility rate (TFR) and net reproductive rate (NRR) data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database. First, we used country-year panel data to assess exposure-response functions. Second, we estimated spatiotemporal distributions and inequalities of global, regional, and national fertility losses attributable to natural disasters.ResultsHere we show that, globally, floods and storms contribute to annual TFR losses of 103.79 and 38.75 per 100,000 women of childbearing age. Flood-attributable TFR loss is most pronounced in South Asia, particularly in low-SDI and low-income countries, whereas storm-attributable TFR loss is greater in high-SDI and middle-income countries. For NRR, epidemics, storms, floods, and earthquakes contribute to losses of 35.26, 32.36, 31.96, and 24.53 per 100,000 women of childbearing age annually. South Asia shows the highest NRR losses attributable to floods, storms, and earthquakes, whereas Sub-Saharan Africa shows the highest epidemic-attributable NRR loss. Epidemic- and flood-attributable NRR losses are higher in low-SDI and low-income countries, whereas earthquake- and storm-attributable NRR losses are higher in middle- to high-SDI and income countries.ConclusionFloods, storms, earthquakes, and epidemics contribute to significant fertility loss worldwide, with geographical and socioeconomic disparities. Regions prone to these disasters should implement local-specific reproductive health strategies to maintain sustainable demographic structures.

Introduction

Accurate assessment of contemporary and projected fertility trends, including the changing demographic structures across various regions, is crucial for formulating strategies to address significant social, economic, and environmental challenges1. Fertility rates directly govern future labor supply, healthcare system demand, and global resource allocation as the primary determinants of population dynamics2. This urgency intensifies as annual live births have declined from a peak of 142 million in 2016 to 129 million in 2021, while the total fertility rate (TFR) has fallen globally since 1950. By 2021, only 46% of the nations had replacement-level fertility (≥2.1 live births per woman)1. Projections by the United Nations and global researchers indicate that the TFR will decline to 1.6 to 1.8 in 2100, contingent upon achievement of sustainable development goal (SDG) targets for education and contraceptive access, and the pace of fertility transition in high-fertility regions2–5.

While these demographic trajectories are well modeled, there is a lack of comprehensive consideration of the potential impact of natural disasters on global fertility rates. Disasters can affect reproductive health through direct and indirect factors such as malnutrition, reproductive tract infections, limited access to healthcare, psychological stress, and economic hardship, which all result in changes in fertility6. For instance, natural disasters may directly influence the human body’s capacity to conceive and carry a pregnancy to term, which are often immediate but can have long-lasting consequences for both the individual and the broader population structure7–9. Specific disasters such as floods or hurricanes, may carry endocrine-disrupting chemicals when damaging industrial infrastructure and cause secondary influence on fertility rates7. Meanwhile, disasters are inevitably accompanied by disruption of healthcare and family planning services, as well as economic shocks such as asset loss and insurance change, which all result in changes of fertility levels10–12. However, due to the wide variety of natural disasters and their geographically dispersed nature, it is difficult to provide a comprehensive overview of their underlying mechanisms of fertility changes. Although the frequencies and severity of natural disasters both increased in this century, their impact pose significant concerns for human fertility13,14. Over the past three decades, most studies have been conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa and the United States. In contrast, several countries with substantial populations and histories of disasters, such as India and China, have a relatively low research representation on this topic6. It is also uncertain how natural disasters would influence fertility rates.

Historical evidence upon certain disaster events and from certain regions suggests that natural disasters are associated with subsequent changes in local fertility outcomes, while the results are mixed12,15,16. Some studies of physical disasters, including drought, earthquake, flood, and hurricane, may lead to an increase in live births17. For instance, the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami was followed by a sustained fertility increase at the aggregate level, which was 13% attributable to mothers who lost one or more children in the disaster were significantly more likely to bear additional children after the tsunami12. Studies regarding Hurricane Mitch in the United States also found higher odds of post-disaster fertility in municipalities receiving higher precipitation levels, but normalized between disaster and non-disaster areas 4 to 6 years after the storm18. A similar pattern was also found in the case of a major earthquake in India, where the “baby boom” occurred within a year after the disaster, but normalized 1 to 5 years afterwards19. Other studies from the United States, Bangladesh, and Jamaica, however, reported opposite fertility responses to natural disasters20–22. To date, global estimates of fertility change attributable to natural disasters remain scarce, especially when it comes to systematic assessments across countries, socioeconomic contexts, and temporal trends. Most existing studies focus on high-income settings or high-cost disasters17, leaving fertility changes attributable to disasters insufficiently investigated at global, regional, and national levels. This gap is especially pronounced for analyses using recent data, resulting in limited comparability across studies.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study conducted a systematic analysis using data from the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT) and the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021). Specifically, we aimed to: (1) examine the associations between natural disasters and fertility changes worldwide from 1990 to 2021; (2) estimate fertility loss attributable to different types of natural disasters at global, regional, and national levels during the same period; and (3) evaluate inequalities in disaster-related fertility loss across geographic and socioeconomic groups. By providing this evidence, our goal is to inform policymaking and support targeted interventions, including strengthening the resilience of reproductive healthcare systems in high-risk regions and integrating maternal and child health services into disaster recovery plans. We show that floods, storms, earthquakes, and epidemics contribute to substantial fertility losses worldwide, with the heaviest burdens concentrated in South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and lower-income regions. These findings underscore the critical need for localized, disaster-resilient reproductive health strategies to maintain sustainable demographic structures in vulnerable areas.

Methods

Study overview

This study followed a standard two-stage health impact assessment framework to quantify the fertility burden attributable to natural disasters across 197 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. This framework is a strategy widely adopted in global environmental epidemiology to bridge the gap between statistical association and public health impact assessment23–25. In the first stage, we quantified longitudinal associations between natural disaster exposure and fertility indicators using country-year panel data, accounting for longitudinal correlations and potential confounders. In the second stage, we further estimated fertility losses attributable to specific disasters that showed statistically significant associations. We performed a quantitative synthesis by integrating exposure-response associations with localized disaster prevalence and demographic data. Analyses were further performed to explore spatiotemporal distributions and inequalities of fertility losses attributable to disasters across socioeconomic and geographic settings.

Data sources and processing

Epidemic event data were systematically extracted from the EM-DAT, an internationally recognized disaster database26,27. The database compiles its extensive records from a diverse array of sources, including various United Nations agencies, non-governmental organizations, reinsurance companies, research institutes, and press agencies. The inclusion criteria for disaster records of EM-DAT are: (a) at least ten fatalities, (b) 100 or more affected people, (c) a formal declaration of a state of emergency by the relevant authority, and/or (d) a formal call for international assistance. Event counts were aggregated annually per country or territory from 1990 to 2021. To ensure enough data for robust estimates, we further excluded (a) natural disaster records <50 times across all countries or territories from 1990 to 2021 to ensure robust estimates in further analyses.

Fertility indicators, including total fertility rate (TFR) and net reproductive rate (NRR) of women aged 10 to 54 years, in line with previous studies, were retrieved from the GBD 2021 database1. TFR estimates the average number of children a woman would bear given current age-specific fertility rates, while NRR adjusts this measure for female mortality during reproductive years. GBD fertility estimates integrate over 8,700 country-years of vital records, more than 1400 household surveys and censuses, and hundreds of supplemental sources, modeling age-specific fertility rates via mixed-effects and Gaussian process regression.

To account for potential confounding in the association between natural disasters and fertility outcomes, we included a comprehensive set of covariates capturing demographic, socioeconomic, and development-related factors. Specifically, we incorporated urban population proportion and population density, both obtained from the World Bank database (https://data.worldbank.org.cn/)28. We also included World Bank income classifications, which categorize countries annually into four income groups (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and high income) based on gross national income (GNI) per capita. To further capture development gradients, we used the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) from the Global Burden of Disease Study28. SDI is a composite indicator combining lag-distributed income per capita, mean educational attainment among those aged 15 and older, and the total fertility rate among females under 25 years of age. This index, scaled from 0 to 1, reflects a country’s overall socio-demographic development, and was grouped into five levels: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high29. In addition, countries and territories were geographically grouped into seven GBD super regions and 21 regions based on epidemiological similarity and geographical proximity, allowing for stratified analyses across macro-regional contexts. We also collected data on gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and the Human Development Index (HDI). GDP per capita, sourced from the World Bank, represents the economic productivity of a country normalized to its population size28. HDI, obtained from Our World in Data, is a widely used summary measure of national development that incorporates life expectancy at birth, average and expected years of schooling, and GNI per capita30.

Detailed descriptions of each covariate, along with their definitions and sources, are provided in Supplementary Information (Table S1).

To evaluate the distribution of natural disaster-attributable fertility loss globally, we applied inequality metrics, including the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curves. The Gini coefficient, ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality), summarizes the extent of inequality in natural disaster-related fertility loss across countries. A Gini index larger than 0.4 was commonly acknowledged as significant inequality33. The Lorenz curve was generated by arranging countries in ascending order based on the variable of interest and plotting their cumulative percentage contribution against the cumulative percentage of the total value. A 45-degree diagonal line represents perfect equality, and the degree of deviation between the Lorenz curve and this line illustrates the extent of inequality across countries31,34.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we repeated the GEE models with additional adjustments for GDP per capita and HDI, which were included to capture broader socioeconomic development and institutional capacity. Second, we fitted the GEE models by transforming the natural disaster to binary variables (existence or not for a specific country-year), in order to analyze the associations between the existence of natural disasters and TFR and NRR. Third, to evaluate whether the observed associations between natural disasters and fertility were confounded by temporal trends in birth control, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis by incorporating annual contraceptive prevalence rates from the World Bank database (detailed descriptions are shown in Supplementary Information Table S1)28. Due to the limited availability of contraceptive prevalence data in the World Bank database, this analysis was restricted to observations where contraceptive data were available. Fourth, sensitivity analyses were conducted for these rare disaster types (<50 records) to evaluate their potential impacts, despite their limited sample sizes. Fifth, to account for the potential confounding effect of the unique global scale of the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding all data from the years 2020 and 2021

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1 using “geepack” for GEE modeling and “ineq” for inequality metrics, with statistical significance defined as two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Distributions and trends of natural disasters and fertility metrics

In this study, the finally included natural disaster types were drought (496 records across 124 countries), earthquake (851 records across 107 countries), epidemic (1213 records across 143 countries), extreme temperature (523 records across 80 countries), flood (4498 records across 182 countries), mass movement (wet) (553 records across 80 countries), storm (3155 records across 187 countries), volcanic activity (166 records across 32 countries), and wildfire (363 records across 74 countries) from 1990–2021 (Supplementary Information Table S2). The total numbers of natural disasters by country are shown in Supplementary Information Fig. S1. Earthquakes were most frequent in China (133 events) and Indonesia (89 events). For epidemics, the Democratic Republic of the Congo reported the highest count (74 events), followed by Nigeria (58 events). Floods were most frequent in China (251 events) and India (228 events). In addition, for storms, the United States of America recorded the highest incidence (483 events), with China (274 events) also severely impacted.

From 1990–2021, the leading five countries with the highest averaged annual TFR were Niger (7.57), Chad (7.35), Somalia (7.16), Mali (6.93), and Afghanistan (6.52), while the corresponding NRR values for these countries were 2.89, 2.85, 2.74, 2.66, and 2.62, respectively (Supplementary Information Fig. S2). Notably, all these countries belong to low SDI regions. Meanwhile, the results indicated declining trends in TFR and NRR across most countries from 1990 to 2021. Saudi Arabia exhibited the steepest decline in TFR (−3.08), followed by Libya (−2.86), the Maldives (−2.68), Oman (−2.58), and Nepal (−2.49).

Exposure-response associations between natural disasters and fertility

As presented in Supplementary Information Fig. S3, the most robust and significant negative associations between natural disasters and fertility consistently occurred in the current year exposure, whereas the associations for lagged years were diminished or non-significant. Accordingly, our primary analysis focuses on current-year exposure-response associations. Figure 1 illustrates the associations between natural disasters and fertility. Floods and storms were significantly associated with reductions in TFR, with estimated decreases of −0.05% (95% CI: −0.08 to −0.02%) and −0.03% (95% CI: −0.06 to −0.00%), respectively. No significant associations on TFR were observed for other disaster types, including drought, earthquake, epidemic, extreme temperature, mass movement, volcanic activity, and wildfire (P > 0.05). Meanwhile, both floods and storms were also associated with significant reductions in NRR, with corresponding estimates of −0.03% (95% CI: −0.06 to −0.00%) and −0.06% (95% CI: −0.11 to −0.01%). Furthermore, earthquakes and epidemics were significantly associated with decreases in NRR, with effects of −0.16% (95% CI: −0.28 to −0.04%) and −0.09% (95% CI: −0.18 to −0.00%), respectively. Fertility loss attributable to natural disasters

As shown in Table 1, floods resulted in a global TFR loss of 103.79 (95% CI: 44.17 to 163.36) per 100,000 women of childbearing age, whereas storms accounted for 38.75 (95% CI: 5.68 to 71.78). Among GBD super regions, flood-attributable TFR loss exhibited substantial geographical variation, with South Asia experiencing the highest burden (401.71; 95% CI: 171.01 to 632.05), followed by sub-Saharan Africa (154.86; 95% CI: 65.89 to 243.78) and North Africa & Middle East (113.03; 95% CI: 48.10 to 177.91). For storm-attributable TFR losses, South Asia also reported the highest burden (163.67; 95% CI: 23.98 to 303.20), followed by Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania (69.64; 95% CI: 10.20 to 128.96), and the High Income region (67.36; 95% CI: 9.88 to 124.68). For SDI level groups, TFR loss attributed to floods was highest in the low SDI group (207.78; 95% CI: 88.41 to 327.08) and lowest in the high-SDI group (39.52; 95% CI: 16.82 to 62.19). Conversely, TFR loss attributed to storms was highest in the high-SDI group (55.11; 95% CI: 8.08 to 102.01) and lowest in the high-middle SDI group (20.78; 95% CI: 3.04 to 38.48). For World Bank income groups, flood-attributable TFR loss was highest in low-income countries (199.09; 95% CI: 84.71 to 313.40) and lowest in high-income countries (32.99; 95% CI: 14.04 to 51.92), whereas storm-attributable loss was highest in lower-middle-income countries (47.36; 95% CI: 6.94 to 87.73) and lowest in upper-middle-income countries (26.83; 95% CI: 3.93 to 49.70). Significant inequality (exceeding the threshold of 0.4) was observed across most GBD super regions, SDI levels, and World Bank income groups, particularly in high-SDI and high-income regions, for TFR loss per 100,000 women of childbearing age attributable to floods and storms (Supplementary Information Fig. S5). The distribution of NRR loss per 100,000 women of childbearing age attributable to earthquakes, epidemics, floods, and storms is shown in Supplementary Information Fig. S6. Earthquake-attributable NRR loss displayed the most extreme inequality globally, with higher inequality for countries in higher SDI and income groups. Epidemic-attributable NRR loss also showed pronounced inequality, particularly in the North Africa and Middle East region and in Sub-Saharan Africa. In addition, flood- and storm-attributable NRR loss showed more significant inequality in Southeast Asia, East Asia, and the Oceania region. The Gini index for TFR loss attributable to floods and storms, as well as for NRR loss attributable to earthquakes, floods, and storms, showed a downward trend from 1990 to 2021, indicating a reduction in the inequality of their impact across different regions (Supplementary Information Figs. S7–8). In contrast, the Gini index for NRR loss attributable to epidemics was volatile and did not follow a clear long-term trend. Analyses by regional, income, and SDI categories showed more year-to-year variability but largely reflected the same general patterns.

Results of sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our findings. As shown in Supplementary Information Fig. S9, the existence of floods [−0.14% (95% CI: −0.18 to −0.10%)] and storms [−0.09% (95% CI: −0.14 to −0.05%)] remained significantly associated with TFR. In addition, the existence of extreme temperature events [−0.07% (95% CI: −0.14 to −0.01%)], floods [−0.11% (95% CI: −0.15 to −0.06%)], storms [−0.17% (95% CI: −0.27 to −0.08%)], and wildfires [−0.10% (95% CI: −0.20 to −0.01%)] were significantly associated with NRR, while the results for earthquake and epidemic became statistically insignificant. The exposure-response associations between natural disasters and TFR and NRR remained robust after additional adjustment for GDP per capita and HDI (Supplementary Information Table S4). After adjusting for annual contraceptive prevalence, the exposure-response associations between natural disasters and fertility indicators remained stable (Supplementary Information Table S5), indicating that the observed associations were independent of broader trends in contraceptive uptake. Analyses for rare disasters with fewer than 50 records [infestation and mass movement (dry)] yielded no statistically significant associations with TFR or NRR (Supplementary Information Table S6). After excluding the 2020–2021 period, the associations remained robust, confirming that the primary findings were not exclusively driven by the COVID-19 pandemic (Supplementary Information Table S7).

Discussion

Utilizing the most recent data from the EM-DAT and fertility estimates during the last three decades, this study, for the first time, to our best knowledge, evaluated the association between natural disasters and fertility indicators globally, and provided the first systematic estimations of global, regional, and national fertility loss attributable to major natural disasters. Our results found that floods and storms were associated with reduced TFR, while earthquakes, epidemics, floods, and storms were associated with reduced NRR. We also provided quantitative estimates of the fertility losses attributable to these natural disasters. Specifically, floods accounted for the most global TFR loss, followed by storms, whereas earthquakes accounted for the greatest NRR losses. The burden of fertility loss was not evenly distributed among geographical and socioeconomic contexts. South Asia had the largest TFR and NRR losses attributable to floods, and the largest NRR loss attributable to earthquakes, mainly driven by the burdens in India and Afghanistan. Sub-Saharan Africa had the largest epidemic-attributable NRR loss, with the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Nigeria, and Niger accounting for most of the burden. High-income North America, East Asia, and South Asia showed the greatest TFR and NRR loss attributable to storms. Although there were no clear temporal trends, World Bank low-income countries experienced consistently high TFR and NRR losses attributable to floods, whereas low-SDI countries experienced the largest NRR losses attributable to epidemics and floods.

Accurate estimates and projections of fertility across countries and regions are necessary to inform policies involving resource and health-care needs, labor supply, education, gender equality, and family planning and support1. Given that natural disasters have been recognized as an unpredictable driver of fertility change3,4, it is essential to conduct a systematic analysis of the associations between natural disasters and fertility, and to provide accurate quantifications of fertility losses attributable to specific types of natural disasters in order to ensure reliable population structure modeling. In the present study, based on systematic analyses across 197 countries, we found that floods and storms were associated with reductions in both TFR and NRR, while earthquakes and epidemics were significantly associated with decreases in NRR alone. These findings are broadly consistent with evidence from previous regional studies. For example, research in Bangladesh has shown that floods and storms can reduce the number of live births and TFR20–22, while studies from Africa have reported fertility declines following prolonged flooding35. In the United States, epidemics have also been linked to reductions in fertility36. Most previous studies have relied primarily on TFR, which reflects fertility levels but does not incorporate female mortality. By contrast, NRR inherently integrates current age-specific female mortality rates from birth through the end of reproductive years, calculating the actual capacity for generational population replacement37. To comprehensively capture associations between fertility and natural disasters, we used both TFR and NRR in our analyses. The observed differences in their associations with disasters may reflect the distinct demographic mechanisms. Floods and storms disrupt households and essential services, delay marriages and pregnancies, and increase mortality among infants, children, and women of reproductive age12,38,39. These processes can reduce both fertility and survival, leading to declines in TFR and NRR at the same time. In contrast, earthquakes and epidemics are acute events that frequently cause immediate and severe mortality spikes, particularly affecting vulnerable demographic groups, including women of reproductive age. However, the surviving female population may not significantly alter their reproductive behavior, which may potentially explain the relatively stable TFR, as potentially indicated by mixed evidence from previous studies19,40,41.

Our results consistently show that the most robust negative significant associations between natural disasters and fertility were presented in the current year than in lagged years. There might be several potential explanations. First, disasters may act as an immediate pregnancy disruptor. High-stress events, physical trauma, and sudden environmental displacement during disasters are well-documented to significantly increase the rates of spontaneous abortion, miscarriage, and stillbirth42–44. These events directly reduce the number of live births among women who were already pregnant at the time of the natural disasters. Second, a disaster occurring in the first half of the year can also lead to reductions in births that same year, especially through the combined effects of pregnancy loss and immediate cessation of conception43,45. Our analyses for different lagged years might also indicate that these immediate biological and behavioral shocks overshadow delayed compensatory fertility effects. Interestingly, our sensitivity analyses showed that using binary indicators for disaster exposure attenuated the associations between earthquakes and epidemics and NRR. This attenuation underscores a critical dose-response relationship. Therefore, measuring disaster frequency is essential for accurately capturing the true demographic impact of natural disasters.

Based on exposure-response functions for natural disasters and TFR and NRR derived from the observational analyses, we further provided the first spatiotemporal estimations of fertility losses attributable to four major natural disasters, including floods, storms, earthquakes, and epidemics. Notably, the attributable fertility losses differed across geographic and socioeconomic contexts. Specifically, flood-attributable TFR and NRR losses were the greatest in low SDI countries and low-income countries. Epidemic-attributable NRR losses were the greatest in low SDI countries and low-income countries. In contrast, storm-attributable fertility losses were relatively higher in high-SDI countries, while the highest earthquake-attributable fertility losses were observed in middle SDI countries, and lower-middle- and upper-middle-income countries. The fertility losses attributable to floods in low-income and low SDI countries may be due to the compounding effects of socioeconomic disruptions and disaster-derived waterborne diseases, which pose a high risk of secondary health crises (such as diarrhea and cholera) that adversely affect pregnancy outcomes and mortality in women and children46,47. Although the underlying drivers of the fertility losses warrant further investigation due to the lack of long-term longitudinal studies, possible explanations may include differences in complex socioeconomic disparities, such as disaster response capacity, the resilience of reproductive health care systems, and changes in family formation plans during the disaster and post-disaster periods48–51. Strengthening post-disaster reproductive health services, improving water and sanitation infrastructures, and enhancing socioeconomic resilience in the regions with heavier attributable fertility losses may help mitigate fertility losses attributable to natural disasters.

Our analyses also identified some regions and countries with particularly high fertility losses attributable to natural disasters, concentrated primarily in South Asia, East Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa. Overall, South and East Asia experienced the greatest fertility losses attributable to floods and earthquakes, while Sub-Saharan Africa showed the greatest fertility loss attributable to epidemics. High-income North America, on the other hand, had the greatest TFR and NRR losses attributable to storms, followed by East Asia and South Asia. According to our current analyses and previous regional studies, the pathways by which these natural disasters affect fertility losses vary across regions. For instance, floods lead to more pregnancy losses in South Asia than in other regions, the threat from these disasters also changes the people’s subjective willingness and objective conditions of having more children45,52. On the other hand, certain studies also reported that some parents from these regions also tend to seek additional children as both a replacement for lost offspring and an insurance mechanism against future child loss22. For high-income North America, though the conclusions remain obscure, fertility losses may be related to the use of contraceptives after certain disasters53. Regardless of the underlying mechanisms, it is noteworthy that Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and East Asia currently have the highest fertility levels worldwide1,3,4. Fertility changes in these regions are therefore critical for global population projections. At the same time, these regions are also highly vulnerable to natural disasters. As human-induced climate change is elevating the frequency and severity of various natural disasters, such as compound flooding (e.g., concurrent storm surge, extreme rainfall, and river flow), a larger proportion of areas will be affected by natural disasters, resulting in greater fertility losses in the future13,54. Therefore, it is imperative to enhance emergency response systems for natural disasters and to integrate reproductive health services into context-specific disaster reduction strategies, in order to help mitigate the long-term negative effects on fertility and population health in these regions.

Our study has strengths compared with previous studies, which were mostly regional and focused on specific natural disaster events18,21,35,36,48. By quantifying global, regional, and national distributions over the past three decades, our study provided a more comprehensive and informative assessment of fertility loss from various natural disasters. We have also provided insights into spatiotemporal trends and socioeconomic disparities of disaster-attributed fertility losses.

Our study has several limitations. First, a limitation of this study is the reliance on EM-DAT’s operational definitions55. While EM-DAT is the most authoritative source for national-level disaster research, its operational definitions may differ from those of other databases (such as DesInventar), which could influence the captured disaster intensity and subsequent fertility impact estimates. Additionally, the inclusion criteria may lead to selection bias by excluding smaller-scale environmental events. Consequently, our primary results may underestimate the true global fertility burden, particularly in regions with more frequent but smaller-scale disasters. Future studies should use localized, sub-national datasets to investigate the cumulative impact of low-grade events. In addition, changes in political boundaries and differences in national reporting capacity may influence event records, since EM-DAT relies mainly on government reports and media coverage. This uneven reporting can lead to higher counts in well-resourced settings and underreporting in resource-limited regions, thereby reducing comparability across countries and likely underestimating inequalities in vulnerable areas. Furthermore, the reliance on official reports of the EM-DAT database introduces potential systematic reporting bias. Political and economic motivations, such as underreporting in middle-income countries to preserve socio-political stability, may influence the recorded frequency and intensity of disasters. While the inclusion of country-level cluster effects in our models helps to control for stable, country-specific reporting tendencies, the dynamic nature of these biases remains an inherent limitation of national-level disaster data. Second, GBD fertility estimates are derived from heterogeneous data sources. In regions with weak vital registration systems, such as Sub-Saharan Africa, extensive statistical imputation is required. The resulting estimates may attenuate true between-country variation and bias effect sizes toward the null, meaning that our estimates of disaster-attributable fertility losses are conservative results56. Third, national fertility policies introduced during the study period were not considered57. Although national fertility policies have substantial influence on reproductive behavior and demographic trends, they are not determinants of disaster exposure and therefore do not meet strict epidemiological criteria for classical confounders. Nevertheless, they may modify disaster-related fertility responses, and future country-level studies should examine these potential interactions. Finally, our study relied on country-level panel data for all analyses, which may introduce the risk of ecological fallacy. The observed macro-level correlations between natural disasters and fertility losses represent population-level associations and do not necessarily reflect individual-level reproductive choices or causal mechanisms. While these findings are instrumental for macro-policy and resource allocation, future research utilizing individual-level longitudinal data is warranted to further understand the underlying micro-behavioral pathways.

Conclusions

Using the EM-DAT and GBD fertility data across 197 countries, this study provides the first global assessment of fertility loss attributable to major natural disasters from 1990 to 2021. Floods, storms, earthquakes, and epidemics have significant impacts on fertility loss worldwide, with notable geographic and socioeconomic disparities. Regions with the greatest attributable fertility losses, such as South Asia, East Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa, should incorporate disaster-resilient reproductive health services into national risk reduction strategies, particularly in low-income and low-SDI contexts. By addressing these context-specific vulnerabilities through strengthened health infrastructure and service continuity, policymakers can better attenuate the demographic shocks caused by natural disasters and maintain sustainable demographic structures.
