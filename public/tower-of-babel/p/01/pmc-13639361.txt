Global variability in colorectal cancer screening guidelines: panel composition and recommendations
Authors: Koki Nakajima, Hiroaki Saito, Yoshitaka Nishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveColorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Although many countries have developed CRC screening guidelines, how CRC screening guidelines are developed across countries remains unclear. This study examined guideline-development funding, stakeholders, and their conflicts of interest (COI), and compared CRC screening recommendations across Group of Seven (G7) countries.MethodsWe reviewed the most recent CRC screening guidelines issued by official research organizations or governmental bodies in G7 countries. We extracted information on responsible organizations, panel composition, recommendations, target ages, screening tests and intervals, supporting evidence, funding, and COI.ResultsWhile CRC screening recommendations showed some similarities across G7 countries, some differences were observed in starting age, screening modality, and implementation structure. Differences were also identified in funding, COI reporting and management, and stakeholder composition, including limited involvement of patient and public representatives.ConclusionsColorectal cancer screening guidelines among G7 countries varied despite shared international evidence. These differences likely reflect country-specific factors, including population characteristics, screening participation, health system capacity, and cultural context. Given that guideline development involves diverse stakeholders, ensuring transparency in the guideline-development process is essential.Keywords: colorectal neoplasms, screening, guideline, conflict of interest, international comparison

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide, accounting for approximately 10% of all cancer cases, and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally.1 While screening programs reduce CRC incidence and mortality,2 their effect on overall life expectancy remains a subject of ongoing debate, as previous research has raised debate over whether such programs extend overall life expectancy.3 When developing clinical practice guidelines, it is crucial not only to consider the strength and type of evidence, but also to examine how each recommendation was made. However, this process across different countries remains little understood. This study aimed to examine guideline-development stakeholders and compare recommendations in Group of Seven (G7) nations, which have relatively high CRC incidence and established screening guidelines.

We reviewed the most recent CRC screening guidelines issued by official research organizations or governmental bodies in each G7 country (Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom (UK), and the United States (US)). For the evaluation items used in this study, in addition to guideline recommendations, we referred to selected AGREE II, especially domains 2 and 6, which were considered most relevant to the purpose of this analysis. Specifically, we collected information on the organizations responsible for guideline development, funding of guideline development, the composition of their development panels, conflicts of interest (COI) of panel members, recommendations (recommended tests and screening intervals, target age ranges), and the evidence cited to support each recommendation. Regarding the composition of the guideline development committees, panel members were categorized into 6 groups based on their roles: Clinicians, Public Health Experts, Methodologists, Non-clinical Advisors, Government Officers, and Patient and Public Representatives (PPR).

In addition, to reduce subjectivity in the assessment process, the funding and COI items within AGREE II domain 6 were independently evaluated by the author and a co-authors in accordance with the AGREE II manual (score range, 1-7; 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), and average scores were calculated.

Further details on data collection and guideline access are provided in the Appendix S1.

Table 1 summarizes the CRC screening guideline characteristics across G7 nations. Regarding the funding of the organizations responsible for guideline development and the COI management of panel members, these organizations are generally structured to operate independently from their funders and to prevent inappropriate influence on committee members (Appendix S2). However, based on our evaluation using the AGREE II instrument (domain 6, items 22 and 23), the extent to which this independence is explicitly described varies across guidelines; some provide detailed statements in the guideline affirming that funders did not influence the recommendations and that COIs were strictly managed, whereas others offer only limited or no information regarding funding and COI in the guideline.

Regarding the composition of panel members, while clinicians and public health experts made up the majority in all countries, the proportion between these 2 groups, as well as the inclusion of members from other categories such as patient or public representatives (PPRs), varied across nations. Despite the recognized importance of PPR in trustworthy guideline development,4 their inclusion remained limited. As for the target age, most guidelines recommended the early 50 s through the mid-70s, while Japan lowered at 40 and the United States at 45. Japan’s earlier start age aligned with existing nationwide screening programs, while the U.S. guidelines recommended starting screening at 45 years old based on a study that showed that lowering the age from 50 to 45 resulted in 22-27 additional life-years gained per 1000 individuals, supporting earlier screening.5 The UK adjusted the target age range for colorectal cancer screening based on cost-effectiveness analyses, with the aim of optimizing resource allocation. The actual age range, however, was constrained by colonoscopy capacity for individuals with positive screening results. Under current capacity, the range was 51-65 years, whereas under ideal capacity, it would be extended to 50-74 years. In terms of screening tests, most G7 guidelines recommended the Fecal Immunochemical Test (FIT) or the Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT) as the primary screening, while Germany and the United States recommended multiple testing options. According to their guidelines, both countries proposed multiple methods because each screening test has its own advantages and disadvantages. In the case of Germany, the guideline emphasized the result of the NordICC trial, which demonstrated that colonoscopy reduced both the incidence and mortality of colorectal cancer.6 In line with this evidence, colonoscopy is currently implemented as the first-line screening method in the German national program.7 By contrast, colorectal cancer screening in the United States is conducted in an opportunistic manner within primary care rather than through a centrally organized national program. This distinction highlighted a structural difference in implementation, even though both countries recommended multiple testing strategies. For the interval, countries recommending FIT or FOBT typically recommended screening every 2 years, whereas those recommending sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy generally recommended screening every 10 years.

Variation also existed within countries. In Canada, while most provinces recommended FIT every 2 years for average-risk individuals aged 50-74 years, some provinces used shorter screening intervals, and 1 province lowered the starting age to 45 years.8 Similarly, although FIT-based screening has been implemented nationwide in Japan, some municipalities have introduced colonoscopy-based screening programs for selected populations.9

CRC screening guidelines among G7 nations showed variability in their recommended practices, despite the availability of numerous global studies, including randomized controlled trials, on colorectal cancer prevention. This is partly because the development and implementation of screening guidelines cannot rely solely on international evidence; they must also incorporate country-specific factors such as population demographics, participation rates, health system capacity, and cultural context. Addressing these complexities requires contributions from a broad range of stakeholders, ensuring that panels are composed not only of clinical and methodological experts but also of governmental representatives, public health specialists, and patient and public representatives. Moreover, because diverse stakeholders participate in guideline development, the deliberation must be explicitly documented and publicly accountable. The documentation should specify the evidence considered, the value judgments applied, and the capacity constraints considered, so that decisions are legitimate and comparable across settings. Accordingly, ensuring transparency in the guideline development process is essential.
