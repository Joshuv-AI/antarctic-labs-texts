Medicare Part B Discarded Drug Spending After the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act
Authors: Aleena Imran, John N. Mafi, Sitaram Vangala, Artem Romanov, Timothy S. Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study examines national trends in Medicare Part B discarded drug utilization and spending between 2017 and 2023, including changes before and after implementation of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act.

Introduction

Health care waste costs the US more than $935 billion each year,1 yet few interventions have successfully addressed it. Discarded drug vial waste alone costs the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid (CMS) billions of dollars, making it a prime target for reform.2,3,4

The 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) requires drug manufacturers to refund Medicare for discarded drug vial waste of 10% or more of total allowed charges, with a financial penalty of 125% of the original refund obligation for nonpayment beginning on January 1, 2023.5 We examined national trends in Medicare Part B discarded drug utilization and spending from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2023, including changes before and after implementation of the IIJA.

Methods

In this cohort study, we analyzed national CMS data on clinician- and health system–reported discarded drug amounts for all fee-for-service beneficiaries from 2017 to 2023.5 For each year, we calculated total Part B drug spending, spending on discarded drug units, and the percentage attributable to discarded drug units. We then calculated drug-level discarded drug spending as a percentage of total spending for each drug and calculated annual drug-level descriptive characteristics. We identified the drugs contributing the greatest total discarded drug spending during the study period, as well as drugs with the highest discarded percentage. All spending was adjusted to 2023 dollars using the Consumer Price Index. To assess changes in reporting fidelity, we also examined the number and proportion of drugs with missing discarded drug data annually. Further methodologic details are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

This study followed the STROBE reporting guideline. Because we used publicly available, deidentified data, the UCLA Institutional Review Board determined the study to be exempt from review and waived the need for informed consent. Data were analyzed from June 1, 2025, to April 15, 2026.

Results

From 2017 to 2023, we identified 862 Medicare Part B drugs with total discarded drug spending of $4.8 billion, representing 1.9% of total Part B drug spending. The number of Part B drugs increased from 598 in 2017 to 823 in 2023. Annual discarded drug spending increased from $559 million in 2017 to $934 million in 2023, whereas the percentage of discarded drug spending declined from 2.2% in 2017 to 1.8% in 2020 and remained stable at approximately 1.8% through 2023 (Table 1). The number of drugs with missing discarded drug data remained relatively stable.

The median percentage of discarded drug spending remained stable and low throughout the study period (0.2%-0.3%). However, outliers were common, and variability in discarded drug spending increased over time, with rising SDs and maximum values exceeding 20% in multiple years and reaching 81.6% in 2023. Drugs contributing the greatest absolute discarded drug spending were not consistently those with the highest discarded percentages. Among the top 10 drugs by total discarded spending, several widely used agents had overall discarded percentages less than 10% (Table 2).

Discussion

In this national analysis of Medicare Part B drugs from 2017 to 2023, discarded drug spending declined prior to 2020 and remained stable at 1.8% thereafter, including during the first year of IIJA implementation. However, driven by the massive and growing volume of Part B spending, even this 1.8% yielded an absolute waste approaching $1 billion each year by 2023.

These findings suggest that discarded drug waste had already reached a relatively low and stable level by the time the IIJA refund provision took effect, leaving limited room for additional measurable reductions. Prior declines may reflect earlier policy signals, such as the introduction of the JW modifier, as well as manufacturer or clinical adaptations (eg, changes in vial size, fixed dosing, or dose banding). The persistence of approximately 2% discarded drug spending from 2020 through 2023 may reflect a practical lower bound, below which further reductions may be difficult to achieve. In this context, additional threshold-based policy changes may offer limited incremental benefit and could introduce tradeoffs, including increased administrative burden or changes in drug pricing.

Despite stable mean levels, discarded drug spending was highly heterogeneous across drugs. Most drugs exhibited minimal waste, but a subset demonstrated markedly elevated discarded percentages, with maximum values exceeding 80%. Notably, drugs contributing the greatest absolute discarded drug spending were not consistently those with the highest discarded percentages, indicating that aggregate waste is not driven solely by high-percentage outliers.

This study has several limitations. As a descriptive analysis, we cannot attribute changes to specific policies. The analysis was limited to Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries and may not generalize to other populations. Reported discarded drug amounts may be subject to underreporting, albeit this potential bias is mitigated by stable and unchanged amounts of missingness from 2017 to 2023.6 Clustering of missing data could not be assessed due to lack of facility-level identifiers. Additionally, lack of data on vial size, dosing, and coding changes limits assessment of underlying mechanisms. Future efforts may need to focus on underlying drivers of waste rather than threshold-based incentives alone.
