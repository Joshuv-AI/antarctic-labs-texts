IKKα-STAT3-S727 axis: a novel mechanism in DOX-induced cardiomyopathy
Authors: Ganyi Chen, Yiwei Yao, Yafeng Liu, Ruoyu Zhang, Chenghao Wen, Qiang Zhou, Yueyue Xu, Wuwei Wang, Hongwei Jiang, Zhonghao Tao, Wen Chen, Zhibing Qiu, Xin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoxorubicin (DOX) is an effective chemotherapeutic drug, but its use can lead to cardiomyopathy, which is the leading cause of mortality among cancer patients. Macrophages play a role in DOX-induced cardiomyopathy (DCM), but the mechanisms undlerlying this relationship remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate how IKKα regulates macrophage activation and contributes to DCM in a mouse model. Specifically, the role of macrophage IKKα was evaluated in macrophage-specific IKKα knockout mice that received DOX injections. The findings revealed increased expression of IKKα in heart tissues after DOX administration. In mice lacking macrophage IKKα, myocardial injury, ventricular remodeling, inflammation, and proinflammatory macrophage activation worsened in response to DOX administration. Bone marrow transplant studies confirmed that IKKα deficiency exacerbated cardiac dysfunction. Macrophage IKKα knockout also led to mitochondrial damage and metabolic dysfunction in macrophages, thereby resulting in increased cardiomyocyte injury and oxidative stress. Single-cell sequencing analysis revealed that IKKα directly binds to STAT3, leading to the activation of STAT3 phosphorylation at S727. Interestingly, the inhibition of STAT3-S727 phosphorylation suppressed both DCM and cardiomyocyte injury. In conclusion, the IKKα-STAT3-S727 signaling pathway was found to play a crucial role in DOX-induced cardiomyopathy. Targeting this pathway could be a promising therapeutic strategy for treating DOX-related heart failure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-024-05439-1.Keywords: Doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy, IKKα, Inflammation, Macrophage reprogramming, Mitochondrial injury

Introduction

Doxorubicin (DOX)is an anthracycline chemotherapy drug that is widely used for the treatment of various tumours due to its ability to significantly prolong patient survival [1]. Despite its clinical effectiveness, the dose-dependent cardiotoxicity of DOX limits its application [2, 3]. DOX-induced cardiomyopathy (DCM) is characterized by left ventricular dilation and dysfunction, systolic dysfunction, progressive heart enlargement, and, ultimately, heart failure [4]. Approximately 50% of patients treated with DOX develop cardiomyopathy [5]. Currently, only symptomatic treatments are available for DCM. Although the pathogenesis of DCM has been widely studied [6], the specific mechanisms involved have yet to be fully elucidated. Therefore, due to the serious consequences of DCM, it is important to reveal its underlying mechanisms and to explore potential preventive strategies.

Recent research has highlighted the roles that inflammation and immune cell activation play in cardiomyopathy [7]. Inflammation can exacerbate cardiac dysfunction and adversely affect the prognosis of cardiovascular diseases [8, 9]. Macrophages are an important subset of immune cells that play a significant role in the pathogenesis of heart diseases by regulating inflammatory responses [10]. Previous studies have shown that mononuclear macrophages can be recruited to damaged cardiomyocytes, further aggravating heart inflammation [11]. Macrophages exhibit distinct functional phenotypes in response to different stimuli. Proinflammatory macrophages secrete inflammatory factors and contribute to immune responses, whereas reparative macrophages counteract inflammatory responses and participate in tissue repair and cell proliferation [12]. Furthermore, Qi Chen et al. reported characteristic dynamic changes in the populations of proinflammatory and reparative macrophages in DCM patients [13]. Therefore, it is important to investigate the role of macrophages in DCM is of great significance.

The IκB kinase (IKK) complex is a major regulator of the NF-κB family and plays a critical role in cardiovascular disease [14]. Specifically, IKKα, which is a component of the IKK complex, has been reported to play multiple regulatory roles in inflammatory responses [15]. Furthermore, IKKα has been observed to modulate macrophage activity in various diseases [16]. Additional research has shown that IKKαplays a significant role in the negative feedback of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby limiting the activation of proinflammatory macrophages [17]. Moreover, IKKα has been shown to protect mitochondrial function and promote oxidative metabolism [18, 19]. In the context of ischaemic cardiomyopathy, IKKα in macrophages is required for the clearance of damage-associated molecular patterns [20]. However, the impact of IKKα-triggered pathways on macrophage function remains unclear.

Herein, we aimed to investigate the role of IKKα in a mouse model of DCM. Our findings revealed elevated expression of IKKα in macrophages. Moreover, we observed that IKKα deficiency exacerbated DCM in mice. IKKα deficiency also worsened mitochondrial damage and metabolic reprogramming in macrophages. Importantly, through single-cell sequencing and mechanistic studies of macrophages, we discovered that IKKα directly binds to STAT3 and thus initiates inflammatory responses. These results indicate that IKKα could serve as a potential therapeutic target for DCM.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and study protocol

Blood samples were obtained from 5 patients who were treated with DOX 3–6 months prior to sample collection and form 3 healthy individuals at Nanjing First Hospital. In addition, heart samples were obtained from 5 patients who were diagnosed with dilated cardiomyopathy and underwent cardiac transplantation at the Department of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery, Nanjing First Hospital. Furthermore, healthy cardiac tissues were collected from 3 donors at the Department of Anatomy, Nanjing Medical University.

Euthanasia

Isoflurane was used to induce (3%) and maintain (0.5%) anaesthesia for the mice included in this study. Euthanasia was achieved by subjecting the mice to 100% CO2 inhalation and performing cervical dislocation.

Animal model establishment and experimental design

In this study, three types of mice were utilized: wild-type mice, IKKαflox/flox mice, and mIKKα−/− (macrophage IKKα knockout) mice. IKKαflox/flox mice and mIKKα−/− mice were bred and identified by the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University. Wild-type mice, IKKαflox/flox mice, and mIKKα−/− (macrophage IKKα knockout) mice, which weighed between 23and 25 g and were8 weeks old, were obtainedfrom the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China). The mice were housed in a pathogen-free facility under standard conditions: temperature maintained at 22–24 °C, humidity ranging from 50 to 60%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The micewere provided a standard rodent diet and allowedto acclimate to the laboratory environment for a minimum of 2 weeks before the experiments commenced.

To induce DCM, the mice were randomly assigned to different groups. Intraperitoneal injections of either doxorubicin (DOX, Sigma-Aldrich, D1515) or saline were administered. The injections were administered seven times over a span of 2 weeks at doses of 3 mg/kg body weight. The mice were monitored daily for survival, and after 4 weeks of DOX or saline injection, their cardiac function was assessed viaecho cardiography. Thesurviving mice were subsequently euthanized for further experiments.

Evaluation of cardiac function in mice after dox or saline administration

Four weeks after DOX or saline administration, the mice were anaesthetized with 1% isoflurane for the evaluation of cardiac function. Echocardiography was performed via a Vevo 2100 Ultrasonic system (VisualSonics, Canada). Leftventricular EF%, FS%, and left ventricular volume (LV vol) were measured by trained personnel from the animal centreat Nanjing Medical University.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of cardiac tissues

The hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Slices (5 μm thick) were obtained from the paraffin-embedded tissues. Histological staining techniques, including haematoxylin-eosin (HE), Picro Sirius Red (PSR), and Masson, were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for routine histology and assessment of cardiac fibrosis. Analysis was conducted on six nonoverlapping fields in transverse sections of the heart. To determine the extent of fibrosis, all areas containing connective tissue were divided by the total area. For immunohistochemical staining, the paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. The sections were subsequently treated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min and subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate solution at 100 °C for 15 min. The sections were then incubated in goat serum (ZLI-9022, Beijing Zhongshan Biotechnology) for 1 h and left overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Col 1 (14695-1-AP, Proteintech), Fn (15613-1-AP, Proteintech), and 4-HNE (ab48506, Abcam). The next day, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (KIT-5220, MXB) for 25 min at room temperature. The signal was developed via DAB (ZLI-9018, Beijing Zhongshan Biotechnology). Finally, the sections were counterstained with haematoxylin (E607317, Sangon Biotech) for 10 min, dehydrated, and mounted. Images were obtained using a ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

For immunofluorescence staining, 5-µm-thick heart sections embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and treated with 0.5% Triton-X for 10 min. The sections were then blocked with goat serum (ZLI-9022, Beijing Zhongshan Biotechnology) for 1 h. Following the blocking step, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: IKKα (NB100-56704, Novus), CD68 (ab53444, Abcam), STAT3 (ab119352, Abcam), and iNOS (ab178945, Abcam). The next day, the sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (A-11008, A-11005, A-21247, A-21202 and A-11012, Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (B-2261, Sigma), and images were obtained using ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

Immunofluorescent labelling and imaging of OCT-embedded sections

The 5-µm-thick OCT-embedded sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were subsequently incubated with 0.5% Triton-X for 20 min at 37 °C. Next, WGA (W7024, Invitrogen) was applied for 10 min. Nuclei were labelled with Hoechst (B-2261, Sigma). The resulting visual images were captured with a ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

Apoptosis detection via a TUNEL assay in OCT-embedded sections and cells

The TUNEL assay was conducted following the instructions provided with the TUNEL Bright Apoptosis detection kit (Vazyme). For the 5-µm-thick OCT-embedded sections or cells, fixation was performed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Protease K treatment was subsequently carried out for 20 min. Then, incubation with equilibration reagent was carried out for 30 min, followed by TDT incubation buffer for 1 h under dark and humidified conditions at room temperature. Nuclei were labelled with Hoechst (B-2261, Sigma). The resulting images were obtained using a ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

Micro PET imaging of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 in mice

The mice were injected with 3.7 MBq of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 through the tail vein. After 1 h, the mice were anaesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and microPET imaging was conducted at 80 kV and 500 µA for 10 min. Subsequently, coronal, cross-sectional, and sagittal plane tomographic images were reconstructed via filtered back projection. A region of interest (ROI) was delineated within the target area for quantitative analysis.

Generation of chimeric mice for bone marrow transplantation and Dox administration

To generate chimeric mice, we followed the previously described method of bone marrow transplantation [13]. Eight-week-old IKKαflox/flox and mIKKα−/− mice were administered acidified water containing neomycin (100 mg) and polymyxin B sulfate (10 mg) for one week. Prior to transplantation, the recipient IKKαflox/flox and mIKKα−/− mice were irradiated with 900 cGray from an X-ray source. Bone marrow cells (BMCs) were collected from 4-week-old IKKαflox/flox and mIKKα−/− mice. The recipient IKKαflox/flox mice received 5 × 106 BMCs from mIKKα−/− mice, whereas the mIKKα−/− mice received BMCs from IKKαflox/flox mice. The chimeric mice were then provided acidified antibiotic water and sterilized food for a duration of 4 weeks. The chimeric mice were subsequently administered DOX.

Serum collection from murine blood for oxidative stress detection

Murine blood was collected from the orbital sinus following previously established protocols. The collected blood was allowed to stand at room temperature for 1–2 h to enable the separation of the serum. The blood samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the serum fraction. The isolated serum samples were then utilized for the detection of oxidative stress.

Isolation and sequencing of CD45 + cells from cardiac tissues for transcriptomic analysis

CD45 + cells were isolated from cardiac tissues from IKKαflox/flox mice stimulated with DOX and from mIKKα−/− mice. The hearts were then digested by CapitalBio Technology (Beijing, China). Using 10X Genomics GemCode technology, single cells were encapsulated in droplets and processed by the same technology provider. Each cell and transcript were barcoded via a unique molecular identifier, and cDNA was generated for sequencing on Illumina platforms via the Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kit v2 (10X Genomics). The libraries were subsequently sequenced on a HiSeq 2500 platform via paired-end sequencing.

Culture and coculture of BMDMs and HiPSC-CMs for stimulation studies

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were cultured according to a previously described protocol [21], and BMCs were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 4-week-old IKKαflox/flox mice and mIKKα−/− mice. Red blood cells were lysed, and the remaining BMCs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 20 ng/ml MCSF (PeproTech) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 7 days. BMDMs were stimulated with 100 ng/ml LPS for 24 h. Human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (HiPSC-CMs) were cultured following a previously described method [22]. The cells were cultured in RPMI/B-27 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HiPSC-CMs were stimulated with DOX for 12 h.

Subsequently, LPS-stimulated BMDMs and DOX-stimulated HiPSC-CMs were cocultured for 24 h at a 1:5 ratio, and Falcon Cell Culture Inserts (BD Biosciences) were used to separate the two chambers. The BMDMs were placed in the upper chamber, while the HiPSC-CMs were placed in the lower chamber.

Fluorescence imaging of OCT-embedded cells and HiPSC-CMs using dihydroethidium

The 5-µm thick sections of OCT-embedded cells or HiPSC-CMs were cultured with 100 µM dihydroethidium (R001, Vigorous) for 30 min at room temperature. The resulting snapshots images were obtained using a ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

Assessment of mitochondrial depolarization in BMDMs via JC-1

Mitochondrial depolarization was assessed via JC-1 (Beyotime, China). The BMDMs were incubated with JC-1 at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by washing with washing buffer. The resulting images were obtained using a ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

Visualization of mitochondrial superoxide in BMDMs via a MitoSOX red probe

A 5-mM storage solution of MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (40778ES50, Yeasen) was diluted to a final concentration of 5 µM. The BMDMs were then treated with the probe mixture and placed in a dark, humidified chamber at 37 °C for 10 min. Photographs were recorded with the use of a ZEISS SCOPE.A1 camera.

Flow cytometric analysis of BMDMs stimulated with saline or LPS

BMDMs stimulated with saline or LPS were collected from culture dishes after digestion and subsequently washed with precooled PBS three times. The cells were then stained with flow-specific antibodies (CD11c, F4/80) at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by another round of washing with precooled PBS three times. Finally, the stained cells were analysed via flow cytometry.

Coimmunoprecipitation and detection of IKKα and STAT3 in LPS-stimulated BMDMs

For the coimmunoprecipitation assay, BMDMs stimulated with LPS were first washed with cold PBS and then lysed with RIPA buffer (P0013D, Beyotime, China). The cell lysates were subjected to preclearing for 1 h via the Protein G Magnetic Bead System (LSKMAGG02, Millipore). The lysates were subsequently incubated overnight with 3 µg of anti-IKKα antibody (ab32041, Abcam) and the Protein G Magnetic Bead System for an additional hour. An equal amount of protein from each sample was then separated by SDS‒PAGE, transferred onto a membrane, and probed with anti-IKKα and anti-STAT3 antibodies (ab119352, Abcam).

Real-time measurement of the OCR and ECAR in LPS-stimulated primary macrophages and BMDMs via an XF96 analyser

Primary macrophages isolated from mouse hearts or BMDMs (3 × 104/well) stimulated with LPS were resuspended in medium and seeded onto a specialized cell culture plate for XF96 analysis. The ECAR and OCR were then measured in real-time via an the XF96 extracellular flux analyser, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

The OCR was measured under the following conditions: 1 µM oligomycin, 2 µM FCCP, and 0.5 µM rotenone + 0.5 µM antimycin. The ECAR was measured under the following conditions: 10 mM glucose, 1 µM oligo, and 50 mM 2-DG. Both the OCR and ECAR were normalized to total protein levels.

In vivo and in vitro effects of niclosamide on mice and BMDMs: a comprehensive assessment

In vivo

The mice were intravenously injected with niclosamide at a dose of 50 mg/kg for 14 consecutive days, concurrently with DOX administration. After 4 weeks of stimulation, the mice were examined via echocardiography.

In vitro

BMDMs were pretreated with 4 µM niclosamide for 4 h, followed by washing with PBS. The cells were subsequently stimulated with LPS for 24 h.

Molecular analysis of gene expression via qRT‒PCR

RNA was extracted from both cells and heart tissues using TRIzol (Takara). cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis was conducted via the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Gene-specific primers were obtained from Thermo Fisher, and the sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S1. The mRNA expression levels of the target genes were normalized to those of the housekeeping gene Actb.

Protein extraction, western blotting, and antibody analysis for molecular characterization of heart tissue

A protein extraction kit (KGP250, Keygen Biotech) was used to extract total protein from whole heart, and the amino acid chain density was determined via a BCA assay (KGP902, Keygen Biotech). The peptide lysates were then subjected to SDS‒PAGE after being electroblotted onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: IKKα (ab32041, Abcam), STAT3 (ab119352, Abcam), STAT3-S727 (ab32143, Abcam), STAT3-Y705 (ab267373, Abcam). The next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5000,7074P2, Cell Signaling Technology or bs-0296G-HRP, Bioss) at room temperature. Protein expression was visualized using chemiluminescence detection reagents (WBKLS0500; Millipore).

Luciferase assay

RAW264.7 cells were transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA) IKKα for 6 h. The next day, the cells were exposed to LPS for 24 h. The sequence was as follows: 5’CCTCTTCTGGCAATGGAGTACTGTT-3’. Next, transfections of STAT3-Luc were performed in RAW264.7 cells. After 48 h, the cells were lysed and processed for the detection of the luciferase activities using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SEs. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t- test, whereas comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc analysis after evaluating the homogeneity of variance and normality. Life expectancy plots were analysed using Kaplan-Meier curves. Theanalyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Increased expression of IKKα in mononuclear cells and macrophages induced by doxorubicin in DCM

To investigate the potential role of IKKα in DCM, we isolated mononuclear cells from the peripheral blood of patients with tumours treated withDOX. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in the number of peripheral mononuclear cells in tumour patients treated with DOX (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, we observed high expression of IKKα in mononuclear cells treated with DOX (Fig. 1B). Moreover, through immunofluorescence staining, we detected the colocalization of IKKα with macrophages in the hearts of patients with DCM (Fig. 1C).

To validate these findings further, we conducted in vivo experiments using C57BL/6 mice. DOX was administered intraperitoneally for two weeks. Western blotting analysis revealed elevated expression of IKKα in heart tissue following DOX administration (Fig. 1D). IKKα has been reported to be highly expressed in macrophages. To determine the expression of IKKα in the macrophages examined herein, we performed dualistic fluorescence-based immunological assay staining of Cardiac tissues for both IKKα and the macrophage marker CD68. Our results confirmed that DOX administration led to an increased presence of CD68 + macrophages in DCM hearts and demonstrated the expression of IKKα in these macrophages (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, we found that IKKα protein expression was highest in CD45+F4/80+cells and lowest in CD45− cells (Fig. S1). These results confirmed the expression of IKKα in macrophages in patients with DCM.

Role of macrophage IKKα in exacerbating doxorubicin-induced cardiac dysfunction and fibrotic remodeling in DCM

To investigate the functional significance of macrophage IKKα in patients with DCM, we utilized mIKKα−/− mice. Following stimulation with DOX, the survival rate of mIKKα−/− mice was lower than that of IKKαflox/flox mice over a 4-week period (Fig. 2A). We subsequently assessed cardiac function via echocardiography. DOX treatment resulted in a reduction in ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%), as well as an increase in left ventricular volume at both end diastole and systole in IKKαflox/flox mice. Interestingly, these DOX-induced impairments in cardiac function were significantly exacerbated in mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 2. B, C). Furthermore, we examined histopathological alterations in heart tissues. The results demonstrated that DOX administration led to disordered cardiac cell arrangement, accumulation of inflammatory cells, and eosinophilia. Importantly, these pathological changes were markedly worsened in mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 2D). Additionally, Picro Sirius Red and Masson’s trichrome staining of collagen in heart tissues revealed prominent fibrotic alterations in DOX-treated mice. Intriguingly, these fibrotic changes were significantly aggravated in mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 2D, E). To explore the impact of IKKα on fibrosis in DCM, we employed Fapi-PET imaging technology. The results demonstrated a significant increase in cardiac fibrosis in DOX- treated mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, we examined extracellular matrix-related markers, collagen type 1 (Col1) and fibronectin (Fn). Staining of heart tissues with Col1 and Fn revealed that DOX-treated mIKKα−/− mice presented more severe pathological ventricular remodelling (Fig. 2G). Taken together, these findings provide evidence that a deficiency in macrophage IKKα aggravates DCM and pathological ventricular remodelling, thus highlighting the functional significance of macrophage IKKα in DCM.

Role of IKKα in macrophages in the development of doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy: evidence from the bone marrow transplantation study

To further analyse the crucial impact of IKKα on mononuclear phagocytes, we performed a haematopoietic progenitor cell reimplantation study. We transplanted bone marrow cells from mIKKα−/− and IKKαflox/flox mice into IKKαflox/flox mice and mIKKα−/− mice, respectively, to generate chimeric mice: mIKKα−/− mice with IKKαflox/flox bone marrow (IKKαflox/flox→mIKKα−/−) and IKKαflox/flox mice with IKKα-deficient bone marrow (mIKKα−/−→IKKαflox/flox) (Fig. 3A). To confirm successful transplantation, we extracted peripheral blood monocytes from the mice and performed PCR, which indicated that bone marrow reconstitution (Fig. 3B). Both IKKαflox/flox→mIKKα−/− and mIKKα−/−→IKKαflox/flox chimeric mice were subsequently subjected to a 2-week doxorubicin infusion. Echocardiography was performed to evaluate cardiac function and revealed that mIKKα−/−→IKKαflox/flox mice presented a substantial decrease in ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%) and a significant increase in left ventricular volume at end-diastole and systole (Fig. 3C, D). These findings collectively demonstrate that IKKα in myeloid cells contributes to the development of DCM.

Macrophage IKKα deficiency exacerbates oxidative stress in DCM

Oxidative stress has been identified as the primary cause of DCM. Herein, we aimed to investigate the role of macrophage IKKα in oxidative stress in DCM. Immunofluorescence staining of heart tissues was performed to localize iNOS (a marker for proinflammatory macrophages) to CD68-positive macrophages. Our findings revealed an increase in iNOS-positive macrophages in mice treated with DOX. Interestingly, the expression of iNOS was significantly greater in mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 4A). Consistent with these findings, DOX-treated mIKKα−/− mice presented significant increases in the mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) (Fig. 4B). Moreover, dihydroethidium (DHE) staining revealed an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) in DOX-treated mice, which was even more pronounced in mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 4C). TUNEL staining further revealed a higher degree of cardiomyocyte apoptosis in mIKKα−/− mice than in IKKαflox/flox mice following DOX treatment (Fig. 4C). Next, we conducted immunohistochemical staining and used an oxidative stress kit to assess the levels of 4-HNE, LDH, MDA, GSH, and SOD. As expected, DOX-treated mIKKα−/− mice presented significant increases in 4-HNE, LDH, and MDA levels as well as decreases in GSH and SOD levels (Fig. 4D, E). To evaluate the metabolic alterations in macrophages, we isolated macrophages from mice with or without DOX administration. The Seahorse assay revealed that IKKα deficiency primarily increased macrophage glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 4F). These findings collectively suggest that macrophage IKKα deficiency exacerbates DOX-induced oxidative stress and promotes macrophage reprogramming.

IKKα deficiency enhances proinflammatory macrophage polarization and mitochondrial injury

To more closely examine the role of IKKα in phagocytes, we isolated and cultured primary bone marrow macrophages and stimulated them with LPS for 24 h. We used F4/80 to label the macrophages and assessed the levels of proinflammatory macrophages on the basis of the expression of the cell surface marker CD11c. Flow cytometry analyses revealed that LPS stimulation increased the number of proinflammatory macrophages. Interestingly, IKKα knockout (KO) further increased the population of proinflammatory macrophages (Fig. S2 A, B). Consistent with these findings, IKKα KO significantly enhanced the production of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) (Fig. S2 C). Additionally, DHE staining demonstrated that LPS stimulation led to an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), which was further elevated in the absence of IKKα (Fig. 5A). Since mitochondrial function is crucial for macrophage activation, we examined the role of IKKα in mitochondria. Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) staining revealed that LPS stimulation increased mtROS levels, and this effect was significantly amplified in IKKα KO macrophages (Fig. 5B). Moreover, IKKα deficiency resulted in a significant increase in the mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5C). Mitochondrial injury can influence macrophage metabolic remodelling. Therefore, we assessed the impact of IKKα on macrophage metabolism via Seahorse analysis. We observed a significant reduction in oxidative phosphorylation in both wild-type (WT) and IKKα KO macrophages upon LPS stimulation, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 5D). However, glycolysis was significantly increased in both WT and IKKα KO macrophages, particularly in the absence of IKKα (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, IKKα KO led to a significant increase in the LPS-induced mRNA levels of glycolysis-related cytokines (HK1, HK2, PGK1, and Slc2a) (Fig. 5E).

Investigating the molecular mechanisms of macrophage ikkα in dilated cardiomyopathy: single-cell analysis and STAT3 interaction

We aimed to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the functional role of macrophage IKKα in DCM. In this study, we isolated CD45 + cells from the heart tissues of mice with DCM (confirmed via echocardiography). After applying quality control filters, we included 10,797 and 7,781 cells from the DOX-treated IKKαflox/flox and DOX-induced mIKKα−/− groups, respectively, for single-cell RNA-seq analysis (Fig. S4 A, B). By examining specificity markers, we identified seven types of immune cells in the single-cell populations. Notably, the proportions of monocytes and macrophages were significantly increased (Fig. S4 C). To determine the macrophage subset associated with DOX-induced injury, we categorized cardiac macrophages into ten subgroups via k-means clustering (Fig. S5 A). Our analysis revealed a significant increase in Cluster 3, which consisted of peripheral mononuclear cells and proinflammatory macrophages (Fig. S5 B). In Cluster 3, the expression of the proinflammatory cytokine osteopontin M (OSM) was greater, suggesting its potential contribution to cardiac dysfunction (Fig. S5 B). We subsequently assessed the proinflammatory score of each gene expression subgroup on the basis of a set of 20 pro-inflammatory genes. The results revealed a significant increase in the proinflammatory score in Cluster 3 (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, GO analysis and KEGG analysis revealed the involvement of Cluster 3 genes in processes such as oxidative phosphorylation, reactive oxygen species production, and leukocyte migration (Fig. 6B, C).

Effects of blocking STAT3-S727 on DCM and macrophage-mediated cardiomyocyte injury

Our previous objective was to investigate whether STAT3-S727 is involved in the development of DCM. We examined changes in STAT3-S727 in the hearts of mice stimulated with DOX, and the phosphorylation of STAT3-S727 was significantly increased in mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. S8 A). To achieve this, we used niclosamide to block STAT3-S727 in IKKαflox/flox mice and mIKKα−/− mice, and the application of niclosamide significantly reduced the phosphorylation of STAT3-S727(Fig. S8 B). After administering DOX, we assessed cardiac function via echocardiography. Blocking STAT3-S727 increased the EF% and FS% and reduced the LV vol at end-diastole and systole in both IKKαflox/flox and mIKKα−/− mice (Fig. 8A, B). To investigate the effect of macrophages on cardiac cells, we cocultured wild-type (WT) macrophages or IKKα-knockout (KO) macrophages stimulated with LPS with DOX-treated HiPSC-CMs for 24 h. We found that niclosamide significantly reduced the level of STAT3-S727 and STAT3-Y705 in macrophages (Fig. 8C and Fig. S9 A). Additionally, DHE staining revealed that DOX-treated HiPSC-CMs exhibited increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, particularly when cocultured with LPS-induced IKKα-KO macrophages (Fig. 8D). However, blocking STAT3-S727 reduced the ROS levels in DOX-treated HiPSC-CMs (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, TUNEL staining revealed that DOX administration resulted in the apoptosis of HiPSC-CMs, especially when they were cocultured with LPS-induced IKKα-KO macrophages (Fig. 8E). However, blocking STAT3-S727 reduced DOX-induced apoptosis in HiPSC-CMs (Fig. 8E). These findings suggest that a deficiency of macrophage IKKα exacerbates DCM and that this effect is mediated by STAT3-S727.

Discussion

In this study, we revealed that IKKα plays a significant role in the development of DOX-induced cardiac remodeling and dysfunction. Previous research has demonstrated the crucial role of IKKα in cardiac inflammation in a mouse model of ischaemic cardiomyopathy; however, the role of IKKα in DCM has not been elucidated to date. Understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which IKKα is regulated in DCM is crucial.

Our findings indicate that IKKα is highly expressed in macrophages in DCM. Deficiency of IKKα exacerbates ventricular changes and malfunction by promoting the accumulation of proinflammatory professional phagocytes and the release of immune system cytokines. Additionally, it worsens fibrotic changes in the heart. Furthermore, IKKα deficiency amplifies mitochondrial damage and alters macrophage metabolism. Bone marrow transplantation studies further support the contribution of IKKα in macrophages to DCM. Through single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, we identified a relationship between IKKα deficiency in macrophages and oxidative phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species production.

Interestingly, our in vitro investigations have revealed that IKKα directly interacts with STAT3 and activates the STAT3-S727 phosphorylation signaling pathway in macrophages. Theinhibition of STAT3-S727 phosphorylation suppresses DCM. Moreover, inhibiting STAT3-S727 in macrophages, which communicate with HiPSC-CMs, decreases ROS levels and apoptosis in HiPSC-CMs.

Overall, our study highlights the crucial influence of IKKα in modulating DOX-induced ventricular adaptation and abnormalities in DCM. We discovered that IKKα in macrophages contributes to these pathological processes through mechanisms involving inflammation, fibrosis, mitochondrial damage, and metabolic alterations. Additionally, we identified a direct interaction between IKKα and STAT3, leading to STAT3-S727 phosphorylation and subsequent effects on cardiac function. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying DCM and provides guidance for potential therapeutic interventions.

Studies have shown that immune cells, particularly macrophages, play a crucial role in the development of cardiovascular diseases [23, 24]. We observed that IKKα is expressed primarily in the heart tissues of CD68-positive macrophages. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that DOX administration increases the expression of IKKα in the hearts of mice. Research has shown that resident macrophages and monocytes/macrophages contribute to the development of cardiovascular diseases [11]. To confirm the involvement of IKKα in DCM, we performed bone marrow transplantation experiments. Consistent with these studies and our own observations, we conducted mechanistic studies using bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). Qi Chen et al. reported that monocytes/macrophages are recruited to the heart upon DOX administration and differentiate into proinflammatory macrophages [13]. In our study, we specifically investigated the role of IKKα in macrophages within the heart under DOX administration. As expected, we found that knockout of IKKα in DOX-treated mice resulted in increased infiltration of proinflammatory macrophages and elevated production of cytokines in the heart. These results are consistent with those of previous studies that examined IKKα in ischaemic cardiomyopathy. Notably, the increased production of proinflammatory factors contributes to cardiac fibrosis and adverse ventricular remodeling. Additionally, we observed increased cardiac fibrosis in the hearts of DOX-treated mice upon IKKα knockout. Previous studies have identified mitochondrial damage as the primary cause of DCM but targeted clinical therapies have shown limited efficacy [25, 26]. In our study, we found that knockout of IKKα in macrophages led to increased oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes through the activation of proinflammatory macrophages and associated factors. Therefore, targeting proinflammatory macrophages to mitigate oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes holds promise as a potential treatment for DCM.

Recent evidence suggests that mitochondria may play a crucial role in regulating immune responses as an intracellular signaling platform [27]. In our study, we used single-cell sequencing to investigate the impact of IKKα KO in DOX-stimulated macrophages on mitochondrial function. We discovered that this knockout disrupted the mitochondrial membrane potential and increased mitochondrial ROS levels. Macroautophagy relies on intact mitochondria, and impaired mitochondrial function can lead to metabolic reprogramming in macrophages. For example, proinflammatory macrophages primarily use glycolysis for rapid killing, whereas repair macrophages depend on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [28]. We found that by disrupting IKKα in macrophages stimulated with LPS, there was an increase in proinflammatory factor secretion and reliance on glycolysis. These findings align with recent studies showing that mitochondrial dysfunction impairs the transition from proinflammatory to repair macrophages [29]. Another important finding of this study was that STAT3 plays an important role in IKKα KO macrophages.

STAT3 is a transcriptional regulator that plays a crucial role in various biological processes, including inflammation and immunity [30]. Dysregulation of STAT3 activity can lead to human diseases. Its role in macrophages has been extensively studied, particularly in terms of macrophage polarization and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [31, 32]. STAT3 expression in macrophages is closely associated with the expression of proinflammatory cytokines. It possesses a Src homology 2 (SH2) domain and a C-terminal tyrosine residue (Y705 in mice) that can be phosphorylated by JAK kinase in response to cytokine stimulation or intracellular protein tyrosine kinase activation [33]. Additionally, STAT3 can undergo serine phosphorylation (S727) at site 727, which enhances its transcriptional activity [34]. In our study, we observed that IKKα KO in macrophages led to the upregulation of STAT3-S727 but not STAT3-Y705, thereby directly exacerbating cardiac injury. This injury process is characterized by increased ROS levels and cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Importantly, when we inhibited STAT3, we detected a decrease in cardiac injury, suggesting that the effect of IKKα depends on STAT3 expression in macrophages.

One limitation of this study is that the phenotypic transformation and metabolic reprogramming of macrophages are only some of the important aspects of DCM. Further research should examine the interaction mechanism with cardiomyocytes. In addition, niclosamide can affect the phosphorylation of STAT3-S727 and STAT3-Y705 but cannot specifically inhibit the expression of STAT3-S727. In the future, we will conduct in-depth research on the specific mechanism underlying the role of STAT3 in DCM.

In conclusion, our study identified IKKα as a potential therapeutic target in DCM and highlights its crucial role in regulating macrophage function during the repair process of the injured heart. Inhibiting IKKα can have detrimental effects on the heart, as it leads to increased expression of proinflammatory factors, increases cardiomyocyte oxidative stress, and exacerbates pathological remodeling. This study revealed the underlying mechanism through which the loss of IKKα enhances the injury process via proinflammatory macrophages and metabolic reprogramming. Moreover, loss of IKKα promotes the phosphorylation of STAT3-S727, further impairing cardiac function. These findings highlight the bidirectional crosstalk between macrophages and cardiomyocytes and provide guidance for future research in this field.
