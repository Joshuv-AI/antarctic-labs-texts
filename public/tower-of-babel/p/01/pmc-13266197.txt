A lymphoid organ-enriched C-type lectin, functions as a pattern recognition receptor and inflammatory regulator in Litopenaeus vannamei
Authors: Xiaoyang Huang, Yuan Liu, Yi Li, Zhanyuan Yang, Fuhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•LvccCTL was a lymphoid organ-specific C-type lectin with a QPV motif.•LvccCTL bound diverse PAMPs and agglutinated bacteria in a Ca2+-dependent manner.•LvccCTL knockdown downregulated NF-κB, JAK-STAT pathways and inflammation genes.•LvccCTL knockdown changed ROS levels and iNOS activity upon LPS stimulation.

Introduction

The Pacific white shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei is one of the most extensively cultured and economically important aquaculture species worldwide [1]. However, infectious diseases caused by pathogens such as Vibrio parahaemolyticus and white spot syndrome virus (WSSV) continue to pose severe threats to shrimp aquaculture [[2], [3], [4]]. Further investigation on shrimp immunity, particularly the identification of novel pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), is essential for disease prevention and control.

Among PRRs, C-type lectins (CTLs) represent a large family of Ca2+-dependent carbohydrate-binding proteins present in diverse organisms [5]. They contain at least one carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD), which is critically responsible for non-self recognition and binding [6]. In vertebrates, CTLs are diverse and activate various signaling pathways, such as NF-κB pathway, MAPK signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [7,8]. In invertebrates, most CTLs have a single CRD, whereas many crustacean CTLs contain additional domains [9], such as coiled-coil region [[10], [11], [12]], leucine-rich repeat [13], low-density lipoprotein receptor class A (LDLa) [14] and immunoglobulin-like domain [15]. These additional structural components may expand the biological functions of crustacean CTLs. For example, MjCC-CL from Marsupenaeus japonicus and Pc-ccCL from Procambarus clarkii could activate JAK-STAT pathway by the coiled-coil region [10,12]. SpccCTL from Scylla paramamosain could participate in antiviral immunity by binding viral protein and inducing antimicrobial peptide (AMP) expression [11]. LRRCTL from L. vannamei could enhance shrimp resistance to V. parahaemolyticus by regulating AMP production [13]. The LDLa domain of LvLdlrCTL from L. vannamei had opsonic activity by enhancing hemocyte phagocytosis [14]. Notably, most crustacean CTLs were mainly expressed in hepatopancreas and hemocytes, with their functions primarily validated in hemocytes. Therefore, the structure and function of CTLs in other immune tissues of crustacean remain largely unexplored.

The lymphoid organ, also known as Oka, is an essential component of the shrimp immune system involved in both cellular and humoral immune responses [16,17]. This organ functions in hemolymph filtering and pathogen clearance, and is regarded as the major phagocytic organ in shrimp [15,18,19]. Recent evidence further indicate functional similarities between shrimp Oka and vertebrate lymphoid organs [20]. Several immune effectors and transcriptomes have been reported in Oka [[21], [22], [23]], highlighting its importance in shrimp humoral immunity. However, the immune recognition mechanism of Oka and its mediated inflammation-like responses remain poorly understood. In this study, we identified a novel coiled-coil region-containing C-type lectin gene from the Oka of L. vannamei, designated as LvccCTL. The structural features, expression patterns and biological activities of LvccCTL were analyzed. The PAMP recognition, microbial binding and agglutination abilities of recombinant LvccCTL (rLvccCTL) were examined. Furthermore, using double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)-mediated knockdown, the roles of LvccCTL in regulating phagocytosis, inflammation and immune signaling transduction were investigated.

Material and methods

Experimental animals, tissue collection and immune challenge

Healthy adult L. vannamei, with an average body weight of 8.1 ± 0.5 g, were acclimatized in the laboratory (Qingdao, China). The shrimp were maintained in aerated seawater at 25 ± 1 °C and fed three times daily.

For tissue distribution analysis, samples including hemocytes, lymphoid organs, hepatopancreas, gills, heart, stomach, brain, epithelium, eyestalks, intestine and muscle were collected from five healthy shrimp. Hemolymph was withdrawn from the ventral sinus of the first abdominal segment using a sterile syringe pre-filled with an equal volume of ice-cold anticoagulant solution (115 mM glucose, 27 mM sodium citrate, 336 mM NaCl, 9 mM EDTA•Na2•2H2O, pH 7.4). The hemolymph was immediately centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the hemocytes, which were then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All other tissues were dissected directly and preserved similarly.

For the immune challenge assay, healthy shrimp were randomly divided into three groups, including a control group, a V. parahaemolyticus-challenged group and a white spot syndrome virus (WSSV)-challenged group. V. parahaemolyticus and WSSV were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to final concentrations of 3.2 × 104 colony-forming units (cfus) per shrimp and 1 × 103 copies per shrimp, respectively. Each shrimp in the challenged groups received a 10 μL intramuscular injection of the respective pathogen suspension into the third or fourth abdominal segment using a microsyringe. The control group received an equivalent volume of PBS. Lymphoid organ samples were collected at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection from the control and V. parahaemolyticus-challenged groups, and at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection from the WSSV-challenged group. At each time point, four biological replicates were collected per group, with each replicate consisting of pooled tissue from five shrimp.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA cloning

Total RNA was extracted from the collected tissues using RNAisoPlus reagent (Takara, China). RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA).

First-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To clone the open reading frame (ORF) of LvccCTL, a pair of gene-specific primers, LvccCTL-19T-F and LvccCTL-19T-R (Table S1), were designed based on the transcriptomic sequence. PCR amplification was performed in a total volume of 25 μL. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified PCR products were then analyzed by gel electrophoresis or purified for sequencing.

Bioinformatics and structural characterization

Sequence homology analysis was performed using the BLAST tool on NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The open reading frame (ORF) was predicted using the ORF finder on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). Signal peptide and potential protein domain within the amino acid sequence were predicted using SMART (https://smart.embl.de/smart/set_mode.cgi?NORMAL=1). Molecular weight and isoelectric point (pI) of the protein were calculated using ExPASy (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi). Potential N-glycosylation sites were predicted using NetNGlyc-1.0 (NetNGlyc 1.0 - DTU Health Tech - Bioinformatic Services). The structure model of LvccCTL was constructed using AlphaFold3 server (https://alphafoldserver.com).

Expression analysis by real-time quantitative PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to examine the temporal expression profiles of LvccCTL in the lymphoid organs of shrimp following microbial challenge. The cDNA templates were diluted 30-fold with RNase-free water. Each qRT-PCR reaction was carried out in a total volume of 10 μL, containing 5.06 μL of 2 × SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, China), 0.33 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 1 μL of diluted cDNA, and 3.28 μL of RNase-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and extension at 60 °C for 35 s. Melting curve analysis was performed by gradually heating the PCR products from 60 °C to 95 °C, with fluorescence signals acquired at 0.1 °C intervals. All reactions were performed in triplicate. The relative expression levels of LvccCTL were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with 18S rRNA as the internal reference gene.

Expression and purification of recombinant LvccCTL

A primer pair, LvccCTL-32a-F and LvccCTL-32a-R (Table S1), was used to amplify the sequence encoding the mature peptide of LvccCTL. The pET-32a vector was linearized by double digestion with Bam HI and Hind III (TaKaRa, China). The purified PCR product was ligated into the linearized vector using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (TaKaRa, USA). The resulting recombinant plasmid, pET-32a-LvccCTL, was verified by DNA sequencing. Both the verified recombinant plasmid and the empty pET-32a vector were then transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells (Transgen, China) for protein expression.

Recombinant LvccCTL (rLvccCTL) protein expression was induced by adding isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. The rLvccCTL protein was purified from inclusion bodies using TALON Metal Affinity Resin (Clontech, USA) under denaturing conditions. The purified protein was refolded by stepwise dialysis against Tris-buffered saline (TBS) glycerol buffers containing decreasing concentrations of urea and was subsequently concentrated using an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter (Millipore, Ireland). Protein concentrations of rLvccCTL and the recombinant thioredoxin (rTrx), which served as a control, were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

PAMP binding assay

PAMP binding ability of rLvccCTL was assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Briefly, 96-well microtiter plates (NEST, China) were coated overnight at 4 °C with 100 μL per well of carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (50 mM, pH 9.6) containing 20 μg of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Yuanye, China), peptidoglycan (PGN) (Macklin, China), β-glucan (GLU) (Yuanye, China), and polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)] (Yuanye, China). After removal of the coating solution, wells were washed three times with PBS-T (pH 7.5) and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, 100 μL of purified rLvccCTL diluted in PBS containing 0.1 mg/mL BSA and 5 mM CaCl2 was added to each well and incubated for 3 h at 18 °C. Serial dilutions of recombinant thioredoxin (rTrx) were used as a negative control. Following three washes, plates were incubated with 100 μL of mouse anti-His tag primary antibody (Transgen, China) diluted 1:1000 in PBS containing 1 mg/mL BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. After another washing step, 100 μL of goat anti-mouse Ig-HRP conjugate (Transgen, China) diluted 1:1000 in PBS with 1 mg/mL BSA was added and incubated for 45 min at 37 °C. Color development was achieved using an EL-TMB Chromogenic Kit (Sangon, China). All assays were performed in triplicate.

Microbial binding assay

The microbial binding activities of the recombinant proteins and synthetic peptide were evaluated against two Gram-negative bacteria (V. parahaemolyticus and V. alginolyticus), two Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Micrococcus luteus), and one fungus (Pichia pastoris). Microorganisms were cultured in appropriate media to logarithmic phase, harvested, and fixed by adding 1 mL of 37% formaldehyde. After fixation, the cells were washed three times with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.0) and resuspended in the same buffer. Equal volumes of microbial suspension and protein solution (final concentration 1 mg/mL) were mixed and incubated. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected (initial supernatant). The microbial pellets were washed three times with 1 mL of PBS, and each wash fraction was collected separately. After the final wash, the pellets were resuspended in PBS (pellet fraction). Recombinant thioredoxin (rTrx) was used as a negative control. All fractions (initial supernatant, each wash, and final pellet suspension) were analyzed by 15% SDS-PAGE. The intensity of protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software [24].

Microbial agglutination and inhibition assays

V. parahaemolyticus, V. alginolyticus, M. luteus, S. aureus, and P. pastoris were cultured to the exponential phase. The microorganisms were washed three times with PBS and labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Excess FITC was removed by additional PBS washes, and the cell concentration was adjusted to 1 × 108 cfu/mL with Tris–HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.0). Recombinant protein solution (0.2 mg/mL) containing 10 mM CaCl2 was mixed with the microbial suspension at a volume ratio of 2.5:1 and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. To assess the calcium dependence of agglutination, EDTA was added to the protein‑microorganism mixture to a final concentration of 5 mM. Recombinant thioredoxin (rTrx) and Tris–HCl buffer served as negative and blank controls, respectively.

For the carbohydrate competition assay, a panel of carbohydrates was selected, d-galactose (Yuanye, China), d-mannose (Yuanye, China), d-glucose (Hushi, China), sucrose (Hushi, China), GLU (Yuanye, China), LPS (Yuanye, China), and PGN (Macklin, China). Each carbohydrate was dissolved and serially diluted to concentrations of 200, 100, 50, and 25 mM. Then, 25 μL of rLvccCTL solution was pre‑incubated with 15 μL of each carbohydrate dilution for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, FITC‑labeled V. parahaemolyticus and CaCl2 (final concentration 10 mM) were added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 h. A 10 μL aliquot of the mixture was placed on a slide and examined for agglutination under a fluorescence microscope, and images were captured.

Double-strand RNA interference assay

Primers specific for LvccCTL and EGFP, each containing the T7 promoter sequence at their 5′ ends, were designed using Primer 3 Plus software to generate templates for double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) synthesis. The specificity of the dsRNA sequence was confirmed by BLASTn against the L. vannamei genome/transcriptome, showing no significant homology (≤17 nt continuous match) to any other known genes. Using a cDNA clone inserted into the pMD19-T vector as the template, PCR amplification was performed with the primers dsLvccCTL-F and dsLvccCTL-R using Accurate Taq polymerase (Accurate Biotechnology, China). After confirmation of a single amplicon by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, the PCR products were purified using the SteadyPure PCR DNA Purification Kit (Accurate Biology, China). The purified DNA fragments, flanked by T7 promoter sequences, were used as templates for in vitro transcription using the T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Thermo, USA). The synthesized dsRNA was purified using the SteadyPure PCR DNA Purification Kit (Accurate Biology, China), and its concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). The integrity of the dsRNA was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis before and after RNase A digestion.

For in vivo RNA interference, dsLvccCTL and dsEGFP were diluted in PBS. A volume of 10 µL containing dsRNA at doses of 0.125 and 0.25 µg/g shrimp body weight was injected into the second abdominal segment using a microsyringe. Control shrimp received an equivalent volume of PBS. Each experimental group consisted of four biological replicates, with four shrimp per replicate. Lymphoid organs (Oka) were collected at 48 h post-injection, and the RNA interference efficiency was evaluated by quantitative PCR using the primers LvccCTL-qF and LvccCTL-qR.

Expression of immune-related genes in the LvccCTL-knockdown shrimp

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to examine the expression of multiple immune genes in the lymphoid organs of LvccCTL-knockdown shrimp. The immune-related genes analyzed encompassed several functional categories. Phagocytosis-associated genes included LvArf (ADP-ribosyl transferase 4, MK471369.1), LvRab (Rab GTPase, KJ742828.1) and LvRan (Ras-like nuclear protein, JX644455.1). NF-κB pathway genes comprised LvToll1 (DQ923424.1), LvToll2 (JN180637.1), LvToll3 (JN180638.1), LvCactus (JX014314.1), LvDorsal (FJ998202.1) and LvRelish (EF432734.1). JAK-STAT pathway genes included LvJAK (Janus kinase, KP310054.1), LvSTAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription, HQ228176.1), and LvDOME (Domeless, KC346866.1). Additionally, one MAPK signaling pathway gene p38 (mitogen-activated protein kinase, JN035902.2), two antimicrobial peptide genes LvALF4 (anti-lipopolysaccharide factor 4, MF135540.1) and LvALF8 (anti-lipopolysaccharide factor 8, XM_027364058.2), nine inflammation-related genes, including LvLITAF (lipopolysaccharide-induced TNF-α factor, JN180640.1), LvIL-16 (interleukin-16-like protein, KY052164.1), LvTRAF6 (tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6, HM581680.1), LvTNFSF (tumor necrosis factor superfamily, JN180639.1), LvTNFRSF (tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, JN180641.1), LvNFIL3 (nuclear factor leukemogen-induced factor 3, XM_027355256.1), LvSVC1 (interferon-like factor 1, HQ541158.1), LvSVC4 (interferon-like factor 4, HQ541161.1) and LvSVC5 (interferon-like factor 5, HQ541162.1), and eight redox-related genes LvNOX (NADPH oxidase, XM_027352105.1), LvSOD (superoxide dismutase, DQ005531.1), LvNOS (nitric oxide synthase, GQ429217.1), LvNOX4 (NADPH oxidase 4, XM_027383600.1), LvNOX4L (NADPH oxidase 4-like protein, XM_027383048.1), LvSOD5 (copper/zinc superoxide dismutase isoform 5, XM_027361275.1), LvTrx (thioredoxin, XM_027360172.1) and LvTrxDC (thioredoxin-like domain-containing protein, XM_027382472.1), were also analyzed.

Assay of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

To investigate the role of LvccCTL in ROS production, ROS levels were measured in the lymphoid organs of LvccCTL-knockdown and control shrimp following LPS challenge. The assay was performed using the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) based on the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe. Briefly, shrimp previously injected with PBS, dsEGFP, or dsLvccCTL were then injected with LPS at a final concentration of 2.5 μg/g shrimp body weight. Each experimental group consisted of four biological replicates, with four shrimp per replicate. At 6 and 12 h post-LPS injection, lymphoid organs were collected from each shrimp and homogenized in ice-cold PBS. After centrifugation, the resulting supernatants were incubated with 5 μM DCFH-DA solution for 30 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan, Austria) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 nm and 585 nm, respectively. Protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) for normalization. Relative ROS levels were expressed as fluorescence intensity per milligram of protein. All samples were measured in four replicates.

Assay of inducible NOS (iNOS) activity

Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity was measured in the lymphoid organs of LvccCTL-knockdown and control shrimp following LPS challenge using a commercial NOS Assay Kit (A014, Jiancheng Institute of Biotechnology, China). The assay was performed based on the catalysis of the reaction between O2 and l-arginine, with absorbance measured at 530 nm. Protein concentrations in the samples were determined using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) for normalization. iNOS activity was expressed as units per milligram of protein. All samples were measured in four replicates.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were presented as mean ± S.D. and analyzed via one-way ANOVA test using SPSS 16.0. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and p < 0.01 was regarded as extremely significant compared with the control group.

Results

Sequence characteristics of LvccCTL

The cDNA sequence of LvccCTL was cloned from the lymphoid organ (Oka) and submitted to GenBank under the accession no. PZ111322. The obtained sequence was 1172 bp in length, and contained a 69 bp 5′-untranslated region (UTR), a 1035 bp open reading frame (ORF), and a 68 bp 3′-UTR (Fig. 1A). The ORF of LvccCTL encoded a 344 amino acid polypeptide including a signal peptide, a coiled-coil region and a CRD domain (Fig. 1B). There were six conserved cysteine residues in the CRD domain, including four conserved cysteine residues (Cys233, Cys304, Cys317 and Cys325) forming two internal disulfide bridges and two additional cysteine residues (Cys205 and Cys216) located at the N-terminus. A potential carbohydrate-binding motif QPV (Gln283-Pro284-Val285), a potential glycosylation site NKD (Asn272-Lys273-Asp274) and two conserved Ca2+-binding sites (Asp208 and Asp269) were also detected in the CRD domain. Agglutinating activity of rLvccCTL to microorganisms

The microorganism agglutination function of rLvccCTL was determined by incubation with FITC-labeled microorganisms (Fig. 7). The rLvccCTL exhibited agglutinating effects against the test microorganisms except for P. pastoris in the presence of Ca2+. The microbial agglutination of rLvccCTL could completely disappear with incubation of Ca2+ and EDTA, indicating that this activity was Ca2+-dependent. Discussion

C-type lectins have attracted increasing research attention due to their fundamental roles in crustacean innate immunity. Most shrimp CTLs contain only a single CRD [25]. In the present study, a C-type lectin (LvccCTL) with an additional coiled-coil region was identified and characterized from L. vannamei. This coiled-coil region has also been reported in SpccCTL from S. paramamosain [11], MjCC-CL from M. japonicus [10]. Such structural features may further stabilize the CRD or mediate downstream signaling [10,12]. Consistent with other C-type lectins [[26], [27], [28]], the conserved CRD of LvccCTL adopted a long-form double-loop structure with three α-helices and five β-sheets, stabilized by six cysteine residues forming three disulfide bridges. These disulfide bonds are critical for maintaining the proper folding and structural integrity of the CRD, which is a prerequisite for carbohydrate recognition [29]. Unlike the classical QPD (Gln-Pro-Asp) motif found in vertebrates, a non-canonical QPV motif was identified in the Ca2+ binding site 2 of LvccCTL, which may contribute to carbohydrate-binding specificity. This motif variation has been observed in other crustacean CTLs, such as PvCTL2 with QAP motif from L. vannamei [30], PcLec-EPS with EPS motif and PcLec-QPS with QPS motif from P. clarkii [31], and SpCTL-C with QPA motif and SpCTL-D with MPA motif from the S. paramamosain [32]. The presence of diverse non-canonical motifs in crustacean CTLs suggests an evolutionary trend away from strict monosaccharide specificity toward broader carbohydrate recognition. These structural features collectively suggested that LvccCTL was a novel member of the shrimp CTL family.

Unlike most crustacean CTLs that are typically hepatopancreas-specific [25,33], LvccCTL in our study was dominantly expressed in lymphoid organ (Oka). This tissue-specific expression pattern suggested a specialized role for LvccCTL in Oka-mediated immunity. As a key immune organ in shrimp, Oka has been shown to express several immune-related genes, including those encoding ALF, lysozyme, prophenoloxidase activating factor-III, transglutaminase and cathepsin [34]. The preferential expression of LvccCTL in Oka, rather than in hemocytes or hepatopancreas, might reflect a compartmentalized immune strategy wherein specific PRRs are allocated to distinct sites to optimize pathogen detection and clearance. Notably, the sustained upregulation of LvccCTL following V. parahaemolyticus challenge (up to 72 h) further supported its role in long-term immune surveillance within this organ, possibly contributing to the lymphoid organ’s capacity for prolonged responses against bacterial infection.

C-type lectins function as PRRs that mediate self-nonself discrimination by binding to PAMPs on pathogen surfaces in the presence of Ca2+ [35]. In the present study, rLvccCTL was shown to bind LPS, PGN, GLU and poly(I:C) in the presence of Ca2+, indicating that LvccCTL possessed a broad PAMP-binding spectrum. This binding profile was consistent with those of reported in rPtCLec2 from Portunus trituberculatus [28], and rSpCTL-C/D from the S. paramamosain [32]. Additionally, rLvccCTL exhibited a broad microbial binding spectrum, including two Gram-negative bacteria, two Gram-positive bacteria and one fungus. Similar broad-spectrum recognition has been reported for PvMR1 from L. vannamei [36], EcTR-CTL from Exopalaemon carinicauda [37], and MjCC-CL from M. japonicus [10], all of which were capable of binding diverse bacteria, fungi and even viruses. The capacity of rLvccCTL to recognize a wide range of PAMPs and microorganisms further support the role of LvccCTL as a key PRR in shrimp immune defense.

In addition to their binding capabilities, most shrimp CTLs exhibit microbial agglutinating activity [25]. In the present study, rLvccCTL agglutinated the tested bacteria but not yeast P. pastoris in a Ca2+-dependent manner. Similar agglutinating patterns have been reported for other CTLs, such as PvMR1 from L. vannamei [36], and PtCLec1 and PtCLec2 from P. trituberculatus [27,28]. The carbohydrate-binding specificity of rLvccCTL was further investigated using microbial agglutination inhibition assays. The results demonstrated that rLvccCTL bound to d-galactose, d-mannose and d-glucose, with a higher affinity for d-galactose. In vertebrate C-type lectins, the QPD motif is known to determine galactose-binding specificity [38]. Some mutated motifs with broader carbohydrate recognition have been reported in several crustacean CTLs, such as PvCTL2 with QAP from L. vannamei [30] and PtCLec1 with YPD from P. trituberculatus [27]. While further mutational analysis of the QPV motif of LvccCTL is needed, such diversified carbohydrate recognition patterns may be important for expanding the recognition spectrum of crustacean CTLs.

As a class of PRRs that detect invading pathogens, CTLs play important roles in initiating the activation of immune signaling pathways [11]. Previous studies have shown that MjCC-CL from M. japonicas could activate the JAK-STAT pathway to regulate AMP expression [10]. A recent study in L. vannamei reported that knockdown of LRRCTL increased the expression of Cactus, an inhibitor of NF-κB transcription factor Dorsal, while suppressing the expression of ALFs and LYZs expression [13]. In the present study, knockdown of LvccCTL resulted in the downregulation of LvALF4, as well as key components in NF-κB pathway (LvToll1, LvToll2, LvCactus and LvRelish) and JAK-STAT pathway (LvDOME, LvJAK and LvSTAT). In mammals, NF-κB and JAK-STAT pathways are known to crosstalk and form a regulatory network [39,40]. Although such crosstalk is less studied in shrimp, previous evidence shows that the JAK-STAT and IMD-Relish pathways shared a common target gene (LvSWD5), suggesting promoter-level convergence [41]. Our findings suggest that LvccCTL may regulate ALF synthesis through both pathways, underscoring its critical role in immune regulation.

As the primary phagocytic organ in shrimp, Oka undergoes enlargement during the filtration of hemolymph and the phagocytic clearance of pathogens [17,42]. For instance, F. chinensis infected with virus exhibited marked Oka enlargement, with a diameter exceeding 2 mm [43], and Penaeus stylirostris infected with rhabdovirus showed Oka weighted six- to seven-fold greater than those of healthy controls [44]. These phenomena resemble inflammatory responses observed in vertebrates. We therefore investigated the involvement of LvccCTL in phagocytosis and inflammatory-like responses in Oka. Unlike its no effect on phagocytosis-related genes, knockdown of LvccCTL could significantly inhibit the expression of inflammatory cytokines (LvIL-16, LvTNFSF, LvSVC4 and LvSVC5). These findings indicate that LvccCTL may play a critical role in the inflammatory process rather than in phagocytosis of Oka.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) serve as key signaling molecules in the progression of inflammatory disorders [45,46]. Therefore, we examined the expression of redox-related genes in LvccCTL-knockdown shrimp, as well as ROS production and iNOS activity in the Oka following LPS challenge. Knockdown of LvccCTL downregulated the expression of three antioxidant-related genes (LvSOD, LvNOS and LvTrxDC), and two ROS-generating enzymes (LvNOX and LvNOX4L), decreased iNOS activity, but increased ROS content. This paradox might be explained by several mechanisms. First, under normal conditions, iNOS-derived NO rapidly consumes superoxide (O2⁻) to form peroxynitrite, reducing the amount of free superoxide detected by DCFH‑DA [47]. After LvccCTL knockdown, iNOS activity decreased, which led to lower NO production. As a result, less superoxide was scavenged, causing superoxide to accumulate and producing a higher fluorescence signal. Second, although ROS production from LvNOX4/LvNOX4L was reduced, the simultaneous downregulation of antioxidant genes (LvSOD and LvTrxDC) may impair ROS scavenging, leading to a net rise in steady-state ROS levels. Third, other unexamined ROS sources (e.g., the mitochondrial respiratory chain or other NADPH oxidases) may also contribute [48]. These results further supported the role of LvccCTL in immune inflammatory response of shrimp.

In conclusion, a lymphoid organ-enriched C-type lectin LvccCTL with immune regulatory functions was identified from L. vannamei. The recombinant LvccCTL protein exhibited significant binding and agglutinating activities to multiple pathogens. Knockdown of LvccCTL downregulated the expression of LvALF4, key genes in NF-κB and JAK-STAT pathways, and inflammation- and redox-related genes. Furthermore, it influenced the LPS-induced respiratory burst in Oka by increasing ROS production and decreasing iNOS activity. All these results suggest that LvccCTL may participate in immune recognition and inflammatory response of shrimp Oka. Future studies using the specific antibody against LvccCTL will be valuable to detect protein expression and explore its interactions with downstream signaling molecules.
