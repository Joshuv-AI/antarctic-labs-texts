Whole-exome sequencing in a patient with a history of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma to discriminate recurrent disease from a second primary tumor
Authors: Guoqing Zhang, Kaiyuan Li, Zheng Ding, Jia Zhao, Xue Pan, Xiangnan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A 52-year-old man who previously underwent McKeown esophagectomy to treat lower thoracic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and subsequently received radical radiotherapy to treat cervical lymph node malignancy (squamous cell carcinoma on pathology; presumed to be metastasis of the primary tumor) presented with recurrent cervical lymphadenopathy during periodic reexamination. On admission, gastroscopy revealed a 0.5 × 0.5-cm lesion on the cervical remnant esophagus, and biopsies of both the lesion and cervical lymph nodes confirmed SCC. Whole-exome sequencing revealed that there were no comutations between the tumor samples from the primary ESCC from 2017 and the other malignancies (tumor samples from the remaining ESCC in 2022 and cervical lymph node malignancies on two occasions). Interestingly, the first and second cervical lymph node malignancies and the tumor in the cervical remnant esophagus shared many comutations, suggesting that the tumor in the cervical remnant esophagus is a second primary tumor with cervical lymph node metastasis and is not amenable to radical radiotherapy. Significant regression of lymphadenopathy was achieved after 2 cycles of immunochemotherapy; however, the patient declined further surgical intervention. Six months later, positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) revealed tumor progression with multiple metastases. Herein, we show the importance of regular examination of the remnant esophagus and the significance of whole-exome sequencing for distinguishing the source of malignancy, which could aid in the formulation of appropriate treatment plans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03453-8.

Background

Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and chemotherapy, followed by esophagectomy, have been shown to improve the prognosis of patients with locally advanced esophageal cancer; however, the prevalence of disease recurrence still ranges from 33.7 to 41.9%, and distant metastasis accounts for the majority of cases of recurrence [1, 2]. Even in early-stage esophageal cancer, patients still face a 13.6–16.54% risk of disease recurrence, a 0.85–7.97% risk of local recurrence and a 12.5–12.7% risk of distant metastasis [3, 4]with a similar recurrence pattern as that of locally advanced esophageal cancer.

In addition to experiencing recurrent clinical conditions, patients may experience a second primary malignancy after esophagectomy, which could theoretically affect any organ, such as the aerodigestive tract mucosa [5, 6]. The rate of second malignancies has been reported to reach 13.8%. Among these conditions, a second primary malignancy of the residual cervical esophageal stump is one of the most difficult conditions to diagnose and treat, with an incidence rate of 1.767% [6].

In patients with disease recurrence and those with a second primary malignancy, individualized clinical decisions should be made. Upon disease recurrence, patients are usually considered to have a low chance of receiving curative treatments and have a poor survival. Fortunately, concurrent chemoradiation and occasional reoperation with R0 resection have been reported to improve the prognosis of patients with local and regional recurrence [7]. Similarly, studies have demonstrated that patients with localized disease can benefit from local therapy for a second primary malignancy [5]. Occasionally, disease recurrence and a second primary malignancy may be difficult to distinguish and could lead to incorrect clinical decision making. Herein, we report a case of esophageal cancer that was initially misdiagnosed as disease recurrence but subsequently confirmed as a second primary cancer via whole-exome sequencing.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male patient with a 20-year history of alcohol consumption had McKeown esophagectomy due to lower thoracic ESCC (labeled T1) at our hospital on August 7, 2017. Postoperative pathology classified the tumor as stage pT1bN0M0 or IB, according to the 8th edition of the AJCC. Postoperative follow-ups were conducted regularly without adjuvant therapy. An ultrasound examination on May 26, 2020, revealed multiple enlarged cervical lymph nodes. Needle biopsy pathology confirmed the presence of squamous cell carcinoma (labeled N1), suggestive of ESCC metastasis. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET‒CT) revealed no other suspected lesions. The multidisciplinary team (MDT) recommended radical radiotherapy (63.60 Gy, 2.12 Gy/30f), and subsequent follow-ups revealed complete clinical remission. One year later, the patient underwent endoscopic hemostasis due to anastomosis bleeding.

Unfortunately, the patient’s cervical lymphadenopathy reappeared 26 months after radical radiotherapy, and biopsy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma (labeled N2). Furthermore, gastroscopy revealed a 0.5 × 0.5-cm lesion on the cervical remnant esophagus, and biopsy pathology confirmed the presence of squamous cell carcinoma (labeled T2) (Fig. 3).

To investigate the clonal relatedness and genomic heterogeneity of T1, T2, N1 and N2, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues with matched germline DNA (Supplementary Material). Diverse extents of private mutations were observed, suggesting high levels of intratumoral heterogeneity in T1, T2, N1 and N2 (Fig. 1). WES revealed no overlapping genetic mutations between the tumor samples from T1 and the subsequent tumors (T2, N1 and N2). Interestingly, the tumor samples from N1, N2 and T2 shared many comutations. Furthermore, a phylogenetic tree was constructed on the basis of somatic mutations. No trunk events were observed in T1 or the subsequent tumors (T2, N1 and N2), indicating that the subsequent tumor was a second primary remnant esophageal cancer with cervical lymph node metastasis. As shown in Fig. 2, T2, N1 and N2 had different percentages of trunk genes, and SYNPO2 had a shared mutation in all subsequent tumors (T2, N1 and N2). OBSCN had a shared mutation between T2 and N2. Therefore, we suspected that the missed esophageal cancer on the cervical remnant esophagus had first developed from a cancerized field and then acquired multiple tumor-specific mutations to evolve (T2) and metastasize (N1 and N2).

Another MDT recommended chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy (sintilimab + cisplatin + paclitaxel). Cervical lymphadenopathy significantly regressed after 2 cycles of immunochemotherapy. The lesion on the cervical remnant esophagus disappeared under gastroscopy. The clinical assessment results of cervical lymphadenopathy were classified as partial response (PR), and cervical esophagectomy and lymphadenectomy were recommended. However, the patient declined further treatment and was discharged.

Half a year later, the patient was readmitted with cervical lymphadenopathy. PET–CT revealed tumor progression with multiple metastases at the esophageal mucosa above the anastomosis and in multiple cervical lymph nodes, left axillary lymph nodes and the second vertebral bone. The patient returned to the local hospital for antitumor therapy (beginning on May 20, 2023). The first-line treatment remained unchanged and included sintilimab, cisplatin and paclitaxel for 6 cycles. The patient subsequently received multiple lines of antitumor therapy, and the treatment is still ongoing (Fig. 3).

Discussion

We report a patient with a cervical lymph node malignancy that was presumed to result from metastasis of the primary tumor but was subsequently confirmed by WES to be a second primary cervical remnant esophageal cancer with cervical lymph node metastasis. According to a previous study, male sex and a drinking history may be high-risk factors for the second primary cancer in this case [6]. This case highlights the need for regular gastroscopy after esophagectomy, even for early-stage ESCC. The anastomosis, gastric tube and cervical remnant esophagus are important areas for endoscopic examination. The reasons for the initial misdiagnosis in this case were a tiny second primary cancer that was overlooked under gastroscopy and that had no hypermetabolic activity on the PET‒CT scan. Considering the 4.2–9.7% prevalence of cervical recurrence [2, 8] and the fact that the time interval between esophagectomy and recurrence was less than 2 years, disease recurrence was possible. Thus, the patient was misdiagnosed with postesophagectomy cervical recurrence.

Considering the aspects of treatment and prognosis, for esophageal cancer patients with combined or newly detected tumors, actively distinguishing between primary tumors and metastatic tumors is necessary. Currently, distinguishing a second primary tumor from tumor metastasis mainly relies on clinicopathological criteria. However, tumors often have similar or even identical clinicopathological features, making the differential diagnosis between a second primary cancer and metastasis difficult. Recently, WES has been widely used to distinguish second primary tumors from tumor metastases [9, 10]. Xue et al. reported different genomic profiles in ESCC patients with lung tumors. Shared genomic profiles between esophageal cancer and lung cancer indicate that the lung tumor is metastatic, and distinct genomic profiles indicate that the lung tumor is a second primary tumor [10]. Similarly, Li et al. used WES to discriminate multicentric origins from intramural metastasis in patients with multifocal ESCC [9]. In this case, we were confronted with a more complex clinical scenario, with two distinct primary esophageal lesions (T1 and T2) and two separate lymph node metastatic lesions (N1 and N2). If the cervical lymphadenopathies (N1 and N2) originated from the first primary carcinoma (T1), we would need to perform only endoscopic resection or radical radiotherapy of the tumor in the cervical remnant esophagus (T2) combined with cervical lymph node dissection. If the cervical lymphadenopathies (N1 and N2) originated from the second primary carcinoma (T2), considering the potential risk of lymphovascular invasion, cervical esophagectomy combined with neck dissection would become necessary. Therefore, thoroughly clarifying the pathological relationship between tumor lesions before treatment is essential, as this distinction has profound implications for therapeutic decision making. To determine the detailed relationship between the malignancies and identify potential therapeutic targets [11]WES was performed and revealed that T1 and the subsequent tumors (T2, N1 and N2) were of different origins. In combination with a phylogenetic tree, we suspected that the missed esophageal cancer on the cervical remnant esophagus had first developed from a cancerized field, followed by cervical lymph node metastasis (N1). Despite achieving clinical complete remission of lymph node metastasis following radical chemoradiotherapy, the second primary lesion remained undiagnosed and led to the recurrence of cervical lymphadenopathy (N2), with multiple tumor-specific mutations.

Esophageal cancer can develop as multiple primary malignancies, synchronously or asynchronously, which can be explained by the concept of “field cancerization” [12]. Thus, in previous studies, tumors in the cervical remnant esophagi were generally regarded as second primary carcinomas [13, 14]. Regular postoperative follow-ups can detect the disease at an early stage. When a tumor is left undiagnosed, there is a risk of tumor progression and metastasis. Several studies have reported that lymphatic flow may be altered by initial distal or segmental gastrectomy [15, 16]. Honda et al. demonstrated that in patients with carcinoma in the remnant stomach after gastrectomy for malignant disease, there were relatively high incidence rates of lymph node metastasis along the greater curvature, splenic hilum, and splenic artery [15]. Nakanishi et al. reported no abdominal lymph node metastasis in patients with esophageal cancer after gastrectomy for malignant disease [16]. However, in similar patients who underwent gastrectomy for benign disease, lymph node metastasis was more extensive [15, 16]. The alteration of lymphatic flow in these circumstances may be due to previous lymph node dissection. The presence of esophageal cancer with cervical lymph node metastasis in this case can be explained by a similar mechanism. The clinical significance of this phenomenon is that the disease is relatively limited until hematogenous metastases occur, and a gastric tube preservation procedure may be indicated in our case.

There is currently no consensus on the treatment of a second primary remnant esophageal cancer. Early detection of a second primary residual esophageal cancer, followed by endoscopic resection, is the best treatment strategy with a promising prognosis [5, 13, 14]. Usami et al. recommended endoscopic resection for mucosal and SM1 cancers without lymph node metastasis and definitive CRT or surgical resection for more advanced cancers. For patients with previous radiotherapy, the only cure is highly invasive surgical resection [5]. A previous study reported the use of laser irradiation, surgical mucosectomy, and segmental resection of the esophagus [13]. In this case, the patient received radical radiotherapy, which targeted the cervical lymph nodes. We intended to perform cervical esophagectomy and salvage cervical lymph node dissection with colon reconstruction via the retrosternal route or with free jejunal interposition. However, the patient was intimidated by potential perioperative complications and refused further surgery. Unfortunately, the disease progressed with widespread metastases, and the chance of curative treatment was lost.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case emphasizes the need for regular gastroscopy after esophagectomy and the importance of WES in distinguishing the source of a malignancy, which can facilitate the development of appropriate treatment plans.
