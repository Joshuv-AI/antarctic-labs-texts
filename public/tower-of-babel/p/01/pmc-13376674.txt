Neurological Manifestations of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Infection in Hospitalized Adults During the 2023–2024 French Epidemic, Clinical Characterization and Comparative Outcomes: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Matthieu Holub, Ariane Gavaud, Céline Louapre, Alexandre Bleibtreu, Morgane Mailhe, Francois Goehringer, Gwenael Le Moal, Louis Bohard, Anne Sophie Lecompte, Marine Avenel, Charlotte Wemmert, David Luque Paz, Victor Gerber, Amélie Dureault, Yannick Fedun, Pierre Girardie, Natacha Mrozek, Sophie Leautez-Nainville, Annabelle Pourbaix, Marion Le Marechal, David Lebeaux, Charles Cazanave, Valérie Pourcher, Guillaume Martin-Blondel, Romain Palich, MYCADO Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) primarily causes respiratory infections but can lead to neurological manifestations. This study aimed to characterize these manifestations in adults during an epidemic.MethodsThe national MYCADO cohort included 1309 adults hospitalized with MP infection during the 2023–2024 French epidemic. An ancillary study identified and described MP-related central (CNS) and peripheral (PNS) nervous system manifestations. Data were retrospectively extracted from medical records, with clinical follow-up up to 3 months after discharge.ResultsTwenty-eight patients with neurological manifestations attributable to MP were identified (2.1% of the cohort). Of these, 19 affected the CNS, including encephalitis (n = 8), myelitis (n = 3), encephalo-myelitis (n = 2), isolated meningitis (n = 6), and cerebral vasculitis (n = 1). Nine involved the PNS: Guillain-Barré syndrome (n = 6), peripheral polyneuropathy (n = 2), and multifocal neuropathy (n = 1). The median age (interquartile range) was 35 years (29–49) years; 60.7% were men. Neurological symptoms appeared a median of 7 days after MP infection onset. Nearly half (46.4%, n = 13) required intensive care; neurological failure was the leading cause of intensive care unit transfer (12/13, 92.3%). Compared with MP patients without neurological manifestations, those with neurological involvement had longer hospital stays (13 vs 9 days; P < .001), more frequent invasive mechanical ventilation (35.7% vs 4.2%; P < .001), and more rehabilitation transfers (42.8% vs 4.3%; P < .001). At discharge, neurological sequelae were present in 47.3% of CNS patients and all Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) patients. At 3 months, sequelae persisted in 36.8% of CNS patients and all GBS patients.ConclusionsIn adults, MP infection causes rare but diverse and frequently severe neurological manifestations that worsen clinical course and result in long-term disability.Keywords: encephalitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, infection, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, neurological

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a leading cause of bacterial pneumonia in children and adults [1, 2]. In Europe, epidemics typically occur every 3 to 4 years [3], with the most recent one occurring in 2023–2024 [4–6]. In France, as in other European countries, the autumn–winter 2023–2024 epidemic was larger than previous ones [7], most likely due to a loss of collective immunity to MP as a result of the barrier measures observed during the years of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [8].

MP is known to cause extrarespiratory clinical manifestations. After dermatological manifestations and hematologic manifestations, neurological manifestations are the most common [9]. It can affect both the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) [10]. The most frequently reported CNS damage is encephalitis, particularly in children, where it can lead to significant morbidity, including lasting neurological sequelae and even death. At the peripheral level, MP is involved in syndromes such as Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) [11, 12] and other peripheral neuropathies. Although neurological manifestations are well documented in pediatric cases [13, 14], they are rarely described in adults.

During the MP infection epidemic in France in 2023–2024, we set up the national, observational MYCADO cohort, with the aim of describing the clinical, biological, and radiological presentations in hospitalized adults [15]. One thousand three hundred nine patients were included from 76 centers, making it the largest series of adults with MP infection published to date. The initial data collection allowed us to identify extrarespiratory manifestations of MP infection. We conducted an ancillary study focused specifically on the neurological manifestations presented by patients included in the MYCADO cohort.

Our objectives were to (i) determine the prevalence of neurological manifestations of MP infection in hospitalized adults, (ii) describe the different clinical syndromes and the results of complementary examinations performed, (iii) assess the presence of neurological sequelae at discharge and 3 months after discharge from the hospital, and (iv) compare the characteristics of patients with and without neurological manifestations within the cohort.

METHODS

Study Design

This ancillary study on neurological manifestations is nested within the MYCADO cohort, which is a national, multicenter observational cohort that included French patients aged 15 years or older with a documented MP infection (confirmed by a positive nucleic acid amplification test [NAAT], regardless of the type of sample, and/or positive immunoglobulin [Ig] M serology or seroconversion), who were hospitalized for >24 hours between September 2023 and February 2024. The neurological manifestations initially reported by clinicians had to meet a combination of criteria, including evidence of MP infection, compatible clinical context, time frame, absence of any alternative etiology, and suggestive clinical context, in order to be included.

Data Collection

All data collected came from clinical records from hospital admission to the last known clinical visit. Data included demographics, medical background, clinical presentation, laboratory and imaging findings, treatments, and clinical course. Data were collected via an anonymous electronic case report form.

Outcomes and Definitions

Encephalitis cases included in this study were required to meet the criteria defined by the International Encephalitis Consortium (IEC-2013) for encephalitis and encephalopathy of presumed infectious or autoimmune etiology [16]. Cases of reported myelitis were considered to be associated with MP and were classified as acute transverse myelitis (ATM) or acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM). Meningitis was defined as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis >4 cells/mm3 and considered isolated if not associated with encephalitis or myelitis. GBS cases fulfilled modified National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke criteria (NINDS) [17], with electroneuromyography (ENMG) performed to classify the subtypes. Other peripheral neuropathies and cerebral vasculitis were diagnosed by the treating clinician and reviewed by the principal investigator.

MP attribution was based on confirmed MP infection, temporal compatibility, exclusion of alternatives, and supportive context, assessed by the clinician responsible for the patient, and confirmed by the main investigators of this ancillary study (M.H., R.P.). Cases with insufficient clinical information or whose neurological diagnosis was revised after multidisciplinary review were excluded. Subgroups of CNS involvement were classified as “early-onset” if symptoms appeared within 7 days of the first clinical signs and as “late-onset” if they appeared after this period.

Sequelae were evaluated at discharge and at 3 months based on clinical examination. Functional independence was retrospectively assessed from medical records using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). This comprehensive scale measures the degree of disability or dependence in daily activities after neurological damage [18]; a score of 2 or less is considered to indicate a good functional prognosis, while a score of 3 or more indicates a poor prognosis.

Ethical Approval

All patients were retrospectively included in the MYCADO study, and their neurological manifestations were retrospectively characterized in this ancillary study if they did not refuse the collection and use of their medical data after receiving information about the study verbally, by mail, or by email. In compliance with French regulation for observational studies, where signed consent is not required, the patient (or their legal guardians or families) received an ethics committee–approved information sheet. The MYCADO study was approved by the Sorbonne University Ethics Committee on December 12, 2023 (No. 2023120412030900000020000360).

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Quantitative variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using either the Mann-Whitney test or the t test, depending on their distribution. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were performed using STATA software (version 14).

RESULTS

Prevalence and Type of Neurological Manifestations

Of the 1309 patients enrolled in the MYCADO cohort across 76 centers, 156 (11.9%) had extrarespiratory manifestations, including 31 (2.3%) with neurological manifestations across 21 centers. After excluding 3 patients whose neurological involvement could not be attributed to MP, 28 were included in this series (2.1%) (Figure 1). The median age (IQR) was 39 (29–48) years, 10 (35.7%) were women, 3 (10.7%) were aged 15–17 years, 3 (10.7%) had immune-mediated inflammatory disease, 2 (7.1%) were immunocompromised, and 1 had a history of neurologic disease (Table 1). The median time between onset of initial symptoms and neurological manifestations (IQR) was 7 (4–13) days. The most common prodromal manifestations were fever and respiratory symptoms, reported in 22 patients (78.6%), with a median onset (IQR) of 6 days before neurological symptoms (2–8 and 2–14, respectively). These were followed by ear, nose, and throat (ENT) symptoms in 12 patients (42.8%), occurring a median (IQR) of 6 (1–12) days prior. Three patients (10.7%) presented with no extraneurological symptoms before the onset of neurological signs. Of these patients, only 1 (3.6%) showed no clinical manifestations that could specifically be attributed to an MP infection. On admission, the median (IQR) C-reactive protein (CRP) was 61 (9–145) mg/L, and leukocyte count was 10.8 (9–14.2) cells/µL. Among the 11 patients who underwent NAAT for MP in CSF, 3 (27.3%) were positive. Neurological manifestations were seen in 19/28 (67.9%) with CNS involvement and 9/28 (32.1%) with PNS involvement (Figure 1). CNS cases comprised 8 encephalitis, 3 myelitis, 2 encephalomyelitis, 5 isolated meningitis, and 1 cerebral vasculitis; PNS cases included 6 GBS, 2 polyneuropathies, and 1 multifocal neuropathy. Among CNS cases, 10/19 (52.6%) were early-onset and 9/19 (47.4%) late-onset; 4/19 (21.1%) had a poor prognosis at 3 months, and 15/19 (78.9%) had a good prognosis (Supplementary Figure 1 and Table 1).

Encephalitis and Myelitis

Most patients presented with sensory–motor deficits (9/13, 69.2%) and altered consciousness (8/13, 61.5%), while meningeal syndrome (4/13, 30.8%) and epileptic seizures (2/13, 15.3%) were less frequent (Table 2). CSF analysis showed median cellularity (IQR) of 220 (8–278) cells/μL, with lymphocyte predominance in 5/8 (186 [79–261] cells/µL) and neutrophil predominance in 3/8 (333 [174–856] cells/µL); median CSF protein (IQR) was 0.95 (0.51–1.66) g/L. No hypoglycorrhachia was reported. Of 10 encephalitis patients, 9 underwent magnetic resonance imaging; 7 showed abnormalities, mostly white matter (n = 6), with 3 involving the corpus callosum (Supplementary Figure 2). Lesions were mainly supratentorial (6/7, 85.7%), and infratentorial in 4 (57.1%). One encephalomyelitis case was classified as ADEM (Supplementary Figure 3). Among 5 myelitis patients, 2 were ATM, 2 had extensive myelitis, and 2 multifocal involvements. Almost all patients received antibiotics (12/13, 92.3%), with median delay (IQR) of 10 (6–18) days, including macrolides (spiramycin, azithromycin, erythromycin, or clarithromycin), a fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin) and a cycline (doxycycline), corticosteroids in 8/13 (61.5%), intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIg) in 2 (15.4%), and plasma exchange in 3 (23.1%). Seven (53.8%) were transferred to ICU, mainly for neurological failure (6/7), with a median delay (IQR) of 9 (8–10) days after the first symptoms, with 5 requiring mechanical ventilation. Eight patients (61.5%) presented with sequelae, including motor (n = 6), vesico-sphincteric (n = 4), sensory (n = 3), and cognitive (n = 1) involvement; 38.5% of the patients required rehabilitation. The median mRS at discharge (IQR) and 3 months were 2 (1–4) and 1 (0–2), with poor outcomes in 4 CNS patients (21%) (Table 2).

Guillain-Barré Syndrome

All GBS patients presented with involvement of all 4 limbs; 3 had sensory–motor involvement. Neurological symptoms appeared after a median (IQR) of 9 (6–13) days from the initial infection. The median CSF protein (IQR) was 0.62 (0.32–1.39) g/L, and median cellularity (IQR) was 10 (0–34) cells/μL. Antiganglioside antibody testing was performed in 5 of 6 patients and was positive in 4 (80%). Detected antibodies included anti-GM1 IgG and/or IgM (n = 3), anti-GM2 IgM (n = 2), anti-GM3 IgM (n = 1), anti-GD1a IgM (n = 1), and antisulfatides IgG and IgM (n = 1). ENMG revealed axonal involvement in 4 cases and demyelination in 3, including 2 cases of acute motor axonal neuropathies (AMANs) and 1 of acute motor–sensory axonal neuropathy (ASMAN). All patients received IVIg (2 g/kg), and 1 underwent plasma exchange. Four patients received antibiotics for pulmonary involvement, and 1 received doxycycline for neurological disease. Five patients required transfer to the ICU and mechanical ventilation (Figure 2). The median hospital stay (IQR) was 29 (18–39) days. All patients had motor sequelae at discharge, primarily tetraparesis, and sensory sequelae in 2 cases. The median mRS (IQR) was 3.5 (3.0–4.25). They were all transferred to a rehabilitation unit, with a median mRS (IQR) of 2.0 (1.75–3.25) after 3 months.

Other Neurological Manifestations: Isolated Meningitis, Polyneuropathy, Multifocal Neuropathy, and Cerebral Vasculitis

Five patients developed isolated meningitis, with only 1 requiring transfer to the ICU (Table 2). This patient was treated with antibiotics and corticosteroids and made a full recovery within 48 hours. All cases had a favorable outcome without sequelae. All 3 neuropathies were sensory. Two presented as peripheral polyneuropathies: 1 with antiganglioside antibodies but no GBS on ENMG and the other linked to cold agglutinins. The third presented as a multifocal neuropathy affecting the trigeminal and occipital nerves, appearing 2 days after MP infection. All cases resolved without treatment. Additionally, a 23-year-old man developed small-vessel cerebral vasculitis 14 days after the onset of infection (Supplementary Figure 4). He received sequential antibiotics and corticosteroids and was discharged with an improved neurological status, but with residual fluctuating aphasia and tremor (mRS = 2).

Patient Characteristics According to Presence or Absence of Neurological Manifestations

We compared the 28 patients exhibiting neurological manifestations with the 1281 patients who did not (Table 3). The demographics were similar between the 2 groups. Patients with neurological involvement exhibited fewer respiratory symptoms (78.6% vs 95.7%; P < .001) and lower CRP levels (61 vs 132 mg/L; P < .001). Additionally, serology was employed more frequently for diagnosis: 12 (42.8%) were diagnosed by NAAT alone, 6 (21.4%) by both NAAT and serology, and 10 (35.7%) by serology alone. Effective antibiotic therapy against MP was administered less frequently (78.6% vs 96.7%; P < .001), while immunomodulatory treatments were used more frequently. Patients with neurological manifestations experienced poorer outcomes, including longer hospital stays (9 vs 13 days; P < .001), more frequent mechanical ventilation (35.7% vs 4.2%; P < .001), and a greater need for rehabilitation or follow-up care after discharge (42.8% vs 4.3%; P < .001).

DISCUSSION

In the MYCADO cohort study, which was conducted during the autumn–winter epidemic of 2023–2024 in France, neurological manifestations of MP infection were identified in 2.1% of the 1309 adults. Although uncommon, these manifestations affected both the CNS and PNS and were often severe. They were associated with ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, prolonged hospitalization, and a high rate of neurological sequelae at discharge and after 3 months. Notably, these manifestations could occur in the absence of respiratory symptoms, which could complicate their recognition and diagnosis.

Radiological findings, including white matter lesions and corpus callosum involvement, mirror those described in pediatric MP encephalitis and ADEM [19]. However, the clinical presentations differed; unlike in pediatric cases, where seizures are frequent [20], patients in our study predominantly presented with motor or sensory deficits. Reported sequelae rates in pediatric encephalitis range from 20% to 34% [21], which is lower than the rate observed in our adult cohort. This difference may reflect reduced neuroplasticity and a more pronounced immune response in adults. Nevertheless, when compared with adult infectious encephalitis overall [22], MP encephalitis appeared to result in fewer long-term functional deficits. MP is a recognized cause of GBS, ranking second only to Campylobacter jejuni [23]. In our cohort, cases of GBS were often severe, with frequent axonal subtypes (AMAN and ASMAN), admission to ICU, and prolonged mechanical ventilation. This is consistent with the poorer prognosis associated with the condition [24]. The diagnosis of MP-related neurological disease is challenging [16], largely due to its complex and heterogeneous pathophysiology [25]. While direct CNS invasion may occur, most evidence supports an immune-mediated mechanism [26]. This is supported by the low rate of intrathecal MP detection [27], the temporal delay between infection and the onset of neurological symptoms in many cases, and the frequent involvement of the white matter. Molecular mimicry between MP antigens and neural glycolipids likely plays a key role, particularly in PNS manifestations. Antibodies directed against gangliosides and galactocerebrosides have previously been described [28] and were also observed in our study. The coexistence of early- and late-onset neurological forms suggests that para- and postinfectious mechanisms may both contribute. Antibiotic therapy was frequently administered, most often macrolides for pulmonary involvement, in accordance with current guidelines [29]. For neurological manifestations, fluoroquinolones or tetracyclines were frequently selected due to their in vitro activity against M. pneumoniae and their favorable CNS penetration compared with macrolides, although robust clinical data supporting their efficacy in this specific setting remain limited [30]. Interestingly, the CSF findings in our adult cohort did not follow the pattern described in the pediatric literature, where early-onset disease is typically associated with direct CNS invasion and late-onset disease with immune-mediated pathology. In our adult patients, CSF MP NAAT was positive only in late-onset cases, and lymphocytic predominance was more frequent in early-onset disease. Although these numbers are small, this observation suggests that the 7-day threshold derived from pediatric data may not directly apply to adults. One possible explanation is that prior immunological exposure to MP, which accumulates with age [31], may alter the temporal dynamics of immune-mediated neurological injury. This high background seroprevalence also implies that causal attribution in the 10 neurological cases (35.7%) diagnosed via serology alone, though supported by the structured adjudication process, is inherently less secure than in NAAT-confirmed cases.

Despite these physiological uncertainties, this study provides a comprehensive description of MP-related neurological manifestations in adults during a large epidemic. It demonstrates that MP is not only a respiratory pathogen, but also a clinically significant cause of severe neurological manifestation in adults. The findings highlight the need for heightened clinical awareness, particularly since neurological complications may occur without respiratory involvement and present with nonspecific features. ENT symptoms are frequently found in the medical history and should also be sought in this context, as they may point to the diagnosis.

The main strengths of this study include its nationwide, multicenter design, the large underlying cohort, and standardized case review within a well-characterized epidemic context. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Lumbar puncture and CSF NAAT for MP were not systematically performed in all cases, although MP attribution was individually reviewed for each patient as described above. Therapeutic strategies depended on clinicians, and the small number of neurological cases limited outcome comparisons and precluded correction for multiple testing. Furthermore, the mRS may not fully capture specific neurological deficits, and the follow-up period of 3 months may have underestimated long-term sequelae, particularly in GBS patients with axonal subtypes (AMAN/AMSAN), for whom recovery can extend over 6 to 12 months or longer [23]. Finally, only hospitalized patients were included, potentially excluding milder neurological forms managed in outpatient settings [32].

Clinicians should consider MP infection in adults presenting with unexplained encephalitis, myelitis, or GBS, particularly during epidemic periods and even in the absence of respiratory symptoms. Diagnostic strategies should combine NAAT and serology. Although antibiotic therapy is usually based on pulmonary involvement, the role of targeted antibiotics and immunomodulatory treatments in neurological cases is unclear and should be determined on a case-by-case basis according to severity. Prospective studies are needed to improve our understanding of the underlying immunological mechanisms. Controlled trials could evaluate the role of immunomodulatory and antibiotic treatments in cases of severe CNS manifestation.
