The Immuno-oncology-Induced Cytokine Release Syndrome Patient Diary: A Content-Valid, Patient-Reported Outcome Measure
Authors: Edward Ted Wells, Laurence Lucats, Giovanni Abbadessa, James Turnbull, Joyce R. Talavera, Sophie R. Van Tomme, Paul Cordero, Benoit Arnould, Matthew Reaney, Catherine Coulouvrat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurs when the immune system reacts excessively to infections or certain immunotherapies, leading to a systemic inflammatory response. The clinical presentation of CRS is highly variable, ranging from mild symptoms, such as fever and fatigue, to severe, life-threatening conditions. Currently, there is no standardized method for collecting patient-reported data on CRS experiences. This study aims to develop a novel patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) to capture patients’ experience with CRS.MethodsA comprehensive, multistep evidence-generation process was employed, including several research steps: a literature review and analysis of Sanofi clinical trial adverse event data, semi-structured interviews with clinical experts, advisory board meetings with clinical trial investigators, concept elicitation interviews with patients who experienced CRS, a PROM development workshop, cognitive debriefing interviews with clinical trial investigators, and cognitive debriefing interviews with patients.ResultsThis review identified various CRS-related signs and symptoms from literature and anonymized safety data from clinical trials. Clinical experts validated the preliminary conceptual model (CM) and identified additional relevant symptoms. Patient interviews revealed further symptoms and impacts, leading to the development of the immuno-oncology (IO)-induced CRS patient diary. This PROM was refined through iterative feedback from clinical experts and patients, ensuring it effectively captures the incidence, severity, and impact of CRS symptoms.ConclusionsThe “IO-induced CRS patient diary” can be considered a content-valid tool for capturing and monitoring the most important patient-reportable symptoms and impacts of CRS in IO clinical trials. This PROM can provide a standardized understanding of IO-induced CRS from the patient perspective, where to date this has been mainly clinically assessed using diverse assessments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-025-00383-x.

Introduction

Immuno-oncology (IO) therapy is a type of cancer treatment that leverages or enhances the immune system’s ability to recognize, target, and destroy tumor cells [1, 2]. Clinical development of IO therapies has advanced exponentially in the past decade, with regulatory bodies worldwide approving the use of chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells, monoclonal and bispecific antibodies, cytokines, and immune checkpoint inhibitors [3, 4]. Compared with traditional anticancer therapies, such as chemotherapy, molecularly targeted therapies, and radiotherapy, IO therapy is considered to be more specific with a wider breadth of response [5]. Particularly in the case of metastatic cancers, IO therapy is associated with improved clinical outcomes and better quality of life than these abovementioned therapies [5].

However, because of immune hyperactivation with IO therapy, patients may experience various adverse events. Such adverse events can be either systemic or localized, affecting specific anatomical regions, including the stomach and intestines, skin, liver, endocrine system, and lungs [6]. IO therapy has been associated with cytokine release syndrome (CRS), a constellation of signs and symptoms that can present as early as a few hours after treatment administration [7].

CRS involves the overwhelming release of cytokines, which causes the immune system to go into a hyperinflammatory, unregulated state [8]. Its clinical presentation is heterogeneous: it can manifest as mild, with fever, nausea, fatigue, headache, rash, arthralgia, myalgia, and chills; or severe, with vascular leakage, disseminated intravascular coagulation, shock, and respiratory compromise, which may lead to multisystem organ failure or death [9, 10].

At present, there is no standardized tool to collect information on patients’ experience of CRS signs, symptoms, and impacts. Existing clinician-reported grading systems do not fully capture the patient perspective, and there is a general lack of understanding of CRS, especially in non-CAR-T therapies [11]. According to clinical criteria, CRS events are mild to moderate; however, the patient-perceived severity of CRS signs or symptoms and impacts on their daily lives is not well understood. This gap highlights the need for tools that can reliably and consistently capture patient-reported experiences to support a more holistic and patient-centered approach to care [12, 13].

In the present qualitative research study, we aimed to address this unmet need by exploring patients’ experiences of CRS, including the most important symptoms and impacts on how patients feel and function. In alignment with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)’s Patient-Focused Drug Development (PFDD) guidance [14], we sought to develop a novel, content-valid patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) to track the onset and resolution of IO therapy-induced CRS events (and their impacts on patients’ lives) in clinical trials.

Methods

This research study involved several steps: a targeted literature review (TLR) of published research and safety data, input from clinical experts, and concept elicitation (CE) interviews with patients informed the development of a conceptual model (CM) of IO therapy-induced (CRS), outlining the signs, symptoms, and impacts on people’s lives. The CM was used to draft a PROM for evaluating the onset and resolution of IO therapy-induced CRS events (and their impacts) in clinical trials; the draft PROM was refined and content-validated through cognitive debriefing (CD) interviews with clinicians and patients, and the PROM was then finalized for exploratory use in clinical trials (Fig. S1).

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its later amendments and is consistent with Good Clinical Practice and applicable regulatory requirements. Centralized approval was obtained from the Western IRB-Copernicus Group (WCG) IRB, including for all recruitment materials. IQVIA is responsible for submitting the application and all study documents for study approval to the IRB. The study was initiated only after IRB approval was granted, including the use of all recruitment materials.

Informed consent was obtained prior to performing any study-related procedures, including screening, and patients provided consent via the online consent form. Patients were informed that, if the results of the study were published for scientific purposes, the patient’s identity would remain confidential. Clinicians who participated in the CD interviews provided verbal consent at the start of the interview after the objectives and logistics of the interview were shared with them. They provided verbal consent to both participate and to also be audio-recorded.

A standard ethics approved informed consent process was followed for all patients involved in this study. This required their review and signature of an informed consent form document that clearly outlined the study background, objectives, and procedures (including screening, risks and benefits, compensation, confidentiality, information use and data sharing, and voluntary participation and withdrawal). Completed and signed forms were reviewed by the study team for completeness prior to any interviews being conducted. For participating clinicians, informed consent was obtained verbally at the start of each interview. Prior to providing consent, clinicians were informed of the study background and objectives, interview logistics, and information regarding their confidentiality and how their data will be used.

Developing a Conceptual Model of IO Therapy-Induced CRS

Targeted Literature Review

A TLR of published literature on patients receiving IO therapies from 2017 to 2022 was conducted to identify patient-reported signs and symptoms potentially associated with IO therapy-induced CRS events. The TLR was enriched with data from anonymized safety reports (Sanofi clinical trial adverse event data). Aggregated weighted prevalence, when available, was calculated for each sign and symptom, along with average onset and duration values. Details of the prevalence of each sign and symptom from the TLR are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Concept Elicitation Research with Clinical Experts

Semi-structured clinical expert interviews were conducted to further understand signs and symptoms of IO therapy-induced CRS events. Specifically, four clinical oncology or hematology specialists from the USA were asked to define IO therapy-induced CRS in 1:1 CE interviews. They were also asked to provide dimensional descriptors (onset, duration, frequency, and resolution) of common signs and symptoms they encounter frequently in the clinic, identify patient-reportable ones, and describe the impacts of CRS on patients’ lives. Following the clinical expert interviews, an advisory board meeting was held with five academic oncologists (four from the USA and one from Australia). They provided further input on the list of patient-reportable symptoms of an IO therapy-induced CRS event that were identified in the TLR and clinical expert interviews. Advisory board members also further expanded on the list of possible impacts experienced by patients who experience these events.

Concept Elicitation Interviews with Patients

Recruitment

In total, 14 patients were recruited via two third-party vendors and through partnership with two patient advocacy groups to add the lived-experience perspective to the list of signs, symptoms, and impacts of CRS, as well as to understand language used by patients. Each patient’s initial eligibility for the study was assessed using the patient-completed screening form and subsequently confirmed by the clinician in the confirmation of diagnosis form, on the basis of the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: age > 18 years; diagnosis of any stage of advanced, metastatic, relapsed, or refractory cancer or hematologic malignancy (any indication); a CRS event ≤ 12 months before screening; and currently on or previously treated with non-CAR-T (including blinatumomab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, avelumab, dostarlizumab, durvalumab, ipilimumab, and nivolumab) or CAR-T therapy in the past 12 months (the first 11 interviews included only patients on non-CAR-T therapy, with the remaining 3 including patients receiving CAR-T therapy). Meanwhile, exclusion criteria were: currently participating in any investigational study for oncological treatment or participated in such a study in the past 12 months, or being unable to participate in a 60-min teleconference interview.

Interviews

Semi-structured CE interviews were conducted with patients on a virtual conferencing platform, following a semi-structured interview guide. These were audio recorded. Patients were first asked to describe and define what they understood to be a CRS event. They were thereafter asked open-ended questions that were designed to elicit spontaneous mentions of signs, symptoms, and impacts they experienced at the time of the CRS event. They were asked to rate the severity and bothersomeness of each symptom they reported and how the CRS event was managed, both by themselves and any healthcare professionals.

Analysis

The interviews were transcribed, with all personal information redacted. Transcripts were coded in MAXQDA Plus® qualitative data analysis software (2022 version) by trained, experienced qualitative analysts. The coding process identified and categorized patients’ descriptions of concepts regarding their CRS experience using both deductive (on the basis of a predefined coding framework developed from prior steps in this study) and inductive coding approaches.

Initial transcripts were dual coded until intercoder agreement was achieved. Overall, about 36% of transcripts from CE interviews were dual coded by assigned coders to establish and maintain intercoder alignment throughout the coding process. Discrepancies were discussed with the study team, and decisions implemented consistently across coders for all future transcripts. The number of patients who reported particular symptoms and impacts was tabulated using a quantitative descriptive approach.

As mentioned in the second FDA PFDD guidance [15], saturation is defined as the point when no new relevant or important information emerges, and collecting additional data will not likely add to the understanding of how patients perceive the concept of interest. In the present study, saturation of concepts was assessed by comparing the reporting of concept codes derived from one group of interviews with the next group of interviews, identifying if the same or new concepts were appearing in subsequent interviews.

CRS Conceptual Model Development

Data from the TLR, clinical expert interviews, oncologist advisory board, and CE interviews with patients were qualitatively collected to inform development of a CM of IO therapy-induced CRS, outlining signs, symptoms, and impacts on patient lives.

PROM Development

A list of prioritized patient-reportable symptoms and impacts was generated for inclusion in the PROM on the basis of the CM. Specifically, symptoms were considered on the basis of weighted prevalence in the TLR and prevalence in anonymized safety reports; whether clinical experts flagged the symptom as common; whether the advisory board recommended its inclusion in the PROM; the average time of onset and resolution identified from anonymized safety reports; and the frequency, severity ratings, and most bothersome symptom reported by patients. For each of these signs and symptoms, prevalence data were extracted from the source reporting the highest prevalence and from two safety reporting datasets; while no minimum prevalence threshold was set, signs and symptoms were organized and examined from highest to lowest weight prevalence (Table S1).

When a list of prioritized symptoms and impacts had been identified, they were mapped to potentially applicable existing PROM item libraries from the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer, Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy, and National Cancer Institute groups. Item wording and response scales of mapped PROM items were examined against the language patients used in the CE interviews, and the appropriateness of the recall period was evaluated on the basis of onset and resolution data and on collected patient descriptions. Ultimately, items were drafted de novo for the PROM using FDA guidance on clinical outcome assessment development as guidance [14] as no existing item libraries had items and features applicable to all prioritized concepts.

Refinement of the PROM

Cognitive Debriefing Interviews with Clinical Oncologists

CD interviews were held with three clinical oncologists who participated in the advisory board to obtain feedback on the draft PROM. Before each interview, the clinician was informed of the development history, purpose, and context of use for the PROM. They were then asked to confirm the comprehensiveness, grouping, clinical relevance, and clinical meaningfulness of each symptom item and response scale. Finally, they were asked to comment on the impact items and their specific phrasing, and for their perspectives on the proposed schedule of assessment and potential patient burden. The PROM was refined prior to patient interviews.

Cognitive Debriefing Interviews with Patients

Recruitment

A total of 15 patients who received non-CAR-T therapy were recruited from among those who participated in the CE interviews, those who were approached for CE but did not qualify, and additional patients from a patient advocacy group.

Interviews

Interviews followed a semi-structured interview guide. Patients were asked to complete each section of the draft PROM using think-aloud techniques. They were thereafter asked for their interpretation of and feedback on instructions, items, and response options in the draft PROM. The interviews were transcribed, with all personal information redacted.

Analysis

Patient CD interviews were conducted in three waves of five patients each. Between each wave, the feedback obtained was analyzed thematically to identify trends across patients. Modifications were made to the draft PROM, interview guide, and approach between each wave. Feedback from the entire sample was reviewed after the third wave to determine whether any final modifications or updates to the PROM were needed.

Finalization of the PROM

The draft PROM was finalized as the IO-induced CRS patient diary. Formatting was updated across the PROM to add emphasis to different components (e.g., recall period, concepts, and severity).

Results

Developing a Conceptual Model of IO Therapy-Induced CRS

Targeted Literature Review

Overall, 19 signs and symptoms from CRS-like events were identified from the TLR, and 26 were identified from anonymized safety data. In total, 10 of the 19 signs and symptoms identified in the TLR were also identified from anonymized safety data, meaning that 35 unique CRS-related signs and symptoms were identified across the sources.

Concept Elicitation Interviews with Clinical Experts

The clinical experts confirmed the relevance of all signs and symptoms from the TLR, except dysfunctional blood regulation, which they felt was nonspecific and poorly understood.

The experts described eight new signs or symptoms that were not identified in the TLR but were considered to be related directly to CRS: malaise, arthralgia, diarrhea, myalgia, edema, redness at site, change in mental status, and brain fog. The advisory board generated a shortlist of the most clinically important CRS signs and symptoms and sorted them by organ class. They recommended removal of four signs (desaturation, capillary leakage, dysfunctional blood regulation, and hypertension) and five symptoms (flu-like symptoms, lethargy, lack of vigor, drowsiness, and loss of following instructions) from the list derived from the TLR and clinical expert interviews. This left 17 signs and 29 symptoms. They then proposed the addition of one further symptom (chest pain). They differentiated between patient-reportable and nonpatient-reportable signs and symptoms. In addition, they identified and described eight broad impacts likely relevant across patients: residual or lingering fatigue, increased desire to rest, physical limitation owing to feeling unwell, impact on desire to do instrumental activities of daily living, avoidance of additional healthcare interactions, anxiety regarding future treatment, emotional burden on family, and symptom-related distress.

CRS Conceptual Model Development

A CM was developed on the basis of the above research to provide a comprehensive understanding of CRS events that could be described and reported by patients in a PROM. In acknowledgement of the fact that CRS can present heterogeneously, the CM included all signs, symptoms, and impacts identified by patients and/or clinical experts in the CE research, as both are important to track to understand IO-induced CRS and patient-reportable signs or symptoms. Signs or symptoms identified only in the TLR but not endorsed through subsequent research were not included. The final CM included 44 signs and symptoms across five organ systems and 31 impacts across six domains (Fig. 2). Patient-Reported Outcome Measure Development

Through an iterative process, 12 patient-reportable symptoms and four impacts were ultimately selected from the final CM for inclusion in the PROM (Fig. 3). The PROM was designed to measure the incidence and severity of each symptom, and the impact of symptoms generally on regular daily activities, worry, and physical ability. Manageability of symptoms and overall perceived health were also prioritized for inclusion in the PROM.Fig. 3Patient-reportable symptoms and impacts from the conceptual model selected to create the patient PROM. PROM patient-reported outcome measures, CD Cognitive debreifing

Patient-reportable symptoms and impacts from the conceptual model selected to create the patient PROM. PROM patient-reported outcome measures, CD Cognitive debreifing

Six PROM development criteria were followed: (1) include measurement of a common set of symptoms representing the most commonly experienced CRS-related symptoms as reported by patients, clinical experts, and the investigator advisory board; (2) measure severity of these symptoms, rather than frequency; (3) capture information about symptoms prior to first treatment, on the day of each treatment, and on subsequent days after treatments; (4) include measurement of a limited set of impacts representing those that are most relevant to patients; (5) capture information about impacts prior to first treatment and weekly after each treatment; and (6) use terminology consistent with (a) language used by patients in CE interviews, and (b) language proposed for measurement of signs and symptoms by physicians in clinical expert interviews and the investigator advisory board.

The structure of the draft PROM is presented in Fig. 4. Three versions of the PROM were developed to be administered at different time points (baseline, day of treatment, and subsequent days) (Fig. 5). The recall period depended on which version of the PROM was administered. An electronic (e)PROM format was used for all three versions.Fig. 4Administration schedule for the IO-induced CRS patient diary. In the first question, patients are presented with a single item showing all 12 symptoms. Patients are asked to indicate which (if any) they have experienced over the recall period. There is also a free-text box for patients to add “other” symptoms that they experienced. This symptom list is a new item drafted for this PROM. For each symptom that they have experienced, patients are then presented with an item about the severity of that symptom on a four-point severity response scale of “mild,” “moderate,” “severe,” and “very severe.” All items used this simple structure to measure the severity of each concept, utilizing the language patients used in the CE interviews. Additional items were developed de novo utilizing the language patients used in the CE interviews to capture the overall impact of symptoms on patients. A numeric rating scale (NRS), Likert scale, or dichotomous scale were used on the basis of the item wording. Not all impacts are measured in all three versions (see Fig. 5).

This PROM is not intended for use as a diagnostic tool but rather to supplement existing grading scales by tracking the onset and resolution of CRS and impacts on patients’ lives.

Refinement and Finalization of the Patient-Reported Outcome Measure

Cognitive Debriefing Interviews with Clinical Oncologists

Clinical oncologists proposed adding headache to the draft PROM, and to separate chills and shaking. They also proposed removing items “stay at home or rest” (measuring symptoms experienced since receiving the study medication that caused the patient to stay at home or rest more than normal), “regular daily activities” (measuring symptoms that had an impact on regular daily activities), and “worry because of symptoms impact” (measuring how much experienced symptoms caused worry), as well as clarifying recall periods to reduce confusion. The response options for impact on “regular daily activities” and “worry due to symptoms” were changed from a NRS to a 4-point Likert scale. Lastly, formatting was amended to remove capitalization and to boldface only symptom severity and recall period.

Cognitive Debriefing Interviews with Patients

CD interviews were conducted with 15 patients in three waves with five different patients each: wave 1 included patients P1–P5, wave 2 included patients P6–P10, and wave 3 included patients P11–P15. Nine of these patients had also participated in the CE interviews (five in wave 1, three in wave 2, and one in wave 3). Patients’ demographic characteristics are provided in Table 1. The 15 total patients who received non-CAR-T therapy were recruited from among those who participated in the CE interviews, those who were approached for CE but did not qualify, and additional patients from a patient advocacy group.

Three versions of the IO-induced CRS patient diary were developed, to be administered at different time points in the treatment journey. This ensures that the patient experience throughout their symptom journey is holistically captured. PROM Baseline Version

The overall instructions for this version were interpreted as intended by all patients across the three waves (n = 15/15). No modifications were recommended or made to the instructions. All patients (n = 15/15) interpreted the symptom list item as intended, and 14 patients (n = 14/15) reported that the symptom list was clear; one patient reported being unclear about whether “symptoms” in the question were referring to symptoms from the IO therapy or symptoms from their cancer. In total, 13 patients (n = 13/15) interpreted the recall as intended, whereas 1 (n = 1/15) expressed some difficulty thinking back over the past week, and 1 patient (n = 1/15) considered the recall period to be more than 7 days. Since three patients reported entering symptoms unrelated to IO therapy in the free-text box, the “other” response option and the corresponding screen with the text box were removed from all versions of the PROM to avoid complications with data analysis to track CRS onset and offset.

All patients providing feedback (n = 14/14), interpreted the symptom severity instructions as intended, and considered the instructions clear. All patients (n = 15/15) interpreted the severity scale for each symptom as intended and were able to report their experience. Of 12 patients who were probed on their preference between a frequency scale and a severity scale for gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, vomiting, and nausea), 5 preferred a frequency scale, 3 preferred the severity scale, and 4 suggested retaining the severity scale but adding an item for gastrointestinal symptoms with a frequency scale. Since this would prolong PROM completion, the severity scale was retained for all symptoms. To improve the clarity and consistency of the introductory instruction, “symptom(s)” was revised to “symptoms.” This change was implemented across all three versions.

The overall perceived health impact item was debriefed for this version of the PROM. In wave 1, two patients (n = 2/5) interpreted this item as intended (linking overall health to the symptoms they reported). Changes were made to the wording of this item for clarity. In waves 2 and 3, nine patients (n = 9/10) interpreted the question in the item as intended, and one patient primarily considered their functionality in relation to their symptoms. Seven patients (n = 7/9) interpreted the response options as intended; two patients (n = 2/9) had some difficulty with the “neither poor nor good” response options. Eight patients (n = 8/9) considered the item to be clear, and one patient (n = 1/9) considered it to be unclear because they did not perceive the response options as relating to health. No further changes to this item were deemed necessary after wave 3.

PROM Day-of-Treatment Version

The instructions for the day-of-treatment version of the PROM were the same as for the baseline version, except for the recall period, which was “since receiving the study medication today” (instead of “in the past week”). Overall,. 13 patients (n = 13/14) interpreted the overall instructions for the day-of-treatment version and the associated recall period as intended. No changes to the instructions were considered necessary.

Patients were not probed on symptom items, since this section in the day-of-treatment version was similar to the baseline version. Impact items were tested in the baseline version (overall health) and the subsequent-days version (other impacts).

PROM Subsequent-Days Version

The instructions for the subsequent-days version were the same as for the baseline except for the recall period, which was “the past 24 h.” All patients providing feedback (n = 14/14), interpreted the overall instructions for the subsequent-days version as intended, and considered the instructions to be clear. After wave 1, “in the past 24 h” was rephrased to “over the past 24 h.” All patients providing feedback (n = 13/13) interpreted the recall as intended.

All impacts except “overall perceived health” were debriefed for this version. The “stay at home or rest” impact item was revised twice to improve clarity and reporting during CD. After wave 1, the response scale was changed from “yes/no” to a Likert scale, which was preferred by eight patients (three preferred a dichotomous scale), and to “…over the past 24 h” from “…in the past 24 h” on the basis of discussions within the study team. After wave 3, the item was revised only to ask about “rest” rather than “stay at home and rest.” Since this change was made after wave 3, the final item has not undergone CD.

For the “regular daily activities” impact item, all patients interpreted the question (n = 15/15) and recall (n = 11/11) as intended, and considered it to be clear (n = 15/15). In total, 13 patients (n = 13/15) interpreted the response options as intended, 1 patient (n = 1/15) reported difficulty with quantifying the response options, and 1 patient (n = 1/15) reported difficulty with differentiating between “quite a bit” and “very much.”

For the “manageability of symptoms” impact item, 13 patients (n = 13/15) interpreted the question in the item as intended, 1 patient (n = 1/15) interpreted the question slightly differently as the ability to withstand symptoms, and 1 patient (n = 1/15) interpreted it to mean one’s physical ability while experiencing symptoms. Overall, 12 patients (n = 12/14) interpreted the response options as intended, 1 patient (n = 1/14) reported difficulty with defining “neither difficult nor easy” and recommended removing the response option, and 1 patient (n = 1/14) reported difficulty differentiating between “easy” and “very easy” or “difficult” or “very difficult.” In total, 14 patients (n = 14/15) interpreted the item as clear, although 1 patient suggested replacing “manage” with “handle symptoms” on the basis of their interpretation.

Fourteen patients (n = 14/15) interpreted the question in the “worry” impact item as intended, and one patient selected a response option on the basis of their experience with their cancer. Twelve patients (n = 12/14) interpreted the response options as intended, and two patients (n = 2/14) interpreted “quite a bit” and “very much” as similar response options. Fourteen patients (n = 14/15) considered the item to be clear, and one patient (n = 1/15) suggested that the question could be shortened.

Apart from a revision after wave 1 to improve the consistency of phrasing across the PROM, no other changes were considered necessary to the “regular daily activities,” “manageability of symptoms,” and “worry” impact items.

Overall

Overall, patients described the PROM as easy to understand and complete. When asked whether they would be able to complete the PROM daily after receipt of an IO treatment, 11 patients (n = 11/14) indicated that they would, while 3 patients (n = 3/14) indicated that they would not be able to complete a daily diary immediately after IO treatment owing to the way they may feel.

Six patients (n = 6/14) reported that they would be able to complete the PROM even while experiencing severe symptoms, five patients (n = 5/14) reported that they would not be able to complete it, and three patients (n = 3/14) reported that they would be able to complete it but would require assistance. Patients most frequently reported vomiting, diarrhea, headaches, and pounding or racing heartbeat as symptoms (two mentions each) which, if they became severe, would hamper PROM completion.

Discussion

In the present study, a novel PROM—the IO-induced CRS patient diary—was developed to track the onset, resolution, and impact of IO therapy-induced CRS events in clinical trials. This study ties into FDA initiatives to better understand the tolerability of IO therapies [16].

The development of this PROM followed the FDA’s best-practice recommendations for qualitative research methods [15], as the methodology included an interview plan tailored to the intended population, study characteristics, and design; study materials such as a study protocol, interview guide, and training materials; and a detailed qualitative analysis plan. In line with the relevant FDA guidance, the content validity of this PROM was established through CD interviews with patients [15] and its clinical relevance was ensured through CD interviews with clinical oncologists.

One of the key strengths of this study was that the patient population interviewed showed considerable diversity in various aspects. Patients had a mix of cancer diagnoses (including solid and hematologic cancers) of different stages, the sites of metastases were varied, and they were receiving various types of cancer treatments. CRS presentation and symptom severity were also remarkably different among the patients in the present study, and patients receiving CAR-T and non-CAR-T therapy were included. Thus, our findings generate a comprehensive picture of what it is like to experience a CRS event.

The IO-induced CRS patient diary therefore demonstrates potential for use alongside existing clinician-reported tools, offering a more detailed and time-sensitive account of CRS experiences. By capturing symptom onset, progression, and resolution in real time, the diary enhances the granularity of adverse event reporting. Together, the clinician-reported tools and the diary converge to comprehensively and accurately identify and monitor CRS, contributing to a more patient-centered understanding of IO therapy tolerability.

This study has certain limitations. A few concepts included in the CM may correspond to CRS-like adverse events which cannot be distinguished from clinically confirmed CRS events. Although the patient population was diverse in terms of race and ethnicity, the research was US-based, so any further cultural factors may not have been captured. Lastly, minor modifications were made to the PROM after the CD interviews, and these were not debriefed.

Conclusions

The IO-induced CRS patient diary is a first-of-its-kind, well-developed, content-valid PROM for tracking the onset, resolution, and impact of IO-induced CRS on patients’ lives. It provides a comprehensive picture of the patient experience of IO therapy-induced CRS. Although this PROM was developed for use in clinical trials, it may also help to understand CRS events in clinical practice by complementing the clinical description with the patient perspective. Future studies should test the validity of this PROM in a broader and larger sample, and confirm what constitutes onset and resolution in the PROM. Its psychometric performance in clinical trials should also be tested. Finally, additional CD interviews are required to confirm the interpretability and relevance of the PROM, especially among patients on CAR-T therapy.
