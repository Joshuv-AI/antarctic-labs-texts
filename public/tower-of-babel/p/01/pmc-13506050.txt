Effects of distance and vibration isolation during transportation on subprimal and case-ready steak quality of beef sirloins
Authors: Kylie N Krueger, Keith R Underwood, Amanda D Blair, J Kyle Grubbs, Christina E Bakker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objectives of this study were to determine the impacts of transportation distance and vibration isolation on beef sirloin subprimal and case-ready steak quality. Cases (n = 45) of beef sirloin top butts were obtained from a commercial processing facility and allocated to one of three treatments: (i) regular pallet transported 240 km (RP240), (ii) regular pallet transported 640 km (RP640), or (iii) vibration isolation pallet transported 640 km (VI640). After initial transportation, one sirloin from each case was subset for further analysis. Sirloins were fabricated into three—2.54 cm steaks for evaluation of color, purge loss, cook loss, and Warner-Bratzler shear force (WBSF). The steaks were further bisected into medial and lateral halves. Steaks from the medial side were evaluated fresh and steaks from the lateral side were frozen prior to evaluation. Steaks were allocated into three evaluation time points: (i) subprimal fabrication, (ii) case-ready steaks evaluated prior to retail display (d 0), or (iii) case-ready steaks evaluated after 6 d of retail display (d 5). Data were analyzed as a completely randomized design with fixed effects of treatment and day of simulated display. Least-squares means were calculated, and two orthogonal contrasts were used to test the hypotheses (i) “Distance” RP240 vs RP640 and (ii) “Pallet” RP640 vs VI640. Significance was determined at α ≤ .05. Increased distance resulted in decreased (P < 0.05) L*, a*, and b* of sirloin subprimals. Additionally, VI640 steaks had increased (P = 0.005) WBSF values for fresh steaks at the time of subprimal fabrication. Fresh case-ready RP240 steaks had increased (P ≤ 0.005) purge loss, cook loss, total moisture loss, and L* values compared to RP640 steaks. Fresh case-ready VP640 steaks had increased (P ≤ 0.011) purge loss and L* values compared to RP640 steaks. Fresh steaks had decreased (P ≤ 0.008) purge loss and WBSF on d 5 of simulated retail display compared to d 0. Frozen steaks had decreased (P = 0.023) purge loss on d 5 of simulated retail display compared to d 0. Instrumental L* did not change (P = 0.604), but a* and b* values were reduced (P < 0.001) over the course of simulated retail display. Minimal differences (P > 0.05) were observed on frozen steaks. These data indicated that increased subprimal transportation distance and vibration exposure may be beneficial to water holding capacity, color, and tenderness of fresh beef sirloin steaks.

Introduction

Over the past 25 years, American grocery stores have shifted from employing in-house butchers to stocking case-ready meat packages. While service counters are still utilized in some places, approximately 83% of all packages in the meat case are case-ready; a drastic increase from 49% in 2002 (Roerink 2022). While beef is the species most likely to be cut in store, 71% of beef was sold in case ready packaging in 2022 (Roerink 2022).

The implementation of case-ready packaging can be attributed to a variety of factors including labor shortages, increased shelf life, and reduced waste. The shift to case-ready packaging has changed how the meat supply chain functions. Previously, meat processors harvested animals and fabricated carcasses into wholesale cuts that were further fabricated by skilled butchers in grocery stores. Now, processors operate entire plants dedicated to fabricating primals and subprimals into case-ready retail packages. This adds a substantial amount of time between when an animal is harvested and when the meat is available in a retail setting. While all companies have different practices and timelines for their products, the meat in many case-ready packages undergo several transportations prior to arriving at the grocery store. The primals and subprimals originate at a harvest facility and are transported to case-ready facilities where they may hold for several days prior to fabrication and packaging. The retail packages can then either be transported to distribution facilities to hold for another period of time or be shipped directly to grocery stores.

An unknown in the meat industry is if this increase in handling through transportation impacts meat quality. Pressure can force water from meat products (Barbut 2024), so it stands to reason that meat stacked in cases and subjected to roadway vibrations during transportation may have increased water loss compared to meat products subjected to less pressure. This may be in the form of a decreased duration of transportation experienced by meat products marketed on a wholesale basis that are not further processed at case-ready plants, or it may be through less vibration due to different handling or packaging methods. Additionally, as myoglobin is a water soluble pigment responsible for the color of meat, an increase in purge may impact color (AMSA 2023). However, decreasing vibration may negatively impact tenderness by reducing the potential for mechanical tenderization through the prevention of microstructural damage previously shown to occur with resonance vibration (Peng et al. 2022).

The produce industry has observed that transportation can drastically impact the quality of a variety of fruits and vegetables. The amount of damage caused by transportation varies by type of produce, ranging from 2% for potatoes to 23% for strawberries including bruising, soft spots, discoloration, and reduction of shelf life (Aiello et al. 2012). Two primary causes of produce damage are packaging methods and roadway vibrations (Scalia et al. 2016; Cherono and Workneh 2018; Fernando et al. 2018). Scalia and others (2016) investigated effects of vibrations experienced during transport on strawberries over various periods of time and identified that 6 h of vibrations resulted in less damage to produce than exposure to 48 h of vibrations. Cherono and Workneh (2018) reported that transporting tomatoes in 7.94 kg capacity boxes improved marketability by 8% and reduced weight loss by 1% when compared to tomatoes transported in a 467.65 kg crate. Moreover, the authors reported that tomatoes transported on good quality roads were 5%–10% more marketable than tomatoes transported on poor quality roads (Cherono and Workneh 2018). While transportation has been shown to impact produce quality, limited research has been conducted on transportation of meat products. Thus, the objectives of this study were to determine the impact of exposure to roadway vibration and distance traveled during the transport on water holding capacity, color stability, and tenderness of beef center-cut sirloin top butt subprimals and case-ready steaks. We hypothesized that reduced road vibrations would positively impact water holding capacity and color, but negatively impact tenderness.

Materials and methods

Vibration isolation pallet construction

A vibration isolation pallet was constructed with two 122 cm × 101 cm aluminum pallets (H-4062, Uline, Pleasant Prairie, WI). Four stainless steel eight wire rope insulators (TYXC-0015-6_69, PGFUN, Huzhou City, Zhejiang, China) were placed between the pallets. Vibration isolation capacity was validated prior to meat transport by loading 544 kg onto the pallet and transporting on interstate roadways for 45 min.

Product selection and first transportation

Cases of USDA Choice center-cut sirloin top butts (IMPS 184, Top Sirloin Butt, Center Cut, 2-Piece, Boneless; n = 45; approximately 36 kg/case) were collected from a commercial processing facility and allocated to one of three treatments (n = 15 cases per treatment) based on pallet type and transportation distance. Product was marketed as commodity Choice, collected from a single fabrication lot at ∼ 3 d postmortem and left packaged in original vacuum packaging within each case (n = 8–10 pieces/case). Treatments included: (i) regular wood pallet transported ∼240 km (RP240), (ii) regular pallet transported ∼640 km (RP640), and (iii) vibration isolation pallet transported ∼640 km (VI640). Each pallet was placed onto a refrigerated truck that was pre-chilled to −2.2 °C prior to transport. Pallets were stacked with five cases per layer for a total of three full layers. Temperature data loggers (ThermaData stainless steel USB temp data logger; ThermoWorks, American Fork, UT) were placed into one case per layer to monitor case temperature during transport. Three accelerometers (Human Activity Monitor X16, Gulf Coast Data Concepts, Waveland, MS) were placed in on the top of each pallet (left, center, and middle) in the same orientation to measure roadway vibrations at a frequency of 25 Hz. The refrigerated truck was also loaded with a 946-liter tank filled with cold water to simulate thermal mass within the refrigerated truck. The loaded truck was driven ∼240 km on primarily interstate roadways prior to arriving at the South Dakota State University (SDSU) Meat Laboratory. The RP240 pallet was unloaded using a pallet jack and placed into a holding cooler at 1.4 ± 1.4 °C. The remaining two treatments were driven an additional 400 km on primarily interstate roadways before returning to the SDSU Meat Laboratory and placed into the same holding cooler described above. Product was held for 10 d to mimic standard holding and aging practices in a case ready plant prior to fabrication. Cases were subsampled on d 11. One piece from the center of the bottom layer was collected from each case for further analysis.

Subprimal purge loss and pH

Subsampled pieces (n = 45) were weighed (Weigh-Tronix, Fairmont, MN, Model #QC-3265, Serial #002156) prior to opening the package. Purge loss was conducted as described in Bakker et al. (2023). Briefly, the subprimal was removed from packaging, allowed to drip for 15 minutes, and then reweighed. The packaging was rinsed, air dried, and weighed to determine packaging weight. Percent of subprimal purge loss was calculated as the difference between the weight of the unopened package and the subprimal and packaging. Ultimate pH was measured on the posterior end of each subprimal (Thermo-Scientific Orion Star, Beverly, MA, Model #A221 and Star A321 Portable pH probe) as described in Newton et al. (2024).

Steak fabrication

A diagram of steak fabrication is shown in Fig. 1. The anterior end of each subprimal was faced to create a uniform cut surface before three 2.54-cm thick steaks were removed. Each steak was bisected between the lateral and medial halves (n = 6 steaks/subprimal). Steaks from the lateral side of the sirloin were labeled A, B, and C, and designated to be frozen after a predetermined aging period prior to evaluation. Steaks from the medial side were labeled D, E, and F and designated as a fresh steak that was evaluated after aging. This sampling method was selected to be comparable across time periods for tenderness according to data by Apple et al. (2014). Collection of instrumental color was collected as described in Bakker et al. (2023) with modification of the spectrophotometer. Two instrumental color measurements were taken 30 minutes post-fabrication with a dual-beam spectrophotometer (MiniScan EZ 4500 L, HunterLab, Reston, VA) with a 2° observer, 25.4 mm aperture, and a D65 illuminant, on steak A and averaged to represent initial subprimal color.

Diagram of sirloin fabrication. Steaks D, E, and F were evaluated fresh and steaks A, B, and C were evaluated after freezing. Steaks a and D represented wholesale sirloin quality. Steaks E and B represented case-ready steaks after a 6 d retail display. Steaks C and F represented case-ready steaks at d 0 of retail display.

Steaks A and D were vacuum sealed immediately after fabrication to represent steaks taken from sirloins transported for wholesale purposes. Steak A was frozen on d 12 and steak D was evaluated on d 12 for purge loss, cook loss, and Warner Bratzler shear force (WBSF). Steaks B, C, E, and F (designated for as second transport to replicate case-ready products) were placed in black 21.59 cm × 16.51 cm × 2.54 cm polystyrene trays with oxygen permeable polyvinyl chloride overwrap (15,500–16,275 cm3/m2/24 h oxygen transmission rate). All of the overwrapped trays were then placed into mother bags (two trays per bag) and gas flushed with a tri-gas mix of 0.4% CO, 30% CO2, and 69.6% N2. Steaks B and E were sealed in the same bag, while steaks C and F were sealed in the same bag. The mother bags were placed in plastic collapsable trays (60.96 cm × 40.64 cm × 11.43 cm) and stacked on two separate regular wooden pallets (n = 24 trays per pallet) and stored in a holding cooler at −0.6 ± 1.5 °C for six days to await a second transport on d 17.

Second transport and allocation to storage method

A transport of the overwrapped steaks in mother bags was conducted to mimic transportation from a case-ready plant to a distribution center under the same refrigeration conditions as subprimal transport. Steaks were driven ∼480 km on primarily interstate roadways. Upon completion of the second transport, pallets were placed into the holding cooler and stored at −0.6 ± 1.5 °C for 6 days to represent product holding at a distribution center. Steaks C and F represented case-ready steaks without a simulated retail display and will be referred to as d 0 steaks. At the conclusion of the 6 day post transport holding period, the overwrapped steaks were removed from the mother bags. Steak C was vacuum sealed and frozen and steak F was evaluated fresh for purge loss, cook loss, and WBSF. Steaks B and E were left in overwrap packaging and represented case-ready steaks after a 6-day simulated retail display and will be referred to as d 5 steaks. At the conclusion of the simulated retail display, steak B was vacuum sealed and frozen and steak E was evaluated for purge loss, cook loss, and Warner-Bratzler shear force. Steaks selected for frozen storage were frozen for ∼1 month prior to evaluation.

Steak case life and instrumental color

Steaks designated for simulated retail display were placed under continuous fluorescent lighting (F32 T8, 3000 lumens, 2.54 cm diameter fluorescent bulbs; General Electric, Boston, Mass) for 6 d. Light intensity was 1886 ± 71 lux. Steaks were rotated daily to reduce the effect of location on steak color. Two L*, a*, and b* measurements were taken daily at 1600 h on steak E as described previously. The color measurements were averaged for each steak for each day of the panel.

Warner-Bratzler shear force and cook loss

Fresh steaks were maintained in the holding cooler as described above (−0.6 ± 1.5 °C) and frozen steaks were thawed for 24 h in the same cooler prior to cooking. All steaks were weighed (Model MWP, CAS, East Rutherford, NJ) prior to cooking to a target peak internal temperature of 71 °C. Steaks were cooked on an electric clamshell grill (George Forman Model #GR2144P, Middleton, WI). Internal temperature was monitored using a digital thermometer (Cooper-Atkins Aqua Tuff NSF Series, Middlefield, CT, Model #41 983430-5) placed near the geometric center of each steak. After cooking, steaks were held in the holding cooler for 24 h. Steaks were allowed to acclimate to room temperature before they were reweighed (Model MWP, CAS, East Rutherford, NJ) to determine cook loss. Six 1.27-cm diameter cores were removed parallel to orientation of muscle fibers and sheared once perpendicularly using a texture analyzer (Shimadzu Scientific Instruments Inc., Lenexa, KS, Model #30825535050) with a Warner-Bratzler attachment. Peak force required to shear each core was recorded, and an average shear force value was reported for each steak (AMSA 2016).

Steak moisture loss calculations

Moisture loss was determined by weighing (Model MWP, CAS, East Rutherford, NJ) each steak at the time of fabrication (initial weight) and prior to and after cooking. Purge loss was calculated as the difference in weight of the steak at fabrication and before cooking. Cook loss was calculated as the difference in weight before and after cooking of each steak. Total moisture loss was calculated as the sum of purge loss and cook loss values.

Vibration analysis

Triaxial acceleration data from the X-, Y-, and Z-axes were imported into R statistical software (R version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for processing and analysis. Acceleration data from all devices were trimmed to a common recording duration corresponding to the shortest valid recording interval among accelerometers to ensure equivalent comparison periods across treatments. A sequential sample index and corresponding time variable were generated for each accelerometer based on the 25 Hz sampling frequency.

where X, Y, and Z represent acceleration values from the three orthogonal axes.

Power spectral density (PSD) analysis was performed on VM acceleration signals using Welch spectral estimation implemented through the gsignal package in R. Prior to PSD estimation, the mean acceleration value was removed from each VM signal to isolate oscillatory vibration behavior. PSD spectra were calculated across frequencies from 0 to 12.5 Hz based on the Nyquist frequency corresponding to the 25 Hz sampling rate. Frequency-domain vibration energy was summarized within three frequency bands: low frequency (0–2 Hz), mid frequency (2–6 Hz), and high frequency (6–12.5 Hz).

Power spectral density energy values were summed within frequency bands for each accelerometer. Accelerometer served as the experimental unit. Frequency-band PSD energy was analyzed using linear models with treatment, frequency band, and their interaction included as fixed effects. Statistical significance was considered at an alpha level of < 0.05 with tendencies considered at 0.05 ≤ α ≥ .10.

Meat quality statistical analysis

Meat quality data were analyzed as a completely randomized design using the mixed model procedures of SAS (SAS Institute Inc. 2026. SAS® OnDemand for Academics. Cary, NC: SAS Institute Inc.). Treatment and day of simulated retail display (where appropriate) were specified as fixed effects. Interactions of treatment and day were evaluated and found not to be significant, therefore only main effects are reported. Peak temperature was included as a covariate for WBSF and cook loss. Least-squares means were calculated, and two orthogonal contrasts were used to test hypotheses: (i) “Distance” RP240 vs RP640 and (ii) “Pallet” RP640 vs VI640. Separation of least square means was performed using a Tukey’s adjustment. Statistical significance was considered at an alpha level of < 0.05 for all contrasts with tendencies considered at 0.05 ≤ α ≥ .10.

Results and discussion

Roadway vibration analysis

Pallet treatment influenced both the amount and type of vibration experienced during interstate transport (Fig. 2). The effects of pallet treatment differed across vibration frequency ranges, indicating that pallet system changed how vibration was transferred to meat products during transportation rather than simply increasing or decreasing overall vibration.

Mean power spectral density for RP240, RP640, and VI640 pallet treatments during transport.

The RP240 treatment exhibited greater low-frequency vibration energy (<2 Hz) compared with the RP640 treatment (P < 0.001; Fig. 2), indicating increased slower oscillatory movement during transport. Similarly, the VI640 vibration-dampening pallet exhibited increased low-frequency vibration energy (P = 0.005; Fig. 2) while showing a flatter vibration profile across higher frequencies compared to the RP640 treatment.

In contrast, the RP640 treatment exhibited several distinct mid-frequency vibration peaks, particularly between 4 and 5 Hz (Fig. 2), indicating greater transmission of repeated roadway vibration within those frequency ranges. Collectively, these findings suggest that pallet configuration substantially influenced transport vibration patterns experienced by palletized meat products during trucking.

Subprimal purge loss, pH, and instrumental color

Sirloin subprimal purge loss, pH, and instrumental color data are presented in Table 1. Purge loss tended to be increased (P = 0.081) for RP240 compared to RP640. Additionally, purge loss tended to be increased (P = 0.060) for VI640 compared to RP640. Therefore, increased distance traveled and the absence of vibration dampening during transportation improved water holding capacity for sirloin subprimals. While the impact of transportation vibrations is relatively unstudied in meat, it has been heavily studied in produce. Produce transport research has suggested repeated forces of roadway vibrations weaken produce cells and cause them to rupture, leading to quality damage, reduction of shelf stability, and changes to color (Fernando et al. 2018). Vibrations experienced during transport could impart similar effects on meat if muscle cell structure becomes damaged. In fact, as the cell structure of plant material includes a cell wall and vacuole system designed to provide rigid structure and withstand pressures of up to 5 MPa, muscle cells may be more susceptible to damage in transit than produce (Toshiki et al. 2025). Previous literature has identified damage to muscle structure through postmortem proteolysis can improve water holding capacity, either through relaxation of the muscle structure from a contracted state, which enables water reabsorption, or by degraded proteins blocking the channels that water can exit, thus reducing the expulsion of water outside of the muscle cell (Kristensen and Purslow 2001; Farouk et al. 2012). Therefore, it is possible that prolonged exposure to vibrations during transportation could increase damage to muscle structure and impart similar effects to water holding capacity, where more water is contained within the muscle rather than lost.

Ultimate subprimal pH was decreased (P = 0.029) for RP240 (5.56 ± 0.008) vs RP640 (5.59 ± 0.008) and unchanged (P = 0.119) when comparing RP640 and VI640 (5.60 ± 0.008). However, as the difference in pH of the subprimals was minimal, and likely did not impact meat quality.

Instrumental L* values of sirloin subprimals were increased (P = 0.028) for the RP240 treatment compared to the RP640 treatment and increased (P = 0.013) for the VI640 treatment compared to the RP640 treatment. These differences may be attributed to the tendency observed for increased purge loss in the RP240 and VI640 treatments. Hunt and Hedrick (1977) observed a correlation of increased purge loss to lighter colored beef products theorized to be the result of loss of myoglobin within the muscle structure. A darker color observed within beef products with improved water holding capacity can also be explained by the increase in water remaining within the muscle that causes myofibers to swell, decreasing space between myofibrils, and decreasing light scattering, which could contribute to lower L* values (Ijaz et al. 2020). Increased transportation distance decreased instrumental a* (P = 0.045) and b* (P = 0.044) values. Vibration isolation tended (P = 0.060) to increase b* values but did not affect (P = 0.180) a* values when measured on steaks following subprimal fabrication.

Purge loss, cook loss, and WBSF data for steaks fabricated to represent wholesale sirloin quality are presented in Table 2. Distance did not impact purge loss (P = 0.331), cook loss (P = 0.290) or WBSF (P = 0.825) of steaks evaluated after fresh storage. Pallet type did not impact fresh steak purge loss (P = 0.754), but VI640 steaks did have increased cook loss (P = 0.037) compared to RP640 steaks. However, total moisture loss (purge and cook loss combined) was not impacted by distance (P = 0.093) or pallet (P = 0.132). Pallet did impact tenderness as VI640 steaks exhibited increased WBSF (P = 0.005) compared to RP640 steaks. Previous research suggests that vibration during transportation may improve tenderness. Peng et al. (2022) observed that resonance vibration at a frequency of 25 Hz for up to 3 hours reduced WBSF and increased myofibril fragmentation index values of yak longissimus thoracis et lumborum muscle. The authors attributed the improvements in tenderness to degradation of myofibrils and sarcomeres through the detachment of the endomysium from muscle fibers and the disappearance of the M-band. The authors believed the resonance vibration ruptured the structural components of the M-lines which allowed for the hydrolysis of myomesin, obscurin and titin (Agarkova and Perriard 2005; Peng et al. 2022).

Combined purge loss and cook loss.

Distance impacted the purge loss and cook loss of steaks frozen prior to evaluation with RP240 steaks having a decreased (P = 0.015) purge loss but increased (P = 0.010) cook loss compared to RP640 steaks. Additionally, a tendency for increased (P = 0.057) purge loss and decreased (P = 0.015) cook loss were exhibited for RP640 compared to VI640 frozen steaks. However, total moisture loss was not impacted by distance (P = 0.280) and a tendency for increased (P = 0.093) moisture loss was observed for frozen VI640 vs RP640 steaks indicating that while the amount of moisture lost at different timepoints was impacted, overall moisture loss was mostly unchanged. No impact of distance (P = 0.123) or pallet (P = 0.134) was observed for WBSF of steaks fabricated after subprimal transportation and immediately frozen. Numerous studies have shown freezing beef can equalize tenderness differences observed over aging periods, likely due to ice crystal formation disrupting muscle structure, (Hergenreder et al. 2013; Aroeira et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2017; Arnold et al. 2018; Setyabrata and Kim 2019; Lawrence and Lawrence 2023). In particular, Vieira et al. (2009) identified that after aging product for 10 d and then freezing, no differences were observed in WBSF.

Case-ready steak moisture loss, instrumental color, and WBSF

The impact of treatment on moisture loss, WBSF, and instrumental color of case-ready steaks is shown in Table 3. Fresh steaks from sirloins transported 240 km had increased purge loss (P < 0.001), cook loss (P = 0.005), and total moisture loss (P < 0.001) compared to steaks from sirloins transported 640 km. Additionally, steaks from sirloins transported on the RP640 pallet had decreased (P = 0.011) purge loss but similar cook loss (P = 0.837) and total moisture loss (P = 0.333) compared to the VI640 pallet. The improvement in moisture loss for the RP640 compared to the RP240 treatments could be explained by the structural muscle damage caused by vibration potentially blocking moisture expulsion. Farouk et al. (2012) hypothesized that improved water holding capacity with increased aging was the result of the “sponge effect.” This theory suggests that as postmortem proteolysis occurs, structural debris collects in the microtubule channels and holds water with the muscle structure (Farouk et al. 2012). While this theory centers around postmortem proteolysis, we liken the presence of degradation products to the structural damage observed by Peng et al. (2022) when evaluating resonance vibration similar to the vibrations experienced by sirloins transported a long distance on a regular pallet. Warner-Bratzler shear force was not impacted by distance (P = 0.433) or pallet type (P = 0.528) in steaks after case-ready transportation and fresh storage. For steaks subjected to a simulated retail display, increased distance decreased (P < 0.001) L* and tended to decrease (P = 0.075) a* but did not impact (P = 0.899) b*. The VI640 pallet decreased (P = 0.001) L* but did not impact a* (P = 0.815) or b* (P = 0.157) values. The increased L* values may be the result of increased purge loss of both RP240 and VI640 treatments compared to RP640. This is supported by findings that increased purge loss is correlated to lighter colored beef products, likely resulting from loss of myoglobin within the muscle, which contributes to the pigmentation and colors observed within meat (Hunt and Hedrick 1977) and increased water holding capacity resulting in myofiber swelling and decreased light scattering (Ijaz et al. 2020).

Steaks subjected to case-ready transportation and frozen prior to evaluation showed no differences (P > 0.05) for cook loss, total moisture loss, or WBSF regardless of distance or pallet. Steaks from the RP640 treatment did have less purge loss compared to RP240 (P < 0.001) and VI640 (P = 0.006) treatments. As previously discussed, this decrease in purge loss could be the result of structural damage caused by transportation vibration blocking water loss and the lack of differences in cook loss and total moisture loss may be caused by the inability of structural damage to maintain the free water through the cooking process.

The impact of simulated retail display day on moisture loss and WBSF is found in Table 4. In steaks evaluated fresh, purge loss decreased (P < 0.001) and cook loss increased (P = 0.042) from d 0 of retail display to d 5. However, total moisture loss was not impacted (P = 0.810). Kristensen and Purslow (2001) proposed that the expulsion of water from muscle cells can be halted with degradation of the cytoskeleton with increased aging, which relaxes muscle structure, thus reducing the force that causes water to flood the extracellular space. This could enable free water to re-enter the product and reduce purge loss over time (Kristensen and Purslow 2001). As this free water would be weakly held within muscle structure, the increased cook loss and overall lack of change in total moisture loss is consistent with the expectation that forces generated by cooking would drive the reabsorbed free water back out of the muscle structure. Increased aging improved (P = 0.008) WBSF as expected with the well characterized events of postmortem aging. As aging occurs, tenderness improvements are expected as postmortem proteolytic enzymes degrading muscle structure. Improvements in tenderness within the gluteus medius muscle have been observed up to 28 d post-mortem (Gruber et al. 2006), which aligns with the results observed in fresh sirloin steaks in this study.

The impact of simulated retail display day on instrumental color is reported in Table 5. No d effect was observed (P = 0.604) for L*, but a* and b* values decreased (P < 0.001) over the course of the display period. This has been observed in other retail display studies; where a decline in redness was observed over 10 days (Sapp et al. 1999) and, in a separate transportation project, followed by a simulated retail display of 5 days (Bakker et al. 2023). This decline in a* and b* values has been correlated with a decrease in oxymyoglobin over the display period (Sapp et al. 1999). Overall, during the simulated retail display, a* values did not fall below 14.5 until day 4, indicating color was likely acceptable until that point of the study (Holman et al. 2016).

Conclusion

In summary, this study indicated that the presence of roadway vibrations during the transportation of beef sirloin subprimals has either beneficial or neutral effects on product moisture loss and subprimal tenderness of sirloins stored at refrigerated temperatures. The data also show that increased subprimal transportation distances may reduce moisture loss in steaks in a retail setting. However, frozen storage negated many of the differences observed in fresh steaks. This information may be used to inform the packaging and transportation practices within the beef industry to help reduce losses in yield caused by moisture loss. Future research is warranted to determine optimal transportation distance or vibration exposure to maintain product quality. Moreover, additional research is warranted to determine if these results can be replicated in other beef cuts.
