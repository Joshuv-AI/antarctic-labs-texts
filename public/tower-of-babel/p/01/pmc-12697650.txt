Effect of Time from Puncture to Recanalization on Poststroke Disposition and Associated Costs
Authors: Akash Agrawal, Arjun Agrawal, Jase Howell, Sevin Barringer‐Hoonhout, James Fleck, Jason Mackey, Andrew DeNardo, Daniel Gibson, Krishna Amuluru, Yasir Saleem, Charles Kulwin, Troy Payner, Kushal Shah, J. Mocco, Daniel Sahlein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThis study investigates the relationship between procedure time during stroke thrombectomy and acute posthospital disposition, as well as the associated costs. Despite extensive literature focusing on long‐term outcomes, immediate posthospital patient disposition and its economic impact remain underexplored.METHODSThis retrospective study analyzed 721 patients undergoing thrombectomy at a single neuroendovascular practice from 2011 to 2020. Key metrics included disposition and time from groin puncture to recanalization (PTR). Patient dispositions were categorized into 4 groups: acute/subacute rehabilitation, home/home with physical therapy (PT), death/hospice, and long‐term care. Multinomial logistic regression, adjusted for age, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and comorbidities, was used to model predicted disposition probabilities over a range of PTR times. Disposition‐based direct acute care costs were applied to estimate the cost impact of PTR delays.RESULTSPTR was significantly associated with disposition (P = 0.003). In adjusted multinomial regression, each 15‐minute PTR increase was associated with higher odds of death/hospice (odds ratio [OR]: 1.020, 95% CI: [1.008–1.032], P = 0.001) and no significant change in acute/subacute rehab (OR: 1.007, 95% CI: [0.997–1.017], P = 0.152) and long‐term care (OR: 1.007, 95% CI: [0.990–1.025], P = 0.432) relative to home/home with PT. Additionally, every 15‐minute delay was associated with a 2–4.6 percentage point increase in the likelihood of death/hospice, and a 1.5–2.5 percentage point decrease in the likelihood of home/home with PT. A cost analysis suggested that each 15‐minute delay in PTR resulted in an approximate $190 (95% CI: [$184–$196], P<0.001) increase in direct acute care costs per stroke episode.CONCLUSIONLonger PTR is associated with increased death/hospice dispositions and measurable cost increases. Extrapolating nationally, every 15‐minute PTR delay results in a $7.2–$7.7 million annual increase in stroke‐related health care costs.

Posthospital disposition is an underappreciated component of the stroke care continuum. Whether patients are discharged home, rehabilitation, long‐term care, or hospice or death, these immediate outcomes offer important insight into early neurological recovery, caregiver burden, and quality of life following intervention.

Beyond the immediate clinical impact on patients and families, the societal and economic burden of disposition following stroke thrombectomy includes extensive health care costs and the need for rehabilitation services. As of 2016, the mean cost of a single stroke in the United States was approximately $140 000, and the total direct and indirect annual costs of all strokes were estimated to be between $36.5 and $65 billion. 1 , 2 Differences in costs between patient dispositions, including discharge to home (without physical therapy) or home with physical therapy (PT), acute or subacute rehabilitation, chronic care facilities, and death, can have profound impacts on patients and the total cost to society.

Current literature has identified several predictors of disposition following stroke, including age, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation. 3 However, most studies are designed around the potential cost of long‐term disability. The percentage of patients living independently at 90 days (modified Rankin Scale) is the standard primary endpoint for randomized controlled trials in stroke literature. 4 This endpoint ignores immediate posthospital care.

Rapid treatment has a well‐documented benefit on infarct volume and 90‐day clinical outcomes, as established by the 5 landmark multicenter trials in 2015, including MR CLEAN (Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands), 5 ESCAPE (Endovascular Treatment for Small Core and Anterior Circulation Proximal Occlusion With Emphasis on Minimizing CT (Computed Tomography)to Recanalization Times), 6 EXTEND‐IA (Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke with Perfusion‐Imaging Studies), 7 SWIFT PRIME (Solitaire With the Intention for Thrombectomy as Primary Endovascular Treatment), 8 and REVASCAT (Randomized Trial of Revascularization With Solitaire FR Device Versus Best Medical Therapy in the Treatment of Acute Stroke Due to Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusion Presenting Within 8 Hours of Symptom Onset). 9 Nevertheless, the relationship between treatment times in patients with thrombectomy and acute posthospital disposition is less well understood. Moreover, procedural time is often intertwined with stroke severity, anatomical complexity, and pre‐morbid status—all of which may confound outcomes such as post‐discharge disposition.

Although successful recanalization of large vessel occlusions is clearly associated with better outcomes, 10 , 11 we hypothesized that time has a profound impact on acute hospital disposition, even within the cohort of patients with good recanalization (modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction [mTICI]≥2B). This represents the vast majority of patients in modern thrombectomy series. 12 , 13 , 14 This study analyzed a large, retrospective thrombectomy database from a high‐volume single neuroendovascular practice to explore the impact of various time metrics on patient disposition post‐thrombectomy and the associated costs.

This retrospective analysis used data from a single practice's consecutive stroke thrombectomy database. Some data elements were collected prospectively as part of participation in Get with the Guidelines. These included acute posthospitalization patient disposition. No additional adjudication of patient disposition occurred during the study period. Some of the data were originally documented before to the introduction of mTICI 2C as a category for recanalization. All of the mTICI 2B and 3 scores were reviewed by 2 neurointerventionalists to adjudicate between mTICI 2B, 2C, and 3 for the purpose of this study. All patients who underwent a stroke thrombectomy from our practice between January 2011 and June 2020 were screened for this study. A complete case analysis was performed, and patients with missing data were excluded.

The following data were collected from the stroke database: demographics (sex and age at time of procedure), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores upon arrival, comorbidities, time from groin puncture to recanalization (PTR), and patient disposition post thrombectomy (acute rehabilitation, death, home, home with PT, hospice, long‐term acute care, skilled nursing facility, and subacute rehabilitation). Disposition categories were consolidated into acute/subacute rehab, home/home with PT, death/hospice, and long‐term care due to limited data and for the sake of clarity. Events post hospitalization, such as transfers from facilities (eg, rehab to long‐term care), are not differentiated in this analysis but still captured by the average costs that were assigned to each disposition.

Patients with atypical stroke workup, largely related to mild or waxing and waning symptoms followed by acute decompensation, who eventually underwent thrombectomy were excluded from the analysis because of a lack of clarity surrounding the definition of symptom onset (the mild or severe stroke symptoms) and the resultant impact on treatment times and outcome from these outliers. Therefore, patients whose time from arrival to groin puncture exceeded 24 hours were excluded. Extreme outliers (>3 SD) for any time metric across each disposition were excluded due to the relatively high impact on group means and regression models. Patients with mTICI scores below 2B were excluded to reduce the large impact of poor recanalization on outcome and because our hypothesis focused on the profound impact of time, even with good recanalization. Degree of recanalization was determined to be a significant confounder in the relationship between PTR and disposition—in some sense, patients with suboptimal technical outcome were not successfully reperfused, thereby significantly altering the relationship between PTR and outcome.

Statistical analyses and histograms were generated using R Studio. 15 The following R Libraries were used: ggplot2, tidyverse, dplyr, and nnet. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Significance was assumed at α = 0.05. ANOVA tests were performed for PTR, followed by a Tukey's honest significant difference post hoc test. Based on trends in the data, a multinomial regression model was created using disposition (response variable) and PTR (explanatory variable) to predict the probabilities of each disposition for any given PTR. Comorbidities and arrival National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score were included as covariates to account for confounding. Pseudo R2 values, including McFadden's, were computed to evaluate model fit. A likelihood ratio test was conducted to compare the full model to an intercept‐only model. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors computed from a linear proxy model. The above model assumptions were tested and met.

The independence of irrelevant alternatives assumption was tested using the Hausman–McFadden test via the mlogit package in R. Due to model complexity and data structure, the irrelevant alternatives assumption could not be fully evaluated, and this limitation is acknowledged.

Dispositions were assigned estimated post‐hospitalization costs retrieved from a study on the direct costs and payments of a coordinated stroke center and regional stroke network initially valuated in 2010. 20 Dollar values were inflation adjusted to 2025 dollars using the Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index inflation calculator. 21

To estimate the average change in cost associated with increased PTR, we predicted disposition probabilities for each patient under their observed PTR and again under a hypothetical 15‐minute increase. The difference in expected costs was computed for each patient and averaged to yield the mean marginal cost increase.

CIs and P values for both cost and probability changes were obtained using nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates). Percentile‐based 95% CIs were computed, and 2‐sided P values were derived from the empirical bootstrap distributions.

During the study period, 721 out of 1041 (50% female) met the inclusion criteria. DISCUSSION

The results presented here demonstrate that prolonged PTR times are significantly associated with worse immediate disposition. Each 15‐minute delay in PTR was associated with a 2%–3.5% increased risk of death/hospice. This suggests that delays in achieving reperfusion translates into clinically meaningful harm and increased costs despite technical success.

This aligns with the well‐established pathophysiology of the ischemic cascade, where time‐dependent neuronal death can result in irreversible infarction even if reperfusion is ultimately achieved. 22 , 23 Prior studies, including the NINDS tPA (National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke tissue plasminogen activator) trial, pivotal 2015 thrombectomy trials (eg, MR CLEAN 5 , ESCAPE 6 , EXTEND‐IA 7 , SWIFT PRIME 8 , and REVASCAT 9 ), and many single‐center studies 24 have demonstrated that earlier reperfusion yields better functional outcome and reduced infarct volume, highlighting the importance of time as a modifiable determinant of clinical outcome. 22

Additionally, brain integrity in the context of large vessel occlusion can be split into 2 categories: penumbra and core. The penumbral tissue is understood as tissue at risk, that is ischemic but fundamentally salvageable. Over time, the penumbral tissue is converted to a core infarct. This physiology explains the therapeutic window, allowing for an intervention that will ultimately save the penumbra from permanent injury. More recently, CT perfusion imaging has yielded further insight into this physiology, demonstrating that there is a spectrum of speed of progression of penumbra to infarction (always assumed based on variability of the integrity of pial collaterals but never shown in vivo). 25 CT perfusion studies have further refined this understanding with 2 metrics, each of which has been validated empirically to predict slow versus fast progression. 26 This study very much aligns with this understanding of stroke physiology. The size of the infarct is enlarging with time, and early reperfusion results in greater clinical benefit and ultimately changes the acute disposition.

From a health economics perspective, our analysis revealed that every 15‐minute delay in PTR is associated with an increase of approximately $184 to$196 in direct acute care costs per patient. This cost differential persisted even after adjusting for multiple covariates, including age and mTICI score, indicating that treatment timing contributes independently to resource utilization. These increased costs were largely driven by higher rates of severe dispositions (eg, death/hospice or long‐term care), which typically require more intensive or prolonged hospitalization.

Many studies examining stroke‐related health care costs have not incorporated explicit time metrics such as PTR in addition to postacute care disposition when assessing financial outcomes. 27 For example, a US Department of Health and Human Services report comparing costs among inpatient rehabilitation facilities, skilled nursing facilities, and home health after acute stroke care focused on postacute expenses without considering the effects of time to recanalization on outcomes or subsequent costs. 28 Likewise, a study investigating predictors of acute hospital costs in an academic center evaluated cost determinants in patients with ischemic stroke but did not include mechanical thrombectomy–specific timing variables. 29 Although both reports provide valuable data relevant to stroke economics, neither addresses the direct impact of time delays on patient disposition or immediate health care expenditures. Our study adds to this body of work by demonstrating that even small delays in thrombectomy recanalization can translate into measurable increases in acute care costs.

Based on national procedure volumes (∼39 000 thrombectomies/year in 2021), 30 we estimate that a 15‐minute increase in mean PTR nationwide could lead to an additional $7.4 million, 95% CI: $7.2–$7.7] in annual acute stroke care costs. This projection assumes a linear cost increase based on our cohort data, an 89% good (>2B) recanalization rate, and similar disposition and PTR distributions across institutions.

Given the relationship between PTR and acute disposition demonstrated here, both from the perspective of clinical outcome as well as cost, optimizing workflow to reduce treatment time is critically important. An experienced and stable base of neuro‐dedicated technologists, in our practice's experience, is the most important starting point for streamlining procedural workflows. Setting up catheter systems on the back table such that no additional prep work is needed once access is obtained with a guide catheter is a workflow that is favored by our practice to optimize PTR. Other components that our practice considers important but remain relatively understudied are fluoroscopy ergonomics and workflows enabled by modern biplane technology.

Our findings must be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, this was a single‐center observational study, which may limit generalizability to other populations and practice settings. Second, our data are derived from a high‐volume practice, which may limit generalizability to lower‐volume centers. Third, although we adjusted for key covariates, unmeasured confounders such as infarct core size may still influence both PTR and disposition. Fourth, cost estimates were derived from a disposition‐based model and do not capture postacute expenditures or indirect costs such as long‐term disability. Lastly, national extrapolations assume uniform practice patterns and outcomes, which may not reflect real‐world heterogeneity.

CONCLUSION

Our findings underscore 2 critical insights. First, there is a clear and statistically significant signal toward decreased disposition to home/home with PT and acute rehabilitation and a trend toward increased death and hospice with time from puncture to recanalization, even among patients who achieve technically successful thrombectomy (mTICI ≥2b). Second, delays in recanalization are associated with measurable increases in acute care costs, reinforcing the economic value of workflow optimization. These results suggest that procedural speed remains a crucial determinant of clinical outcome, and reducing procedural time should be prioritized not only to improve individual patient outcomes but also to support institutional cost efficiency and inform national stroke care policies.
