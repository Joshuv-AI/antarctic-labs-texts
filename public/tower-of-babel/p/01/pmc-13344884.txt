Independent prognostic value of peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity on clinical progression in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Jonas Alexander Jarholm, Sandra Tecelão, Bjørn‐Eivind Kirsebom, Mostafa Mehdipour Ghazi, Lene Pålhaugen, Berglind Gísladóttir, Fernando Gonzalez‐Ortiz, Mehdi Sadat Akhavi, Einar Hopp, Anders Martin Fjell, Santiago Timón‐Reina, Mads Nielsen, Kaj Blennow, Dag Aarsland, Atle Bjørnerud, Per Selnes, Tormod Fladby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPeak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) is a candidate magnetic resonance imaging marker for global white matter integrity. We investigated the value of PSMD in predicting clinical progression in amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative individuals.METHODSPSMD, cognition, and amyloid status were measured in 254 subjects from the Dementia Disease Initiation (DDI) cohort and 211 subjects from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort. Survival analysis was conducted on 166 DDI subjects and validated in 178 ADNI subjects using Clinical Dementia Rating scale.RESULTSHigh PSMD amyloid‐positive individuals had an increased risk of clinical progression relative to those with low PSMD, driven by independent additive effects of amyloid pathology and PSMD. Higher PSMD was predominantly associated with slower processing speed and reduced executive function, but not with memory performance.DISCUSSIONCombining PSMD with amyloid status may improve the prediction of clinical progression in Alzheimer's disease, particularly in relation to executive and processing speed domains.

BACKGROUND

The hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease (AD) are amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau. 1 , 2 , 3 Cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) is a common AD co‐pathology linked to white matter (WM) abnormalities on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), 4 , 5 and is associated with accelerated disease progression. 6 , 7 Vascular impairment is included in a proposed biomarker‐based classification framework for AD. 8

WM pathology in AD may reflect interplay involving vascular and neurodegenerative mechanisms contributing to cognitive decline. SVD may arise from age‐ and arteriosclerosis‐related vascular‐risk factors, or concomitant cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). 9 , 10 WM degeneration secondary to cortical AD pathology may also involve demyelination, glial activation, and Wallerian degeneration. 11 Conventional MRI markers for SVD, such as WM hyperintensities, lacunes, perivascular spaces, and microbleeds, have variable pathologic specificity and sensitivity, and inconsistent associations with cognition. 5 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15

Peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) is an MRI diffusion‐based measure of global WM integrity, proposed as a marker reflecting SVD, but putatively more sensitive to diffuse microstructural damage and more strongly associated with cognitive function than conventional SVD markers. 16 Notably, impaired microstructural integrity has been observed in normal‐appearing WM in autosomal dominant AD. 17 However, PSMD may also be elevated in other disorders, such as multiple sclerosis, 18 where demyelination and inflammatory processes are prominent.

PSMD is elevated in AD, 19 , 20 and in CAA, driven by vascular amyloid deposition. 21 Compared to other diffusion‐based MRI methods, PSMD requires less post‐processing time, 16 facilitating clinical implementation. However, the role of PSMD in AD remains insufficiently characterized, particularly in relation to biomarker‐defined pathology using emerging plasma biomarkers compared to established cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and positron emission tomography (PET) measures. Clarifying the prognostic value of PSMD may improve risk‐stratification and disease‐modeling, and give information relevant for treatment choices, for example, for anti‐amyloid treatments.

The aim of this study was to determine whether high baseline PSMD predicts clinical progression in cognitively unimpaired (CU) or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) subjects, stratified by amyloid status, and to identify the cognitive domains most strongly associated with PSMD.

METHODS

Participants

We analyzed participants from the Dementia Disease Initiation (DDI) cohort 22 and validated the results using the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort. 23 Subjects were included if they had at least one diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) MR scan, amyloid biomarkers (PET or CSF), and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) cognitive assessment.

Recent studies suggest that PSMD may also reflect broader WM pathology, including contributions from neurodegenerative processes such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and have linked PSMD to cognitive impairment in AD. Interpretation: Across two independent cohorts and different measures of AD pathology, we show that PSMD is an independent predictor of clinical progression showing no interaction with amyloid burden. We show an additive rather than synergistic contribution to cognitive decline in AD, significantly affecting processing speed and executive function. The DDI study was approved by the Norwegian regional ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. Participants included individuals with MCI and CU controls, with individuals at risk for dementia particularly sought. Inclusion criteria were age 40–80 years and a Scandinavian mother tongue. Exclusion criteria included dementia, non‐neurodegenerative brain disorders possibly affecting cognition (e.g., stroke or traumatic brain injury), severe psychiatric or somatic illness, treatments that may affect cognition, or developmental disorders. Participants underwent follow‐up approximately every second year, with dementia as the study endpoint. The study protocol included medical history, cognitive testing, neurological examination, blood sampling, lumbar puncture, and brain MRI. All examinations at each time point were performed within 6 months. The inclusion and selection process leading to the final DDI samples is detailed in Figure S1.

The ADNI cohort was launched in 2003, with the primary goal of testing whether serial MRI, PET, other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and early AD. The data included in the validation cohort were derived from the ADNI merged dataset (ADNI‐MERGE), which combines data from ADNI‐1, ADNI‐GO, ADNI‐2, and ADNI‐3. and consisted of all participants who had at least one DTI MR scan, amyloid PET, and CDR, resulting in a dataset of 634 subjects. To maintain the similarity between ADNI and DDI samples, we applied the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as described (see Figure S2).

Biomarker measurements

In the DDI cohort, amyloid status was defined using the CSF amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40 ratio and, in a subset of subjects, using plasma phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217). Although plasma p‐tau217 reflects tau proteinopathy, it becomes abnormal approximately at the same time as amyloid‐PET, and may therefore serve as a proxy for amyloid pathology. 24

In the ADNI cohort, the amyloid status was defined using standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) values from Florbetapir (18F‐AV‐45) PET scans. Detailed biomarker measurement protocols and cutoffs are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Cognitive staging and assessment

The cognitive staging of all participants in both cohorts was determined using the CDR scale, 25 to ensure comparability across cohorts. Individuals with a global CDR score of 0 were classified as CU, 0.5 as MCI, and 1 or higher as dementia. AD was defined biologically, by amyloid positivity verified by fluid or imaging biomarkers, as described.

Cognitive assessment was performed using the following neuropsychological tests: Trail Making Test Part A (TMT‐A) to assess processing speed, Trail Making Test Part B (TMT‐B) to assess executive function (available in the ADNI and DDI cohorts) and Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease delayed verbal memory recall (CERAD recall) (available in DDI), and Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) recall (available in ADNI), to assess verbal memory.

MRI acquisition

MRI in the DDI cohort was acquired on six different scanners across three hospitals in Norway and included 3D T1, 3D fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequences. Detailed DTI parameters for each scanner are provided in Table S1. In the ADNI cohort, MRI was acquired on 12 different scanners. Detailed DTI parameters for each scanner are presented in Table S2.

Image post processing

PSMD was calculated using the online pipeline (http://www.psmd‐marker.com/ 16 ), with the fully processed option. A detailed description of the DTI analysis is provided in the Supplementary Material.

We corrected PSMD results for scanner effect using ComBat (https://github.com/Jfortin1/ComBatHarmonization), a harmonization tool originally developed to correct for batch effects in genomics, 26 which has been shown to effectively reduce inter‐site variability in fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps. 27 ComBat harmonized PSMD was computed while controlling for demographic features, including sex, age, cognitive staging, and amyloid status at baseline. To ensure the quality of the harmonization results, a minimum of 10 subjects was required per scanner. Throughout the manuscript, PSMD refers to the ComBat harmonized PSMD.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.3 28 ). Baseline demographic and clinical differences between cohorts and diagnostic groups were assessed using parametric or non‐parametric tests as appropriate, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Cross‐sectional PSMD was compared across groups using linear regression adjusted for age at baseline and sex, with FDR correction for multiple comparisons.

Associations between PSMD and cognitive outcomes were tested using linear regression models adjusted for age, education, and sex when appropriate and with interaction between PSMD and amyloid status. PSMD, TMT‐A and TMT‐B were log‐transformed due to skewed distributions.

Survival analyses were performed using the “survival” and “survminer” packages. 29 , 30 Time‐to‐event was defined as the interval from baseline to clinical progression (transition from CU to MCI, or from MCI to dementia), with participants censored at last follow‐up.

Cox proportional‐hazards models, including continuous PSMD, amyloid status, or their interaction, were fitted and compared using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) and Akaike's information criterion (AIC). All models were adjusted for age at baseline, sex, and years of education. Adjusted survival curves and hazard ratio (HR) heatmaps were derived from the best‐fitted Cox model. HR heatmaps were generated using predefined PSMD values (0, ± 1 SD, ± 2 SD) and amyloid status (A−, A+), with HRs expressed relative to the reference group (PSMD = 0, A−). The p‐values were obtained using the Wald test, while covariates were held constant.

To account for cardiovascular risk factors, a sensitivity analysis was performed including a modified Framingham Risk Score (FRS) as an additional covariate in the Cox model. A detailed description of FRS computation is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Further details on the statistical analysis are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Cross‐sectional analysis

In the DDI Aβ42/40 ratio sample, PSMD was significantly higher in the MCI A+ group than in the CU A− group (p < 0.001), MCI A− group (p < 0.01), and CU A+ group (p < 0.001). No differences were observed between the CU A+ and the CU A− groups (p = 0.685), or between the MCI A− and the CU A− groups (p = 0.354) (Figure 1A).

Baseline distribution of PSMD across groups. (A) DDI Aβ42/40 ratio sample, (B) DDI plasma p‐tau217 sample, and (C) ADNI sample. Black points represent the PSMD estimated geometrical mean for each group. CU–, cognitively unimpaired and amyloid negative; CU+, cognitively unimpaired and amyloid positive; MCI–, mild cognitive impairment and amyloid negative; MCI+, mild cognitive impairment and amyloid positive.

Similar results were obtained for the DDI plasma p‐tau217 sample. PSMD in the MCI A+ group was significantly higher than in the CU A− group (p < 0.001) and in the CU A+ group (p < 0.05). No differences were observed between CU A+ and CU A− groups (p = 0.473), between MCI A− and CU A− groups (p = 0.098), and between MCI A+ and MCI A− groups (p = 0.065) (Figure 1B).

The findings for the DDI Aβ42/40 ratio sample were replicated in the ADNI cohort. In this sample, PSMD in the MCI A+ group was significantly higher than in the CU A− group (p‐value < 0.001), MCI A− group (p < 0.05), and CU A+ group (p‐value < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found for PSMD between the CU A+ group and the CU A− group (p = 0.507), or between the MCI A− and the CU A− groups (p = 0.334) (see Figure 1C).

Association between PSMD and cognitive test performance

For the DDI Aβ42/40 ratio sample, there was no significant association between PSMD and CERAD memory recall (p = 0.15, Figure 2A). There was a statistically significant relationship between higher PSMD and slower TMT‐A completion times (processing speed, p < 0.01, Figure 2B), not dependent on amyloid status (p = 0.48). Slower TMT‐B completion times (executive function) were significantly associated with higher PSMD (p < 0.05, Figure 2C). Given the known correlation between TMT‐A and TMT‐B, we conducted a follow‐up model adjusted for TMT‐A. In this adjusted model, the association between PSMD and TMT‐B was no longer significant (p = 0.19, Figure 2D), but significant for TMT‐A (p < 0.001).

Association between PSMD and cognitive test performance in the DDI Aβ42/40 ratio sample. (A) CERAD recall, (B) TMT‐A, (C) TMT‐B, and (D) TMT‐B adjusted for TMT‐A. TMT‐A and TMT‐B were log‐transformed in the regression models to meet model assumptions. For visualization purposes, cognitive test values are displayed back‐transformed to their original units, resulting in non‐linear fitted curves. The reported β coefficients represent the association between standardized values.

Regarding the DDI plasma p‐tau217 sample, no significant association was found between PSMD and CERAD memory recall (p = 0.07, Figure S3A). A statistically significant association was found between higher PSMD and worse TMT‐A performance (p < 0.05, Figure S3B), independent of amyloid status (p = 0.85). In addition, worse TMT‐B results were associated with higher PSMD (p < 0.01, Figure S3C), and this association remained statistically significant after adjusting for TMT‐A (p < 0.05, Figure S3D).

In the ADNI cohort, the association between PSMD and RAVLT recall was of borderline significance (p < 0.05, Figure S3E). No statistically significant association was found between PSMD and worse TMT‐A performance (p = 0.07, Figure S3F). Worse TMT‐B results were associated with increased PSMD (p < 0.01, Figure S3G), and this association remained significant after correcting for TMT‐A (p < 0.05, Figure S3H). To evaluate whether differences in memory test results reflected inherent differences between CERAD and RAVLT, we repeated the analysis using t‐scores. Across cohorts, PSMD showed no association with memory performance when using t‐scores (Figure S4). Figure S5 shows that partial R2 was higher for executive function and processing speed than for memory. Partial R2 also indicated that PSMD contributed most to TMT‐A across cohorts, with a reduced contribution to TMT‐B after adjusting for TMT‐A.

Survival analysis of clinical progression

Table S7 compares the three evaluated Cox proportional‐hazards models across the three samples (CSF Aβ42/40 ratio and plasma p‐tau217 in DDI, and florbetapir PET in ADNI). In both DDI samples, the model including PSMD and amyloid status at baseline showed the lowest AIC and a significant chi‐square (χ2) test (p < 0.05). In ADNI, the same model showed the lowest AIC, with a borderline χ2 (p‐value = 0.091).

Adjusted survival curves (Figure 3A1−C1) showed consistent patterns across all samples, with higher PSMD associated with increased risk of clinical progression relative to subjects with low PSMD and A−. Correspondingly, HR heatmaps (Figure 3A2−C2) revealed a consistent pattern across cohorts for A+ subjects: HR increases as PSMD rises from −2 SD to +2 SD. A similar pattern was observed for A− subjects in the DDI cohort, whereas in the ADNI, a similar but non‐significant trend was observed. These associations remained essentially unchanged after additional adjustment for modified FRS (Figure S6).

DISCUSSION

Summary of key findings

We demonstrate that increased PSMD is associated with a significantly higher risk of clinical progression in individuals with biomarker‐confirmed AD, consistently across cohorts and amyloid verification methods (CSF Aβ42/40 ratio and plasma p‐tau217 in DDI, and amyloid PET in ADNI). There was a significantly higher baseline PSMD in the MCI A+ group compared to CU A+ and CU A− groups, whereas there were no statistically significant differences in PSMD levels between CU A ± . Higher PSMD was associated with slower processing speed (DDI Aβ42/40 ratio and plasma p‐tau217 samples) and poorer executive function (DDI p‐tau217 sample and ADNI cohort), but not significantly with memory performance.

Biological and Clinical interpretation

These results indicate that PSMD captures WM pathology that contributes additively to disease progression in AD, beyond core AD pathology. Although originally proposed as a sensitive imaging marker for SVD, accumulating evidence, including the present findings, suggests that PSMD captures not only contributions to WM pathology from non‐amyloid SVD, 19 but also CAA, 31 , 32 and axonal degeneration in neurodegenerative diseases including AD. 19 In line with this interpretation, PSMD was linked to reduced processing speed and executive function. These domain‐specific effects suggest that PSMD primarily reflects disruptions in network connectivity and represents a distinct dimension of disease staging and risk stratification.

PSMD was associated with increased progression risk among A− individuals in the DDI cohort but not in ADNI. These findings may partly reflect differences in socio‐economic background, as the composition of the ADNI cohort, including education level, has been reported to limit comparability with other cohorts. 33 Although our results were adjusted for years of education, other linked socio‐economic factors may contribute to cognitive reserve and resilience to cognitive decline. 34 Apart from education, no other comparable variables related to socio‐economic status (e.g., lifestyle‐related) were available across the cohorts. Cohort‐specific differences in the relationship between socio‐economic status, cognition, and brain structure may also be relevant, as these associations have been reported to be stronger in U.S.‐based samples compared to European cohorts. 35

In addition, the subjects in the ADNI cohort were older, and elevated PSMD in A− individuals may reflect age‐related WM changes with weaker associations with clinical progression. In the younger DDI cohort, increased PSMD may more often reflect pathological processes related to clinical progression, even in the absence of detectable amyloid pathology. Consistent with population studies showing age‐related increases in PSMD after midlife, 36 age differences may therefore modulate the prognostic relevance of high PSMD values. As both cohorts excluded individuals with clinically significant cerebrovascular disease, 22 , 23 overt vascular pathology is unlikely to fully explain these findings. After including vascular risk factors (represented by FRS) in the survival models, PSMD still remained a predictive factor of future clinical progression, and the conclusions did not change. These findings suggest that PSMD does not only represent conventional SVD pathology, but also other co‐pathologies. Overall, these observations support PSMD as a marker of accumulated WM injury that modulates clinical trajectories, particularly once AD disease‐related processes are established. This is consistent with our finding that PSMD contributes additively, rather than interactively, to amyloid‐related risk.

Methodological differences between the DDI and ADNI studies should be noted. The CSF Aβ42/40 ratio displays a bimodal distribution and is tied closely to early pathophysiological changes. In contrast, PET SUVRs tends to have a less distinct distribution near threshold and may capture a more heterogeneous A+ group. For plasma p‐tau217, this verification method is more sensitive for amyloid pathology in MCI or later stages, and less sensitive to amyloid pathology in earlier stages, compared to Aβ42/40 ratio or amyloid‐PET. 37 Nevertheless, these findings suggest that PSMD reflects a downstream burden of WM injury. Its additive prognostic value may be strongest in individuals who entered a symptomatic phase in AD.

Cognitive domain associations

Across all samples, increased PSMD was associated with poorer processing speed or executive function. This is in line with prior research linking WM disruption to decline in attention and processing speed, 38 particularly in the context of subcortical or vascular pathology. 39 PSMD was not associated with memory performance in DDI; in ADNI, the association was borderline when using raw scores but did not remain significant when using standardized t‐scores. Effect‐size and partial R2 analyses confirmed that PSMD has a stronger and consistent influence on cognitive domains related to executive function and processing speed (TMT‐A/B), with minimal contribution to memory across cohorts. These results suggest that nominal statistical significance for the ADNI memory finding depends on the scoring metric (raw vs t‐score) and should be interpreted cautiously. Although memory is a hallmark of early AD, this dissociation underscores PSMD's domain specificity: it likely reflects microstructural WM damage in circuits subserving attention and cognitive control, rather than hippocampal or medial temporal lobe degeneration. This domain‐specific pattern is consistent with classical models of frontal−subcortical circuit dysfunction, in which disruption of long‐range WM connections preferentially affects processing speed, attention, and executive control rather than episodic memory. 39 , 40 These circuits depend on distributed fronto‐striatal and fronto‐thalamic connectivity and are particularly vulnerable to diffuse microstructural injury, whereas medial temporal lobe pathology may be less directly captured by global dispersion measures such as PSMD. This framework may explain why PSMD predicts clinical progression without showing strong associations with memory performance, supporting its interpretation as a marker of network efficiency and cognitive control rather than core mnemonic dysfunction. This supports the interpretation that PSMD captures an additive, domain‐specific burden that contributes to disease heterogeneity, especially in early AD, where cortical atrophy may be absent. Mechanisms linking increased PSMD to faster disease progression in A+ subjects remain unclear. Our findings support additive rather than synergistic effects of PSMD and amyloid pathology, with no significant interaction in Cox models using continuous variables, and no significant association between PSMD and memory, consistent with prior reports. 41

Comparison to previous literature

Our findings extend prior work linking PSMD to disease progression and cognitive decline in AD. Previous studies have associated increased PSMD with cognitive impairment and longitudinal clinical progression, 16 , 17 , 42 and predicted disease progression in plasma biomarker‐supported AD. 20 We are among the first to show this effect across CSF‐, PET‐, and plasma‐verified AD cases, strengthening the clinical validity of PSMD as an additive risk marker. Replication in one independent cohort has further strengthened generalizability. Our results also align with earlier findings where PSMD was more strongly related to processing speed and executive function than to episodic memory. 16 , 21 , 32 , 41 , 43 , 44 , 45 Together with earlier work from our group, showing that DTI measures outperform CSF biomarkers in predicting dementia conversion and medial temporal atrophy, 46 our results support that WM microstructure may serve as a sensitive early marker of disease progression in AD, particularly when combined with molecular biomarkers.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of two independent, well‐characterized cohorts: the DDI cohort with comprehensive biomarker data and the external validation cohort ADNI. This design enabled stratification by CSF and plasma markers, assessment of longitudinal decline, and cross‐validation of findings. Despite demographic and methodological differences, the use of consistent amyloid definitions and image‐processing pipelines strengthens generalizability. Limitations include reliance on CDR for disease staging, which may be less sensitive in early stages, although it aligned with cognitive performance in the DDI cohort and was considered adequately for group classification. Although the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) was available across cohorts, its limitations in predementia staging are well established. 47 Both cohorts included multicenter imaging data, and despite ComBat harmonization, residual scanner‐related variability cannot be excluded. Of note, PSMD is a global measure that may overlook region‐specific WM changes relevant to AD. 32 , 48

Clinical relevance and future use

Our findings suggest that PSMD, a simple and reproducible measure of WM integrity, predicts disease progression in A+ individuals across independent cohorts, within different age and educational levels, and independent of vascular risk status. PSMD may enhance early detection and risk stratification when combined with established biomarkers. Its compatibility with standard diffusion MRI and minimal processing requirements makes it well suited for clinical implementation, in memory clinics or research trials. Integration of PSMD with blood‐based biomarkers such as plasma p‐tau217, could provide a cost‐effective strategy to identify high‐risk cases. Future studies should explore regional or composite WM indices, interaction with other co‐pathologies, and treatment response. For clinical implementation, normative measures are needed. As AD biomarkers evolve toward scalable solutions, PSMD may help bridge neuroimaging and clinical decision‐making.
