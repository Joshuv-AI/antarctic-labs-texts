The Beck Depression Inventory‐II scores of adults with type 1 diabetes in Japan: Analysis using the Juntendo‐Aso Type 1 Diabetes (JAT‐1) Cohort
Authors: Fuki Ikeda, Junko Sato, Kenichi Nakajima, Mami Koshibu, Ayako Sato, Katsumi Aso, Yuya Nishida, Hirotaka Watada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDepressive tendencies associated with difficulty in the treatment of type 1 diabetes (T1D) could hinder appropriate intervention. Factors related to depressive tendencies in Japan remain unclear, though recent advances in medication may have affected them.Materials and methodsThree hundred and fifty‐two Japanese patients with T1D registered in the Juntendo‐Aso Type 1 Diabetes (JAT‐1) Cohort Study were divided into two groups based on depressive tendencies assessed with the Beck Depression Inventory‐II score. We compared background characteristics of the patients between the groups and also analyzed additional clinical factors and quality of life scores.ResultsThe patients with a Beck Depression Inventory‐II score ≧14 (35.5%) are classified as having depressive tendencies. Compared to the individuals without depressive characteristics, those with depressive tendencies had significantly higher proportions of females, welfare recipients, and shift workers; a higher proportion of individuals with microvascular complications; higher diastolic pressure; eating out or taking out food more frequently for dinner; lower protein intake; and higher scores in the total score of diabetes‐related problem domains and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Multiple regression analysis revealed that diastolic blood pressure, welfare recipient status, total score of diabetes‐related problem domains (PAID), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index were significantly associated factors with BDI‐II score, and the PAID score showed the strongest association.ConclusionsThis study revealed that diabetes‐specific psychological burden, evaluated with the PAID score, is strongly associated with depressive tendencies. Routine use of the PAID could support the strategies to prevent depression in people with type 1 diabetes by identifying those at risk.Keywords: Japan, The Beck Depression Inventory‐II scores, Type 1 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic disease characterized by insulin deficiency resulting from the destruction of pancreatic betacells 1 . People living with T1D require frequent insulin injections to survive; however, frequent and unpredictable episodes of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia 2 make strict glycemic control challenging, which is related to depressive symptoms that hinder the effectiveness of T1D treatment. In contrast, recent advances in T1D management, such as the widespread use of insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), have improved glycemic control and may have altered the factors associated with depressive symptoms in people living with T1D in Japan. For example, previous studies have shown that various factors, aside from those directly related to glycemic control, are associated with depressive symptoms 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , and the improvement of T1D treatment may shift the focus on intervention to enhance clinical care further by providing appropriate support.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify factors associated with depressive symptoms by analyzing baseline registration data and Beck Depression Inventory II (BDIII) scores of patients in the Juntenso‐Aso Type 1 Diabetes (JAT‐1) Cohort Study.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This study was conducted using registered data from the JAT‐1 Cohort Study, whose protocol was published in 2024 8 . A total of 477 individuals with T1D using insulin who regularly attended the outpatient diabetes clinics at Juntendo University Hospital (Tokyo) and Aso Clinic (Shizuoka), Japan, were enrolled between December 2019 and September 2021. Of these, 70 individuals were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, met one or more exclusion criteria, or had transferred to another clinic. In addition, 19 did not provide informed consent, and 36 either declined to complete the questionnaire or submitted incomplete responses. Consequently, a total of 352 participants were included in the analysis.

The inclusion criteria of study subjects were as follows: (1) aged 20 years or older (regardless of gender), (2) clinically diagnosed with T1D according to the diagnostic criteria of the Japan Diabetes Society 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , and (3) receiving insulin therapy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diabetes mellitus other than T1D; (2) inability to understand the nature and purpose of the study, inability to read or write, or inability to follow the study protocol, such as inability or refusal to complete questionnaires.

Observation parameters

As shown in protocol 8 , demographics, clinical characteristics, medical history, lifestyle habits, and treatment history were compiled at baseline using medical records and standardized case record forms. In addition, clinical and biochemical data were collected at baseline.

The questionnaire data were collected in person on the day of the outpatient consultation, and if the patient preferred to complete the form at home, the data were returned by post. These data were used for the analysis in this study.

The analysis in this study focuses in particular on BDI‐II 13 . The BDI was first published in 1961 and was extensively revised in 1996 to bring it in line with more modern diagnostic criteria for major depression. Kojima et al. validated its Japanese version 14 , 15 . It consists of 21 items, each of which is scored on a 4‐point scale, with higher total scores indicating greater severity. For each item, if the individual has no symptoms associated with that item, he/she would select a score of zero. If symptoms of each item are present, assign a score ranging from 1 to 3, based on the severity. The final total score is calculated by adding the scores from each item. The severity is categorized as none or minimal (0–13 points), mild (14–19 points), moderate (20–28 points), or severe (29–63 points). A total score of 14 is borderline, and ≧14 indicates depressive tendencies 13 , 14 , 15 .

In addition to the BDI‐II, the following self‐survey questionnaires were administered at the same time: (1) The Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID), on diabetes and quality of life (QOL), with questions divided into four main areas, specifically diabetes‐related emotional problems, social support‐related problems, treatment‐related problems, and food‐related problems 16 . A cutoff score of 40 is adequate for detecting people with high levels of diabetes distress; a score of 0–16 indicates low diabetes distress, and a score of 17–39 indicates moderate diabetes distress; (2) the Morningness–eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ), an inquiry to determine whether a patient is a day or night person 17 ; (3) the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a sleep quality analysis 18 , 19 ; higher PSQI scores indicate poorer sleep quality. The PSQI score is calculated by adding the seven component scores, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. (4) The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), on physical activity 20 ; and (5) the Brief, Self‐administered Diet History Questionnaire (BDHQ), on dietary energy and various nutrition intake 21 .

Patients with a diagnosis of retinopathy or a history of related treatments by an ophthalmologist were classified as having retinopathy. Participants with stage 2 or higher microalbuminuria, based on regular assessments, were classified as having nephropathy. Neuropathy was diagnosed when the medical records noted bilateral sensory impairment, absent or decreased Achilles tendon reflexes, reduced vibration or tactile sensation, or other typical findings. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease were diagnosed based on documentation in the medical records. Patients with depression were diagnosed and treated at other institutions and recorded in the medical records based on self‐reported information, and we included them in this study.

Data on living with someone, shift working, current smoking, and the frequency of eating out or taking out food for dinner were obtained from questionnaires used for the educational program at our institution.

Ethical approval

The study has been registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN‐CTR), a Japan‐based nonprofit organization that meets the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) standards (UMIN000044088). The protocol was authorized by each participating institution's institutional review board following the Declaration of Helsinki and current Japanese legal regulations (Juntendo University Ethics Committee Approval Nos. H19‐0169, H19‐0231, Aso Clinic Ethics Committee Approval Nos. A2020001, A2019002). All participants provided written informed consent to participate and for publication following a thorough explanation of the study.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and medians (interquartile range [IQR, i.e., 25th–75th percentiles]) for skewed distributions. To evaluate the primary study outcome, questionnaire scores were compared to various factors using t‐tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, paired t‐tests, or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests as needed for comparing two groups. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. A multiple regression analysis was used to identify significant predictors of the total score of BDI‐II. First, factors showing significant differences between individuals with BDI‐II scores over 14 and those under 14 were analyzed. Thus, factors with P‐values below 0.05 and age were included in the multivariate multiple regression analysis. To eliminate multicollinearity, items with a VIF (variance inflation factor) of 10 or more should be deleted. However, there were no items with a VIF of 10 or more in this study.

The statistical tests were two‐sided with a 5% significance level and were performed with the JMP Pro statistical software package, version 18.1.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

The mean BDI‐II score for all patients was 10 (6–16) points. The distribution of the subjects in severity classification was as follows: 227 (64%) were classified as none or minimal (0–13 points), 69 (20%) as mild (14–19 points), 43 (12%) as moderate (20–28 points), and 13 (4%) as severe (29–63 points; Table 1). Note that seven people were originally diagnosed with depression and were receiving treatment by a psychiatrist, 5 of whom scored 14 or higher on the BDI‐II.

One hundred and twenty‐five patients with a score of 14 or higher were defined as those with depressive tendencies. The percentage of females (66% vs 55%, P = 0.032), welfare recipients (4% vs 0.4%. P = 0.023), shift workers (15% vs 8%, P = 0.045), people with retinopathy (28% vs 18%, P = 0.039), people with neuropathy (42% vs 29%, P = 0.034), and people with higher scores on the PAID total (58 (47–67) vs 40 (29–51), P < 0.001) and subitems, and the PSQI (7 (5–9) vs 5 (3–7), P < 0.001) were higher in depressive patients compared to those without depressive tendencies. The subjects with depressive tendencies ate out or took out food more frequently for dinner (2 (1–2) vs 1 (1–2), P = 0.007) and consumed less protein (57 (47–78) vs 66 (54–82), P = 0.006). Diastolic blood pressure was also elevated in the group with depressive tendencies (72 (66.5–80) vs 69.5 (63.8–79), P = 0.030). Insulin pump and CGM use were not significantly associated with depressive tendencies (Table 1).

Furthermore, multiple regression analysis demonstrated that diastolic pressure, social welfare status, the scores of PAID, and the PSQI are the factors associated with BDI‐II scores, and the PAID showed the strongest association (Table 2).

As we found stronger depressive tendencies in females, we also performed a gender‐based analysis (Table S1). When stratified by sex, females had significantly higher BDI‐II scores and higher scores on the emotional and treatment‐related subscales of the PAID compared to males. The age of onset was younger in females, and the use of diabetes devices such as insulin pumps and CGM was more prevalent. The prevalence of shift workers was higher in females. On the other hand, males reported higher consumption of alcohol and tobacco and had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension. Liver function and lipid levels were also higher in males. The higher levels of physical activity and energy intake observed in males may reflect underlying sex‐based differences.

DISCUSSION

This study on our Japanese T1D patient cohort demonstrated that more than one‐third of the subjects had depressive tendencies based on the BDI‐II score, and those with depressive tendencies had certain characteristics, such as being female, welfare recipients, and shift workers. Microvascular complications and high diastolic pressure were more prevalent among them, and dietary habits such as frequent eating out for dinner and lower protein intake, as well as disturbances in sleep, were identified. Multiple regression analysis determined that the PAID score showed the strongest association with the BDI‐II score.

Previous data indicate that depression is approximately twice as common in both T1D and type 2 diabetes (T2D) compared to the general population 22 , 23 . In particular, T1D is considered to impose significant burdens on people's lives, including psychological, economic, and social aspects. Additionally, social isolation due to stigma can significantly impact mental health. Based on health checkup data from the general Japanese population (n = 886, mean age 47.4 ± 10.5 years), the mean BDI‐II score was reported as 8.7 ± 6.4 15 . In Japanese with T2D (n = 734, mean age 57.8 ± 8.5 years, HbA1c 7.0 ± 1.0%), the mean score was 9.9 ± 7.6 24 . A direct comparison is not feasible; however, the current T1D cohort showed a higher score (10 (6–16) points; 11.7 ± 8.3 points) than both reference groups.

In addition to the frequently reported relationship between complications and depression 4 , 6 , 23 , 25 , the associations between depressive tendencies and a higher frequency of eating out or consuming take‐out meals, lower protein intake, and elevated blood pressure—have likely been underrecognized in studies of individuals with T1D in Japan. Individuals who frequently rely on eating out or take‐out meals rather than home‐cooked dinners may experience psychological distress, possibly due to excessive caloric intake and difficulties in glycemic and weight management 26 . Elevated diastolic blood pressure, more commonly observed in patients with depressive tendencies, may suggest a connection with high sodium intake and impaired sleep quality 27 , 28 , as low blood glucose and fear of hypoglycemia impair quality of sleep 29 . The association between lower protein intake and depressive symptoms remains unclear; however, it is plausible that frequent eating out or taking out observed in depressive subjects leads to bias in nutrition. It is noteworthy that few studies in Japan have shown that T1D patients receiving public welfare exhibit stronger depressive tendencies. The finding that insulin pump and CGM use were unrelated to the presence or absence of depressive tendencies is valuable.

In the multivariate analysis, the PAID score emerged as the strongest contributing factor to depressive tendencies, followed by the PSQI score (Table 2). The finding that the PAID score is the strongest predictor of the BDI‐II score indicates that diabetes‐specific stressors, such as feeling overwhelmed with the demands of diabetes management, fear of complications, and dissatisfaction with social support, play a significant role in both the onset and severity of depressive symptoms in individuals with T1D. In other words, depressive symptoms in this population are shaped not only by general life stressors but also by the unique psychological burden of managing diabetes. These results highlight the importance of targeting diabetes‐specific stress, as assessed by the PAID, when developing interventions for depression in patients with T1D, rather than relying solely on pharmacological or general psychological approaches. Furthermore, this relationship appears to be bidirectional: diabetes‐related stress can worsen depressive symptoms, which in turn can impair self‐management behaviors, thereby amplifying diabetes‐related stress and perpetuating a harmful cycle.

We also investigated our data on a gender basis (Table S1) following the stronger depressive tendencies among females, which are also reported in previous studies 4 , 22 , 30 , 31 . This analysis unveiled that females had a higher proportion diagnosed before the age of 15 (male 5.7%, female 12.4%, P = 0.042), which endorses the fact that individuals diagnosed during childhood or adolescence tend to exhibit moderate depressive tendencies 7 , and suggests that age at onset may influence psychological outcomes. Additionally, the proportion of females using costly technologies such as insulin pumps (male 15%, female 28%, P = 0.005), CGM systems (male 33%, female 46%, P = 0.015) was high, as was the proportion of shift workers. The reason for the higher use of insulin pumps and CGM among females is unclear; however, it is possible that a greater concern about glycemic control may lead them to use more advanced devices. These findings highlight the need to consider the potential impact of economic burden on mental health. On the other hand, the greater prevalence of smoking, alcohol consumption, and hypertension observed among males should be considered carefully in routine clinical practice.

Among several studies of adult T1D in Japan 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , it is noteworthy that our study performed a comprehensive analysis in Japanese T1D patients from a sociopsychological perspective following the questionnaires on diet, exercise, sleep, and QOL. However, our study has several limitations; first, our research is a cross‐sectional study, which does not establish causality. Second, the scores of the BDI‐II to assess depressive tendencies may not always correspond to a definitive diagnosis of depression by a psychiatrist, which is difficult in patients under diabetes care. Note that the Japan Society of Mood Disorders treatment guidelines state that questionnaires such as the BDI‐II are important for quantifying the severity of depression and are considered a useful supplementary diagnostic tool 37 . Third, the use of a two‐center (Juntendo University Hospital and Aso Clinic) cohort in Japan may limit the usefulness of our conclusion. Furthermore, the results may not have accurately reflected reality due to the withdrawal of subject consent, missing questionnaire data, and the fact that questionnaires were not obtained from all subjects due to exclusion criteria. In principle, 352 people have filled in all the questionnaires.

In summary, we identified several profiles related to the depressive tendencies in Japanese T1D patients in our original cohort and found that the PAID scale is a strong determinant factor. Taking the risk factors identified in this study into consideration and regular assessments of the PAID scale may improve clinical care in T1D patients by alleviating depressive tendencies.
