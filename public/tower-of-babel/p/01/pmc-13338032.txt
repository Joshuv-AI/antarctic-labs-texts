Molecular investigation of tick-borne and zoonotic pathogen dissemination and host-pathogen interactions in canines
Authors: Areej Fatima, Muhammad Umer, Madiha Rasool, Tahira Yasmeen, Hira Muqaddas, Tauqir Un Nisa, Muhammad Farhan Danish, Muhammad Asif Khalil, Esmael M. Alyami, Ohoud A. Alghamdi, Gehan M. Elossaily, Mulugeta Tesemma, Wajiha Shahzadi, Shakir Ullah, Furhan Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dogs are among the most widespread and beloved domestic animals worldwide. As close companions of humans, they are commonly exposed to the environment and to various vectors, making them potential reservoirs for a wide range of vector-borne pathogens. This study was aimed to determine the molecular prevalence and genetic diversity of Anaplasma (A.) ovis, Toxoplasma (T.) gondii and Theileria (T.) ovis among domestic dogs in Pakistan. A total of 275 canine blood samples were collected from five districts in Punjab, Dera Ghazi Khan (n = 57), Vehari (n = 7), Layyah (n = 34), Bahawalpur (n = 38) and Muzaffargarh (n = 139), during March till October 2024. Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) based analysis revealed that 8.7% (24/275), 16.7% (46/275) and 11.6% (32/275) dogs were infected with A. ovis, T. gondii and T. ovis, respectively. Co-infections involving two or all three pathogens were also detected. The presence of all three pathogens was confirmed through Sanger sequencing followed by BLAST analysis and the amplified genes were similar to the pathogenic sequences deposited from countries worldwide. Prevalence of all three pathogens varied significantly between the sampling sites. Theileria ovis infection varied significantly between the screened dog breeds. Data analysis revealed that male dogs had a significantly higher of A. ovis infection rate as compared to females (p = 0.008). Overall, our findings indicate moderate infection rates of A. ovis, T. gondii and T. ovis in dogs from Pakistan. These findings highlight the need to include local dog populations in future vector-borne disease surveillance programs across Pakistan, following the One Health approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44033-2.

Introduction

In Pakistan, dogs are common companion animals as they are kept as pets in urban areas as well as used as watch dogs and in sports in the rural areas1. Due to their frequent exposure to the environment, they are more likely to be exposed to vectors and transmit a variety of vector-borne infections to them2,3. Due to favorable temperature and humidity, vector-borne diseases in both pets and livestock are quite common in subtropical countries like Pakistan1. Dogs sub-clinically infected with pathogens act as reservoirs of these diseases for their owners and for other in-contact animals4–6.

Anaplasmosis is a significant tick-borne disease that affects a broad range of wild and domestic animals7. Dogs are known reservoirs for a variety of Anaplasma species including A. phagocytophilum and A. ovis8. Anaplasma ovis is an obligate intra-erythrocytic, Gram-negative bacterium that is known to be transmitted by a number of tick species belonging to the Rhipicephalus and Dermacentor genera9. Loss of productivity and weight, hemolytic anemia, hemoglobinuria, abortion and death in extreme cases are the major consequences of A. ovis infections8. Usually small ruminants are treated with intramuscular injection of 20 mg/Kg of oxytetracycline to treat anaplasmosis10.

Toxoplasmosis caused by an apicomplexan parasite, T. gondii, that is known to infect almost all forms of domestic and wild warm blooded animals11. Cats are known to be the definitive hosts and excrete T. gondii’s oocysts in the environment through their feces12. Transmission of T. gondii to new hosts occurs through the ingestion of food or water contaminated with sporulated oocysts13. Toxoplasma gondii do not usually cause severe symptoms however various animals are known to suffer from retinal degeneration, encephalomyelitis, fever and ataxia. Cases of abortion or stillbirth have also been reported in pregnant animals14.

Species belonging to genus Theileria are tick-borne intracellular protozoan parasites that cause mild to severe disease forms in a variety of domestic and wild animals including dogs but limited reports are available in literature regarding canine theileriosis despite the fact that these parasites are well characterized in ruminants15. Dogs are known to be infected with a variety of Theileria species including T. annulata, T. annae, T. ovis and T. equi16. Theileriosis in dogs results in fever, anemia, thrombocytopenia and renal failure. Canine theileriosis is treated with atovaquone or buparvaquone plus azithromycin therapy15.

The standard protocol for the detection of pathogens in dog blood typically involves a combination of serological and microscopic methods. However, PCR is often considered superior to these techniques due to its higher sensitivity and ability to differentiate between closely related species1. As per a recent estimate, Pakistan has 1,90,200 registered and more than 3 million stray dogs17 but prior studies have largely overlooked dogs in pathogen surveillance and there are few documented reports regarding the presence various blood borne parasites and bacteria in local dogs18,19. The scarcity of available data in the literature motivated us to conduct this epidemiological survey across five districts of Punjab, where dogs had not previously been screened for the presence of pathogens. Although A. ovis and T. ovis are typically associated with small ruminants, recent molecular evidence from various countries has identified these pathogens in dogs, suggesting possible cross-species transmission via shared tick vectors8,16. Considering the close association between dogs, livestock, and humans in rural Pakistan, investigating these agents in dogs may provide insights into their epidemiological significance and potential One Health implications. Hence, the present study was designed for the molecular detection and phylogenetic analysis of A. ovis, T. gondii and T. ovis, in domestic dogs from Dera Ghazi Khan, Vehari, Layyah, Bahawalpur and Muzaffargarh districts in Punjab, Pakistan.

Materials and methods

Study area, blood sample collection and data recording

We investigated the molecular presence of A. ovis, T. gondii, and T. ovis in canine blood samples collected from five districts of Punjab, Pakistan. To assess regional variations in parasite prevalence, samples were obtained from geographically distinct locations within the province (Fig. 1). The required sample size was calculated using the Thrusfield formula for prevalence studies, assuming an expected prevalence of 15%, a 95% confidence level, and a desired precision of 5%: n = (Z² × Pexp × (1 − Pexp))/d²19. This yielded a minimum sample size of 196 dogs. A total of 275 apparently healthy dogs were included in the study to enhance the precision and reliability of prevalence estimates. Informed verbal consent was obtained from dog owners, where applicable, and sampling was conducted between September 2023 and October 2024. A field-based convenience sampling approach was employed, with efforts made to include animals of different ages, sexes, and breeds based on feasibility and accessibility during the study period. To enhance representativeness, samples were collected from multiple locations within all sampling districts. Verbal consent was documented through a structured questionnaire, which was completed with the assistance of dog owners to collect relevant information about each enrolled dog. The collected data were subsequently used to assess potential risk factors associated with the prevalence of the targeted pathogens. The study population comprised a mix of pet dogs, guard dogs stationed in agricultural fields, and free-roaming strays. Sampling was conducted across five districts in Punjab: Muzaffargarh (n = 139), Dera Ghazi Khan (n = 57), Bahawalpur (n = 38), Layyah (n = 34), and Vehari (n = 7). The dogs represented a wide genetic background, encompassing 33 distinct breeds. Blood samples were aseptically drawn from the jugular vein of each animal and collected into labeled tubes containing 0.5 M EDTA as an anticoagulant. Blood samples were stored on ice and transported to the laboratory storage within 3 h. Blood samples were then processed for subsequent molecular analysis.

NA extraction and detection of pathogens by PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from all blood samples following the method described by Qamar et al.18. The integrity of the extracted DNA was verified through agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples were then screened for the presence of A. ovis (targeting the msp4 gene), T. gondii (targeting the ITS-1 gene), and T. ovis (targeting the 18 S rRNA gene) using previously reported primers and established protocols (see Supplementary Table 1)20–22. DNA amplification was carried out using a GeneAmp® PCR System 2700 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems Inc., UK). Previously confirmed A. ovis, T. gondii, and T. ovis DNA (validated through Sanger sequencing in earlier research projects) were used as positive controls, while nuclease-free water was included as a negative control in each PCR assay.

DNA sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

To confirm the presence of pathogenic infections, five PCR-positive products each for A. ovis, T. gondii, and T. ovis were randomly selected, purified, and subjected to sequencing via a commercial service (First BASE Laboratories, Selangor, Malaysia), using the same primers as in the original PCR amplifications. The resulting electropherograms were visualized using FinchTV software (Geospiza, Seattle, WA, USA) and manually inspected for base-calling accuracy. Sequence identity and reference alignment were assessed using the NCBI BLAST tool. The sequences submitted to the GenBank database included the msp4 gene of A. ovis (345 bp; Accession numbers PP553182 and PQ553183), the ITS-1 gene of T. gondii (300 bp; Accession numbers PQ640590 to PQ640592), and the 18 S rRNA gene of T. ovis (520 bp; Accession numbers PQ836082 to PQ836084). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using ClustalW, followed by model selection based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values via MEGA’s integrated model selection tool. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replications. The final tree visualizations were generated through the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) server. For phylogenetic analysis, the following sequences served as outgroups: A. capra (KM206277) isolated from human serum in China for A. ovis; Hammondia hammondi (AH008381), H. heydorni (FJ176225), Neospora caninum (HQ873010), and Sarcocystis tenella (MH413042) for T. gondii and Babesia bovis (18 S rRNA, HM466919) for T. ovis (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Minitab software (Minitab Inc., USA), with a significance threshold set at p ≤ 0.05. Prevalence of Anaplasma ovis, Toxoplasma gondii and Theileria ovis was compared among the districts and dog breeds through univariable binary logistic regression analysis. Odds ratio (OR) were calculated along with 95% confidence interval (CI). District was treated as a nominal categorical predictor with Muzaffargarh as the reference category. Districts with zero-positive cases had odds ratios that could not be estimated. Dog breeds with very small sample sizes were grouped into broader categories (stray, pure breeds, and local/desi and mixed breeds) to avoid sparse data bias. Stray dogs were the reference category. Fisher’s exact test was used to examine associations between pathogen prevalence and various epidemiological factors. A two-sample t-test was applied to assess differences in CBC parameters between pathogen-positive and pathogen-negative dogs. Additionally, the Chi-square test was used to compare the overall prevalence of the three pathogens among the enrolled dogs.

Results

Molecular detection of Anaplasma ovis in dogs

PCR successfully amplified a 347 bp fragment specific to the msp4 gene of A. ovis in 24 out of 275 (8.7%) dog blood samples collected during present investigation (Table 1). Anaplasma ovis prevalence varied significantly by district, ranging from 0% in Bahawalpur and Vehari to 33.3% in Dera Ghazi Khan. Binary logistic regression indicated that dogs from Dera Ghazi Khan had significantly higher odds of infection compared with Muzaffargarh (OR = 36.0, 95% CI: 7.8–166.1, p < 0.001). Layyah also showed increased odds (OR = 6.3, 95% CI: 1.4–28.0, p = 0.02), while ORs for districts with zero-positive cases (Bahawalpur and Vehari) could not be estimated (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis based on msp4 gene of Anaplasma ovis

The two A. ovis isolates from Pakistan used in this phylogenetic analysis exhibited total genetic similarity, clustering together with previously reported A. ovis sequences from Pakistan (OP978206, OR947204, OR947205, OP620758), as well as from India (OP501871), Kenya (MF360029), Italy (GQ130289), Egypt (MN882170), China (MK134854, OQ789237), and Malawi (LC553532) (Fig. 2).

Molecular detection of Toxoplasma gondii in dogs

PCR successfully amplified a 300 bp fragment specific to the ITS-1 gene of T. gondii in 46 out of 275 (16.7%) dog blood samples that were enrolled from Punjab province in Pakistan during the present study (Table 4.1). Toxoplasma gondii prevalence ranged from 11% in Muzaffargarh to 32% in Bahawalpur. Compared with Muzaffargarh, donkeys from Bahawalpur had significantly higher odds of infection (OR = 4.05, 95% CI: 1.4–11.4, p = 0.01). Other districts showed non-significant differences, and ORs could not be estimated for districts with very few positives (Table 1).

Data analysis showed that the prevalence of T. gondii did not differ significantly among dog breeds (Table 2). Risk factor analysis indicated that dog sex (p = 0.5), presence or absence of ecto-parasites on the screened dogs had no association with T. gondii infection during this investigation (p = 0.2) (Table 3).

Phylogenetic analysis based on ITS-1 gene of Toxoplasma gondii

A BLAST analysis of three partial ITS-1 gene sequences amplified in this study revealed that they were similar to ITS-1 gene sequences of T. gondii. These sequences were deposited in GenBank with the accession numbers PQ640590, PQ640591 and PQ640592. The three Pakistani isolates showed genetic diversity despite the fact that they were residing in the same clad, they were not clustered together. The ITS-1 partial gene sequences of T. gondii amplified in this study resembled with the ITS-1 gene sequences of birds, mammals and humans that were deposited from Brazil (MF766454, FJ176227, FJ966049, OL323074, JF810953 and GQ160468), Magnolia (MH423902), Norway (KM657806), China (AJ628251), Iraq (OR672852 and OR672856), Canada (MN153989), USA (AY577826) and Thailand (KP895872) (Fig. 3).

Molecular detection of Theileria ovis in dogs

PCR amplified a 520 base pairs fragment specific for 18 S rRNA gene of T. ovis in 32 out of 275 (11.6%) dog blood samples that were enrolled from five districts in Punjab province of Pakistan during the present study (Table 1). Theileria ovis prevalence ranged from 0% in Vehari to 34.2% in Bahawalpur. Donkeys from Bahawalpur had significantly higher odds of infection than Muzaffargarh (OR = 9.9, 95% CI: 3.5–27.7, p < 0.001), while Layyah showed moderately increased odds (OR = 3.5, 95% CI: 1.0–11.8, p = 0.04). ORs for districts with zero-positive cases could not be estimated (Table 1).

Binary logistic regression analysis indicated no statistically significant association between dog breeds and infection status for T. ovis (Table 2). Overall risk factor analysis revealed that T. ovis infection was neither limited to a specific dog sex (p = 0.5) nor associated with the presence or absence of ecto-parasites on the enrolled dogs (p = 1) (Table 3).

Phylogenetic analysis based on 18 S rRNA gene of Theileria ovis

The three T. ovis isolates from Pakistan included in the phylogenetic analysis showed high genetic similarity, clustering together with previously reported sequences from Pakistan (MT498783), Iran (KU507511), Kyrgyzstan (OR116238), Ghana (PQ775212) and South Africa (MK643269). Pakistani isolates were distinct from 18 S rRNA sequences of other Theileria species including T. velifera, T. orientalis, T. luwenshuni, T. lestoquardi, T. equi, T. bicornis as well as from T. ovis detected in ruminants from South Africa (MF033367 and PP754492), Portugal (OQ024170 and OQ024172) and Iraq (OP001588) (Fig. 4).

Co-infection of pathogens among the dog blood samples

A significant difference in the prevalence of the three screened pathogens was observed among the enrolled dogs (p = 0.04). Toxoplasma gondii exhibited the highest prevalence (16.7%), followed by T. ovis (11.6%) and A. ovis (8.7%). Instances of co-infection were also recorded. Six dogs (2.2%) from Dera Ghazi Khan and Layyah were concurrently infected with A. ovis and T. gondii. Additionally, one dog (0.36%) from Layyah was simultaneously positive for T. gondii and T. ovis, while another (0.36%) from the same district harbored both A. ovis and T. ovis. Notably, a Bull Terrier from Layyah was found to be infected with all three pathogens (0.36%) screened during this study (Table 4).

Discussion

Owing to their frequent environmental exposure, dogs can harbor a variety of bacterial and parasitic pathogens, thereby acting as potential reservoirs of zoonotic importance23,24. In light of this, the present study provides novel insights into the prevalence and phylogenetic analysis of A. ovis, T. gondii and T. ovis in canine populations from five districts of Punjab, Pakistan.

In the present study, A. ovis was detected in 8.7% of the screened dog blood samples. To date, reports on the prevalence of A. ovis in dogs are limited and primarily originate from China. For instance, Yang et al.8 reported an 8.5% infection rate among stray dogs in Anhui Province. Similarly, Cui et al.25 found 4.1% prevalence in dog samples from Henan, while a more recent study by Jian et al.26 documented a 2.6% infection rate in dogs from Tianjin. Compared to these findings, the prevalence observed in our study is relatively higher. This relatively higher prevalence in Pakistan may be due to regional differences in tick density, environmental exposure, or the inclusion of outdoor-working dogs in our sample population. Additionally, the observed variation in prevalence across studies could be influenced by factors such as differences in geographic and climatic conditions, sampling period, sample size, and the abundance of tick vectors in the respective regions27.

Accurate identification of tick-borne parasites is essential for proper taxonomic classification, selection of appropriate treatment, and development of effective prevention strategies28. This study provides preliminary insights into the genetic diversity of A. ovis in dogs from Pakistan. Two msp4 gene amplicons were used for phylogenetic analysis. The phylogenetic analysis of sequences obtained from PCR-positive samples revealed that the local isolates clustered with previously reported strains, demonstrating both the presence of the pathogens and their genetic relatedness. This integrated approach provides a comprehensive understanding of pathogen prevalence and diversity. The sequences obtained showed high similarity to A. ovis msp4 gene sequences previously reported in small ruminants from Pakistan29, as well as unpublished data from rodents in Pakistan, small ruminants in India, sheep in Kenya, Italy, and Egypt30, goats and sheep in Malawi31, and ticks from China32. The limited data available from canine hosts highlights the lack of detailed studies on the genetic diversity of A. ovis in dogs. This underscores the need for broader surveillance and molecular screening of dogs across diverse geo-climatic regions in Pakistan and globally. Expanding the genotyping database of A. ovis will be instrumental in improving our understanding of its epidemiology and in devising targeted control strategies.

In the present study, A. ovis was detected in dogs from three out of the five surveyed districts and the bacterial prevalence varied significantly between the sampling districts. Additionally, male dogs exhibited a significantly higher infection rate than females. These contrasts with the findings of Yang et al.8 as they had reported a slightly higher prevalence of A. ovis in female dogs, attributing it to reproductive cycle associated physiological changes that may increase susceptibility to infection. During this investigation, we found that stray dogs were more susceptible to A. ovis infection as compared to pure breed and local dog breeds. These observations are contrary to Cui et al.25 as they found no significant association between A. ovis infection and dog breed in that were screened in Henan, China. The limited epidemiological data currently available highlight the need for broader, large-scale surveys to better understand host–pathogen dynamics. Such studies are essential to inform disease prevention strategies and ultimately improve the health and well-being of companion animals worldwide.

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides initial molecular evidence of T. gondii in blood samples of local dogs from Pakistan, with a detected prevalence of 16.7%. A previous report from Pakistan by Said et al.33 detected antibodies against T. gondii in 45.18% of dogs using serological assays in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The higher seroprevalence compared to the present PCR-based findings may reflect differences in diagnostic approaches, as serology indicates prior exposure whereas PCR detects current infection. Additionally, variations in management practices, environmental contamination, and feeding habits in rural areas may also contribute to differences in infection estimates. There are few reports from various countries of the world documenting T. gondii infection in dogs. Prevalence of T. gondii was 46.3% in the blood samples of German Shepard dogs from South Korea34, 26.7% in blood of street dogs in Srilanka35, 10% in dog blood samples from Iran36 and 4.4% in the fecal material of dogs from China37. This limited data available in literature suggests that more and large scale studies must be conducted globally as well as in different parts of Pakistan for better understanding of T. gondii infection among dogs.

This study provides initial insights into T. gondii in dogs from Pakistan, based on phylogenetic analysis of three short ITS-1 gene fragments. The nucleotide sequences amplified in this study showed high similarity to ITS-1 gene sequences of T. gondii previously reported from dog semen38, goats39, beef cattle40 and wild birds41, free ranging chicken42 and sparrows43 from Brazil, from milk of livestock in Magnolia (unpublished data), Eurasian otters from Norway44, ruminants in China (unpublished data), human in Iraq (unpublished data), birds in Canada (unpublished data), dogs from USA (unpublished data) and from cat feces from Thailand45. As there is very limited information about T. gondii from felids and canines, this phylogenetic analysis clearly indicates that genetic diversity of this most common parasite need to be explored further. Therefore, additional dogs should be enrolled and screened from diverse regions of Pakistan and globally to enhance our understanding of T. gondii prevalence and genetic diversity. Such data will be instrumental in developing effective control and prevention strategies.

In the present study, T. gondii infection was found to vary across sampling districts; however, no significant association of this infection was observed with dog breed, sex, or age. These findings align with the only available report from Pakistan by Said et al.33, who similarly reported that the seroprevalence of T. gondii in dogs from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was not restricted to any specific age group or sex. Said et al.33 observed that the T. gondii infection was widespread in dogs in the locations that had contaminated environment and the human population was more susceptible to infection because oocytes of parasite were spread in the environment. Ferraz et al.46 and Asghari et al.36 had also reported that age, sex and breed of the screened dogs were not associated with T. gondii infection in Brazil and Iran respectively. These findings are further supported by studies from central China, where researchers also documented that the seroprevalence of T. gondii was not influenced by the age or sex of the dogs examined37. Lee et al.34 had also reported that the T. gondii prevalence did not vary with age or gender of enrolled dogs. In contrast, Selim et al.47 reported a significant variation in T. gondii prevalence based on sex, age, and breed of dogs screened in northern Egypt. Toxoplasma gondii was higher in females, dogs more 5 years age, German shepherd breed and in those areas where rodents were present. Zhu et al.37 documented that dogs from pet stores had a considerably greater incidence of T. gondii than dogs from other sources in China.

Theileria ovis is known to cause benign theileriosis in sheep and goats, and its detection in dogs has been reported in a few studies48. This finding raises the hypothesis of possible cross-species transmission involving ruminants and canids via shared tick vectors. However, the transmission pathways remain unclear, warranting integrated studies involving dogs, small ruminants, ticks, and potentially humans to better understand the epidemiological cycle. There are just a couple of studies that has reported lower prevalence of this parasite in dogs as compared to our observation. Gholami et al.15 had found that 1/23 (4.3%) Shepard dog in Iran was positive for T. ovis. Altay et al.49 had reported that 1/337 (0.29%) dogs enrolled from Kyrgyzstan, Most of the dogs screened in this study were associated with sheep, goat or dairy farms and potentially they got T. ovis infection from ruminants. Given the scarcity of global reports on T. ovis in dogs, further large-scale investigations are needed to better understand its occurrence in canine populations.

The evolutionary history of vertebrates is often investigated using 18 S rRNA genes, which are relatively conserved and evolve at a slow rate50. This study provides initial insights into the genetic diversity of T. ovis in Pakistani dogs based on partial 18 S rRNA gene sequences. The nucleotide sequences amplified in this study showed similarities with the 18 S rRNA gene sequence of T. ovis reported from sheep in Pakistan48, stray dogs in Iran (unpublished data), dogs in Kyrgyzstan (unpublished data), ruminants in Ghana (unpublished data) and domestic animals in South Africa (unpublished data). 18 S rRNA sequences of T. ovis generated in this study were distinct from T. ovis detected in small ruminants from South Africa51, Portugal (unpublished data) and Iraq (unpublished data) indicating that 18 S rRNA sequences of T. ovis are genetically diverse globally although the sequences generated in this study presented genetic similarity. This limited and mostly unpublished data from dogs is clearly indicating that the genetic diversity of T. ovis need to be investigated in detailed not only in Pakistan but also in regions in the world to have more information about the prevalence and genotyping of common apicomplexan parasite.

In the present study, the prevalence of T. ovis in dogs varied across the sampling districts suggesting that geographical or environmental factors may influence the transmission dynamics of this parasite. To our knowledge, epidemiological determinants associated with T. ovis infection in dogs have not been previously investigated. Therefore, the findings of this study provide important baseline data that may support future research aimed at identifying risk factors, transmission patterns, and control strategies for this infection in canine populations.

This study has certain limitations, which also highlight directions for future research. Although sampling was limited to selected districts of Punjab with unequal sample sizes, the detection of A. ovis and T. ovis in dogs from Pakistan indicates spillover of small-ruminant tick-borne pathogens to atypical hosts due to shared tick exposure. It suggests close ecological interaction among dogs, livestock, and ticks, highlighting dogs as potential sentinel hosts for pathogen circulation. These findings are relevant to One Health surveillance, as they provide baseline data that will help in developing strategies for monitoring and controlling pathogens of zoonotic and veterinary importance in Pakistan. Our focus on molecular detection ensured high specificity and reliability, even though vector identification and host data were not included. The cross-sectional design of the study prevented evaluation of seasonal variations in infection prevalence. Therefore, longitudinal studies covering different seasons are recommended in future research to better understand temporal trends. Also, we did not perform complete blood count analysis in this study; future investigations should include standardized hematology assessments across sites to improve data reliability and comparability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings revealed a relatively high prevalence of A. ovis, T. gondii, and T. ovis among dogs from five districts of Punjab, with significant variation observed across locations. Our investigation of co-infections offers important information on the concurrent circulation of multiple pathogens in dogs, which is critical for understanding their epidemiology and potential health risks. We recommend expanding surveillance to include unexplored regions of Pakistan to gain deeper insight into the epidemiology and risk factors of these pathogens. Such efforts will support the development of effective control strategies for canine infections.
