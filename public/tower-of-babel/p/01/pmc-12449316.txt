The double-edged sword of PI3Kδ pathway-related immune dysregulation: insights from two case reports
Authors: Marta Dafne Cabanero-Navalon, Victor Garcia-Bustos, Santos Ibanez-Barcelo, Héctor Balastegui-Martin, Javier Grimaldos-Lodares, Pedro Moral-Moral
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3Ks), particularly the PI3Kδ pathway, play a crucial role in regulating immune functions. Alterations in this pathway, either as hyperactivation, such as in activated PI3Kδ syndrome (APDS), or rarely described hypoactivation, profoundly influence immune function and are linked to a spectrum of immunodeficiencies and autoimmune conditions. This report describes two cases of late-onset immunodeficiencies associated with PI3Kδ pathway dysregulation, each presenting with unique mutations and clinical manifestations. The first case involves a heterozygous mutation in PI3KR1 (c.5A > T, p.Tyr2Phe) indicative of PI3Kδ hyperactivation, effectively managed with sirolimus. The second case is characterized by a homozygous mutation in PIK3CD (c.2608C > T, p.Arg870Ter), suggesting PI3Kδ hypoactivation, with clinical features including psoriatic arthritis and ulcerative colitis. These cases underscore the heterogeneous clinical features and the challenges in managing such rare genetic variants. These cases underscore the importance of considering primary immunodeficiency in individuals exhibiting signs of both infectious and non-infectious autoimmune or immune dysregulation complications. Prompt genetic screening and strategic therapeutic approaches are crucial for effectively managing these conditions and mitigating the risks associated with immunosuppressive treatments. These insights emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of genetic factors in immunodeficiencies to devise personalized treatment strategies that substantially improve patients’ quality of life.

Introduction

Phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3Ks), identified by Whitman et al. in 1985, comprise a diverse family of enzymes crucial for regulating growth, metabolism, survival, and motility across various cell types [5, 20]. These enzymes are categorized into three classes (I, II, and III) based on structural and functional similarities [15]. Class I PI3Ks, which are heterodimers of regulatory and catalytic subunits, primarily function to convert phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-triphosphate (PIP3), facilitating the binding and activation of intracellular enzymes such as AKT/PKB that promote cell proliferation and growth [14]. The regulatory p85 subunit, existing in α and β isoforms, binds to the catalytic p110 subunit to maintain its inhibition [4]. Notably, the p110 subunit exists in four isoforms (α, β, γ, and δ), with class 1 A (α, β, and δ) being associated with p85 and class IB (γ) being associated with p101 and p87. The α and β isoforms are ubiquitously expressed, while γ and δ are predominantly found in immune cells, making PI3Kδ a key isoform in B and T lymphocyte regulation [4, 5].

Over the past 10 years, the identification of germline mutations in genes associated with the PI3Kδ pathway (including PIK3CD, PIK3R1, or PTEN) has confirmed that the altered activity of PI3Kδ, whether through hyperactivation or hypoactivation, results in compromised and dysregulated immune responses [13]. Hyperactivation of the pathway, resulting from mutations in PIK3CD and PIK3R1, manifests as activated PI3Kδ syndrome (APDS) and similar conditions [11]. These activating mutations lead to immunodeficiency with recurrent infections by multiple pathogens [1, 7, 12]. There are two main subtypes of APDS, which are caused by variants in the PIK3CD and PIK3R1 genes [9, 19]. The PIK3CD gene encodes the PI3Kδ catalytic subunit p110δ, and gain-of-function (GOF) mutations give rise to the APDS1 phenotype. Loss-of-function (LOF) mutations in the PI3KR1 gene, which encodes the regulatory p85α subunit, give rise to the APDS2 phenotype [19]. However, as mutated p85α is less able to inhibit PI3Kδ, hyperactivation of the catalytic subunit occurs, and therefore, LOF mutations can appear as an overall upregulation in the PI3Kδ enzyme and downstream pathway [16].

APDS is a rare inherited autosomal dominant primary immune deficiency (PID) first reported independently by Angulo and Lucas in 2013–2014 [1, 12]. It typically presents in childhood with recurrent infections, although a minority of patients are not identified until adulthood [2, 17]. The most common infections are bacterial and viral pneumonias, upper respiratory tract infections, rhinosinusitis, and otitis. However, patients may also develop non-infectious complications, including cytopenia, autoimmunity, lymphadenopathy, and, in some cases, can lead to malignant disease [8]. Despite the prevalence of similar presentations associated with recurrent infections, clinical manifestations of APDS are highly heterogeneous and can also include extra-pulmonary manifestations. To date, over 250 APDS patients have been reported, and reviews and meta-analyses have highlighted the features of this newly recognized disease [6, 10]. Conversely, hypoactivation of the PI3K pathway, although less frequently reported, also results in dysregulated immune responses. Clinically, these patients present with recurrent sinopulmonary infections and enterocolitis/autoimmunity (e.g., psoriasis and arthritis), while lymphoproliferation is less prominent than in hyperactivation disorders. Immunologically, the phenotype is marked by hypogammaglobulinemia, poor vaccine responses, and reduced class-switched memory B cells, with T-cell numbers generally preserved but defective TCR signaling (reduced PIP3 generation/AKT phosphorylation) and impaired cytokine responses in some cases; management relies on immunoglobulin replacement, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and HSCT in selected scenarios. Hypoactivation most commonly results from biallelic LOF variants in PIK3CD (p110δ) or PIK3R1 (p85α), both of which decrease PI3Kδ signaling. Heterozygous PIK3R1 LOF underlies SHORT syndrome (short stature, ocular and dental anomalies, and dysmorphisms) with variably reported immune abnormalities. By contrast, PTEN LOF produces an APDS-like hyperactivation phenotype rather than hypoactivation, yet is mechanistically relevant as a negative regulator of PI3K. Of note, concurrent SKAP mutations have been described in siblings with PIK3CD LOF and are thought to explain dysmorphic/developmental features rather than the immune defect [13, 17]. Therefore, these manifestations differ from those observed in patients with hyperactivation of the PI3Kδ pathway, illustrating the dual roles of this pathway in immune regulation.

This report aims to outline the distinct clinical phenotype of two patients with immune dysregulation caused by novel mutations in the PI3Kδ pathway, respectively leading to its hyperactivation and hypoactivation. Additionally, it highlights the importance of molecular target exploration based on genetic evidence in PIDs.

Case description

The first case is that of a female whose clinical onset was at 56 years old with a diagnosis of hemolytic anemia due to the presence of warm antibodies and inguinal lymphadenopathies in 2006. There was no relevant family history. No dysmorphic features or neurodevelopmental abnormalities were observed, and the patient had no history of learning difficulties. Treatment with prednisone 1.5 mg/kg/day in a descending regimen for 3 months was initiated, resulting in the resolution of the anemia.

However, she continued to exhibit dry cough, asthenia, and diaphoresis after completing the course of corticosteroids. Computerized tomography (CT) scan evidenced pre-vascular, subcarinal, and right hilum lymphadenopathy. An inguinal lymphadenopathy biopsy revealed chronic granulomatous sarcoid-type inflammation without necrosis, which led to the diagnosis of sarcoidosis. Radiological resolution was achieved after a new course of tapering 0.5 mg/kg prednisone for 6 months.

Between 2007 and 2013, the patient continued with a persistent cough and recurring episodes of bronchitis, which required multiple antibiotic courses. A CT scan in 2013 revealed alveolar condensation in the right inferior lobe, bronchiectasis with bronchiolitis, 1.5-cm mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes, and homogenous splenomegaly of 14 cm. Additionally, in 2014, the patient presented with acute perforated appendicitis and suffered a complex postoperative period involving wound infection requiring re-operation, abdominal wall dehiscence, and further surgery, which led to the suspicion of immunodeficiency. Complete hypogammaglobulinemia was detected, and she showed a suboptimal response to vaccination after polysaccharide, peptide, and conjugated vaccination. At this stage, her diagnosis was redefined as common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) with immune dysregulation.

From 2015 to 2017, immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT) was started, and the number of recurrent infections decreased. In 2018, due to dyspnea and fever of unknown origin, a positron emission tomography (PET-CT) scan was performed (Fig. 1A), which revealed splenomegaly, multiple lymphadenopathies with mediastinal, abdominal, and retroperitoneal involvement, and radiological worsening of lung affectation with multiple ground-glass opacities in a peri-bronchovascular and septal distribution, corresponding to CVID-associated interstitial lung disease (ILD) (Fig. 1B). In 2019, the patient underwent clinical exome sequencing targeting genes associated with primary immunodeficiencies, which identified a heterozygous variant in the PIK3R1 gene (c.5A > T; p.Tyr2Phe, NM_181504.3), classified as of uncertain significance. This missense variant is located in exon 1 and results in an amino acid substitution at codon 2 (p.Tyr2Phe). It is very rare in population frequency databases, being reported in gnomAD with a maximum frequency of 0.3004% (30 alleles out of 9,988) and in gnomAD exome data at 0.0173% (43 alleles out of 248,708), classified as a variant of uncertain significance. Given her clinical history and after in silico analysis, a suspected diagnosis of hyperactivation of the PI3Kδ pathway was made. A further CT scan in 2021 showed progression of lung nodules and mediastinal lymphadenopathies. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was also performed, repeatedly yielding negative results for CMV, EBV, herpes simplex virus, Pneumocystis jirovecii, Nocardia, fungi, and mycobacteria, including galactomannan testing and Ziehl–Neelsen staining. Therefore, a pulmonary cryobiopsy was performed in July 2021, showing the presence of granulomatous-lymphocytic interstitial lung disease (GLILD) (Fig. 1B). Due to clinical and radiological progression, empiric treatment was started with sirolimus 2 mg per day to downregulate the pathway, with subsequent improvement in control CT. At present, manifestations of immune dysregulation remain stable, with recurrent mild upper respiratory tract infections constituting the main ongoing clinical feature prompting prophylactic antibiotic treatment with azithromycin. Thus, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) was not considered, given the patient’s stability under sirolimus therapy. Functional assays were inconclusive due to the confounding effect of sirolimus, which interfered with the results and could not be ethically discontinued, as the patient was well controlled on the treatment. The second case of interest that we report here is that of a 13-year-old female who presented in 2002 with recurrent diarrhea, fever, and bloody diarrhea. The patient was diagnosed with ulcerative colitis (UC) and treated with oral corticosteroids. No dysmorphic features were present, and she had normal psychomotor and cognitive development, with no evidence of intellectual disability. Family history revealed a maternal grandmother with rheumatoid arthritis, and her eldest sister died at 4 months due to septic shock after enteritis.

Between 2003 and 2007, the patient suffered from salmonellosis, pneumonia, and multiple episodes of acute bronchitis. In 2007, she presented with a relapse of UC, which was treated with IV corticosteroids. In 2010, she had pancolitis due to Clostridioides difficile infection, which responded well to oral vancomycin. In 2012, the patient presented with signs of psoriasis and psoriatic arthropathy affecting large joints, coinciding with a new outbreak of UC and was again treated with IV corticosteroids and methotrexate with improvement, which was maintained in follow-up. At that time, hypogammaglobulinemia and a reduced B-cell compartment were noted, along with an unresponsive vaccination test (Table 1). Additionally, bronchiectasis was detected on chest CT, suggesting CVID diagnosis. Treatment with IV IgRT was started, which was switched to subcutaneous (SC) administration in 2015. In 2018, the patient presented with worsening psoriatic lesions and a new relapse of UC. Treatment with adalimumab 40 mg SC every 2 weeks was added.

In 2021, clinical exome sequencing showed a homozygous mutation in the PIK3CD gene: PIK3CD:c.2608C > T (p.Arg870*, NM_005026.5), predicted to generate a premature stop codon resulting in loss of p110δ protein expression. Therefore, a complete LOF of p110δ is expected, suggesting hypoactivation of the PI3Kδ pathway. It is reported at an extremely low frequency in population databases, with a maximum frequency of 0.0003% (3 alleles out of 1,180,016) and 0.0002% in gnomAD exome data (2 alleles out of 1,461,832). However, no validated or widely available functional assay exists to confirm the pathway’s hypofunction directly.

Due to clinical stability, methotrexate was removed, and she became pregnant in 2022. At 33 weeks of pregnancy, she showed worsening joint pain together with worsening psoriatic plaques. Adalimumab was switched to certolizumab 200 mg SC every 2 weeks, resulting in improvement.

In 2023, the patient presented with progressive mid-upper esophageal dysphagia. She was treated with multiple antibiotic courses. Upper endoscopy revealed a ringed appearance of the esophagus (Fig. 1C). Biopsy showed an inflammatory polymorphonuclear infiltrate, without eosinophils, compatible with neutrophilic esophagitis. Allergy Explorer test (ALEX) was performed with no specific IgE detected. Currently, her dysphagia is improving with prophylactic antibiotic treatment with azithromycin, and her UC and psoriatic lesions and arthropathy are stable with certolizumab. HSCT was not considered in this case, as the patient harbors a novel homozygous PIK3CD variant with suspected loss of function, for which only very few similar cases have been reported in the literature. In the absence of robust outcome data on HSCT in this specific setting, and given the patient’s stable condition under targeted therapy, the potential benefit and risk profile of transplantation remains uncertain.

Discussion

These two case reports highlight the importance of suspecting PID not only when patients show infectious diseases, but also with autoimmune and immune dysregulation-related symptoms, or unexplained/refractory enteropathies, in order to avoid diagnostic delay (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Schematic representation of the main representative clinical manifestations of both patients with hyperactivation and hypoactivation of the PI3Kδ pathway, respectively

In addition, the importance of genetic testing in CVID patients is highlighted, especially when they exhibit immune complications, which often lead clinicians to initiate corticosteroid therapies or other immunosuppressants. These treatments carry significant morbidity, further immunosuppressing an already compromised patient. Nowadays, specific treatments such as leniolisib for APDS or abatacept for CTLA-4 deficiency are available, mitigating the need for immunosuppressants [3].

The first case involves a heterozygous PI3KR1 mutation (c.5A > T, p.Tyr2Phe) of unknown significance, yet it displays phenotypic characteristics suggesting hyperactivation of the PI3Kδ signaling pathway. The effective clinical management of this patient with sirolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, lends further support to the hypothesis of PI3Kδ pathway hyperactivation, similar to observations in APDS, despite the mutation’s atypical and undefined nature [13, 17]. However, a marked impairment in B-cell class switching is evident, likely attributable to chronic sirolimus therapy, as reflected in the most recent lymphocyte subset analysis (Table 1) [18]. Notably, this mutation has not been previously described. Considering functional tests have been deferred as they would necessitate discontinuing the sirolimus treatment, which is crucial for maintaining her clinical stability. Nevertheless, although functional confirmation through approaches such as mutagenesis/transfection studies or patient-derived fibroblast assays was not performed, these would be of great interest for future research to further characterize the functional impact of this novel variant.

The second case, characterized by a homozygous mutation in the PIK3CD gene (c.2608C > T, p.Arg870Ter), also presents with clinical manifestations of immune dysregulation. A detailed literature review suggests these manifestations are suggestive of hypoactivation of the PI3Kδ signaling pathway [17]. Notable conditions associated with this case include psoriatic arthritis and UC. Furthermore, the presence of neutrophilic esophagitis, an atypical feature, adds complexity to the phenotypic expression of this genetic alteration. Notably, in silico predictive models suggest that this mutation is suspected to lead to hypoactivation of the PI3Kδ pathway. Intriguingly, the literature describes only about 11 cases of such hypoactivation, with none displaying the mutation observed in our patient. Hypoactivation of PI3Kδ typically results in markedly reduced to nearly absent B-cells in most affected individuals, indicating an early block in B-cell development, as in our case [13, 17].

This report underscores the importance of considering PID not only in patients presenting with infectious diseases but also in those with autoimmune symptoms, immune dysregulation, or refractory enteropathy to prevent diagnostic delays. It provides insights into the complexities of these understudied conditions and highlights the value of genetic testing. Identifying specific genetic targets can facilitate targeted therapies, potentially reducing reliance on immunosuppressants. In both cases, HSCT was not pursued: in case one, due to late diagnosis and sustained stability with sirolimus; in case two, because the patient harbors a novel homozygous PIK3CD variant with suspected loss of function, for which outcome data on HSCT are lacking, and the clinical situation remains stable under targeted therapy. These decisions illustrate the need for individualized treatment strategies that carefully balance potential risks and benefits, particularly in rare primary immunodeficiencies with limited evidence to guide management.

Patient perspective

Both patients experienced a profound and sustained impact on their quality of life, extending beyond physical morbidity to substantial social, emotional, and psychological burdens. In case one, a previously healthy adult with an active professional and family life progressively developed severe functional limitations due to recurrent infections, respiratory involvement, and immune dysregulation. Loss of occupational capacity, dependence on disability benefits, and the demands of long-term therapies such as sirolimus and IgRT resulted in social isolation, financial strain, and deterioration in mental health, often necessitating psychological support. The chronic nature of the disease, prolonged diagnostic delay, and frequent referral to healthcare settings—combined with the iatrogenic effects of immunosuppressive therapies—further intensified the overall disease burden for both the patient and her caregivers.

In case two, early-onset UC, psoriatic arthritis, and bronchiectasis disrupted education, limited social integration, and imposed long-term restrictions on daily activities and travel. Frequent flares, recurrent infections, and treatment-related side effects contributed to persistent anxiety and uncertainty, even after the identification of a causative PIK3CD mutation. Family members faced considerable emotional and practical demands, exacerbated by the prior death of a sibling due to septic shock and concerns over the heritability of the disorder. The need for continuous monitoring and adaptation of therapy—including biologics—underscored the complexity of managing PI3Kδ pathway–related immune dysregulation and its sustained impact on patients’ personal, professional, and family lives.
