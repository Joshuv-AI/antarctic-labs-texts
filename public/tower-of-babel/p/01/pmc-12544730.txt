Development and Application of an Algorithm for Statin‐Induced Myopathy Based on Electronic Health Record‐Derived Structured Elements
Authors: Akinyemi Oni‐Orisan, Meng Lu, Jonathan A Peng, Ronald M Krauss, Carlos Iribarren, Marisa W Medina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeConsidering the non‐specific nature of muscle symptoms, studies of statin‐induced myopathy (SIM) in electronic health records require accurate algorithms that can reliably identify true statin‐causing cases. Prior algorithms were constructed in study populations that preclude broad applicability.MethodsUsing only structured data elements of electronic health records, we developed a two‐step algorithm to identify true SIM cases. Step 1 excluded potential cases that were linked to secondary (non‐statin) causes. Step 2 required evidence of statin discontinuation to deem remaining potential cases as true. To validate our algorithm, we first randomly chose a subset of statin users with potential SIM. From this subset, we then identified true SIM cases using two different methods: (1) our algorithm and (2) manual chart review. Algorithm performance was assessed by comparing the algorithm‐derived SIM cases to those from manual chart review, which was considered the gold standard. Metrics of algorithm performance included sensitivity and specificity. We then applied this algorithm to identify predictors of SIM.ResultsOur algorithm had 76% sensitivity, 77% specificity, and 48% positive predictive value for detecting gold standard cases. Applying our algorithm, we identified 1257 algorithm‐derived SIM cases from 5430 potential cases. Pravastatin use was associated with a 2.18 odds (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.39–3.40, p = 0.0007) for SIM compared to lovastatin use after adjusting for prespecified factors. Hypothyroidism had the most dramatic increase in risk (odds ratio 15.65, 95% CI 9.12–26.83, p < 0.0001).ConclusionsWe have produced an efficient, easy‐to‐apply methodological tool that can improve the quality of future research on SIM.Keywords: adverse drug reaction, creatine kinase, EHR, myotoxicity, real‐world evidence

Introduction

Statin therapy reduces morbidity and mortality associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Specifically, results from randomized controlled trials estimate that for every ~40 mg/dL lowering of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) by statin treatment, the risk of an ASCVD event is reduced by 21% and the risk of all‐cause death is reduced by 12% [1]. Consequently, national guidelines recommend moderate‐ to high‐intensity LDL‐C lowering by statin therapy first‐line for the primary and secondary prevention of ASCVD [2]. This endorsement has led to high prescribing rates of intensive statin therapy in the United States [3]. Despite the aforementioned well‐documented efficacy and increased prescribing of statins, ASCVD remains the leading cause of mortality in the United States and globally. More widespread statin use, which would translate to improved cardiovascular outcomes on a population‐wide scale, is hampered by patient non‐adherence [4]. The 1‐year discontinuation rate for statin initiators is as high as 70% in some populations [5]. Importantly, statin‐associated muscle symptoms (SAMS) are a major cause of statin discontinuation [6] with reported rates as high as ~25% in multiple observational studies [6, 7, 8].

SAMS represent a broad spectrum of statin‐induced myotoxicities ranging from mild symptoms to life‐threatening rhabdomyolysis. Further investigation of SAMS can provide guidance on detecting, avoiding, and managing this adverse drug reaction. Among the broad spectrum of SAMS, statin‐induced myopathy (SIM) is a more severe manifestation. SIM involves statin‐mediated disruption of the muscle cell membrane and subsequent creatine kinase (CK) leakage into the bloodstream [9]. Unlike self‐reported muscle symptoms, which are often documented through free text in the electronic health records and subjective in nature, elevated CK is an objective measure allowing for efficient detection of SIM in the records of large population‐based cohorts where manual chart review is not feasible and natural language processing techniques are prohibitive [10]. However, CK is a non‐specific biomarker for muscle injury. We need robust algorithms that can correctly differentiate myopathy caused by statins versus other causes. The quality of these algorithms has implications for genetic and epidemiologic studies that rely on identifying true SIM cases to produce accurate results. Prior algorithms for this purpose (e.g., using CK, diagnosis codes, and other structured data elements) have been constructed in small or homogenous study populations and may not have broad applicability [11, 12].

The objective of this study was to develop and validate an algorithm for identifying true SIM cases using only structured electronic health record data elements in a large, diverse population of patients undergoing routine clinical care. Validation was conducted through protocol‐driven manual chart review of clinical notes as the gold standard for detecting of SIM. As a final layer of validation, we identified clinical factors increasing patient risk of algorithm‐derived SIM and compared these to previously known associations. The findings from this study have produced a methodological tool that can improve the quality of future research on SIM and aid clinicians in avoiding this adverse effect.

Methods

Data Source

All participants were members of Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), an integrated health care delivery system featuring all‐inclusive services (e.g., inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy, laboratory) with the exception of dental care and vision. The “integrated” feature of KPNC benefits pharmacoepidemiologic research by providing more comprehensive data elements compared to other sources. For example, we have substantial confidence that our participants did not obtain their prescribed drugs from pharmacies external to the KPNC network [13]. KPNC electronic health records containing demographics, laboratory records, pharmacy records, and International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnostic codes (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10) between the years 2000 and 2018 were used (i.e., the “data availability” period). Laboratory records included CK and fasting total cholesterol, LDL‐C, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), triglycerides, glucose, and hemoglobin A1c levels. Pharmacy data (both inpatient and outpatient) included dispensing records for simvastatin, lovastatin, atorvastatin, pravastatin, and rosuvastatin. Diagnostic codes included hypothyroidism, hypertension, gout, diabetes, opioid abuse, skeletal disease, human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, acute myocardial infarction, major surgery, acute kidney failure (or dialysis), accidents/injuries, falls, sepsis/septic shock, coma, stroke, convulsions, paraplegia, dehydration, pneumonia, and overexertion from strenuous movement/load (codes are shown in Table S1). Other electronic health records included for this study were systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). The study was approved by the Kaiser Foundation Research Institute and the University of California San Francisco Institutional Review Boards.

Study Population

Statin users were defined as new initiators of simvastatin, lovastatin, atorvastatin, pravastatin, or rosuvastatin monotherapy at age 45–70 years. We used fold change from the upper limit of normal (ULN) as the unit for CK and set the threshold for elevated levels at ≥ 4 × ULN (336 IU/L in males and 176 IU/L in females) [14]. Within the data availability period, the “data analysis follow‐up” window for each participant spanned from the time of statin initiation to index CK.

A statin user met the criteria for a “potential” SIM case if they had [1] ≥ 1 incident of all‐cause elevated CK within the data analysis follow‐up window following statin initiation and [2] no incidents of all‐cause elevated CK within the 5 years prior to statin initiation. Thus, participants had to be members of the health system for at least 5 years to be eligible for study enrollment. However, there was no minimum duration of statin use required for inclusion. If a potential SIM case had > 1 record of elevated CK within the data analysis follow‐up window after statin initiation, the earliest level was chosen as the index CK. The statin dispensing record with a day's supply window overlapping the index CK was considered the index statin. There was no allowable grace period following the end of the day's supply window to capture additional CK measurements. If multiple statins within the data analysis follow‐up window for a participant overlapped with the index CK, the dispensing record most immediately preceding the elevated CK temporally was considered the index statin. All statin users meeting the criteria for a potential SIM case were included in the analysis. A statin user met the criteria for a potential SIM control if they had ≥ 1 CK level < 2 × ULN, but no CK levels ≥ 2 × ULN within the follow‐up time frame of the study in addition to the 5‐year washout period described above. If a potential SIM control had > 1 CK level within the time frame of the study, the earliest CK was chosen as the index. Consistent with the objective of this study, we did not capture SAMS events less severe than SIM (e.g., statin‐induced myalgia). See Figure 1 for more details.

Algorithm Development

As stated above, the objective of this study was to develop and validate an algorithm for identifying true SIM cases using only electronic health record structured data elements in a large, diverse population of patients undergoing routine clinical care. The algorithm contains two steps, which are intended to mimic recommended procedures for how a health care provider could care for a patient presenting with potential SIM [15]. The first step detects potential secondary causes of SIM. The second step determines if there was a discontinuation of statin therapy. Further details are shown below.

Step 1: Among the full set of potential SIM cases (as defined above), participants were carried forward to step 2 if their index CK level could not be attributed to a secondary cause of elevation. Potential secondary causes were considered linked to the elevated CK if they occurred within a 7‐day window before the elevation. We prespecified the following secondary causes based on prior literature: acute myocardial infarction, major surgery, acute kidney failure/dialysis, accidents/injuries, falls, sepsis/septic shock, coma, stroke, convulsions, paraplegia, dehydration, pneumonia, overexertion, uncontrolled diabetes (hemoglobin A1C > 9%), uncontrolled hypothyroidism (thyroid stimulating hormone > 3 mIU/L), hypertensive urgency (SBP > 180 mmHg and/or DBP > 110 mmHg), and uncontrolled gout (uric acid > 7.2 mg/dL in males and > 6.5 mg/dL in females) [12, 16, 17, 18].

Step 2: Among participants carried forward to Step 2, those with evidence of statin discontinuation (no subsequent statin dispensing record within 60 days following the day's supply of the index statin) were kept.

The index CK levels among participants kept in Step 2 were deemed to be attributed to true cases of SIM. The index CK levels of all other participants were considered to be elevated due to reasons other than statin use.

Algorithm Validation

The electronic charts of 99 participants among the potential SIM cases were randomly selected for manual chart review by two independent reviewers (AO and JP) to determine the likelihood of a true SIM case. Assessment of algorithm performance requires distribution across all possible test outcomes (e.g., examples of false positives, false negatives, true positives, and true negatives), but true positive SIM cases are known to be rare [9]. To increase the chance of uniform distribution, the 99 participants for chart review were randomly chosen in a stratified manner such that 33 were chosen from the subset of potential SIM cases that were not carried forward to step 2 (i.e., index CK level linked to a potential secondary cause), 33 were chosen from the subset that were carried forward to Step 2 but not meeting Step 2 criteria (i.e., index CK not linked to a potential secondary cause, but no evidence of discontinuation), and 33 were chosen from the subset that met both Step 1 and 2 criteria (i.e., index CK level not linked to a potential secondary cause and evidence of discontinuation). This sampling strategy, known as “block randomization,” enables investigation of rare events [19]. Each of the 99 charts was manually reviewed and classified into one of four categories describing how likely the index CK was statin‐induced versus another cause (Certain, Probable, Possible, and Unlikely) based on the modified version of a prior classification system (Table S2) [11]. Inconsistencies between reviewers were resolved by a final joint review to reach a consensus (there was > 75% concordance between the reviewers). Gold standard SIM cases through manual review were defined in three different ways from the four categories based on the level of confidence that statins were the causative factor (i. Certain, ii. Certain + Probable, iii. Certain + Probable + Possible) and were used to measure the performance of the algorithm. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated as performance metrics of the algorithm. Sensitivity and specificity were considered the primary performance metrics since they are not impacted by prevalence [20], which was altered by our stratified sampling strategy. For the same reason, the calculation of sensitivity and specificity did not require the use of weighted numbers. Power calculations were conducted for specificity and the Certain SIM case definition to determine the minimum sample size necessary [21]. Based on the anticipation that 20% of potential SIM cases would be identified as true Certain SIM from manual review and a desired ability to detect a change in the value of specificity by 20% (from 50% to 70% specificity), a minimum sample size of 61 subjects (including 12 subjects with true positives) was required to achieve > 80% statistical power at a significance level of 0.05. Thus, our dataset was appropriately powered to assess algorithm performance.

Identification of Factors Associated With Algorithm‐Derived SIM Cases

As a final layer of validation, we identified clinical factors increasing patient risk of algorithm‐derived SIM and compared these to previously known associations. We identified predictors of SIM using logistic regression among the algorithm‐derived SIM cases and matching controls (matched by age at statin initiation, sex, and race/ethnicity) with a ratio of 1 case to 2 controls. Prespecified comorbidities included hypothyroidism, hypertension, gout, diabetes, opioid abuse, and dialysis [12, 16, 17, 18]. Prespecified statin regimen covariates added to this model included type, daily milligram strength, and defined daily dose units, as previously described [22]. Briefly, the statin defined daily dose accounts for differences in LDL‐C lowering potency among statin types [22]. We set 1.0 defined daily dose units as equal to 40 mg of lovastatin daily and all of its equipotent counterparts. Relative LDL‐C lowering potency among statin types was based on the revised equivalency chart [22]. Rare covariates (prevalence < 0.1%) were removed from this list. The relationship between the above prespecified factors and algorithm‐derived SIM was estimated using conditional logistic regression. Associations with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Identifying Potential SIM Cases and Controls

We identified 998 054 statin users in the electronic health records. Among these, 370 859 had measured CK levels for whom 5430 met the definition of a potential SIM case and 13 171 were potential SIM controls.

Algorithm to Predict SIM Cases Among Potential SIM Cases

The algorithm was applied to all 5430 potential SIM cases. Following Step 1 of the algorithm, 1942 participants remained (Figure 2). The final step of the algorithm (Step 2) yielded a total of 1257 participants deemed to have true SIM.

Algorithm Performance and Validation

Of the electronic charts from the 99 potential SIM cases selected for manual chart review, 95 were available for evaluation (i.e., charts from four participants could not be accessed). Based on this manual review, the likelihood of SIM was determined to be Certain for 21, Probable for 8, Possible for 14, and Unlikely for 52.

When the gold standard definition of SIM was set as “certain” only, our algorithm performed with 76% sensitivity, 77% specificity, and 48% PPV. Using the most liberal definition of SIM (certain + probable + possible), our algorithm had a sensitivity of 58%, 85% specificity, and 76% PPV. Table 1 and Table S3 show more details.

Each step of the algorithm resulted in additive improvement for specificity and PPV metrics based on improved ability to correctly identify true negatives (Table S3). However, as expected, each step of the algorithm also resulted in reduced sensitivity due to the appearance of false negatives. For example, when the certain + probable + possible gold standard definition was selected, Step 1 alone of the algorithm improved specificity from 0% to 58% and reduced sensitivity from 100% to 93%. Using the same gold standard, implementation of the full algorithm (Steps 1 + 2) resulted in improved specificity (58% from 85%) and reduced sensitivity (from 93% to 58%) compared to Step 1 alone.

Characteristics of Algorithm‐Derived SIM Cases

Among our participants with algorithm‐derived SIM (n = 1257), the mean adherence for statin use was a mean possession ratio of 83% (mean possession ratio was calculated as the cumulative days' supply dispensed within the data analysis follow‐up window ÷ total days of follow‐up). The mean age of statin initiation in this group was 59 years, 52% of the participants were female, and 42% of participants identified as Black, Hispanic, or Asian. Of the index statins, lovastatin was the most common statin type, and moderate was the most common LDL‐C lowering intensity. Predictors of Algorithm‐Derived SIM

Both statin type and dose were independently associated with SIM risk (Table 3) at the time of index CK. For example, use of atorvastatin (odds ratio [OR] 1.35, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01–1.81, p = 0.04), pravastatin (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.39–3.42, p = 0.0007), and simvastatin (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.01–1.51, p = 0.04) were each associated with greater risk of SIM compared to lovastatin reference (Table 3). Rosuvastatin users were omitted from the analysis due to low sample size. In terms of statin daily dose, a significant relationship was observed between increasing the milligram daily dose and SIM (OR 1.15 per standard deviation, 95% CI 1.07–1.25, p = 0.0004) after adjusting for statin type and comorbidities, demonstrating a step‐wise dose response. Moreover, statin users with a 40–79 mg daily dose had a 1.47 OR for SIM (95% CI 1.17–1.83, p = 0.0007) compared to the lowest reference dose of < 20 mg daily dose (Table 3). When defined daily dose was added to the regression model (i.e., adjusting for LDL‐C lowering potency differences across type), the association between milligram daily dose and SIM of OR 1.15 became even stronger (OR 1.46 per standard deviation, 95% CI 1.19–1.80, p = 0.0003).

Discussion

In this three‐part study, we developed an algorithm of SIM, measured its performance through a variety of metrics, and applied it to our dataset to reveal clinical characteristics of SIM. To our knowledge, this is the largest and most racially diverse study of its kind. Our resulting algorithm has applicability to a wide range of SIM studies from observational datasets and is easy to apply in a high‐throughput manner.

Existing algorithms designed to identify SIM in electronic health records are derived from study populations that are smaller or more homogeneous than the current study. For example, Sai et al. [12] generated an algorithm from 5109 statin users at a Japanese hospital. Likewise, the Chan et al. [11] algorithms were derived from electronic health records of ~1200 SIM cases from a Singapore hospital: this population was diverse (57% Chinese, 17% Malays, 13% Indian, and 13% Other), but not representative of a typical United States health system. In contrast, our study population was 55% white, 12% Black, 11% Hispanic, and 14% Asian by self‐identified race/ethnicity. Although the algorithm steps are not impacted by population demographics, algorithm performance metrics may vary by population as response is known to differ by group [23]. Thus, the results of our study may be more generalizable to North American populations compared to those of other publications.

The performance of our full algorithm (applying Steps 1 and 2) varied based on our manual chart review definition of a gold standard statin myopathy case. Among the randomly selected set of potential SIM cases, our algorithm was most sensitive (77%) for detecting SIM when our gold standard definition was set as “certain” only (compared to certain + probable or certain + probable + possible). This indicates that the algorithm is best at detecting SIM cases that are more clear‐cut (e.g., certain). The specificity of the algorithm was best as the gold standard became more inclusive (e.g., for certain + probable + possible gold standard cases, algorithm specificity was 85%). This suggests that the algorithm performs well at differentiating completely unlikely cases from all potential true cases. The sensitivity for the algorithm in detecting certain + probable + possible cases was 58%. This is a similar performance as an algorithm developed by Sai et al. that had a sensitivity of 56% [12], but a lower performance metric value than the best performing algorithm by Chan et al. (72%–78% sensitivity) [11]. However, the Chan et al. algorithm required keyword searching in the clinical notes, which may not be feasible or high‐throughput in many datasets derived from electronic health records. Altogether, these findings have implications for selecting which situations are most suitable for the algorithm. Our algorithm would perform well for studies of large sample sizes where it's more important to “rule out” CK elevation cases unrelated to statins than to “rule in” the most certain SIM cases (e.g., a SIM phenotype for a large genome‐wide association study or candidate variant investigation) [24, 25]. For smaller studies, where it's important to include only the most certain cases (e.g., sequencing the genome from a handful of statin users to identify rare, high‐penetrant variants associated with SIM), our algorithm may be a good starting point for initial screening but may need to be performed in conjunction with additional tool(s) to increase confidence.

Following algorithm validation, we conducted a series of secondary analyses in our algorithm‐derived SIM cases to characterize this newly derived phenotype. As expected, we found a strong dose‐response with higher statin doses linked to higher SIM risk. When comparing our statin dosing variables, daily milligram strength had a stronger association with SIM compared to defined daily dose (LDL‐C lowering intensity). These results are consistent with clinical trial data, which show that milligram dose, but not LDL‐C lowering efficacy, is associated with myopathy prevalence [26]. Moreover, we observed that susceptibility to SIM varied by statin type. Compared to our lovastatin reference, the use of atorvastatin, pravastatin, and simvastatin was each associated with an increased risk of SIM. Our data also showed that pravastatin was the statin type with the strongest risk for SIM after adjusting for dose and other factors. It is commonly believed among clinical circles that hydrophilic statins (e.g., pravastatin, rosuvastatin) are less likely to cause SIM compared to those that are lipophilic (e.g., atorvastatin, simvastatin) due to an assumed reduction in skeletal muscle cell exposure. However, data, including that of the current analysis, do not corroborate this belief. For example, clinical trial data showed that the incidence of SIM with rosuvastatin 80 mg daily was greater than 80 mg per day of atorvastatin or simvastatin (and contributed to rosuvastatin 80 mg daily never being approved) [26]. An observational study with a similar design to the current report also does not show a strong correlation between lipophilicity and risk for SIM [27]. Lipophilic statins are more prone to drug–drug interactions [28] and are more strongly influenced by genetic polymorphisms in pharmacogenes [29], both of which could increase drug levels and the risk of SIM. These factors may be confounding associations between lipophilicity and SIM observed anecdotally. Among all comorbidities tested, hypothyroidism was by far the strongest predictor of SIM. Prior studies demonstrate an association between SIM and hypothyroidism, whereas a link connecting myopathy with isolated hypothyroidism (i.e., hypothyroidism in the absence of concurrent statin use) has not been reported [30]. With a 16‐fold increased SIM risk in the presence of hypothyroidism (no other comorbidity had > 3‐fold odds), our findings underscore current guideline recommendations [2] to monitor for and manage hypothyroidism prior to statin initiation. Altogether, the characterization of our algorithm‐derived SIM phenotype provides additional confirmation for the accuracy and future utility of our algorithm by replicating well‐established SIM findings.

Our study is not without limitations. First, our block randomization sampling strategy for manual chart review resulted in an artificially constructed SIM prevalence. Thus, caution is warranted in the interpretation of our reported PPV and NPV metrics (since they are influenced by prevalence). However, this sampling strategy is an established approach for algorithms of rare events such as SIM (which would have otherwise required reviewing thousands of potential SIM cases via manual chart review) [19]. Moreover, our reported sensitivity and specificity metrics are not influenced by prevalence. Second, our algorithm results were intended for SIM and cannot be extrapolated to other SAMS events (such as statin‐induced myalgia). However, our SIM rate of 1257 algorithm‐derived SIM cases out of 998 054 total statin users is within the range of 0.1%–0.2% reported in the literature [31, 32]. This provides us with further confidence in our algorithm. Third, without qualitative patient interviews, we cannot definitively know that each study participant administered the drug as prescribed. Likewise, we cannot be fully certain that the participant discontinued the therapy (or if discontinuation was due to SIM). However, a key strength of this study is the use of pharmacy dispensing records, which (compared to commonly used prescribing records) provides evidence that the patient did pick up their drug from the pharmacy and that out‐of‐network pharmacies were likely not involved [13]. Fourth, our data does not account for temporary lapses in a participant's health plan membership. Nevertheless, the risk of potential missing data having an impact on results was mitigated by the exceptionally long follow‐up time for KPNC electronic health records (available as early as 1995) as well as conservative study design features (e.g., a 5‐year run‐in period). Fifth, this algorithm may not be applicable to datasets that do not have similar structured data elements as the current study (e.g., diagnostic codes, laboratory values, pharmacy records). Although electronic health record data resources have become increasingly available for pharmacoepidemiological studies [33], including the data elements that are necessary for our algorithm, future studies will be necessary to determine its broad generalizability.

In summary, we have developed a high‐throughput algorithm for detecting SIM from structured data elements of electronic health records and validated its accuracy using manual chart review. This methodological tool can enhance the quality of statin phenotypic data for large pharmacogenomic and pharmacoepidemiologic studies, the results of which could inform health care providers in better optimizing statin therapy regimens to avoid adverse patient outcomes.

Plain Language Summary

Statin‐related muscle problems, known as SIM, are the most common adverse effects experienced by statin users. This study aimed to create a reliable way to identify patients with SIM using electronic health records. While these records are often used for large studies, current methods to detect SIM are labor‐intensive to implement. To address this, we developed a two‐step method that uses only standard data such as lab results. We compared this method to a detailed, manual review of patient records to confirm how well it identified true cases of SIM. The algorithm correctly identified SIM cases with 76% accuracy (sensitivity) and 77% precision (specificity). When applied to a larger group, the algorithm flagged 1257 cases out of 5430 possible ones. Statins like pravastatin were found to double the risk of SIM compared to lovastatin, and conditions like hypothyroidism increased the risk dramatically. This new method could help future research by quickly and reliably identifying SIM cases from large health records, improving research quality.
