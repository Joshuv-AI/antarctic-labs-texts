Introduction

Scabies is a common parasitic skin infestation caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, affecting over 200 million people worldwide. 1 It typically presents as intensely pruritic papules, nodules or burrows. Pruritus (itching) – often worse at night – is the hallmark of scabies in all age groups.2,3 Transmission requires close, prolonged skin contact; risk factors include crowded living conditions and poor hygiene.1,4 Classic scabetic lesions are small, erythematous papules or vesicles often located on interdigital webs, flexor wrists, axillae, waist, genitalia and buttocks.2,4 Diagnosis is usually clinical, but definitive diagnosis relies on identifying mites or eggs in skin scrapings.2,4

Scabies has age-related distribution patterns. In infants and young children, lesions often involve the head, neck, scalp and palms/soles.2,4 By contrast, in older children and adults, palmar and plantar surfaces are usually spared.3,4 For example, adults typically have lesions in web spaces of hands and flexor wrists, whereas palms and soles are rare sites in this age group. This reflects the fact that infant skin is thinner and more susceptible to mite infestation on thick-skinned areas.2,3 A recent study emphasizes this point, noting palms are common in infants but typically absent in older age groups. 5 Thus, palmoplantar scabies is classically an infantile distribution.

Despite its rarity, palmoplantar involvement may occur in older children or adolescents, particularly in cases of severe or prolonged infestation. Previous reports have documented palm involvement in patients over eight years of age, suggesting that untreated or extensive scabies in warm climates can extend to the palms in older children and adults. However, such presentations remain exceptional. In a study examining acral vesicular lesions in childhood, dyshidrotic eczema was the predominant cause in older children, whereas scabies-associated palmoplantar vesicles were observed almost exclusively in children under four years of age. Accordingly, the occurrence of palmoplantar lesions in a 13-year-old patient, as described in this case, represents a highly atypical presentation.5,6

Case Presentation

We report a case of a 13-year-old male who presented to a dermatology clinic with a two-week history of an intensely pruritic papular skin eruption. The itching was most intense at night, causing sleep disturbance, whereas during the daytime, the pruritus was tolerable but persistent. The nocturnal pruritus significantly disrupted the patient’s sleep and school performance. He lived with multiple family members in the same household.

The patient had previously attempted treatment with topical corticosteroids, which yielded no clinical improvement. There was no significant past medical history, and the patient was not taking any regular medications. The family history was notable for similar symptoms in a close household contact.

Dermatological examination revealed multiple small erythematous papules distributed over the anterior trunk (Figure 1) and acral regions of the upper and lower limbs (Figure 2). Both palms and soles showed diffuse erythema with symmetrical papulovesicular lesions and focal crustation, particularly along the lateral aspects. Excoriated and crusted lesions were also noted. Based on the history and physical examination, including nocturnal pruritus, lesion distribution, and failure of topical corticosteroids, a clinical diagnosis of scabies was made. Differential diagnoses considered included dyshidrotic eczema and contact dermatitis, given the presence of palmoplantar vesicular lesions. However, the diagnosis of scabies was favored due to the marked nocturnal exacerbation of pruritus, involvement of typical anatomical sites, and a history of similar symptoms in a close household contact. With prompt and appropriate treatment, the prognosis was considered excellent.

The patient was treated with 6% sulfur ointment for three consecutive days with 24-hour applications. The patient was instructed to apply the treatment to the entire skin surface, including skin folds, groins, navel, external genitalia, and subungual areas. Environmental decontamination measures were advised, including washing clothes and bedding at temperatures exceeding 50°C or sealing them in plastic bags for up to 72 hours. Non-washable items were advised to be treated with insecticidal products. 6% sulfur ointment was selected as the treatment of choice due to the high rate of treatment failure observed with permethrin in our clinical setting. This decision was further supported by the patient’s prior unsuccessful treatments, initially managed as eczema, and the physician’s experience with resistance patterns.

At one-week follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of pruritus and marked improvement in skin lesions, with disappearance of active papulovesicular lesions and significant healing of crusted areas (Figure 3). Treatment adherence was confirmed by patient and parent report. No adverse effects were reported.

The patient reported significant relief after treatment with sulfur ointment. The intense nocturnal itching had previously disturbed his sleep and daily activities. Following therapy, the itching resolved and the skin lesions improved within one week. The patient and his parents were satisfied with the treatment outcome and the return to normal daily activities.

Discussion

Palmoplantar scabies often mimic other dermatoses. Key differentials include dyshidrotic eczema (pompholyx), allergic contact dermatitis, atopic or nummular eczema, and various infectious eczemas. Dyshidrotic eczema accounted for most acral vesiculobullous eruptions in late childhood, whereas scabies was seen mainly in much younger kids. 6 Contact dermatitis to shoes or detergents can also produce symmetrical hand/foot vesicles. In practice, clinicians should consider scabies whenever itching is intense and nocturnal, even if the rash location is unusual.1,3 Key clues favoring scabies include family members with similar itching, poor response to steroids, and involvement of typical sites (e.g. trunk and genitalia) alongside the acral lesions.1,4 Scabies can mimic a variety of dermatologic conditions and has been frequently misdiagnosed as urticaria, prurigo, or drug eruptions.1,7 A broad differential diagnosis is warranted, as the condition is frequently mistaken for other dermatologic disorders. Reported misdiagnoses include atopic dermatitis, contact dermatitis, nummular eczema, arthropod bites, drug-induced eruptions, and autoimmune skin diseases. 1

An important variant is scabies incognito. Prior use of topical or systemic corticosteroids can suppress the inflammation of scabies, leaving only faint scratch marks or atypical lesions.4,7 In scabies incognito, classic burrows may be obscured, and dermatitis-like changes predominate. A classic report describes an infant on steroids whose scabies mimicked mastocytosis. 7 In our case, the patient’s palmoplantar lesions were initially treated with steroids without improvement, likely altering their appearance and delaying the diagnosis. Clinicians should remember that any unexplained pruritic rash – especially with nocturnal itching or any family contacts – could be scabies, even if it looks eczematous. Scabies should be excluded before accepting a diagnosis of allergic eczema. 1

The gold standard for scabies diagnosis is visualization of the mite or its eggs/feces by skin scraping (microscopy).2,4 Dermoscopy serves as a valuable diagnostic adjunct, allowing visualization of curvilinear burrow tracks, with the mite appearing as a dark triangular structure resembling a ‘jet with contrail. 3 In practice, however, many cases are diagnosed clinically based on history and examination, since microscopy is insensitive (mites are few) and not always performed. 4 According to international criteria, scabies is diagnosed on typical lesions and distribution together with itching and contact history. In infants/children, involvement of palms or head does not exclude scabies and in fact is expected. 4 In contrast, finding acral vesicles in a teenager should prompt examination for burrows or papules elsewhere (fingerwebs, wrists, buttocks) and careful history of nocturnal itch and household spread.3,5 When in doubt, a trial of therapy can also be diagnostic (see below).

The mainstay of scabies treatment is topical scabicides, with permethrin 5% cream the first-line agent in most guidelines.4,8 Permethrin should be applied to the entire skin (neck down in older children; head/scalp included in infants) and washed off 8–12 hours later, with a second application after one week. Proper application is crucial: permethrin is very effective when used correctly. 4 The British and US practice is two applications one week apart, as mites laid eggs after the first application. In non-endemic or individual cases, benzyl benzoate lotion (10–25%) is an alternative therapy, also recommended by European guidelines. Ivermectin (oral, 200 µg/kg) is another effective option: two doses given two weeks apart cure most cases and are comparable to permethrin. However, ivermectin is generally reserved for outbreaks, institutional outbreaks or when topical therapy fails; it is not approved for very young infants or small children in many countries. Other treatments (lindane, malathion, crotamiton) are less commonly used due to toxicity or lower efficacy.4,8

In our case, permethrin was not used and instead sulfur ointment was chosen. Sulfur (precipitated sulfur in petrolatum) is an old but effective scabicide, especially where newer agents are unavailable or resistance is a concern. International guidelines list sulfur 6–33% (cream, ointment or lotion) as an alternative treatment. 8 Clinical trials confirm its efficacy: for example, a randomized trial showed that three consecutive daily applications of 8–10% sulfur ointment cured 90–97% of patients. 9 Another series found a 100% cure rate using 10% sulfur (in cold cream) given once, although many patients experienced mild irritation. 10 Other studies report 100% cure by four weeks, albeit with slower response, while some regimens (e.g., a 3-day 10% sulfur course) show lower short-term efficacy (∼59.5% at 4 weeks). 11 In practice, 5–10% sulfur ointment applied nightly for 2–3 nights can eliminate scabies in most cases. The 6% concentration used here falls within this effective range. 10 Sulfur is generally safe and well tolerated; its main side effect is mild local irritation or odor. In resource-limited settings, sulfur is inexpensive and remains a reliable option. The rapid resolution of lesions within one week in our patient is consistent with the known high potency of sulfur ointment.9,10

The selection of 6% sulfur over 5% permethrin or ivermectin was supported by both clinical considerations and existing evidence. Increasing reports of permethrin treatment failure and emerging resistance have been documented in systematic reviews, suggesting reduced mite susceptibility over time; accordingly, alternative scabicidal agents are recommended when first-line therapy is ineffective. 12 International guidelines recognize sulfur (6–33% ointment) as a safe and effective second-line therapeutic option for scabies management. 10 In our setting, prior permethrin treatments had already failed, and clinician experience suggested local permethrin efficacy was poor. Sulfur ointment, however, has consistently demonstrated high cure rates (>90%) with minimal toxicity and remains a markedly cost-effective option compared with commercial scabicide.9,10 Given increasing permethrin failure and established sulfur efficacy, a three-night course of 6% sulfur was a rational alternative, supported by its long-standing use and favorable safety profile.

All close contacts of an index case should be treated simultaneously, even if asymptomatic, to prevent reinfestation. Clothing and bedding should be washed in hot water (>50 °C) or machine-dried; items that cannot be washed can be sealed in plastic bags for 3–5 days (mites die without human contact). It is also important to treat any secondary bacterial infection or eczema (with appropriate antibiotics or emollients) and to alleviate itching with antihistamines or calamine lotions as needed. Patients should be counselled that residual itching may persist for days to weeks after successful treatment due to hypersensitivity reactions. 4

With appropriate therapy and hygiene measures, scabies in an adolescent usually resolves completely without complications. The case patient’s rapid improvement reflects this – the inflammatory papules and vesicles cleared in about one-week after sulfur treatment. Follow-up should confirm that no new lesions appear; any persistent pruritus beyond 2–3 weeks may represent a treatment reaction rather than active scabies. 4 Importantly, awareness of atypical presentations is the key lesson. As noted above, palmoplantar involvement in an adolescent is unusual but documented. Clinicians should not exclude scabies solely because palms/soles are involved in an older child. A high index of suspicion, especially with nocturnal itch and affected contacts, avoids misdiagnosis. Mismanagement – for instance, prolonged steroid use – can mask scabies (incognito) and prolong transmission. Early recognition allows prompt treatment and cure.5,6

The literature describing palmoplantar scabies in adolescents is limited. Koley et al (2021) reported a series of patients aged ≥8 years with palmar involvement, suggesting that severe or prolonged infestations may occasionally extend to the palms in older children and adults. 5 In contrast, earlier studies reported that dyshidrotic eczema is the predominant cause of acral vesicular eruptions in late childhood, while scabies-associated palmoplantar lesions occur mainly in children younger than four years. 6 Outside of these reports, palmoplantar involvement is more frequently described in crusted scabies affecting immunocompromised or elderly patients. Symmetrical palm and sole eruptions in immunocompetent adolescents with ordinary scabies remain rarely emphasized in the literature. 13

Our case adds novel information by documenting classic scabies lesions on the palms and soles of an immunocompetent 13-year-old, an age at which palmoplantar involvement is rare. Unlike previous accounts focusing on infants, nodular scabies, or crusted disease, this patient’s presentation bridges the gap between childhood and adult patterns. Furthermore, most published treatments for older patients used standard permethrin or ivermectin, whereas we achieved rapid cure with a 6% sulfur regimen. Thus, our report highlights that typical infant-type distribution can occur in adolescents and underscores sulfur ointment as an effective alternative therapy when permethrin is not used. This case therefore extends prior observations by emphasizing the need to suspect scabies in age-atypical settings and by providing evidence on management with sulfur in such scenarios.5,6

This report has several limitations. As a single case report, its findings are inherently limited in generalizability and do not allow for definitive conclusions regarding the frequency or risk factors associated with palmoplantar involvement in adolescents. In addition, the observed clinical improvement following sulfur ointment therapy cannot be attributed solely to the intervention, as spontaneous partial resolution or the natural disease course may have contributed, and the absence of a comparator treatment or standardized severity scoring limits objective assessment of treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the relatively short follow-up period may not fully capture late relapse or persistent post-scabetic pruritus beyond the initial weeks after treatment.

Conclusion

Palmoplantar scabies in adolescents is uncommon and can lead to misdiagnosis, particularly as dyshidrotic eczema or contact dermatitis. Clinicians should consider scabies in adolescents presenting with acral vesiculopapular lesions, especially when nocturnal pruritus and affected household contacts are reported. Thorough examination of typical sites and early recognition are essential to avoid inappropriate corticosteroid use and delayed treatment. Sulfur ointment is an effective alternative when first-line therapies are unavailable, and simultaneous treatment of close contacts with environmental decontamination prevents reinfestation. Future studies should investigate the prevalence and risk factors of atypical scabies in older children and adolescents, evaluate the comparative effectiveness of alternative scabicides, and develop diagnostic algorithms to aid recognition of unusual presentations. Such research may improve timely diagnosis, reduce mismanagement, and guide targeted interventions for age-atypical scabies.
