A telehealth lifestyle change program for rural Wisconsin, U.S. families at risk for diabetes: feasibility pilot results
Authors: Susan J Andreae, Emily Hickey, Carol Van Hulle, Angela J Flickinger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFamily-based programs acknowledge that health behaviors are shaped by familial and environmental contexts. Since family history increases the risk for type-2 diabetes (T2DM), focusing on families may reduce modifiable risks in adults while promoting healthy habits in children. We conducted a 12-week telehealth pilot study with rural Wisconsin families to improve family health climate and reduce diabetes risk.MethodsWhile caregiver-child dyads with a family history of T2DM were enrolled spring 2023 and 2024, all family members were included in the intervention. Primary outcomes related to feasibility were assessed through semi-structured interviews. Secondary outcomes, collected pre- and post-intervention, included objective measures of physical activity and self-reported global health, physical activity, and social support.ResultsSeventeen dyads (N = 34) enrolled, fifteen completed the study with 86 % adherence. Interviews indicated the program was feasible and well-received, with participants offering suggestions for improvement. At post-intervention caregivers increased weekly physical activity by 42.4 min (p = .01), and children's mean daily step counts increased by 1226.3 steps (p = .08).ConclusionsThis telehealth, family-focused lifestyle change program was feasible, with promising results in physical activity and social support. Including caregivers and children may positively influence adult health while fostering healthier habits in youth at elevated risk for T2DM.Keywords: Family-based, Lifestyle change, Physical activity, Program development, Telehealth

Background

Preventing type-2 diabetes (T2DM) could improve public health by increasing life expectancy, enhancing quality of life, and conserving health resources (Li et al., 2019; Parker et al., 2024). Currently, 38.4 million people in the U.S. have diabetes (CDC, 2020), and early-onset T2DM – diagnosed before age 40 – is rising (Ouyang et al., 2024). Although proven prevention strategies exist, many at-risk individuals are not being reached effectively (Cannon et al., 2020; LeBlanc et al., 2018).

Effective diabetes prevention is especially needed in rural communities where social, physical, and economic conditions hinder healthy behaviors and contribute to higher prevalence of modifiable diabetes risk factors (Pender et al., 2019). Rural families frequently face geographic and social isolation, along with limited access to health resources (Pender et al., 2019). Rural areas face limited access to diabetes screening, prevention, and management compared to non-rural areas (Brown et al., 2016). Furthermore, rural residents are often underrepresented in research due to barriers like cost, time, trust, and distance (Pender et al., 2019). As a result, many health programs may overlook the unique cultural contexts, barriers, and strengths of rural communities – factors critical for engagement and adherence (Brown et al., 2016).

Although T2DM is often associated with older adults, prevention is increasingly needed for youth. From 1999 to 2020, adolescent prediabetes tripled, and early-onset cases steadily increased (Ouyang et al., 2024). However, prevention programs often do not include younger groups. Only 20 % of CDC National Diabetes Prevention program participants were under 45 (Cannon et al., 2020). This is concerning, as earlier onset is associated with higher mortality and worse complications (Constantino et al., 2013). With increasing early-onset cases (Ouyang et al., 2024) and ongoing rural-urban disparities (Khavjou et al., 2025), accessible, culturally-respectful, evidence-based prevention programs are needed to engage younger and underserved populations.

Family-based health programs show promise for preventing conditions like T2DM, which involve individual, socioenvironmental, and genetic risks. These programs recognize that behaviors are shaped by social and environmental contexts, the complex interaction of genetics, and individual behaviors in chronic disease risk (Nadeau et al., 2016).

Individual-level interventions are often less effective in addressing family and environmental influences on behavior (Nadeau et al., 2016). Moreover, effective early interventions for at-risk youth can improve behavioral capacity, self-efficacy, and self-regulation, improving health outcomes (Pandey et al., 2018). Since childhood health behaviors often persist into adulthood, early engagement is critical (Hearst et al., 2012). For adults, family support helps sustain healthy habits, and one member's positive changes can benefit the entire household (Pamungkas et al., 2017).

Family-focused programs could cost-effectively broaden access to health promotion while engaging youth. Thus, we developed and piloted a telehealth family-centered lifestyle program for rural caregivers and children at risk for T2DM.

Methods

Study activities were guided by the RE-AIM framework (Glasgow et al., 2019) to maximize program generalizability and approved by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Institutional Review Board (Protocol #2019–0556).

Intervention description.

The program was developed iteratively with input from rural families to promote resilience through positive behavior change strategies. These strategies aimed to improve the family health climate while addressing modifiable diabetes risk factors. A detailed report of the formative work has been published (Andreae et al., 2024).

Program activities were grounded in behavior change methods informed by Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) (Bandura, 2004), Self-Determination Theory (SDT) (Ng et al., 2012), Family Resilience Framework (belief systems, organizational processes, communication and problem-solving) (Walsh, 2021), and principles of entertainment education. SCT emphasizes the interplay of cognitive, behavioral, and socioenvironmental factors, including knowledge and skills, self-regulation, capacity to reflect and analyze experiences, and supportive environments. SDT highlights the role of social contexts in fulfilling three core needs: autonomy, relatedness, and competence. Entertainment education guided the integration of health information into engaging, interactive games and activities.

The 12-week program included an intensive phase of four weekly 30-min sessions, followed by four maintenance-focused sessions held bi-weekly and then monthly. All family members were invited to participate in virtual group sessions via Zoom. Before each intensive session, participants viewed a brief (10–20 min) video on the session's health topic—physical activity, reducing sedentary behavior, healthy eating, or sleep health. Sessions reinforced video content through discussions and interactive activities. Maintenance sessions focused on sustaining behavior change by tracking progress and establishing systems for reassessing and adjusting health goals. These sessions emphasized peer support, identifying community resources, problem-solving, and sharing activity ideas. Each session was offered twice—a weekday evening and a weekend morning—to accommodate changing schedules. Table 1 summarizes session topics and activities.

Intensive phase sessions met weekly, participants were asked to view a health video prior these meetings.

Participants received an activity book, health calendar, and health videos. Family goals, based on games in the activity book, focused on physical activity, healthy eating, or strengthening family connections. Between sessions, participants used the calendar to track goals, daily activity, mood, and note barriers and facilitators. Calendars were reviewed during sessions to connect health behaviors to daily wellness and mood.

Recruitment and enrollment

The program was implemented twice, recruiting families in spring 2023 and 2024 through community presentations, flyers distributed by partner organizations (Head Starts, State Extension Offices), and invitations from Survey of the Health of Wisconsin (representative cohort study of Wisconsin) targeting US Census-defined rural areas (Malecki et al., 2022; US Census Bureau, 2010). Interested individuals were screened for eligibility by phone. While activities included all family members; caregiver-child dyads (children aged 7–11) were enrolled for data collection. Eligible caregivers – mothers or grandmothers with a self-reported first-degree relative with T2DM – had to confirm they did not have T2DM and be willing to engage in physical activity like walking and group videoconference sessions. Families without stable internet access were excluded. The child age was chosen to reach children before adolescence – a critical stage of physical and social development when parental influence on health decisions tends to decline (Sawyer et al., 2018).

Data collection

Data collection was completed over Zoom at baseline and post-intervention, with visits lasting approximately 60 min. Materials were mailed to participants prior to the visit. Caregivers received $40 per visit, and children kept the Garmin monitors used during the program. Data were managed in REDCap, hosted at University of Wisconsin-Madison (Harris et al., 2019).

After obtaining caregiver consent and child assent, caregivers completed assessments, and dyads were fitted with accelerometers for seven day wear. At follow-up, assessments were repeated, and dyads participated in separate semi-structured interviews on program feasibility and acceptability. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcriptions were reviewed for accuracy and de-identified for analysis. Interviewers completed debriefing notes after each visit to reflect on the interview tone, flow, and emerging findings.

Study outcomes.

Primary outcomes focused on feasibility, guided by RE-AIM (Glasgow et al., 2019), are presented in Table 2. Secondary outcomes included caregiver self-reported physical activity, global health, and social support and caregiver proxy report of child physical activity and global health (Table 2). Caregiver activity was objectively measured using hip worn ActiGraphs; children's activity was measured using wrist-worn Garmin monitors.

Analysis

Descriptive analyses included frequency distributions for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Changes in secondary outcomes from baseline to post-intervention were analyzed using paired t-tests.

Thematic analysis of the qualitative data focused on summarizing feasibility and acceptability of protocols, measurement tools, and intervention content (Braun and Clarke, 2012). The coding team was led by SJA, trained in public health, qualitative methods, and community-engaged research, working with kinesiology student researchers. Two coders independently analyzed the first two transcripts to develop the initial codebook. Codes and definitions were discussed as a team, with differences resolved by consensus. The remaining transcripts were coded using this framework. Coding teams met for each transcript to review code application, resolve differences collaboratively, and update the codebook as needed. Data were examined for patterns, similarities, and differences, with codes grouped into categories reflecting common themes and representative quotes identified. To enhance rigor, each transcript was analyzed by multiple coders, and an audit trail documented the development of codes and themes (Nowell et al., 2017). NVivo (version 15) was used to organize the data.

Results

Twenty-eight caregivers were screened. Eight were ineligible (five due to no family history of T2DM, two because their children were outside the eligible age range, and one whose child declined to participate). An additional three caregivers were not interested or declined to participate. Seventeen dyads were enrolled, with 15 completing the study. Two withdrew due to changes in caregivers' work schedules. As shown in Table 3, caregivers were female, with an average age of 42 years (SD = 7.9); 94 % had some college or higher, and most reported annual incomes of $40,000 or more. Family health was rated high (60 %), moderate (27 %), or poor (13 %). Children averaged 8.7 years (SD = 1.3) with most in grades three or four (53 %) and grades one or two (35 %).

Program feasibility and acceptance

Overall session attendance was 86 %, with 95 % during the intensive phase and 78 % during the maintenance phase. Excluding one dyad that withdrew before starting, attendance rose to 91 %. Missed sessions were due to travel and scheduling conflicts. Intensive sessions averaged 41 min (range 30–50), and maintenance sessions averaged 24 min (range 15–45). Program satisfaction was high: 100 % of participants reported being extremely satisfied or satisfied, and all found the program helpful. Additionally, 93 % said they would recommend it to a friend. Semi-structured interviews confirmed the program's feasibility and offered feedback to improve the content and protocols. After each session, program staff documented the tone and flow and changes to the session's structure, discussion prompts, or activities. Most notes focused on refining discussion prompts and clarifying activity instructions. One key adaptation made during implementation was moving the caregiver goal and progress discussions to the end of the session, allowing children to exit early and enabling uninterrupted caregiver conversations. These notes, combined with participant feedback, informed post program revisions.

Experience participating in the program and feedback of program activities

Caregivers appreciated the program's focus on promoting positive behavior change. One shared, “…we're looking for those options that encourages fun, this has been one, really, that's one of the more positive programs that I've seen” (c11). They also valued its focus on caregiver wellbeing. As another noted, “…it was eye opening…you, the mom, really has a tendency to forget about herself. Right? Because it's like, in my mind, it automatically would go to how I am taking care of [child]. So this program kind of made you see that, well, you're leading by example….And then, like, the sleep section… was interesting as well, because…I would make sure that…that [child] had a regular routine, but myself, I would stay up. I'd try to get stuff done and then that would end up late snacking…. And, and until somebody like, kind of, you know, holds up that mirror, you don't really realize it” (c05).

Caregivers appreciated the flexibility to choose and adapt activities to fit their families, which helped engage their children, “I love just asking the kids to go through the [activity] book and pick something out…it stirred their imagination” (c10). They noted that some activities suited certain age groups better and suggested adding age recommendations. One caregiver mentioned that while some activities did not suit her older child, they still found ones they both enjoyed and involved younger siblings in simpler games. She shared, “…some games, like the scavenger hunt, like we were kind of doing that, you know, getting him outside and going and finding these different items. And I had my [younger children] doing it, too….And [child] actually started…making his own maps of like, treasure hunt stuff. And then [younger sibling] had to go find the treasure… It was really cool to see him actually interacting with [sibling] and doing that” (c05).

Caregivers appreciated that all activities included conversation prompts. One noted that prompts were “…a good…conversation starter” (c11). Another shared that an activity led to a “great conversation” with her children: “…the one where they were hiding something in their pocket…that one created a really good connection and a lot of fun with everyone” (c10).

Daily monitoring feedback

Dyads tracked mood and physical activity daily. Caregivers found it like journaling and useful for monitoring progress. Mood tracking also sparked meaningful conversations. One caregiver shared, “…the mood tracking is great. [Documenting that] this is what our overall mood for today and then a quick place to jot down any like extra notes… like these were our wins for the day and this, this is where I could improve or something like that” (c03).

Most caregivers found daily tracking time-consuming and suggested ways to reduce the burden. Estimating total activity was especially challenging, as the program emphasized finding opportunities for activity throughout the day. One noted, “…it's hard to monitor how many minutes we spent being active because…a lot of the study was to get up and move whenever we can…but how do you count the little things and how do you keep track of them throughout your day” (c03). Suggestions included using smartphone or activity monitor data, goal-oriented questions (e.g., satisfaction with activity), and better child engagement.

Feedback of the group sessions

Caregivers had mixed views on teleconference delivery, with most preferring a hybrid format. Virtual sessions offered convenience for rural families, but many struggled to build meaningful connections. All but two valued sessions for sharing ideas, engaging with others, and reassessing goals. One caregiver said, “I think they were important for like regrouping…Some accountability, I guess, more than anything else, that was helpful” (C13). Another shared that they provided an opportunity exchange ideas, “I know a lot of the families mentioned doing Nerf wars, which my kids are totally into right now, too” (C03).

Scheduling was the most common barrier to attending sessions, especially during summer when children missed other activities. Still, caregivers found even brief meetings helpful. One shared that despite a busy schedule, the sessions helped “reset and restart” her family's health goals.

Two caregivers noted their children initially felt anxious about appearing on camera during sessions. One shared, “[child] struggles with, with new things and anxiety. And to get [them] on camera and to participate…was a lot of times very difficult” (c09). To ease this, caregivers suggested using icebreaker questions and offering smaller breakout groups in virtual rooms.

Although facilitators kept sessions within the scheduled times, caregivers often stayed longer to chat. The facilitator left the meeting room open for those who wished to continue. Still, several caregivers found it hard to connect with others and felt less accountable in the virtual format; three noted less interaction than expected. Suggestions included adding ways to connect during the program, such as texting or forming small groups by geographic areas.

Feedback on study protocols

Caregivers found the study protocols acceptable and easy to follow. Eligibility screening averaged 10 min, and data collection about 60 min, with participants noting that the time required was acceptable. All data collections occurred over Zoom, offering flexible scheduling despite occasional challenges in coordinating caregiver and child availability. Technical issues were effectively managed by participants and staff. One-on-one visits helped participants get comfortable with the platform before group sessions, with staff offering phone support and time for access and navigation assistance.

Finally, participants found activity monitors well accepted by children, with minimal impact on routines and little burden on caregivers. Both caregivers and children expressed interest in integrating the devices into the program, especially for daily tracking.

Secondary outcomes

At baseline, children averaged 8512.4 steps (SD = 3034.3) and 75.8 active minutes (SD = 23.8) per day. Post-intervention, steps increased by 1226.3, 95 % CI [−184.3, 2637.0] and activity minutes by 9.9, 95 % CI [−1.5, 21.2]. Caregiver-reported global health scores were within one standard deviation of the population mean at baseline and showed no significant change. Caregiver-reported child physical activity significantly improved, with t-scores increasing by 5.5 points, 95 % CI [2.3, 8.7].

Caregivers' ActiGraph data showed a 42.4-min weekly increase in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, 95 % CI [22.8, 62.0]. Self-reported activity increased by 545.4 METs-min/week, 95 % CI [33.0, 1057.7]. Global health scores remained within one standard deviation of the population mean, with no significant change. However, social support improved, increasing from 5.3 (SD = 0.8) to 6.2 (SD = 0.8), a 0.99 change, 95 % CI [0.5, 1.5].

Discussion

This family-focused lifestyle program was feasible for rural families. Participants shared experiences with both the program and data collection. While the one-group pre-post design limits definitive conclusions, promising improvements were observed in physical activity and social support. Feedback on the content, outcomes, and measurement tools, as well as information collected regarding program staff time and the resources required for implementation, has informed refinements to enhance future offerings.

During intervention development, we identified the need for programs that support both caregivers and children in making health-enhancing choices. Findings showed that youth often learned about T2DM by observing relatives' care routines, highlighting the role of modeling in health education. This reinforced the importance of involving families to support lasting behavior change, as noted in prior research (Beets et al., 2010).

Caregivers were especially motivated by activities that fostered meaningful family connections and helped them to understand each other's motivation for pursuing health goals (Andreae et al., 2024). This aligns with research showing that social support is critical for behavior change (Wilson et al., 2017). Our formative work found that family members encouraged caregivers to try new physical activities and provided instrumental support, such as childcare or sharing household duties (Andreae et al., 2024). Similarly, Kinsey et al. found women were more likely to sustain physical activity with a supportive social network and activity variety (Kinsey et al., 2019). These findings supported our use of SCT and SDT, which emphasize self-regulation, intrinsic motivation (Ng et al., 2012), and supportive environments (Bandura, 2004). The program highlighted the immediate, positive impact of physical activity and other health behaviors to foster lasting change.

Research shows that programs emphasizing intrinsic motivation are more sustainable. For example, those promoting physical activity for well-being had higher participation than those focused on weight loss (Segar et al., 2008). Consistent with our pretest findings (Andreae et al., 2024), caregivers preferred integrating healthy behaviors into daily routines over setting weight or exercise-specific goals and favored behavior changes that enhanced wellness and quality of life. Enjoyable activities often reframed physical activity as a way to connect with family, with caregivers noting that engaging in health behaviors together provided opportunities to model healthy habits for their children.

Caregivers appreciated the program's focus on their own health and wellness goals, which increased motivation and engagement despite busy schedules. Rural mothers often face barriers to health behaviors due to caregiving demands, limited resources, sociocultural norms, and the lack of immediate benefits from lifestyle changes (Peterson et al., 2013). Our formative work echoed these findings, with caregivers prioritizing family needs over their own (Andreae et al., 2024). Women often carry a greater share of household responsibilities (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2021), influencing their health decisions and contributing to gender disparities in physical activity and chronic disease risk (Chomistek et al., 2018). Thus, the program included discussions encouraging caregivers to reflect on how prioritizing their health could positively influence their children's habits.

Participants shared barriers encountered during the program. While daily tracking was time-consuming, caregivers noted it created valuable moments to check-in with their children. Although daily monitoring supports behavior change (Epton et al., 2017), it can be difficult to sustain (Middleton et al., 2013). Participants suggested simplifying questions to reduce time and cognitive load and better integrating children into the activity. Sharing tracking responsibilities could enhance children's self-regulation—an established benefit of physical activity interventions (Pandey et al., 2018)—while easing the burden on caregivers.

Caregivers appreciated the flexibility of videoconferencing, especially in rural areas. While the program's social aspect was seen as a benefit, the virtual sessions made it harder to build last support networks. Research shows mixed results on forming meaningful telehealth communities (Banbury et al., 2018). Balancing access with travel and connectivity challenges is well documented (Banbury et al., 2018). To address this, participants suggested a hybrid peer support model combining in-person, phone, and virtual options, which will be included in future iterations.

Expanding family-focused health initiatives may help address the complex social and environmental factors influencing health decisions. These programs typically offer education, goal setting, supportive environments, and parental role modeling, though past efforts have shown mixed results (Brown et al., 2016). Variability in targeted mechanisms has made it difficult to identify effective, scalable strategies (Brown et al., 2016). Therefore, we collaborated with intended users to identify meaningful behavioral determinants and theoretical constructs, informing program content and outcome selection. This work supports the next step: conducting an efficacy study.

A strength of this study was the involvement of rural families throughout program development. However, limitations included a lack of participant diversity — all were white, female caregivers with higher education and income than the broader rural Wisconsin population (US Census Bureau).

Participants also reported average physical activity, better family health, and fewer internet issues that might be expected more broadly. Future implementations should include community-specific tailoring, input from a more representative sample, expanded recruitment strategies, and intentional recruitment targets with monitoring systems. While activity was measured objectively, family history of T2DM and other outcomes relied on self-report and may be biased. Diet and sleep measures were excluded to reduce caregiver burden but should be included in future iterations, given their importance in diabetes prevention. The program also spanned the school year and summer, potentially influencing secondary outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provided valuable insights into feasibility and acceptability, informing the next phase to assess health impacts.

Conclusions

We piloted a family-centered program for caregivers and children with a family history of T2DM. These programs may offer a cost- and time-efficient way to promote healthier habits in higher risk families by fostering supportive environments that encourage lasting behavior change. To enhance feasibility and acceptability, we co-developed the intervention with input from intended users. The resulting program emphasized wellness and intrinsic motivation for engaging in family-based health- activities. Findings suggest the program was generally feasible and relevant for rural caregivers and children, while highlighting key areas for improvement in future offerings.
