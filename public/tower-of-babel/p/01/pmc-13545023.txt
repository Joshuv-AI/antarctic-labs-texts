Low Tacrolimus C0/Dose Ratio is Associated with Higher Cmax and AUC Despite Similar Pre-dose Concentrations
Authors: Amar D Levens, Teun van Gelder, Armin Gelinck, Soufian Meziyerh, Aiko P J de Vries, Danny van der Helm, Jesse J Swen, Dirk Jan A R Moes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveTacrolimus therapeutic drug monitoring after kidney transplantation is primarily guided by the pre-dose concentration (C0), assuming it reflects overall drug exposure. At the same time, the tacrolimus concentration-to-dose ratio (C0/dose) has emerged as a marker associated with clinical outcomes after transplantation, suggesting that differences in peak (maximum concentration [Cmax]) and total tacrolimus exposure (area under the concentration–time curve from 0 to 24 h [AUC0–24h]) may not be fully captured by C0 alone.MethodsIn 534 kidney transplant recipients followed for up to 13 months post-transplantation, 635 tacrolimus pharmacokinetic profiles with corresponding C0 values were analyzed. The C0/dose category was defined using the C0/dose ratio and classified as fast (<1.05 ng·mL⁻1·mg⁻1, n = 344), intermediate (1.05–1.53, n = 127), or slow (≥1.54, n = 164). Analyses were restricted to measurements with C0 between 5.0 and 6.0 ng/mL to compare Cmax and AUC0–24h across C0/dose categories and tacrolimus formulations (immediate release vs extended release) using linear mixed-effects models. Sensitivity analyses evaluated C0-adjusted Cmax and AUC0–24h in the full cohort (4271 pharmacokinetic profiles) and assessed consistency when Cmax was predicted using population pharmacokinetic models.ResultsDespite comparable C0 levels, the fast C0/dose category was associated with substantially higher tacrolimus doses and had higher peak concentration and total exposure. Under immediate-release tacrolimus, Cmax and AUC0–24h were 30 and 18% higher, respectively, compared with the slow category. Under extended-release tacrolimus, differences were more pronounced, with 86% higher Cmax (estimate: 1.86, 95% confidence interval 1.61–2.13) and 46% higher AUC0–24h (estimate: 1.45, 95% confidence interval 1.35–1.55). The intermediate category showed a smaller but consistent increase in exposure. Higher Cmax and AUC0–24h relative to C0 in fast metabolizers remained consistent in the full cohort and when Cmax was model predicted.ConclusionsTacrolimus C0 alone incompletely reflects the full pharmacokinetic profile and may miss clinically relevant peak-related toxicity. The C0/dose ratio may help identify patients with disproportionate exposure, in whom more individualized therapeutic drug monitoring beyond C0 monitoring is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40262-026-01683-9.

Key Points

A low pre-dose concentration (C0)/dose ratio was associated with substantially higher tacrolimus maximum concentration and area under the concentration–time curve from 0 to 24 h, showing that similar pre-dose levels can mask clinically relevant pharmacokinetic differences between C0/dose categories.Exposure differences were formulation dependent: under extended-release tacrolimus, a low C0/dose ratio was associated with notably higher maximum concentration and area under the concentration–time curve from 0 to 24 h compared with immediate-release tacrolimus.The C0/dose ratio may help identify patients with disproportionate tacrolimus exposure not captured by C0 alone who could benefit from area under the concentration–time curve-guided tacrolimus therapeutic drug monitoring.

Introduction

Tacrolimus is the most frequently prescribed calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) in patients who have received a kidney transplantation. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is applied universally to correct for the large between-patient variability in tacrolimus pharmacokinetics (PK) and to avoid underexposure and overexposure to this narrow therapeutic index drug. In this context, the Immunosuppressive Drugs Scientific Committee of the International Association of Therapeutic Drug Monitoring and Clinical Toxicity (IATDMCT) published a consensus report on TDM for tacrolimus to guide clinical practice in solid organ transplantation [1]. In the 2009 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) clinical practice guideline on the monitoring, management, and treatment of kidney transplant recipients, it is recommended to measure the tacrolimus pre-dose concentrations (henceforth C0) [2]. The preference for C0 is based on the assumption that it reflects overall tacrolimus exposure, as approximated by the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), while remaining relatively easy to obtain in routine clinical practice. It is typically measured immediately before the morning tacrolimus dose together with blood for other laboratory tests (such as renal function, blood counts, and liver enzymes).

Over the past decade, several studies have evaluated the predictive value of the tacrolimus C0/dose ratio for clinical outcomes after solid organ transplantation. Early studies categorized patients into three metabolizer groups based on predefined thresholds: fast (<1.05 ng·mL⁻1·mg⁻1), intermediate (1.05–1.54), and slow (>1.54) metabolizers [3]. Other studies used a simplified dichotomous classification, defining fast metabolizers as C0/dose <1.05 and slow metabolizers as C0/dose ≥1.05 [4–7]. The nephrology group from the University Hospital of Münster (Germany) demonstrated that patients with a low C0/dose ratio had worse renal function at multiple timepoints within the first 5 years after kidney transplantation, a more pronounced deterioration of renal function (change in estimated glomerular filtration rate), and a higher incidence of CNI nephrotoxicity on biopsy and BK virus-associated nephropathy [3, 8]. In the Tacrolimus Metabolization in Kidney Transplantation (TOMATO) study, a retrospective cohort of 1029 kidney transplant recipients, Jouve et al. confirmed that the C0/dose ratio was an independent early predictor of death-censored graft survival [4]. Similar associations between a low C0/dose ratio and deterioration of renal function have also been reported after liver transplantation, suggesting increased CNI-related nephrotoxicity in fast metabolizers [6].

The mechanisms underlying poorer clinical outcomes in patients with a low C0/dose ratio remain unclear. Because tacrolimus dosing is adjusted to achieve target C0 concentrations, these patients require higher daily doses, whereas those with a higher ratio reach target C0 with lower doses. Tacrolimus concentrations during the absorption phase (e.g., 1–2 h post-dose) are rarely measured in routine practice. Consequently, higher doses may lead to elevated peak concentrations (Cmax) that remain undetected when monitoring relies solely on C0, potentially contributing to CNI nephrotoxicity, increased BK virus activity, and poorer graft outcomes.

In our center, a periodic tacrolimus AUC assessment has been implemented in routine patient care. We hypothesized that patients with a low C0/dose ratio have higher tacrolimus Cmax and AUC despite similar C0 levels. We therefore investigated whether Cmax and AUC differ across C0/dose phenotypes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted using real-world clinical data from the transplant center database of the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), the Netherlands. The study population originated from the same transplant cohort reported in our prior pharmacogenetic analysis [9]. We included adult patients (aged ≥18 years) who underwent kidney transplantation between January 2011 and December 2024 and received an immunosuppressive regimen consisting of tacrolimus (immediate-release [Prograft®/Adport®] or extended-release formulations [Advagraf®/Dailiport®]), mycophenolic acid, and prednisolone. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria:

At least one tacrolimus AUC assessment, including the corresponding C0 obtained as part of the AUC sampling profile, performed as part of routine TDM.

At least one tacrolimus AUC assessment, including the corresponding C0 obtained as part of the AUC sampling profile, performed as part of routine TDM.

Follow-up data available until 13 months post-transplantation, or until censoring due to death, graft loss, tacrolimus discontinuation, or loss to follow-up.

Follow-up data available until 13 months post-transplantation, or until censoring due to death, graft loss, tacrolimus discontinuation, or loss to follow-up.

All available AUC0–24 assessments with paired C0 measurements during the first 13 months post-transplantation were included in the longitudinal analyses. The following patients were excluded:

Recipients of combined organ transplantation involving the kidney (e.g., kidney-pancreas or kidney-liver).

Recipients of combined organ transplantation involving the kidney (e.g., kidney-pancreas or kidney-liver).

Patients with a prior liver transplantation, given the potential contribution of donor hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP)3A activity to tacrolimus metabolism.

Patients with a prior liver transplantation, given the potential contribution of donor hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP)3A activity to tacrolimus metabolism.

Patients initiated on corticosteroid-free immunosuppressive regimens, to avoid confounding by corticosteroid-induced CYP3A induction.

Patients initiated on corticosteroid-free immunosuppressive regimens, to avoid confounding by corticosteroid-induced CYP3A induction.

Patients treated with MeltDose® extended-release tacrolimus (LCPT; Envarsus®), due to its distinct PK profile characterized by distal intestinal absorption [10], which may attenuate observable exposure differences related to the C0/dose phenotype.

Patients treated with MeltDose® extended-release tacrolimus (LCPT; Envarsus®), due to its distinct PK profile characterized by distal intestinal absorption [10], which may attenuate observable exposure differences related to the C0/dose phenotype.

Patients on the generic extended-release (ER) tacrolimus formulation Dailiport® were included in the study, because it has been demonstrated to be bioequivalent with Advagraf® [11]. Similarly, the immediate-release (IR) formulations Prograft® and its generic equivalent Adport® were considered interchangeable [12].

Definition of Pre-dose Concentration (C0)/Dose Category

The tacrolimus C0/dose category was defined using the C0/dose ratio, calculated as the tacrolimus pre-dose concentration (C0, ng/mL) divided by the corresponding total daily dose (mg/day) at each PK assessment. The C0/dose category was therefore assigned at the PK measurement level rather than the patient level and could vary over time. Pharmacokinetic profiles were categorized into fast, intermediate, and slow metabolizer groups based on the C0/dose, as originally proposed by Thölking et al. [3], corresponding to low (<1.05 ng·mL⁻1·mg⁻1), intermediate (1.05–1.53), and high (≥1.54) C0/dose ratios, respectively. In the study by Thölking et al., these thresholds were pragmatically defined to obtain groups of comparable size, with the intermediate group centered around the mean C0/dose ratio.

Pharmacokinetic (PK) Assessment and Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM)

Tacrolimus concentrations were measured at the ISO15189-certified Clinical Pharmacy and Toxicology Laboratory of the LUMC using validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry assays [9]. Pharmacokinetic parameters of interest were C0, Cmax, and AUC. Area under the concentration–time curve monitoring is structurally embedded in clinical care at our center and was routinely performed during weeks 1–2 and again at weeks 6, 24, and 52 post-transplantation. Sparse sampling with tacrolimus AUC was performed using maximum a posteriori Bayesian estimation based on established population PK models for tacrolimus. For IR tacrolimus, AUC estimation was performed using a Bayesian modeling approach informed by previously published tacrolimus population PK models in kidney transplant recipients [13, 14]. For ER tacrolimus, an Advagraf®-based population PK model closely related to previously published models by Woillard et al. [15] and Moes et al. was used [16]. The models used two-compartment structures with first-order elimination and delayed absorption described by three transit compartments. This model was developed for the same formulation types included in the present study. Area under the concentration–time curve was expressed as 24-h exposure (AUC0–24h) to enable comparison between once-daily and twice-daily formulations. Further details on the selected population PK model, its applicability to the present cohort, and its use for Bayesian AUC estimation are provided in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). PK profiles were obtained using one of three sampling strategies: abbreviated outpatient venous sampling at 0, 2, and 3 h post-dose; dried blood spot (DBS) microsampling at 0, 1, 2, and 3 h post-dose; or intensive venous sampling over 0–6 h post-dose. The 0-, 2-, and 3-h outpatient venous sampling strategy is consistent with earlier work from our center, in which Bayesian estimation using 0-, 2-, and 3-h samples showed strong agreement with trapezoidal tacrolimus AUC0–24h in kidney transplant recipients [13]. Dried blood spot microsampling for tacrolimus TDM has been previously validated in kidney transplant recipients and implemented in routine clinical care at our center [17]. Cmax was defined as the highest measured tacrolimus concentration within each PK sampling occasion and was therefore derived from three, four, or seven timepoints depending on the sampling schedule applied. Since 2011, routine genotyping for CYP3A5*3 (rs776746) and CYP3A5*6 (rs10264272) has been performed for all kidney transplant recipients at our center. CYP3A5 genotype data were therefore available for all included patients.

Tacrolimus exposure targets were time dependent and defined according to the institutional immunosuppressive protocol, as described previously [9]. Targets could be adjusted at the discretion of the treating physician in response to toxicity or infection.

Statistical Analysis

Tacrolimus PK parameters were evaluated across time-varying C0/dose metabolizer categories. To assess whether patients with similar C0 can mask differences in peak and total exposure, PK profiles were restricted to those with C0 between 5.0 and 6.0 ng/mL, corresponding to a long-term therapeutic range for stable kidney transplant recipients [1]. Within this window, Cmax and AUC0–24h were compared across metabolizer groups and tacrolimus formulation (IR vs ER). PK characteristics were also evaluated in the full cohort without restriction to C0 5.0–6.0 ng/mL. Because pre-dose concentrations were not restricted in this analysis, Cmax/C0 and AUC0–24h/C0 were evaluated to account for differences in C0 and to compare peak and total exposure relative to the pre-dose concentration across C0/dose categories.

Associations between C0/dose-defined metabolizer category and tacrolimus Cmax and AUC0–24h were examined using linear mixed-effects (LME) models with a random intercept for transplantation episode to account for repeated PK profiles within individuals. Separate models were constructed for log-transformed Cmax and log-transformed AUC0–24h. Fixed effects included C0/dose category, formulation, sampling method (microsampling vs venous sampling), and time post-transplantation, modeled using linear and quadratic terms to capture potential nonlinear PK trends. Effect modification by tacrolimus formulation was evaluated by including an interaction term between C0/dose category and formulation. Model fit was compared using likelihood ratio testing.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. To assess whether the highest observed concentration within the sampling window approximated the estimated true peak concentration, observed Cmax was compared with model-predicted Cmax. Observed was defined as the highest measured tacrolimus concentration within the available sampling window, whereas model-predicted Cmax was defined as the maximum individual predicted concentration from the continuous concentration–time profile generated using a population PK model. This sensitivity analysis was performed in a subset of venous PK profiles for which individual sampling times could be efficiently retrieved from the source data and transformed into a NONMEM-ready dataset. This subset included abbreviated outpatient venous profiles sampled at 0, 2, and 3 h post-dose and intensive venous profiles sampled over 0–6 h post-dose. In this subset, an additional LME model was fitted using log-transformed model-predicted Cmax as the outcome, with the same fixed-effects and random-effects structure as the primary Cmax model.

To assess the robustness of formulation-specific estimates given the smaller number of ER tacrolimus PK profiles, a cluster bootstrap sensitivity analysis was performed with resampling at the transplantation level. The primary multivariable LME models for Cmax and AUC0–24h were refitted in 1000 bootstrap samples using the same fixed-effects and random-effects structure as the primary models.

For the full-cohort sensitivity analysis, additional LME models were fitted using log-transformed Cmax/C0 and log-transformed AUC0–24h/C0 as outcomes, with the same fixed effects and random-effects structure as the primary models. Because daily dose is mathematically linked to the C0/dose ratio, it was not included in the primary model. However, exploratory sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for daily tacrolimus dose were performed to evaluate whether formulation-specific findings were robust to dose adjustment.

As an additional sensitivity analysis, C0/dose metabolizer category defined at the latest available PK measurement during follow-up was used in place of the time-varying classification to evaluate whether associations were robust to alternative exposure definitions. In this analysis, the final observed C0/dose category for each patient was assigned to all PK measurements from that patient, thereby approximating a more stable patient-level C0/dose phenotype. Models were further adjusted for the CYP3A5 phenotype (expressor vs non-expressor) to evaluate whether the C0/dose ratio remained an independent predictor of tacrolimus exposure, after adjustment for the CYP3A5 genotype. Finally, because hematocrit can influence measured whole-blood tacrolimus concentrations, hematocrit-adjusted sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the main findings. All analyses were performed in R using the following packages: dplyr, lubridate, tidyr, lme4, lmerTest, and broom.mixed. Analyses were performed using available data without imputation. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics Statement

The Medical Ethics Review Committee Leiden Den Haag Delft (METC LDD) reviewed the study protocol and confirmed that it does not fall under the scope of the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). Therefore, obtaining written informed consent was not required (nWMO-D4-2025-005).

Results

Patients and Samples

During follow-up to 13 months post-transplantation, 635 PK profiles within the predefined C0 range of 5.0–6.0 ng/mL were available for the primary restricted-C0 analysis, originating from 534 kidney transplant recipients. This represented 15% of the full cohort, which comprised 4271 PK profiles and is described in Table S1 of the ESM. Table 1 shows that most C0/dose assessments fell into the low C0/dose category. Pharmacokinetic profiles assigned to the fast C0/dose metabolizer category were observed markedly earlier post-transplantation (median 42 days [interquartile range 6–174]) than those assigned to the intermediate (208 [168–344]) or slow categories (222 [166–342]). As illustrated in Fig. 1, the fast C0/dose category predominated in the early post-transplant period, whereas intermediate and slow categories became more frequent later during follow-up.

Under both IR and ER tacrolimus, daily dose requirements were highest in the fast C0/dose category and lowest in the slow category. Despite similar C0 values, Cmax and AUC0–24h were higher in the fast C0/dose category than in the intermediate and slow categories, with the most pronounced differences observed under ER tacrolimus. For example, median Cmax under ER tacrolimus was 23 ng/mL (interquartile range 16–26) in the fast category compared with 9.0 ng/mL (interquartile range 8.4–11) in the slow category. This divergence between C0 and both peak and total exposure was also reflected by higher Cmax/C0 and AUC0–24h/C0 ratios in the fast C0/dose category. In a sensitivity analysis using the C0/dose category defined at the latest available PK assessment, results were comparable to the time-varying classification, with similar trends in Cmax, AUC0–24h, and Cmax/C0 and AUC0–24h/C0 ratios across categories (Table S3 of the ESM).

Multivariable Mixed-Effects Analyses

In multivariable mixed-effects analyses, and using PK assessments in the slow C0/dose category under IR tacrolimus as the reference, those classified in the fast category had a 30% higher Cmax (estimate: 1.30; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.20–1.41; p < 0.001, Table 3). Significant effect modification by tacrolimus formulation was observed. The interaction term indicated an additional 56% increase in Cmax for the fast category under ER tacrolimus (interaction estimate: 1.56; 95% CI 1.22–1.99; p < 0.001), while ER tacrolimus alone was not independently associated with Cmax (estimate: 0.92; 95% CI 0.74–1.13; p = 0.410). Compared with the slow C0/dose category under IR tacrolimus, Cmax was 30% higher in the fast C0/dose category under IR tacrolimus and 86% higher in the fast C0/dose category under ER tacrolimus. For the intermediate C0/dose category, Cmax was 8% higher under IR tacrolimus and 34% higher under ER tacrolimus.

Discussion

Within a predefined C0 concentration window of 5.0–6.0 ng/mL, we observed that both tacrolimus peak concentration and total exposure differed substantially across C0/dose categories. In a setting with comparable pre-dose concentration, a low C0/dose ratio was associated with higher Cmax and AUC0–24h than intermediate or high ratios. These differences were formulation dependent: inclusion of a C0/dose category × formulation interaction significantly improved model fit for both Cmax and AUC0–24h, showing that the relationship between C0/dose category and exposure varies between IR and ER tacrolimus. Under ER tacrolimus, a low C0/dose ratio was associated with 86% higher Cmax and 46% higher AUC0–24h relative to slow metabolizers. Under IR tacrolimus, the corresponding differences were 30% higher for Cmax (estimate: 1.30; 95% CI 1.20–1.41) and 18% for AUC0–24h (estimate: 1.18; 95% CI 1.13–1.23). In a sensitivity analysis using the full cohort of 4271 PK profiles, a similar pattern was observed for Cmax and AUC0–24h relative to C0, based on Cmax/C0 and AUC0–24h/C0.The proportion of low C0/dose ratio categories declined over follow-up, whereas higher ratios became more prevalent, suggesting a decrease in apparent tacrolimus clearance over time after transplantation. These associations were independent of the CYP3A5 phenotype, supporting that the C0/dose ratio captures clinically relevant PK variability not fully explained by the CYP3A5 genotype alone.

The predefined C0/dose categories used in the present study were adopted from the framework proposed by Thölking et al. and have subsequently been applied in multiple studies evaluating the relationship between tacrolimus metabolism rate and transplant outcomes [3]. In the current study, these categories were used as pragmatic clinical thresholds to evaluate whether patients with a low C0/dose ratio have differences in tacrolimus peak concentration and total exposure despite similar C0 levels. Previous studies assigning tacrolimus C0/dose metabolizer phenotypes at the patient level, typically based on one or more C0/dose measurements, showed that reliance on tacrolimus C0 alone incompletely captures clinically relevant exposure heterogeneity. Across multiple cohorts of kidney transplant recipients, fast metabolizers, defined by a low C0/dose ratio (<1.05 ng·mL⁻1·mg⁻1), high dose requirement, high apparent clearance (dose/C0), or CYP3A5*1 carriage, consistently demonstrate inferior renal allograft outcomes. In patients classified as fast metabolizers based on a low C0/dose ratio, higher C2 levels and higher rates of CNI nephrotoxicity were observed despite similar C0 [18]. These fast metabolizers more frequently undergo for-cause biopsies, revealing an increased incidence of CNI nephrotoxicity and BK nephropathy [3]. Importantly, Kuypers et al. [29] further showed that higher tacrolimus dose requirements (hazard ratio [HR] 2.06) and CYP3A5*1 carriage (HR 2.38) independently predicted chronic nephrotoxicity, even though both C0 and AUC0–12 were similar between groups. A low C0/dose ratio has also been associated with increased rejection risk. In a German multicenter analysis, a low C0/dose at month 3 was associated with subsequent rejection and impaired renal function [5]. Similarly, fast metabolizers had lower rejection-free survival, reduced 5-year graft and patient survival, and a more rapid decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate compared with slow metabolizers [7, 19]. Beyond C0/dose ratio classification, individuals in the highest tacrolimus clearance quartile likewise exhibited a significantly higher incidence of biopsy-proven acute rejection in the first 3 months, despite similar early C0 concentrations and comparable time to target C0 [20].

Mechanistically, the adverse outcomes observed in fast metabolizers may reflect both overimmunosuppression and underimmunosuppression. In a Norwegian cohort, higher estimated tacrolimus clearance was independently associated with the development of de novo interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy within the first post-transplant year, despite comparable early protocol biopsy findings [21]. The authors noted that the underlying mechanism could not be distinguished, as high-clearance patients require higher doses to achieve similar C0, resulting in higher Cmax, while rapid elimination increases susceptibility to transient subtherapeutic exposure. This is particularly relevant in the setting of delayed or missed doses, as supported by a PK modeling study showing that a single missed dose can reduce AUC0–12 by up to 70% in high-clearance individuals [22]. Consistent with this PK rationale, in our study, we observed a disproportionately larger difference in Cmax (86% under ER; 30% under IR) than in AUC0–24h (46% under ER; 18% under IR) between fast and slow metabolizers.

The magnitude of the exposure differences observed in our study between ER and IR tacrolimus was notable, as both formulations are generally considered pharmacokinetically comparable. In the head-to-head PK comparison of tacrolimus IR and ER (ASTCOFF) study, ER tacrolimus and IR tacrolimus demonstrated similar Cmax and AUC0–24h profiles, with the main difference being a lower C0 under ER (5.1 vs 6.1 ng/mL) [23]. In our study, however, C0 was constrained within the same predefined window. This restricted C0 design may have amplified formulation-specific differences, particularly because the lower C0 relative to Cmax and AUC observed with ER tacrolimus in ASTCOFF implies that ER profiles selected within the same C0 window may represent a comparatively higher overall exposure. Importantly, in the full-cohort sensitivity analysis without restriction to C0 5.0–6.0 ng/mL, Cmax relative to C0 also remained higher to a similar extent in the low C0/dose category under ER tacrolimus. As shown in Table 2, the median daily dose in the low C0/dose category was modestly higher under ER than IR tacrolimus, whereas the corresponding Cmax/C0 ratio was substantially higher. In exploratory dose-adjusted analyses, the interaction between low C0/dose ratio and ER tacrolimus remained statistically significant for both Cmax and AUC0–24h, while the main effects of the low C0/dose ratio were attenuated, as expected, after adjustment for daily tacrolimus dose. These findings suggest that higher dose requirements contributed to, but did not fully explain, the larger increase in Cmax and AUC0–24h observed under ER tacrolimus compared with IR tacrolimus. The fact that AUC0–24h remained comparable between ER and IR in the ASTCOFF study was somewhat unexpected. One possible explanation is that the ASTCOFF trial included patients 6 years post-transplantation. In our study, a low C0/dose ratio reflecting fast metabolism predominated early after transplantation and progressively declined over time. Consequently, the ASTCOFF population may have consisted primarily of slower or more stable metabolizers, potentially attenuating Cmax–C0 dissociation compared with the first-year post-transplantation setting represented in our study. Given the established link between high peak concentrations, nephrotoxicity, and impaired graft function, the amplified peak exposure observed under ER tacrolimus may be of concern in patients with a low C0/dose phenotype. In contrast, ER MeltDose tacrolimus (LCPT) has been associated with higher C0/dose ratios compared with IR tacrolimus [24], reflecting slower apparent metabolism and improved bioavailability, likely related to enhanced distal intestinal absorption where CYP3A5 expression is lower [10, 25]. Moreover, early conversion to LCPT increased C0/dose ratios and was associated with recovery of renal function in fast metabolizers [26]. Collectively, these findings suggest that LCPT may represent a more favorable formulation in patients with a low C0/dose phenotype, although prospective studies are required to confirm this hypothesis.

Routine collection of tacrolimus AUC profiles for TDM requires additional effort from both patients and healthcare providers. In large transplant programs with hundreds to thousands of patients in long-term follow-up, performing repeated AUC assessments through conventional venous sampling at centralized blood collection facilities may be challenging to integrate into routine clinical workflows. However, home-based sampling using microsampling devices has proven feasible [27], and we have extensive experience training patients to perform self-sampling. These approaches may facilitate broader implementation of periodic AUC assessments, which could ultimately contribute to improved long-term transplant outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, analyses were performed at the PK assessment level using a time-varying C0/dose classification, which is methodologically appropriate for evaluating contemporaneous exposure, but does not establish whether a patient-level C0/dose phenotype is consistently associated with higher Cmax or AUC over time. This is particularly relevant because the distribution of C0/dose categories shifted during follow-up. However, this limitation was partly addressed in a sensitivity analysis using the C0/dose category at the latest available PK assessment as a fixed patient-level exposure definition. This analysis showed comparable trends in Cmax and AUC0–24h across categories. Nevertheless, the optimal definition of a stable patient-level C0/dose metabolizer phenotype, particularly in patients contributing multiple PK measurements over time, remains uncertain and warrants further study. Second, we did not evaluate clinical outcomes, and the present study therefore addresses the PK plausibility of the C0/dose ratio rather than its direct relationship with graft-related endpoints. While multiple studies have demonstrated the clinical relevance of the C0/dose metabolizer phenotype, the present study focused on the underlying differences in tacrolimus exposure and provides novel PK data [28]. Third, Cmax was approximated using the highest measured concentration within the limited sampling schedule and may therefore not fully capture the true Cmax. However, in the subset used for the model-predicted Cmax sensitivity analysis, observed Cmax closely approximated model-predicted Cmax, supporting the robustness of the main Cmax findings. Although agreement was strong and uncertainty was evaluated using bootstrap resampling, only 37 ER tacrolimus profiles were included in this sensitivity analysis. Confirmation of these results in a larger cohort would therefore be valuable. Fourth, tacrolimus AUC0–24h was estimated using MAP Bayesian estimation with covariate-free population PK models, based on sparse sampling rather than full 12-h or 24-h concentration–time profiles. Although additional sampling points or covariate-rich models may further improve precision, the 0-, 2-, and 3-h strategy has become the standardized limited sampling strategy in our routine clinical practice because it provides a practical balance between precision, feasibility, and clinical applicability in a high-throughput AUC monitoring program. Finally, as an observational single-center study, residual confounding cannot be excluded. This includes potential confounding by drug–drug interactions, as concomitant medication data at the time of each PK assessment were not available and could therefore not be adjusted for directly. Nevertheless, at our center, tacrolimus AUC monitoring is generally performed during clinically stable outpatient follow-up. In periods of clinical instability or known major drug–drug interactions, AUC assessment is generally postponed, and tacrolimus dose adjustments are guided by C0 monitoring in the context of prior AUC information. We acknowledge that weaker or less clinically apparent interactions may have occurred and were not systematically captured. However, CYP3A inhibition would be expected to increase tacrolimus exposure while also increasing the apparent C0/dose ratio, thereby potentially shifting high-exposure observations from the fast C0/dose category into intermediate or slow C0/dose categories. If present, such misclassification would be expected to attenuate rather than create the observed association between a fast C0/dose phenotype and higher Cmax/AUC0–24h. The study also has important strengths. It is, to our knowledge, one of the few studies to evaluate both Cmax and AUC in relation to the C0/dose ratio, and it does so in a large longitudinal transplant cohort with repeated PK assessments obtained during routine care. The sample size allowed restriction to a narrow C0 window (5.0–6.0 ng/mL), enabling assessment of whether clinically “similar” pre-dose concentrations can mask differences in Cmax and AUC. In addition, tacrolimus formulation was recorded for every AUC assessment, minimizing exposure misclassification due to formulation switching, and the inclusion of both IR and ER tacrolimus allowed assessment of formulation-dependent differences. Finally, the three-category C0/dose classification enabled a more granular evaluation than the commonly used dichotomous high- versus low-ratio approaches.

Conclusions

In kidney transplant recipients, substantial differences in peak concentration and total exposure persist across C0/dose categories. Low and intermediate C0/dose ratios are characterized by higher Cmax and AUC0–24h despite comparable C0 levels, with the magnitude of these differences being most pronounced for low C0/dose ratios and particularly under ER tacrolimus in the present study. These findings demonstrate that C0 alone incompletely reflects the full PK profile and may miss clinically relevant peak-related toxicity or intermittent underexposure to tacrolimus. By incorporating the simple C0/dose ratio, patients at risk for clinically relevant exposure heterogeneity can be identified. In such patients, TDM beyond C0 monitoring, for example with limited sampling AUC monitoring, should be considered.
