Unique electron transfer system of cytochrome P450 monooxygenase includes a mechanism of fatty acid β-oxidation
Authors: Saito Kojima, Kyosei Shinji, Hana Namiki, Kouta Suzuki, Motoyuki Shimizu, Chihiro Kadooka, Nozomi Katsuki, Shunsuke Masuo, Madoka Amahisa, Yuki Doi, Norio Takeshita, Naoki Takaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytochrome P450 comprises a group of monooxygenases that hydroxylate xenobiotics and natural compounds with diverse electron transfer systems. Here we identify a natural fusion protein of cytochrome (Cyt) b5 and Cyt b5 reductase (CBBR) that transfers electrons from NADH to the cytochrome P450 CYP540A2. This cytochrome P450 system hydroxylates medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs) to generate (R)-β-hydroxy-MCFAs with 7–12 carbon atoms. Kinetic studies of CYP540A2 mutants indicate that side chains of Ser431 and Gln542 residues bind the carboxyl moiety of MCFAs for hydroxylation at their β-carbons. Pre-steady state kinetics also indicate that a predicted linker region between the FAD- and Cyt b5-domains of CBBR modulates electron transfer from NADH to CYP540A2. The present study also identifies a dehydrogenase that oxidizes (R)-β-hydroxy-MCFAs to β-oxo-fatty acids that are substrates in the general β-oxidation mechanism of fatty acid degradation. The genes encoding CBBR, CYP540A2, and (R)-β-hydroxy-MCFA dehydrogenase are clustered in the genome of the fungus Aspergillus nidulans and related fungi. The A. nidulans genes are induced by MCFAs, and disrupting CBBR and CYP540A2 genes accumulated more intracellular decanoic acid. Our findings reveal an adaptive monooxygenase-dependent β-oxidation mechanism that alternates with conventional β-oxidation, thus allowing fungi to metabolize MCFAs.Subject terms: Oxidoreductases, Fungal physiology

Introduction

Live organisms transfer electrons to maintain biological activity using proteins containing redox active flavins, heme, and other cofactors1. Cytochromes are hemoproteins that comprise a cellular electron transfer system, and cytochrome b has various cellular functions that include electron transfer proteins in mitochondrial respiratory complexes and bacterial membranes1,2. The cytochrome b-like domains are associated with the electron transfer domains of L-lactate dehydrogenase, nitrate reductases, and sulfite oxidase1,3. The Cyt b5-dependent electron transfer systems comprise NADH-Cyt b5 reductase, Cyt b5, and have various functions such as synthesizing sterols and unsaturated fatty acids, drug metabolism on cell membranes, and methemoglobin reduction on erythrocytes3,4. Mammalian Ncb5or is an endogenous fusion of Cyt b5 with its reductase; however, their physiological electron acceptor is obscure5.

Cytochrome P450s (P450s) are hemoproteins that detoxify xenobiotics and toxins, and biosynthesize steroids, vitamins, and other compounds6,7. They have attracted attention in terms of pharmaceutical development and understanding cellular homeostasis8. Typical P450s are monooxygenases that hydroxylate substrates by activating dioxygen (O2)9,10. The catalytic cycle requires the transfer of two electrons from NADPH or NADH to heme thiolate and P450-reducing proteins for mediation. When a substrate enters a heme active site during catalysis, a P450-reducing protein delivers one electron to heme to generate an oxyferrous intermediate by binding O2 to the ferrous iron of P450 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The oxyferrous intermediate that receives the second electron and two protons cleaves the O–O bond to generate an oxoferryl intermediate, which is a powerful oxidant that can hydroxylate substrates. Removing reaction products restores ferric heme iron to the resting state.

The P450 redox systems are grouped by P450-reducing proteins. The Class I system transfers electrons through NADPH or NADH ferredoxin reductase and ferredoxin, as found in camphor-hydroxylating Pseudomonas putida P450cam (also known as CYP101A111; Supplementary Fig. 1b). Class II P450s associate with membranous P450 reductase (CPR) comprising FAD- and FMN-domains that transfer electrons to P450s, and represent typical eukaryotic P450s6,8,9. The involvement of Cyt b5 in mammalian P450 reactions has remained controversial4,9. Cytochrome b5 works as an electron donor to P450 and preferably donates a second electron because of the high midpoint electron potential of oxyferrous P450. An allosteric effect of Cyt b5 is a modulated P450 conformation that stimulates its activity, and these functions depend on the P450 system and reactions. Self-sufficient P450s in natural fusion with CPR include P450BM-3 (CYP102) and P450foxy (CYP505A1)12,13. The P450RhF (CYP116B2) is fused with a ferredoxin-like center and its reductase11. Thus, electron transfer mechanisms for P450 are diverse, whereas a natural fusion of Cyt b5 and its reductase that transfers electrons to P450 remains obscure.

Fatty acids are essential cellular components that store carbon as a cellular nutrient and energy source14. Medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs) comprise aliphatic chains of 6–12 carbon atoms, and their lipophilic nature disrupts membrane organization to inhibit microbial growth15. Some P450s hydroxylate MCFAs. Both P450BM-3 and P450foxy are microbial fatty acid monooxygenases and hydroxylate ω-1 to ω-3 carbons of MCFAs12,13. The P450SPα (CYP152B1) from Sphingomonas paucimobilis was the first P450 peroxygenase to hydroxylate fatty acids at the α-position dependently on hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)16. The related bacterial peroxygenases P450Bsβ (CYP152A1) and P450CLA (CYP152A2) hydroxylate α-and β-carbon atoms with a higher preference for the β-position17,18. A natural O2-dependent P450 monooxygenase system that hydroxylates α- and β-carbons remains unknown, whereas P450CLA acquires such activity when exogenous P450-reducing proteins are present18.

Ubiquitous β-oxidation degrades fatty acids to generate nutrients. It activates fatty acids with coenzyme A (CoA), and resulting acyl-CoAs undergo successive desaturation, (S)-hydration, oxidation, and thiolysis to generate acetyl-CoA19. We revealed that the P450 monooxygenase CYP540A2 activates the β-carbon of MCFAs and specific dehydrogenases oxidize the resulting (R)-β-hydroxy-MCFAs to become CoA transferase substrates in the β-oxidation mechanism. This reaction is an alternative pathway of fatty acid β-oxidation, and it is characterized by being activated before fatty acids are esterified with CoA and by (R)-specific reactions. The natural fusion of Cyt b5 and its reductase (CBBR) transfers electrons to P450, and a unique linker region between the FAD and Cyt b5 domains of CBBR regulates the electron transfer mechanism. Biochemical and genetic analyses presented here proposed that this mechanism in the fungus Aspergillus nidulans degrades MCFAs, thereby contributing to its saprobic growth.

Results

Identifying CYP540A2 and CBBR

The A. nidulans genome predicts that 111 genes encode P45020, the natural substrates of which are mostly unknown. The present study focused on the CYP540A2 gene (Gene ID: AN8919.4) that is located next to a gene encoding FAD-binding protein (AN8920.4), which is its presumed redox partner20, on chromosome VII (Fig. 1a). Their predicted amino acid sequences showed that CYP540A2 and P450cam of P. putida (P00183 in UniProt database) are similar with an amino acid identity of 15% and conserve consensus amino acid sequences FXXGXXXCXG for the heme-binding region, and Thr363 in the I-helix21 (Supplementary Fig. 2). AlphaFold222 predicts overlapping CYP540A2 and P450cam tertiary structures, the heme axial ligand Cys, and helixes (Supplementary Fig. 3). Database searches could not predict a function of the N-terminal 79 amino acids that fold to form a domain that is absent in P450cam and other P450s.

AN8920.4 is organized into N-terminal Cyt b5 (29.2% amino acid identity with CYB5/YNL111C from Saccharomyces cerevisiae), and the C-terminal NADH-Cyt b5 reductase (27.8% to MCR1/YKL150W from S. cerevisiae)-domains. The natural fusion of Cyt b5 and its CBBR (Fig. 1a) is unique except for mammalian microsomal Ncb5or5, whereas electron transfer to P450 remains obscure. The A. nidulans CBBR connects the two domains by a linker region with two α-helical structures and a low confidence score; it is dissimilar to the corresponding hinge region in Ncb5or related to the Cysteine and Histidine-rich domain (CHORD)-Suppressor of G2 allele of Skp1 (SGT1)23. Predictions indicate that none of these CBBRs and P450s possess N-terminal signal peptides or hydrophobic transmembrane residues, suggesting that CBBR shares physiological functions with and transfers electrons to P450 in the cytosol.

Electron transfer from NADH to CYP540A2 by CBBR

Recombinant CYP540A2 and CBBR generated in Escherichia coli were soluble and purified to apparent homogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The CYP540A2 preparation had visible light absorption spectra for typical oxidized and reduced P450 (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 4b). The absorption peak of the reduced-carbon monoxide (CO) complex was 444 nm, indicating that it was a P450. Absorption peaks at 424, 527, and 557 nm of dithionite-reduced CBBR were typical for Cyt b5 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4b). The absorption of reduced CBBR was decreased at 450–480 nm, which agrees with FAD being located in the BR domain. Steady-state kinetics of NADH-dependent reduction of 2,6-dichloroindophenol indicated a catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) of 0.61 μM−1 s−1. This was two orders of magnitude larger than that dependent on NADPH (Supplementary Fig. 5), implying that NADH is a physiological electron or hydride donor of CBBR.

Generation of the reduced-CO complex with an absorption peak at 444 nm indicated that CBBR reduced CYP540A2 in the presence of saturated NADH and CO (Fig. 1d) with a catalytic efficiency of 0.024 μM−1 s−1 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Cytochrome c and methemoglobin were also reduced by CBBR, but the physiological roles of these reactions await further investigation. Purification of CYP540A2 from E. coli cells that produce both histidine-tagged CYP540A2 and CBBR resulted in CBBR being co-purified with CYP540A2 at a ratio of 8.8% ± 2.1% of CYP540A2 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). These results indicated that CBBR is an NADH: CYP540A2 oxidoreductase that forms a complex with CYP540A2. AlphaFold3 predicted that the structure of this complex faces the proximal side of CYP540A2 and propionyl moieties of heme in the Cyt b5 domain (Fig. 1e), which agrees with an intermolecular electron transfer model between Cyt b5 and P45024 (Fig. 2a). Electrostatic interaction was identified between a positively charged surface in a groove at the heme proximal region of CYP540A2 and a negative surface on Cyt b5 in CBBR (Fig. 2b). The energy of interface binding calculated by PDBePISA25 was ΔG = −24.8 kcal mol−1, and 31 salt bridges were identified between the amino-acid side chains on the protein interfaces. They comprise side chains of Arg179/Lys183 (P450) vs. Glu63 (CBBR), Lys192 vs. Asp35, Arg195 vs. Glu34, and Arg491 vs. Glu57 (Fig. 2c), and conserve binding energy between the CYP540A2 and Cyt b5 at their interface. Electrostatic interaction through surface amino acids has also been found between P450cam and its electron donor ferredoxin26. Predictions indicate that Lys 30 and Arg36 in the unique N-terminal domain of CYP540A2 bind Glu83 in the CBBR Cyt b5 domain (Fig. 2d), suggesting a role of the N-terminal domain in protein-protein interaction. The C-terminal loop of the CBBR linker region resides along with F- and G-helixes of CYP540A2, and the Asp194, Glu198, and R205 side chains salt-bridge with K271/Arg275, Arg289, and Asp252/Glu220 of CYP540A2 to stabilize both helixes (Fig. 2e). Analysis using CAVER27 showed that the salt bridges minimize the volume of the catalytic pocket in the CYP540A2 distal region from 2250 to 683 Å3 (Fig. 2f). A structural comparison with the CYP540A2 monomer showed that CBBR bound in the complex moves the F-and G-helixes towards a more “closed” state, narrows the substrate entry channel. This affords tighter or specific substrate binding like P450cam28, and indicates that the C-terminal loop of the CBBR linker regulates substrate entry and binding in CYP540A2. Therefore, this natural CBBR transfers electrons to P450 and moderates its substrate binding properties.

CYP540A2 is a medium-chain fatty acid β-hydroxylase

We screened the substrates of co-purified CYP540A2 and CBBR. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) of the reaction containing decanoic acid (DA) and the NADH-regeneration system detected a peak (Fig. 3a), which MS identified as β-hydroxydecanoic acid (βHDA) (Supplementary Fig. 9). A prolonged reaction generated some γ-, but scant α-hydroxydecanoic acid. The absolute configuration of the produced βHDA was the (R)-form with 98% optical purity (Fig. 3b). This indicated that the reaction is stereospecific. The CYP540A2 and CBBR reaction hydroxylated saturated fatty acids with chains of 7–16 carbon atoms, and a few with 14 and 16 (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). Trans-2-decenoic acid and trans-3-decenoic acid had very little activity (< 0.001 mmol min−1 mmol P450−1). Thus, CYP540A2 is a monooxygenase that hydroxylates at β-carbon atoms of MCFAs (Fig. 3d). The catalytic efficiency of the steady-state reaction under (kcat/Km) was 6.0 × 10−5 μM−1 s−1 against DA, and 3.7 × 10−5 μM−1 s−1 against nonanoic acid. (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 8c). Recombinant CPR of A. nidulans (AN0595.4) that lacks the N-terminal transmembrane region was prepared to homogeneity using SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 7e). It produces high levels of NADPH:cytochrome c reductase activity (19.2 μmol min−1 mg−1) like those of S. cerevisiae CPR (24.3 μmol min−1 mg−1)29. Replacing CBBR and NADH with CPR and NADPH in the CYP540A2-dependent DA hydroxylation reaction decreased the ratio of βHDA:hydroxylated DA production to 5.5% (0.011 mmol min−1 mmol P450−1; Fig. 3a), indicating that CBBR specifically donates electrons to CYP540A2 for the monooxygenase reaction. Only H2O2-dependent P450 peroxygenases catalyze hydroxylation at the β-carbons of fatty acids16–18. Both CYP540A2 and its endogenous electron donor (CBBR) comprise a monooxygenase system that hydroxylates the β-carbon of fatty acids, whereas P450CLA has such activity in the presence of artificial electron transfer proteins18.

Phylogenetic analysis of P450s revealed that CYP540A2 and CYP505A8 (AN6835.4) are related in A. nidulans (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Cytochrome P505A8 belongs to the CYP505 family20. It is related to fatty acid ω-1 to ω-3 hydroxylase P450foxy13, and CYP505B1 (Fum6p) from the plant pathogenic fungus that hydroxylates at the C-14 and C-15 carbons of aliphatic intermediates in fumonisin biosynthesis30. CYP540A2 resides in the same branch in the phylogenetic tree of human P450s that hydroxylate fatty acids and eicosanoids (Supplementary Fig. 10b). These phylogenetic relationships agree with the fatty acid monooxygenase activity of CYP540A2 and the P450s.

Fatty acid recognition by CYP540A2

The P450SPα and P450BSβ peroxygenases, respectively, hydroxylate the α-carbon of fatty acids exclusively16 and the α- and β-carbons17. Amino acid sequence alignment indicated that CYP540A2 lacks the essential Arg242 residue for substrate binding (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting a mechanism of regiospecific hydroxylation by CYP540A2. The structure of CYP540A2 and the DA complex predicted side chains of Ser431 and Gln542 in conserved substrate-recognition sites 5 and 6 among P450s31 within hydrogen-bond distance to the carboxylate oxygens of DA (Fig. 3f). A mutation at Ser431 to Ala or Thr decreased kcat and kcat/Km values for one-order magnitudes for DA hydroxylation (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 11a). Mutations at Gln542 similarly affected kcat and kcat/Km values, whereas the Q542E mutation decreased the kcat/Km 7.1-fold. These results indicated that both residues participate in CYP540A2 catalysis.

Adding DA induced a type I spectral change32 to CYP540A2 with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 64 ± 13 μM (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Neither Ser431Ala (28 ± 5 μM) nor Gln542Ala (33 ± 4 μM) mutations substantially altered the Kd (Supplementary Fig. 12b) or the stereoselectivity of hydroxylation (Fig. 3e). Therefore, these residues are not essential for initial binding the substrate during catalytic turnover. Considering the mutations affecting the catalytic rate constants of DA hydroxylation (Fig. 3e), Ser431 and Gln542 rather stabilize transition state intermediates in enzyme-substrate complexes (Fig. 3f, g). Their amino acid side chains could mediate such transition-state stabilization through regulating the binding orientation by facing the substrate β-carbon atom to the oxoferryl intermediate for efficient oxidation. AlphaFold3 predicted that the mutations move the β-carbon of DA away from catalytic heme iron (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). This was accompanied by fewer changes in the geometry of the C5–C10 carbon atoms of DA in the hydrophobic tunnel where the substrate can access the catalytic center (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Although other MCFAs with low Kd values such as DA might bind through this tunnel (Supplementary Fig. 12c), they have no primary role in determining the substrate specificity of the CYP540A2 reaction (Fig. 3c).

Intra-CBBR and inter-CBBR electron transfer

We constructed CBBR without the linker region (CBBRΔL), as well as mutant proteins of the Cyt b5 (CB) and its reductase (BR) domains (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7b–d) to determine the electron transfer properties of fused Cyt b5 and its reductase. A mixture of CB and BR (CB + BR) reduced CYP540A2 to 4.1 ± 0.3 mol min−1 mol−1, which was 3.2-fold lower than the fused CBBR at steady state in the presence of NADH (Fig. 4a). This value exceeded that of the non-intrinsic electron transfer from BR to CYP540A2, indicating that CB reduces CYP540A2 more efficiently by fusion with BR. Under these conditions, CBBRΔL reduced CYP540A2 at a 6.8-fold higher rate of 89 ± 15 mol min−1 mol−1 than wild-type CBBR (Fig. 4a). Reduced rates of other electron acceptors were also higher in CBBRΔL (Supplementary Fig. 6). Wild-type CBBR and CBBRΔL were coupled to the same extent of NADH oxidation and CYP540A2 reduction (3.3 vs. 3.1; Fig. 4a). Therefore, the linker region delays the electron transfer of NADH to CYP540A2 with minimal impact on the reaction of the reduced FAD and Cyt b5 with O2.

Single-turnover kinetics investigated using stopped-flow rapid-scan spectrometry indicated that the pseudo-first order rate constant for FAD reduction (k1) in CBBR by NADH was 5.4 ± 0.2 s−1, and lower than that in CBBRΔL, where 68% of FAD was rapidly reduced within the dead-time of the apparatus (~3 ms) (Fig. 4b–d). Subsequent reduction of CB by FADH2 in CBBR proceeded at k2 = 5.0 ± 0.2 s−1, which was comparable to the k1 value for FAD reduction in CBBR. The k2 value of CBBRΔL was 2.4-fold higher than CBBR, indicating that the linker region decreased rates of hydride/electron transfer of NADH to FAD and CB (Fig. 4d). The rate for the CYP540A2 reduction via reduced CBBR (k3) was 9.1 ± 0.4 × 10−3 s−1 (Fig. 4b–d), which resembled the kinetic constant (kcat) for steady-state DA hydroxylation (Fig. 3e). These were lower than the k1 and k2 values, indicating that the intramolecular transfer of the first electron (k3) limits the NADH-dependent hydroxylation by CYP540A2. The k3 values for transferring the first electron to P450 by CBBR and CBBRΔL were notably comparable (Fig. 4d), whereas CBBRΔL assisted CYP540A2 generated little hydroxylation product (<1.0 × 10−5s−1) in the steady-state reaction. This implied that the linker region is required for the catalytic steps after the first electron is transferred to CYP540A2, presumably for the second electron transfer from CB to CYP540A2.

CYP540A2 constitutes an inducible mechanism of MCFA oxidation

Our finding that the CYP540A2 and CBBR system β-hydroxylates MCFAs indicated that it functions in the fungal fatty acid metabolism. The AN8921.4 gene located near CYP540A2 and CBBR (Fig. 1a) encoded a predicted protein in the FadB superfamily, including N-terminal 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase and C-terminal low-density lipoprotein-receptor YWTD domains (Fig. 5a). Recombinant AN8921.4 protein acted as an NAD+-dehydrogenase of (R)-βHDA (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). This reaction was stereoselective and converted (R)-βHDA to β-oxodecanoic acid, but little (S)-βHDA (<0.01 µmol min−1 mg−1) (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e). The Km, kcat, and kcat/Km values of the reaction were 60 ± 3 µM, 12 ± 0 s−1, and 0.20 ± 0.01 µM−1 s−1, respectively. The catalytic efficiency was between the ranges of known hydroxy fatty acid and hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenases (0.005–13 µM−1 s−1)33–35. The protein oxidized β-hydroxy fatty acids with 8–14 carbon atoms (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e), indicating that AN8921.4 encodes β-hydroxy-MCFA dehydrogenase (βHDH).

The reconstituted reactions of CBBR, CYP540A2, and βHDH convert MCFAs to β-oxo-MCFAs, which are substrates of the ubiquitous 3-ketoacid coenzyme A transferase that generates β-oxo-acyl-CoA, substrates of the β-oxidation mechanism. Thus, the successive reactions activate and degrade MCFAs. Quantitative PCR showed that MCFAs added to the cultures upregulated the expression of A. nidulans genes encoding CYP540A2, CBBR, and the βHDH 1.5–46-fold (Fig. 5c). Among the predicted fungal 3-ketoacid CoA transferases, MCFAs induced the predicted cytosolic isoform (AN9394.3) the most effectively (Fig. 5c). Disruption of the genes encoding CYP540A2 and CBBR in A. nidulans TN02A3 resulted in 1.5- and 4.9-fold accumulation of intracellular DA compared to the host strain (TN02A3, Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 14a, b), indicating that CYP540A2 and CBBR lower cellular DA, which presents as a metabolic intermediate of fatty acid synthesis and degradation. These results indicated that CYP540A2, CBBR, βHDH, along with the 3-ketoacid CoA transferases, constitute a β-oxidation mechanism of MCFAs (Fig. 5e) that bypasses the conventional β-oxidation mechanism (genes listed in Supplementary Table 2). The mechanism allows A. nidulans to adapt to MCFAs because exogenous DA added to the TN02A3 culture increased cellular DA levels 33-fold, and gene disruptants further elevated these levels another 5.0- and 22-fold. Besides being nutrients, high concentrations of MCFAs are toxic to microbial cells36. We found that MCFAs with 8–10 carbon atoms impaired A. nidulans growth when glucose was the carbon source, and their β-hydroxy derivatives also did so to a lesser degree (Fig. 5f). This suggested that the mechanism tolerates toxic MCFAs. However, this conclusion must await further studies because the growth rates between gene disruptants the TN02A3 strain did not differ very much (Supplementary Fig. 14c), probably due to metabolic compensation by the conventional β-oxidation mechanism and/or their isozymes with redundant functions tolerated by MCFAs.

Distribution of the CBBR-dependent P450 redox system

Searching the NCBI database for gene clusters organized by the CYP540A2, CBBR, and βHDH orthologs of A. nidulans (Fig. 1a) using cblaster37 detected 714 clusters in genomes across 662 strains, which exclusively belong to filamentous fungi in the phylum Ascomycota (Supplementary Table 3). The strains comprise 133, 38, 9, and 1 species that belong to the classes Eurotiomycetes, Dothideomycetes, Sordariomycetes, and Lecanoromycetes, respectively. Among them, 167 strains harbored orthologs of CYP540A2, CBBR, and βHDH, and 115 strains harbored additional AN8921.4-like transcription factors to them. The search found that the second gene cluster in the A. nidulans genome comprised paralogous genes encoding CBBR (AN3862.4), P450 (AN10479.4, CYP540B4), βHDH (AN10471.4) and the AN8918.4-like transcription factor (Supplementary Fig. 15). Gene clusters were numerous in the genomes of 40 of the 167 strains, and in 16 Aspergillus, 5 Penicillium, 3 Sordaria, and 1 Pseudoneurospora species. Supplementary Fig. 15 shows the phylogeny of the fungal strains and the organization of gene clusters. Most fungal genomes adopt the CBBR and the P450 genes in head-to-head organization, suggesting evolutional conservation of the clusters in Ascomycota fungi. These results support that CBBR- and P450-mediated electron transfer systems and their function in β-oxidation mechanisms are widely distributed among these fungi.

Discussion

This study identified a P450 monooxygenase and a unique electron transfer protein comprising Cyt b5 fused with Cyt b5 reductase that functions an MCFA β-hydroxylase. Among the diverse proteins that transfer electrons to P450, P450 reductase reduces P450, while Cyt b5 and ferredoxin-like protein with respective reductases comprise a two-component system to reduce P450. This finding expands the diversity of P450 electron transfer systems. The solubility of CYP540A2 and CBBR has led to more investigations into the P450 system in membranes, and provides an excellent model with which to understand the biochemistry and thermodynamics of electron transfer from BR to Cyt b5, and hence P450.

The electron transfer properties of CBBR and Ncb5or, which have comprised the only known electron transfer proteins of Cyt b5-Cyt b5 reductase fusion, were comparable. Splitting the Cyt b5 and its reductase domains results in lost Cyt b5 reductase activity of Ncb5or5,38, whereas this effect was limited in CBBR (Fig. 4). This suggested that interactions between Cyt b5 and Cyt b5 reductase differ where their domains interface. The linker region in CBBR delayed electron transfer from NADH to FAD and Cyt b5, unlike Ncb5or, of which the corresponding hinge region connects those of Cyt b5, and its reductase increases the NADH-dependent reduction activity of the Cyt b5 domain38. This indicated that these regions and their amino acid sequences are functionally diverse. Comparing the intramolecular electron transfer properties, both CBBR and Ncb5or share cytochrome c as an electron acceptor with a similar kcat of 7.4 s−1 for reduction by CBBR (Supplementary Fig. 6f) and 8.0 s−1 for that by Ncb5or39, and P450 is a unique physiological electron donor to CBBR. That CBBR uses only NADH in the physiological range as an electron donor (Supplementary Fig. 5) is consistent with its cytosolic function, unlike Ncb5or that uses both NADH and NADPH39.

Our kinetic analysis of CBBR without the linker region indicated that CBBR modulates the catalytic cycle after transfer of the first electron (Fig. 4). This is notable because Cyt b5 affects the rate of the second electron from Cyt b5 to oxyferrous P450 in conventional P450 systems4. Allosteric stimulation of P450 by Cyt b5 might explain this phenomenon, as proposed in the mammalian P450s40. However, the precise mechanism remains elusive. Our structural prediction indicated that bound CBBR modulates the spatial configuration of the substrate binding site of CYP540A2, particularly through the linker region and its binding to the distal site of P450 (Fig. 2). This is consistent with our finding that the linker region is essential for CYP540A2 to produce the hydroxylated products. It is also consistent with the notion that the linker region functions as an allosteric regulator that positively modulates substrate hydroxylation following the transfer of the first and second electrons, in addition to its function as a mere electron transfer protein in CYP540A2 catalysis.

To date, the only known enzymes that hydroxylate fatty acids at the β-position are P450s in the CYP152 family and H2O2-dependent peroxygenases. Our finding of CYP540A2 provides mechanistic insights into β-hydroxylation by P450 monooxygenases. We showed that Ser431 and Gln542 in the heme distal region bind substrate carboxyl residues and orient their β-carbon atoms towards the appropriate position for hydroxylation (Fig. 3f). The crucial Arg241 of CYP152 peroxygenases also binds substrate carboxylates to activate heme-bound H2O2 to generate a reactive intermediate41, via a mechanism that is not involved in the monooxygenase reactions of P450s. A predicted hydrophobic tunnel stabilized substrate alkyl chains in CYP540A2 (Supplementary Fig. 11d). The CYP152 peroxygenases and fatty acid subterminal hydroxylases P450BM-3 (CYP102) use similar hydrophobic binding mechanisms of alkyl chains42,43, indicating that this fatty acid binding mechanism is conserved beyond the P450 family.

We consider that the unique fungal MCFA-degrading mechanism described herein is a partial bypass of the ubiquitous β-oxidation mechanism (Fig. 5e) that is characterized by the following features. The P450 monooxygenase generates a powerful oxoferryl oxidant that activates the MCFA β-carbon to transfer oxygen atoms in addition to the conventional β-oxidation mechanism that generates β-hydroxy fatty acids via multiple enoylation and hydration steps19. The hydroxylation of free fatty acids by CYP540A2 is distinct from the conventional β-oxidation that mediates the generation of acyl-CoA thioesters. This in turn increases the electron-withdrawing properties of the carbonyl group, the electrophilicity of the α-carbon, and hence fatty acid reactivity. Both CYP540A2 and βHDH utilize (R)-β-hydroxy fatty acids as a product and substrate, which is distinct from the typical (S)-isoform-utilizing β-oxidation mechanism (Fig. 5e). Achiral β-oxo-fatty acids generated by βHDH converge the two metabolic pathways. The CYP540A2 mechanism of MCFA tolerance is consistent with the general concept that P450s are detoxifying enzymes. Another gene cluster containing the CYP540A2 isozyme (CYP540B2, Supplementary Fig. 15) could provide redundant function with this gene cluster, since the gene disruption did not affect fungal growth tolerance to DA. We presume that it is a cytosolic mechanism because the amino acid sequences did not predict any obvious targeting signals to organelles. It also contrasts with the fungal β-oxidation mechanism in mitochondria and peroxisomes. The reconstituted reactions described herein are significant as specific alternatives for the fungus to activate free fatty acids for metabolism by β-oxidation.

MCFAs are emerging as materials for producing sustainable and valuable green chemicals and fuels. The range of β-hydroxy-derivative applications in surfactant, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical production is wide44–46, and they are also potential biomass-derived monomers of bacterial plastic polyhydroxyalkanoate47,48. The β-hydroxylation mechanism revealed herein provides a tool for the simple chiral hydroxylation of free MCFAs while avoiding thioesterification with CoA, which is currently required to produce them via synthetic biochemistry. The soluble nature of the enzymes is also a benefit compared with conventional membranous P450-dependent hydroxylation that can often be a disadvantage to heterologous production.

Methods

Materials and culture media

A. nidulans FGSC A4 and Aspergillus fumigatus AF293 (Fungal Genetic Stock Center, Manhattan, KS, USA) were cultured in M medium (10 g L−1 glucose, 6 g L−1 NaNO3, 7 mM KCl, 2 mM MgSO4, 10 mM potassium phosphate-NaOH (pH 6.5), 0.4 mg L−1 biotin, and Hutner’s trace elements49). The medium for the TN02A3 strain also contained 0.05 mg L−1 pyridoxine, 1.22 g L−1 uracil + 1.21 g L−1 uridine50. E. coli NEB turbo (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), BL21 Star (DE3), and BL21-CodonPlus(DE3)-RIPL strains (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were cultured in Luria Bertani (LB) medium, 10 g L−1 tryptone, 5 g L−1 yeast extract, and 10 g L−1 NaCl. and Terrific broth (TB, 12 g L−1 tryptone, 24 g L−1 yeast extract, 13 g L−1 K2HPO4, 2.3 g L−1 KH2PO4, and 5 g L−1 glycerol). The following were obtained from the respective suppliers: isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and 2,6-dichloroindophenol (DCIP; Wako Pure Chemicals Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan), methemoglobin (human), and cytochrome c (horse heart) (Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA), NADH and NADPH (Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan), (S)-βHDA and (R)-βHDA (Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc., Alabaster, AL, USA).

Preparation of recombinant CYP540A2, CBBR, CPR, and βHDH

Complementary DNA from the CYP540A2 (AN8919.4) was amplified by PCR using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 1 and cDNA from the A. nidulans FGSC A4 strain. Resulting DNA fragments were digested with NdeI and HindIII, cloned into pET21a using Ligation High ver. 2 (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) to generate pET21a-CYP540A2. Complementary DNA of the CBBR (AN8920.4) and βHDH (AN8921.4) genes synthesized after codon-optimization for E. coli were amplified by PCR using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 1. Thereafter, they were cloned into pET28a TIR-251 digested with NdeI and XhoI or HindIII using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) to yield pET28a-CBBR and pET28a-βHDH. Complementary DNA from the N-terminal truncated CPR (AN0595.4) was amplified by PCR using the primers (Supplementary Table 1). Thereafter, it was cloned into pET28a TIR-2 digested with NheI and HindIII by the same method to yield pET28a-CPR.

Escherichia coli BL21-CodonPlus(DE3)-RIPL harboring pET21a-CYP540A2 was cultured in 5 mL of LB at 37 °C overnight. Portions (1 mL) of cultures were agitated in 100 mL of TB at 30 °C at 90 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm reached 1.0. Both media contained 100 mg L−1 ampicillin and 30 mg L−1 chloramphenicol. Thereafter, the cells were incubated with 1 mM IPTG and 0.5 mM 5-aminolevulinic acid (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan) at 20 °C and 90 rpm for 24 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation, suspended in 10 mL buffer A (50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4), 200 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM dithiothreitol, 20 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol) and sonicated. The soluble fractions were separated by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min, then proteins were purified using HisTrap FF columns (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Recombinant CYP540A2 bound to columns were washed with 10-column volumes of buffer A, then eluted with buffer A containing 300 mM imidazole and concentrated with an excess of buffer B (50 mM MOPS-NaOH, pH 7.4, 10% glycerol) using Amicon Ultra-0.5 mL filters with a 10 kDa cutoff (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and stored at −80 °C. Both CYP540A2 and its mutants were quantified by measuring the absorption of the CO-bound reduced form at 444 nm using an extinction coefficient of ε = 91 mM−1 cm−152.

Escherichia coli BL21 Star (DE3) harboring pET28a-CBBR, pET28a-CPR, and pET28a-βHDH were cultured essentially as described above, but antibiotics were replaced with 50 mg L−1 kanamycin sulfate, and 5-aminolevulinic acid was not added when the optical density at 600 nm reached 1.0 and 0.6. Recombinant CBBR, CPR, and βHDH were purified as described above. We quantified CBBR as absorption of the reduced dithionite form at 557 nm and the oxidized ferricyanide form at 454 nm using an extinction coefficient of ε = 21.0 mM−1 cm−1 52. We quantified CPR and βHDH using Protein Assay Dye Reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA).

Reduction rates of CYP540A2 and other electron acceptors by CBBR and CPR

We measured CYP540A2 reduction in reaction mixtures (100 μL) of 50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4), 0.2 mM NADH, 1 μM FAD, 10 units mL−1 superoxide dismutase (SOD; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 10 units mL−1 catalase (CAT; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan), 10% glycerol, ~20 μM CYP540A2, and 100 nM CBBR, in saturated CO. The absorbance at 444 nm was monitored at 25 °C using a U3900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The extinction coefficient of the reduced CO-bound CYP540A2 was ε = 91 mM−1 cm−1 53. The initial velocity of reduction was determined in triplicate and fitted to the Michaelis–Menten equation according to nonlinear regression to calculate apparent Km and kcat values. Coupling ratios represent the initial velocity of NADH consumption relative to CYP540A2 reduction.

Reduction rates of DCIP, cytochrome c, and methemoglobin by CBBR and its mutants were determined using the same reaction as that for CYP540A2 reduction that contained ~100 μM electron acceptor, 30 nM CBBR, but not CO. We measured Cyt c reduction by CPR in reaction mixtures containing 50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4), 0.2 mM NADPH, 1 µM FAD, 1 µM FMN, 10 units mL−1 SOD, 10 units mL−1 CAT, 10% glycerol, and 90 nM CPR at 25 °C. The extinction coefficients (ε) were oxidized DCIP, hemoglobin, and reduced Cyt c were 19.0, 53.2, and 23.6 mM−1 cm−1 at 600, 540 and 550 nm, respectively.

Co-production of CYP540A2 and CBBR complex

The plasmid pET28a-CBBR was digested with NdeI and XhoI, then DNA fragments encoding the CBBR gene were ligated to pRSFDuet-1 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) that was digested beforehand with the same restriction enzymes to obtain pRSF-CBBR to generate CBBR without a His-tag. Escherichia coli BL21-CodonPlus(DE3)-RIPL was transformed with pET21a-CYP540A2 and pRSF-CBBR. Thereafter, the transformants were cultured, and proteins were purified as described for CYP540A2.

Fatty acid β-hydroxylation by CYP540A2 and CBBR

Reaction mixtures containing 50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4), 0.5 mM NADH, 0.6 μM CBBR, 10% glycerol, 1 μM CYP540A2, 1 μM FAD, 10 units mL−1 SOD, 10 units mL−1 CAT, 0.1 M D-glucose, and 10 units mL−1 NAD(P)+: glucose 1-dehydrogenase (Wako Pure Chemicals) were incubated with various concentrations of fatty acids in 50 mM dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) at 30 °C for 0–30 min. Reactions of CPR and CYP540A2 were measured by replacing NADH and CBBR with NADPH and CPR, and by adding 1 µM FMN. Unsealed tubes were shaken at 180 rpm under aerobic conditions. Reactions were acidified with 2 M HCl, added to nonanoic acid as internal standards, extracted with diethyl ether twice, and evaporated to dryness. The residues were suspended in trimethylchlorosilane (Sigma-Aldrich Corp.) containing N,O- bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide 1% (v/v) and analyzed by GC-MS. The initial velocity of reduction was determined by quantifying time-dependent changes in the reaction products (n = 3) and fitted to the Michaelis−Menten equation according to the nonlinear regression to calculate apparent Km and kcat values.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

Fatty acids and their derivatives were quantified in electron impact mode at 70 eV using a QP2010 GC-MS (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a ZB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm; thickness, 0.25 μm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The oven temperature was programmed to ramp from 60 °C to 200 °C for enzyme reactions or 300 °C for cellular fatty acids at 7 °C min−1, with an injection temperature of 250 °C.

Determination of dissociation constants

The absorption spectra of CYP540A2 (3 μM) in 50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4) and 10% glycerol were determined at 25 °C using a U3900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan) and titrated with fatty acid concentrates. Peak height differences between peak (395 nm) and trough (425 nm) of difference spectra (ΔA) were plotted against the concentrations of added compounds (L). The Kd constant was calculated after fitting values to the quadratic equation: ΔA = (Bmax/2[E]){(Kd + [E] + [L]) - {(Kd + [E] + [L])2 – 4[E][L]}1/2}, where Bmax = maximum absorption change.

Absolute conformation of βHDA

The absolute conformation of the reaction products was determined after converting their β-methoxy-(S)-phenylethylamide derivatives as described in ref. 54. The mass spectra of derivatives and commercial racemic- and (S)-β-hydroxy-DA were analyzed by GC-MS as described above except the oven temperature increased from 70 °C to 260 °C at 10 °C min−1, with an injection temperature of 270 °C.

Preparation of CYP540A2 and CBBR mutants

We constructed expression vectors to produce CYP540A2 mutants. Vectors with a mutation in a cloned gene were amplified by PCR using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 1 and pET21a-CYP540A2 as the template. The resulting DNA fragments were digested with DpnI, and incubated with T4 polynucleotide kinase (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan) and Ligation high Ver. 2 (Toyobo) at 16 °C to generate expression vectors. We generated CBBR mutants by amplifying DNA fragments encoding the Cyt b5 domain (CB) and Cyt b5 reductase domain (BR) of CBBR, and CBBR that lack linker regions (CBBRΔL) by PCR using primers shown in Supplementary Table 1. and pET28a-CBBR as the template. The mutants were cloned into pET28a51 modified by digestion with NdeI and XhoI using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England BioLabs) to yield pET28a-CB, pET28a-BR, and pET28a-CBBRΔL. These plasmids were introduced into E. coli strains and mutants were generated as described above for wild-type proteins. The CB domain and CBBRΔL were determined as absorption of the reduced dithionite form at 557 nm (ε = 21 mM−1 cm−1). The BR domain was quantified by measuring FAD absorption at 450 nm using extinction coefficients of 11.3 mM−1 cm−1 55.

Pre-steady state reaction

A stopped-flow rapid-scan spectrophotometer with an RSP-2000 photodiode-array detector (Unisoku, Osaka, Japan) was conditioned three to five times with nitrogen gas-purged solution A (50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4), 10% glycerol, 2 mM D-glucose, glucose oxidase, SOD, and CAT 10 units mL−1 each). We then flushed nitrogen gas into the reservoirs to replace the headspace air.

Reduction of CBBR by NADH. We rapidly mixed NADH (2 μM) and CBBR or CBBRΔL (4 μM) in solution A containing 4 μM FAD, then 5000 absorption spectra were acquired at 25 °C for 5 s. Absorption changes at 436 nm (isosbestic point of reduced vs. oxidized CB) were fitted to single exponential functions to determine rate constants for FAD (k1) reduction. The rate for CB reduction (k2) was calculated based on absorption at 557 nm (isosbestic point of reduced vs. oxidized BR). Mutant CB and BR were measured in solution A.

Reduction of CYP540A2 by CBBR. We rapidly mixed reduced CBBR or CBBRΔL (1 μM) and CYP540A2 (4 μM) saturated in advance with CO in solution A containing 1 μM FAD, then 200 absorption spectra were collected at 25 °C for 400 s. Absorption changes at 438 nm (isosbestic point of the reduced vs oxidized CBBR) were fitted to single exponential functions to determine apparent rate constants for P450 reduction (k3). Reduced CBBR and CBBRΔL were prepared by adding 1.0 μM NADH under anaerobic conditions.

βHDH assay

Enzyme activity was measured in 100-μL volumes containing 50 mM MOPS-NaOH (pH 7.4), 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM NAD+, β-hydroxy fatty acid in ethanol (Wako Pure Chemicals Co. Ltd.), and 10 μg mL−1 purified βHDH. The reaction was started by adding NAD+, then absorbance at 340 nm was monitored at 25 °C using the U3900 spectrophotometer. The extinction coefficient of NADH was ε = 6.3 mM−1 cm−1. The initial velocity of NADH formation was fitted to the Michaelis−Menten equation to calculate apparent Km and kcat values. For substrate inhibition kinetics for β-hydroxydodecanoic acid, data were fitted to the equation v/e = kcat [S]/(Km + [S](1 + [S]/Ks). To determine the reaction product, the reaction was treated with 14% boron trifluoride-methanol (GL Science Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide with 1% (v/v) trimethylchlorosilane (Sigma-Aldrich Corp.), and analyzed by GC-MS as described above. β-oxo-fatty acids were identified by the formation of diagnostic methylester/enol-TMS ions at m/z = 17356.

Quantitative PCR

Aspergillus nidulans TN02A3 conidia (1 × 108) were cultured in 100 mL of M medium at 37 °C and 120 rpm for 14 h, followed by octanoic, nonanoic acids or DA (5 mM each) for 3 h. Total RNA was recovered from mycelia using RNeasy Plant mini kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and reverse transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc.). Specific sequences were amplified by qPCR using IQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.) and the primers shown in Supplementary Table 1 with cDNA as the template as described by the manufacturer. Target gene expression was calculated relative to that of actin (actA).

Measurement of cellular MCFAs in gene disruptants

The CYP540A2 and CBBR genes were replaced with the pyrG and pyroA genes in A. nidulans TN02A3. We amplified DNA fragments encoding the 5′-and 3′-regions of the CYP540A2 and CBBR genes by PCR using the primer sets (Supplementary Table 1). The PCR template was the genomic DNA of A. nidulans FGSC A4. The pyrG and pyroA genes were amplified from the total DNA of A. fumigatus AF293. Amplified fragments were fused by PCR using nested primers (Supplementary Table 1), and introduced into A. nidulans TN02A3 as described in ref. 57. Transformants were cultured on M medium containing 0.8% agar. Gene disruption due to double crossover between the introduced DNA and host cell genomes was confirmed by PCR using the fungal conidia and the primers used in Supplementary Table 1. The resulting disruptants were called CYP540A2Δ and CBBRΔ (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Cellular MCFAs were determined as follows. Aspergillus nidulans conidia (1 × 108) in 100 mL of liquid M medium were rotary shaken at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 18 h, followed by another 3 h incubation after adding 2 M DA in DMSO to a final concentration of 5 mM DA and 0.25% DMSO. Mycelia were harvested, washed three times with 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 0.25% DMSO, and then lipid fractions were extracted using chloroform: methanol (1: 1). The extracts were suspended in trimethylchlorosilane (Sigma-Aldrich Corp.) containing N,O- bis(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide 1% (v/v) and analyzed by GC-MS as described above and nonanoic acid as the internal standard.

Other methods

All oligonucleotides used in this study were synthesized by Eurofin Genomics Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) using their Custom Oligonucleotide Synthesis Service. The sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE58. The gene cluster was analyzed using cblaster37 for boundary delimitation, while synteny analysis was performed using clinker59. Amino acid sequence alignments and phylogenetic trees were constructed according to the neighbor-joining method or the minimum linkage clustering method using the MAFFT sequence alignment server ver. 760 with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The amino acid sequences of P450cam, CYP540A2, P450SPα, P450 OleTJE, and P450BSβ were aligned using Clustal W61 and visualized using ESPript62. The tertiary structure was predicted using AlphaFold222 and AlphaFold363. The shortest electron transfer pathway predicted by HARLEM64, and PDBePISA estimated protein interfaces25. We estimated the volume of the catalytic center using CAVER Web 2.027. Molecular graphic images were prepared using PyMOL (Schrödinger LCC., New York, NY, USA) and UCSF ChimeraX 1.465.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as means ± standard deviation. Paired groups were compared using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. The number of biological replicates, P-values, and details on statistical analyses are provided in the respective figure legends. Exact P values are shown in Supplementary Data 1. Due to the small sample size (n = 3), extremely small P values were reported as P < 0.01 for clarity. Error bars in figures represent standard deviations.
