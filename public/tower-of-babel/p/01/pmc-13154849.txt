Computerized predictive models in hospital settings for detecting severe maternal complications: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Graziele Telles Vieira, Stefhanie Conceição de Jesus, Fiona Ann Lynn, Tifany Colomé Leal, Laís Antunes Wilhelm, Maria de Lourdes de Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextMaternal mortality remains a public health problem, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Complications such as hypertension, gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, and infections are among the main causes of preventable deaths. In this scenario, computerized predictive models have emerged as tools for the early identification of risks and the prevention of serious complications associated with maternal death in hospital care. Therefore, this systematic review is designed to assess the available evidence on the effectiveness of these models when applied in intra-hospital environments in relation to adverse outcomes associated with maternal mortality.MethodologyThe review will be conducted following established methodological guidance and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P). Randomized and non-randomized studies will be included, identified through systematic searches in nine data sources: Base de Dados de Enfermagem, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, The Cochrane Library, Excerpta Medica Database, Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature, Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online via Public Medline, Scientific Electronic Library Online, Scopus – Abstract and Citation Database, and Web of Science. Studies in Portuguese, English, and Spanish will be included, without restrictions in terms of publication date. Data selection and extraction will be conducted independently by two researchers, with disagreements resolved by a third. Methodological quality will be assessed using the GRADE and ROBINS-I tools. The synthesis will be narrative according to the SWiM guidelines, with the possibility of carrying out a meta-analysis, if feasible.DiscussionThe results aim to expand knowledge about the use of predictive technologies in maternal health, offering support for clinical decisions, educational strategies, and health service planning in hospital settings.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42024573613Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03169-y.

Context

Maternal mortality remains one of the main public health problems, particularly in non-specialized hospitals equipped with advanced life-support capabilities, especially in poor countries, where socioeconomic inequalities and weak health systems contribute to high rates of preventable deaths. Globally, the majority of maternal deaths occur in low- and middle-income regions, with causes directly related to poor access to qualified health services, inadequate prenatal care, and complications during childbirth [1].

In Brazil, although progress has been made in recent decades, regional disparities are visible in the maternal mortality ratio (MMR), despite the acknowledged under-recording of data. While states in the South and Southeast have better indicators, the North and Northeast regions register an MMR similar to that of African countries, reflecting the unequal access to emergency obstetric services and the precariousness of primary care [2].

A recent study integrated into the Birth in Brazil II [3] survey highlights the importance of standardized protocols for investigating maternal deaths, as most could be avoided with timely and evidence-based interventions. Conditions such as gestational hypertension, diabetes, infections, pre-eclampsia, and postpartum hemorrhage are among the main causes, which are often not diagnosed or treated in a timely manner [4, 5].

Advances in the digitalization of health systems have enabled the development and application of computerized predictive models, which use algorithms based on clinical, epidemiological, and sociodemographic data to identify patterns and anticipate serious complications. These models have been recognized as tools to support obstetric surveillance and real-time clinical decision-making, especially in the hospital context [6, 7].

Recent studies have shown the potential of predictive models in the early identification of obstetric complications such as pre-eclampsia and postpartum hemorrhage [8, 9].

Studies cited in references 8 and 9 illustrate both the promise and the limitations of contemporary predictive approaches. Farooq et al. [8] demonstrated that deep learning models can identify early signals associated with pre-eclampsia, suggesting potential for earlier risk stratification; however, the study was largely based on specific cohorts and requires broader external validation across diverse hospital settings before routine clinical adoption.

Saria et al. [9] conducted a multicenter validation study of an AI-based risk stratification tool for postpartum hemorrhage, showing that such models can be evaluated across multiple hospitals. Nevertheless, the generalizability and real-world clinical impact of these tools depend on local data quality, workflow integration, and consistent outcome definitions.

Across these studies, there remains marked heterogeneity in predictors, modeling approaches, and validation strategies, and relatively few investigations report downstream clinical outcomes such as changes in management, time to intervention, or maternal mortality-related endpoints.

Nevertheless, important gaps remain, particularly the limited and inconsistent external validation of these models across diverse hospital contexts, including resource-constrained settings with high maternal mortality ratios.

Despite their potential, there is a lack of literature on the effectiveness of these models in routine clinical practice. Several existing models have not yet been validated in real clinical environments, which limits their applicability and safety in obstetric care [10]. Moreover, in countries with poor infrastructure, the adoption of these technologies faces additional barriers, including limited availability of high-quality data and technological resources [11].

Given these limitations, this protocol establishes the methodological design of a systematic review aimed at synthesizing the available evidence on computerized predictive models applied in intra-hospital settings for the detection of severe maternal complications associated with maternal mortality.

While maternal mortality is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries, the availability and maturity of computerized predictive tools in hospital services vary considerably across settings. For this reason, this review focuses on evidence from hospital environments regardless of country income level and will explore contextual differences when data allow, rather than assuming uniform access to artificial intelligence tools.

Methodology/design

Study design

This protocol is registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the reference number CRD42024573613 [12]. Any future changes will be documented in the PROSPERO registry, with justification for modifications (inclusion of new databases or adjustments to eligibility criteria). Guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) approach to evidence synthesis, this protocol was developed following established methodological guidance for systematic reviews and will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) (see Additional File 1) [13]. Randomized clinical trials, cohort, case-control, quasi-experimental, and cross-sectional studies will be included. The studies should use computerized predictive models in the hospital context.

Definition of computerized predictive models

For the purposes of this review, “computerized predictive models” are defined as digital tools or systems that use computational algorithms (statistical models or machine learning approaches) to process individual patient data and generate a patient-level prediction of risk for maternal complications [6, 8, 9].

Electronic resources that do not incorporate an embedded predictive algorithm, such as static PDF assessment forms, digital checklists, or Excel spreadsheets used solely for data recording, will not be considered computerized predictive models for inclusion in this review.

Eligibility criteria

Population (P)

Women during pregnancy, childbirth, or the postpartum period receiving care in hospital settings.

Intervention (I)

Computerized predictive models applied in intra-hospital environments to predict or detect severe maternal complications.

Outcomes (O)

Primary outcomes include severe maternal complications such as pre-eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, severe infections, or other life-threatening obstetric conditions.

Secondary outcomes include maternal mortality, predictive performance of the models (sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve), and when reported, clinical impact measures such as time to intervention or changes in clinical management.

Context (C)

Studies conducted in hospital settings, including obstetric wards, emergency departments, delivery rooms, and intensive care units.

Studies will be included regardless of whether they demonstrate a reduction in maternal mortality, provided that they evaluate a computerized predictive model in the specified context and report relevant outcomes.

Information sources

The data sources to be used will be Base de Dados de Enfermagem (BDENF), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), The Cochrane Library, Excerpta Medica Database (Embase), Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS), Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online via Public Medline (MEDLINE/PubMed), Scientific Electronic Library Online (SciELO), Scopus – Abstract and Citation Database, and Web of Science Core.

Eligibility criteria

Quantitative studies using computerized predictive models in the intra-hospital environment involving women during pregnancy, childbirth, or the puerperium will be included. Qualitative studies, reviews, dissertations, books, editorials, and studies outside the hospital context will be excluded.

Search strategy

The search strategies were developed with the help of the librarian G.O., from the Sector Library of the Health Sciences Center – Medicine (BSCCSM, as per its Portuguese acronym), linked to the University Library (BU, as per its Portuguese acronym) of the Federal University of Santa Catarina (UFSC, as per its Portuguese acronym). The complete search strategy for PubMed/MEDLINE is available as supplementary material (Additional File 2). Similar strategies will be adapted for other data sources. The searches were carried out in July 2024 via the UFSC VPN, guaranteeing access to the used scientific data sources.

To ensure that the review reflects the most current evidence, the searches will be updated prior to the final analysis to capture any relevant studies published after the initial search period.

Study selection

Data extraction will be carried out by two independent researchers, and divergences will be resolved by a third researcher, in order to define inclusion or exclusion, using the Rayyan Platform—a web and mobile app for systematic reviews [14], which allows data to be collected in the same period and simultaneously with blinding. Additional File 3 shows the data collection matrix, where the variables to be extracted are specified.

In addition, a standardized data extraction form will be used. Two reviewers will independently extract information on study design, population characteristics, hospital setting, type of predictive model, input variables, validation methods, outcomes reported, and performance metrics.

Any discrepancies will be discussed and resolved by consensus, and if agreement cannot be reached, a third reviewer will make the final decision. Reasons for exclusion at full-text stage will be documented and reported in the review.

Data extraction

The synthesis will be narrative according to the guidelines of Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) [15] and a meta-analysis will be conducted if feasible. The quality of the evidence will be assessed with the help of the following tools: Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) system, GDT: GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (GRADEpro) [16] and Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions (ROBINS-I) [17].

The types of the used models, their predictive accuracy (such as sensitivity, specificity and Area Under the Curve (AUC)), impact on clinical management and time between detection and intervention will be among the contributions.

Planned analytical approach

Extracted data on predictive performance will be analyzed using the metrics reported in the original studies, including sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, positive and negative predictive values, calibration measures, and decision curve analysis when available. Where multiple performance metrics are reported for the same model, all will be recorded and compared to avoid selective interpretation of results.

For studies reporting clinical outcomes, we will extract information on changes in clinical management, time to intervention, escalation of care, and maternal morbidity or mortality related endpoints. When such outcomes are not reported, this will be explicitly documented as a limitation of the evidence base rather than interpreted as absence of effect.

Data will be organized in standardized tables summarizing study design, population characteristics, hospital setting, type of predictive model, predictors used, validation strategy, performance metrics, and clinical outcomes. Discrepancies in data extraction between reviewers will be jointly reviewed with a third researcher.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality will be assessed using the GRADE and ROBINS-I tools for the appropriate study designs. Two researchers will independently assess the risk of bias, with disagreements resolved by discussion or consultation with a third researcher.

Each study will be assessed separately according to its design. For non-randomized studies, ROBINS-I domains will be evaluated including confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results.

For the overall certainty of evidence across studies for each main outcome, GRADE will be applied considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Disagreements in ratings will be jointly reviewed with a third researcher.

Data synthesis

A structured narrative synthesis will be carried out following the SWiM guidelines. Meta-analysis will be carried out if ten or more studies show methodological homogeneity, and publication bias will be assessed by funnel plots and statistical tests (Egger). In the event of asymmetry, sensitivity analyses (random effects model) will be applied. If quantitative synthesis is not possible, the bias will be discussed qualitatively, considering the heterogeneity of the studies and the representativeness of the included sources.

Prior to any quantitative synthesis, clinical and methodological homogeneity will be assessed by comparing study design, population characteristics, hospital setting, type of predictive model, and outcome measures, and by visually inspecting the overlap of confidence intervals across studies.

Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be considered according to hospital setting (obstetric ward, emergency department, intensive care unit) and country income level. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted, when appropriate, by excluding studies at high risk of bias to explore the robustness of the findings.

The results will be systematically organized and presented according to a hierarchical structure that includes (1) characteristics of the predictive models (type of algorithm, predictor variables, performance metrics); (2) care context (hospital environment, intensive care unit, emergency room, delivery room); (3) gestational period (trimester of pregnancy, childbirth and puerperium); (4) primary and secondary clinical outcomes (maternal mortality, severe morbidity, specific complications); and (5) target population (demographic and clinical characteristics of pregnant women). This organization will make it possible to identify patterns, gaps in knowledge, and variations in the applicability of predictive models in different clinical scenarios.

Discussion

The implementation of computerized predictive models in maternal health has gained prominence as a strategic tool for reducing serious complications such as pre-eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage and maternal mortality. Recent studies show that the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning can improve early detection and risk stratification. For example, Farooq et al. demonstrate that algorithms based on neural networks can identify subclinical signs of pre-eclampsia with up to 90% sensitivity, outperforming traditional methods [8].

In addition to predictive accuracy, the interpretation of models is a component of their clinical adoption. As highlighted by Bosschieter et al., transparent and explainable models increase the confidence of health professionals and favor safer decisions. This approach is shared by Saria et al., who developed an early warning system for postpartum hemorrhage, integrating clinical, laboratory, and electronic medical record data, with validation in various hospital environments.

Nonetheless, the effectiveness of these models depends not only on their accuracy but also on adequate infrastructure and external validation. Medvedev et al. warn of the need for technological adaptation in regions with limited resources, where the lack of structured data and connectivity can compromise the application of solutions based on artificial intelligence. In addition, Papageorghiou et al. emphasize that validation in diverse populations is essential to ensure robustness and fairness in the results.

Thus, the systematic review should demonstrate the scientific evidence available on computerized predictive models in maternal health, which will contribute to discussions on their practical application in clinical care, education, and the organization of health services.
