Polypharmacy and cardiovascular co-morbidity as key contributors to frailty in geriatric patients with AF – insights of the Dutch-GERAF cohort study
Authors: JR Spruit, LAR Zwart, RWMM Jansen, M Muller, JR de Groot, MEW Hemels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent cardiac arrhythmia in older adults and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Frailty further exacerbates adverse health outcomes in AF patients. This study assessed the presence of impairments in geriatric domains in geriatric patients with AF and normal sinus rhythm (SR).MethodsThis prospective, multicenter cohort study included patients aged ≥ 65 years from six geriatric outpatient clinics in the Netherlands. A comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) was performed to evaluate frailty, polypharmacy, cognitive function, functional status, physical, and social status. Frailty was assessed using a CGA-based Frailty Index (FI). Patients were categorized as robust (FI < 0.18), moderately frail (FI 0.18–0.25), or severely frail (FI > 0.25). Geriatric domains include somatic (polypharmacy), cognitive, physical, social and functional dependence. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to assess the association between AF and geriatric domain impairments adjusted for age, sex, stroke and heart failure.ResultsA total of 952 patients were included, of whom 197 (21%) had established AF, and 755 had SR. AF patients were older (median 81; Inter Quartile Range (IQR) 77–86 vs. 78 years; IQR 74–83, p < 0.001) and had higher rates of cardiovascular comorbidities, including heart failure (26% vs. 3%, p < 0.001) and stroke (26% vs. 19%, p = 0.024). Frailty was significantly more prevalent in AF patients (49% vs. 28%, p < 0.001), with a higher mean Frailty Index (0.19 vs. 0.15, p < 0.001). Patients with AF had higher odds of having polypharmacy (≥5 drugs) (adjusted odds ratio (OR) 3.01; 95%-confidence interval (CI) 1.98–4.59) and hyperpolypharmacy (≥ 10 drugs) (adjusted OR 2.67; 95%-CI 1.73–4.10). There was a trend between AF and physical problems (a composite of gait disturbances, falls, use of walking aids or decreased hand grip strength) (unadjusted OR 1.39; 95%-CI 0.99–1.94, mainly explained by the significant association between AF and the use of a walking device aOR 1.80, 95%-CI 1.20–2.71). The association between AF and functional dependence in activities of daily living (ADL) or Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (iADL) (unadjusted OR 1.44; 95%-CI 1.06–1.97), was not significant anymore after adjustment for age and sex. AF was not associated with other geriatric domains.ConclusionPatients with AF have a higher prevalence of frailty compared with those in SR. Polypharmacy was the largest difference between AF and SR patients, and it seems that comorbidities, in particular cardiovascular comorbidities, appear to largely account for the higher frailty index in AF patients. Our findings suggest that within an already frail geriatric population, AF is associated with a distinct profile characterized by high cardiovascular comorbidity and polypharmacy, without conferring additional risk across cognitive, physical, or social domains.Trial registrationreference number 2019–5889 2020-04-15. Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia in older adults and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. Underlying multimorbidity, functional dependency, and other aging-related vulnerabilities make AF particularly burdensome in geriatric populations, where it has been associated with impaired mobility, falls, and loss of independence [2]. Yet, despite the high prevalence of aging-related vulnerabilities in this group, the specific geriatric profile of older adults with AF remains insufficiently characterized.

Frailty—a multidimensional syndrome of reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability—is highly relevant in this context [3]. Several studies report that frailty is more common in patients with AF than in those without AF, and that frailty may modify treatment decisions, outcomes, and prognosis [4–6]. However, reported prevalence varies widely due to the use of heterogeneous frailty definitions. Instruments range from physical-based models such as the Fried phenotype [7], to multidomain frailty indices that capture comorbidities, disabilities, and cognitive impairments [8, 9]. Additional approaches, such as the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) further complicate direct comparisons across studies [10]. This variability makes it difficult to compare studies and to establish how AF relates to frailty across specific geriatric domains. The Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)–based Frailty Index offers a more standardized approach and may help improve comparability across populations [7, 8].

Prior literature suggests a potentially bidirectional relationship between AF and frailty. Shared cardiovascular risk factors—including hypertension, diabetes, and inactivity—predispose to both AF and frailty, while AF-related hemodynamic compromise, stroke risk, and medication burden may in turn accelerate functional and cognitive deterioration [11]. Observational studies have linked AF to cognitive decline and dementia, though mechanisms and confounding factors remain debated [12].

Most available studies analyze single domains (e.g., physical function or cognition) or rely on community-based, relatively healthy populations. Few studies have examined frailty and domain-specific impairments using a standardized multidomain CGA in geriatric outpatients—a population in whom AF is common, treatment decisions are complex, and vulnerability is high.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the multidomain geriatric profile of older adults with AF compared to those in SR using data from the Dutch GERAF cohort, a geriatric outpatient population in which frailty and multimorbidity are highly prevalent. In contrast to most previous studies, which have largely focused on community-based samples or single domains of health, we applied a CGA approach to evaluate multiple physical, cognitive, psychological, and social domains simultaneously. This design allows us to examine whether AF is associated with a distinct pattern of frailty and geriatric impairments in a population already at high risk of vulnerability. We additionally examined the association between age, frailty, and new-onset AF to better understand geriatric contributors to AF vulnerability.

Methods

The Dutch opportunistic screening in Geriatric patients for AF (GERAF) study was a prospective, multicenter cohort study, recruiting consecutive patients aged ≥ 65 years attending outpatient Geriatric Medicine clinics from six hospitals in The Netherlands [13]. At study entry, all patients underwent a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) to evaluate frailty, polypharmacy, cognitive function, functional status, physical, and social status. The CGA included evaluation of comorbidities, vital function tests, standard laboratory tests and a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). Opportunistic screening for new-onset atrial fibrillation was performed during the six-month follow-up period. At one-year follow-up, medical records were reviewed to determine whether a new diagnosis of atrial fibrillation had been documented.

This analysis investigates the prevalence of frailty in patients with and without AF. Also, differences in geriatric domains between patients with AF and SR will be studied. Furthermore, guideline adherence concerning anticoagulation therapy in AF patients was assessed. Lastly, baseline characteristics and frailty status were compared between patients who developed new-onset AF at one-year follow-up and those who remained in SR. The study complies with the declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the medical Ethical Committee Oost Nederland (reference number 2019–5889). All participants provided written informed consent. The design and first results of the GERAF study have been published [13, 14].

Geriatric domains

Four geriatric domains were identified: somatic, cognitive, physical, and social. For the somatic domain the number of prescribed medications was recorded, as a proxy of presence of comorbidities. Polypharmacy was assessed as a discrete value and dichotomized as 0–4 and ≥ 5 prescribed drugs. Hyperpolypharmacy was defined as ≥ 10 drugs. Short courses of antibiotics were not included.

The cognitive domain included cognitive impairment (MCI or dementia) and abnormal test results on the Standardized Mini-Mental State Exam (SMMSE) [15, 16] and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [17]. SMMSE and MOCA were used as cognitive screening tools both with a maximum of 30 points. Lower scores indicate a lower level of cognitive functioning. A total score on either the SMMSE or the MOCA of < 26 defined impairment in cognitive domain [18]. Cognitive assessment was performed when cognitive impairment was suspected, either via cognitive screening or full neuropsychological evaluation.

The physical domain was considered affected when a patient had either low grip strength, gait disturbances, falls or use of walking devices. The dependence domain was considered affected when a patient was classified as dependent in activities of daily living (ADL) or Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (iADL). A patient was either (i)ADL independent or dependent.

The social domain was affected when a patient lived alone, or lived in a residence with assistance or a nursing home. See supplementary table S2 for an overview of all individual items contributing to each domain.

Frailty index

Frailty was quantified using a previously validated CGA-based Frailty Index (FI), based on the Accumulation of Deficits model [8]. The FI consists of 46 factors, each scored as absent (0), present [1], and in some cases, severe [2], with a maximum possible score of 51, of which twenty items were directly related to comorbidity or chronic disease, with the remaining items reflecting functional, cognitive, psychological, and social domains (see also Supplementary table S1) [13]. The FI is calculated as the sum of present factors divided by 51. Patients were classified as robust (FI < 0.18), moderately frail (FI 0.18–0.25), or severely frail (FI > 0.25), as proposed by Rockwood and colleagues [19]. Statistical analysis

Data were managed and collected using the online platform CASTOR EDC. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows, version 28. A forest plot was constructed in R. Univariate analysis using T-tests, Kruskal–Wallis, or chi-square tests (depending on the distribution of the variable) were used to calculate differences in variables between patients with AF and SR.

Multivariate logistic regression was performed to assess the association between AF (vs. SR) and geriatric domain impairments adjusted for age and sex (Model 1), and age, sex, stroke and heart failure (Model 2). Sensitivity analyses were subsequently performed using additional multivariate models adjusting for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease (Model 3), and a fully adjusted model including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, stroke, and heart failure (Model 4).

Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression was used to assess the association between age, frailty and geriatric domains on new-onset AF. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Medication burden was notably higher among AF patients, with an average of 8 medications compared to 6 in SR patients (p < 0.001). Differences in the number of medications were not solely attributable to the use of anticoagulation and antiarrhythmic therapy; medications commonly prescribed for several cardiovascular conditions such as hypertension, heart failure, diabetes and angina pectoris were also significantly more frequently used. Patients with AF had a mean of 4.0 cardiovascular medications and 3.4 non-cardiovascular medications, compared to 2.2 and 3.0 in patients with SR (p < 0.001), see also Table 1.

In the AF group, 178 patients (90%) were prescribed anticoagulation therapy with either a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC, n = 123 or 62%) or a vitamin K antagonist (n = 55 or 28%).

Frailty and geriatric domains

Frailty analysis showed that AF patients were significantly frailer than SR patients, with a higher frailty index (mean 0.19 ± 0.09 vs. 0.15 ± 0.08, p < 0.001). A lower proportion of AF patients were classified as robust (51% vs. 72%, p < 0.001), while a higher percentage were moderately frail (24% vs. 17%) or severely frail (25% vs. 11%). At baseline, AF prevalence was 16% in robust patients, 27% in moderately frail patients, and 37% in severely frail patients.

A non-significant trend toward a higher frequency of impairments in the physical domain was observed in patients with AF (p = 0.066). However, the association between AF and physical domain impairments was no longer present after adjustment for age and sex (Model 1). To explore whether specific components of the physical domain accounted for this pattern, additional analyses were performed, since gait disturbances and the use of walking aids were significantly higher in the AF group. In age- and sex-adjusted analyses, AF was associated with gait disturbances (Model 1: OR 1.47, 95% CI 0.95–1.82), although this association lost statistical significance after further adjustment for stroke and heart failure (Model 2: OR 1.28, 95% CI 0.87–1.89). In contrast, AF remained significantly associated with the use of walking aids across all models (Model 1: OR 2.16, 95% CI 1.47–3.16; Model 2: OR 1.80, 95% CI 1.20–2.71). No significant associations were observed between AF and fall incidence or reduced handgrip strength.

Functional dependence was more prevalent in the AF group (49% vs. 40%, p = 0.024), primarily due to a higher rate of iADL dependency in AF patients − 46% AF patients versus 38% in SR patients, with a significant association in de unadjusted model. However, when adjusted in model 1 and 2, there was no longer a significant association (Fig. 2). Which indicates that the observed difference may be explained by the greater burden of comorbidities among AF patients rather than AF itself.

There was no significant association between AF and impairments in the social domain.

Sensitivity analyses

Models additionally adjusting for hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease (Model 3), as well as a fully adjusted model including all comorbidities (Model 4), yielded nearly identical patterns (see Supplementary table S2). Across all adjusted models, polypharmacy (Model 4 OR 3.18, 95% CI 2.00–5.04) and hyperpolypharmacy (Model 4 OR 2.76, 95% CI 1.73–4.41) remained strongly associated with AF. Consistent with the physical-domain sub-analyses, the use of a walking device also remained significantly associated with AF across all models. In contrast, no significant associations were observed for cognitive, overall physical, or social impairment, nor for functional dependence.

New onset AF

After 1 year, 31 new cases (4.1%) of AF were diagnosed. Compared to the 724 patients remaining in SR, patients with new-onset AF were significantly older than those in SR (mean 82 ± 6.7) vs. 78 ± 6.2) P < 0.001, and increasing age was significantly associated with new-onset AF (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.05–1.18). Surprisingly, patients with new-onset AF were prescribed fewer medications (mean 4.5± 3.0 vs. 5.8 ± 4.1; p = 0.020), and had a trend in lower rates of polypharmacy (29% vs. 47% p = 0.059) at baseline compared to patients remaining in SR. Also, patients with new onset AF exhibited a lower prevalence of cognitive disorders (16.1% vs. 44.8% p = 0.031) at baseline compared with those remaining in SR. The frailty index as well as frailty categories were similar across groups. Cognitive dysfunction at baseline was associated with a lower risk of developing AF at one year (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18–0.87). Frailty status was not associated with incident AF at one year.

Discussion

In this geriatric outpatient cohort, patients with AF had a higher prevalence of frailty (as reflected by the distribution across frailty categories), and exhibited a higher degree of frailty, as indicated by a significantly and clinically relevant higher Frailty Index. However, this difference was not accompanied by consistent impairments across individual geriatric domains. Instead, patients with AF have higher odds of polypharmacy and hyperpolypharmacy, but the other domains did not have a significant association. There were some differences in the physical domain, in particular the use of a walking device.

Importantly, our analyses do not suggest that AF patients are frailer in a distinct or domain-specific manner compared with patients in sinus rhythm (SR). Rather, the observed differences appear to reflect the composition of frailty within an already vulnerable cohort. The primary comparison in this study was between geriatric outpatients with AF and those with SR, not between frail and non-frail individuals. Consequently, higher FI scores in AF patients should be interpreted as reflecting greater cardiovascular disease burden and treatment complexity, rather than implying that AF independently causes a broader or more severe frailty phenotype.

Frailty composition and the role of polypharmacy

Polypharmacy and hyperpolypharmacy emerged as the clearest discriminator between AF and SR patients. This finding remained robust across multiple adjusted models, including sensitivity analyses accounting for major cardiovascular comorbidities. At the same time, several components of the FI capture comorbidity-related deficits, raising the possibility of partial measurement overlap between frailty and medication burden. Rather than indicating circular reasoning, this overlap reflects the clinical reality that cardiovascular multimorbidity and its treatment contribute meaningfully to vulnerability in older adults.

Our findings therefore support an interpretation in which AF functions as part of a broader cardiovascular disease constellation that manifests clinically as increased medical complexity. In this context, polypharmacy should not be viewed as a cause of frailty, but as a marker of cumulative disease burden and therapeutic intensity. This distinction is important, as it reframes frailty in AF as a phenotype dominated by cardiovascular vulnerability rather than uniform deterioration across physical, cognitive, or social domains.

Recent guidance from the European Society of Hypertension highlights frailty as a key modifier of cardiovascular risk in older adults and recommends routine frailty assessment and active screening for atrial fibrillation in elderly patients with hypertensive heart disease [21].

Recent evidence further supports the interpretation of atrial fibrillation as a marker of cardiovascular vulnerability with important prognostic implications in older adults. AF has been shown to independently predict postoperative and long-term mortality in elderly and frail populations. For example, this association has been demonstrated in octogenarians undergoing hip fracture surgery without pre-existing heart failure [22] and in patients implanted with dual-chamber pacemakers [23].

Similarly, in older patients with atrial high-rate episodes or established AF, the presence of malnutrition further amplifies mortality risk [24]. Together, these findings suggest that AF often reflects an advanced cardiovascular substrate and systemic vulnerability that confers excess risk beyond chronological age alone.

In this context, our finding that frail patients with AF are primarily distinguished by cardiovascular comorbidity and hyperpolypharmacy aligns with literature emphasizing cumulative cardiovascular burden as a major determinant of adverse outcomes in older adults. Rather than indicating a distinct multisystem frailty phenotype, AF may therefore function as a clinical signal identifying patients who warrant heightened surveillance and structured risk assessment. The integration of validated risk prediction models—such as perioperative myocardial injury risk scores in elderly patients undergoing non-elective surgery [22] and mortality prediction tools in older patients with heart failure and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators [23] may support individualized decision-making and closer clinical follow-up in this vulnerable population.

Cognitive outcomes and dementia subtypes

In contrast to several large population-based studies [24–26], we found no significant difference in the prevalence of cognitive impairment or dementia between patients with AF and those with SR. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by characteristics of the study population. Geriatric outpatients are frequently referred due to generalized decline, memory complaints, or recurrent falls, resulting in a cohort already enriched for cognitive vulnerability. Indeed, baseline cognitive impairment was highly prevalent in both groups, limiting the ability to detect incremental effects attributable specifically to AF. This likely represents a ceiling effect: in a cohort characterized by advanced frailty and high baseline cognitive burden, the additional impact of AF may be comparatively small and therefore less detectable than in community-dwelling, more robust populations. This ceiling effect may partly explain why associations observed in community-based cohorts were not replicated here.

Although overall dementia prevalence did not differ, the distribution of dementia subtypes showed a noteworthy pattern. Alzheimer’s disease was proportionally less frequent among AF patients with dementia, while mixed-type dementia was more common. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that AF contributes predominantly to vascular or mixed dementia through mechanisms such as cerebral hypoperfusion, microembolism, and silent infarction. While our study was not powered to test subtype-specific hypotheses, these findings suggest that AF may influence the nature of dementia rather than its overall prevalence.

Other geriatric domains

Beyond cognition, we observed no consistent associations between AF and impairments in physical, social, or functional domains after adjustment. These null findings should be interpreted cautiously. The cohort exhibited high baseline levels of multimorbidity and functional impairment, resulting in limited variability across domains and reduced power to detect subtle differences. Thus, absence of evidence should not be interpreted as evidence of absence, but rather as reflecting the challenges of detecting domain-specific associations within an already frail population.

Taken together, these findings reinforce the concept that the geriatric impact of AF is context-dependent. In more robust populations, AF may represent a salient destabilizing factor with measurable multidomain consequences. In contrast, within a highly vulnerable geriatric outpatient cohort, AF appears to integrate into an already established frailty profile, without producing a distinct pattern of additional multidomain impairment.

New-onset AF

The analysis of incident AF yielded a seemingly paradoxical finding: patients who developed AF during follow-up were older but had fewer medications and less cognitive impairment at baseline. This pattern likely reflects a combination of ascertainment bias and underlying frailty trajectories. AF detection relied on opportunistic screening, structured rhythm monitoring, and medical record review [13]. Patients with preserved cognition are more likely to adhere to follow-up visits, cooperate with rhythm monitoring, and report symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia, thereby increasing the likelihood of AF detection. In contrast, AF episodes in more cognitively impaired individuals may have remained unrecognized, particularly if asymptomatic. Unfortunately, no data are available regarding the frequency with which patients were screened for AF outside the study protocol.

Second, severe cognitive impairment may represent a distinct, non-cardiovascular terminal trajectory characterized by competing mortality risks. In such individuals, the opportunity to develop or clinically manifest incident AF may be reduced due to shorter survival or limited physiological reserve. From this perspective, lower observed AF incidence in cognitively impaired participants may reflect competing risks rather than a truly protective association.

An alternative, complementary explanation is that AF may preferentially develop earlier in the frailty continuum. Previous studies suggest that AF incidence is highest among relatively robust older adults, while prevalence accumulates in severely frail individuals [27]. From this perspective, AF may contribute to subsequent frailty progression rather than emerging predominantly in advanced frailty stages. Our findings are consistent with this model and highlight the importance of longitudinal studies with systematic rhythm surveillance and repeated frailty assessments.

Importantly, the number of incident AF cases in our cohort was small (n = 31). Therefore, this analysis should be considered exploratory, and the observed inverse association between baseline cognitive impairment and incident AF requires confirmation in larger cohorts with systematic rhythm surveillance, repeated cognitive assessment, and formal competing-risk analyses.

In addition, further data on the risk of new-onset heart failure and stroke in geriatric patients with AF are needed to better characterize the longitudinal cardiovascular trajectory and clinical consequences of AF in this vulnerable population.

Anticoagulation and treatment considerations

Anticoagulation rates in this cohort were high and consistent with guideline recommendations, underscoring the feasibility of stroke prevention even in very old and frail patients. Although a small proportion of eligible patients remained untreated, this group remains at elevated risk of stroke and related complications. Comprehensive geriatric assessment may help distinguish patients with true contraindications from those in whom anticoagulation is inappropriately withheld due to perceived frailty or cognitive impairment.

Strengths, limitations, and generalizability

Strengths of this study include its large, well-characterized geriatric cohort, prospective design, and use of comprehensive geriatric assessment. Several limitations warrant consideration. AF subtype could not be distinguished, potentially obscuring associations specific to paroxysmal versus persistent AF. Incident AF may have been underdiagnosed, particularly among cognitively impaired patients. Finally, the cohort consisted of motivated geriatric outpatients, which may limit generalizability to primary care, nursing home, or more severely frail populations.

Clinical implications

Taken together, our findings suggest that frailty in older patients with AF should primarily be interpreted as a manifestation of cardiovascular vulnerability and treatment burden rather than a distinct, global frailty syndrome. Hyperpolypharmacy and cardiovascular comorbidity most clearly differentiated frail AF patients from frail SR patients, indicating that medication burden and cardiometabolic risk clustering are their most prominent vulnerabilities.

The contrast with community-based studies is clinically informative. While AF in relatively robust older adults may signal increased risk of cognitive decline and functional deterioration, in highly frail geriatric outpatients it primarily identifies individuals with advanced cardiovascular disease burden and therapeutic complexity.

Our data therefore support prioritizing structured medication review and targeted deprescribing as central components of care for frail older adults with AF. Polypharmacy was the most distinguishing feature in this group, underscoring the importance of critically reassessing ongoing indications, minimizing drug–drug interactions, and simplifying treatment regimens where feasible. Comprehensive cardiovascular optimization—including blood pressure control, heart failure management, and appropriate anticoagulation—should be systematically integrated into geriatric assessment pathways.

Routine screening for AF in geriatric outpatient clinics should be maintained. However, in this highly vulnerable population, a diagnosis of AF should primarily trigger a thorough cardiovascular and pharmacotherapy review rather than automatic assumptions of accelerated multidomain functional decline. In other words, AF in this setting functions less as a driver of global geriatric deterioration and more as a clinical marker of cardiovascular vulnerability embedded within advanced frailty. These findings argue for structured cardiology–geriatrics co-management models in which AF serves as a clinical signal for intensified cardiovascular risk stratification, medication optimization, and individualized care planning.

Conclusion

In this geriatric outpatient cohort, patients with AF showed a higher degree of frailty compared with those in sinus rhythm, primarily reflected by markedly higher rates of polypharmacy and hyperpolypharmacy. Other geriatric domains, including cognition, mobility, and functional dependence, did not differ significantly after adjustment for age and comorbidities. These findings suggest that the increased frailty burden in AF is associated with a greater comorbidity and medication load rather than distinct multidomain geriatric impairment.

These observations suggest that AF can be considered a marker of cardiovascular vulnerability, while the additional contribution of other geriatric domains may be less apparent in already frail populations.

Importantly, cognitive function did not differ between patients with and without AF, and individuals with cognitive decline had lower rates of new onset AF. More than 90% of AF patients received oral anticoagulation, underscoring the feasibility of adequate anticoagulant therapy in this group.
