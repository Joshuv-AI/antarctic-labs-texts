Intramedullary nail fixation for tibial plateau fractures: a systematic review of techniques and outcomes
Authors: McKenna W. Box, Jarrod Q. Cooper, Joseph Stanke, Grayson A. Domingue, Edwin Dudoussat, Troy B. Puga, Adam P. Breceda, John T. Riehl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

1. Introduction

Tibial plateau fractures (TPFs) account for 1.2% of all fractures, frequently resulting from high-energy injuries.1 Multiple fixation methods are used including external fixation, percutaneous screw fixation, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), and minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis.2 Although ORIF is the primary treatment for these fractures, several complications can occur, including infection, secondary damage to soft tissue, and devascularization of the fracture site.1,3

Intramedullary nail (IMN) fixation, traditionally used for diaphyseal tibial fractures, has gained traction for treating distal and proximal tibial metaphyseal fractures (often called “extreme nailing”).4,5 IMN fixation for extra-articular proximal tibia fractures is associated with shorter hospital stays, reduced time to weight bearing, decreased infection rates, and faster union times.4–6 Weight bearing can be particularly important for geriatric patients with TPFs to prevent increased morbidity and mortality.7,8 These results have increased the interest in IMN in TPFs.7 To our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic review focused on the techniques, outcomes, and complications of IMN fixation for tibial plateau fractures. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the reported techniques, outcomes, and complications of IMN fixation of TPFs.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and eligibility

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the standards of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines.9 This systematic review does not involve any human or animal subjects and was deemed exempt from IRB review.

2.2. Eligibility criteria

Studies were evaluated based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) study population age 18 and older, (2) patients with tibial plateau fractures (classified by the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO)/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) as 41B or 41C fractures), and (3) fixation was performed with IMN with or without ORIF. Studies including tibial plateau fractures with an associated ipsilateral tibial shaft fracture were eligible for inclusion only if outcomes specific to the tibial plateau injury were reported. These injuries were analyzed as complex periarticular fracture patterns involving the proximal tibia, rather than as isolated diaphyseal fractures. Exclusion criteria were the following: (1) study population younger than 18 years of age; (2) proximal tibia fractures that were not intra-articular (i.e., OTA/AO 41A or 42); (3) biomechanical studies; and (3) reviews, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Only peer-reviewed publications, articles with human subjects, and articles with an English manuscript were considered for inclusion.

2.3. Search strategy

The MEDLINE, Cochrane, Embase, and Google Scholar databases were systematically searched from inception through January 1, 2025. For MEDLINE and Cochrane, the following medical subject heading (MeSH) terms were used: “fracture fixation, intramedullary” AND (“tibial plateau fractures” OR “proximal tibia fractures” OR “tibia fractures”). The Embase search included: (tibia) OR (tibial plateau) OR (proximal tibia) AND (intramedullary). Google Scholar was searched with the terms: “intramedullary nail” AND “tibia plateau.” The first 10 search pages were evaluated for inclusion.

One author (M.W.B.) searched and excluded irrelevant articles and duplicates based on the title and abstract. The remaining articles underwent an independent full text review by 2 reviewers (J.Q.C., J.S.) and were assessed for eligibility based on established criteria. Any conflicts were resolved by discussion with the lead author (M.W.B.).

2.4. Data extraction

Baseline study information was collected, including the first author, study design, and level of evidence. Patient characteristics collected included demographic information (age, sex, follow-up), fracture classification, and operative technique and times. Outcomes collected included radiographic outcomes (time to union, malalignment described as “varus or valgus deformity of 5 degrees or more,” nonunion, malunion) and clinical outcomes (knee pain, knee outcome scores). Knee function scoring systems used in the included studies included the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) Knee score,10 Oxford Knee Score (OKS),11 Lysholm and Tegner score, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS),12,13 and American Knee Society Score (AKSS)10 (Table 1). Two reviewers (J.Q.C., J.S.) independently extracted study data using a standardized data extraction form. The lead author (M.W.B.) resolved discrepancies through a third independent review.

2.5. Quality assessment

Two reviewers (M.W.B. and T.B.P.) independently assessed methodological quality of the nonrandomized studies included in the review using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) Criteria for noncomparative studies and comparative studies.14 The MINORS Criteria for noncomparative studies uses a validated 8-item checklist and a 12-item checklist to ensure a critical appraisal of the study design through a standardized assessment. A maximum of 24 points is possible for retrospective comparative studies, and studies with scores less than 14 were considered poor quality. A maximum of 16 points is possible for retrospective comparative studies, with a score less than 8 regarded as poor quality.

2.6. Synthesis methods

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate whether continuous variables followed a normal distribution. For those normally distributed variables, means represented the central tendency, whereas SDs indicated the variance. In cases of skewed data, medians served as the central tendency, and interquartile ranges represented the variance. A significance level of P values less than 0.05 was set for this study. Data analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

3.3. Study characteristics and patient demographics

Overall, 7 case series, 1 case report, and 2 cohort studies met the inclusion criteria (Table 2). There were 205 patients in total, of whom 52 were treated with IMN with or without percutaneous screws (group 1), 45 with IMN with supplemental plate fixation (group 2), 30 with IMN plus condylar bolt support (group 3), and 78 with ORIF alone (group 4). Kubiak et al19 evaluated ipsilateral TPFs and tibial shaft fractures (OTA/AO 41 B/C + 42) treated with IMN with supplemental plate fixation. Lim et al20 also evaluated ipsilateral TPFs and tibial shaft fractures treated with either IMN or ORIF alone.

3.4. Fracture characteristics

Bogdan et al,22 Jianbiao et al,15 and Lim et al20 did not report the OTA/AO classification type in their studies. Of the remaining studies, 151 fractures (groups 1–3, n = 108; group 4, n = 43) were classified according to the OTA/AO classification23 as types 41B1-41C3 (Table 3). The most common fracture types in group 1 were 41C1 (34%); in group 2, 41C2 (50%); in group 3, 41C2 (40%); and in group 4, 41C3 (70%). All studies included the Schatzker classification.24 Group 3 patients had the highest proportion of Schatzker IV fractures (80%), followed by group 4 (78%), group 1 (69%), and group 2 (53%).

3.7. Complications

Knee pain was evaluated in 9 studies (Table 6).1,5,15–19,21 Jia et al1 and Wright et al21 reported a high rate of knee pain (42.11% and 43.75%, respectively). In Jia et al,1 a suprapatellar approach was used. Patients did not report knee pain with either an infrapatellar approach (transpatellar tendon or lateral parapatellar) or a suprapatellar approach. No cases of nonunion or malunion were reported in any IMN treatment groups. Chapman et al5 reported no radiographic differences between group 1 and group 4 patients in postoperative tibial slope changes and tibia-to-femur width ratio when evaluating patients with OTA 41-C classification fractures. In group 1, Jia et al1 had one patient with malalignment into 20° varus (the patient walked early against medical advice). Lim et al20 reported 2 coronal malalignments in group 1 patients and 5 coronal and 2 sagittal malalignments in group 4.

4. Discussion

This systematic review summarizes the existing clinical literature on IMN-based fixation of adult TPFs. Across 10 predominantly Level IV studies, IMN fixation, with or without supplemental plate or condylar bolt fixation, yielded acceptable functional and radiographic outcomes in selected fracture patterns. Although the current evidence is limited, these findings support IMN as a primary or adjunct fixation strategy for select TPFs and highlight the need for well-designed comparative studies to better define indications and construct selection.

In bicondylar TPFs (Schatzker V/VI or OTA/AO type C fractures), IMN-based fixation, often combined with supplemental articular fixation with percutaneous lag screws, condylar bolts, or unicondylar plating, has emerged as an alternative strategy to traditional plate constructs.5,15,25 Recent advances in implant technology have introduced multiplanar interlocking screws clustered near the ends of nails. This allows for enhanced purchase in proximal segments with the addition of threads that link the interlocking screws to the nail, thereby creating a load-sharing, fixed-angle construct while minimizing soft tissue disruption.5 In TPFs with metadiaphyseal dissociation, IMN is particularly advantageous for spanning comminution.26 Biomechanical studies demonstrate that IMN-based constructs in bicondylar TPFs provide superior axial load sharing and resistance to varus deformity, with medial plateau subsidence resistance comparable to that of dual plating when augmented with condylar bolts or supplemental fixation (albeit with lower torsional stiffness and a lower ultimate load-to-failure, reflecting a more elastic construct).27–30 As demonstrated by the techniques used in the studies evaluated, C- and B-type articular fractures must be converted to A-type fractures by achieving anatomic reduction and stable fixation of the articular surface before IMN insertion. Fixation of the articular surface can then be achieved using percutaneous screws, condylar bolts, or unicondylar plates, with attention to the IMN entry trajectory.

Despite favorable outcomes in reported studies, IMN fixation may not be appropriate for all TPF patterns, and several relative contraindications have been noted across the included studies. These encompass severe articular impaction or comminution necessitating bone grafting in conjunction with ORIF, medial plateau fracture dislocations, significant metaphyseal displacement requiring the use of blocking screws, fractures involving the tibial tubercle, and an inability to reconstruct the articular surface outside the planned nail trajectory.1,5,16–18,20,31,32 Fractures involving tibial tuberosity fragments are deemed unsuitable candidates because of the potential for the nail to induce a considerable anteriorly directed deforming force.33

In contrast to bicondylar injuries, the role of IMN in isolated unicondylar TPFs (Schatzker I, II, IV; OTA/AO type B) remains poorly defined and is likely better treated with unicondylar buttress plating.34 Biomechanically, the IMN typically does not provide direct fixation to the articular portion of the condylar fragment or subchondral support of the articular surface. However, the use of additional subchondral percutaneous screws can theoretically provide adequate subchondral support and fracture compression.25,31 One indication for IMN use in unicondylar fractures would be a concomitant ipsilateral diaphyseal fracture (OTA/AO type 42). Again, 41B-type fractures must be converted to 41A-type fractures before IMN insertion. Most unicondylar TPFs in the included studies were likely treated with IMN with independent screws or plate fixation because of the presence of ipsilateral diaphyseal fractures.1,16,19,20,22 No reasoning or technique descriptions were given for the fixation of isolated unicondylar TPFs in the included studies. The use of IMN-only fixation for simple intra-articular TPFs represents a current gap in the literature that warrants further attention.

Operative times were similar across all studies (range: 94.6–150 minutes) aside from Chapman et al5 [group 1 (mean, SD: 312 ± 174 minutes), group 4 single plating (mean, SD: 330 ± 162 minutes), and group 4 dual plating (mean, SD: 456 ± 156 minutes)]. One explanation is that Chapman et al5 included only Schatzker VI fractures and had a high proportion of OTA/AO 41C3 TPFs (60%). There was a significant difference in treatment allocation among OTA 41C1, 41C2, and 41C3 fractures, with a preference for dual plate fixation in OTA/AO 41C3 fractures (group 1: 36.8%; group 4: 82.1%; P = 0.004). However, these operative times are longer than those in current literature for similar fractures, and the reasons for this are unknown.35,36 Gavarnos (2017)17 was the only study with a similar Schatzker VI makeup, and in that study, operative time was 1/3 the length. However, these were primarily 41C1 TPFs. Increased operative time with TPFs is associated with increased infection rate.36–38 Overall, operative time was lower in the IMN studies compared with previous ORIF studies.35,36 However, comparisons of similar fracture patterns among the same operating surgeons are needed to understand this relationship further.

High-energy TPFs (Schatzker IV, V, VI) and using 2 or more plates can increase infection rate.36 One theorized advantage of IMN for TPFs is reduced soft tissue disruption, thereby reducing infection and soft tissue complications. The infection rates for groups 1 to 3 were lower than or comparable to those reported in previous studies37–41 of ORIF for high-energy TPFs (groups 1–3: 0%–10.5% vs previous ORIF studies37–41: 7.64%–16.67%). A meta-analysis by Monahan et al42 reported significantly lower superficial infection rates but no difference in deep infection rates between IMN and ORIF for extra-articular proximal tibia fractures (OTA/AO 41A). They did report a significantly lower risk of compartment syndrome with ORIF than IMN (P = 0.018). In the current review, only Kubiak et al19 reported compartment syndromes in ipsilateral TPFs and shaft fractures. Larger comparative studies are needed to determine actual differences in infection and soft tissue complications between TPFs treated with ORIF and IMN.

Postoperative knee scores were either excellent or good using the various outcome measures.1,15–18 A recent Cochrane review reported a mean HSS score of 83.1 points (good) for dual plating ORIF of tibial plateau fractures, which is lower than the scores reported in the studies included in this review.36 However, multiple factors influence postoperative knee function.43 The OTA/AO classification is better associated with predicting functional outcomes.44 Overall, no conclusions can be drawn without direct comparative studies with similar fracture patterns and postoperative protocols.

With TPFs, knee pain is expected postoperatively because of the fracture's proximity to the knee. Rates of postoperative knee pain were low across most studies in groups 1 to 3. However, there was a paucity of quantifiable pain scores reported, which limits comparisons with prior ORIF literature. Postoperative knee pain scores after ORIF of tibial plateau fractures generally improve significantly over time.45 The extent of recovery depends on various patient and injury factors, and the quality of reduction.45–49 Pain usually stabilizes by 1 year after surgery, with no notable changes observed between the 1- and 2-year follow-up periods.47 Postoperative knee pain after tibia IMN may be influenced by the chosen approach.48–50 A meta-analysis by Wang et al48 found lower Visual Analogue Scale scores for knee pain at 12 months with the suprapatellar approach compared with the infrapatellar approach. Bakhsh et al49 found no significant differences in knee pain or function between the lateral parapatellar, medial parapatellar, and tendon-splitting approaches to tibia IMN. Despite the suprapatellar approach used by Wright et al,21 the incidence of knee pain remained high (43.75%), comparable to that reported by Jia et al1 (42.1%), in which an infrapatellar approach was used. Because of limited sample sizes, no direct comparisons were made between IMN and ORIF alone, precluding any conclusions regarding knee pain. When using IMN, known techniques to minimize anterior knee pain risk such as preventing intra-articular prominence of the proximal IMN by less than 5 mm or burying it in the anterior cortex should be followed.50,51

Fracture healing and alignment outcomes after IMN-based fixation were generally favorable but should be interpreted in light of study heterogeneity and limited comparative data. The time to union among groups 1 and 3 constructs was relatively consistent, with reported means ranging from 3.53 to 3.95 months. In contrast, the longest union times were observed in group 2 constructs, most notably in the series by Bogdan et al22 (mean 7.2 ± 2.2 months). Given small sample sizes and the absence of direct, controlled comparisons, it remains unclear whether adjunctive plating independently contributed to prolonged union or reflected greater fracture complexity. From a biological standpoint, periosteal disruption associated with ORIF has been linked to higher nonunion risk, a factor mitigated by minimally invasive techniques such as minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis or IMN.42,52,53 Across the included studies, nonunion rates after IMN, with or without supplemental fixation, were low (0%) and lower than those reported in historical ORIF series (1.6%–5%).41,43,54–57 Most TPF nonunions occur with Schatzker VI fracture patterns and along the posteromedial and medial plateau.58 It can be hypothesized that periosteal stripping is reduced with IMN fixation, regardless of supplemental fixation, compared with dual plate ORIF, and in combination with the load-sharing biomechanics of IMN could contribute to a lower rate of nonunion. However, further longitudinal evidence in larger, more diverse populations is needed to identify contributors to these differences.

Muscular deforming forces around proximal tibial fractures can lead to malalignment of the knee and altered limb kinematics.58 In Lim et al,20 the occurrence of malalignment was similar between ORIF and IMN for TPFs (15% vs. 20%). Notably, malalignment rates in extra-articular series7 treated with both modalities (9.1%–11.7%) are lower, which illustrates baseline alignment challenges related to proximal tibial anatomy and IMN mechanics (rather than to imply direct equivalence between fracture types). IMN design and tibial entry point can exacerbate or counteract malalignment, and the use of blocking screws in proximal third tibia fractures can improve alignment by decreasing the effective diameter of the tibial medullary space.59

Although this review provides evidence that IMN has outcomes and complications similar to ORIF in TPF, it should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. The primary limitation of this study is that the populations in the included studies are heterogeneous, with different fracture classification makeups and constructs evaluated, which limits the generalizability of these findings. In addition, some studies assessed ipsilateral tibial plateau and shaft fractures, which represent a distinct injury pattern compared with isolated TPFs and introduce heterogeneity and may confound interpretation of outcomes attributable solely to tibial plateau fixation. However, excluding these studies would substantially underrepresent the clinical scenarios in which IMN is currently applied to address metaphyseal–diaphyseal dissociation and proximal segment stability, which conceptually overlap with indications for IMN fixation in complex tibial plateau fractures.

Another limitation of this study is that the same lead author published 3 of the 9 studies.16–18 Also, only one study that met the inclusion criteria compared IMN with ORIF with plate-only constructs for the same classification of TPFs. In addition, 3 studies used hybrid fixation with IMN and ORIF. Another limitation is that 6 studies were retrospective, which may have introduced selection bias. Particularly, differences in outcomes between groups 1 and 3 and group 2 likely reflect fracture complexity and surgeon selection rather than the effect of supplemental plating alone.5 Group 2 constructs were possibly more frequently used for more “difficult” (and potentially higher-energy) bicondylar Schatzker V to VI injuries or OTA/AO 41C3 patterns, and fractures with significant articular instability.5 Also, these more complex fractures could have been treated with ORIF alone and thus been excluded from evaluation altogether. In contrast, groups 1 and 3 constructs might be preferentially used in fracture patterns amenable to percutaneous articular reduction and fixation. As a result, direct comparison of outcomes between groups should be interpreted with caution.

5. Conclusion

The studies evaluated here show similar or improved clinical and radiographic outcomes, and comparable complication rates, between IMN and ORIF for the fixation of TPFs. Overall, IMN has been shown to achieve equal rates of bony union, support early postoperative weight bearing, and result in a greater range of motion after surgery. Furthermore, patients with IMN alone had lower infection and soft tissue reconstruction rates. Although IMN can be a safe and effective treatment option for some TPFs, current studies are heterogeneous, and the level of evidence is limited, preventing a definitive definition of inclusion and exclusion criteria. More prospective studies are needed to compare IMN with ORIF in TPFs with similar fracture patterns.
