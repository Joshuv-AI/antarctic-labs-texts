Active surveillance and antimicrobial prophylaxis to prevent hyperammonemia syndrome in lung transplant recipients: A single center cohort study
Authors: Drew W Charles, Sara Lee, Ananth Charya, Julie Riggs, Robert M Reed, Michael Eberlein, Kapil K Saharia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHyperammonemia syndrome (HS) is a life-threatening complication primarily affecting lung transplant recipients (LTR). HS is strongly associated with mollicute infection. We implemented an active surveillance and prophylaxis protocol for HS in LTR. Herein, we evaluate the impact of our protocol on rates and outcomes of HS.MethodsThis was a single-center retrospective cohort study of adult LTR performed from 1/1/2019 to 6/6/2024. Our surveillance and prophylaxis protocol was implemented on 8/1/2022 and included PCR testing for mollicutes on post-operative bronchoalveolar lavage samples, measurement of serum ammonia levels, and azithromycin prophylaxis. Comparisons between pre- and post-protocol implementation groups were performed.Results151 patients received a lung transplant during the study period, 54 (35.8%) following protocol implementation. Mollicute testing was performed in 57 LTR with most tests sent after protocol implementation (87.0% vs 10.3%, p<0.0001). Ammonia levels were checked in 53.7%; however, only 24.1% had levels checked by post-operative day 3. Ten LTR (6.6%) were diagnosed with mollicute infection, six in the pre-protocol and four in the post-protocol arm, with 70% of infections due to Mycoplasma spp. All LTR received antimicrobial prophylaxis following protocol implementation. Pre-protocol, 3.1% of LTR were diagnosed with definite HS compared to 0% in the post-protocol arm, despite 4 patients being diagnosed with mollicute infection.ConclusionsImplementing a mollicute screening and prophylaxis protocol reduced cases of HS in LTR despite some LTR developing mollicute infection. Antimicrobial prophylaxis with azithromycin and doxycycline may further optimize LTR prophylaxis. Larger studies are needed to determine optimal screening and prophylaxis strategies for HS in LTR.Keywords: Lung transplant, Hyperammonemia Syndrome, Mollicute Infection, Prophylaxis, Ureaplasma, Mycoplasma

Introduction

Hyperammonemia syndrome (HS) is a rare complication affecting immunocompromised hosts, particularly lung transplant recipients (LTR). It is characterized by progressive neurologic dysfunction in the setting of elevated serum ammonia levels and normal liver enzymes. It can result in seizures, cerebral edema, and death.1 The incidence of HS after lung transplantation is estimated between 1–5% with mortality rates approaching 75%.2, 3, 4 In LTR, HS is associated with infection by urease-producing mollicutes, which are believed to be transmitted from lung donors whose airways are colonized by urogenital mollicutes such as Mycoplasma hominis, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Ureaplasma parvum.5 Airway colonization is purported to result from oral-genital sexual practices with subsequent aspiration of mollicutes into the respiratory tract.6 This mechanism is supported by studies identifying young donor age and sexual activity as risk factors for donor-derived mollicute infection.7, 8

Mollicutes are fastidious organisms which lack a cell wall and are difficult to detect using standard bacterial culture techniques; special agar media is required and isolation of mollicutes can be labor intensive. Hence, mollicute culture may not be performed at many medical centers. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays have been utilized more recently for detecting infection in donor bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples. While PCR assays may have similar sensitivity compared to culture, mollicute culture has a higher positive predictive value for identifying LTR with more serious mollicute infections.9

Despite the high morbidity and mortality associated with HS, no guidelines exist for HS screening or prevention, and peri-transplant screening for HS is variable.2, 8, 10 A recent survey of lung transplant programs found that fewer than half of the lung transplant centers surveyed perform mollicute screening or prophylaxis, and among those who did, there was considerable variability in these practices.11 However, centers that have implemented screening and prophylaxis for HS among LTR have reported a reduction in cases of HS.7

Following two cases of severe HS among LTR at our institution, we instituted a HS and mollicute screening and prophylaxis protocol. Herein we describe the impact of this protocol on rates of HS and severe mollicute infection and aim to contribute to the growing scientific literature on the impact of screening and prophylaxis for HS on outcomes of LTR.

Methods

Procedures

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study of adult LTR performed from 1/1/2019 to 6/6/2024. Prior to 8/1/2022, there was no formal protocol for HS or mollicute screening. Ammonia levels and mollicute testing were performed if there was clinical suspicion for HS or severe mollicute infection.

Beginning 8/1/2022, LTR were required to have a BAL sample sent for Urogenital Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma Species PCR (ARUP Laboratories, test code 2011172) within the first seven days post-transplant. Although we recommended administering levofloxacin to lung donors prior to organ procurement as prophylaxis against mollicutes, we were unable to verify if this was consistently implemented for all donors following protocol initiation. Adult LTR at our center routinely undergo bronchoscopy within the first 24–72hr following lung transplantation to evaluate airways and anastomoses. BAL samples are routinely obtained for bacterial, fungal, and acid-fast bacilli cultures and respiratory viral testing.

Additionally, we recommended measurement of serum ammonia levels by post-operative day 3 with frequency of repeat levels at the discretion of the clinical team. We implemented universal prophylaxis against mollicute infection with azithromycin which was continued through post-transplant day 5 or until Ureaplasma/Mycoplasma PCR returned negative. If azithromycin could not be administered, either because of prior intolerance or pre-existing QTc interval prolongation, doxycycline or minocycline were substituted. LTR with positive PCR tests for Ureaplasma/Mycoplasma were started on dual therapy with doxycycline and levofloxacin (or ciprofloxacin) for 14 days or until otherwise specified by the infectious disease consult team.

Outcomes and data collection

The primary outcome was the development of HS. For the purpose of this study, definite HS was defined as presence of mollicutes detected in microbiologic studies, new onset altered mental status without any other identifiable cause, and a concurrent serum ammonia level of ≥100 μmol/L. Probable HS was defined as the presence of mollicutes plus either altered mental status or serum ammonia level of >100 μmol/L. Disseminated mollicute infection was defined as mollicute detection outside of the pulmonary parenchyma.

We collected basic demographic and clinical variables including recipient gender, age at time of transplant, baseline pulmonary disease necessitating lung transplantation, induction and maintenance immunosuppressive regimens. Among LTR with mollicute infection, we collected data on outcomes of infection and antimicrobial therapy. Donor data was reviewed in UNOS DonorNet only for LTR with mollicute infection. Donor variables of interest included gender, age, cause of death, high-risk sexual behavior, and illicit substance use.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical variables were reported as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons between groups were performed using Chi-square or Fischer’s exact test for categorical variables and Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U for continuous variables.

Median age was 64 years and 58.9% were male. Most patients received a bilateral lung transplantation (84.8%) and the most common indication for lung transplantation was interstitial lung disease or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (65.6%). A total of ten LTR, representing 6.6% of the study population, were diagnosed with a mollicute infection. Of these, six cases occurred in the pre-protocol cohort and four cases in the post-protocol cohort (Table 2). Mollicute infection was identified by PCR testing in nine of the ten cases. In one case, the infection was detected by culture, and this result was confirmed by PCR from a subsequent bronchoalveolar lavage sample. Additionally, two patients who were initially diagnosed by PCR later had the same mollicute species detected by culture in follow-up samples. The majority of mollicute infections were due to Mycoplasma species (70%, 7/10); however, two of those cases were co-infected with Ureaplasma species. Combination therapy with doxycycline and a fluoroquinolone was administered to 9 patients with mollicute infection while one patient received doxycycline alone (Table 2). Therapy was administered for a median of 18 days (IQR: 14–35 days).

Following protocol implementation, all LTR received antimicrobial prophylaxis (48 received azithromycin, 6 received either doxycycline or minocycline) for a median of 5 days (IQR: 5–6 days). Nineteen patients (19/54) stopped prophylaxis before mollicute PCR results were available. Among this subgroup, prophylaxis was administered for a median of 5 days and stopped at a median of 1 day before PCR results were available. The turnaround time for mollicute PCR testing was 3 days for the entire post-protocol cohort. In the subgroup of patients who discontinued prophylaxis before PCR results were available, the median turnaround time for mollicute PCR testing was 4 days (IQR 3–6 days).

Two patients had azithromycin prophylaxis discontinued because of prolonged QTc intervals. Both patients were switched to doxycycline prophylaxis and completed therapy without further complications. We did not appreciate any increased risk of C. difficile colitis in the first 30 days post-lung transplant in our post-protocol cohort.

Ammonia screening

Ammonia levels were not measured prior to protocol implementation in 68% of cases (66/97). Following protocol implementation, ammonia levels were checked in 53.7% of LTR (29/54), however, only 24.1% (13/54) had levels checked within 3 days of lung transplantation. Median time from transplant to first ammonia level was 4 days (IQR, 3–7 days). Ammonia levels were <50 μmol/L in all LTR who had levels checked following protocol implementation. In contrast, 4.1% of LTR (4/97) had ammonia levels >100 μmol/L within the first month after lung transplant in the pre-protocol period.

Hyperammonemia syndrome and disseminated mycoplasma/ureaplasma infection

Pre-protocol, 3.1% of LTR (3/97) were diagnosed with definite hyperammonemia syndrome at a median of 14 days from lung transplantation. One patient was infected with Ureaplasma species, and two patients were infected with both Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma species. Two patients with HS developed cerebral edema and severe neurologic dysfunction. Both had care withdrawn as neither patient made any meaningful neurologic recovery (case 48 and 84, Table 3). A third patient with HS had poor mental status on post-transplant day 13 but no abnormal neuroimaging findings and eventually had an improvement in neurologic status (case 97, Table 3). The median time from lung transplant to peak ammonia level among these three patients was 14 days (IQR: 10–15 days) (Table 3). One patient had an ammonia level >100 μmol/L and altered mental status but did not have mollicutes detected. In our post-protocol cohort, 0% of LTR (0/54) were diagnosed with HS, despite four having mollicute infection.

Disseminated Ureaplasma or Mycoplasma species infection was diagnosed in four LTR, with two cases occurring after protocol implementation. In both cases, infection was due to M. hominis and azithromycin prophylaxis was stopped after 5 days but before mollicute PCR test results were available. Both patients received dual therapy with doxycycline and levofloxacin for at least 21 days followed by 6 to 9 weeks of doxycycline monotherapy. Despite having disseminated mollicute infection, neither of these two patients developed HS.

We reviewed donor characteristics of the ten LTR with mollicute infection and found nine donors were under 40 years of age, eight donors were sexually active, with six of the eight either engaging in high-risk sexual behavior or using illicit substances (Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective, single-center, cohort study, we sought to evaluate the impact of implementing a HS screening and prophylaxis protocol on the rates and outcomes of HS among LTR. We found that near universal screening and antimicrobial prophylaxis for mollicutes reduced rates of HS from 3% to 0%. While compliance with antimicrobial prophylaxis (azithromycin) targeting mollicutes was 100%, and 87% of LTR had appropriate surveillance for mollicute infection after transplantation, only 24.1% (13/54) of LTR had serum ammonia levels drawn within 72 h of lung transplantation. During our study period, there were no significant changes to either our induction or maintenance immunosuppression protocols.

Compliance with this protocol was challenging due to the introduction of multiple interventions and need to coordinate interventions amongst a multidisciplinary group of health care providers including transplant surgeons, transplant pulmonologists, and intensivists. We were able to achieve universal compliance with antimicrobial prophylaxis due to integration of this intervention into the postoperative care electronic order set. The majority of LTR (35/54, 64.8%) remained on prophylaxis until mollicute PCR results returned. In the remaining 19 LTR (35.2%) prophylaxis was stopped on average one day before mollicute PCR testing results were available, because of a longer turn-around-time of the test.

Low compliance with ammonia level screening likely reflected limited awareness of this intervention among the clinical care team and lack of integration into the post-operative electronic order set. In our protocol, ammonia screening also received less emphasis than universal prophylaxis and mollicute screening. Implementing a standardized HS protocol order set may improve adherence by reducing variability in ordering practices associated with changes in clinical care team composition. Some groups have recommended serial ammonia testing for HS screening as it may enable earlier diagnosis of HS and potentially lower mortality.11 However, patients with HS often experience a rapid rise in ammonia levels and typically present with neurological symptoms by the time elevated ammonia levels are detected. Thus, frequent ammonia testing would be required early after transplant, often more than once daily, with rapid implementation of measures to reduce ammonia in patients with rising levels. Following this quality review, we re-educated clinical team members regarding the need for ammonia screening within 72 h after lung transplant and incorporated ammonia screening into the post-transplant order set.

We achieved 87% compliance with mollicute PCR screening following protocol implementation. There were seven protocol deviations related to mollicute PCR screening. Six deviations were due to Mycoplasma pneumoniae PCR being ordered from BAL samples rather than Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma species PCR. These deviations occurred within the first few months of protocol implementation. With appropriate education and standardized ordering, we have achieved 100% compliance with mollicute PCR screening over the past one year. The seventh protocol deviation was due to delayed testing. This stemmed from post-surgical complications requiring multiple intra-operative interventions over the first week post-transplantation.

When implementing a universal prophylaxis protocol, it is essential to weigh the potential adverse effects of antimicrobial use, especially when the majority of patients (50/54, 92.6%) received antibiotics despite ultimately not having mollicute infection, thus deriving no potential therapeutic benefit. In our cohort, azithromycin prophylaxis was generally well tolerated, with only two patients experiencing QTc interval prolongation leading to treatment discontinuation. Azithromycin is known to increase the QTc interval which can predispose patients to arrhythmias. Both patients developed QTc prolongation after two doses of azithromycin; however, each was receiving concomitant medications that could also contribute to QTc interval prolongation, including loop diuretics, azole antifungals, and fluoroquinolones.12 Thus, it is unclear whether azithromycin alone was responsible. Both patients were successfully transitioned from azithromycin to doxycycline prophylaxis, after which their QTc intervals improved without any arrhythmic events.

Antimicrobial use is associated with increased risk of C. difficile colitis, however the risk for C. difficile colitis is generally lower with azithromycin compared to other antimicrobials.13 In our post-protocol period, we did not observe an increased incidence of C. difficile colitis.

Given the low rates of mollicute infection (4/54, 7.4%), an alternative to universal prophylaxis could be implementing a test and treat strategy. While this strategy would reduce unnecessary antimicrobial exposure and decrease risk of side effects associated with antimicrobial prophylaxis, it would introduce a delay in treatment initiation. Patients with HS often experience a rapid increase in ammonia levels coinciding with a sudden decline in neurologic function. Any potential delay in therapy could negatively impact patient outcome.

Our rates of HS among LTR prior to protocol initiation (3%) are in line with previously published reports.2, 3, 4 HS was diagnosed within two weeks of lung transplantation and mean peak ammonia level was 332.7 μmol/L, consistent with findings from a recent systematic review, which found the mean time to presentation of HS was 11 days and mean ammonia level at diagnosis was 326 μmol/L.14 Despite aggressive measures to treat HS, which resulted in a rapid decline in ammonia levels, two patients developed severe neurologic dysfunction and died within 1 year of transplant.

Several studies have reported young donor age and sexual activity as risk factors for donor airway colonization with mollicutes.7, 15 We reviewed donor characteristics of the ten LTR with mollicute infection and found nine donors were under 40 years of age while eight donors were sexually active, with six of the eight either engaging in high risk sexual behavior or using illicit drugs (Table 3).

While rates of HS decreased in the post-protocol period, rates of mollicute infection among our LTR did not differ in the pre-protocol and post-protocol period (6.2% vs 7.4%). Our prophylaxis protocol mainly targeted lung transplant recipients which would not impact donor airway colonization. Some groups have suggested administering mollicute directed antimicrobials to lung donors prior to organ procurement to disrupt transmission to recipients.16 However, the optimal dosing strategy is not known and there may be insufficient time to administer antimicrobials to donors in advance of organ procurement. While we recommended administering levofloxacin to lung donors prior to organ procurement as prophylaxis against mollicutes, we could not verify if this was consistently implemented for all donors following protocol initiation.

While mollicutes were detected by PCR in several LTR following protocol implementation, no LTR developed HS. This could be related to universal azithromycin prophylaxis in our LTR. We chose azithromycin prophylaxis because earlier studies reported a stronger association of elevated ammonia levels and HS with Ureaplasma spp and known susceptibility of Ureaplasma spp to macrolides.8, 14 However, in our post-protocol implementation cohort, only one LTR had infection with Ureaplasma spp., the other three were infected with M hominis, which is intrinsically resistant to macrolides.17 Therefore, our prophylaxis protocol may not be solely responsible for the reduction in HS cases.

Another reason we observed cases of mollicute infection without HS could be our protocol employed PCR-testing for detection of mollicute infection. A recent study found mollicute PCR testing had a lower positive predictive value for severe infection and HS when compared to mollicute culture.9 This could be because mollicute PCR positivity may reflect detection of nonviable organisms or low organism burden, neither of which would pose risk of a clinically significant infection. However, early screening for mollicutes by PCR could lead to earlier detection and interventions to reduce organism burden thereby decreasing the risk of hyperammonemia. In fact, our protocol called for immediate treatment with dual antimicrobial therapy for any LTR who tested positive for mollicutes by PCR. We opted to use mollicute PCR testing rather than mollicute culture for several reasons. First, mollicute culture was not available at our center, and our reference laboratory does not accept BAL specimens for culture in adult patients. Furthermore, logistical barriers limited the feasibility of culture, including the need for special transport media and stringent criteria for sample transport and shipment (ie. dry ice).

Finally, not all LTR with mollicute infection develop HS, indicating that certain host factors may also contribute to the accumulation of ammonia. One theory is that the physiological stress of lung transplantation may induce a catabolic state unmasking a glutamine synthetase deficiency.18 Another potential contributor is the development of acute kidney injury, which occurs at a high rate after lung transplantation, resulting in decreased urinary excretion of ammonia.19

One notable finding from our study was that despite mollicute screening and prophylaxis two LTR still developed disseminated mollicute infection. Disseminated mollicute infection post-protocol implementation was observed only in LTR with mollicute detected by PCR; no disseminated infection was identified in LTR who screened negative for mollicutes by PCR, suggesting that mollicute PCR has a high negative predicative value. Both cases of disseminated mollicute infection post-protocol occurred in individuals in whom prophylaxis was discontinued prior to mollicute PCR results becoming available. Case 9 had a delay in obtaining BAL for mollicute PCR because of post-operative complications requiring several return trips to the operating room; Ureaplasma/Mycoplasma PCR testing was eventually sent on post-operative day 10. Case 10 had an indeterminate value on initial BAL mollicute PCR testing with discontinuation of prophylaxis on post-operative day 5. A second BAL sample sent for Ureaplasma/Mycoplasma PCR on POD 9 returned positive. In both cases M. hominis was detected which is intrinsically resistant to macrolides.20 Given these findings, we modified our prophylaxis protocol to include both azithromycin and doxycycline to better target M. hominis, and prophylaxis is now continued until there is a negative mollicute PCR test from BAL. One potential strategy to avoid diagnostic delays would be to perform back-table lavage of donor lungs and submit lavage fluid for mollicute testing. We elected not to implement this approach for several reasons. First, mollicute PCR testing is not performed at our center and requires special orders, which are difficult to coordinate with intraoperative specimens, particularly when lung transplants occur outside of regular business hours. Second, back-table lavage adds complexity to an already intricate surgery. Finally, most lung transplant recipients at our center undergo bronchoscopy for airway evaluation within 48 h of transplantation, during which BAL samples are obtained for mollicute PCR testing. This practice avoids meaningful delays while allowing closer coordination of testing with the multidisciplinary team.

While we did not formally evaluate the cost-effectiveness of implementing this prophylaxis and screening protocol, the cost of PCR testing ($196 per test, ARUP) and antimicrobial prophylaxis with azithromycin appears low compared to the healthcare utilization costs for a case of HS (need for dialysis, neuroimaging studies, prolonged ICU and hospital stay, possible mechanical ventilatory support for airway protection). A formal cost-efficacy analysis, while beyond the scope of this study, would be important. One notable limitation of this study is that we introduced multiple interventions simultaneously, making it difficult to determine which components were more effective in reducing the rate of HS.

In conclusion we noted a reduction in cases of HS in LTR following implementation of a mollicute screening and prophylaxis protocol but continued to see cases of disseminated mollicute infection. This could be related to deficiencies in our prophylaxis protocol (monotherapy with azithromycin) which we have since optimized. Given that HS is rare, larger multicenter, prospective studies are needed to determine optimal screening and prophylaxis strategies for HS in lung transplant recipients.
