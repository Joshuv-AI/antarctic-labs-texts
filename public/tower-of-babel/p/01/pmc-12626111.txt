Assessment of Long‐Term Mild and Major Neurocognitive Disorders 48 Months After Cardiac Surgery
Authors: Mercedes Florido‐Santiago, Luis M Pérez‐Belmonte, José P Lara, Mercedes Millán‐Gómez, Miguel A Pérez‐Velasco, Julio Osuna‐Sánchez, Natalia García‐Casares, Ricardo Gómez‐Huelgas, Encarnación Blanco‐Reina, Miguel A Barbancho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo assess the incidence of both Mild and Major Neurocognitive Disorders (NCD), according to the DSM‐5 criteria, after cardiac surgery (off‐pump an on‐pump procedures) and to propose some risk associated factors.MethodsProspective and sequential study. Patients (n = 70, 65.9 years old, mean age) were evaluated for cognitive function (Neuronorma Battery) and daily life activities (Barthel Index and Bayer‐Activities of Daily Living Scale) before surgery and at 1, 6, 12, and 48 months postoperatively. Off‐pump (n = 36) and on‐pump (n = 34) patients groups were compared. DSM‐5 criteria for Mild NCD and Major NCD were applied. Independent associated risk factors were described.ResultsA 40.8% (off‐pump) and 39.3% (on‐pump) of patients met DSM‐5 criteria for Mild NCD 48 months after cardiac surgery and a further 7.1% (on‐pump) of them, for Major NCD. Clinical factors were associated with Mild NCD and both clinical and surgical factors, were associated with Major NCD.ConclusionA relevant incidence of long‐term Mild NCD and Major NCD has been described 48 months after cardiac surgery, more intense in the on‐pump group of patients. Assessment (identifying those with higher risk), prevention and treatment strategies for these patients should be provided. The DSM‐5 criteria to classify NCD could be applied to characterize any cognitive decline due to any disease or surgical procedure.Keywords: dementia, DSM‐5, heart surgery, mild and major neurocognitive disorders, postoperative neurocognitive disorder

Introduction

Aging population is more likely to present cognitive dysfunction. An increasing proportion of patients undergone surgical procedures are older adults. Postoperative Neurocognitive Disorders (PONCD) have become a relevant personal and public health problem [1]. The PONCD is defined as a postoperative decline in cognitive performance compared to preoperative baseline levels as measured via batteries of psychometric tests [2, 3]. It has been described as a frequent complication following heart surgery [4, 5]. Cardiovascular risk factors and other related to more extensive surgery, the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (on‐pump), and anesthesia have traditionally been associated with PONCD [5, 6]. In recent years, patient‐related factors such as old age and preexisting cerebral diseases have also been shown to be potential risk factors [5, 7, 8]. Although several predictors of PONCD have been identified, they have inconsistently been reported as significant and further research is needed [9, 10].

The understanding and management of PONCD has been hindered by the high degree of heterogeneity in the definitions of PONCD used across studies, the wide variety of tests used to assess the cognitive performance, the variable timing of testing, and the statistical methods used [5, 11, 12].

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders fifth edition (DSM‐5) has classified the neurocognitive disorders (NCD) into two categories: mild NCD and major NCD (both requiring ruling out delirium or other mental disorders) [13]. The DSM‐5 has been referred as the golden standard for diagnosing perioperative NCD proposing for its classification the denomination of Delayed Neurocognitive Recovery (< 1 month after surgery, until the expected recovery of the acute effects of hospitalization and surgery), Mild/Major NCD postoperative (1–12 months after surgery, indicating a temporal association with surgery and not accounting for by any other medical condition) and Mild/Major Neurocognitive Disorders (if continuing of further declining > 12 months after surgery) [1, 14]. The resulting cognitive declines can persist for months or years and causing a detrimental impact on self‐dependence, quality of life, risk of developing dementia, and even long‐term survival [1, 11].

Classification of Mild and Major NCD requires assessment of cognition and activities of daily living (ADL) with appropriate tools to measure subtle changes [13, 14]. No strategies for the detection of NCD have been established as part of the routine perioperative evaluation [6, 8, 15, 16]. They are necessary for providing comprehensive postoperative care as a complement to the routine clinical evaluation and provide a better approach to risk stratification, that is pivotal for prevention [17, 18, 19].

This work aims to characterize the presence of Mild and Major NCD (DSM‐5 criteria) 48 months after heart surgery (according to the use of cardiopulmonary bypass, off‐pump vs. on‐pump techniques) using conormalized tests for the neuropsychological assessment [20]. We have previously published data about the presence of PONCD 1, 6, and 12 months after heart surgery (although DSM‐5 criteria were not applied) [8, 15, 21]. The incidence of very long‐term NCD after cardiac surgery has been scarcely assessed and the presence of persistent PONCD may conduct to develop a dementia [22, 23, 24]. We hypothesize a high prevalence of NCD, been more severe in patients with on‐pump surgery. It is relevant to know the characteristics of these patients who may require psychological and medical care and to define the profiles of risk.

Methods

Study Sample and Clinical Data Collection

We conducted a prospective, sequential study on patients who underwent elective off‐pump heart surgery (coronary artery bypass grafting) or on‐pump heart surgery (concomitant aortic valve replacement and coronary artery grafting) at the Hospital Regional Universitario de Málaga and Hospital Clínico Universitario Virgen de la Victoria in Málaga (Spain) between May 2012 and December 2013. A wide range of sociodemographic, clinical, surgical, and anesthesia‐related variables was collected. The risk of in‐hospital death after heart surgery was determined (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation, EuroSCORE) [25].

Inclusion criteria: all patients who were scheduled for heart surgery were initially preselected to be included in this study. Exclusion criteria: patients with critical medical conditions, dependence of activities of daily living, diagnosis of dementia, hearing or visual impairment or low schooling that made not possible to complete the neurocognitive assessment and treatment at the current time or in the previous month with drugs with proven action on cognitive function. Follow‐up was standardized and conducted before surgery (baseline) and at 1, 6, 12, and 48 months postoperatively.”

Cognitive function of the patients was evaluated before surgery (4.8 ± 0.7 days) and at 1, 6, 12, and 48 months postoperatively. The neurocognitive assessment included six specific and conormalized tests for age and education included in the NEURONORMA battery [20]: the Trail Making Test (TMT) and Stroop Color‐Word Interference Test (SCWIT) for measuring attention‐executive functions, the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT) for memory, the Semantic Verbal Fluency Test (SVFT) and Phonemic Verbal Fluency Test (PVFT) for verbal fluency, and the Judgment of Line Orientation Test (JLOT) for visuospatial perception. ADL were also assessed using the Barthel Index (BI) and the Bayer‐Activities of Daily Living Scale (B‐ADL). The percentages of change in the performance of the cognitive tests for both groups after surgery with respect to preoperative values were determined. All perioperative assessments were conducted by trained nurses and physicians.

The presence of NCD was established according to the DSM‐5 definition [13]: mild NCD was defined as a neurocognitive decline of 1–2 standard deviations in cognitive performance in at least one cognitive domain without substantial interference in the independence of ADL and major NCD was defined as a neurocognitive decline of > 2 standard deviations in cognitive performance in at least one cognitive domain with interference in the independence of ADL. The presence of PONCD was defined as any significant worsening after surgery compared to the preoperative level.

The study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Málaga (Ethics Committee code: DCP‐2011) and written informed consent for the consultation of patient medical records was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measured Outcomes: Our primary endpoint was to assess the long‐term neurocognitive function, determining the presence of mild and major NCD after heart surgery according to the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (off‐pump vs. on‐pump) and comparing to the preoperative (baseline) cognitive performance. Secondary outcomes included the evaluation of possible sociodemographic, clinical and perioperative factors associated with NCD.

Statistical Analyses

Quantitative variables are shown as means ± standard deviation whereas qualitative variables are shown as absolute values and percentages. Quantitative variables were compared using Student's t‐test and the repeated measures analysis of variance whereas qualitative variables were compared using Pearson's chi‐square and McNemar's tests. Postoperative performance was compared to preoperative levels using repeated measures analysis of variance.

Multivariate logistic regression models were calculated and adjusted for confounding variables to identify independent risk factors of PONCD, determining the odds ratio (OD) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The overall significance of each model was evaluated using the likelihood ratio Chi‐square test. Model calibration was assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, where a p‐value > 0.05 was considered indicative of adequate fit. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corporation, Chicago, Illinois).

Discussion

This study described that a 40% of patients who underwent heart surgery (either off‐pump or on‐pump procedures) met criteria for Mild NCD 48 months after that surgery and that a further 7% of patients on the on‐pump group met criteria for Major NCD. To our knowledge, this is the first study to classify the postoperative NCD after heart surgery with the DSM‐5 criteria [13], as recommended [1, 14]. Interestingly, several cardiovascular risk factors and the presence of PONCD at 1 month of follow‐up were associated with long‐term Mild NCD whereas both surgical and clinical factors were associated with long‐term Major NCD.

PONCD following heart surgery is a major concern in real‐world postoperative clinical care [1, 7, 16]. Aside from its association with adverse outcomes during the early postoperative period—including increased mortality—the presence of PONCD has also been linked to long‐term outcomes such as reduced quality of life and deterioration in ADL or the possibility of developing dementia [19, 22]. A recent meta‐analysis has shown that patients undergoing cardiac and noncardiac surgeries had poorer outcomes and an increased risk of premature death [11].

The understanding and management of PONCD has been hindered by the high degree of heterogeneity in the definitions of PONCD used across studies, the wide variety of tests to assess the cognitive performance, the variable timing of testing and the statistical methods used [3, 11, 12]. Moreover, PONCD is not formally described in the DSM‐5 [13] or the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD‐10) [26].

In this study, we have applied the consensus recommendation criteria [1, 14] regarding the timing of testing and categorization of PONCD following heart surgery: a cognitive decline diagnosed up to 30 days after a procedure should be called “delayed neurocognitive recovery”; if observed between 30 days and 12 months, “Mild or Major NCD postoperative” and if observed 12 months after surgery, just “Mild or Major NCD”. If the PONCD is first diagnosed 12 months after surgery without prospective follow‐up, its etiology cannot reasonably be linked to the (prior heart surgery) [14].

In this study, the PONCD remained significant up to 48 months after heart surgery; about a 40% of patients of both groups had Mild NCD and 7% had Major NCD. There is very limited evidence on the presence of very long term PONCD after surgery [22, 23, 24] with different design and methodology that makes difficult comparative approaches. The prevalence of dementia at 7.5 years after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery was greatly increased (31% of patients) suggesting that impaired cognition before surgery or the presence of cardiovascular disease may contribute to the high prevalence [22]; the diagnosis of dementia was made by expert opinion on review of performance on the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale and there was no data regarding different surgical procedures. In our study, the proportion of patients with Major NCD 4 years after surgery was minor. In the second study, the effect of off‐pump and on‐pump surgery on long‐term cognitive and cardiac outcomes was compared in low‐risk patients undergoing CABG surgery; they conclude that avoiding the use of cardiopulmonary bypass had no effect on 5‐year cognitive (any cognitive decline) or cardiac outcomes [23]; it contrasts with our results where the proportion of Mild NCD was similar between both groups whilst Major NCD was only present in the on‐pump group. In the third study [24], only cognition was evaluated in patients who underwent CABG; the incidence of any cognitive decline was 42% at five years but there was no comparison between different surgical procedures.

The etiology of perioperative NCDs is multifactorial and includes predisposing and precipitating factors; identifying these risk factors may conduct to preoperative risk stratification and perioperative risk reduction [1, 27]. Although many predictors of PONCD have been described ‐up to 61 of them‐, they have inconsistently been reported [1, 9, 10]. Age has emerged as the most common predictor of cognitive outcome after cardiac surgery; older patients and those with lower educational levels should be prioritized when developing and trialing interventions to improve cognitive function [9]. In a previous study, we identified age was as a risk factor for NOCD 12 months after heart surgery [21].

When studying these risk factors, it is relevant to differentiate between both patient‐related and surgery‐related factors [28]. Interestingly, we found that Mild NCD was associated with patient‐related factors: history of smoking, presence of diabetes mellitus, peripheral arterial disease, hypertension, triple vessel coronary artery disease, Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina grade and the presence of PONCD at 1 month of follow‐up. Major NCS was associated with both patient related factors (presence of heart failure, left ventricular hypertrophy, history of smoking, diabetes mellitus, and peripheral arterial disease) and surgery related factors (mean intraoperative oxygen saturation, intraoperative partial pressure of oxygen, use of cardiopulmonary bypass, and EuroSCORE).

It this context, the preoperative factors, especially cardiovascular disease and baseline cognition, may have more to do with postoperative cognitive outcome than surgical factors [14, 28]. Cardiovascular disease is a well‐established risk factor for cognitive impairment and dementia irrespective of surgery [29]. In two recent reviews, the physiopathology, clinical implications, and therapeutic considerations of the cognitive impairment in patients with heart failure has been described [30, 31]. Precisely, the presence of cardiovascular risk factors or established cardiovascular disease are especially relevant as predictors of short and long‐term PONCD [1, 7, 30, 32]. A preexisting cardiovascular disease may be the main driver of cognitive decline in these patients [28]. Recently, in the first‐time prospective study of the entire spectrum of vascular patients, nearly 4 of every 5 vascular surgery patients have undiagnosed cognitive impairment and having congestive heart failure was associated with a higher likelihood [33].

Our findings support the hypothesis that the high cardiovascular risk of the patients who undergo extensive heart surgery would entail an increased risk of PONCD due to surgery related factors, including the possibility of developing dementia 48 months after surgery. This could contribute to diffusely altering the brain's hemodynamic status, which could favor the development of PONCD that lasts for a longer period [22, 27, 30, 34].

Surgery‐related factors have traditionally been related to PONCD, linked to cerebral embolism, hypoperfusion, and inflammatory response after major heart surgery [8, 9, 10, 27]; although they have been associated more frequently with short‐term PONCD, may also participate in the NCD several months after surgery [6, 8, 9, 35]. In our study, as mentioned, some surgery‐related factors were associated with long‐term Major NCD.

The presence of cognitive impairment before surgery has been confirmed as an independent risk factor for PONCD [19, 24, 27]; a 24% of our participants presented Mild NCD preoperatively, with major proportion on the off‐pump group. However, short‐term PONCD has not been widely described as a predictor of long‐term PONCD [21, 27, 36]. In our study, the occurrence of PONCD 1 month after surgery was identified as an independent risk factor for very long‐term mild NCD. In other studies, POCD after CABG was proposed as an essential risk factor for long‐term deterioration and an indication for neuropsychological follow‐up, particularly in the domain of executive functioning [36] and it was an essential predictor for long‐term cognitive deterioration 6 years after surgery, particularly when executive functioning was affected [27].

The decline in independence for ADL is associated with PONCD [37, 38, 39]. In fact, the definition of major NCD includes impairment in the functional capacity for ADL [13]. We have described that following heart surgery, patients had a significant deterioration in the Barthel Index and the B‐ADL scale, a decline that was significantly greater in patients in the on‐pump group. It was observed that patients in both groups had progressively worse functional performances throughout the follow‐up period, although they did not reach a severe degree of dependence. These findings highlight the need for a combined perioperative long‐term neurocognitive and ADL evaluation to be included in routine clinical practice [34]. Although postoperative cognitive dysfunction is a relevant complication after surgery, its assessment is not realized in clinical practice; a brief domain‐specific cognitive evaluation was proposed for our group [15]. Early recognition of perioperative neurocognitive disorders in at‐risk patients may enable early intervention [11]. A recent review shows that motor‐cognitive dual task training is a potential approach to improve cognitive performance in individuals with coronary artery disease and/or heart failure [31].

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of our study include its prospective design, the comparison of two groups of patients with advanced cardiac disease and high cardio‐vascular risk who undergone different surgical procedures, the extensive clinical and procedural data collected, its adequate long‐term neurocognitive evaluation, and to our knowledge, it is the first study that have characterized the long‐term PONCD after heart surgery applying the DSM‐5 criteria.

This study has also several limitations. The limited number of patients recruited as well as the observational nature of our data might have led to possible unmeasured confounding factors. No clinical‐radiological correlations have been established due to a lack of neuroimaging tests (although they are not included in the DSM‐5 criteria). We did not provide a nonsurgical control subjects but we counts with the normative data of the Neuronorma Project consistent of a battery of conormalized tests adjusted for age an education [20].

Conclusions

The criteria of the DSM‐5 to classify the NCD into the categories of Mild and Major NCD has been very useful to characterize the PONCD after heart surgery. These criteria could be applied to any cognitive decline due to any disease or surgical procedure.

A relevant incidence of long‐term Mild NCD (associated with clinical factors) and Major NCD (associated with clinical and surgical factors) has been described 48 months after cardiac surgery, been more intense in the on‐pump group of patients.

A significant percentage of patients may develop dementia postoperatively. The adequate assessment before and after heart surgery and the identification of patients with high risk could be helpful to implement personal preventive interventions. Further research is necessary to provide more evidence.
