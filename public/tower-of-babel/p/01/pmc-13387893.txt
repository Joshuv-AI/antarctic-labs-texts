Spatial Regulation of Silicon Accumulation in Peduncle Confers Sheathed Spike in Barley
Authors: Wenxue Wu, Congcong Jiang, Guangqi Gao, Namiki Mitani‐Ueno, Yu Guo, Jinhong Kan, Yehui Xiong, Martin Mascher, Zhongfu Ni, Jian Feng Ma, Ke Wang, Ping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeduncle, the uppermost internode in cereals, connects the stem to the inflorescence and is critical for the transport of water, nutrients and photosynthetic assimilates. While peduncle length associates with plant height and its elongation is primarily regulated by phytohormones, we report a previously unrecognized mechanism involving the spatial distribution of silicon (Si). We identified a barley mutant, sheathed spike 2 (ss2), characterized by a specifically shortened peduncle that traps the spike within the flag leaf sheath. Positional cloning and analysis of allelic mutants revealed that the wild‐type SS2 gene encodes a putative silicon efflux transporter. SS2 is expressed throughout the lifecycle, with higher transcriptional levels in the rachis and stem internodes, and its encoded protein localizes to the plasma membrane. We demonstrate that SS2 is required for polarized Si partitioning. Unlike wild‐type plants, which ultimately deposit Si in spikes, the ss2 mutant exhibits an 8‐fold increase in Si accumulation in the peduncle and a significant increase in the flag leaf. Hydroponic experiments without Si supply restored normal peduncle elongation in the ss2 mutant, demonstrating that local Si hyper‐accumulation directly inhibits elongation. The conserved role of SS2 was supported by diversity analysis across barley and common wheat, as well as by the similar sheathed spike phenotype in tetraploid wheat lines carrying non‐functional SS2 homologues. Collectively, our findings uncover an evolutionarily conserved, silicon‐dependent mechanism that regulates peduncle elongation and spike emergence in Triticeae crops like barley and wheat.

Introduction

The stem of cereal plants consists of multiple internodes interconnected by nodes containing vascular bundles. Stem serves dual physiological functions on providing mechanical support for the inflorescence, and facilitating the transport of water, root‐absorbed nutrients, and photosynthetic assimilates from leaves and stem itself to developing grains (McKim 2019). The peduncle, defined as the uppermost internode of stem, forms the critical junction between the lower stem and the inflorescence—specifically the panicle in rice ( Oryza sativa L.) and the spike in wheat ( Triticum aestivum L.) and barley ( Hordeum vulgare L.). Peduncle length exhibits significant agronomic implications due to its pleiotropic functions. Increased peduncle length enhances photoassimilate allocation to the inflorescence while simultaneously reducing humidity‐related fungal infection risks. Conversely, moderate peduncle shortening can improve lodging resistance through height reduction. This trade‐off makes peduncle length optimization as a crucial target for plant architecture improvement and yield stabilization. A well‐documented example is the Green Revolution's semi‐dwarf varieties, where the application of lodging‐resistant cultivars carrying dwarfing genes (e.g., Rht‐B1b, Rht‐D1b and sd1) led to substantial yield increases in cereal crops (Peng et al. 1999; Sasaki et al. 2002). These cultivars typically exhibit uniform reduction of all internodes (including the peduncle) and are associated with certain penalties, such as impaired nitrogen use efficiency (Sun et al. 2014). Current understand of stem elongation mechanisms in cereals primarily involves phytohormone homeostasis (Sasaki et al. 2002; Chai et al. 2022; Xiong et al. 2022) and signalling pathways (Peng et al. 1999; Liu et al. 2025).

Spatial‐specific regulators offer the potential for targeted modification of peduncle length without affecting other internodes. In rice, the Elongated Uppermost Internode (EUI) phenotype results from mutations in a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase gene, which involves in gibberellin (GA4) inactivation through 16α, 17‐epoxidation (Luo et al. 2006; Zhu et al. 2006). The homeodomain‐leucine zipper transcription factor HOX12 regulates peduncle elongation by physically interacting with the EUI1 promoter, to modulate its transcriptional activity (Gao et al. 2016). Another regulator, Shortened Uppermost Internode 1 (SUI1), encodes a putative phosphatidylserine synthase (PSS) family protein that influences peduncle elongation through GA‐independent pathways (Zhu et al. 2011). In Triticeae species such as wheat and barley, while several genetic loci associated with peduncle length variation have been mapped, most remain to be molecularly dissected (Sameri et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2023). The wheat pseudo‐response regulator (TaPRR95) gene has been proved to shorten the peduncle by down‐regulating photosynthetic genes and suppressing auxin signalling (Fu et al. 2024). In addition, another gene TaPL1 modulates peduncle elongation through transcriptional inhibiting of the BR‐related genes TaBle KI1‐A1/B1, which negatively regulate the BR receptor BRI1 (Liu et al. 2025). A dominant barley mutant, Sheathed Spike 1 (SS1), presents an interesting case for peduncle development studies, though the causal gene remains to be cloned (Pu et al. 2021).

Silicon (Si), a major soil element, is not classified as essential for plant growth but significantly enhances plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses (Ma 2004). Cereal plants have evolved specialized mechanisms for Si transport, including the phloem‐mobile protein Shoot‐Silicon‐Signal (SSS), which coordinates root Si uptake and shoot accumulation (Yamaji et al. 2024). The Si transport system involves multiple transporters that regulate Si uptake (Ma et al. 2006, 2011), xylem loading/unloading (Ma et al. 2007; Huang et al. 2022), and tissue deposition (Yamaji et al. 2008; Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023), encompassing both passive cytoplasmic influx and active cellular efflux. In rice, seven distinct silicon transporters have been characterized (Yamaji et al. 2015). The influx transporters Lsi1 localizes on the plasma membrane at the distal side of root exodermis/endodermis cells (Ma et al. 2006), while its homologue Lsi6 localizes in the leaf xylem parenchyma cells (Yamaji et al. 2008). The efflux transport system includes five silicon efflux transporters (SIET): Lsi2/SIET1 that localizes to the proximal side of root exodermis/endodermis cells (Ma et al. 2007), Lsi3/SIET2 that shows non‐polarity localization in root pericycle cells (Huang et al. 2022), and SIET3 to SIET5 (Yamaji et al. 2015). A recent study has demonstrated the critical role of SIET4 in mediating Si transport from mesophyll cells to leaf epidermis for deposition (Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023). Comparative analysis of Si transporters in barley orthologs (e.g., HvLsi1 and HvLsi2‐like) revealed conserved transportation abilities but divergent cellular localization patterns and membrane polarities (Chiba et al. 2009; Mitani et al. 2009; Yamaji et al. 2012), suggesting species‐specific adaptations in Si transport.

Through a forward genetics approach, this study identified a barley mutant ss2, exhibiting shortened peduncle and sheathed spike (ss) phenotype. This morphological alternation results from a deficiency in a vascular tissue‐preferentially expressed gene HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407900, which is the homologue of rice silicon efflux transporter gene SIET4. The mutant has Si over‐deposition in the peduncle due to disrupted Si transport to the spikes. Elucidating the cross‐talk between tissue‐specific Si deposition and peduncle elongation offers mechanistic insights into conserved processes governing plant morphogenesis and environmental adaptation.

Results

Characterization and Map‐Based Cloning of ss2

Two independent mutants ‘M4081’ and ‘M4950’ were identified from a previously established mutagenesis population (Jiang et al. 2022). Both mutants exhibited a sheathed spike (ss) phenotype, characterized by mature spike being enclosed within the flag leaf sheath and a significant reduction in plant height compared to the wild‐type plant (Figure 1A–C). The sheathed spike phenotype is attributed to an approximately 60% reduction in peduncle length (the uppermost internode, IND1) (Figure 1D), which leads to decreased spike exsertion below the auricle of flag leaf (Figure 1A,E). Shortened peduncle in both mutants contributed to an overall reduction in plant height of approximately 27% (Figure S1A,B). Longitudinal section analysis of the peduncle at maturity revealed a significant decrease in cell length in the mutants (Figure 1F,G). Reciprocal crosses between the wild‐type and each mutant produced F1 plants with long peduncles and exserted spikes (Figure S2A). Phenotypic segregation analysis in the F2 populations revealed a 3:1 ratio of wild‐type to ss plants (Figure S2B). These results indicated the ss phenotype in both ‘M4081’ and ‘M4950’ is controlled by a single recessive mutation, respectively. F1 plants derived from crosses between the two mutants exhibited the ss phenotype (Figure S2A), confirming that the two independent mutants are allelic at the ss locus. Given that a dominant sheathed spike mutant SS1 has been previously mapped (Pu et al. 2021), the recessive mutation identified from this study represents a novel genetic locus, designated ss2.

Through whole genome sequencing (WGS) of the two allelic ss2 mutants, 116 and 158 SNPs causing non‐synonymous mutations (including missense, premature stop codon, and frame‐shift variants) were identified in ‘M4081’ and ‘M4950’, respectively (Figure 1H and Tables S1 and S2). Three genes HORVU.MOREX.r3.2HG0201180, HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407900, and HORVU.MOREX.r3.5HG0519480 were detected with non‐synonymous mutations simultaneously in both mutants. The bulked segregants analysis (BSA) using the ‘M4950 × HTX’ F2 population through high‐throughput sequencing revealed a unique deviation peak of the allele frequency on the long arm of chromosome 4H, spanning an association interval of ca. 52 Mb (Figure 1I). Refined linkage mapping delimited the target interval to approx. 12.3 Mb (4H: 569092943 to 581 389 829 bp), where only one SNP with non‐synonymous variation was maintained applying ∆SNP index > 0.5 (Table S3). This SNP in ‘M4950’ resulted in a Leu520Gln substitution in HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407900, while a Ser546Phe substitution in the same gene was detected in ‘M4081’ (Table S2). Both missense mutations were considered to be causal for the ss2 phenotype.

Functional Validation in Barley and Durum Wheat

To determine whether this gene regulates peduncle elongation, we identified 14 independent mutations on HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407900 using Targeting Induced Local Lesions In Genomes (TILLING) from the ‘HTX’ mutagenesis population (Table S4). Five mutations occurred in non‐coding regions or resulted in synonymous within the coding region, while the remaining nine, including ‘M4950’ and ‘M4081’, resulted in either premature stop codons or missense alterations, and homozygous M4 plants derived from these lines were phenotyped. Compared to the wild‐type ‘HTX’, eight of the nine non‐synonymous mutants, except ‘M9957’, exhibited significant reduction in peduncle length, either fully or partially enclosed spikes, as well as reduced plant height (Figure 2A–C and Figure S3A). These results demonstrate that the sheathed spike phenotype is attributable to the deficiency of the gene HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407900 (designated SS2).

We further investigated the SS2 orthologous genes in durum wheat. Two independent mutations resulting in premature variants of gene TRITD4Av1G226940 and TRITD4Bv1G179540 in A‐ and B‐subgenome were identified from induced mutants of variety ‘Kronos’ (Krasileva et al. 2017), which were carried by the mutant ‘K2085’ (aaBB) and the mutant ‘K2179’ (AAbb), respectively (Figure 2D). Following the cross combination of the two mutants and marker‐assisted selection of the segregated F2 individuals, a double mutant at the A‐ and B‐ subgenomes (aabb) was obtained (Figure S3B). The double mutant showed significantly reduced peduncle length and plant height (Figure 2D and Figure S3C), and had a sheathed spike as observed in barley ss2 mutants. The single mutant along with the wild‐type ‘Kronos’ showed similar performance on their peduncle length and plant height, and all these three genotypes had spikes exserted from the flag leaf sheath (Figure 2D). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the functions of SS2 and its orthologs are conserved in Triticeae species including barley and durum wheat, ensuring programmed normal elongation of their peduncle.

Molecular Characterization of SS2

HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407900 was annotated encoding a Na(+)/H(+) anti‐porter containing 572 amino acids in the barley reference genome ‘MorexV3’ (Mascher et al. 2021). Conserved domain searching analysis predicted that the SS2 protein consists of 11 transmembrane helices, and the eight non‐synonymous mutations were found within different protein domains, including transmembrane helices, intra‐membrane loop, and extra‐membrane loop (Figure 2A). Phylogenetic analysis identified SS2 as the orthologous gene of Os03G0147400 in rice (Figure 3A), a silicon (Si) efflux transporter SIET4 (Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023). Both genes have their encoded amino acid identity exhibiting 98.4% at the transmembrane helices but only ca. 70% at the large intra‐membrane loop (Figure S4), implying possible functional diversification in both crops.

We investigated the expression of barley SS2 in multiple tissues at three developmental stages. Its transcripts were detectable in all samples tested throughout the lifecycle, with higher abundance in leaf sheath (SH) at the tillering stage, and in rachis and internodes at the booting stage (Figure 3B). We also generated transgenic plants harbouring an SS2pro:GUS reporter, to analyse the tissue localization of SS2 protein. The GUS staining was observed apparently in all internodes, and presented in vascular bundles and parenchyma cells of each internode, as well as the epidermal cells in the upper internodes (IND1 and IND2; Figure 3C). SS2 promoter also exhibited activity in the flag leaf blade, flag leaf sheath, grain lemma/palea and roots, especially with higher activity in the first node and spike rachis (Figure 3C). The localization of SS2 protein on a subcellular level was examined via transient expression of SS2‐GFP (green fluorescent protein) fusion protein in tobacco leaves. Both the wild‐type SS2 protein (SS2HTX) and its missense variant carried by mutant ‘M4950’ (ss2M4950) were localized on the plasma membrane exclusively (Figure 3D).

Silicon (Si) Hyper‐Accumulation in Peduncle Suppressed Its Elongation

Previous study has elucidated the importance of SIET4, the ortholog of barley SS2, in healthy growth of rice via regulating Si deposition (Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023). While the knockout of rice SIET4 results in lethal in soil, deficiency of barley SS2 leads to sheathed spike after heading. To further investigate the molecular mechanism of SS2, we generated near‐isogenic lines ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ from the ‘M4950×HTX’ population using single seed descent (SSD) and marker assisted selection (MAS) (Figure 4A). Compared to ‘NIL‐HTX’, ‘NIL‐4950’ exhibited shortened peduncle, sheathed spike, and reduced plant height (Figure 4B,C), as well as reduced peduncle cell length (Figure 4D). SS2 was detected with especially higher expression in the peduncle of ‘NIL‐4950’ compared to ‘NIL‐HTX’ (Figure 4E).

To verify if SS2 is involved in Si deposition, we examined the Si deposition in vivo along peduncle to spike by the μ‐XRF analysis. The Si deposition at the peduncle of both NILs gradually decreased from top to bottom; however, the Si mainly deposited at the spike‐peduncle junction in ‘NIL‐HTX’, but evenly distributed throughout the entire peduncle in ‘NIL‐4950’ (Figure 4F). As control assays, no difference was observed in the deposition patterns of other elements like potassium (K), iron (Fe), and aluminium (Al) between ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ (Figure S5).

In addition, we measured the Si concentration at various organs including the peduncle, spike, flag leaf sheath and leaf blade in NILs that were cultivated in normal soil. Si concentration in ‘NIL‐4950’, in contrast to ‘NIL‐HTX’, exhibited ca. 54.68% decrease in spike, while ca. 8.77‐fold increase in peduncle and 2.22‐fold in flag leaf blade and 1.49‐fold in flag leaf sheath (Figure 4G). At lower internode such as IND4, there was no significant difference on Si concentration, and both genotypes maintained low level of Si accumulation at IND4 as that was observed at the peduncle of ‘NIL‐HTX’ (Figure 4G). The spikes, peduncle, flag leaf sheath and flag leaf blade of ‘NIL‐HTX’ exhibited 59.35%, 1.88%, 22.80% and 15.96% on Si deposition, respectively (Figure 4H). In ‘NIL‐4950’, SS2 deficiency resulted in a shift of Si partitioning, with significant reduction in spike and increases in peduncle, flag leaf sheath and blade. These results indicated that the shortened peduncle in ss2 plant was associated to Si over‐accumulation.

Moreover, both NILs were subjected to hydroponic culture in solutions with or without silicon supply. Interestingly, both ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ exhibited spikes exserted in the hydroponic culture without Si supply, while only ‘NIL‐4950’ exhibited sheathed spike phenotype in the culture supplied with Si (Figure 5A). No significant differences on peduncle length or plant height between ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ were observed without Si supply (Figure 5B–F). We further harvested the peduncles of both NILs at the grain‐filling stage for Si concentration measurement. In culturing with Si supply, Si concentration in ‘NIL‐4950’ peduncle was approx. 6.17‐fold higher compared to that of ‘NIL‐HTX’ (Figure 5F). The Si over‐accumulation was accordant with the observations in the soil‐culturing experiment (Figure 4G). We further measured the Si transport activity of SS2 in a Xenopus laevis oocytes feeding assay using 68Ge as previous description (Ma et al. 2007). We didn't detect the efflux activity in wild‐type SS2 protein (Figure S6), in contrast to the performance of Si efflux transporter OsLsi2 in rice (Ma et al. 2007). Similarly, the transport activity was not observed for the rice SIET4 neither via the same assay (Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023).

To decipher the regulatory networks underlying the over‐accumulation of Si‐suppressed peduncle elongation, transcriptomic analysis was carried out with the peduncles of both ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ harvested from hydroponic culture with or without Si supply, respectively. 36 634 genes were detected with transcripts. Under culture conditions with or without Si supply, there were no differentially expressed genes (DEGs; Log2FoldChange ≥ |1| and FDR < 0.05) in ‘NIL‐HTX’, but 28 DEGs consisting of 13 up‐regulated and 15 down‐regulated in ‘NIL‐4950’ (Figure S7). Several up‐regulated genes were annotated to participate in cell wall remodelling, such as glycosyltransferase, xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase and arabinogalactan protein. Genes involved in energy metabolism and protein degradation, such as phosphoglucose isomerase and ubiquitin carboxyl‐terminal hydrolase, were found down‐regulated. These results implied the involvement of SS2 in cell wall remodelling and suppressed cell metabolic activities.

Collectively, these results, especially the recovery of the exserted spike in ‘NIL‐4950’ in the hydroponic culture without Si supply, demonstrated that the sheathed spike in ss2 was due to Si over‐accumulation in its peduncle (Figure 5G).

Natural Variation of SS2 in Barley and Common Wheat

We further investigated the haplotype diversity of SS2 in global barley and wheat populations, using the following datasets: 1689 barley accessions including 381 wild barley from the WBDC and WILD100 collections (Jayakodi et al. 2020; Spanner et al. 2024), 993 cultivated accessions from CORE1000 (Jayakodi et al. 2024), 315 elite European cultivars from SHAPE_cv315 (Jayakodi et al. 2024), and 1051 wheat accessions from the Watkins collection (Cheng et al. 2024) (Tables S5–S10). In barley, 58 haplotypes of the SS2 coding sequence were identified, 11 of which contained amino acid substitutions (Figure 6A). Seven haplotypes were shared between wild and domesticated barley, while 43 and 8 haplotypes were exclusively detected in wild and domesticated barley, respectively (Table S6). Amongst 55 polymorphic sites, 46 were synonymous and 9 resulted in amino acid substitutions. In wheat, analysis of SS2 homologues revealed 3, 3, and 2 haplotypes for TraesCS4A02G412500, TraesCS4B02G312600, and TraesCS4D02G310100, respectively (Figure 6B and Table S7). The variants included two synonymous and one amino acid substitution in TraesCS4A02G412500 (Table S8), one synonymous and one amino acid change in TraesCS4B02G312600 (Table S9), and 13 synonymous and two amino acid substitutions amongst the 15 polymorphic sites at TraesCS4D02G310100 (Table S10). No pre‐stop or frame‐shift variant was detected in either species. The representative genotype of hap01 is Morex in barley and it is Chinese Spring for wheat. These results revealed a highly sequence conservation of SS2 within the gene pools of barley and wheat.

Discussion

Previous studies have established that elongation of stalk/stem and spike/panicle internodes in cereal crop plants primarily involves phytohormone‐associated pathways (Peng et al. 1999; Zhu et al. 2006; Tong et al. 2009; Patil et al. 2019; Niu et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2024). This study reports, for the first time, a novel type of recessive variant, ss2, which confers shortened peduncle and sheathed spike through altered Si partitioning and excessive accumulation in peduncle. SS2 encodes an evolutionarily conserved putative silicon efflux transporter gene that exhibits constitutive expression across multiple barley organs, with pronounced activity in the upper stem internodes. Unlike rice SIET4, which mediates Si transport from mesophyll cells to epidermal cells (Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023), SS2 governs tissue‐specific Si deposition. The deficient variants disrupt spatial Si allocation, resulting in shifted Si accumulation in peduncles and flag leaf sheaths/blades instead of spikes. The aberrant partitioning correlates with transcriptional reprogramming of genes involved in cell wall remodelling and energy metabolism pathways. Recent genetic mapping of a dominant sheathed‐spike variant implicated a mechanism involved in gibberellic acid (GA) associated pathway (Pu et al. 2021). Future investigations combining exogenous phytohormone treatments along with molecular and physiological analysis in ss2‐plants could elucidate Si‐phytohormone crosstalk in peduncle elongation.

Numerous transporter genes, including high‐affinity K+ transporter HKTs (Huang et al. 2020), boron efflux transporter Bot1 (Sutton et al. 2007), silicon/boron influx transporter OsLsi1/HvNIP2;1 (Schnurbusch et al. 2010), and cadmium transporter NTP1 (Wang et al. 2025), have been characterized in barley and rice for their roles in abiotic stress adaptation. Si, a beneficial element absorbed through roots, undergoes xylem‐mediated transport to shoots (Mitani et al. 2009; Yamaji et al. 2015; Huang et al. 2022), with preferential deposition in grain husks (Ma et al. 2003). Tissue‐specific Si accumulation is considered to exhibit benefits for their defence against biotic and abiotic stresses (Ma 2004). Si deposition on the tissue surface in the form of a polymer of hydrated amorphous silica acts as a physical barrier to physically impede pathogens and insects, and it also functions as a signal to induce enzymatic responses improving the adaptation of the cells to stress conditions (Ma 2004). Notably, disrupted Si homeostasis via SIET4 knockout leads to Si hyperaccumulation‐conferred death in soil‐grown rice plants (Mitani‐Ueno et al. 2023). Here we identified a transmembrane‐localized putative silicon transporter that is critical for maintaining plant architecture. Impaired Si efflux in ss2‐peduncle induces a toxicity phenotype characterized by suppressed cell elongation (sheathed spike). These findings highlight the necessity of spatially programmed Si deposition for crop health. Furthermore, as peduncle length influences cereal architecture optimization, our work demonstrates that fine‐tuning Si transport and deposition could enable precision modulation of this trait.

Collectively, this study identifies and characterizes SS2 as a critical regulator of peduncle elongation through maintaining programmed Si transport and deposition to prevent Si toxicity.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

Barley mutants ‘M4081’ and ‘M4950’ were identified from the recently developed ‘HTX’ ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis population (Jiang et al. 2022). These mutants were characterized by shortened peduncle and sheathed spike. Reciprocal crosses were performed between each mutant and the parental line ‘HTX’ for genetic analysis. An allelism test was conducted through hybridization between ‘M4950’ and ‘M4081’. The resulting F1 hybrids and four F2 populations (designated pop1–pop4; Figure S2B) were cultivated in the field at Xinxiang, Henan Province, China (N35°18′13.71″, E113°55′15.05″) for phenotyping. A F2:3 heterozygous individual was propagated for subsequent generations through marker‐assisted selection of heterozygous individuals followed by self‐pollination. Homozygous lines from F5:6 generation were designated as near‐isogenic lines (‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’) for subsequent analysis.

Durum wheat EMS mutants ‘K2085’ and ‘K2179’ were derived from cultivar ‘Kronos’, both containing premature stop codons in SS2 homologues (Krasileva et al. 2017). All tetraploid wheat plants were cultivated in soil pots in a growth chamber under greenhouse conditions (16‐h light at 24°C and 8‐h dark at 18°C).

Histological Section Observation

The middle part of peduncle at grain filling stage were sampled for cellular characterization. Segments were fixed in FAA solution (10% (v/v) formaldehyde, 50% (v/v) alcohol, 5% (v/v) acetic acid and 35% (v/v) water) for 24 h, followed by standard steps of dehydration and paraffin embedding (Pu et al. 2021). Longitudinal sections (8 μm in thickness) were stained with 0.1% (w/v) toluidine blue O (Sigma, Germany) and imaged using a microscope imaging system (DS‐U3, Nikon, Japan). Cell dimensions were quantified using CaseViewer 2.3 software (3DHISTECH).

Whole‐Genome Sequencing (WGS) and Bulked‐Segregants Analysis (BSA)

Genomic DNAs were extracted from seedling leaves, qualified and quantified using established protocols (Yan et al. 2021). Whole‐genome sequencing was conducted on the MGISEQ‐T7 platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) for two bulked DNA samples (30 short vs. 30 long spike exsertion individuals from pop4) and two parental mutant lines ‘M4081’ and ‘M4950’. The WGS data processing, including quality control, reads mapping, and SNP calling and filtration, followed published methodologies (Huang et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2024).

KASP Genotyping

Competitive allele‐specific PCR (KSAP) markers were developed based on identified SNPs. The KASP assays were performed in 384‐well plates using HiGeno 2× Probe Mix Kit (JasonGen, China). Genotyping data were analysed using QuantStudio Software v1.2.

TILLING in the ‘HTX’ Mutagenesis Population

An EMS‐mutagenized library comprising 5184 lines of barley cultivar ‘HTX’ was screened for SS2 mutants using gene‐specific primers (Table S11). Targeting Induced Local Lesions In Genomes (TILLING) was conducted using established amplicon‐seq based protocols (Jiang et al. 2022). Nine M3 plants of each of the lines, which were detected with missense, frame‐shift, or premature mutation, were sampled for Sanger sequencing. Homozygous or heterozygous mutant plants were kept for seed multiplication or selecting homozygous plants in the following generation.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Protein sequences of SS2 and SIET family orthologs from Poaceae crops were retrieved via BLAST search (Ensembl Plants database). Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MUSCLE, followed by phylogenetic tree construction via Neighbour‐Joining method with 1000 bootstraps in MEGA 7.0.

Quantitative RT‐PCR Analysis

Total RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays were conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) with gene‐specific primers (Table S11).

Transformation of pSS2::GUS and Tissue Staining

A 2.5 kb promoter fragment upstream of SS2 was amplified (Table S11) and cloned into the pWMB110 GUS reporter vector using BamHI/HindIII restriction digestion and T4 ligation. The reporter construct was introduced into barley cultivar ‘Ganpi 6′ using Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. The T0 and T1 transgenic barley plants were grown under 16 h of light at 24°C and 8 h of dark at 16°C in a greenhouse. The tissues or sections of transgenic plants were sampled and incubated at 37°C overnight in commercial GUS staining solution (Solarbio, China), followed by destaining in 75% ethanol. Images were captured with a camera (Canon, Japan) imaging or microscopic (SZX16, Olympus, Japan) observation.

Transient Expression in Nicotiana benthamiana Leaves

The full‐length coding sequences of SS2 HTX and ss2 M4950 were cloned into the pUBN‐GFP‐Dest vector (Grefen et al. 2010) using the Gateway approach (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), in fusion with the green fluorescent protein (GFP) gene at C'‐terminal. The plasma membrane marker AtPIP2A‐mCherry (PMck) (Nelson et al. 2007) was co‐expressed for subcellular localization. The plasmid constructs were individually transformed into the A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana epidermal cells. The fluorescence was visualized 48 h post‐infiltration using a confocal microscope (LSM900, Zeiss, Germany).

Treatments Under Soil and Hydroponic Conditions

For soil‐culturing, 7‐day‐old seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots containing normal soil and cultivated with daily watering. For hydroponic culture, 7‐day‐old seedlings were maintained in 1/5‐strength Hoagland solution containing either 1 mM potassium silicate (K2SiO3) or 2 mM potassium chloride (KCl) (Mitani et al. 2009; Ye et al. 2013), with nutrient solution replacement every 48 h.

Si Concentration Determination

For Si quantification, tissues from soil‐grown or hydroponic‐cultured plants at grain‐filling stage were harvested and dried (65°C for 72 h). Plant tissue samples were microwave‐digested (TOPEX, PreeKem, China) using a mixture of 4 mL Aqua Regia, 1 mL H2O2, and 1 mL hydrofluoric acid. The digested solution was diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water, and Si concentration was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS; Optima 8000, Perkin Elmer, USA).

Si Mapping Using μ‐XRF

Tissues of ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ at grain‐filling stage were sampled and scanned by X‐ray fluorescence spectrometry (μ‐XRF; Bruker M4 Tornado PLUS). Scanning was performed using the parameters: 50 kV excitation voltage, 600 μA beam current, 1.5 mbar chamber air pressure, and 7.5 mm/s stage speed. The detector were configured for maximum pulse throughput (275 000 cps).

Transport Activity in Oocytes

The full‐length coding sequences of SS2 and OsLsi2 were cloned into the Xenopus oocyte expression vector pXbG‐ev1 (Preston et al. 1992). Capped RNA (cRNA) synthesis, oocyte microinjection, and Si efflux transport assays were performed according to previously established protocols (Ma et al. 2007).

Transcriptomic Analysis

Seedlings of the ‘NIL‐HTX’ and ‘NIL‐4950’ plants that were pre‐cultured in Si‐free solution, were treated with in 1/5‐strength Hoagland solution concluding either 2 mM potassium chloride or 1 mM potassium silicate (K2SiO3) for 36 h at the booting stage (with approximately 5 cm peduncle). Peduncles after treatments were harvested for total RNA extraction, library construction and RNA sequencing. Clean reads were aligned to the barley reference genome (Morex v3) (Mascher et al. 2021) using Hisat2 v2.1.0 with default parameters (Kim et al. 2015). Differentially expressed genes were defined by a minimum 2‐fold change in transcript abundance and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were performed using the clusterProfiler R package in R, following the established bioinformatics pipelines (Sun et al. 2024).

Haplotype Diversity Analysis

Haplotype diversity analysis followed the previously established procedure (Gao et al. 2024). Sequence variation of the gene coding sequences was analysed using Sequencher v4.8 (Gene Codes Corporation, https://www.genecodes.com/). Median‐joining networks of the haplotypes were generated using PopART v1.7 (Leigh and Bryant 2015).
