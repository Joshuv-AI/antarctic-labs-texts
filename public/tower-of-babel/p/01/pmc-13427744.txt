ECHIDNA: Extreme Climate Historical and Future Indices Data under Numerous Approaches across Major Chinese River Basins Based on CMIP6 Multi-Model Ensemble
Authors: Le Xinlong, Kang Ling, Chen Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate extremes are intensifying under global warming, posing unprecedented challenges to ecosystems, water resources, and human societies. However, high-resolution, basin-specific extreme climate datasets remain scarce, particularly in climatically diverse regions like China. Here, we present ECHIDNA (Extreme Climate Historical and Future Indices Data under Numerous Approaches), a comprehensive database of 33 ETCCDI indices derived from an ensemble of eight CMIP6 Global Climate Models (GCMs), statistically downscaled using seven methods including CDFt, ECDFM, ISIMIP, LS, QDM, QM, and SDM. Covering 1979–2100 under three SSP scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5), the dataset focuses on four major river basin regions: the Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, Hai River Basin, and Southwest River Basins. Evaluation using PCC, NSE, KGE, and RMSE demonstrates significant improvements in capturing temporal variability and extreme event intensity compared to raw model outputs. By incorporating multi-model projections, ECHIDNA enables robust assessments of uncertainty in future climate risks and supports hydrological, agricultural, and infrastructure resilience planning. It is openly available to facilitate climate impact studies, adaptation strategies, and international research collaboration.

Background & Summary

Climate change is accelerating the frequency, intensity, and spatial extent of extreme climate events across the globe. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report, human-induced global warming has unequivocally intensified the magnitude and frequency of compound climate extremes, including heatwaves, droughts, heavy precipitation, and floods1. These extremes are not only altering terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems but also reshaping the risk landscape for agriculture, urban infrastructure, water resources, and human health2. Future projections under various Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) suggest that these changes will become more severe and spatially heterogeneous, with many vulnerable regions expected to face unprecedented climate-related disruptions.

In this context, regional assessments of climate extremes have become a key priority for climate impact research and adaptation planning. However, global climate models (GCMs), while essential for capturing large-scale climate dynamics, lack the spatial resolution and precision necessary for basin- or city-level decision-making3. This is particularly true for indices representing climate extremes, whose physical relevance depends heavily on temporal accuracy and the ability to capture distributional tails. Statistical downscaling (SD) methods have emerged as a practical solution to bridge this resolution gap by translating coarse GCM outputs into localized climate variables suitable for sectoral applications4,5.

Among SD methods, a range of techniques has been developed to correct for biases and reproduce observed statistical properties. These include quantile mapping (QM), empirical cumulative distribution function mapping (ECDFM), change-preserving approaches like CDFt6, and standardized frameworks such as ISIMIP3 bias adjustment7,8. Newer variants like scaled distribution matching (SDM)9 and quantile delta mapping (QDM) aim to address temporal consistency and robustness under non-stationary climates10. However, each method exhibits different strengths and limitations when applied to climate extremes. For example, quantile-based methods may effectively correct extreme precipitation biases but distort variability trends if not properly constrained, while distributional fitting methods may preserve trends but misrepresent rare event probabilities11–13.

Due to these methodological discrepancies, growing attention has been given to multi-method ensembles for characterizing structural uncertainty in downscaled climate datasets14–16. Such ensembles not only improve confidence in mean projections but are also crucial for assessing the robustness of extreme climate indices such as the ETCCDI set, which includes metrics like annual maximum temperature (TXx), consecutive dry days (CDD), and very heavy precipitation days (R95p)15. Despite the global popularity of ETCCDI indices, their application in China often lacks consistent long-term, high-resolution, multi-method downscaled datasets—especially across large and climatically diverse basins.

To address this gap, this study presents the ECHIDNA dataset (Extreme Climate Historical and Future Indices Data under Numerous Approaches), a comprehensive extreme climate index database generated through seven statistical downscaling methods applied to a multi-model ensemble of eight high-resolution CMIP6 climate models17. The dataset spans from 1979 to 2100 and covers both historical and future periods under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 scenarios. It includes 33 ETCCDI indices and is targeted at key strategic river basins in China: the Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, Hai River Basin, and the Southwest River Basin18–20.

Given these contextual differences, there is a pressing need for basin-specific, downscaled, and uncertainty-aware extreme climate datasets. ECHIDNA fulfills this need by providing high-resolution (0.1°), bias-corrected, and methodologically diverse outputs that allow researchers and decision-makers to quantify not only mean shifts but also the spread and reliability of future extremes. The incorporation of multiple SD methods—including CDFt, ECDFM, ISIMIP3, linear scaling (LS), QDM, QM, and SDM—ensures that the dataset captures a wide range of plausible climate trajectories. This ensemble-based approach enables users to better assess uncertainty, conduct attribution studies, evaluate model robustness, and derive adaptation-relevant signals.

ECHIDNA is also designed with broad usability in mind. The dataset is directly applicable to hydrological modeling (e.g., VIC, SWAT), agricultural risk mapping, ecosystem vulnerability assessment, and infrastructure resilience planning. Furthermore, it supports national climate adaptation policies and contributes to China’s compliance with the Paris Agreement and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). By improving the availability and reliability of extreme climate data at the basin scale, ECHIDNA enhances both scientific understanding and real-world decision-making capacity.

In summary, ECHIDNA is a scientifically robust, policy-relevant, and openly accessible data resource that addresses the pressing need for high-resolution, multi-method, and long-term extreme climate projections in key Chinese river basins. It contributes to the growing body of open climate data infrastructure and provides a critical foundation for advancing interdisciplinary climate impact assessments, adaptation planning, and international climate cooperation.

Methods

Source data

This study integrates observational reference datasets and CMIP6 climate model simulations to construct a high-resolution dataset of ETCCDI extreme climate indices for major Chinese river basins. The final dataset covers 1979–2100, including a historical evaluation period and future projections under multiple emission scenarios. All inputs are standardized to a common spatial grid and consistent variable conventions prior to downscaling, bias correction, and index computation, thereby ensuring comparability across basins, scenarios, and processing approaches.

Daily station observations from the National Meteorological Information Center (NMIC), accessed via the China Meteorological Data Service Center, provide the primary in situ constraint for developing and validating the observational reference fields(国家气象信息中心-中国气象数据网). The station network comprises more than 2,400 sites across China and offers near-continuous records of daily maximum temperature (TX), daily minimum temperature (TN), and daily total precipitation (PRCP) over 1979–2014. These station observations were used for quality screening and for constraining bias corrections applied to the gridded observational products.

To improve the consistency and reliability of the gridded observational references used for calibration and validation, we implemented a residual-based correction scheme (Fig. 1). We first selected a reference precipitation dataset (CMFD V2 in this workflow) and a set of candidate gridded precipitation products for comparison. For each day, we computed the residual between each candidate product and the reference field, forming a target residual variable. We then trained a residual correction model using multiple covariates, including station-derived constraints, topographic information, and auxiliary meteorological variables. Considering the pronounced seasonality of precipitation processes in China, the correction model was trained separately for each calendar month, which allows the residual–covariate relationship to vary across seasons and reduces the risk of averaging incompatible regimes. The calibrated monthly residual models were subsequently applied to the corresponding month of the candidate products to obtain corrected precipitation fields, after which all corrected outputs were standardized to a consistent grid and variable convention. Importantly, the corrected precipitation products and the final derived indices are provided at a 0.1° spatial resolution, ensuring compatibility with basin-scale analyses and intercomparison across products.Fig. 1Residual-based correction scheme.

Residual-based correction scheme.

Climate model simulations were obtained from the CMIP6 archive(ESGF MetaGrid). In the revised dataset, we expanded from a single driving model to a multi-model ensemble to better represent inter-model uncertainty. The current release includes eight CMIP6 GCMs: ACCESS-ESM1-521, BCC-CSM2-MR17,22,23, CanESM524, GFDL-ESM425, INM-CM4-826, MIROC627, MPI-ESM1-2-LR28, and MRI-ESM2-029. Historical simulations (1979–2014) are used for method evaluation, while future projections (2015–2100) follow multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) (e.g., SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5). All model outputs are processed using an identical pipeline to ensure consistency across models. Given the computational cost of daily-scale downscaling30 and ETCCDI calculation, additional CMIP6 models will be incorporated in subsequent releases to further strengthen ensemble-based uncertainty characterization.

Data processing

This dataset focuses on three daily variables required by the ETCCDI framework: daily minimum temperature (TN, °C), daily maximum temperature (TX, °C), and daily total precipitation (PRCP, mm). Prior to downscaling and index computation, all observational reference fields and model simulations were harmonized in both space and time. Variable names, units, and calendars were standardized; precipitation was represented in consistent daily accumulation units, and temperature variables were converted to consistent °C conventions where necessary. Where model grids differed, fields were remapped onto a unified 0.1° latitude–longitude grid using conservative and/or bilinear schemes appropriate for the variable type, with land–sea masking applied consistently when required.

Missing values were handled transparently to avoid artificial inflation or suppression of extremes. During observational reference construction, station records failing quality screening or flagged as missing were excluded from the correction fitting, and missing values in the baseline gridded products were retained as missing in the corrected reference fields rather than being infilled. During ETCCDI computation, annual index values were produced only when data completeness criteria were satisfied (e.g., requiring a minimum fraction of valid daily values per year); Otherwise the annual index was set to missing. This ensures that reported extremes are not biased by incomplete daily records.

For percentile-based ETCCDI indices (e.g., TX90p, TN10p, R95p, R99p), the percentile thresholds were derived from a fixed baseline period (1961–1978) and applied consistently across all downscaling methods, scenarios, and basins, ensuring comparability across time and across ensemble members. The downscaling and bias-correction procedures were then applied using the observational reference fields as calibration targets, producing daily bias-corrected series from which ETCCDI indices were computed for each basin, model, scenario, and statistical method.

Study basins

The dataset focuses on four major river-basin regions that collectively represent China’s broad hydroclimatic gradients, terrain complexity, and exposure to climate-related risks: the Yangtze River Basin, the Yellow River Basin, the Hai River Basin, and the Southwest River Basin (Fig. 2). Together, these regions span humid monsoon-dominated lowlands, semi-humid to semi-arid transition zones, and highly heterogeneous mountain–valley systems shaped by strong topographic controls. This strategic selection enables a systematic evaluation of statistical downscaling methods across contrasting climatic regimes and impact settings, and provides a robust basis for basin-relevant assessments of future changes in temperature and precipitation extremes.Fig. 2Geographic location and spatial extent of the four study basins in China: the Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, Hai River Basin, and the Southwest River Basin. The map shows basin boundaries overlaid on elevation and administrative borders.

Geographic location and spatial extent of the four study basins in China: the Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, Hai River Basin, and the Southwest River Basin. The map shows basin boundaries overlaid on elevation and administrative borders.

The Yangtze River Basin is China’s largest river system and a core region of monsoon precipitation and socioeconomic activity. It is characterized by a predominantly humid subtropical climate and strong seasonal rainfall concentration, where intensifying heavy precipitation, pluvial flooding, and compound flood hazards are central concerns for climate adaptation and water-resource management. The Yellow River Basin spans semi-humid to semi-arid transition zones and includes extensive water-limited regions that are highly sensitive to warming and hydroclimatic variability. Its ecological fragility and chronic water scarcity, together with the increasing likelihood of drought–heat compound events, make it a priority region for studying climate extremes and their implications for water security.

The Hai River Basin, covering the densely populated North China Plain and major metropolitan areas such as Beijing and Tianjin, exhibits a warm temperate monsoon climate with pronounced drought–flood seasonality and frequent heat extremes. It provides a representative setting for assessing climate extremes under strong human influences, including rapid urbanization, intensive water use, and highly engineered water-management systems. In contrast to the relatively coherent hydroclimatic organization of the eastern plains basins, the Southwest River Basin represent a complex mountain region where monsoon dynamics interact with sharp elevation gradients to produce strong orographic precipitation, highly variable temperature lapse-rate effects, and localized extremes. This region is also prone to high-impact hazards such as rainstorm-triggered flash floods, landslides, and debris flows, and hosts widespread hydropower development and ecologically sensitive headwater environments, making it particularly important for evaluating downscaling performance in topographically complex and risk-prone settings.

Collectively, these four basin regions capture key dimensions of China’s climate system—from humid subtropical plains to water-stressed transition zones, from highly urbanized areas to mountainous headwaters—thereby forming a strategically diverse testbed for characterizing multi-method and ensemble uncertainties in extremes. Their inclusion supports regionally differentiated climate-impact assessments and provides an applied foundation for adaptation planning under future changes in temperature and precipitation extremes.

The technical workflow (Fig. 3) of this study utilizes daily-scale temperature and precipitation data from the CMIP6 multi-model ensemble under SSP126, SSP245, and SSP585 scenarios as driving data sources. First, raw model outputs were bilinearly resampled using CDO to unify spatial resolution to 0.1° × 0.1°, followed by further clipping along watershed boundaries to extract regional subsets for the Haihe, Yangtze, Yellow River, and Southwest Rivers basins. Subsequently, observed data within each basin were paired with CMIP6 historical data. Multiple bias correction and statistical downscaling methods—including CDFt, ECDFM, ISIMIP, LS, QDM, QM, and SDM—were implemented via the IBICUS platform to generate high-resolution, daily-scale downscaled climate data on a consistent grid for all four basins. Based on this, 33 ETCCDI extreme climate indices were computed using Python, yielding three core product categories: observed historical extreme climate indicator datasets, CMIP6 multi-model historical extreme climate indicator datasets, and CMIP6 multi-model future extreme climate indicator datasets. Finally, the results of extreme indices from different methods and scenarios are integrated to establish the ECHIDNA multi-method, multi-model extreme climate index data system, supporting cross-basin comparative assessments and impact studies of extreme events.Fig. 3Workflow of the ECHIDNA dataset construction, including data sources (observations and CMIP6 Multi-Model), downscaling methods, bias correction, ETCCDI index computation, and dataset outputs for historical and future periods.

ETCCDI calculation

The growing frequency and severity of climate extremes under anthropogenic climate change has elevated the need for robust, standardized methods to quantify and monitor these events. In response to this need, the Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI), operating under the auspices of the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP), developed a suite of internationally recognized climate extreme indices that offer a consistent framework for detection and attribution studies.

The ETCCDI indices were designed to detect changes in the frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme temperature and precipitation events using long-term daily meteorological records. Their primary objective is to enhance international comparability of climate extremes, support IPCC assessments, and guide regional climate risk analyses. Unlike absolute thresholds that vary across regions, many ETCCDI indices are defined using percentile-based thresholds, thereby ensuring local relevance while maintaining global comparability32–34.

The ETCCDI defines 27 core indices, broadly categorized into temperature-based, precipitation-based, and duration-based metrics: Temperature indices include metrics such as TXx, TNn, and SU. Percentile-based indices such as TX90p and TN10p are sensitive to local climatologies and are useful for detecting warming asymmetries. Precipitation indices include Rx1day, R95p, and CDD. These metrics are effective in quantifying hydrometeorological extremes, such as droughts and flash floods. Duration and variability metrics such as DTR provide insights into intra-day temperature dynamics and climate variability at sub-seasonal timescales. Each index is computed from daily data and can be aggregated to monthly or annual scales. Most percentile-based indices use a reference period of 1981–2010, as recommended by WMO35

ETCCDI indices have been widely applied in global and regional studies, including: IPCC Assessment Reports, where trends in ETCCDI indices provided empirical evidence of climate change impacts1. National climate assessments, for example in China36, where changes in R95p and TXx have been used to evaluate future flooding and heat risk. Sectoral vulnerability analyses, especially in agriculture, water resources, and health, due to the indices’ direct linkage with climate stress thresholds. Their integration with downscaled climate projections allows for more detailed regional analysis. When combined with multiple GCMs and downscaling techniques, they serve as a key tool for multi-model intercomparison, uncertainty analysis, and extreme event attribution37,38.

A set of extreme weather indices was proposed by the World Meteorological Organization at its climate change monitoring conferences from 1998 to 2001 and has become a uniform standard for climate change research. They are calculated from daily temperature and daily precipitation data and are characterized by weak extremes, low noise and high significance. Validation metrics

In this study, four widely used statistical metrics were selected to comprehensively evaluate the performance of the downscaling methods and the quality of the derived extreme climate indices: Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC), Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency coefficient (NSE), Kling-Gupta efficiency coefficient (KGE), and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). Each metric captures distinct aspects of model performance, providing a robust and complementary assessment framework.

Relying solely on a single metric may lead to incomplete or potentially misleading evaluations. Each indicator captures a particular aspect of model performance: Pearson’s r reflects temporal alignment but ignores systematic bias; NSE emphasizes overall variance matching but is sensitive to extreme events; KGE improves upon NSE by explicitly separating bias, variability, and correlation components41; while RMSE highlights large errors but does not distinguish systematic versus random errors42.

Therefore, a multi-metric approach is critical for a holistic evaluation of complex datasets like ECHIDNA. This integrated evaluation ensures that both the dynamic structure and the amplitude characteristics of extreme climate indices are properly assessed. Such a strategy enables more accurate identification of model strengths and weaknesses across different regions and climatic conditions.

Moreover, a comprehensive evaluation enhances user confidence in the applicability of the dataset across a range of scientific and operational uses, such as climate risk assessments, extreme event attribution, and future scenario analysis. By triangulating across different performance dimensions, uncertainties can be better diagnosed and managed, leading to more robust conclusions.

PCC (Pearson’s correlation coefficient)

PCC measures the linear relationship between observed and simulated values, reflecting the degree to which the temporal variability is captured43. Data Records

The ECHIDNA dataset is openly available on Zenodo and is distributed across six deposits to facilitate downloading and long term maintenance, with a total compressed volume of approximately 300 GB58–63. The shared files are provided as compressed.tar.gz archives. Each archive follows a consistent naming convention: icclim_ET_OUT__ < bias-correction method > __ < GCM > .tar.gz. where icclim_ET_OUT__ is the fixed prefix for the extreme climate index outputs, <bias-correction method> denotes the bias-correction method, such as QM, and< GCM> denotes the source CMIP6 global climate model. The eight GCM identifiers used in the file names are ACCESS-ESM1-5, BCC-CSM2-MR, CanESM5, GFDL-ESM4, INM-CM4-8, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, and MRI-ESM2-0. After decompression, the archives are organized using a scenario-, temporal-scale-, variable-, index-, and basin-based folder structure. The first-level folders correspond to the CMIP6 experiment or scenario, including historical, ssp126, ssp245, and ssp585, where applicable. Within each scenario folder, the data are further separated by temporal aggregation into month and year folders. These folders contain subdirectories for the source climate variables used to calculate the indices, including pr, tas, tasmin, and tasmax, representing precipitation, near-surface air temperature, daily minimum near-surface air temperature, and daily maximum near-surface air temperature, respectively. Each variable folder contains subfolders named after the corresponding extreme climate indicators. The NetCDF files stored within each indicator folder provide basin-specific gridded outputs for the Haihe River Basin, Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, and Southwest Rivers Basin. A typical file path after decompression is structured as follows:

icclim_ET_OUT__<bias-correction method>__<GCM>/<scenario>/<temporal scale>/<variable>/<indicator>/<basin>.nc

For example, a monthly precipitation-related index derived from the ACCESS-ESM1-5 model under the historical experiment and corrected using QM would be stored under a path following the structure:

icclim_ET_OUT__QM__ACCESS-ESM1-5/historical/month/pr/<indicator>/<basin>.nc

All data files are provided in NetCDF format at 0.1° × 0.1° spatial resolution. The main coordinate variables are time, lat, and lon, representing the temporal dimension, latitude, and longitude, respectively. The primary data variable in each NetCDF file corresponds to the extreme climate indicator identified by the indicator folder and file name. Standard NetCDF metadata, including units and descriptive attributes, are included where applicable. Because the dataset is distributed as gridded NetCDF files rather than tabular files, there are no column headings. The spatial domain covers 18–54°N and 72–136°E, and the temporal coverage extends from 1979 to 2100.Technical Validation

Technical Validation

Validation of baseline extremes

Based on the ETCCDI portfolio, we concentrate on seven indices that jointly describe the intensity, frequency, and persistence of extreme conditions that are most relevant to hydroclimatic risk in the Haihe River Basin, Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, and Southwest Rivers Basin. Two temperature indices are used to represent hot and cold intensity. TXx captures the annual maximum of daily maximum temperature and characterizes severe heat exposure. TNn captures the annual minimum of daily minimum temperature and reflects the severity of cold extremes. Five precipitation indices are used to represent heavy precipitation contribution, wet day intensity, wet day frequency, and the persistence of dry and wet spells. R95p measures the total precipitation from very wet days and is therefore sensitive to upper tail behavior and flood potential. SDII represents the mean wet day intensity and describes how precipitation amount is distributed among wet days. RR1 represents wet day frequency and is directly linked to changes in rainfall occurrence. CDD and CWD describe the maximum length of consecutive dry days and consecutive wet days, respectively, and therefore provide process relevant information for drought development, soil moisture persistence, and event clustering. These indices are all derived from daily temperature and precipitation, ensuring consistent comparability among statistical downscaling methods and enabling basin scale interpretations that connect to flood, drought, and heat related impacts.

For TNn, Fig. 5 shows that NSE values are high across most of the four basins for both the raw and downscaled outputs, highlighting that cold extreme temperature variability is more spatially coherent and therefore easier to reproduce than precipitation extremes. Most downscaling methods further raise NSE and reduce spatial fragmentation, and the basin wise distributions indicate relatively small inter method dispersion compared with precipitation indices, suggesting that TNn is less sensitive to methodological choice under the evaluated settings.

For TXx, Fig. 6 indicates uniformly strong agreement in terms of KGE across the basins after downscaling. The spatial fields show that downscaling substantially reduces residual biases and improves the combined reproduction of mean, variability, and temporal correspondence. The narrow spread in basin wise distributions supports the conclusion that TXx is robustly captured once daily temperature fields are bias adjusted, and that residual differences are dominated by regional observational representativeness and terrain related heterogeneity rather than by the choice among the stronger methods.

For SDII, Fig. 7 uses the correlation coefficient to evaluate temporal consistency in wet day intensity. Compared with temperature indices, SDII displays more pronounced basin and region dependence. Higher correlations tend to occur in the Haihe and Yangtze River Basins, while weaker correlations cluster in the upstream Yellow River and Southwest Rivers regions where precipitation timing and intensity are strongly affected by complex circulation and terrain driven convection. Methods such as ISIMIP3, QDM, and SDM generally maintain higher correlations and show tighter basin wise distributions, indicating better preservation of the temporal sequencing and relative variations of precipitation intensity. In contrast, LS and the raw field show lower correlations and larger dispersion, consistent with weaker event level alignment.

Overall, the combined map and distribution diagnostics demonstrate that temperature extremes are reproduced with consistently high skill across methods and basins, whereas precipitation extremes exhibit larger uncertainty and stronger sensitivity to both basin characteristics and methodological design. This contrast is most evident in the Yellow River and Southwest Rivers basins, where the complexity of precipitation processes and observational constraints magnify differences among downscaling approaches.

Usage Notes

Patterns of extreme indices

The spatial and temporal patterns of the ETCCDI indices in Figs. 4 to 13 reflect both the thermodynamic effect of warming and the strong heterogeneity of regional climate systems across the Haihe River Basin, Yangtze River Basin, Yellow River Basin, and Southwest Rivers Basin20,46. The widespread increases in hot extremes, represented by TXx and TX90p, are consistent with enhanced background warming and strengthened land atmosphere feedbacks in lowland regions, with clearer and more coherent signals in the Haihe and Yangtze basins47,48. The generally high baseline agreement for temperature indices in Figs. 6 and 7 supports the robustness of temperature downscaling across methods.

By contrast, TNn shows larger regional variability in high elevation and complex terrain subregions, especially in the upstream Yellow River Basin and parts of the Southwest Rivers Basin, where cryospheric influence and elevation dependent processes enhance diurnal and interannual fluctuations49,50. The projected rise in TNn under SSP5 8.5 implies a weakening of cold extremes that may affect permafrost stability and shift snowmelt timing in headwater areas51,52.

For precipitation extremes, increases in R95p, SDII, and RX5day are more spatially heterogeneous and exhibit stronger method dependence, particularly in the Yellow River headwaters and the Southwest Rivers Basin. These responses are consistent with thermodynamic moisture intensification and potential circulation changes, while the larger inter method spread indicates that precipitation extremes remain sensitive to both downscaling formulation and driving model precipitation biases, in line with IPCC AR6.

Methodological uncertainty

Multi-method comparisons indicate that temperature extremes exhibit overall lower sensitivity to downscaling methods compared to precipitation extremes53,54. This is visually supported in Figs. 5 to 7, where temperature-related metrics generally exhibit higher central values and narrower dispersion ranges across NSE, KGE, and PCC, indicating greater stability in spatial continuity and observational constraints for daily-scale temperature fields. Conversely, uncertainties in precipitation extremes are concentrated in tail-heavy precipitation and persistence statistics, with R95p, RX5day, CDD, and CWD exhibiting greater dispersion in the Yellow River and southwestern river basins55,56. These differences primarily stem from representational errors caused by uneven observational constraints and complex topography. They are also methodologically driven, as varying approaches to variance correction, quantile tail fitting, and seasonal bias handling directly influence extreme threshold crossing frequencies and multi-day event clustering characteristics10,57.

Taylor plots further reveal how these structural differences manifest in correlation and variance reproduction. Figures 8 to 10 demonstrate that partial distribution matching and trend-preserving methods more readily achieve both high correlation and reasonable variance levels. In contrast, simplified linear adjustments or empirical quantile mapping may exhibit under- or over-smoothed variance in certain basins, leading to excessive smoothing or amplification of wet-day frequency and wet/dry persistence10,57. This has direct implications for impact assessments, as hydrological models are highly sensitive to precipitation frequency and duration. Methodological differences can amplify into disparities in runoff and drought risk, particularly in simulating runoff thresholds, soil moisture memory, and dry spell duration. Consequently, selecting a single method may introduce systematic biases in assessing the magnitude of precipitation extremes and risk levels. Multi-method ensembles provide users with quantifiable uncertainty ranges and more robust decision-making foundations.

Applications

From an applied perspective, the sustained intensification of TXx and TX90p in the Haihe and Yangtze River basins signifies rising heat risks, holding direct implications for urban thermal environment management, public health early warning systems, and electricity load management47,48. Concurrently, the intensification of R95p and RX5day in the Yangtze River Basin and southwestern river basins indicates heightened contributions from heavy precipitation and increased risks of short-duration torrential rains. This may exacerbate mountainous flash floods and flood pressures in the middle and lower reaches, while imposing greater demands on reservoir operations and urban drainage capacities. IPCC AR6. In the Yellow River basin, changes in CDD and regional adjustments to CWD indicate potential reshaping of dry and wet persistence. This will affect ecological water demand security and the stability of seasonal runoff processes, thereby increasing the complexity of agricultural irrigation and ecological water replenishment management49,50. Given that persistence indicators are more sensitive to methodological structure and observational sparsity, risk assessments should prioritize multi-method ensemble results and incorporate zoned characteristics at the basin scale for scenario comparisons. This approach enhances the interpretability and applicability of conclusions20,46. Therefore, ECHIDNA provides not just a data repository, but also a diagnostic tool for exploring physical mechanisms behind extreme event changes under climate change.

Limitations

First, although the four basin regions are selected to capture China’s key hydroclimatic gradients and risk contexts, they still represent a subset of the country’s full spatial heterogeneity. Important hydroclimatic features outside the current domain—such as additional inland basins, coastal sub-regions, and high-relief mountain systems—may exhibit distinct extremes dynamics and downscaling sensitivities. Future work will therefore extend the spatial scope to include all major river basins of China and, ultimately, move toward a nationwide, gridded extremes-index product to improve coverage, inter-regional comparability, and applicability for national-scale assessments.

Second, the current multi-model design is based on eight CMIP6 GCMs, which marks a clear step beyond single-model projections but still does not exhaust the breadth of structural uncertainty in CMIP6. Additional GCMs, alternative ensemble weighting/selection strategies, and explicit treatment of model independence and skill diversity would further strengthen ensemble robustness. Future releases will expand the GCM ensemble and provide enhanced ensemble diagnostics, enabling more comprehensive quantification of inter-model spread and scenario uncertainty in ETCCDI indices.

Third, ECHIDNA currently emphasizes statistical downscaling and bias correction methods. Although these approaches are computationally efficient and widely used, they may be limited in representing physically consistent multivariate relationships, circulation-driven extremes, and complex terrain processes. With the growing availability of high-resolution regional climate simulations, future work will incorporate additional methodological families beyond the current statistical toolkit, including dynamical downscaling products and emerging hybrid approaches. Such integration is expected to be particularly beneficial in regions with strong topographic controls and non-linear processes, where the added physical constraints of dynamical models or hybrid schemes may improve extremes realism. Addressing these directions will further improve the robustness, representativeness, and scientific utility of ECHIDNA for climate-risk applications, impact modeling, and adaptation planning.
