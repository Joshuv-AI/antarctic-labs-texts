Remodeling of the tumor microenvironment in muscle-invasive bladder cancer: insights from single-cell and spatial transcriptomics after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy
Authors: Guozhi Zhao, Yuanyuan Bai, Xin Chen, Junwu Li, Hai Chang, Yongtang Zhu, Haoyu Xu, Yiqing You, Gautam Sethi, Deyu Zuo, Wei Tang, Lei Yang, Weiyang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT), defined in this study as PD-L1 blockade combined with cisplatin-based chemotherapy, provides substantial clinical benefits in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). However, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is highly heterogeneous, resulting in variable patient responses and persistent therapy resistance. This study investigates key immune cell subsets, intercellular communication networks, and spatial distributions within the TIME of MIBC, with a focus on cellular and spatial features associated with nICT response.MethodsPatients were classified into responder and non-responder groups according to RECIST 1.1-based radiological evaluation. Responders were defined as patients achieving complete or partial response, whereas non-responders were defined as patients with stable or progressive disease after adequate nICT exposure and evaluable imaging. Representative computed tomography images and fresh tissue samples were collected from four no-treatment controls (NT), three nICT responders (R), and three nICT non-responders (NR). Samples underwent single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics sequencing. Cell populations were annotated to assess infiltration abundance, cell–cell communication networks, and pseudotime trajectories. Resistance-associated cell subsets and their spatial niches were identified.ResultsFollowing quality control, the tumor microenvironment (TME) was classified into ten major cell subsets based on canonical markers. Cross-sectional group comparative analyses showed significant remodeling of the TME in the NR and R groups. B-cell infiltration was elevated in the NR group, whereas macrophages, fibroblasts, and mast cells were enriched in the R group. Functional alterations in activated CD4 + T cells and impaired differentiation of naive CD8 + T cells were identified as key drivers of acquired therapy resistance. Accumulation of macrophage-derived CXCL8 was also observed as a resistance driver. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) served as a physical barrier, contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME. Notably, strong spatial co-localization between macrophage-derived CXCL8 and CAFs was observed in NR tumors, suggesting a cooperative role in mediating resistance to nICT. Further cell–cell communication analysis using spatial transcriptomics indicated that SPP1 signaling from macrophages (CXCL8) to CAFs contributes to TME reprogramming and resistance to nICT.ConclusionThe integrated analysis identifies distinct immunological features that underlie differential nICT responses in MIBC. These findings provide a theoretical basis for optimizing personalized neoadjuvant therapy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02738-y.

Introduction

Muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) is the 4th most common urinary tract malignancy in males [1]. Currently, the main treatments include radical cystectomy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or combinations of these modalities. Platinum-based chemotherapy remains the first-line option for locally advanced and metastatic disease, but its objective response rate is limited [2, 3]. In recent years, immunotherapy has made significant advances in MIBC. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are a prominent class of therapeutic agents widely used in MIBC treatment [4, 5]. Immunotherapy is now the primary treatment for MIBC. Combining it with chemotherapy offers better outcomes than chemotherapy alone [3, 6]. Neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) before surgery leads to longer event-free survival and higher rates of pathological complete remission (pCR) [7, 8]. However, some patients remain unresponsive to ICIs. The reasons for this resistance are not well understood.

Immunotherapy resistance involves not only PD-L1 expression in the TME but also tumor antigen presentation, mutation load, epigenetic changes, immunoregulatory molecules, and intracellular signaling pathways [9–11]. The TME is the local environment of tumor cells, encompassing tumor cells, supporting cells, the extracellular matrix, microvasculature, immune cells, and signaling molecules [12]. The TME is a dynamic and complex system that evolves with tumor progression, significantly influencing tumor growth, metastasis, and treatment response [13, 14]. The immunosuppressive nature of the TME is a significant factor contributing to the ineffectiveness of tumor immunotherapy [15]. Therefore, activating immune cells within the TME and enhancing the anti-tumor immune response are crucial for the success of immunotherapeutic strategies. ICIs can alter the TME, potentially increasing treatment sensitivity or driving resistance, leading to outcomes such as pCR or immune escape [16]. Owing to the high heterogeneity of the MIBC tumor microenvironment, the efficacy of a single immunotherapy approach is constrained [5]. Consequently, combining ICI with chemotherapy is now the primary treatment for advanced MIBC before surgery [8]. Data from clinical trials support the notion that nICT can markedly enhance pCR and reduce the risk of postoperative recurrence [6]. Nevertheless, some patients remain unresponsive to treatment, and acquired drug resistance is common [17]. Consequently, it is imperative to comprehensively elucidate the impact of ICIs on the reconstitution of the TME in MIBC, as well as to understand how modifications in TME responsiveness to ICIs influence the trajectory of treatment response.

Recently, increased efforts have focused on analyzing how complex regulatory interactions between the immune microenvironment and tumors affect clinical treatment outcomes [18, 19]. T cells are pivotal effector cells within the adaptive immune system. They are the main force in the body's immune response against tumors and play a crucial role in ICI efficacy [20]. Specific T cell subtypes, such as PD1hi CD200hi CD4 + exhausted T cells, can confer resistance to immunotherapy in MIBC patients. Macrophages link innate and adaptive immunity and are also crucial for tumor treatment [21, 22]. Recent research shows that macrophages within the tumor microenvironment can shift to an inhibitory phenotype, which promotes immune escape and reduces the effectiveness of ICIs [23]. Neutrophils contribute to tumor inflammation and progression by promoting angiogenesis, enhancing epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and increasing drug resistance [24]. A recent study comprehensively analyzed single-cell sequencing data from neutrophils across 17 cancer types. It identified a subset of HLA-DR + CD74 + neutrophils with antigen-presenting capabilities. These neutrophils may predict tumor immune response and sensitize therapeutic interventions [25]. Fibroblasts and extracellular matrix components, such as collagen and hyaluronic acid, create a barrier within the tumor matrix. This limits immune cell access to cancer cells and promotes immune escape [26]. For example, research shows that COL11A1 + CAFs and EPB41L2 + CAFs can predict the response of MIBC patients to immunotherapy [27]. Single-cell sequencing (scRNA-Seq) is increasingly used to explore immune cell composition, function, and interactions in the TME. This provides important insights for personalized immunotherapy. However, there is a lack of systematic delineation of TME infiltration in MIBC patients with respect to immunotherapy response and non-response. Moreover, scRNA-Seq may miss fragile cells and unevenly represent heterogeneous tissues. Spatial transcriptomics sequencing (stRNA-Seq), in contrast, captures cell expression in junctional regions, which are crucial for understanding disease heterogeneity and identifying treatment targets.

To elucidate dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment of MIBC patients following nICT, scRNA-Seq and stRNA-Seq were performed on tumor tissue samples from ten patients obtained after surgery. The cohort included four patients without nICT, three non-responders, and three responders. The study examined alterations in gene expression, biological functions, cellular development, and interactions among immune, stromal, and malignant cells during treatment.

Materials and methods

Human sample acquisition

The study included 10 patients who had their tumors surgically removed at the Department of Urology Surgery, First Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, between August 2024 and March 2025, with a pathological diagnosis of MIBC. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from each patient before sample collection. Among the enrolled patients, four underwent radical cystectomy without neoadjuvant therapy and were assigned to the no-treatment group. The remaining six patients received 3–5 cycles of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, consisting of PD-L1 blockade combined with cisplatin-based chemotherapy, before surgery. Treatment response was evaluated by comparing baseline and preoperative computed tomography images according to RECIST 1.1. Responders were defined as patients who achieved complete response or partial response, with partial response requiring at least a 30% decrease in the sum of target lesion diameters from baseline and no evidence of new lesions or unequivocal disease progression. Non-responders were defined as patients with adequate treatment exposure and evaluable imaging who failed to meet the criteria for complete or partial response, including those with stable disease or progressive disease according to RECIST 1.1. Patients with inadequate treatment exposure, non-evaluable imaging, early discontinuation unrelated to tumor progression, or mixed/indeterminate response patterns were excluded from the R/NR comparison. To minimize potential sampling and collection bias among the NT, R and NR groups, all tumor specimens were obtained under a standardized institutional workflow. All samples were collected at the time of surgical tumor removal, and fresh tumor tissues for scRNA-seq were obtained immediately after resection. Viable tumor regions were selected for downstream analysis, while grossly necrotic, hemorrhagic, cauterized, or non-tumor areas were avoided. Fresh-frozen sections for spatial transcriptomics were prepared from matched surgical tumor specimens.

Tissue dissociation and scRNA-seq

Tissue processing was performed uniformly across all groups. Fresh MIBC tumor tissues were collected from patients, rinsed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline, and processed using a tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) to prepare single-cell suspensions. The single-cell suspensions were loaded into a 10X Genomics GemCode single-cell device, which produces single-cell gel-bead-in-emulsion (GEM) libraries. Libraries were generated from the cDNAs using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ Reagent Kits v3 and sequenced. Full-length cDNAs with barcodes were reverse-transcribed from polyadenylated mRNAs to provide sufficient material for library generation, and these were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Gene Denovo Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China).

Single-cell RNA-Seq data processing

The scRNA-seq data were subjected to quality control prior to integration. To minimize potential noise and artifacts, cells with UMI counts less than 200, gene expression outside the 500–7000 range, and mitochondrial-derived UMI counts greater than 20% were removed. Integration of the scRNA-seq data was performed using Harmony43 (v1.2.1).

Integration and clustering of single-cell data

After quality control, filtered cells from all samples were merged into a Seurat object. Quality control was performed on each sample individually before integration, including the removal of low-quality cells and predicted doublets. Gene expression values were normalized, highly variable genes were identified, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the scaled expression matrix.

To correct for inter-sample batch effects, Harmony was applied using sample identity as the batch variable. The first 30 principal components were used as input for Harmony, and the first 30 Harmony dimensions were used for downstream analyses.

Clustering was performed in the Harmony-corrected low-dimensional space, not on UMAP or tSNE coordinates. Specifically, FindNeighbors () and FindClusters () were run using the Harmony reduction, and UMAP and tSNE was generated from the same Harmony dimensions for visualization only.

Statistical analysis for patient-level comparisons

To avoid pseudoreplication, patient/sample rather than individual cells or spatial spots was treated as the biological replicate for group-level statistical comparisons. For analyses of cell-type or cell-subtype abundance, the proportion of each cell population was first calculated within each individual sample as the number of cells assigned to that population divided by the total number of cells in the corresponding analysis set. Group comparisons were then performed at the patient level.

For score-based analyses, including representative functional/signature scores, values were first summarized at the sample level by calculating the mean score of the relevant cell population within each patient/sample, followed by group comparison across patients.

Comparisons between groups (e.g., NT vs NR and NR vs R) were performed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. When multiple comparisons were performed within the same analysis, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. In the supplementary figures, each patient/sample is shown as an individual dot whenever possible.

Cell type annotation and cluster markers identification

UMAP was used for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and two-dimensional projection, yielding cell clustering based on common attributes. We used the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell cluster during annotation. To filter DEGs, a t-test was applied with an absolute log fold-change threshold of 0.25 and a p-value threshold of 0.01. Cell clusters were identified and labeled based on the expression of standard markers for specific cell types.

Differentially gene expression analysis

We used the "FindMarkers" function in Seurat with default settings to identify differentially expressed genes. The selection process involved selecting genes expressed in at least 25% of cells in the target cluster, followed by filtering for a minimum log2 fold change of 0.5 and a maximum false discovery rate of 0.01.

Differentially expressed gene analysis for groups.

Seurat (version 4.1.0) was used for differential expression analysis. New individuals were assigned to "group_cluster" or "group_cell type" for analysis. We then used a hurdle model in MAST (Model-based Analysis of Single-cell Transcriptomics) to identify differentially expressed genes within a single cluster. We identified DEGs using the following criteria: 1) |logFC|≥ 1; 2) p-value-adj ≤ 0.05; and 3) the percentage of cells in which the gene was detected in a specific cluster was greater than 10%.

Enrichment analysis and gene set scoring

Functional enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed using the clusterProfiler48 (v4.6.2) package. The "AddModuleScore" function in Seurat facilitated gene set scoring, and the background gene set for Gene Ontology (GO) was downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database. To improve reproducibility, the gene sets used for AddModuleScore-based module scoring were explicitly defined. The immunosuppressive module included canonical immune-inhibitory and immune-tolerance-associated genes, including ARG1, PDCD1, CD274, PDCD1LG2, IDO1, IL10, TGFB1, TGFB2, TGFB3, PTGS2, VSIG4, FOXP3, ENTPD1, NT5E, VSIR and LGALS9. The angiogenesis module included representative angiogenesis-related genes, including VEGFA, VCAN, VAV2, TYMP, TNFAIP6, STC1, ANGPT2, SPP1, PTK2, PDGFA, NOTCH1, MMP9, JAG2, JAG1, ITGAV, HEY1, FYN, FGFR1, FGF18, EZH2, EDN1, E2F3,CXCR4,CD44,CCNE1 and CCND2. Genes not detected in the single-cell RNA-seq expression matrix were excluded before scoring.

Cell-to-cell interaction network

Using the R package "CellChat" (v1.1.3), we quantitatively inferred and analyzed intercellular communication networks between different cell types from scRNA-seq data.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Cell trajectories were inferred using Monocle2 and RNA velocity analysis. In Monocle2, the trajectory was constructed using the top 2,000 highly variable genes identified by Seurat's "FindVariableFeatures" function, along with the Texp and Tex cells.

Spatial transcriptomic library preparation

The 2-methylbutane (isopentane) approach was used to embed 10-µm-thick cryosections of tissues in OCT. RNA quality in the tissue sections was assessed, ensuring a RIN above 7.0. The Visium Spatial Gene Expression kits from 10 × Genomics were used for the ST experiment, following the manufacturer's standard guidelines. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten.

Preprocessing of ST data

For all subsequent data processing steps, such as filtering, normalization, dimensionality reduction, and visualization, count matrices were loaded into Seurat and BayesSpace.

Independent tissue sections were normalized using the variance-stabilizing transformation (SCTransform) in Seurat. We improved the clustering map's resolution by dividing each spot into 4 sub-spots, using spatial information with BayesSpace's spatialEnhance function. Using scRNA-seq data as a reference, we applied the R package RCTD to deconvolute cell mixtures. ST cell types were inferred from scRNA-seq data, and an NMF regression model was used to estimate their proportions for each spot.

Identification of malignant cells

To identify copy number variations (CNVs) and malignant cells, gene expression data and cell type annotations from the Seurat object were input into the InferCNV package. Nonmalignant-derived endothelial cells served as the control group. To minimize false-positive CNVs detections, the default Bayesian latent-mixture model was used to estimate the posterior probability of alterations in each cell. CNVs with low probability were filtered out using the default threshold of "0.5". Cells exhibiting consistent CNVs patterns were grouped using the default hierarchical clustering method.

Distance of spatial transcriptomics (ST) spots and cell-type co-localization

The "GetTissueCoordinates" function in Seurat was used to extract coordinates for calculating the distance between ST spots. The 'raster' package in R provides the 'pointDistance' function to calculate the Euclidean distance between a point and its nearest neighbor. The distance obtained was adjusted by dividing it by the square root of the tissue section's area. To determine whether the cell types of interest were co-localized within the same ST spot, the correlation between their proportions in each spot was analyzed.

Survival analyses

The Receiver operating characteristic curve was utilized in survival analysis to identify the optimal threshold of gene expression for categorizing patients.The Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to assess the prognostic significance of gene expression levels or cell cluster fractions in MIBC samples with survival information (IMvigor210). Survival outcomes between patient groups with high and low expression levels of genes or cell cluster fractions were compared using a two-sided Cox test. Patients were categorized into Complete Response (CR)/Partial Response (PR) and Stable Disease (SD)/Progressive Disease (PD) groups based on their treatment effectiveness. SPP1-CAF scores for each group were gathered, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare score differences between the groups.

Cell culture and plasmid.

Human lung fibroblast cells (MRC-5) and THP-1 cells were purchased from Type Culture Bank (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 (Corning, USA) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Pricella, China) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Beyotime). All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. CXCL8 overexpression and knockout plasmid were designed by Obio Technology Corp (Shanghai, China). Then, THP-1 cells were transfected with the plasmid, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Overexpression and knockout efficiencies of CXCL8 were assessed by Western blot.

Western blot

Cell pellets were disrupted in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime) following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations in the supernatants were measured, after which the samples were denatured in loading buffer (YamayBio) at 95°C for 10 min, separated on 8–12% gradient SDS-PAGE gels, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dried milk for 1 h, followed by incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C for 16 h, washed with TBST 3 times for 10 min each, and then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. The signals were detected through chemiluminescence using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP with automatic exposure settings. Primary antibodies included anti-CXCL8 (Proteintech, Cat. no. 27095–1-AP, 1:1000), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, Cat. no. 66009–1-Ig, 1:1000). Secondary antibodies included anti-rabbit IgG (CST, Cat. no. 7074, 1:2000) and anti-mouse IgG (CST, Cat. no. 7076, 1:2000).

Co-culture assay

Co-culture assays were performed to assess the effect of macrophages on fibroblast cells activation. Following PMA-induced polarization of THP-1 cells, the cell supernatant was collected and used to treat MRC-5 cells for 48 h.

Flow cytometry

After collection, MRCmo-5 cells were stained with a FAP noclonal antibody (Proteintech, Cat# 98,622–1-RR). All samples were acquired on CytoFLEX (Beckman) and analyzed in FlowJo (V10) with Boolean gating.

CD8 + T cell migration assays

The isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the whole blood of healthy donors was achieved by density gradient centrifugation. The blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS, placed over Ficoll, and centrifuged at 400 × g for 20 min at 25 °C. The buffy coat layer was harvested, rinsed three times with PBS, and then cultured in a medium containing 10% FBS, Anti-CD3/CD28 (KMS310, Proteintech), and recombinant IL-2 (10 ng/mL, PeproTech) for ex vivo maintenance. The Human CD8 + T cell Isolation Kit was used to select human CD8 + T cell from patient peripheral blood (130–096–495, Miltenyi Biotec). A total of 500,000 purified CD8 + T cells/PBMCs in serum-free DMEM were seeded into Transwell inserts with polyester membranes, with tumor cell-conditioned media from experimental groups acting as chemotactic gradients in the lower chambers. After incubating for 48 h, the migrated cells in the receiver chambers were collected and quantified through flow cytometry and the CCK8 assay.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded bladder cancer tissue sections. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific binding, sections were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies against SPP1 (22,952–1-AP, Proteintech) PD-1 (18,106–1-AP, Proteintech), CXCL8 (27,095–1-AP, Proteintech), FAP (11,779–1-AP, Proteintech), CD206 (18,704–1-AP, Proteintech), and DAPI (PR30021, Proteintech) followed by species-matched HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and tyramide signal amplification. After each staining cycle, antibody stripping was performed by microwave-based antigen retrieval before the next round of primary antibody incubation. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence slide scanner or confocal microscope, and representative regions of interest were analyzed for marker expression and spatial co-localization.

Statistics

R software, version 4.1.3, was used for all statistical analyses and presentations. All statistical tests are described in the figure legends, and statistical significance was determined by a P value or an adjusted P value < 0.05.

Results

Integrated high-resolution single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiling delineated the TME landscape in MIBC following nICT

The newly obtained MIBC samples were promptly processed into single-cell suspensions, and all single-cell transcriptomes were generated using the commercial 10X Genomics platform. The fresh-frozen samples were collected and analyzed using spatial transcriptomics. The study's overall design and patient enrollment are depicted in Fig. 1A. The Methods and Materials section outline the specific technical procedures, including quality control and filtering steps. After stringent quality control, a total of 106,618 high-quality single cells were retained from all MIBC samples for downstream analysis. All cells were annotated into 10 cell types based on well-known marker genes: Epithelial cells, Endothelial cells, Macrophages, Fibroblasts, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T-NK cells, Pericyte cells, Plasma cells, Mast cells, and B cells. t-SNE maps illustrate the distribution of cells across cell types (Fig. 1B). Typical marker gene expression patterns for each cell type are depicted in Fig. 1C. The percentages of each subpopulation in the NT, NR and R groups were examined (Figure S1A). Strong heterogeneity was observed in the cell cluster fractions across patients and groups (Fig. 1D, E). We observed a marked shift in the cellular composition of MIBC following nICT. The NT group were predominantly composed of epithelial cells, whereas both the NR and R group exhibited a reduced epithelial fraction and increased immune-stromal components. Compared with NR group, R group showed relatively higher proportions of macrophages, fibroblasts, plasma cells, mast cells, endothelial cells and CD4 + T cells, while B cells were more prominently represented in NR group. To analyze the transcriptomic profile of tumor tissues after nICT, fresh-frozen sections from 10 patients were collected for spatial transcriptomic analysis using the Visium platform (10 × Genomics). To determine tumor status at baseline and after treatment, computed tomography (CT) images were obtained before and after nICT (Fig. 1F). Using the Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) algorithm, a spatial atlas was developed to identify major cell types in MIBC sections, thereby highlighting the cellular composition of each section (Fig. 1G). We also analyzed the proportions of 10 cell types in each sample and group (Fig. 1H, Figure S1B, C). Spatial deconvolution analysis revealed marked remodeling of the cellular composition of MIBC tissues across the NT, NR, and R groups. Significant group-dependent differences were observed in plasma cells, macrophages, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts. Compared with the NR group, the R group exhibited significantly increased fractions of plasma cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts, whereas the proportion of epithelial cells was significantly decreased in the R group, as assessed by pairwise statistical comparison (P < 0.05). These results indicate that response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy is accompanied by reduced epithelial-cell dominance and increased stromal/immune remodeling within the tumor microenvironment. Although B cells appeared relatively more abundant in the NR group, and mast cells, endothelial cells, and CD4 + T cells showed a trend toward higher abundance in the R group, these differences did not reach statistical significance based on the current analysis. Therefore, these changes were described as trends rather than statistically supported alterations. We conducted a neighboring analysis of the main cell types after establishing the spatial architecture of the tumor tissues (Fig. 1I, Figure S1D). nICT not only creates a more complex tumor microenvironment but also reduces the spatial distance between cell types. In the R group, B cells, mast cells, and plasma cells predominantly formed spatially adjacent relationships with other cells. Mast cells and CD8 + T cells were located next to epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and macrophages in the R group. In summary, the ST analysis revealed that tumor tissues displayed varied cellular structures, and the TME landscapes from MIBC sections demonstrated a dynamic shift in response to nICT.

Malignant epithelial cells induce reduced anti-tumor effects by forming an inhibitory tumor immune microenvironment

We explored the 79,065 epithelial cells, identifying seven distinct subclusters (Fig. 2A). The cell distributions for each group were displayed using t-SNE plots (Fig. 2B). The NR group was primarily composed of Cycling Epithelial cells, basal 2 epithelial cells, and Luminal Epithelial cells. The NT group was mainly composed of basal 1 epithelial cells, whereas Group R was composed mainly of inflamed and EMT epithelial cells. Neoadjuvant therapy enhanced epithelial cell differentiation, leading to tumors that elicit distinct immune responses to immunotherapy. We also analyzed the proportions of 7 cell subtypes in each sample and group (Figure S2A, B). Malignant and normal cells were differentiated using the infer algorithm, which relied on CNVs data. We further investigated the distribution of epithelial cells between the aneuploid and diploid groups (Fig. 2C) and found that most epithelial cells were aneuploid. The marker genes of each subcluster are shown in Fig. 2D. The fractions of cells in each patient and group are shown in Figures S2A and S2B. We conducted further analysis of epithelial cell distribution in the aneuploid and diploid groups (Fig. 2E). Cycling epithelial and luminal epithelial cells represent a notably higher percentage of species in the aneuploid group than in the diploid group, serving as the main subgroup of the NR group, while the infiltration of inflamed epithelial cells is more pronounced in the diploid group, pointing to the potential importance of cycling and luminal epithelial cells in malignant cell formation and immunotherapy resistance. We then determined the proportions of aneuploid and diploid cells in each subgroup and group (Fig. 2F, G). Group R had fewer aneuploid cells than the other groups. Given that the NT, R, and NR groups comprise distinct epithelial cell subsets, we characterized changes in endogenous signaling in these cells (Fig. 2H-J). Our observations indicate that epithelial cells in the NT group are predominantly involved in glycolysis and HIF signaling pathways, both of which are strongly linked to tumor progression. We analyzed cell structures and spatial layouts in MIBC tumors using spatial transcriptomic data. Paraffin sections were annotated with the main cell types using the RCTD algorithm (Fig. 2K). We determined the proportions of subgroups within each sample and across groups (Fig. 2L, Figure S2C). In the NT group, epithelial cells were mostly of a single type, whereas the R and NR groups exhibited a wider variety of epithelial cell subtypes. A neighboring analysis was conducted to measure the spatial distance between the dominant cell types (Fig. 2M, Figure S2D). The results showed that Cycling Epithelial cells in the NR group were close to B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T-NK cells, and mast cells. Conversely, in the R group, inflamed-epithelial cells were spatially co-localized with B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T-NK cells, endothelial cells, and Cycling-epithelial cells, whereas Cycling-epithelial cells were far from B cells, CD4 + T cells, and CD8 + T-NK cells, indicating that Cycling-epithelial cells and inflammatory epithelial cells play an important role in the efficacy of immunotherapy.

nICT impairs the immune activation function of activated CD4 + T cells and confers resistance to MIBC immunotherapy

Lymphocytes are vital for immune responses against cancer. The proliferation of T cells is an important indicator of immunotherapy effectiveness. Consequently, we examined CD4 + and CD8 + T cells using single-cell RNA sequencing to analyze T-cell dynamics in MIBC patients following neoadjuvant therapy.

Seven unique cell subtypes were identified after replating 3862 CD4 + T cells, including activated CD4 + T cells, memory CD4 + T cells, naive CD4 + T cells, follicular helper CD4 + T cells (Tfh), helper CD4 + T cells (Ths), regulatory CD4 + T cells (Tregs), and resident memory CD4 + T cells (Trm). Activated CD4 + T cells can facilitate the expansion, differentiation, and cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells through cytokines such as IL-2 and IL-21, thereby strengthening their tumor-killing effects [28]. They can also express cytotoxic molecules, such as granzyme and perforin, to directly lyse tumor cells [29]. However, our study found a significant increase in the proportion of activated CD4 + T cells in the NR group, and we speculated that the function of activated CD4 + T cells changed during neoadjuvant therapy. Thus, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis of the differential genes in activated CD4 + T cells between the NR and R groups to explore functional changes in activated CD4 + T cells across distinct immune response states (Fig. 3G). We discovered that in the NR group, activated CD4 + T cells suppress the immune response, mainly by upregulating the NF-κB and TGF-β pathways and promoting IL-10 production, thereby contributing to an inhibitory TME. In contrast, they reduce T- and B-cell activation, decrease PD-1 blockade, and trigger immune escape. To better understand the relationship between changes in activated CD4 + T cells and memory CD4 + T cells, and the sensitivity of nICT, the proportions of these cells were analyzed across the 3 groups. There was a significant difference in the proportions of activated and memory CD4 + T cells between the groups, which we believe could predict the effectiveness of nICT (Fig. 3H-J).

To elucidate the differentiation pathways of CD4 + T cells and the genes characteristic of their differentiation states, we reconstructed the proposed temporal differentiation trajectories using Monocle 2 (Fig. 3K). At the onset of CD4 + T cell differentiation, there is an abundance of activated CD4 + T cells, which gradually differentiate into Tfh, Trm, and Tregs. We provide a detailed developmental trajectory for each CD4 + T cell subtype (Fig. 3L, Figure S3C).

Following nICT, CAF subpopulations engaged in self-sustaining regulatory circuits and exhibited phenotypic plasticity. Notably, signaling from CAFs to macrophages and naive CD8 + T cells was detected exclusively in the NR group, implying that CAFs may contribute to an immunosuppressive TME by impairing T-cell differentiation and promoting macrophage polarization. Consequently, our next step is to analyze CAF and macrophage subsets in relation to drug resistance signals (Fig. 8G). Our research showed that macrophage subsets were the exclusive source of MHC II and SPP1 signals, with SPP1 absent in group R. In group NR, only Macro-CXCL8 was secreted. Our research also revealed that ITGB1 received the signal from SPP1 (Figure S7G). Using the RCTD algorithm, we mapped the spatial propagation patterns of SPP1 signaling among cellular subsets in representative samples from each group (Fig. 8J).

The supernatant of THP-1 cells was collected to culture MRC-5 cells. Flow cytometry revealed the intensity of FAP expression, demonstrating that CXCL8 is capable of activating CAFs (Fig. 8K-L). By treating MRC-5 cells with SRT6, we discovered that Macro-CXCL8 communicates via CD44 on CAFs (Fig. 8M). CD8 + T cell migration experiments were conducted to confirm that Macro-CXCL8 restricted the migration speed of CD8 + T cells through CAFs (Fig. 8N). Notably, NR tumors exhibited markedly higher fluorescence intensity for SPP1 (red), FAP (cyan), CXCL8 (orange), CD206 (yellow), and PD1 (green) compared with R tumors, indicating enhanced macrophage infiltration, CAF activation, and immune checkpoint expression in resistant tumors. These findings suggest that CXCL8 and FAP/SPP1 may serve as potential biomarkers for nICT sensitivity in MIBC (Fig. 8O).

Clinical relevance and external validation of Macro-CXCL8 and SPP1–CAF signatures in immunotherapy-related bladder cancer cohorts

To evaluate the clinical relevance and treatment specificity of the Macro-CXCL8 and SPP1–CAF signatures, we further validated these two programs across multiple independent MIBC cohorts. IMvigor, ABACUS, and GSE176307 were immunotherapy-only cohorts, whereas GSE169455 and GSE212810 were chemotherapy-only cohorts.

In the IMvigor immunotherapy cohort, patients with high CXCL8–Mac scores exhibited significantly poorer overall survival than those with low CXCL8–Mac scores (P = 0.023; Fig. 9A). Similarly, high SPP1–CAF scores were associated with inferior survival compared with low SPP1–CAF scores (P = 0.020; Fig. 9B). We next compared these signatures according to clinical response. The CXCL8–Mac score showed an increasing trend in patients with stable disease/progressive disease (SD/PD) compared with those with complete response/partial response (CR/PR), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.075; Fig. 9C). In the ABACUS immunotherapy cohort with paired pre- and post-treatment samples, the Macro-CXCL8 ssGSEA score did not significantly change after treatment compared with matched baseline samples (P = 0.268; Fig. 9E). However, the FAP-high area within tumors was significantly increased after immunotherapy (P < 0.05; Fig. 9F), and the SPP1–CAF ssGSEA score was also significantly elevated in post-treatment samples compared with matched pre-treatment samples (P < 0.01; Fig. 9G). When post-treatment tumors were stratified by response status, the SPP1–CAF ssGSEA score showed no significant difference between non-responders and responders (P = 0.519; Fig. 9H). In the GSE176307 immunotherapy cohort, the Macro-CXCL8 ssGSEA score did not differ significantly between resistant and sensitive tumors (P = 0.451; Fig. 9L). However, the SPP1–CAF ssGSEA score was significantly higher in resistant tumors than in sensitive tumors (P < 0.05; Fig. 9M), further supporting the relationship between the SPP1–CAF stromal program and impaired immunotherapy response.

To determine whether these signatures were specifically associated with immunotherapy resistance rather than general treatment resistance, we analyzed two chemotherapy-only cohorts. In GSE169455, neither the Macro-CXCL8 ssGSEA score nor the SPP1–CAF ssGSEA score differed significantly between resistant and sensitive tumors (P = 0.986 and P = 0.136, respectively; Fig. 9N, O). Similarly, in GSE212810, no significant differences were observed in either the Macro-CXCL8 ssGSEA score or the SPP1-CAF ssGSEA score between responders and non-responders (P = 0.148 and P = 0.707, respectively; Fig. 9P, Q).

Discussion

Immunotherapies, including ICIs and adoptive CAR-T cell therapies, have provided additional and more effective treatment strategies for cancer patients, particularly in locally advanced and metastatic disease. However, the widespread application of immunotherapy is limited by low response rates, largely due to the heterogeneity of the TIME. This study aims to characterize the immune microenvironmental landscape of MIBC in relation to immunotherapy responsiveness, with the goal of elucidating the relationship between microenvironmental heterogeneity and therapeutic response. Specifically, we included 3 MIBC patients who responded or did not respond to nICT, along with 4 patients who underwent direct tumor resection, to perform single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses of tumor tissue. By resolving the tumor immune microenvironment at single-cell and subspatial resolution, we delineated dynamic changes in cellular composition, spatial architecture, gene expression, and intercellular communication across patient groups. In addition, we assembled a high-resolution spatial transcriptomic atlas of the MIBC tumor microenvironment, stratified by response status, before and after immunotherapy. In tumor immunotherapy, T cell subpopulations cooperatively regulate anti-tumor immune responses through synergistic and complementary interactions [30, 31]. CD4 + T cells primarily modulate and activate immune responses by recognizing antigens and secreting cytokines [32]. Several conventional CD4 + T cell subsets—including Th1, Th2, Th17, Th9, Tfhs, and Treg—have been identified within the TME, underscoring the functional diversity and complexity of CD4 + T cells in cancer [33]. Environmental influences, including specific cytokines or metabolic shifts, might modify the fate and effector roles of CD4 + T cell subsets, creating potential for therapeutic intervention [34]. Traditionally, activated CD4 + T cells serve as initiators of immune responses, characterized by high expression of CD69, CD25, and HLA-DR [35]. They secrete IL-2 to promote T cell expansion or activate dendritic cells (DCs) via CD40L–CD40 signaling to enhance cross-presentation, thereby indirectly stimulating CD8 + T cells [36]. Our study identified elevated levels of activated CD4 + T cells in NR patients, suggesting that activated CD4 + T cells, which typically initiate immune responses, may paradoxically contribute to resistance in MIBC patients undergoing nICT, possibly due to functional exhaustion. Comparative functional analysis of activated CD4 + T cells between the NR and R groups revealed enrichment in immunosuppressive pathways, including the TGF-β signaling pathway and IL-10 production. TGF-β signaling reduces T cell proliferation and its impact on the immune system [37, 38]. IL-10 suppresses the proliferation, differentiation, and cytokine release of pro-inflammatory T cells, such as Th1 and Th17, while boosting the activity and stability of Treg and fostering immune tolerance [39]. Importantly, several marker genes of this cluster further support a stress-activated rather than beneficial effector state. HSPA1A and HSPA1B are canonical heat-shock response genes. The previous research identified a unique T-cell stress-response state, characterized by heat-shock gene expression, and found that heat-shock gene expression in intratumoral CD4 +/CD8 + T cells was particularly increased in nonresponsive tumors after immune checkpoint blockade, suggesting a link between stress-response T cells and immunotherapy resistance [40]. In addition, a single-cell study of CD4 + T-cell activation identified an HSPhi T-cell subset derived from stimulated naïve T cells, indicating that strong HSP expression may reflect a stress/transitional activation program rather than necessarily a productive antitumor effector state [41]. Furthermore, KLF2 is a key transcriptional regulator of T-cell quiescence, trafficking and homeostasis, which generally associated with naïve/memory CD4 + T cells trafficking programs and incomplete effector differentiation [42]. Collectively, our findings challenge the traditional view that increased immune cell infiltration predicts better therapeutic response. This activated-yet-suppressive CD4 + T-cell phenotype may contribute to immune escape and nICT resistance in MIBC, highlighting the importance of evaluating immune cell functional states rather than infiltration abundance alone.

CD8 + T cells can specifically recognize antigen peptide–MHC class I complexes presented on tumor cell surfaces via their T cell receptors (TCRs), enabling targeted elimination of malignant cells [43]. Naive CD8 + T cells, which remain in an unactivated state, differentiate into effector cells upon tumor antigen presentation and serve as a reservoir for immunological memory formation [44]. In this study, minimal infiltration of naive CD8 + T cells was observed in the R and NT groups. In contrast, the NR group showed substantial enrichment of naive CD8 + T cells, which were the predominant CD8 + T subset in these patients. The enrichment of naive CD8 + T cells in NR tumors should not be interpreted as evidence of enhanced antitumor immunity. Rather, it may represent an ineffective priming state in which CD8 + T cells are recruited or retained within the tumor but fail to acquire cytotoxic effector programs. In our trajectory analysis, naive CD8 + T cells were positioned at the initial differentiation stage, whereas effector and cytotoxic CD8 + T cells were at the terminal stage of differentiation and enriched in responders. Moreover, genes associated with the transition toward effector/cytotoxic states were enriched in antigen presentation, TCR signaling, NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and immune response pathways, supporting the notion that productive antigen presentation is required for CD8 + T-cell maturation. Furthermore, the marker profile of this cluster, including H3-3B, H4C3, LINC-PINT, HSP90AA1, and NIBAN1, which indicates chromatin remodeling, cellular stress adaptation, and an incompletely activated state rather than a mature cytotoxic phenotype [45]. Spatially, NR tumors displayed a highly complex stromal–immune architecture, whereas R tumors showed stronger antigen-presentation and immune co-stimulatory pathways, including MHC-I and MHC-II signaling. Although the current spatial transcriptomic resolution did not allow definitive DC subset-level localization, our results suggest that naive CD8 + T cells in NR tumors may be spatially and functionally uncoupled from competent antigen-presenting niches. This impaired priming environment may explain why naive CD8 + T cells accumulate in NR tumors but fail to differentiate into effector CD8 + T cells, thereby contributing to resistance to nICT.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), one of the most abundant immune cell populations within the TME, exhibit marked functional heterogeneity and plasticity, which are tightly regulated by local cellular signals [46]. Both our single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data indicate a higher proportion of Macro-CXCL8 in the NR group, with a tendency toward the M1 subtype, yet its proangiogenic and ECM remodeling capabilities are more pronounced. Prior studies have shown that CXCL8 produced by tumor cells induces an immunosuppressive phenotype in macrophages by activating purine metabolism and associated signaling pathways, thereby decreasing tumor immunity and reducing the effectiveness of immunotherapy [47]. Additional reports indicate that macrophages primarily secrete CXCL8, which helps create an immunosuppressive microenvironment by promoting PD-L1 + macrophages in gastric cancer [48]. Our spatial neighborhood analysis demonstrated that in NR samples, Macro-CXCL8 co-localized with epithelial cells and CAFs at the tumor–stroma interface, while maintaining substantial spatial separation from CD8 + T-NK cells. This finding further confirms that Macro-CXCL8-enriched regions showed preferential spatial association with epithelial cells and CAFs, with reduced spatial adjacency to CD8 + T/NK cells in non-responders. These findings suggest the presence of a stromal-myeloid spatial niche that may be associated with restricted immune effector infiltration, although this inference requires further validation by orthogonal imaging approaches. We propose that Macro-CXCL8 serves as a robust predictor of immunotherapy resistance. Thus, targeting Macro-CXCL8 could offer new opportunities to overcome nICT resistance.

CAFs are critical stromal components that play multifaceted roles in tumor immunotherapy through complex physical, chemical, and immunoregulatory mechanisms [49]. Their functional diversity and dynamic plasticity profoundly influence therapeutic responses [50]. MyCAFs and meCAFs both contribute to altering the spatial structure of the tumor stroma, creating a dense physical barrier that obstructs T cell infiltration [51]. ICAFs secrete IL-6 to expand myeloid-derived suppressor cells and suppress CD8 + T cell function [52]. Our data reveal a substantial increase in the proportion of meCAFs following nICT, accompanied by a reduction in myCAFs. Spatial transcriptomic profiling further confirms extensive infiltration of meCAFs in NR patients. Functional annotation indicates that meCAFs are primarily involved in immunomodulation, ECM organization, angiogenesis, and tumor-progressive signaling, suggesting their active participation in shaping the immunosuppressive network and stromal architecture of MIBC, thereby promoting disease progression. Furthermore, in NT tissues, meCAFs and myCAFs exhibited spatial co-localization primarily with macrophages. After nICT, both CAF subsets displayed closer proximity to epithelial cells, macrophages, and T cells. However, no direct adjacency was observed between epithelial cells and immune effector cells, such as macrophages or T cells. These findings once again demonstrate that CAF-enriched regions exhibited a spatial organization characterized by close proximity to epithelial and myeloid compartments but reduced adjacency between tumor epithelial cells and immune effector cells. This spatial pattern is consistent with a potential stromal-associated restriction of immune cell infiltration and ultimately diminishing the efficacy of nICT.

Given the pivotal role of CAFs in remodeling the TME of MIBC, we assessed spatial distances among all cell subsets. In NT samples, meCAFs, myCAFs, and mast cells primarily localized adjacent to epithelial cells, suggesting that CAFs may be closely linked to tumor progression, potentially by transmitting aberrant signals that drive malignant transformation of epithelial cells. The NR group exhibited a highly interconnected TME, with close proximity among meCAFs, myCAFs, epithelial cells, T cells, and macrophages, reflecting a complex, densely interacting stromal–immune network. In the R group, the dominant spatial adjacency was observed between inflamed epithelial cells and naive CD8 + T cells. Single-cell data were used to analyze cell–cell chat and compare communication differences between the NR and R groups. Consistent with this, meCAFs and myCAFs in the NR group demonstrated significantly enhanced communication with other cell subsets. Moreover, while the R group displayed a predominance of immune-activating and growth-promoting signals, the NR group was enriched for immunosuppressive and matrix-remodeling pathways.

These results offer a fresh perspective on how CAFs communicate within cells during MIBC immunotherapy responses and highlight the essential role of CAFs in tumor immunotherapy, whether through spatial separation of immune cells or through molecular signaling. Subsequently, we focused on the link between drug-resistant signaling molecules and the interaction between CAFs and other cell types. Among these signaling molecules, both myCAFs and meCAFs exhibited broad microenvironment-modulatory capacities, extending beyond stromal remodeling to encompass immune regulation and epithelial cell reprogramming. Notably, signaling from meCAFs to myCAFs supports the concept of dynamic interconversion and functional complementarity between CAF subtypes, collectively shaping a tumor-promoting stromal niche. Moreover, robust outgoing signals from both CAF subsets toward CD8 + T-Exhausted and Macrophage-CXCL8 suggest that CAFs may foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment by driving T cell exhaustion.

Furthermore, we elucidated a critical TAMs-CAFs communication axis mediated by SPP1, which is secreted by macrophages and received by myCAFs. SPP1 is a multifunctional extracellular matrix protein whose expression correlates strongly with the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, resistance to immunotherapy, and poor prognosis [53]. SPP1 can trigger macrophage conversion to the M2 phenotype [54]. Liu et al. found that SPP1 stimulates CAFs to produce CXCL12 via the β-catenin/HIF-1α signaling pathway, thereby supporting EMT in CRC cells and limiting CD8 + T cell infiltration [55]. Because patients in the R and NR groups received PD-L1 blockade combined with cisplatin-based chemotherapy, we further examined whether the resistance-associated Macro-CXCL8 and SPP-CAF signatures could be explained by immunotherapy alone. The ABACUS cohort study strengthens this conclusion. Although the overall Macro-CXCL8 score did not significantly increase after immunotherapy in paired samples, post-treatment non-responders displayed significantly higher Macro-CXCL8 activity and markedly increased FAP-high stromal areas compared with responders. The positive correlation between Macro-CXCL8 ssGSEA score and FAP-high tumor area suggests that CXCL8-enriched macrophages may cooperate with CAF activation or stromal expansion during the development of immunotherapy resistance. Importantly, these associations were not observed in chemotherapy-only cohorts, indicating that the Macro-CXCL8/SPP1-CAF axis may unlikely to be merely chemotherapy-induced alterations. Instead, it may represent an immunotherapy-specific resistance niche characterized by macrophage-driven inflammation, CAF-mediated stromal remodeling, and impaired antitumor immune activation. Nevertheless, given the combined treatment design of our discovery cohort and the limited availability of independent nICT datasets with matched single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiles, these findings should be interpreted as immunotherapy-resistance-associated rather than definitively immunotherapy-induced, and future cohorts with matched chemotherapy-only, immunotherapy-only, and combination-treatment arms will be required for complete mechanistic dissection. Moreover, in our additional multiplex immunofluorescence validation, nICT-insensitive tumors showed stronger CXCL8 expression and enrichment of FAP/SPP1-positive stromal areas, consistent with the macrophage–CAF resistance niche identified by single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses, which supporting the possibility of developing a pre-treatment biomarker panel to identify patients at high risk of nICT failure. Future prospective studies using larger pre-treatment biopsy cohorts are warranted to standardize scoring criteria and validate the clinical utility of this assay.

Previous studies have established that molecular subtype, immune infiltration, stromal exclusion, and spatial immune organization are closely associated with the response of MIBC to nICT or immunotherapy. These findings provide an important framework for understanding therapy responsiveness in BCa. However, most prior studies have focused on bulk transcriptomic signatures or spatial immune niches, while the coordinated single-cell and spatial remodeling of macrophage-CAFs communication after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy remains incompletely defined. In this context, our study confirms several known principles, including the importance of T-cell functional states, macrophage plasticity, CAF-mediated stromal remodeling, and immune exclusion. More importantly, our integrated analysis identifies a resistance-associated Macro-CXCL8 population that spatially co-localizes with CAFs in non-responding tumors and communicates with myCAFs through SPP1-related signaling. Therefore, the major novelty of this study is not the identification of macrophages or CAFs perse, but the definition of a spatially organized Macro-CXCL8-SPP1-CAFs axis that may reconstruct an immunosuppressive and immune-excluded microenvironment in MIBC after nICT.

This study is not without limitations. First, the neoadjuvant regimen used herein combined immunotherapy with chemotherapy rather than using immunotherapy alone. Therefore, the observed changes in the tumor immune microenvironment are attributed not only to immunotherapy but also to chemotherapy. Second, clinical protocols governing surgical approaches and neoadjuvant treatment strategies made it challenging to obtain sufficient tissue from MIBC patients after immunotherapy: transurethral resection specimens were unsuitable for spatial transcriptomic profiling. A larger, more diverse sample cohort would enable a more robust characterization of therapy-associated TME remodeling. Furthermore, the considerable inter-individual heterogeneity observed among patients underscores the intrinsic biological diversity of BCa, which likely contributed to the lack of statistically significant differences in certain comparative analyses across groups. We acknowledge that the lack of matched pre- and post-treatment specimens is an important limitation of this study. The NT samples were obtained from independent untreated cystectomy specimens and therefore cannot serve as within-patient baseline controls for the R and NR groups. Consequently, the observed differences among NT, R and NR tumors should be interpreted as cross-sectional TME features associated with treatment exposure and response status, rather than definitive longitudinal evidence of therapy-induced remodeling. In addition, scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics did not always show complete concordance in inferred cell-type abundance or program activity, which is not unexpected given the inherent differences in tissue processing, transcript capture, spatial resolution, and analytical purpose between the two platforms. Spatial transcriptomics was not intended to serve as a strict replication of scRNA-seq abundance estimates; instead, it was used to provide spatial context for scRNA-seq-defined cell states and transcriptional programs, particularly for assessing cell–cell proximity, spatial co-localization, and resistance-associated tissue niches. These modalities should therefore be regarded as complementary rather than directly interchangeable.

Conclusion

In summary, our study delineates response-associated TME features following nICT in MIBC. The accumulation of activated CD4 + T cells, naive CD8 + T cells, and Macro-CXCL8 serves as a potential biomarker of nICT resistance in MIBC. A central mechanistic insight is that SPP1, secreted by Macro-CXCL8, reprograms CAFs to reshape the immune landscape, thereby driving immune evasion and reconstructing an immunosuppressive TME in MIBC.
