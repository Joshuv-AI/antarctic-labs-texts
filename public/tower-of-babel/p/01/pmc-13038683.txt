A novel methodology for using light-scattering sensors to measure indoor particulate matter in a multi-site clinical trial
Authors: Ethan S Walker, Sara M Cox, David Jones, Taylor Stewart, Jennifer Faiella, Cindy S Leary, Laurie Chassereau, Linda Y Fu, Jeannette Y Lee, Paul G Smith, Kelly Cowan, Erin O Semmens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeasuring airborne fine particulate matter (PM2.5) in the context of health-based field research introduces many logistical challenges including remote equipment setup, data access, and maintenance by both researchers and participants. In preparation for a 17-site, 228-household clinical trial, we developed methods to validate low-cost, light-scattering PM2.5 sensors (model PAII-SD, PurpleAir, Inc, USA) against a Federal Equivalent Method instrument (model BAM 1020, Met One Instruments, USA) for remote measurement of indoor PM2.5 concentrations among participating households over 6 months. To allow real-time, remote data access by the research team, we developed procedures for pairing the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors for each household with a portable Wi-Fi hotspot device (model Solis Lite, Skyroam, Inc, USA) prior to shipping them to participants. Armed with this remote data access, we created a process for daily automated data extractions to ensure sensors remained online and to assess data quality. Our methods support the feasibility of obtaining long-term, continuous PM2.5 measurement reliably and accurately in the context of a research trial using low-cost, light-scattering sensors. The framework we describe in this methods paper can serve as a model for future environmental health research studies.Keywords: PM2.5, Low-cost sensors, Indoor air pollution, Exposure assessment methods

Introduction

Particulate matter air pollution is the leading risk factor for premature mortality globally [1]. There is clear evidence from decades of research that demonstrates causal, adverse effects of air pollution on all-cause, cardiovascular, and respiratory morbidity and mortality [2–4]. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5; particulate matter less than 2.5 µm in aerodynamic diameter) is a key component of air pollution that is associated with numerous adverse health outcomes and is particularly harmful to susceptible subpopulations such as older adults, infants and children, and those with pre-existing health conditions [5].

There are numerous sources of air pollution of both indoor and outdoor origin. While policy initiatives often focus on reducing outdoor air pollution from sources such as traffic and industry, researchers and public health practitioners have also recognized the importance of improving air quality in the indoor environment. In the United States (US), where individuals spend 90% of their time indoors [6], indoor air pollution is a complex mixture from various sources depending on the location of the building and how it is used. Residences can have poor indoor air quality from household cooking, heating, and smoking, as well as from outdoor air pollution infiltrating indoors [7, 8]. Household heating from wood stoves and fireplaces can substantially increase indoor concentrations of PM2.5, particularly in rural parts of the US [9–12]. Wildfire events have increased dramatically over the past few decades, and it is well documented that smoke from local and regional fires can infiltrate indoors and degrade air quality [13–16].

While residential indoor air quality is complex, it is also modifiable through individual- and household-level intervention strategies such as using portable air cleaners (PACs) with high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters [7]. However, many studies assessing PAC impact on indoor air quality and health have been short in duration. Longer-term studies are needed to understand how PAC effectiveness changes over time and how air pollution reduction through PAC use might improve health over longer periods [17, 18].

Reliable and accurate exposure assessment methods are needed that can be feasibly implemented at a household level to help evaluate intervention strategies. PM2.5 is the pollutant most often used to assess PAC effectiveness due to accessible measurement methods and known impacts on health. Measuring PM2.5 in the context of health-based field research introduces many logistical challenges including equipment setup, data access, and maintenance by both researchers and participants [19].

Ground-based monitors used for regulatory compliance as Federal Equivalent Methods (FEM) or Federal Reference Methods (FRM) typically use methods such as gravimetric filter-based sampling or beta attenuation to measure PM2.5 mass concentrations [20]. While these monitors are considered the most accurate method of measuring PM2.5 concentrations, the measurements are logistically burdensome and time consuming to conduct [20]. In addition, FEM and FRM PM2.5 monitors are expensive, costing several thousand dollars per unit. Consequently, FEM and FRM monitors are typically not feasible to use in studies with large sample sizes and/or of longer duration given their requirement for study staff to deploy a unit to each participant’s home and make numerous visits to the households to conduct repeated sampling campaigns.

A more feasible alternative to FEM/FRM monitors is using light-scattering sensors [21]. Light-scattering sensors calculate number and concentration of different particle size fractions by measuring light scattered when particles from a known volume of air pass through a light source. Such sensors are generally low-cost (~ $200–300 United States Dollars per unit), portable, and allow for real-time data collection over long periods of time. However, light scattering sensors are also less accurate than FRM/FEM methods, and measured particle concentrations can be impacted by varying particle density and meteorological conditions such as relative humidity [22]. Concentrations need to be corrected to align with more accurate reference methods.

Given the complexities and the importance of continuous and long-term exposure assessment in the field of environmental health research, we developed novel methods for household-level PM2.5 measurement in preparation for a 17-site clinical trial called the BREATHE Study (Bronchiolitis Recovery and the Use of HEPA Filters). In the BREATHE Study, we planned to use low-cost, light-scattering sensors over a 6-month study among 228 households in rural US states. To assure a robust protocol for data quality assurance and control to measure indoor PM2.5, we performed validation of each of the study sensors in laboratory conditions. Below we describe the methods we developed to validate and correct light-scattering PM2.5 sensor measurements to align with those of an FEM instrument and prepare sensors to allow near-continuous PM2.5 data transmission to the research team for daily data quality assurance checks. We also propose a framework for the research team to feasibly screen the voluminous incoming data for signals of risk early on to alert participants of potentially high-risk PM2.5 levels in their home.

Materials and methods

Trial overview

The household air quality exposure assessment methods described in this manuscript were developed in preparation for conducting the BREATHE Study. The BREATHE Study is a multi-center, parallel, double-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial that took place across 17 sites that are a part of the Institutional Development Award (IDeA) States Pediatric Clinical Trial Network (ISPCTN). The ISPCTN is a part of the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Program of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). A protocol for the BREATHE Study has been published previously [23], and the trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Trial Number: NCT05615870; Initial Registration Date: November 8, 2022). Briefly, 228 infants less than 12 months of age and hospitalized with bronchiolitis were enrolled in a 26 week study to test the efficacy of in-home PAC use on respiratory symptoms, unscheduled healthcare visits for respiratory complaints, infant quality of life, and indoor concentrations of PM2.5. After enrollment and hospital discharge, study coordinators sent caregivers home with study equipment including two low-cost, light-scattering PM2.5 sensors paired with a Wi-Fi hotspot device for data transmission. PM2.5 concentrations were measured over the 26 week study period in the infant’s sleep space and in a common room where the infant spent most of their time when not sleeping. Written, informed consent to participate was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. The BREATHE Study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the study protocol was approved by The University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences Institutional Review Board (FWA0000119) and the Alaska Area Institutional Review Board.

Selection of a light-scattering PM2.5 sensor for household PM2.5 measurement

We chose to use a light-scattering PM2.5 sensor from PurpleAir (PAII-SD, PurpleAir, Inc., USA) to measure indoor PM2.5 concentrations for this trial for several reasons. PAII-SD light-scattering sensors are small, portable devices (approximately 85 mm × 85 mm × 125 mm) that contain dual laser particle counters (PMS5003, Plantower, China) that estimate particle numbers and mass concentrations at 2-min intervals [24]. Mass concentrations are calculated from particle count data using proprietary algorithms developed by the PMS5003 sensor manufacturer and are provided in two data series which are designated “CF = ATM” and “CF = 1” [22]. PAII-SD light-scattering PM2.5 sensors also measure temperature, relative humidity, and barometric pressure at 2-min intervals using a separate sensor (BME280, Bosch Sensortec GmbH, Germany). Data from each sensor are stored on internal Secure Digital (SD) cards and are transferred in real-time to the manufacturer’s remote servers when connected to Wi-Fi.

Pairing sensors with Wi-Fi hotspot devices for real-time data transmission

To be able to help participants troubleshoot unexpected technology issues, thereby improving data completeness and accuracy, the research team felt it was important not to wait until the end of the 6-month intervention period to retrieve sensor data from SD cards. For this reason, prior to disseminating to participants, we paired sensors with Wi-Fi hotspot devices (model Solis Lite, Skyroam, Inc., USA), allowing the research team to monitor and retrieve sensor data from households in real time using the sensor manufacturer’s online database. A cellular data plan of 20 gigabytes was pre-purchased for each Wi-Fi hotspot device to allow each participant’s 2 sensors to transmit household PM2.5 concentrations (and temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure readings) every 2 min over the duration of the study (Fig. 1).

Collocation check of light-scattering sensors with a reference monitor

To ensure data accuracy, we collocated the PM2.5 sensors with a continuous particulate monitor (model BAM1020, Met One Instruments, Inc., USA; hereafter referred as the “reference monitor”) designated as a PM2.5 FEM by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). The collocation step was to verify internal consistency between the two particle counters within each light-scattering PM2.5 sensor, and to ensure that PM2.5 data generated by each light-scattering PM2.5 sensor consistently tracked with the reference monitor prior to deployment to study sites.

The reference monitor was set up according to manufacturer standards at an indoor laboratory and calibrated by a certified air quality specialist from the State of Montana Department of Environmental Quality prior to collocation. Study analysts then performed monthly maintenance and calibration on the reference monitor throughout the duration of the clinical trial. A collocation check of each light-scattering PM2.5 sensor with the reference monitor was conducted for a period of at least 48 h. We chose a minimum of 48 h for collocation to help ensure variation in PM2.5 over the collocation period. Longer collocation periods were logistically not feasible due to the large number of sensors being prepared for deployment to study sites within a limited timeframe.

In collocation checks, a maximum of 20 light-scattering PM2.5 sensors were placed at a similar distance around the PM2.5 reference monitor (within 3 m) on tables approximately 1 m above the floor. To ensure sensors were accurate over a range of airborne PM2.5 concentrations, we temporarily increased PM2.5 concentrations in the laboratory with an incense coil placed adjacent to the reference monitor and sensors. This process raised the PM2.5 concentrations for approximately 4 to 6 h and allowed us to assess data from sensors at a wide range of PM2.5 concentrations to mimic scenarios that would be encountered in homes during the clinical trial. We increased the PM2.5 concentrations at least two times for each collocation check.

Correcting light-scattering PM2.5 sensor concentrations

Following the collocation check, we compared readings from the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors to the reference monitor to ensure all sensors were functioning as expected. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.0 [25]. We cleaned and processed the sensor data with guidance from previous publications, including those within our group [15, 26]. Prior to matching the sensor and reference monitor data, we created new variables for hourly mean PM2.5 concentrations (using the “CF = 1” data series) and relative humidity. When calculating hourly means, we included only hours with at least 50% of expected data (i.e., at least 15 of 30 expected 2-min datapoints). We then corrected the mean hourly PM2.5 concentrations using the correction equation described in the following section. Prior to analysis, we matched the corrected hourly mean PM2.5 concentrations with the reference monitor PM2.5 data that is recorded in hourly means by default.

Correction equation

Previous studies have found that light-scattering PM2.5 sensors overestimate PM2.5 concentrations compared to reference methods [22, 26–30]. Barkjohn et al. developed a nationwide correction equation for light-scattering PM2.5 sensors using collocated light-scattering PM2.5 sensors and FRM/FEM instruments from across the US [26]. Given that the BREATHE Study was to take place across 17 US states, we used this peer-reviewed, nationally representative equation to correct hourly mean PM2.5 concentrations for all sensor data generated in the clinical trial.

Four of the 568 sensors (0.7%) that were collocated for the BREATHE Study did not meet the established criteria during collocation and were returned to the manufacturer. One sensor was not sampling consistently at 2 min intervals, one had a faulty channel that resulted in internal disagreement between the two particle counters, one did not sample correctly during periods with higher PM2.5 concentrations, and one had a faulty temperature/relative humidity sensor. Beyond these four issues, all other sensors passed the collocation check and were used in the clinical trial.

Flow of data and quality control during the clinical trial

The flow of data from the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors to the University of Montana (UM) central site during the study is described in the Figure. This real-time transmission of data began once the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors and hotspot were turned on in a participating household. If the caregiver was unable to acquire an adequate signal for the hotspot to produce a consistent Wi-Fi connection, that household would rely solely on SD data collection. For homes that did have a Wi-Fi connection from the hotspot, data from the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors would upload to the manufacturer’s server at 2 min intervals. UM personnel set up real-time data retrieval where data were downloaded from the manufacturer’s server and saved to a database on a secure UM server. This download to the UM database updated every minute through an application programming interface (API), and every new datapoint was added to the database.

In addition to real-time data retrieval, we also set up automated daily data checks to ensure the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors remained online and functioning properly. Once per day, we downloaded the previous 24 h of data for all sensors. Separately for each sensor, we ran the data through quality checks to assess for missing and extreme values for PM2.5 and relative humidity. We also checked the differences in PM2.5 concentrations between the two particle counters within each sensor to ensure internal consistency. Lastly, the daily automated data checks referenced the timestamp of the last PM2.5 datapoint for each sensor to ensure it was still online and actively transmitting data. If a sensor had been offline for more than 1 h, the UM research team received an automated notification with the sensor ID and timestamp of the last datapoint.

Developing a threshold for reporting high PM2.5 concentrations to participants

A key consideration when collecting real-time indoor air quality data is the ethics of reporting high PM2.5 concentrations back to study participants. In consultation with air quality experts on the research team, we established a threshold and a workflow for notifying participants if the PM2.5 concentration in their home went above this threshold. Our threshold was a weekly mean indoor PM2.5 concentration of 100 µg/m3 using in-home sensor data that had the Eq. 1 correction equation applied. This threshold was approximately equal to an outlier PM2.5 concentration (3 times the interquartile range above the 75th percentile) from previous studies conducted by the UM team among homes with wood heating stoves [9, 11, 12]. Our rationale for the threshold was that, given the rural setting of the BREATHE Study sites, higher indoor PM2.5 concentrations were likely to be common in study households that used wood fuel for heating. We wanted to avoid over-notifying participants of PM2.5 concentrations that are typical in wood stove homes, while also establishing a threshold that would identify PM2.5 concentrations of concern across the spectrum of study households. We used weekly mean PM2.5 concentrations instead of shorter periods to avoid notifying participants unnecessarily if an isolated incident (e.g., cooking) was the source of a temporary elevation in indoor PM2.5 concentration. Overall, our procedure for a high PM2.5 concentration data alert went as follows:

If a sensor exceeded 24-h mean PM2.5 > 100 µg/m3 at least two times in a 7-day period, data for both sensors in the household were examined from the previous 7 days.

If a sensor exceeded 24-h mean PM2.5 > 100 µg/m3 at least two times in a 7-day period, data for both sensors in the household were examined from the previous 7 days.

After checking data quality and applying the Eq. 1 correction equation, the participant would be notified if the 7-day mean PM2.5 concentration exceeded 100 µg/m3 for any sensor in the household.

At the time of notification, the participant would be provided with a written report with figures showing the indoor PM2.5 from their sensors and information on how to improve indoor air quality. Participants would also be informed that while the sensors would continue to be collecting air quality data from the home, study staff would not be reviewing their data again until the end of their participation, at which point the information would be summarized and shared with them.

Active data monitoring during the 4-week period would allow the research team to identify homes with consistently high PM2.5 concentrations regardless of treatment arm assignment, as the active monitoring would span the 2 week pre-intervention period and the first 2 weeks of the intervention period.

Discussion

We have described the methods we developed for indoor PM2.5 exposure monitoring for use during a 17-site, 228 household clinical trial using low-cost, light-scattering sensors paired with Wi-Fi hotspots. Our pre-study workflows, including pairing sensors with Wi-Fi hotspots and quality-checking sensor data through collocation with a FEM monitor, helped ensure data quality while limiting equipment burden on site coordinators and participating families. Our active study workflows with data redundancy through Wi-Fi and SD collection methods helped minimize data loss and ensure high data quality. Our methods allow researchers to collect indoor PM2.5 data remotely without ever visiting a study household, leading to increased feasibility and reduced budget in a large trial setting.

While low-cost sensors are becoming more common in environmental health research, the scope and implementation methods developed for use in the BREATHE Study are innovative and highlight the utility and potential of low-cost sensors in future work (Table 1). Previous clinical trials in residential settings have often been short in duration and use crossover designs in which short-term PM2.5 sampling is more feasible [31–35]. Longer-term studies have often implemented intermittent indoor exposure data collection using light-scattering sensors or gravimetric sampling [11, 36–38]. As low-cost sensor technology has improved and the cost of such sensors decreased, it is becoming more feasible and affordable to deploy sensors to field settings to sample indoor or outdoor air quality data with high spatial and temporal resolution for a fraction of the cost of a FRM or FEM monitor. These data make it possible to explore novel research questions, including the health impacts of repeated, short-term (sub-daily) spikes in PM2.5 such as those seen during wildfire smoke events, or more robust and long-term indoor PM2.5 exposures such as those measured in the BREATHE Study.

As we described in the introduction, light-scattering PM2.5 sensors do not have perfect alignment with FRM or FEM monitors, even after correction equations have been applied. However, as their use becomes more common in research settings, and given the growing network of public sensors and accessible data, correction methods to improve the accuracy of light-scattering sensors continue to advance. Correction equations continue to be developed for light-scattering PM2.5 sensors, including source-specific equations to account for particle characteristics that can vary by source (e.g., density and refractive index), as well as more complex equations that vary depending on the magnitude of the PM2.5 concentration [22, 27, 29, 39]. These advances in the processing and accuracy of PM2.5 sensor data will lead to further application in environmental health research.

Our decision to use a single, nationally representative correction equation for all PM2.5 sensor data was largely based on the wide scope of study sites in the BREATHE Study. The 17 BREATHE Study sites spanned numerous regions across the US, and it was not within the scope of the trial to develop site-specific correction equations representative of each location. The correction equation that we used went through rigorous testing and peer review, including evaluation of performance by US region [40]. The authors reported that the correction equation performed well and bias was reduced across all US regions, particularly after incorporating relative humidity into the correction equation. They also report that their equation aligns with other corrections developed for specific locations or sources, which further strengthens application across the US. These results give us confidence in using this peer-reviewed correction equation for all BREATHE Study sites. Future studies with fewer sites or shorter timeframes may be able to develop and implement site- or source-specific correction equations to low-cost sensor data to further align with FRM/FEM monitors.

Another consideration about the correction equation used in the BREATHE Study is that it was developed using collocated outdoor light-scattering PM2.5 sensors [40]. In contrast, all BREATHE Study sensors were used indoors where pollution sources (e.g., cooking and cleaning) may differ from those used to develop the correction equation. While this is a potential limitation in our study, there is no indoor-specific correction equation for light-scattering PM2.5 sensors due to the wide range of potential indoor air pollution sources that are highly variable across households. In addition, a common source of indoor air pollution is outdoor sources of air pollution that infiltrate to the indoor environment. In the absence of developing building-specific correction equations that capture the nuanced environment of each study household, the nationally representative correction equation is a viable alternative that can easily be implemented in larger studies.

We have also considered real-world implementation considerations and potential adaptation strategies for future research studies, discussed here and highlighted in Table 2. The largest benefit of pairing the light-scattering PM2.5 sensors to Wi-Fi hotspot devices is the ability to set up data alerts to notify participants if a sensor malfunctions or becomes unplugged to limit data loss. In fact, we did have occasions during the trial in which the research team found sensors that had stopped sending data, and were able to replace a sensor that had failed or ask a participant to plug their sensor back in to a power source. However, it is worthwhile to mention that pairing sensors to Wi-Fi hotspot devices does increase pre-study setup logistics. Further, while data alerts can be automated through real-time data pulls, tracking and responding to such alerts requires substantial time commitments by the research team. Logistical considerations such as number of sensors and length of follow-up should be considered when deciding how similar procedures may be implemented in future studies. As with many other aspects of research, there is a difficult balance of cost, logistics, and data quality in environmental exposure assessment.

Methodological steps, key considerations, and potential adaptations for measuring indoor PM2.5 in future clinical trials

A major takeaway from the development and implementation of this methodology is the ethical considerations of working with real-time indoor air quality data. As we described in the methods, we established a workflow for monitoring and notifying participants of high PM2.5 concentrations. While we consulted with the BREATHE Study Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) to develop appropriate thresholds for the clinical trial, investigators should take careful consideration when adapting these methodologies to future studies. Our threshold of high PM2.5 was based on our trial’s likelihood of oversampling rural households which may use wood stoves for primary or secondary heating sources. High PM2.5 concentration alerts only occurred in the first 4 weeks of participation due to logistical and resource limitations across our 17-site trial. Researchers should consider the study population, location, and sampling timeframes when establishing similar thresholds for future studies. Consultation with environmental health experts and study DSMB and/or Institutional Review Board is advised.

Although we have shown the feasibility of implementing these methods in a multi-site field study, these efforts were a large undertaking, and careful consideration should be taken to adapt our methods to future studies (Table 2). Crucially, the central site team at UM was experienced in conducting environmental health field studies in rural areas prior to the BREATHE Study. Our previous experience, including work with light-scattering PM2.5 sensors and Wi-Fi hotspots, made these resource-heavy protocols feasible for our team of PI, Co-I, site coordinator, and three part-time staff members. There was also considerable effort required on the part of clinical-site coordinators and participant caregivers to install the sensors and check that they were working correctly in each household. We have used similar methods in smaller studies and other settings including long-term care facilities and schools. Given appropriate resources, we believe these methods can be scaled and adapted for successful implementation in various other settings.

Conclusion

The indoor PM2.5 exposure monitoring methods we established for the BREATHE Study highlight both the strong potential and the logistical and ethical considerations of utilizing low-cost sensors in future trials. We successfully implemented these methods, in addition to the entire workflow for the remote setup and delivery of the study intervention, through the central site team at UM. Our team has demonstrated the feasibility of remotely delivering low-cost sensors to field sites across a wide geographic region to collect a robust dataset of PM2.5 concentrations in a multi-site clinical trial. The methods we have described provide an adaptable framework for future studies to implement continuous air quality monitoring in field settings that allow investigators to more accurately evaluate intervention efficacy and to understand more temporally resolved estimates of the impact of air pollutant exposure on health outcomes.
