Medium to Long-Term Patient-Oriented Effectiveness of Surgical or Percutaneous Revascularization in Diabetes and Severe Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Dominique Vervoort, Yuchen Dai, Anan Bader Eddeen, Meltem Tuna, Peter C. Austin, Maral Ouzounian, Stephen Fremes, Douglas S. Lee, Harindra C. Wijeysundera, Louise Y. Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with diabetes and coronary artery disease, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) confers a long-term survival advantage over percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI); however, a knowledge gap remains regarding patient-centred outcomes. We therefore compared patient-defined adverse cardiovascular and noncardiovascular events (PACE) after CABG vs PCI in diabetic patients.MethodsWe conducted a population-based, retrospective cohort study of all diabetic patients who underwent CABG or PCI between October 2008 and December 2018 in Ontario, Canada. The primary outcome was PACE, defined as the composite of severe stroke requiring hospitalization > 14 days or inpatient rehabilitation, ventilator dependence, heart failure hospitalization, long-term care admission, and new-onset dialysis. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality and individual PACE components. Overlap weighting using propensity scores was performed to account for baseline differences between groups. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated, and groups were compared using cause-specific hazard models (with death as a competing risk) and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsA total of 58,826 patients (25,751 CABG; 33,075 PCI) were followed for a mean of 4.7 years (standard deviation 3.1; maximum 11.5). Rates of PACE were lower after CABG vs PCI over 1-5 years postintervention (1-year HR 0.88 [95% CI 0.77-1.00]; 5-year HR 0.83 [95% CI 0.71-0.97]). Rates of death were lower after CABG during the first 8 years (30-day HR 0.61 [95% CI 0.50-0.74]; 5-year HR 0.71 [95% CI 0.63-0.80]; 8-year HR 0.82 [95% CI 0.69-0.98]).ConclusionsWe observed lower rates of PACE and all-cause mortality in diabetic patients within the first 5 years after CABG, compared to PCI. Findings could be used to inform shared decision-making to achieve outcomes most valued by patients.

In patients with severe coronary artery disease (CAD), revascularization by coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is preferred over optimal medical therapy to reduce symptoms and improve long-term survival.1 Patients with diabetes are known to have a more aggressive and diffuse atherosclerotic burden,2 as well as higher rates of mortality and repeat revascularization after primary coronary revascularization.3 The Future Revascularization Evaluation in Patients with Diabetes Mellitus: Optimal Management of Multivessel Disease (FREEDOM) trial is the largest study to date comparing CABG vs PCI in 1900 patients with diabetes and multivessel CAD.4 At 5 years, all-cause mortality was lower in the CABG group, regardless of CAD complexity.4 At 7.5 years of follow-up in 943 patients, all-cause mortality was higher after PCI as compared to CABG (23.7% vs 18.7%).5 Other major trials, such as the Bypass Angioplasty Revascularization Investigation 2 Diabetes (BARI-2D)6, the Synergy Between Percutaneous Coronary Intervention With TAXUS and Cardiac Surgery (SYNTAX)7, and the Randomized Comparison of Coronary Artery Bypass Surgery and Everolimus-Eluting Stent Implantation in the Treatment of Patients with Multivessel Coronary Artery Disease (BEST)8 trials, found reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events for CABG in patients with diabetes undergoing revascularization. A recent population-based analysis of patients with diabetes and multivessel CAD requiring coronary revascularization in Ontario, Canada, found that CABG was associated with lower long-term rates of mortality and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) compared to PCI.9 These findings have been confirmed in several large meta-analyses.10, 11, 12 Despite a long-term survival benefit in favour of CABG, great variation remains in the choice of revascularization strategy in this patient population,13 which may in part be related to patients’ preference to avert the early risks of this procedure. Despite the volume of trial and observational data comparing the outcomes of revascularziation strategies, a paucity of data address outcomes that matter most to patients.

Cardiovascular research has traditionally emphasized MACCE and mortality; however, patients often prioritize outcomes related to their ability to maintain personal mobility and independence.14,15 Our group engaged cardiovascular patients, their caregivers, and a multidiscipinary team of clinicians through a national consensus panel to codevelop patient-defined adverse cardiovascular and noncardiovascular events (PACE) as an outcome that more closely reflects patient preferences and values.16 We conducted a population-based study to compare the patient-oriented effectiveness of CABG and PCI in patients with diabetes and severe CAD.

Methods

Study design and study population

A population-based, retrospective cohort study was performed to examine adult diabetic patients aged between 40 and 85 years who underwent index isolated coronary revascularization with either CABG or PCI for multivessel, left anterior descending (LAD), or left main CAD in Ontario, Canada, between October 1, 2008 and December 31, 2018. For patients who underwent multiple revascularization procedures during the study period, the earliest procedure was designated as the index intervention. We excluded patients who were nonresidents of Ontario, ineligible for coverage under the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP), had missing age or sex data, or died on the day of the index procedure. Additional exclusion criteria were as follows: CABG and PCI during the same admission; emergency procedures requiring immediate revascularization on presentation; presenting with cardiogenic shock, non-LAD single-vessel disease; being on dialysis or ventilator dependent within 90 days before revascularization; having metastatic cancer within 5 years; or being long-term care (LTC) residents immediately preceding the index procedure. Our exclusion criteria are consistent with those used in major revascularization trials comparing PCI and CABG (ie, patients with limited life expectancy, serious noncardiac comorbidities, or terminal illness that could confound long-term outcomes, particularly all-cause mortality and, in our study, PACE endpoints).4,7,8 These exclusions help ensure that findings apply to patients most likely to benefit from revascularization. Data source and linkages

The dataset from this study is held securely in coded form at ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences). The use of the data in this project is authorized under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA) and does not require review by a research ethics board.

We linked procedures identified from the CorHealth Ontario clinical registry with population-level administrative healthcare databases at ICES. ICES is an independent, nonprofit research institute, with a legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law that allows it to collect and analyze healthcare and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. Datasets were linked deterministically using confidential identifiers and analyzed at ICES. ICES contains healthcare data of all Ontario residents, thereby eliminating any participation bias. The Canadian Institute for Health Information’s Discharge Abstract Database (DAD) was used to identify comorbidities and hospital admissions based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision Canada (ICD-10CA).17 The Same Day Surgery database (SDS) was used to obtain additional comorbidities. Frailty was defined by the Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups (ACG) frailty indicator.18 The National Rehabilitation Reporting System (NRS) was used to identify severe stroke. The Continuing Care Reporting System (CCRS) was used to identify LTC admissions. The Canadian Organ Replacement Registry (CORR) was used to identify dialysis use. The Assistive Devices Program (ADP) was used to identify ventilator use. The OHIP database was used to obtain physician service claims. Lastly, the Registered Persons Database and Canadian Census were used to obtain patients’ vital statistics and socioeconomic (neighbourhood median income) and rural–urban status, respectively.

CorHealth Ontario is an advisory body to the Ontario Ministry of Health that maintains a detailed clinical registry encompassing data on all invasive cardiovascular procedures performed in Ontario. These include patient demographic, comorbidity, and anatomic data, such as ejection fraction and angiogram results, which have been validated by selected chart audits and core laboratory validation.14 In the angiographic database, CAD was defined as ≥ 50% stenosis in the left main coronary artery and ≥ 70% stenosis for all other coronary arteries.19 Incomplete revascularization was defined as the presence of residual anatomically significant coronary stenoses that were not revascularized during the index procedure, consistent with the angiographic data elements available in the CorHealth Ontario Registry. CorHealth Ontario records were linked to the above described databases using methods previously described by the authors.9,20,21

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was PACE, defined as the composite of postoperative severe stroke requiring hospitalization for at least 14 days or inpatient rehabilitation, ventilator dependence, new-onset or worsening heart failure (HF), LTC admission, and new-onset dialysis.16 The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality. Consistent with our previous studies,14,21 severe stroke was identified using the DAD and the NRS; ventilator dependence was defined using the ADP and OHIP; HF was defined using the ICES Congestive HF Database based on a validated algorithm with 85% sensitivity and 97% specificity for identifying HF cases22; LTC admission was defined using the CCRS, Ontario Drug Benefit, and OHIP; and dialysis was defined using the DAD, SDS, OHIP, and CORR. Mortality was ascertained using the RPDB.

Missing data

Glomerular filtration rate was missing in 4247 patients (7.2%), operative priority in 945 patients (1.6%), median neighbourhood income in 235 patients (0.4%), left ventricular ejection fraction in 8118 patients (13.8%), and rurality in 79 patients (0.1%). These missing values were imputed within the SAS “proc MI” framework (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) in the overall cohort, for which they were predicted using all candidate covariates using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables.23,24 Twenty datasets were imputed, and Rubin’s Rules were used to pool estimated regression coefficients and their associated standrad errors across the 20 complete datasets.

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) and were compared between groups using the Student t test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data were presented as numbers (proportions) and were compared using the χ2 test.

Patients were followed for a maximum of 11.5 years for the development of PACE or death, and were censored when they no longer qualified for OHIP coverage (ie, they moved out of the province). To account for death as a competing risk, we estimated the incidence of PACE and individual PACE components using cumulative incidence functions. Unweighted outcomes were presented as event rates.

To reduce confounding due to differences in baseline characteristics between groups, we performed overlap weighting based on propensity scores to estimate the effect of revascularization modality while controlling for baseline patient and procedure characteristics between groups.25,26 In doing so, our focus is on estimating the effect of CABG in those subjects whose propensity score is close to 0.5. To do this, we first used logistic regression to estimate propensity scores for CABG (vs PCI), using variables that were selected a priori (Table 1). When estimating the propensity-score model, we treated hospitals as a random effect to account for clustering of patients within institutions. In the process of overlap weighting, each patient is assigned a weight that equals the probability of that patient being assigned to the opposite treatment group.26 This method gives higher weight to those subjects whose propensity score is close to 0.5, and less weight to subjects whose propensity score is close to 0 or 1. Comparative analyses were conducted in the overlap weighted cohort, where the association of revascularization strategy and mortality was modelled using Cox proportional hazard regression and that with PACE was modelled using cause-specific hazard regression, with death as a competing risk.28 Estimates were pooled across the 20 imputed samples using Rubin’s Rules.23

As the proportional hazards assumption was violated for most outcomes, we reported both an average hazard ratio (HR) and instantaneous time-specific HRs. When the treatment effect varied over time, the average HR was used to summarize the overall association between revascularization strategy and outcome across the full follow-up period; it represents a time-averaged effect rather than the HR at any single time point. To characterize temporal patterns, we estimated time-specific HRs (with PCI as the reference) at 30 days, 90 days, and 180 days, and then annually up to 10 years. Time-specific HRs were estimated using restricted cubic splines.29 Time-specific HRs were prespecified and are presented to describe temporal patterns in treatment effects rather than as independent hypothesis tests; accordingly, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Additionally, the risk differences between the CABG and PCI groups for each outcome were determined at each point in time in the weighted samples. The standard errors for absolute risks and risk differences were estimated using 200 bootstraps within each of the 20 imputations. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we matched patients based on their propensity to receive PCI vs CABG within each analysis, defined by completeness of revascularization, revascularization modality (ie, drug-eluting [DES] or bare metal stent PCI vs on-pump or off-pump CABG), and insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDM) vs non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM). Second, as death and PACE are both important outcomes from the patient’s perspective, we repeated our analyses with all-cause mortality and PACE as a composite endpoint.

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplemental Table S1).

PACE

During the study period, a total of 12,081 patients (20.5%) developed PACE. This number includes 5507 patients (21.4%) who underwent CABG, and 6574 (19.9%) who underwent PCI (Fig. 1). In the weighted cohort, we observed a higher average rate of severe stroke in the CABG group (HR 1.25 [95% CI 1.08-1.45], P = 0.0033), which was driven by the elevated rate of stroke in the first year (30-day HR 2.33 [95% CI 1.38-3.95], 1-year HR 1.90 [95% CI 1.28-2.80]). After year 1, no difference occurred in the rate of stroke between groups (Supplemental Fig. S7 and Table 3).

After weighting, no statistically significant difference was present in the average rate of HF (HR 0.98 [95% CI 0.92-1.04]), nor in the time-specific HR at 30 days (HR 1.03 [95% CI 0.84-1.26]; Supplemental Fig. S8 and Table 3). However, CABG was associated with a lower rate of HF in years 1-6 (1-year HR 0.82 [95% CI 0.70-0.95]; 6-year HR 0.74 [95% CI 0.60-0.92]).

The average rates of LTC admission (HR 0.87 [95% CI 0.78-0.97], P = 0.0115) and ventilator dependence (HR 0.51 [95% CI 0.29-0.92], P = 0.0250) were lower after CABG, and no time-specific patterns of these outcomes were observed (Supplemental Figs. S9 and S10 and Table 3).

The average rate of new-onset dialysis (HR 1.03 [95% CI 0.88-1.19], P = 0.7484) was similar between groups. However, the rate of new-onset dialysis was higher in years 3-6 (3-year HR 2.18 [95% CI 1.08-4.40], 6-year HR 2.19 [95% CI 1.19-4.04]) in the CABG group (Supplemental Fig. S11 and Table 3).

All-cause death

A total of 12,408 patients (21.1%) died over the course of follow-up. This number included 5001 patients (19.4%) who underwent CABG, and 7407 patients (22.4%) who underwent PCI (Fig. 4). Sensitivity analyses

Complete vs incomplete revascularization

In the weighted cohort of patients who were completely revascularized (CABG, N = 20,335 [86.6%]; PCI, N = 15,729 [47.6%]), the average rate of PACE was significantly higher in those who underwent CABG vs PCI (HR 1.18 [95% CI 1.09-1.28], P < 0.0001; Supplemental Fig. S12). However, a U-shaped relationship was observed (Supplemental Fig. S13), such that CABG was associated with a higher rate of PACE at year 1, a lower rate of PACE in years 3-4, and again a higher rate of PACE vs PCI beyond year 7. The average rate of death was not significantly different between groups after weighting in those who were completely revascularized (HR 0.95 [95% CI 0.88-1.03]; Supplemental Fig. S14). Specifically, CABG was associated with a lower rate of death compared to PCI in years 1-4, and a higher mortality rate compared to PCI beyond year 8 (Supplemental Fig. S15).

Similar trends were observed among patients with incomplete revascularization (CABG, N = 3137 [13.4%]; PCI, N = 17,343 [52.4%]). After weighting, a trend was present toward a higher average rate of PACE in the CABG group (HR 1.09 [95% CI 1.00-1.19], P = 0.05; Supplemental Fig. S16). CABG was associated with a lower rate of PACE in years 2-4, after which no between-group difference was observed (Supplemental Fig. S17). No significant between-group difference occurred in the average rate of death in patients who were incompletely revascularized (HR 0.99 [95% CI 0.91-1.08]; Supplemental Fig. S18). Here, CABG carried a lower upfront rate of death compared to PCI in year 1, and a higher mortality rate beyond year 6 (Supplemental Fig. S19).

Revascularization by stent type and CABG pump technique

Over the course of the follow-up period, the cumulative incidence of PACE remained highest in patients who underwent PCI with a bare metal stent, followed by on-pump CABG, off-pump CABG, and PCI with a drug-eluting stent (Supplemental Fig. S20).

IDM vs NIDDM

In patients with IDM (CABG, N = 5496 [21.3%]; PCI, N = 6113 [18.5%]), the average rate of PACE was not significantly different between groups after weighting (HR 1.00 [95% CI 0.90-1.11]; Supplemental Fig. S21). However, CABG was associated with a lower rate of PACE between 2 and 5 years postprocedure (Supplemental Fig. S22). The average rate of death was lower in the CABG group compared to the PCI group after weighting (HR 0.75 [95% CI 0.68-0.83], P < 0.0001; Supplemental Fig. S23). This difference was driven by a lower rate of death in the CABG group in the first 6 years (Supplemental Fig. S24).

Similar trends were observed among patients with NIDDM (CABG, N = 20,255 [78.7%]; PCI, N = 26,962 [81.5%]), for whom the average rate of PACE after weighting also was not significantly different between groups (HR 0.97 [95% CI 0.92-1.03]; Supplemental Fig. S25). CABG was associated with a lower rate of PACE between 2-6 years (Supplemental Fig. S26). After weighting, the average rate of death was lower in the CABG group (HR 0.78; [95% CI 0.73-0.83], P < 0.0001; Supplemental Fig. S27). This difference is driven by a lower rate of death with CABG in the first 7 years postoperatively (Supplemental Fig. S28).

Composite outcome of all-cause death and PACE

In the weighted cohort, the average rate of the death-PACE composite was significantly lower in the CABG group, compared to the PCI group (HR 0.88 [95% CI 0.85-0.92], P < 0.0001). Notably, the rate of the death-PACE composite outcome in the CABG group was higher upfront (HR 1.10 [95% CI 1.02-1.19] at 30 days), lower during the first 7 years (HR 0.93 [95% CI 0.87-1.00]), and then became higher compared to PCI at 10 years (HR 1.18 [95% CI 1.03-1.35]; Supplemental Fig. S29).

Discussion

We reported the patient-oriented effectiveness of CABG vs PCI in a large, real-world cohort of patients with diabetes. We observed that PACE occurred at similar frequencies as death, and that the rate of PACE was lower after CABG compared to PCI in the first 5 years and the rate of all-cause death for the first 8 years. Additionally, we found that CABG was associated with a higher upfront rate of severe stroke in the first year and a lower rate of new-onset or worsening HF in the first 5 years. The novelty of our finding lies in it being the first to systematically describe the following: PACE as a patient-centred outcome codeveloped by clinicians and patients; its applicability in a real-world setting across a large and diverse population; its capability to provide robust long-term data without loss to follow-up; and our ability to provide data in a time-specific fashion rather than the traditional method of providing an average effect estimate over the entire follow-up period.

In addition, our time-specific analyses introduce the concept of time preference into therapeutic decision-making. These findings provide important insight that may help patients and providers weigh outcomes over time based on priorities they both value.

Patient-Centred Outcomes

We found that, compared to PCI, CABG was associated with a lower mid-term (1-5 years) rate of PACE. This is, to our knowledge, the first comparative study of a patient-defined outcome over a long follow-up period in diabetic patients who undergo revascularization. Our findings are supported by previous reports. In the FREEDOM trial, patients with multivessel CAD and diabetes who underwent CABG had significantly better health status and quality of life at 2 years, as defined by the Seattle Angina Questionnaire and the Rose Dyspnea Scale. This difference was attributed to the higher rates of complete revascularization after CABG in this patient group.30 A cost-effectiveness analysis of the FREEDOM trial suggested that, despite having higher upfront cost, CABG was overall more cost-effective than PCI, owing to substantial gains in quality-adjusted life expectancy.31 Moreover, when data from the FREEDOM trial were used to develop a personalized risk prediction tool for patients with diabetes, these authors found that PCI was never superior to CABG, and that CABG outperformed PCI for MACCE in more than half of all patients (54.5%) and in 100% of those who had a history of smoking.32

We observed changes in the direction of association between PACE and revascularization strategy over time, such that both strategies were associated with comparable early (< 1 year) and long-term (> 5 years) rates of PACE, whereas the mid-term rate of PACE was lower in the CABG group. These observations are in part driven by a higher early rate of severe stroke after CABG—primarily during the perioperative period and the first year—after which stroke rates between CABG and PCI converge, as well as the emergence of non-procedure-related determinants of long-term risk in this complex patient population beyond 5 years. Our findings corroborate those in the literature. In the FREEDOM Trial,4 the rate of stroke was higher in the CABG than in the PCI group at 5 years (5.2% vs 2.4%; P = 0.03). In the centres that reported disaggregated MACE data for the follow-on study (N = 415),5 at a median follow-up of 7.5 years, the rate of stroke in the CABG group was lower than that in the PCI group (1.5% vs 2.3%). By comparison, an individual patient-data meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing CABG and PCI, which included the FREEDOM Trial, found that 5-year stroke rates were significantly higher after CABG compared to PCI, driven largely by higher rates in the 30 days after revascularization.33 Despite the early stroke risk, CABG was associated with lower rates of LTC admission during the first 5 years. This reduction likely reflects patient selection for CABG of those with better baseline functional status, fewer frailty markers, and stronger social support, as shown in large registry analyses and propensity-matched studies.34 In addition, CABG offers a higher likelihood of achieving complete and durable revascularization, leading to lower rates of repeat revascularization, myocardial infarction, and hospital readmission over long-term follow-up compared with PCI.20,34,35 These advantages may reduce the incidence of late cardiac events that contribute to functional decline and the need for institutional care. Consistent with this advantage, the lower long-term incidence of ventilator dependence observed after CABG compared with PCI may be multifactorial. Improved preservation of myocardial function after CABG may result in fewer episodes of progressive heart failure or cardiogenic decompensation that precipitate respiratory failure requiring prolonged ventilatory support. Ventilator dependence is influenced by both cardiac and noncardiac factors, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

In most subgroups, temporal risk patterns for PACE were broadly concordant with the main analysis, supporting the robustness of our findings. For example, in patients with iIDM or NIDDM, CABG was associated with a lower PACE rate during the first 5 years, after which the advantage was diminished or reversed. In contrast, the complete revascularization subgroup, an outcome more often achieved with CABG, showed a significantly higher upfront and late-onset PACE risk with CABG compared with PCI, patterns not observed in the main analysis or in the incomplete revascularization subgroup. The elevated early risk may reflect the procedural complexity, longer operative times, and prolonged recovery associated with CABG.1 The late divergence in risk could be explained by the long-term complications of surgical revascularization, including conduit degeneration, graft attrition, and progression of atherosclerosis in native vessels, as well as the emergence of non-procedure-related determinants of long-term risk,1,36 such as competing comorbidities and frailty, that become prominent drivers of adverse outcomes beyond 5 years. These findings suggest that complete revascularization may carry greater short-term postoperative burden without ensuring a sustained reduction in long-term PACE events, making the decision highly dependent on individual priorities, such as early risk avoidance, symptom relief, and long-term durability. Subgroup-specific differences, combined with patient goals and values, remain critical for tailoring revascularization strategies and long-term care to optimize outcomes after either CABG or PCI.

Beyond clinical outcomes, PACE events are associated with substantial direct medical costs, lost productivity, and increased healthcare expenses from repeat hospitalizations and long-term care needs.16,37 The American Heart Association (AHA) highlights that patient-centred care should integrate both clinical and economic outcomes, including resource use and cost-effectiveness analyses, to inform policy and funding decisions.38,39 Incorporating measures such as health-related quality of life, recurrent rehospitalizations, cognitive decline, and functional impairment provides a more comprehensive assessment of the long-term impact of revascularization interventions.38,39 Our findings also support the use of structured shared decision-making models that present individualized risks for both traditional and patient-centred outcomes such as PACE. These models improve patient empowerment, reduce decisional conflict, and align treatment choices with patient goals.40,41 Both the AHA and the US Preventive Services Task Force endorse such tools to advance patient-centred care in high-risk cardiovascular populations.39,40 This approach promotes truly patient-centred care for this vulnerable population.

Death and major adverse cardiovascular events

The higher rates of long-term survival after CABG found in our study are consistent with existing literature. The individual patient data meta-analysis by Head et al.10 pooled 11 RCTs (N = 11,518) and found higher rates of 5-year mortality in PCI vs CABG in patients with multivessel disease (11.5% vs 8.9%; HR 1.28 [95% CI 1.09-1.49]; P = 0.0019) and diabetes (15.5% vs 10.0%; HR 1.48 [95% CI 1.19-1.84]; P = 0.0004). Liang et al.42 pooled 6 RCTs (N = 4028) and found lower all-cause mortality (relative risk 0.74 [95% CI 0.56-0.98]) after CABG compared to PCI in patients with severe CAD and type 2 diabetes. Our findings are also consistent with previous observational studies in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. In Sweden, patients with type 1 diabetes (N = 683 CABG, N = 1863 PCI) had comparable rates of all-cause mortality, stroke, and HF at a mean follow-up of 10.6 years. However, they had higher CAD mortality, myocardial infarction, and repeat revascularization after PCI, compared to those with CABG.43 In Ontario, Tam et al. reported lower rates of mortality and MACCE in patients with severe CAD and diabetes after CABG as compared to PCI.9

The sensitivity analyses for mortality generally supported the primary findings. Although higher rates of survival with CABG compared to PCI were consistently seen among patients with IDM and NIDDM, outcomes stratified by completeness of revascularization were more nuanced. In both completely and incompletely revascularized groups, CABG conferred an early survival benefit, but this benefit was not sustained and was offset by higher mortality beyond 6-8 years, resulting in an overall nonsignificant difference in average rate of death within both groups. Together, these findings support the main analysis while emphasizing the need for individualized treatment decisions.

Our findings are also consistent with those of contemporary trials. The BARI-2D trial6 found that CABG, but not PCI, reduced major adverse cardiovascular events compared to medical treatment. Prespecified diabetes subgroups in the SYNTAX7 and BEST8 trials found similar benefits for CABG compared to PCI in patients with diabetes and CAD. The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs—Coronary Artery Revascularization in Diabetes (VA-CARDS) trial44 directly compared CABG and PCI in patients with diabetes and severe CAD; however, the trial was not sufficiently powered and stopped at 25% enrollment due to slow recruitment. Similarly, although the Coronary Artery Revascularization in Diabetes (CARDia) trial45 did not find a benefit of CABG over PCI in this patient population at 1 year, the study was not powered to find a difference between revascularization strategies.

Our findings further support professional society guidelines through a patient-centred lens. The recent 2021 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA)/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) guidelines1 recommend that CABG with left internal mammary artery graft to the LAD is the preferred treatment modality in patients with diabetes and multivessel CAD involving the LAD, to reduce mortality and repeat revascularization (class I, level of evidence A). Conversely, PCI is only indicated in diabetic patients with multivessel CAD, who are poor operative candidates (class IIa, level of evidence B). Future guideline revisions ought to incorporate patient-centred outcomes, such as PACE and its subcomponents, in a collaborative heart team–based effort to further optimize patient care.

The concept of time preference

People tend to assign greater value to events that happen in the short-term over those later in time from the present is a good alternative.46 Through that lens, patients may reflexively choose PCI over CABG when presented with higher upfront and overall severe stroke risk, despite high-quality data demonstrating lower rates of mortality and PACE over the long run. This tendency may explain the persistent high rates of PCI in this patient population around the world. Our findings introduce new information, on the varying incidence and comparative risks of patient-centred outcomes over time, that could be used to empower patients in the decision-making process. They also provide an impetus for research in partnership with patients to further explore the psychology of time preference, and drive clinical decisions with evidence-based expectations of early and long-term quality of life.

Limitations

Our study has several important limitations. First, as an observational study using administrative and registry-based data, it is subject to treatment allocation bias and potential misclassification despite rigorous risk adjustment and data linkage, and objective outcome adjudication was not feasible. Second, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons, which may increase the risk of type I error. Third, the administrative dataset lacks physical measures of frailty and disability—such as grip strength, nutritional status, activities of daily living, or the 5-minute walk test—as well as key anatomic and procedural details, including the SYNTAX score and surgical approach. In addition, information on multidisciplinary heart team discussions and formal surgical candidacy assessments was unavailable; therefore, unmeasured treatment-selection factors may have contributed to residual confounding. Fourth, medical therapy at discharge and during follow-up, which is known to influence long-term outcomes, was not captured longitudinally. These factors may account partly for the observed between-group differences. Fifth, the use of overlap weights, although they induce excellent balance between treatment groups, places the emphasis on those subjects whose propensity score is close to 0.5. Characterizing who these patients are is difficult, and therefore so is identifying the population to which the estimated effect applies. Finally, cause-of-death data, which typically lag behind binary mortality status and require substantial manual curation, were unavailable for this analysis.

Conclusion

In patients with severe CAD and diabetes, CABG is associated with lower rates of both PACE at the mid-term and mortality over the long-term, at the cost of a higher upfront rate of stroke. Shared decision-making with patients and caregivers through a heart team approach is essential to ensuring treatment is personally tailored to reflect outcomes most desired by the individual patient.
