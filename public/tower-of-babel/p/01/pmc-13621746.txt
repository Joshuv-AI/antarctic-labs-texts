Association between salivary microbiota-related amino acid metabolic dysregulation and tacrolimus-induced gingival overgrowth following kidney transplantation
Authors: Xuyu Xiang, Yi Zhu, Tianyin Wang, Ke Cheng, Yingzi Ming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKidney transplant (KT) recipients require lifelong immunosuppressive therapy to prevent allograft rejection. Drug-induced gingival overgrowth (DIGO) is a notable adverse effect of tacrolimus, for which effective preventive or therapeutic strategies are lacking. Dysbiosis of the oral microbiota has been implicated as a major risk factor for DIGO. However, its mechanistic role remains poorly understood.ResultsTwenty KT recipients with newly diagnosed DIGO while receiving tacrolimus were enrolled, along with 20 matched controls with stable graft function. Salivary samples were collected and subjected to metagenomic and untargeted metabolomic profiling. Taxonomic analysis revealed greater microbial heterogeneity in DIGO patients compared to more interconnected communities observed in controls. Periodontitis-associated taxon, including Porphyromonas gingivalis, were enriched in the DIGO group. Multiple differentially expressed microbial genes and metabolites were identified, predominantly enriched in disordered amino acid metabolic pathways. Key metabolites—such as L-proline, carnosine, choline, 5-aminolevulinic acid, and spermidine—showed strong associations with DIGO-related taxon.ConclusionA strong association was observed between salivary microbial composition, metabolic profiles, and DIGO. The identified microbiota and metabolite alterations suggest a potential link between amino acid metabolic dysregulation and gingival fibroblast-related pathways in DIGO. These findings provide new insights into the biological features of DIGO and offer a foundation for future mechanistic and therapeutic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09004-z.

Background

Kidney transplantation (KT) is an effective treatments for end-stage renal disease. KT recipients require lifelong immunosuppressive therapy to prevent rejection. Currently, most immunosuppressive regimens for KT recipients are based on calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), with over 90% of patients taking tacrolimus [1].

Drug-induced gingival overgrowth (DIGO) is a frequently overlooked yet significant side effect of tacrolimus in KT recipients [2], with an incidence ranging from 10–30% [3]. Signs and symptoms associated with gingival overgrowth typically manifest within 24 months of starting the medication. Initially, the interdental papillae enlarge into bead-like, mulberry-shaped, firm, pink, resilient lobules, often without periodontal inflammation and bleeding on probing. Over time, the overgrowth can extend to the marginal gingiva, potentially covering the entire tooth crown [4]. As the condition progresses, the enlarged gingiva can interfere with chewing, speech, and aesthetics problems, leading to significant psychological issues [5]. In summary, DIGO can profoundly impact the quality of life for KT recipients, and a deeper understanding of disease progression and corresponding treatments is crucial for patient care.

Histologically, DIGO is described as a connective tissue disorder characterized by metabolic changes in gingival fibroblasts, increased inflammatory response, and vascular alterations, leading to excessive stromal collagen deposition [6]. Diverse responses of gingival connective tissue to CNI stimulation are observed among patients, significantly influenced by the oral microbiota [7, 8]. Poor oral hygiene and plaque exposure are amplifying risk factors for the onset and severity of DIGO [9, 10]. Treatment with metronidazole and azithromycin can significantly improve GO [11, 12]. The importance of oral microbiota as a contributing factor in the etiology of DIGO was recognized in the periodontal disease classification system [13]. However, the specific pathogens that alter gingival susceptibility to CNIs, and the pathways through which these pathogens affect DIGO onset, remain unknown. Moreover, research on the relationship between tacrolimus and DIGO is scarce.

Saliva, as the fluid environment constantly bathing various oral anatomical structures, interacts with microbial communities at different sites, providing a comprehensive reflection of oral microbiota distribution [14]. Therefore, we performed metagenomic sequencing and untargeted metabolomic profiling on the saliva of KT patients with tacrolimus-induced gingival overgrowth. Pathogens and metabolic alterations associated with DIGO are thought to be detectable in saliva.

Methods

Study design

This was a case–control study to evaluate the impact of salivary microbiota and metabolism on DIGO. The patient recruitment was conducted in two phases in our outpatient clinic. Firstly, from October 2022 to May 2023, DIGO patients were recruited based on diagnostic criteria consistent with previous studies [10]: vertical gingival overgrowth, or horizontal gingival enlargement exceeding 1 mm was observed. The inclusion criteria for KT patients with DIGO were: 1) newly developed DIGO diagnosed by both transplant and dental specialists; 2) receiving an immunosuppressive regimen primarily consisting of tacrolimus, mycophenolic acid and steroids, and not receiving calcium channel blockers after KT; 3) with no history of periodontitis or related symptoms. Exclusion criteria included: 1) less than six months post-transplant; 2) multiple or multi-organ transplant patients; 3) patients with unstable renal function (serum creatine ≤ 176 µmol/L), clinical manifestations of rejection or infection [15]; 4) oral antibiotic use within the past month; 5) age less than 18 years old or more than 65 years old.

The second phase was conducted from November 2022 to June 2023, involved enrolling post-KT individuals without DIGO for control, who matched 1:1 to DIGO patients by age, gender, and post-transplant time. Exclusion criteria included: 1) receiving an immunosuppressive regimen does not consist of tacrolimus, mycophenolic acid and steroids, or receiving calcium channel blockers after KT; 2) with history of periodontitis or related symptoms; 3) multiple or multi-organ transplant patients; 4) patients with unstable renal function (serum creatine ≤ 176 µmol/L), clinical manifestations of rejection or infection; 5) oral antibiotic use within the past month. Individuals who were eventually included were considered stable patients.

Sample collection

Before collection, patients fasted for half an hour and rinsed their mouths. Patients spit the saliva into a sterile tube until it reaches 2 ml. Samples were placed on ice and immediately transported to the laboratory. They were centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant and pellet were separately collected and stored at −80 °C.

Extraction of DNA, library preparation and metagenomic analysis of saliva samples

Prepared 96-well deep plates by adding 600 μL Buffer with magnetic beads, 20 μL Proteinase K, 5 μL RNase A, 700 μL Wash 1, 700 μL Wash 2, 700 μL Wash 3, and 100 μL Elution Buffer. Transfered 100–200 mg of each pellet of saliva to a centrifuge tube with grinding beads, add 1 mL Buffer ATL/PVP-10, grind the sample, and incubate at 65 °C for 20 min. Centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min, transfered the supernatant to a new tube, and added 0.6 mL Buffer PCI. Mixed thoroughly by vortexing for 15 s. Centrifuged at 18,213 × g for 10 min and transfered the supernatant to a deep well plate with magnetic beads binding solution. Placed the deep well plate in the machine and started the corresponding program in Kingfisher. Finally, transfered the DNA to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube.

Samples underwent quality control (QC) based on type and product requirements. Genomic DNA was randomly fragmented and selected to a specific average size. The fragments were end-repaired, 3’ adenylated, and adaptors were ligated. PCR amplification was configured and performed. Library quality control followed product-specific protocols. Single-stranded PCR products were denatured and circularized, while uncyclized linear DNA was digested. These single-stranded circles underwent rolling cycle amplification to generate DNA nanoballs. High-quality DNBs were then loaded into patterned nanoarrays and sequenced using combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis.

All raw data were trimmed using SOAPnuke v.2.2.1. The trimmed reads were mapped to the host genome with SOAP2 to identify and remove host-origin reads. High-quality reads were de novo assembled with MEGAHIT, discarding contigs shorter than 300 bp. Gene prediction was performed on contigs using MetaGeneMark, and redundant genes were removed with CD-HIT at 95% identity and 90% coverage cutoffs. Gene abundance was quantified using Salmon. Protein sequences were aligned to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional databases via DIAMOND, with an E value cutoff of 1e − 5 for annotation. Taxonomic annotation was assigned using Kraken's LCA algorithm, and the Nt database was used. Taxonomic and functional abundance profiles were generated with Bracken. Wilcoxon’s rank sum test and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis identified features with significantly different abundances, and differentially enriched KEGG pathways were determined using reporter scores, with a significance threshold of 1.65. Alpha diversity was measured using the Shannon, Chao1, and Simpson indices. Principal component analysis (PCA) reduced the dimensionality of multivariate data, and analysis of similarities (ADONIS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was performed.

Extraction, preparation and untargeted metabolomics analysis of salivary metabolites

Added 100 µL of each supernatant of saliva into the corresponding centrifuge tube. Add 700µL of extractant containing internal standard 1 (methanol: acetonitrile: water = 4:2:1), shaked for 10 min, and placed it in a refrigerator at −20 °C for 2 h. Centrifuged at 25,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min. Removed the sample from the centrifuge and transfered 600µL of supernatant to a new centrifuge tube and drain with a drainer. Added 180µL methanol: pure water (1:1 v/v) and swirled for 10 min until completely dissolved in the complex solution. Centrifuged at 25,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min again. Take 50µL of the remaining samples into three upper plates for the detection of positive and negative ions, used the other plate as the spare plate, and take 20µL of the remaining samples mixed as QC.

Untargeted metabolomics on saliva was conducted using LC–MS/MS with a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in both positive and negative ion modes to enhance metabolite coverage. Data processing, including peak picking, alignment, and compound identification, was carried out with Compound Discoverer 3.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were analyzed using a Waters 2D UPLC (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer featuring a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source, managed by Xcalibur 2.3 software. Separation occurred on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) at 45 °C. The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B) for positive mode, and 10 mM ammonium formate (A) and acetonitrile (B) for negative mode. The gradient: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–9 min, 2%−98% B; 9–12 min, 98% B; 12–12.1 min, 98% B to 2% B; 12.1–15 min, 2% B. Flow rate: 0.35 mL/min; injection volume: 5 μL. Positive/negative ionization settings: spray voltage 3.8/− 3.2 kV; sheath gas flow rate 40 arb; aux gas flow rate 10 arb; aux gas heater at 350 °C; capillary temperature 320 °C. Full scan range: 70–1050 m/z, resolution 70,000, AGC target 3e6, max ion injection time 100 ms. Top 3 precursors selected for MS/MS fragmentation with max ion injection time 50 ms, resolution 17,500, AGC 1e5. Stepped normalized collision energy: 20, 40, 60 eV. Samples were randomized to reduce system errors, with QC samples inserted every 10 samples.

Data preprocessing, statistical analysis, classification, and functional annotation of metabolites were conducted using the R package metaX. PCA reduced to identify groups, trends, and outliers, and ADONIS based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was performed. Differential metabolites were identified using variable influence in projection (VIP) scores from the first two components of the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model, and Wilcoxon’s rank sum test.

Statistical analysis

All other statistical analyses were performed using R (4.2.1, Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). We utilized various statistical tests including the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, unpaired t-test, chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test. Spearman’s rank correlation test were employed to analyze the correlation. The co-occurrence network was visualized with R package igraph. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

According to the KEGG database, a total of 1645 metabolites were identified (Fig. 3A), and 2669 compounds could not be annotated. Principal co-ordinates analysis based on Bray–Curtis distance showed no significant differences in overall metabolite distribution between the two groups, as determined by ADONIS analysis (Fig. 3B). The Q2 value from permutation tests was lower than the Q2 of the OPLS-DA model and had an intercept below zero on the vertical axis, indicating the model's validity (Fig. 3C). Enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites revealed 18 significant metabolic pathways, with the arginine biosynthesis pathway showing notable differences between the two groups (Fig. 3E).

Associations of DIGO-associated gene with salivary metabolites

To further identify key taxon and metabolites involved in the pathogenesis of DIGO, we conducted an integrative analysis of metagenomic sequencing and untargeted metabolomics. Among the 20 pathways annotated in both analyses (Supplemental Fig. 4A), only pathways ABC transporters, arginine and proline metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and histidine metabolism showed significant differences in both functional genes and metabolite enrichment analysis (Fig. 4A). These pathways are all related to amino acid metabolism. Consequently, we performed a correlation analysis between these related amino acid and DIGO-associated taxon, identifying metabolites L-proline, carnosine, choline, 5-aminolevulinic acid and spermidine as closely related to various microbial taxon (Fig. 4B). Literature review indicated a strong link between these metabolites and fibrosis. Discussion

To further investigate salivary microbiota and metabolic alterations associated with DIGO in KT recipients, we enrolled 20 patients with newly developed tacrolimus-induced gingival overgrowth and 20 matched stable recipients. Saliva samples were collected and subjected to metagenomic and untargeted metabolomic sequencing. Taxonomic analysis revealed high heterogeneity in the overall microbiota distribution in DIGO patients, with an enrichment of periodontitis pathogens, such as P. gingivalis. In contrast, stable recipients exhibited strong interconnections among dominant bacterial taxon. Several functional genes and metabolites showed significant differences between the groups, converging on altered amino acid metabolic pathways, including arginine and proline metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and histidine metabolism. Among these pathways, metabolites such as L-proline, carnosine, choline, 5-aminolevulinic acid and spermidine were closely associated with DIGO-related taxon.

Accumulating evidence suggests that oral microbiota may contribute to the clinical severity of DIGO. Poor oral hygiene has been found to promote the onset of DIGO [9]. D.W. Thomas et al. discovered a strong association between gingival overgrowth, and dental plaque and periodontal inflammation through a multivariate linear regression model [16]. Patients with pre-existing periodontal inflammation before CNI therapy are likely to experience more severe gingival enlargement [17]. Antibiotics like metronidazole and azithromycin can alleviate gingival swelling [11, 12]. In animal models, plaque accumulation has also been closely linked to CNIs-induced gingival overgrowth [18]. Accumulating evidence suggests that pathogenic oral microorganisms and periodontal inflammation may contribute to DIGO development. In support of previous conclusions, our findings also showed the enrichment of periodontitis-related P. gingivalis in the saliva of DIGO patients. P. gingivalis is a Gram-negative, obligate anaerobic bacterium implicated in oral and systemic diseases, such as periodontitis, cardiovascular diseases, Alzheimer's disease, and rheumatoid arthritis [19]. It employs multiple strategies to disrupt the structural and functional integrity of gingival epithelial cells, allowing adhesion, invasion, and rapid internalization into human epithelial cells. It evades immune system attacks by inactivating immune cells and molecules, and leads to periodontitis [20]. Proteases, including P. gingivalis gingipains and peptidylarginine deiminase which have been identified [21], are critical virulence factors of P. gingivalis, providing energy to the pathogen and causing host damage. In animal experiments, oral administration of P. gingivalis to C57BL/6 mice resulted in dysregulation of several serum amino acids, suggesting that P. gingivalis may contain additional undiscovered active molecules that significantly impact various amino acid metabolism pathways [22]. Together with our findings, these observations raise the possibility that P. gingivalis-associated metabolic alterations may be linked to DIGO development.

The ultrastructure of diseased gingival tissue shows that the volume increase is primarily due to the connective tissue response rather than involvement of the epithelial cell layer [23]. All types of DIGO display similar pathological features, characterized by varying degrees of inflammatory infiltration [24], and secretion of various extracellular matrix proteins, particularly collagen [25]. The hyperkeratotic epithelium covers and extends into the connective tissue, resulting in irregularly arranged collagen fibers [26]. Abnormal activation of gingival fibroblasts is crucial to gingival overgrowth. Polarized epithelial cells undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition, acquiring a fibroblast phenotype [6]. During this process, fibroblasts exhibit various biochemical changes, including increased migratory capacity [26], enhanced anti-apoptotic abilities [27], vigorous proliferation [28], and significantly elevated synthesis of extracellular matrix components [26].

In our study, differential functional genes and metabolites were significantly enriched in amino acid metabolism pathways. These disrupted pathways included several metabolites, among which L-proline, carnosine, choline, 5-aminolevulinic acid and spermidine were notably associated with DIGO-related microbiota. Although direct evidence linking these metabolites to DIGO pathogenesis is currently lacking, several identified metabolites have previously been implicated in fibroblast biology, extracellular matrix regulation, oxidative stress responses, and fibrotic processes in other biological contexts.

L-proline is a primary precursor for collagen synthesis [29], a key component of the extracellular matrix in DIGO. Extracellular L-proline drastically increased collagen biosynthesis in fibroblasts [30]. L-proline protects fibroblasts from oxidative damage and influences their metabolic activity [31, 32]. Additionally, L-proline induces leftright determination factors and phosphorylation of small mother against decapentaplegic, impacting the TGF-β signaling pathway [33, 34], which is crucial for the activation of fibroblasts [35].

Carnosine has demonstrated protective effects against fibrosis in various animal models. Continuous supplementation of carnosine significantly reduced liver fibrosis and associated liver damage in bile duct-ligated rats [36]. Carnosine protects renal tubular function, and decreases renal inflammation and fibrosis in diabetic nephropathy animal models [37]. In the bleomycin-induced idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis mouse model, carnosine reduces reactive oxygen species-induced pulmonary fibrosis via the NF-κB pathway [38].

Similarly, spermidine is a widely validated metabolite known to reduce fibrosis. Arginase 1 knockout mice with unilateral ureteral obstruction exhibited exacerbated renal fibrosis along with decreased spermidine levels, which were counteracted by exogenous spermidine treatment [39]. Intraperitoneal injection of spermidine in mice attenuated bleomycin-induced apoptosis and endoplasmic reticulum stress-related pathways, thereby reducing associated pulmonary fibrosis [40]. Cells treated with spermidine showed increased microtubule associated protein 1 s stability and autophagic signaling through cytoplasmic histone deacetylase 4 depletion, reducing chemically induced liver fibrosis and hepatocarcinogenesis, and extending lifespan [41].

Choline can counteract cardiac fibrosis, and this protective effect is antagonized by regulators of G protein signaling 4 [42]. Furthermore, fibroblast activity is associated with choline metabolism [43]. 5-aminolevulinic acid is a fibrosis-protective agent found to reduce fibrosis in the skin [44], oral cavity [45], and vocal cords [46] in animal models when used as a photodynamic therapy mediator. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that microbiota-associated metabolic alterations may influence gingival fibroblast behavior through multiple pathways. However, given the observational and cross-sectional nature of this study, the identified microbiota-metabolite associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Second, as a single-center study, the findings may be subject to selection bias. Third, due to limited dental expertise and infrastructure, periodontal status was not formally assessed and may represent a key confounding factor. Future studies incorporating comprehensive periodontal examination and standardized oral hygiene assessment will be necessary to validate the specificity of the identified microbiota-metabolite signatures for DIGO. Most importantly, although plausible hypotheses regarding the pathogenesis were proposed, they were not validated through in vivo or in vitro experiments. Future in vitro and in vivo studies will be required to determine whether the identified microbiota and metabolite alterations directly contribute to DIGO pathogenesis or represent secondary disease-associated changes.

Conclusions

Our study reaffirmed the close association between periodontitis-related pathogens and DIGO. Functional gene and metabolite analyses consistently indicated altered amino acid metabolism associated with differential microbial communities. Together with previous evidence, these findings raise the possibility that microbiota-associated metabolic alterations may influence gingival fibroblast responses in DIGO. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further experimental validation.
