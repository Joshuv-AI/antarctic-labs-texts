First mitochondrial genomes in the Effusa clade (Gastropoda, Ampullariidae): unveiling new insights from Neotropical endemic Pomacea spp.
Authors: Alejandro Mendivil, Rina Ramírez, Jaime Morin, Jorge L. Ramirez, Betty Shiga, Nilda Oliveros, Carlos Congrains, Michael D. Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPomacea is the most diverse genus of the freshwater snail family Ampullariidae, which have been proposed as model organisms in biogeography, speciation and invasive biology. While substantial effort has been put into the mitochondrial genomes of Pomacea clades associated with invasive species (P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii groups), comparatively little is known about the mitogenomic organization and structure within the Effusa clade.ResultsThis study provides the first complete and annotated mitochondrial genomes of the Effusa clade, based on Pomacea nobilis (Reeve, 1856) and a putative undescribed Pomacea lineage (hereafter Pomacea sp.). The mitochondrial genome of P. nobilis (16,279 − 16,471 bp) and Pomacea sp. (16,355 bp) are among the largest recorded for Pomacea and contain the typical 37 animal genes. A comparative analysis with other Ampullariidae revealed that the Effusa clade differs from both P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii clades mainly by the higher proportion of intergenic regions, the size of many genes (ATP8, ATP6, NAD6, COIII and NAD2), the largest ribosomal RNAs among Pomacea and a putative large repetitive unit in the control region.ConclusionThese findings highlight the distinctiveness of Effusa clade mitogenomes in comparison with the P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii groups. Further mitogenomic studies on underexplored Pomacea lineages will be important to broaden our understanding of mitochondrial genome diversity among apple snails.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13191-y.

Background

Ampullariidae (Mollusca, Gastropoda, Caenogastropoda) is a primarily circumtropical family of freshwater snails that reach their highest diversity in the New World, with Pomacea Perry, 1810 as the most diverse genus [1, 2]. Due to their Gondwanan origin, large number of species and wide range of ecological and physiological adaptations, apple snails have been proposed as molluscan models for understanding biogeography, speciation and evolutionary adaptations [1]. Additionally, some Pomacea species have been introduced and established outside their native range [2–3], and recent population genetic studies have examined their genetic diversity and structure in Southeast Asia and China [4, 5], where invasive apple snails are significant agricultural pests. While the taxonomic identification of Pomacea species has traditionally relied on shell characters [6], molecular studies, particularly those based on mitochondrial data, have proven useful to clarify the systematics of very similar species [7, 8]. For example, the mitochondrial gene COI was instrumental in distinguishing Pomacea maculata Perry, 1810 from Pomacea canaliculata (Lamarck, 1822), facilitating the identification of species-diagnostic morphological characters [8].

The mitochondrial genome has been widely used as a genetic marker for phylogenetic and evolutionary studies [9–12] due to its relatively small size compared to the nuclear genome, conserved genetic content and high number of copies per cell [13]. Although the typical animal mitogenome size ranges from 16 to 20 kbp and contains 37 genes organized in 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 2 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs) [14], substantial variation in mitochondrial genome size has been observed among Mollusca [15], including some of the largest mitogenomes recorded in Metazoa (Anadara kagoshimensis, 56170 bp [16]). Additionally, gene order rearrangements have been noted in many groups [17, 18], showing potential for elucidating the evolutionary history of freshwater mussels, particularly in Unionoidea [19].

Based on molecular data, Pomacea has been classified into four major clades: the P. canaliculata, P. bridgesii, Effusa and Flagellata groups [20]. Currently, complete mitogenomes are available on GenBank only for the P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii groups (search conducted on December 12, 2025). These efforts mainly reflect the relevance of Pomacea canaliculata (Lamarck, 1822) and Pomacea maculata Perry, 1810 as invasive species, and Pomacea diffusa Blume, 1957 as aquatic pet [3], although initiatives focusing on non-introduced species of Pomacea have recently increased [21]. In contrast, the Effusa and Flagellata clades remain underrepresented in mitochondrial data. Hayes et al. [20]. included only two species within the Effusa group, Pomacea glauca (Linnaeus, 1758) and Pomacea guyanensis (Lamarck, 1822), but more recently Pomacea nobilis (Reeve, 1856) has also been confirmed as part of this clade [22]. This species is native to South America, and its meat is highly valued and widely consumed in the Peruvian Amazon [23]. The objective of this research was to sequence, assemble, and annotate the mitochondrial genomes of P. nobilis and a putative undescribed Pomacea lineage from the Aguaytía River (hereafter Pomacea sp.), representing the first mitogenomes within the Effusa clade. We then incorporated these new data into the Pomacea dataset to compare the structure and organization of apple snail mitogenomes (including P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii clades). This study provides valuable insights into Ampullariidae systematics and Pomacea mitochondrial genome evolution.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection and DNA extraction

Live specimens of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. were collected in the Peruvian Amazon (Table 1) and maintained in tap water, with lettuce provided ad libitum, in the Molecular Systematics and Phylogeography Laboratory at Universidad Nacional Mayor de San Marcos. Specimens were relaxed with ethanol, euthanized through thermal shock (4 °C) and immediately preserved in 96% ethanol at room temperature. Genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissue using the E.Z.N.A. Mollusc DNA Kit (OMEGA Bio-tek) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated genomic DNA was quantified with the Nanodrop Lite Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). We obtained COI sequences from three P. nobilis (GenBank acc. num. MN325113, PX672272, PX672273) and two Pomacea sp. specimens (GenBank acc. num. PX672274 and PX672275), following the protocols of Ramírez et al. [22, 23]. These sequences were compared against the NCBI Core nucleotide database (core_nt) using BLASTn [24] with default parameters to assess their taxonomic affinities and exclude possible contamination or misidentifications. To avoid redundancy, only the highest-identity BLASTn hit for each Pomacea species was included in Additional file 1. All five specimens were processed for whole genome sequencing and deposited as vouchers in the Museo de Historia Natural of the Universidad Nacional Mayor de San Marcos, Lima, Peru (Table 1).

Genome sequencing and assembly

Genomic DNA of samples of P. nobilis (specimen vouchers: AE174PAH and Z58Mul) and Pomacea sp. (specimen vouchers: X60AGU and X63AGU) were sheared to reach a mean fragment length of 500 bp using a Covaris M220 Focused-ultrasonicator. The resulting fragmented DNA was used to prepare Illumina libraries using the blunt-end single-tube (BEST) protocol for double-stranded DNA [25], incorporating uniquely barcoded P5/P7 adapters during the indexing PCR. Indexing PCR was carried out in 100 µl reactions composed by 10 µl library template, 0.25 mM each dNTP, 0.25 µM each index primer (dual-barcode), 1 µl Herculase II Fusion DNA polymerase, 20 µl 5X Herculase II Reaction Buffer, and 63 µl molecular biology-grade water, under the following conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, 11 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 40 s and a final extension of 72 °C for 5 min. Indexed libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) and diluted in 33 µl EB buffer (Qiagen). The molarity and fragment distribution of each library was measured using HS D1000 ScreenTape assay on a 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent). Equimolar pools were sent to Novogene Europe for whole genome sequencing (paired-end, 150 bp format) on the Illumina NovaSeq X platform. For the remaining specimen voucher of P. nobilis (AE173SHE), gDNA was sent directly to Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) for library preparation and whole genome sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The quality of the data was checked using FastQC 0.12.0 [26]. The number of raw read pairs per sample varied across individuals (Table 2). Raw data was trimmed and filtered using fastp v0.23.4 [27] by removing reads with > 40% low-quality bases (Phred-scaled base quality < Q15) and trimming polyG tails. The mitochondrial genome assembly was performed with GetOrganelle v1.7.7.1 [28] using the Pomacea canaliculata mitochondrial genome (GenBank acc. num. KU052865.1) as reference. We used Bandage v0.9.0 [29] to visualize the assembly graph. To evaluate the reliability of the mitogenome assemblies, filtered reads were mapped back to each assembled genome using BWA 0.7.17 [30]. Coverage depth across the mitochondrial genome was calculated using SAMtools 1.19.2 [31], and coverage profiles were visualized in Microsoft Excel. Tandem repeats were identified in the control region with Tandem repeats finder [32].

Mitochondrial genome annotation

Gene annotation of the mitochondrial genome was performed with MITOS2 v2.1.0 WebServer [33] using the invertebrate genetic code. Annotation and secondary structure of transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were verified using ARWEN 1.2 [34]. Protein-coding genes (PCGs) were manually checked by detecting open reading frames with the NCBI tool ORFfinder [35]. Protein-coding genes were translated using the invertebrate mitochondrial genetic code and inspected for open reading frames. Functional annotation of the ATP6 was verified using InterProScan 5.77–108.0 (InterPro release 108.0) [36], while transmembrane regions were predicted using DeepTMHMM 2.0 [37]. The mitogenome annotation was visualized with Proksee v1.0.0a6 [38]. The ribosomal RNA (rRNA) secondary structures were inferred using R2DT 1.4 [39] and manually optimized based on the reference model of 12S rRNA for Eukaryotes [40] and 16S rRNA for Mollusca [41]. The potential secondary structure of the control region was predicted using RNAfold WebServer [42]. All structures were manually edited using Inkscape 1.1.

Comparative analyses

We compared the mitogenomes of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. with those of nine other species of the Ampullariidae family (Table 3). We calculated base composition and compositional asymmetry for five datasets: whole mitochondrial genome, control region, PCGs, rRNAs, and tRNAs. Base composition was calculated with MEGA X [43]. Compositional asymmetry was calculated using the following formulae: AT-skew = (A − T)/(A + T) and GC-skew = (G − C)/(G + C) [44]. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) was calculated for each amino acid with MEGA X [43]. Nucleotide sequence similarity for each gene was calculated for all Pomacea mitogenomes using MEGA X [43].

Phylogenetic analyses

A total of 34 complete mitochondrial genomes (Table 3) were included in the phylogenetic analyses, with two species of the subclass Heterobranchia, Aplysia californica Cooper, 1863 and Siphonaria pectinata (Linnaeus, 1758) as outgroups. PhyloSuite v2 [45] was used to generate, trim, and concatenate the individual alignments. The nucleotide sequences of the 13 PCGs and 2 rRNAs were aligned individually using MAFFT v7.313 [46] and trimmed with Gblocks 0.91b under relaxed conditions [47] using the following parameters: minimum number of sequences for a conserved/flank position (18/18), maximum number of contiguous non-conserved positions (8), minimum block length (10), and allowed gap positions (with half). The best-fit model and partitioning scheme were selected using the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) with PartitionFinder2 v2.1.1 [48]. Maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) methods were used to reconstruct the phylogenetic trees on the CIPRES Science Gateway web server [49]. ML analysis was conducted in IQ-TREE 2.1.2 [50] with 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. BI analysis was performed in MrBayes 3.2 [51], with two independent runs of six Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains running for 5 million generations, sampling every 1000 generations, and discarding the first 1.25 million generations (25%) as burn-in. Parameter convergence was assessed in Tracer v1.7 [52], ensuring all effective sample sizes (ESS) were above 200. FigTree v1.4.4 [53] was used to visualize and edit the phylogenetic trees. The final tree was formatted in Inkscape 1.1.

Selection pressure analyses

The non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates of the 13 protein-coding genes were estimated using DnaSP v6 [54]. Evidence for episodic positive selection was tested with BUSTED [55] implemented in the Datamonkey Adaptive Evolution Server [56]. An initial screening was conducted using a concatenated dataset of the 13 PCGs across all available Ampullariidae mitogenomes to identify putative clade-specific signals of selection. Subsequently, gene-specific analyses were performed to pinpoint the contribution of individual genes, focusing on the clade that showed evidence of selection in the initial screening. For the remaining clades, only the genes identified in this step were further evaluated. Site-level selection was assessed with FEL [57], and changes in the intensity of selection were analyzed using RELAX [58].

Results

Mitogenome size and structure

The three mitochondrial genomes of P. nobilis ranged from 16,279 to 16,471 bp, whereas the two Pomacea sp. mitogenomes were identical in sequence and length (16355 bp) (Table 1). Each mitogenome contained the typical set of 37 genes (13 PCGs, 2 rRNAs, and 22 tRNAs) plus a control region (CR) (Fig. 1). Twenty-nine genes were encoded on the heavy strand (H), while eight tRNA genes were encoded on the light strand (L) (Fig. 1; Table 4). On average, the PCGs, rRNAs, tRNAs and CR comprised 68.99%, 14.35%, 9.08%, and 3.04% of the mitochondrial genome of P. nobilis, respectively. Very similar values were found in Pomacea sp. (PCGs = 69.19%, rRNAs = 14.37%, tRNAs = 9.10%, CR = 2.78%). The proportion of intergenic regions was very similar among the assembled mitogenomes, representing 4.80–4.86% (791 bp in average) of the mitogenome in P. nobilis and 4.57% (747 bp) in Pomacea sp. In contrast, the assembled control region was more variable, ranging from 375 to 567 bp among the three P. nobilis individuals, whereas both Pomacea sp. specimens exhibited a length of 455 bp.

Within Pomacea, intergenic regions represented an average of 2.55% of the mitogenome in the P. canaliculata clade (P. canaliculata: 2.43%; P. maculata: 2.44%; P. occulta: 2.64%; P. reevei: 2.69%) and 3.39% in the P. bridgesii clade (P. diffusa: 3.32%; P. aulanieri: 3.46%). When compared with the additional Ampullariidae included in our dataset, higher proportions of intergenic regions were observed in Marisa cornuarietis (5.38%), Pila globosa (6.81%), and Pila virens (7.06%). Aside from the control region, the largest intergenic region in both P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. (111 bp) was located between tRNA-Ala and tRNA-Arg (Table 4). Additional non-coding regions with comparable lengths in both lineages were found between COIII/tRNA-Lys (80–81 bp), tRNA-Asn/tRNA-Ile (74 bp), and tRNA-Gln/tRNA-Gly (62 bp). The two lineages also shared the same overlapping regions: ATP6/tRNA-Met (39 bp) and NAD4L/NAD4 (7 bp).

Mitochondrial base composition and protein-coding gene features

The overall base composition of the mitochondrial genome of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. is very similar and aligns with the base composition of other Ampullariidae mitogenomes, although P. nobilis displays the highest AT-skew (followed by Pomacea sp.) and the lowest GC-skew in the family (Table 5). On average, species of the Effusa clade exhibit slightly higher A + T content (69.27–69.64%) than those in the P. bridgesii clade (68.85–69.49%), but lower than species in the P. canaliculata clade (70.98–72.31%). The A + T content, AT-skew and GC-skew for the PCGs, rRNAs, tRNAs and CR of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. are consistent with values observed in other Ampullariidae (Table 6), although the percentage of A + T content varies according to the annotated feature in both lineages, being highest in the control region and lowest for the PCGs (CR> rRNAs> tRNAs> PCGs).

Among the 13 PCGs, NAD5 is the longest (1710 bp) and ATP8 the shortest (156 bp) in both lineages. Mitochondrial rRNAs, tRNAs, and control region features

The 12S rRNA (994–996 bp) and 16S rRNA (1356–1357 bp) of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. are the largest recorded for Pomacea, exceeding those observed in the species of the P. canaliculata clade (909–936 bp for 12S, 1331–1340 bp for 16S) and P. bridgesii clade (952–960 bp for 12S, 1346–1348 bp for 16S), although smaller than in Pila (1012–1017 bp for 12S, 1417–1423 bp for 16S). The alignment of all available complete 12S rRNA sequences of Pomacea resulted in a length of 1002 bp, with 615 conserved sites (61.38%). The predicted secondary structure comprises four domains (I–IV), with Domains III (71.58% of invariant sites) and IV (85.14% of invariant sites) being the most conserved, whereas some regions in the Domains I and II showed high variability, such as stems 8, 24 and 25, and loops 13, 31 and 42 (Fig. 3). Some differences were also observed between P. nobilis and Pomacea sp., especially in stems 8, 13 and 16 of Domain I and stem 23 of Domain II. The alignment of all available complete 16S rRNA sequences of Pomacea resulted in a length of 1385 bp, with 853 conserved sites (61.59%). The predicted secondary structure comprises six domains (I–VI), with Domains IV (80.28% of invariant sites) and V (73.44% of invariant sites) being the most conserved, whereas stem-loops D2 + D4, D13 + D14, D21 + D22, E3 + E4 and H3 show a larger proportion of variable sites, with small size differences in some loops, particularly in stem-loops D13 + D14 and H4 (Figs. 4 and 5).

Phylogenetic analysis and genetic distances

Maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) phylogenetic analyses produced the same topology (Fig. 9), although the BI showed higher support values (0.98–1.00) than the ML (48–100). Viviparoidea, Ampullarioidea, and Cyclophoroidea were each recovered as monophyletic superfamilies. Viviparoidea was recovered as the sister lineage to Sorbeoconcha, although support for this relationship was inconsistent between analyses (BI: 0.98, ML: 48), indicating uncertainty in deeper caenogastropod relationships. Relationships within Ampullariidae were well-supported: the P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii clades were recovered as monophyletic groups, and the Effusa clade (P. nobilis + Pomacea sp.) formed a strongly supported sister group to both clades (BI: 1.00, ML: 100). Based on the available whole mitogenomes, the observed intraspecific variation in P. nobilis was 2.58% among samples from Loreto, whereas the divergence between P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. was 7.23%.

Selection pressure analyses

The Ka/Ks ratios calculated for the 13 PCGs were all below 1, reflecting overall low rates of nonsynonymous substitutions across the mitochondrial genome of Pomacea (Fig. 11). The highest ratios were observed in ATP8 (0.253), NAD2 (0.232), and NAD4L (0.223), whereas the cytochrome oxidase genes showed comparatively lower values, indicating variation in evolutionary rates among genes. No significant signal of positive selection was detected in BUSTED for most genes (p > 0.05). However, the gene encoding cytochrome c oxidase subunit III (COIII) in the P. bridgesii clade showed evidence of episodic diversifying selection (p = 0.025), although this signal was not significant after multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (FDR-adjusted p = 0.076). Site-level analysis with FEL identified numerous codons under purifying selection but no sites under significant positive selection. The RELAX test did not detect a significant change in the intensity of selection either in the concatenated dataset or in the COIII gene within the P. bridgesii clade.

Discussion

Mitogenome size and structure

The size of Pomacea mitogenomes ranges from 15,516 bp in P. occulta [77] to 16,373 bp in P. diffusa [78], placing P. nobilis (16279–16471) and Pomacea sp. (16355 bp) among the largest reported for the genus. However, the largest mitogenomes within Ampullariidae have been observed in Pila, particularly P. virens (16839 bp) and P. globosa (16903 bp) (Ayyagari, unpublished). The comparative mitogenome analyses also revealed that the proportion of intergenic regions in both P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. (∼4.8%) is higher than in other Pomacea species (2.43–3.46%), although lower than in M. cornuarietis (5.38%) and Pila (6.81–7.06%). Likewise, we detected differences in the location and size of their largest intergenic region (111 bp between tRNA-Ala and tRNA-Arg) when compared with other Pomacea, in which the largest intergenic region occurs between COIII and tRNA-Lys (47–50 bp in the P. canaliculata clade and 65–67 bp in the P. bridgesii clade) [21, 76, 79]. In M. cornuarietis, the largest intergenic region (145 bp) is located between tRNA-Ala and tRNA-Arg [74], whereas in Pila this region is longer (214–306 bp) and positioned between tRNA-Gln and tRNA-Gly. Thus, although length variation in PCGs, rRNAs, and tRNAs has been reported within Ampullariidae [21], differences in mitogenome size are primarily driven by the expansion of intergenic regions.

The mitogenomes of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. include a putative 39 bp overlap between ATP6 and tRNA-Met, a feature not previously reported in other Ampullariidae [21]. In contrast, the 7 bp overlap between NAD4L and NAD4 identified in both species corresponds to a highly conserved feature across Ampullariidae [21, 76, 77, 79] and is similarly widespread in most Caenogastropoda [68, 80–82]. Other overlapping regions reported in Pomacea, such as NAD1/tRNA-Pro in P. canaliculata, P. maculata and P. occulta [76, 79], and NAD5/tRNA-Phe in P. reevei, P. aulanieri and P. diffusa [21], were not found in P. nobilis or Pomacea sp. Gene overlaps are relatively common in animal mitochondrial genomes and have been proposed to arise as a consequence of selective pressures toward genome minimization and compact gene organization [83–85]. Previous studies have also suggested that the transcriptional implications of overlapping regions may depend on whether the genes are encoded on the same or opposite strands, with opposite-strand overlaps potentially requiring less complex transcript processing [85]. Notably, the putative ATP6/tRNA-Met overlap identified in the Effusa representatives involves genes encoded on opposite strands. This may facilitate the evolutionary persistence of this overlap, which could represent a distinctive feature of the Effusa clade among available ampullariid mitogenomes.

Mitochondrial base composition and protein-coding gene features

The overall base composition and skew patterns of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. are consistent with other Ampullariidae, although both species exhibit the highest AT-skew and lowest GC-skew in the family. Slight differences in A + T content among Pomacea clades (Effusa, P. bridgesii, and P. canaliculata) reflect minor compositional variation, while differences among genomic features (CR> rRNAs> tRNAs> PCGs) are consistent with previously reported patterns in gastropods [80, 82, 86, 87] and may be related to functional differences [15].

We recovered consistent differences in the length of several PCGs within Pomacea, with both Effusa lineages analyzed here showing the smallest ATP8 (156 bp), and an unusually long ATP6 annotation (768 bp), together with the longest NAD6 (498 bp), COIII (786 bp), and NAD2 (1077 bp) recorded for the genus. Notably, NAD1 and NAD4 in both Effusa lineages match the lengths observed in the P. bridgesii clade, while NAD5 aligns with that of the P. canaliculata clade. These patterns not only confirm the inter-clade differences reported between the P. bridgesii and P. canaliculata groups [21], but also indicate that the Effusa clade possesses a distinct pattern in PCG lengths, reinforcing its differentiation from the other two clades.

The annotation analyses suggested that the ATP6 gene in both P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. may be unusually long (768 bp), exceeding typical lengths reported in both Ampullariidae and other Caenogastropoda, where values below 700 bp are most common (GenBank survey, Dec 2025). Read mapping showed high coverage (102–1376×) and no evidence of indels or ambiguous sites at the terminal region, supporting the integrity of the assembled region and continuity of the predicted open reading frame. Inspection of the nucleotide alignment showed no evidence of premature termination or frame disruption, revealing instead a variation in the stop codon positions across lineages, with a notable 3’ extension in the Effusa clade (Additional file 2). Gene boundaries were defined based on the longest uninterrupted ORF consistent with these read-mapping signals and conserved protein domains. InterPro analysis confirmed the presence of the conserved ATP synthase subunit a domain, and transmembrane prediction recovered the expected five helices, with additional residues forming a short C-terminal extension. Finally, alignment of ATP6 amino acid sequences across available Pomacea and other Ampullariidae revealed that all sampled representatives of the Effusa clade share this extension, whereas other species terminate earlier (Additional file 3).

One alternative explanation could involve the use of an incomplete stop codon, a feature rarely documented in Caenogastropoda [72, 80, 81, 88]. Under this scenario, ATP6 could terminate closer to the adjacent tRNA-Met gene, and the apparent 39 bp overlap would reflect differences in gene boundary annotation rather than a true C-terminal extension. However, incomplete stop codons have not previously been reported in Ampullariidae, and the adjacent tRNA-Met is encoded on the opposite strand, potentially reducing constraints associated with the observed overlap [85]. Together, these observations are consistent with an extended ATP6 coding region in the sampled Effusa representatives, although this interpretation remains tentative and would benefit from future validation using transcriptomic data.

Mitochondrial rRNAs, tRNAs, and control region features

The 12S rRNA and 16S rRNA of P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. are the largest reported within Pomacea, exceeding the ranges observed in the P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii clades, and their predicted secondary structures are consistent with previous reports in the genus [21] and other mollusks [41, 89, 90]. This includes the presence of specific features proposed for Caenogastropoda in the 16S rRNA secondary structure, such as the stem-loop D13-D14 in Domain II, the development of Domain III and the presence of a bulge-stem-loop structure (G16) in Domain V [91], that are present in both P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. Although the comparison of the secondary structure of the mitochondrial RNA in Pomacea and other Caenogastropoda [41] revealed that they share the same number and arrangement of stems and loops, some differences were identified within Pomacea. Notably in 12S rRNA, loop 31 and stem 40 are larger in P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. than in P. aulanieri and P. reevei [21], suggesting that secondary-structure comparisons of the least conserved domains of 12S and 16S rRNA may be useful for distinguishing closely related species.

We found that in both tRNA for serine in P. nobilis and Pomacea sp., the clover-leaf secondary structure is modified by a large bulge replacing the typical stem-loop structure in the D-domain (Fig. 6). Among Ampullaridae, tRNA-Ser1 exhibits this modified structure in Pila, Marisa, and most species of the P. canaliculata clade (P. canaliculata, P. maculata and P. occulta) [21, 76], but not in the P. bridgesii clade, where tRNA-Ser2 is the modified tRNA [21]. The only other Ampullariidae with both tRNAs for serine modified is P. reevei of the P. canaliculata group [21]. Jühling et al. [92]. reported that the loss of the D-domain in tRNA-Ser1 is widespread across many groups of animals, including Mollusca, whereas the modification of tRNA-Ser2 is a rarer feature. Outside Pomacea, the loss of the D-domain in tRNA-Ser2 has been reported in only a few Caenogastropoda [70, 80, 93, 94].

The resulting coverage plots (Fig. 7) show that reads map across the control region in all samples, but the coverage is heterogeneous, with evident peaks and drops, particularly within this region, and its length and structure should therefore be interpreted with caution. Despite these limitations, tandem repeat analysis suggested the presence of (TA)n motifs and short AT-rich repeats, with apparent differences among the three P. nobilis mitogenomes (Table 7). Similar AT-rich repeats have been described in the control region of other molluscan mitogenomes, such as in Haliotis rubra [95], where variation in repeat number has been proposed as a potential genetic marker. Additionally, differences in the number and size of predicted stem-loop structures were observed among P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. (Fig. 8), as well as in comparison with other species [21]. These observations suggest that the control region of Pomacea may harbor variable and potentially informative features, but further validation using long-read sequencing approaches will be necessary to confirm their biological relevance.

Phylogenetic analysis and genetic distances

The phylogenetic analyses recovered Viviparoidea, Ampullarioidea, and Cyclophoroidea as monophyletic groups, consistent with previous mitogenomic studies [21, 70, 79, 80]. However, deeper caenogastropod relationships remain uncertain, and our discussion therefore focuses on relationships within Ampullariidae, where mitogenome data provide more robust resolution. The Effusa group was first recognized as a distinct clade within Pomacea based on molecular data from P. guyanensis and P. glauca [20]. Subsequent work identified P. nobilis as a third confirmed member of this clade [22], a species widely distributed across the Peruvian Amazon basin and characterized by its large and robust shell [22, 23]. The present study further expands the known diversity of the Effusa clade by identifying an additional lineage from the Aguaytía River, suggesting that species diversity within this group may be underestimated in the western Amazon.

Based on the whole mitogenomes, we found an intraspecific variation of 2.58% in P. nobilis, slightly lower than the variation reported by Ramírez et al. [23]. based on COI sequences from Loreto (3.17%). Similarly, the interspecific distance between P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. (7.23%) is comparable to the genetic distance between P. maculata and P. occulta (6.84%), also based on whole mitogenomes, supporting the genetic distinctiveness of the Aguaytía lineage relative to P. nobilis. Comparison of the COI sequence of Pomacea sp. from the Aguaytía River with publicly available COI sequences failed to reveal correspondence with any currently described species. Together with its phylogenetic placement within the Effusa clade, these results support the distinctiveness of the Aguaytía lineage. However, its precise taxonomic identity remains unresolved, and it is therefore treated here as an unidentified Pomacea lineage pending formal taxonomic assessment.

Selection pressure analyses

The selection pressure analyses indicate that mitochondrial protein-coding genes in Pomacea are predominantly evolving under purifying selection, as reflected by Ka/Ks ratios consistently below 1. This pattern is typical for animal mitochondrial genomes, where strong functional constraints maintain the integrity of oxidative phosphorylation genes [96]. Among the genes analyzed, ATP8, NAD2, and NAD4L showed comparatively higher Ka/Ks ratios, suggesting relatively relaxed constraints and higher evolutionary rates. These results are consistent with previous observations that certain NADH dehydrogenase genes and ATP8 tend to evolve more rapidly in gastropod mitogenomes [97, 98]. The comparatively elevated rates in these genes support their potential utility as markers for population-level or phylogeographic studies in Pomacea. Although an episodic signal was detected in COIII within the P. bridgesii clade, this result was not significant after multiple testing correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

This study reports the first complete mitochondrial genomes of the Effusa clade, based on P. nobilis and a putative undescribed Pomacea lineage (Pomacea sp.). Comparisons with the P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii clades revealed notable differences in genome size, intergenic regions, gene lengths, tRNA secondary structures, whereas the control region showed potentially distinct features that require further validation. Similar associations between mitogenome organization and phylogenetic relationships have previously been reported for the P. canaliculata and P. bridgesii clades [21], and our results suggest that this pattern may extend to the Effusa clade. Together, these observations indicate that aspects of mitochondrial genome architecture may retain phylogenetic signal within Pomacea and reflect evolutionary modifications accumulated during the diversification of its major clades. Further exploration of additional lineages may reveal whether the mitogenomic features observed in P. nobilis and Pomacea sp. are widespread within the Effusa clade and could aid in refining our understanding of mitogenome evolution in Pomacea.
