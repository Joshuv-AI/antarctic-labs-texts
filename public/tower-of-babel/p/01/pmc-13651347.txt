General practice workforce retention strategies: an umbrella review
Authors: L Jefferson, S Golder, A Castro-Avila, E Webster, H Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe summarise international evidence of interventions to support retention of the general practice workforce, mapping evidence by country, method and workforce group.DesignUmbrella overview of reviews. We mapped evidence according to occupational group, intervention type and outcome to identify trends and gaps. Varied study designs were included, though only reviews with evaluations of workforce interventions were included. Exploratory studies describing factors associated with retention were excluded.SettingInternational primary care and general practice.ParticipantsGeneral practice workforce, with studies grouped by general practitioners, nurses working in general practice and mixed health professional groups.Main outcome measuresWorkforce retention, including length of employment, stability rates, settlement rates, vacancy rates, unfilled positions, turnover numbers or rates, attrition rates, intentions to stay, leave and return.ResultsOf the 4,919 articles identified through database searching (Medline, Embase, PsycInfo and Medrxiv 6th February 2025) and citation tracking, we included five reviews. Background

Imbalance in healthcare workforce capacity is challenging general practice workforces internationally. In the UK, rising patient demand due to aging populations and backlogs of unmet need generated during the COVID-19 pandemic [1, 2] has not been sufficiently met with workforce increases. The number of patients registered with practices in the England has risen by 14% since 2015 [3] and modelling suggests an additional 6,500 full time general practitioners (GPs) will be needed to meet demand by 2031 [4]. Meanwhile, GP participation rates, measured as full time equivalents (FTE), are decreasing (from 0.80 to 0.69 median FTE since 2015) [5] and around two thirds of GPs in England are considering leaving the profession within five years [6–8]. Internationally, pooled estimates from a meta-analysis of studies reporting turnover intentions suggest that 47% of GPs have a moderate intention to leave practice (CIs 39–55%) [9].

Research has reported increases in burnout and mental health problems driven by rising patient demand [1, 2], toxic workplace cultures, and poor work-life balance, which have been directly linked to intentions to leave [10, 11]. Across the UK health system as a whole, record rates of sickness absence also impact participation rates – in August 2021 almost 2 million sickness days were lost across the NHS in one month alone, with 560,000 of these due to mental health problems [12].

Questions exist around the future of the general practice model if trends continue [13, 14]. Practices are closing in some parts of the country, leaving patients without a local GP and resulting in increased workload for remaining practices; often in deprived areas where health needs are greatest [15, 16]. Fewer GPs are now taking on roles as GP partners – 10% fewer over the past 5 years [14] – fuelling practice closures and resulting in higher workloads and risk for remaining GP partners [14, 17]. GPs also report increasing attractiveness of ‘portfolio roles’ that are associated with fewer clinical hours in order to manage challenging workloads [11, 12, 18].

To solve workforce capacity issues, policy approaches can be grouped into one of the 3 Rs: encouraging either Recruitment, Retention or Return of the workforce. In general practice, recruitment interventions dominate international research, particularly focused on underserved or rural areas [19]. These strategies are an expensive, and long-term solution – at a cost of nearly £500,000 [20] and taking nearly ten years to train a fully-qualified GP in the UK. As a result, a shift has been seen towards retention strategies [21, 22]. The Department of Health and Social Care now recognise ‘shaping and supporting the workforce’ as one of three Areas of Research Interest outlined by the UK Department of Health and Social Care [23]. Developing strategies to support and retain this workforce is needed to meet the quadruple aim of healthcare, giving new emphasis on provider wellbeing alongside previously recognised aims of improving patient experience, population health and reducing healthcare costs. [24] This is welcome, not least because doctor wellbeing is important in its own right, but also since it effects the quality of patient care [25, 26].

In 2023, the Royal College of General Practitioners stressed a need for a comprehensive review of existing retention initiatives to support future policy and research. [14] Our umbrella review aims to meet this need through a summary of the international evidence of interventions to support retention of the general practice workforce. Specifically, we map evidence by country, approach and workforce group to enable insights for future research and policy.

Methods

We conducted our umbrella review according to the Cochrane guidelines for systematic reviews [27] followed the PRIOR guideline for reporting the review [28]. All search strategies were developed by an Information Specialist (SG) and peer reviewed. We searched databases (Medline, Embase, PsycInfo and Medrxiv) and Google Scholar for reviews of general practice workforce retention conducted from 2010 to 6th February 2025. To focus our review on empirical evidence evaluating interventions, we limited our searches to include only systematic reviews and scoping reviews, using the criteria below. Full searches are available in Appendix 1.

Inclusion criteria

Type of participants

Studies in the general practice workplace setting were considered, including doctors, nurses and other primary care health professionals. Trainee groups were excluded. International terminology was considered, for example the use of ‘family practice’ in the USA. Owing to the large number of reviews in hospital care settings, only reviews with a focus on general practice or primary care settings were included. Reviews focusing only on incentivising rural practice retention were excluded since they addressed slightly different aims (retention to a location rather than broader workforce retention). These also tended to be based in countries with particular issues of rurality; such as Australia, Canada and USA, which may not be transferable to other contexts.

Type of intervention

Interventions at either an individual, practice or system level were included. Reviews were excluded if there was no evaluation of an intervention i.e. if they were exploratory in nature.

Type of outcome measures

Measures of workforce retention, turnover or future intentions were included (for example, length of employment, stability rates, settlement rates, vacancy rates, unfilled positions, turnover numbers or rates, attrition rates, intention to stay/leave, intentions to return).

Types of study design

Review methodologies were included, including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, scoping reviews and reviews of reviews.

No language limits were placed on the search. As well as reference checking all the included studies, we conducted forward citation searches using CitationChaser. Reviews of multiple health sector groups were excluded, owing to their tendency to not report outcomes separately for general practice workers.

Selection of studies

We used the software tool Covidence [29] to conduct screening, firstly of titles and abstracts, and later screening full texts of articles deemed potentially relevant. Both stages were undertaken by two independent reviewers (LJ, SG, AC, EW, HA or VD), with arbitration by a third reviewer where necessary. Excluded articles and reasons for exclusion were used to generate a flow diagram as recommended by the PRISMA statement [30].

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed by two reviewers (AC, LJ) using a pre-piloted data extraction form, with a 20% sample cross-checked and inter-rater reliability assessed. Information was extracted regarding country, number of included studies, proportion of studies with focus on rurality, search dates, health professional group, nature and direction of outcomes. To enable a more detailed discussion of the evidence base, we extracted data from individual studies included in reviews, applying the inclusion criteria to describe only relevant included studies.

Data synthesis

We used evidence mapping according to country, outcomes, professional groups and nature of intervention to outline evidence gaps. We categorised interventions as either: 1) Educational interventions, 2) Financial interventions, 3) Regulatory strategies or 4) Personal and professional support strategies using a framework previously used by the World Health Organisation [31] and a Cochrane review [32]. We adapted the framework (Fig. 1) to remove categories solely relevant to rural settings (since these were excluded), and expanded some subcategories to reflect additional interventions identified relating to re-entry schemes, role flexibility and collaboration between GP practices.

Results

Search results

We identified 4,919 articles through database searching and citation tracking. After removing duplicates, we identified 3,025 articles that were screened as titles and abstracts. At this stage, we excluded 2,735, leaving 290 for full text review. In total, five reviews [33–37] were identified that met our inclusion criteria (Fig. 2).

PRISMA diagram

While retention was the focus of our review, it was often a secondary outcome in included studies. For example, policy interventions identified in Pettigrew’s review of working at scale tended to focus on patient outcome measures, rather than workforce outcomes [37]. A review by Harrington et al. [38] explored the use of mentoring in nurse practitioner programmes but no evaluations were identified, so this review was excluded from our search. Reviews by Peckham et al. [19], later updated by Mitchell et al., [39] also did not find any formal evaluations of GP retention strategies, instead providing summaries of studies exploring factors associated with retention. These reviews were excluded from this umbrella review.

Study characteristics

Three reviews were focused on the nursing workforce in primary care settings [33–35] and two with a focus on GPs or primary care doctors [36, 37]. No included reviews explored interventions to support mixed professional groups working in general practice. Studies included in the reviews were undertaken between 1974 and 2019; the most recent search date of included reviews was 2022 (Table 1).

For all five included reviews, the overall confidence in the results was deemed as critically low because they had two or more critical flaws. None of the included reviews conducted a meta-analysis or included a list of excluded studies providing an individual reason for exclusion. Evidence mapping

Our heatmaps by intervention type and outcome highlight potential evidence gaps in strategies to support general practice workforce retention. Across all included reviews and studies, a total of 40 interventions were identified (Table 3), with the majority reported as positive (n = 32). When disaggregated by occupational group (Table 4), 14 interventions related specifically to GPs and 26 to nurses. Outcomes were generally more consistently positive for nurses (n = 24 positive, 2 mixed) than for GPs (n = 8 positive, 5 mixed, 1 negative).

Only one review found a potentially negative effect of interventions when considering retention. Pettigrew et al. [40] explored the use of working at scale in UK general practice and reported potential negative impact on retention. However, this was based on the evidence from one qualitative study.

Education

Educational interventions focused primarily on support for students or trainees, included rotations in primary care, curricula that reflect primary care issues and also providing continuous professional development (CPD) once qualified. Educational strategies were one of the most commonly reported approaches, although only captured in one review of nursing professionals. Positive outcomes were reported for clinical rotations in primary care (5 studies), curricula reflecting primary care issues (5 studies) and CPD (2 studies). However, among the studies looking at clinical rotations in primary care and curricula reflecting primary care issues, only one study had a comparison group [41–45]. The remaining studies measured number of students that worked in underserved primary care areas after graduation or factors contributing to their decision to work in primary care. In contrast, studies that assessed continuous development programmes focused on loan repayment schemes that included a strong professional development focus and factors making them successful at retaining staff in underserved communities [46, 47]. We found no studies evaluating educational interventions in GPs or allied health professionals.

Regulatory

From our included reviews, we found no studies exploring the impact of employing different types of health workers on retention outcomes, nor compulsory service, which is a strategy more often applied to retain rural health workers. One study with nurses found that being able to practice to the full scope of their profession influenced their decision to stay working in underserved primary care areas after completing a ‘National Health Service Corps’ programme [48]. Subsidised education for return of service produced mixed outcomes amongst GPs (5 studies). Three studies that had a comparison group found positive results, but they all assessed different programmes [49–51]. Matsumoto et al. [49] examined a Japanese nine-year mandatory service agreement where the candidate received fully funded undergraduate medical training in exchange for service in their home region. Prefectures with a relative shortage of physicians had higher settlement rates, and participants choosing primary care were more likely to settle (1.80 [95% CI 1.35–2.39]) [47]. Mathews et al. (2013) evaluated two return-for-service schemes in Canada (i.e., Family Medicine Bursary and Special Funded Residency Position), finding that doctors who received funding for their education were 3.2 times less likely to leave an underserved area during the repayment period. Pathman et al. (2004) evaluated five separate support-for-service financial incentives finding that retention rates were statistically significantly higher for obligated versus non-obligated doctors (Hazard ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.96). Two further studies did not include a comparison group, therefore it is difficult to draw conclusions. One study reported on a postgraduate voluntary bonding scheme in New Zealand which recruited trainees to hard-to-staff communities for five years, with repayments starting after the third year. They found that 89% of graduates had opted out of the scheme three years after entering. Meanwhile, Nichols et al. (1977) explored an Arizona programme that subsidised out-of-state medical education by covering the difference between resident and non-resident tuition and offering incentives to medical schools to accept Arizona students. They report 62% of participants repaid their loan through in-state service.

Studies including nurses found negative results (2 studies). Pathman et al. (2012a) surveyed participants in the National Health Service Corps (NHSC) programme, finding clinicians motivated mostly by the financial support were less likely to have intentions to stay in their service sites over time. Negrusa et al. [52] found that NHSC participants were less likely to work in health professional shortage areas than non-participants in programme one year after completion (82% vs 95%).

One included review identified a study with positive retention outcomes in an evaluation of re-entry schemes for GPs [53]. The scheme provided support through tutorials and mentoring to 14 participants. Two participants had return to work after 6 months, while 11 had taken “positive steps” to return or had increased their working hours. In comparison, only one participant from the control group had made plans to return to general practice (denominator not reported).

Financial incentives

Three studies [46–48] found that competitive salary and productivity incentives were associated with intention to stay among nurses participating in loan repayment schemes in the US (i.e., NHSC and Obligatory Health Care Recruitment Program in Delaware). No studies were included reporting the use of financial incentives for GPs.

Professional and personal support interventions

Interventions that provided professional and personal support for both nursing and GP professionals were identified – all with positive retention outcomes. For GPs, evaluations of career development programmes identified positive outcomes in four studies [54–57]and four specifically identified role flexibility as supporting GP retention [53, 58–60]. As aforementioned, one review reported negative potential consequences of GP practices working at scale [40].

For nurses, interventions that created safe and supportive working environments influenced retention and intention to stay (6 studies). These findings came from two qualitative studies [61, 62], and four studies without a comparison group assessing loan repayment schemes (e.g., Obligatory Health Care Recruitment Program in Delaware and the NHSC) [46–48, 63].

Career development programmes (2 studies) were also associated with better retention of nurses in underserved communities. For example, Aysola et al. (2015) found that health centres with career ladder programmes had higher retention rates than centres without those programmes (39.4% vs. 21.2%, p = 0.0001). Similarly, nurses who belong to a practice-based research network had longer mean employment years in underserved areas than non-members (7.0 ± 6.8 years versus 4.0 ± 5.0 years, p = 0.003) [64]. Other strategies identified by the WHO framework of interventions [31] we used, such as outreach support, professional networks and public recognition measures lacked supporting evidence.

Summary

In summary, interventions addressing education and professional support appear most consistently beneficial, particularly for nurses, while career development programmes and role flexibility show promise for GPs. We found no evidence assessing compulsory service, inclusion of different health care workers in primary care, belonging to professional networks or public recognition measures. This evidence comes mainly from the US, the UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Japan.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This review maps and synthesises the evidence base from existing reviews exploring strategies to support workforce retention in primary care settings. There are notable gaps, both in terms of when and where the reviews and included studies were undertaken. The most recent review used searches in 2022 and there was, in general, a lack of coverage of countries outside Australia, the US and the UK. Based on our critical appraisal of the included reviews, our overall confidence in the results was critically low due to two or more critical flaws. Further, the evidence base overall was dominated by observational and qualitative studies, with few comparison groups, which is surprising given this review focused on interventions.

Our evidence mapping reveals a general paucity of evidence across many intervention types. There were more studies from the included reviews exploring strategies to support nursing workforce outcomes and these tended to be consistently favourable, while for GPs there were fewer studies and more mixed results. Educational and professional support interventions showed the most consistent benefits, particularly for nurses. Educational strategies such as primary care rotations, relevant curricula and continuing professional development were associated with improved retention, though evidence for GPs was lacking. Career development, mentoring, role flexibility and supportive work environments were consistently linked to better retention for both groups.

Regulatory and financial approaches produced mixed results: some return-of-service schemes improved GP retention, while others involving nurses, showed negative effects. Evidence from one qualitative study [37] found a negative impact of working at scale on GP retention, though further research is now needed. Significant evidence gaps remain for other areas identified in the WHO framework we applied [31], including compulsory service, workforce mix, living conditions, professional networks and public recognition measures.

Across reviews, we found that workforce retention often featured as a secondary outcome, with many interventions primarily targeting patient outcomes or broader organisational goals. Meanwhile, a review of nurse practitioner mentoring52 and reviews of GP retention [19, 39] highlight a lack of formal evaluations, emphasising the need for more focused research in these areas.

Implications for practice, policy, and future research

As per Cochrane and GRADE guidelines [27, 65], we urge caution in making firm recommendations for policy and practice due to the general lack of comparison groups, heterogeneous outcomes and contexts of included studies. However, when considered in the context of ongoing workforce challenges in UK general practice, some tentative implications can be drawn. Interventions identified in this umbrella review, particularly those focused on improving working conditions, professional support and career development, are consistent with international workforce strategies and recommendations that explicitly prioritise building supportive environments over single, short-term interventions [66]. They also align with findings from recent UK-based research, including a realist review which highlights the importance of relationships, human connection building in general practice, as well as learning and development and psychological safety [67]. Similarly, our analysis of national UK data on GP turnover suggests that positive workplace relationships and a sense of belonging are associated with improved retention [68].

Nevertheless, the lack of overlap between included studies (CCA: 0%) highlights the need for an updated evidence synthesis to build on these reviews, including varied primary care workforce groups and a specific focus on retention. This may highlight whether evidence gaps have been filled and allow a comprehensive assessment of the quality of included studies, since our focus here has been on evaluating the quality of included reviews. Broader global contexts also need to be understood – searches from included reviews were limited by geography.

Strengths and limitations

This umbrella review provides a valuable insight into the existing evidence base, with this approach offering a high-level overview of the research gaps for interventions to support general practice workforce retention. The transferability of these findings is limited by important contextual differences across health systems. While challenges such as workload pressures, burnout and turnover intentions are widely reported, the scale and nature of shortages vary between countries, with some facing overall supply issues and others more localised maldistribution. Primary care workforce composition also differs, with varying use of multidisciplinary teams that may mitigate or redistribute pressures on doctors. In addition, differences in funding models, employment arrangements and the role of private sector provision may influence retention dynamics, including the potential for movement between sectors. As a result, interventions identified in one setting may not be directly applicable elsewhere without adaptation, highlighting the need for context-specific evidence.

Nevertheless, this umbrella review highlights evidence gaps that may help to prioritise future research efforts and policies to support future workforce retention. In addition to the geographical limitations of the searches of included review, there were also challenges in terms of how well evidence was reported in some studies. For example, it was common for reviews to report findings for recruitment and retention together, but often challenging to disentangle the evidence due to reporting.

Conclusion

This research identifies significant gaps in the evidence base in this field and underscores the need for more targeted workforce retention research.
