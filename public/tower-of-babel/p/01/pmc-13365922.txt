Iliac artery endofibrosis treated by endarterectomy with patch angioplasty via a retroperitoneal approach in an amateur triathlete
Authors: Kohei Ueno, Koichi Morisaki, Go Kinoshita, Shinichiro Yoshino, Kentaro Inoue, Tomoharu Yoshizumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iliac artery endofibrosis is a nonatheromatous vascular disease observed in endurance athletes. A 46-year-old female amateur triathlete presented with exercise-induced claudication of the left leg. The resting ankle-brachial index was normal, but the postexercise ankle-brachial index decreased on the left side. Computed tomography angiography showed stenosis of the left common iliac and external iliac arteries, and magnetic resonance angiography after exercise demonstrated worsening of the stenosis, which was consistent with iliac artery endofibrosis. The patient underwent endarterectomy with patch angioplasty via a retroperitoneal approach. Postoperative computed tomography angiography confirmed resolution of the stenosis. The patient subsequently returned to triathlon racing without recurrence of symptoms.

Iliac artery endofibrosis (IAE) is a nonatheromatous vascular disease observed in high-level endurance athletes, such as cyclists, runners, and triathletes.1 The etiology is thought to be related to repeated mechanical stress and compression of the iliac artery, resulting in fibrosis and thickening of the arterial intimal layer.2 Furthermore, IAE may be accompanied by elongation or kinking of the iliac artery.3 The external iliac artery (EIA) is the most commonly involved artery in IAE, although the common iliac artery (CIA) and the common femoral artery may also be affected.4

Patients typically present with exercise-induced claudication, which may significantly limit athletic performance.5 The Delphi consensus, based on expert opinions, represents the only established guideline for the management of IAE.1 According to this consensus, conservative treatment, particularly reduction or cessation of high-intensity training, is generally recommended as the primary management strategy.1 However, for patients who wish to continue competitive activities, invasive management, such as surgical or endovascular revascularization, may be considered.1 This report presents a case of IAE in a 46-year-old female amateur triathlete who was successfully treated with endarterectomy and patch angioplasty via a retroperitoneal approach. This case highlights that surgical treatment, particularly endarterectomy, is an effective treatment for IAE, as suggested by the Delphi consensus. Patient consent for publication was obtained.

Case

A 46-year-old female amateur triathlete presented with intermittent claudication of the left lower extremity during cycling. The patient had no medical history. On physical examination, bilateral lower-extremity pulses were palpable, and the resting ankle-brachial index was normal (right/left, 1.16/1.08). However, the postexercise ankle-brachial index of the left leg was reduced (right/left, 0.91/0.44). Preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) at rest revealed stenosis of the left CIA and EIA (Fig 1). Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) performed 5 minutes after the exercise tolerance test using ergometer cycling revealed stenosis of the left CIA and a signal defect in the left EIA (Fig 2, A). Improvement in stenosis was confirmed by MRA 15 minutes after exercise (Fig 2, B). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with IAE. Surgical revascularization was performed with the patient under general anesthesia. Intraoperative preangiography showed stenotic lesions of the CIA and EIA, consistent with the preoperative CTA and MRA findings. A left pararectal incision was made, and the terminal abdominal aorta, bilateral CIA, left EIA, and left internal iliac artery were exposed using a retroperitoneal approach (Fig 3, A). After systemic heparinization, the arteries were clamped. The anterior wall of the left CIA-EIA was incised, and a thickened fibrotic intima was observed, with the main fibrotic lesion located in the EIA (Fig 3, B). Following endarterectomy using dissecting forceps (Fig 3, C), patch angioplasty was performed with a bovine pericardium patch (XenoSure Biologic Patch; LeMaitre) using 5-0 polypropylene sutures (Fig 3, D). Intraoperative postangiography showed adequate blood flow through the left CIA and EIA, with stenosis resolution. Histopathological examination revealed intimal thickening of the CIA and EIA, characterized by loose connective tissue with haphazardly arranged spindle-shaped cells (Fig 4). Discussion

In the present case, the patient was successfully treated with endarterectomy and patch angioplasty via a retroperitoneal approach. According to the Delphi consensus, surgery is the preferred invasive treatment, whereas endovascular treatment is not recommended because of the risk of recurrence.1

The surgical procedures for IAE include endarterectomy, vessel shortening, bypass, and interposition. Among these, endarterectomy is the only procedure endorsed by the Delphi consensus.1 Endarterectomy with patch angioplasty has achieved favorable long-term patency rates in approximately 90% of cases, enabling many patients to return to high-level athletic activity.6 The patient in the present case was also treated with endarterectomy and patch angioplasty and had achieved an adequate clinical outcome without restenosis at the 18-month follow-up, even though the patient had resumed triathlon training and participated in competitions. However, restenosis can occur even after endarterectomy.6 In order to reduce the risk of restenosis as much as possible, it might be important to perform thorough preoperative imaging evaluations, including exercise stress assessment, and to sufficiently resect the affected lesion when performing endarterectomy.

Vessel shortening is useful when significant arterial elongation and kinking are identified. However, as noted in the Delphi consensus, vessel shortening alone is ineffective for the treatment of IAE because kinking alone rarely causes significant symptoms.1 Therefore, arterial shortening should preferably be performed in addition to endarterectomy.7

Bypass or interposition surgery is typically selected for long stenotic or occlusive lesions, and both prosthetic and autologous vein grafts have been reported.8, 9, 10 Although the majority of specialists supported this strategy in the Delphi consensus, formal agreement was not achieved.1

Furthermore, no specific recommendations regarding the surgical approach were provided.1 In the present case, the retroperitoneal approach was selected because it allows straightforward access and rapid exposure of the CIA-EIA. As demonstrated in abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, this approach is associated with reduced physiological stress, faster postoperative recovery, and a lower risk of wound complications, such as surgical-site infection or dehiscence.11,12 Furthermore, no incision was made in the pararectal muscle to preserve muscular integrity as much as possible, which may have helped reduce the risk of iatrogenic muscle denervation and postoperative bulging.13 These advantages may be particularly beneficial for athletes seeking an early return to training.

Similar to lower-extremity peripheral artery disease, the proportion of IAE cases treated with endovascular treatment has gradually increased.14,15 Recently, self-expanding stents, stent grafts, and drug-coated balloons have been used for IAE treatment.16, 17, 18 Unlike atherosclerotic stenoses, IAE lesions are highly elastic.16 Consequently, endovascular intervention may result in vascular recoil, intimal hyperplasia, or dissection of the endofibrotic segment.19, 20, 21 Therefore, endovascular treatment is not recommended by the Delphi consensus, and further studies are warranted.1

The evidence regarding long-term outcomes remains limited for both surgery and endovascular treatment, and the risk of postoperative restenosis should be taken into consideration regardless of any treatment. Therefore, as recommended by the Delphi consensus, regular surveillance is considered important.1

Conclusions

This case of IAE was successfully treated with endarterectomy and patch angioplasty via a retroperitoneal approach. Endarterectomy is considered an effective treatment for IAE, as suggested by the Delphi consensus.
