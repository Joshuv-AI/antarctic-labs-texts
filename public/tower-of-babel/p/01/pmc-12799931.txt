Editorial: Special issue on application of artificial intelligence in oral and craniofacial care
Authors: Anand Gupta, Naveen Aggarwal
Published: 2025

The field of oral and craniofacial care is on the cusp of a profound transformation, spearheaded by the recent advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI). In this issue of "Application of AI in Oral and Craniofacial Care," our aim is to highlight the fundamental AI revolution in nearly every discipline of oral and craniofacial surgery, from digital imaging and surgical planning to diagnostics. AI-driven diagnostics are bringing unprecedented precision in imaging, enabling the swift and accurate uncovering of early pathologies. The diverse collection of research presented in this issue provides compelling evidence of AI's expanding footprint, moving from theoretical promise to tangible clinical utility across diagnostics, treatment planning, and specialized care. As AI continues to refine treatment efficacy, so the imperative now is to guide its deployment responsibly, ensuring that its immense clinical power is matched by robust ethical frameworks that prioritize patient safety and equity.

Diagnostic and risk assessment precision

The most immediate and profound impact of AI is seen in its capacity to enhance diagnostic accuracy, particularly in areas where human interpretation is challenging or subject to inter-observer variability. Oya et al.2 demonstrate that AI trained on clear-cut cases can reveal previously unnoticed cancerous features in cells that are morphologically ambiguous, validating its potential as an indispensable second opinion in cancer screening. Similarly, Singh et al.12 reinforce this by emphasizing how AI in histopathology image analysis promotes early detection, enhances diagnostic precision by decreasing subjectivity, and allows for remote consultations, positioning it as a critical tool for oral cancer management.

AI models are proving highly effective in analyzing radiographic data for common and complex conditions. Studies by Madhumitha et al.4 for predicting pericoronal radiolucency on orthopantomograms and by Widyaningrum et al.9 for staging of periodontitis on panoramic radiographs, using AI, establish the foundation for automated, high-throughput screening tools that can prioritize cases and reduce diagnostic oversight. The potential for AI to transition from disease management to proactive oral healthcare is vividly illustrated in its application to caries risk assessment (CRA) and management by Bhatia et al.8 and detection of Early Childhood Caries (ECC) on radiographs and intraoral photos by Nayak et al.11 can empower caregivers for at-home screening, which is particularly vital for underserved regions.

Advanced treatment planning and specialized care

AI is moving beyond mere diagnosis to provide sophisticated, personalized roadmaps for specialized treatments, by managing complex root canal systems in endodontics using CBCT-derived features in mandibular molars by AI. Dev et al.3 A scoping review by Aradya et al.1 highlighted the significant technological shift in craniofacial rehabilitation. AI applications provided the automated production of implant-supported auricular prostheses and objective evaluation of facial attractiveness in cleft patients, which helped in improving both functional and aesthetic outcomes.5 AI's utility extends to other nuanced areas like age estimation. Chopra et al.7 updated how AI models, particularly CNNs, are achieving superior diagnostic performance for dental age estimation, with low mean absolute errors (MAEs), which is critical for forensic and legal applications.

The adoption and educational imperative

Despite the demonstrable clinical benefits, the successful integration of AI hinges on the preparedness of the dental workforce and the transparency of its deployment. The survey by Ansari et al.5 among endodontists paints a clear picture of the knowledge-to-practice gap and highlighted an immediate, urgent necessity for proper training programs and workshops to bridge the chasm between technological capability and clinical competence.

Subha et al.10 echo the positive outlook on AI among dental students but expose a critical deficiency in ethical awareness. Their finding is alarming, as the deployment of such powerful technology without a robust ethical framework risks exacerbating health disparities. Several articles raise concerns over the fundamental input that fuels AI models: data. Nayak et al.11 and Chopra et al.7 explicitly mention that lack of population-diverse training datasets and non-representative data can lead to demographic bias, fairness concerns, and algorithmic opacity. Unregulated AI applications could potentially worsen health disparities rather than reduce them.

Rajaraman et al.6 highlight the variability in input features and the lack of standardization in model development protocols in TMD diagnosis, calling for validation across diverse populations. Similarly, Chopra et al.7 advocate for the absence of international validation standards for forensic age estimation.

Conclusions

AI is no longer a future concept; it is a current reality reshaping oral and craniofacial care. From stratifying endodontic complexity to detecting subtle tumor cells and enhancing public health through early caries risk prediction, the evidence is compelling. However, the transition from lab-based accuracy to real-world clinical effectiveness demands a concerted effort in three core areas: Augmentation over Automation, Curricular Reform and Training, Ethical and Regulatory Vigilance.

The integration of AI promises to usher in an era of unparalleled precision medicine in oral and craniofacial care. The challenge now is to navigate the opportunities and hurdles with wisdom, ensuring that technological progress serves the overarching goal of improved patient outcomes and health equity for all. We need to work together in facilitating this critical discourse and championing the research that will define the next generation of patient care.
