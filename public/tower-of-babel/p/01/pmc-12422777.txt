Optimizing Surgical Strategies for Elderly Patients With Femoral Neck Fracture: The Critical Role of Comorbidities
Authors: Ye Peng, Xuezhu Qi, Mingzhi Yu, Gongzi Zhang, Moning Guo, Jianpeng Zheng, Lu Feng, Zaihua Wei, Jianting Su, Danhui Yi, Xinyu Liu, Yao Yao, Lihai Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Femoral neck fractures (FNFs) pose a notable challenge in the elderly population, given the high associated mortality rates and costs. The choice between internal fixation (IF) and hip arthroplasty (HA) has long been debated, yet existing guidelines often overlooked the crucial influence of comorbidities. With the increasing number of hip fracture cases globally and the complexity of patient conditions, it is essential to identify the key factors that truly affect surgical outcomes.Methods:We conducted a large-scale retrospective study across 152 Beijing hospitals, including 25,764 patients aged 60+ years with FNF. After excluding those with severe preexisting conditions, we collected data on patient characteristics and used advanced statistical methods for analysis.Results:Among the patients, 4568 received IF and 21,196 received HA. IF decreased 1-year mortality in patients with fewer than four comorbidities, whereas HA was more beneficial for those with four or more comorbidities. Mortality predicted by comorbidities was notably lower than that by age and sex (2.379% versus 2.790%, P < 0.001), and age had no marked influence on outcomes.Conclusion:In summary, for elderly patients with FNF, comorbidity profile, rather than age or sex, should be the key determinant in surgical choices to reduce all-cause mortality. These findings support the refinement of surgical guidelines and have implications for geriatric care. Future research should focus on enhancing comorbidity assessment in surgical planning.

Hip fracture, a major public health issue that affects millions of people globally, places notable financial burdens on healthcare systems, with annual expenditures of over €32 billion in the European Union and $20 billion in the United States.1-3 Despite advancements in orthopaedic practice, the 1-year mortality rates of patients with hip fractures, ranging from 7.8% to 56%, remains high, and long-term mortality were affected for 8 years at most.4-12

Regarding patient characteristics, most patients with hip fracture are older than 60 years of age, and more than half are older than 80 years of age.13-15 Risk factors include surgical treatment options, age, comorbidities, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification system grade, body mass index (BMI), and mental health status.16-23 Femoral neck fractures (FNFs) account for nearly half of all hip fractures and constitute a substantial part of relevant healthcare challenges.1-4,24

Internal fixation (IF) and hip arthroplasty (HA) are key options for FNF treatment, yet the choice between them is debated.25-33 Some studies suggest HA for patients older than 80 years or with ASA grades III-IV, and IF for those aged 60 to 80 years with lower ASA grades (I to II),26 but these recommendations overlook mortality factors, simplifying the decision process. Consequently, it is crucial to identify factors, excluding confounders, that guide surgical decisions for older patients with FNF to reduce overall mortality.

Current hip fracture guidelines from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence do not incorporate age, sex, or comorbidities into their treatment recommendations because of a lack of evidence.18,19 The identification and quantification of comorbidities in patients play pivotal roles in the surgical decision-making process, markedly affecting patient outcomes and mortality.34-40 Therefore, this study aims to stratify procedure selection by integrating the types and number of comorbidities. We use this classification as a foundational criterion for surgical decision making to ultimately optimize treatment strategies and minimize mortality.

To target most patients with hip fracture, we performed a real-world retrospective study on patients with FNF older than 60 years who were admitted to one of 152 hospitals in Beijing, China, between 2008 and 2016 to analyze the mortality and risk factors associated with IF and HA and identify the optimal choices for individual patients so that the overall mortality can be minimized to the lowest level and to provide solid evidence for clinical decision making.

Methods

Subjects

Patient data were sourced from the Beijing Municipal Health Commission Information Center, encompassing EMRs from 152 centers across 16 districts. Diagnoses were classified using ICD-9/10 codes (Supplemental Data 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A438). Long-term mortality data, including the date and cause of death, were matched using the Beijing CDC until December 31, 2019, with records of all-cause death ending on December 31, 2022. The data set included variables such as sex, age, comorbidities, transfusion status, hospital admission/discharge dates, surgery details, in-hospital mortality, and long-term outcomes.

The study enrolled 25,764 patients aged ≥60 years who sustained hip fractures between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2016. Exclusion criteria were (1) an FNF diagnosis within 12 months before the initial hip fracture; (2) missing age, sex, or key information; (3) a pathological fracture or hip arthritis; (4) opting for conservative treatment; (5) did not receive HA or IF but did receive conservative treatments; and (6) excluded those with conditions that were known to independently and markedly affect mortality, such as advanced-stage cancers and end-stage organ failure.

Exposure

Participants underwent either HA—including total or hemiarthroplasty, cemented or noncemented—or IF using screws or a sliding hip screw. Anesthesia options included general or regional anesthesia. After hospital discharge, all patients were referred for standard rehabilitation in their municipality.

Covariates

Baseline data were collected on age, sex, type of anesthesia, chronic diseases, comorbidity count, and transfusion status. Patients of hospital grade 0 received treatment in first-class hospitals, whereas those of grade 1 did not. Early enrollment refers to patients from the first 3 years, whereas later enrollment refers to those from the following 3 years.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline Characteristics

To compare the two surgical groups, categorical variables were analyzed with the chi square test and continuous variables were analyzed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Propensity score matching was used to adjust for differences and balance baseline factors between the groups.41-43 A Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the associations between surgical methods and mortality over time, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) describing these associations.

Variable Importance

Random forest44,45 was used to show the diagnostic ability of binary classifiers, and class-specific measures computed as the mean decrease in accuracy were calculated and sorted to determine the factors that influenced mortality the most.46 Surgical intervention types and other relevant factors that are known to influence death were considered in the analysis.

Recommended Surgical Treatment

Continuous influential factors in the surgical group were dichotomized using the Youden index as a threshold.47 The treatment with the lowest mortality rate was recommended for each group. This precise recommendation was based on influential factors and relative variables. The predicted number of deaths for each group was calculated using the recommended treatment's mortality rate, and the total number of deaths was obtained by summing across groups. The optimal threshold and grouping method were determined by minimizing deaths. Sensitivity checks confirmed the robustness of the recommendations, with subgroups analyzed to ensure consistency. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0 and Stata version 14.0, with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 25,764 patients, 68.3% were female. The median age was 76.06 years, and the median comorbidity count was 2. HA was performed on 82.3% of patients, whereas 17.7% underwent IF. General anesthesia was used in 39.0% of cases, with 61.0% receiving alternative anesthesia. Heart disease was the most common comorbidity (22.0%), followed by diabetes (21.6%), stroke (14.3%), lung disease (13.7%), and kidney disease (4.6%). In addition, 8.2% required blood transfusions during surgery. Demographics and baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

Mortality Correlates With the Choice of Surgical Treatment

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of patients by surgical method, with adjustments in Supplemental Data 2 (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A439). Postadjustment, feature distribution was balanced across treatment methods. Cox regression was performed on matched data using the treatment method as the independent variable and patient survival and mortality at various time points (30 days, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years) as dependent variables as shown in Supplemental Data 3 (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A440). IF served as the control, and the hazard ratio for HA was assessed. After 1 year, HA markedly reduced the risk of death compared with IF (HR: −0.27, CI: [−0.49 to −0.05]), making 1 year the most notable time point for evaluating treatment effects.

Importance of Vital Variables for 1-Year Mortality

The most critical factors for 1-year mortality were age, comorbidities, anesthesia type, pulmonary disease, diabetes, sex, heart disease, surgical method, kidney disease, stroke, and transfusion. The importance of each factor was assessed based on its ability to predict 1-year mortality (Figure 2). Age and comorbidity count were the top factors, markedly outweighing others. Specific importance scores were age (153.952), comorbidities (82.463), anesthesia (21.758), pulmonary disease (21.481), diabetes (20.554), sex (19.884), heart disease (19.839), surgical method (19.121), kidney disease (17.478), stroke (17.350), and transfusion (8.524). Overall, surgical decision-making had a notable effect on mortality, and decisions based on key factors such as age and comorbidity count substantially influenced patient outcomes.

Patient Choices for Surgical Treatment

Age and the comorbidity count are the two characteristics with the greatest effect on whether a patient dies within one year. The population is identified by variables that have a notable effect on death. Age and comorbidity count are continuous variables. Therefore, age and comorbidity count were divided into two categories so that the distribution of population characteristics was significant; then, the treatment method could be selected.

A total of 21,196 patients underwent HA; among them, 663 died within 1 year and 20,533 did not. A total of 4568 patients underwent IF; 104 of them died within 1 year, and 4464 of them did not. The optimal thresholds corresponding to the age and comorbidity quantitative characteristic Youden index were calculated for all patients and for each patient group undergoing different surgical procedures.

Among the groups of patients who underwent different surgical procedures and the group of all patients, the optimal age thresholds were approximately 72, 79, and 81 years, respectively, and the optimal threshold for the comorbidity count was 2.5, as shown in Supplemental Data 4 (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A441).

Mortality prediction

The Youden indices calculated above were used as binary classification nodes to predict patient mortality, including age (approximately 72, 79, and 81 years) and comorbidity count (approximately 2.5). Subpopulations were created by node division, in which the mortality of surgical methods was calculated and compared. Surgical methods with lower mortality rates, as recommended treatments, were identified for each subpopulation, and the estimated mortality was determined by integrating the predicted deaths under the recommended treatment. The node with minimal estimated mortality was selected as the division of the recommended treatment.

Mortality prediction by age and sex

Sex was controlled to increase the accuracy of the deviation, as the expected lifespan differs between sexes, with women typically having a longer expected lifespan than men do. Patients were then divided into sex subgroups to allow for more specific surgical choices, with an optimal age threshold being selected for each subgroup (Table 2).

Mortality Among the Two Surgical Groups According to Age and Sex

The mortality of all patients was calculated as the sum of the predicted deaths in each group, which was calculated by sex and age. When the age cutoff was 80 years, the mortality rate of female patients was the lowest, whereas when the age cutoff was 72 years, the mortality rate of male patients was the lowest. Therefore, for female patients older than 80 years and male patients older than 72 years, HA was preferred over IF, with mortality rates of 5.728% and 5.402%, respectively, whereas for female and male patients younger than 80/72 years, IF rather than HA was suggested, with 0.986% and 1.161% mortality, respectively. The predicted number of deaths was 719, with 25,045 survivors. The real number of deaths was 767, with 24,997 survivors. A chi square test was performed on the predicted number of deaths after surgical selection and survival and the actual number of deaths and survivors to test the effect of surgical selection, revealing that surgical selection determined by the optimal age cannot significantly decrease mortality (P > 0.05).

Mortality prediction by comorbidity count

Table 3 shows the mortality rates of the two surgical participants in the different comorbidity groups. The chi square test was performed on the predicted number of deaths and survivors and the actual number of deaths and survivors to test whether the mortality after the predicted surgery selection was the same as the actual mortality. The mortality rate of patients was the lowest when the threshold comorbidity count was four. Thus, for patients with four or more comorbidities, HA, with a mortality rate of 5.728%, should be selected as the surgical method over IF, which has a mortality rate of 8.021%. For patients with fewer than four comorbidities, IF should be selected as the surgical method over HA, because the mortality rate for IF is 1.472% and that for HA is 2.341%. Together, these results indicate that surgical selection on the basis of the comorbidity count significantly decreases mortality (P < 0.001). The robustness of the recommended treatment was confirmed across different enrollment time groups, sex subgroups, age subgroups, hospital grade subgroups, and anesthesia groups (as shown in Supplemental Data 5, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A442), indicating a high degree of consistency.

The mortality predictions by age and comorbidity count are shown in the Supplemental Data 6 table (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A443). The statistical analysis was performed as described above. There were 81 age binary classification nodes and four comorbidities. Age ≥81 years and ≥4 comorbidities were associated with IF, age ≥81 years and <4 comorbidities were associated with HA, and age <81 years and ≥4 comorbidities were associated with IF. Age ≥81 years and <4 comorbidities were associated with HA. The total mortality rate was 2.497, which was higher than that among patients who underwent surgery only because of comorbidities. The results revealed that surgical selection on the basis of age and comorbidity count significantly decreased mortality (P < 0.001), but mortality was not better than selection on the basis of only the comorbidity count.

Discussion

This study uses a large real-world data set to explore the associations between FNF mortality and age, sex, comorbidities, and treatment decisions, demonstrating the effect of each risk factor and its significance. This study provides robust evidence for the selection of surgical procedures for FNFs, indicating that comorbidities should be a primary consideration in decision making, rather than age and sex alone. The selection of surgical procedures on the basis of comorbidities can effectively reduce mortality rates, and these findings may further promote the development of clinical guidelines. Our analysis compared mortality between IF and HA in patients older than 60 years. We found a notable association between surgical decisions and mortality rates, particularly within the first year after surgery. Notably, making surgical decisions on the basis of comorbidity counts (≥4 for HA, <4 for IF) rather than age and sex (female ≥80 years: HA, female <80 years: IF; male ≥72 years: HA, male <72 years: IF) can markedly lower mortality rates, showing that this type of decision-making results in a mortality rate of 2.379%, compared with 2.790% when surgical choices are stratified on the basis solely of age and sex, representing a 14.6% decrease in mortality.

Hip fractures were positively associated with all-cause mortality. One-year mortality has been reported to range from 7.8% to 56%.4-7 Many influencing factors, including age, sex, anesthesia, BMI, mental health, and choice of surgical treatment, are associated with mortality in older patients,.16-23 In this study, we analyzed whether the choice of surgical treatment affects mortality and how much surgery decision making can reduce mortality. Our data showed that the choice of appropriate surgical treatment for older people was affected considerably by selection bias; patients in the HA group were older, had more comorbidities, and needed more transfusions than patients in the IF group did. This type of data is much better suited for real-world research.

Our study revealed that the main influencing factors were age, comorbidity count, pulmonary disease, sex, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, diabetes, surgical method, and transfusion. Among all these factors, the only crucial factor was the choice of surgical treatment, in which doctors play a major role and are much concerned. These findings support the recommendation for a comprehensive decision on surgical choice on the basis of age, sex, and comorbidity count is to reduce mortality and improve prognosis. The lowest patient mortality rate was in the comorbidity count group, followed by the age and comorbidity group, the age and sex group and, finally, the specific comorbidity group. Finally, for the comorbidity subgroup, patients with heart disease and pulmonary disease are more suitable for HA, whereas patients with stroke are more suitable for IF.

Clinically, IF is less invasive, requires less surgical time, and involves less blood loss than HA does; however, its functional prognosis is not as good as that of HA.48,49 HA is associated with more blood loss, requires a longer surgery time, and carries a greater risk of infection and dislocation.50 Whether IF or HA should be used for treating FNFs has been controversial for 50 years. When these two methods of treatment were compared, the most important outcome was mortality, followed by functional outcome. This study, with its focus on mortality and a large population with a low missing data rate, clearly demonstrated that mortality within 1 year and 2 years after surgery is associated with the choice of surgical treatment. The influencing factors revealed that the choice of surgical treatment was a lower priority; however, this is indeed a crucial choice made by doctors.

While doctors typically consider factors like age, ASA score, functional needs, bone stock, and mental health for surgical decisions, the optimal standard remains unclear. Age alone does not markedly reduce mortality, especially in patients with comorbidities. Our study emphasized surgical recommendations based on comorbidity count. We found that patients with more than four comorbidities had the lowest mortality with HA, whereas those with fewer than four benefitted most from IF. Future RCTs are needed to explore this further and provide higher levels of evidence.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective study from a Beijing district, regional bias may be present. The HA group was older with more comorbidities, leading to selection bias. Older patients present complex factors that RCTs may not fully balance. Data from the Beijing Electronic Health Record Database were limited in variables such as fracture pattern, BMI, ASA score, and mental health. Patients receiving conservative treatment were excluded, potentially affecting overall mortality for patients with FNF. In addition, factors like functional outcomes, revision rates, readmission data, and hip scores were not considered, which could influence surgical treatment decisions. Although fracture pattern is an important factor in general fracture management, previous studies have shown inconsistent results regarding its effect on mortality and surgical choice in the context of femoral neck fractures. Therefore, we decided to center our analysis on comorbidities, which have been demonstrated to have a more notable and consistent influence on outcomes.

Conclusion

This study suggests using the number of comorbidities to guide surgical decisions between HA and IF to reduce 1-year mortality. It recommends IF for patients with fewer than four comorbidities and HA for those with more than four. These findings highlight the importance of considering comorbidity profiles in surgical decisions and provide valuable real-world evidence for the older Chinese population, indicating a need for guideline updates.
