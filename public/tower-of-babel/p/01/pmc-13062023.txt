Research on lung nodule detection in X-ray plain films based on improved YOLOv12 model
Authors: Minghui Mao, Chengkun Hong, Yuhang Zhang, Hao Huang, Jianfeng Chu, Liyuan Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve automatic lung nodule detection in chest X-ray images, this study proposes an improved YOLOv12-based detection framework by integrating space-to-depth convolution (SPDConv), a dynamic upsampling module (DySample), and a lightweight feature aggregation module (VoVGSCSP). SPDConv enhances spatial information preservation during feature extraction, DySample replaces conventional upsampling to improve multi-scale feature fusion, and VoVGSCSP strengthens feature representation while reducing computational redundancy. The optimized YOLOv12-DSV model was trained and evaluated using a publicly available chest X-ray dataset with annotated lung nodules from the Roboflow platform, with performance assessed through five-fold cross-validation and external testing. Experimental results show that the proposed model achieved an mAP50 of 0.735 and an mAP50-95 of 0.426, outperforming the original YOLOv12 model (mAP50: 0.704; mAP50-95: 0.411). In addition, the proposed model reduced the number of parameters from 2.52 to 2.21 M, decreased FLOPs from 6.0 to 5.2 G, and increased inference speed from 97.6 to 107.8 FPS. These results indicate that the proposed YOLOv12-DSV model improves detection accuracy while reducing computational cost, achieving a more favorable balance between detection performance and model complexity for lung nodule localization in chest X-ray images.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and is associated with a high mortality rate. Its incidence increases progressively with age, posing a significant global health burden1,2. As an important radiographic manifestation of early-stage lung cancer, the early detection of lung nodules plays a critical role in improving patient survival and treatment outcomes3. In clinical practice, lung nodule identification in chest X-ray images is traditionally performed through manual inspection by radiologists4–6. However, with the rapidly increasing number of medical imaging examinations, radiologists are facing growing workloads, which may lead to fatigue and potentially increase the risk of missed or incorrect diagnoses7–9. Furthermore, lung nodules in chest X-ray images typically present with small sizes, diverse morphologies, and low contrast with surrounding anatomical structures such as ribs and blood vessels. These characteristics significantly increase the difficulty of accurate detection10,11.

In recent years, deep learning techniques have achieved remarkable progress in medical image analysis and have been widely applied to various diagnostic tasks12–17. The application of deep learning–based computer-aided detection systems for automatic lung nodule identification in chest X-rays has therefore attracted increasing attention. Several studies have demonstrated promising performance using deep learning methods for lung nodule detection18. For example, Horry et al.19 proposed a deep convolutional neural network (DCNN)-based detection framework for chest X-ray analysis, which improved localization performance through an end-to-end architecture and achieved strong generalization ability. Similarly, Lim et al.20 developed a deep-learning-based automatic detection algorithm (DLAD) capable of both nodule detection and volume estimation. Their approach combined convolutional neural networks with regression analysis to predict nodule volume and achieved reliable quantitative results on a dataset containing 147 X-rays and 208 nodules.

In the field of object detection, two-stage algorithms based on region proposals, such as Faster R-CNN, and one-stage end-to-end algorithms represented by the You Only Look Once (YOLO) series constitute the two dominant detection paradigms. Two-stage detectors first generate candidate regions using a region proposal network and subsequently perform classification and localization on these regions21–23. In contrast, one-stage detectors directly perform classification and bounding box regression on feature maps, enabling a more integrated detection process with significantly higher inference speed. While two-stage methods often achieve higher accuracy, one-stage approaches are more suitable for real-time applications due to their computational efficiency.

Among one-stage detectors, the YOLO series has undergone continuous architectural evolution, leading to progressive improvements in detection accuracy, efficiency, and model flexibility. Earlier work by Chiu et al.24 employed YOLOv4 for lung nodule detection in chest X-ray images, achieving a sensitivity of 84.4% on the external JSRT dataset. Nevertheless, the relatively older YOLOv4 architecture lacks optimization for increasingly complex detection scenarios and may exhibit limited adaptability to diverse imaging conditions.

Following YOLOv4, several important versions of the YOLO framework have introduced key architectural innovations. YOLOv5 adopted a more modular design and improved training strategies, enhancing training stability and deployment efficiency. YOLOv8 further refined the backbone and detection head while introducing anchor-free detection mechanisms, improving both detection accuracy and inference speed. More recent versions such as YOLOv10 and YOLOv11 focused on improving feature extraction efficiency and reducing computational overhead through architectural optimization and lightweight design strategies. These successive improvements have significantly enhanced the capability of YOLO-based detectors in complex visual recognition tasks. Meanwhile, other representative detection frameworks have also contributed important ideas to object detection research. For example, SSD demonstrated the effectiveness of efficient one-stage dense prediction, while RT-DETR-L introduced a transformer-based detection paradigm with strong end-to-end modeling capability. These frameworks provide useful reference baselines for broader comparative evaluation.

Building upon these developments, YOLOv12 represents a newer iteration in the YOLO series that achieves a more balanced trade-off between detection accuracy and computational efficiency. Despite these advancements, challenges remain when applying YOLOv12 to lung nodule detection in chest X-ray images. Lung nodules often exhibit substantial morphological variability and low contrast with surrounding tissues, while small nodules may be easily obscured by ribs, blood vessels, or imaging noise. In addition, variations in imaging quality caused by different acquisition conditions and patient positioning may further complicate detection. These factors may limit the effectiveness of standard YOLOv12 architecture in capturing subtle features of small nodules.

To address these challenges, this study proposes an improved YOLOv12-based detection framework by integrating three complementary modules: space-to-depth convolution (SPDConv), a dynamic upsampling module (DySample), and a lightweight feature aggregation module (VoVGSCSP). Specifically, SPDConv is introduced into the Backbone and Neck to improve spatial information preservation during downsampling; DySample replaces the original upsampling modules to enable content-aware feature reconstruction; and VoVGSCSP substitutes the C3k2 and A2C2f. modules to enhance feature aggregation while reducing computational redundancy. Through the coordinated integration of these modules, the proposed YOLOv12-DSV model aims to improve the detection capability of lung nodules in chest X-ray images while reducing model complexity and computational cost. By enhancing feature representation and improving computational efficiency, the proposed framework seeks to achieve a more favorable balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency for lung nodule detection.

Materials and methods

Original YOLOv12 model

As a recent iteration of the YOLO series, the YOLOv12 model retains the end-to-end direct prediction paradigm. It achieves simultaneous object classification and bounding box localization for all targets within an image through a single forward pass25–27. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the overall architecture of the algorithm adheres to the classic three-stage framework, comprising a Backbone, a Neck, and a Head28. Specifically, the Backbone is utilized for feature extraction29. By stacking standard convolutions and pooling layers for progressive downsampling, it generates feature maps rich in semantic information. The Neck serves as a hub for feature fusion30, producing composite representations with enhanced discriminative power. The YOLOv12 model inherits the C3k2 module from YOLOv1131, which facilitates dynamic structural switching via a Boolean parameter, C3k. When C3k is set to False, the C3k2 module functions as a C2f. structure (a standard bottleneck layer). Conversely, when C3k is set to True, a more complex C3 module structure is activated to enhance feature fusion capabilities.

However, YOLOv12 primarily relies on standard convolutions for feature extraction. This architectural choice limits the receptive field, resulting in insufficient capture of long-range contextual information and restricted detection performance for small targets. In addition, the original upsampling operations in the Neck of YOLOv12 rely on fixed interpolation rules. These methods struggle to dynamically optimize based on actual image content, often leading to blurred edge information or the loss of fine-grained features in feature maps. Furthermore, while the C3k2 and A2C2f. modules in the Neck improve accuracy, they introduce high computational and memory overheads, thereby raising the hardware requirements for deployment. Consequently, there remains room for improvement in the comprehensive performance of the YOLOv12 model.

Algorithm improvements

SPDConv module

The SPDConv module is based on the space-to-depth reorganization mechanism32, which rearranges spatial information into the channel dimension to perform downsampling while preserving detailed features33. Unlike standard convolution-based downsampling, this operation reduces spatial resolution while maintaining more structural information from the original feature map, thereby alleviating the loss of fine-grained details.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the input feature map X1 is divided into multiple spatial regions and then concatenated along the channel dimension to generate the reorganized feature map X2. DySample module

DySample is an efficient dynamic upsampling method whose core innovation lies in the adoption of a dynamic point sampling mechanism to reconstruct high-resolution feature maps34–36. Due to its simple structure and clear logic, the method can be easily integrated into deep learning frameworks. Traditional upsampling processes lack content-adaptive capabilities37. Consequently, when processing low-contrast features in chest X-rays, they are prone to causing boundary blurring and the loss of detailed information. To address this issue, DySample employs a point resampling strategy. In the detection of morphologically diverse lung nodule features on chest X-rays, this module can adaptively upscale the input feature maps while preserving sufficient detailed information. Simultaneously, DySample reduces computational burden and latency by avoiding time-consuming dynamic convolutions and the need for additional sub-networks to generate dynamic kernels. By incorporating DySample, the algorithm is expected to capture the detailed boundary features of lung nodules more precisely.

VoVGSCSP module

Unlike the traditional C3k2 and A2C2f. modules, the VoVGSCSP module integrates the gradient optimization capability of the cross-stage partial (CSP) network, the one-shot aggregation (OSA) feature fusion mechanism from VoVNet, and the lightweight characteristics of Ghost Convolution. This design forms a feature enhancement structure that balances feature representation and computational efficiency38–40.

First, the module employs 1 × 1 convolutions to preserve spatial information in the original feature maps. First, replacing specific standard convolutions (Conv) in the Backbone and Neck with SPDConv is of significant value for enhancing the small object detection capability of the YOLOv12 model. Notably, since the weights of the first standard Conv in the Backbone have a critical impact on algorithm convergence, and its uniform weight distribution is more conducive to the stable propagation of gradients, this study retained the first standard Conv of the Backbone during the improvement process.

Second, all Upsample modules in the Neck were replaced with upgraded DySample modules. This implements a dynamic, content-aware upsampling process capable of generating clearer feature maps rich in semantic information, which is particularly beneficial for edge and detail recovery. Through content-aware sampling points, multi-scale features are fused more intelligently, thereby enhancing the feature extraction capability of the YOLOv12 model for lung nodules in chest X-rays.

Finally, the C3k2 and A2C2f. modules in the Neck were replaced with lightweight VoVGSCSP modules. This optimized the channel compression ratio of feature maps and the lightweight design of activation functions. It avoided the issues of feature redundancy and surging memory usage caused by the complex stacking of traditional C3k2 and A2C2f. modules. This approach not only maintained the capture precision of fine-grained lung nodule features but also made the overall algorithm more lightweight, effectively reducing video memory requirements and computational barriers during hardware deployment, thereby further balancing detection performance and operational efficiency.

The comprehensive improvements of the YOLOv12-DSV model not only address the limitations of the original YOLOv12 architecture but also construct a jointly optimized feature extraction system through the complementary design of the three modules. These modules were deliberately combined to address key challenges in lung nodule detection on chest X-rays, including small target representation, preservation of subtle structural details, and efficient feature aggregation. This task-oriented design provides a more reliable algorithmic foundation for accurate lung nodule detection compared with the original YOLOv12 model.

Datasets

In this study, chest X-ray images containing lung nodules were sourced from two public detection datasets available on the Roboflow platform. The first dataset, designated as Dataset 1, contains a total of 3,600 chest X-ray images of lung nodules. It was used for model training and internal evaluation. The second dataset, named Dataset 2, comprises 1,000 chest X-ray images of lung nodules. This dataset was employed for external testing to evaluate the performance of both the original and improved algorithms.

These datasets consist of chest X-ray images collected from publicly available sources and include images acquired under different imaging conditions. The images exhibit variability in radiographic characteristics and image quality. All chest X-ray images in the dataset contain annotated lung nodules, as the datasets were specifically constructed for lung nodule detection tasks. Some radiographs contain a single nodule, while others contain multiple nodules. The annotated nodules vary in size and spatial location within the lung fields.

All chest X-ray images were annotated using the LabelImg software. The annotation process was performed independently by two senior radiologists with more than eight years of experience in thoracic imaging. The radiologists manually delineated bounding boxes for lung nodules according to standardized radiological diagnostic criteria. After the initial annotation stage, all labeled images were reviewed by a third senior radiologist with more than ten years of clinical experience. In cases where discrepancies occurred between the two primary annotators, the images were jointly re-examined and discussed until a consensus annotation was reached. This multi-stage quality control procedure helped ensure the accuracy and consistency of the training labels used in this study.

Furthermore, to evaluate the robustness and generalization capability of the algorithm, five-fold cross-validation was performed during model development. Specifically, 3,000 images were randomly selected from Dataset 1 and partitioned into five mutually exclusive folds. In each run, four folds were used for training and the remaining fold was used for validation, resulting in an approximate training-to-validation ratio of 8:2. This procedure was repeated five times, and the results were reported as the mean ± standard deviation across the five folds. The remaining 600 images from Dataset 1 were reserved as an internal test set for performance evaluation. Subsequently, Dataset 2 was used as an external test set to further assess the generalization ability of the model. During data partitioning, care was taken to avoid obvious sampling bias across the different subsets. In addition, images with poor quality or severe artifacts were excluded through manual screening to ensure reliable model training and evaluation.

Computational environment and training settings

All experiments in this study were conducted on a workstation running the Ubuntu 20.04 operating system. The experimental environment was configured with Python 3.10, and the YOLOv12 architecture was implemented using the PyTorch 2.2.2 deep learning framework. CUDA 11.8 was employed to enable GPU acceleration for efficient model training.

During training, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) was used as the optimizer with a momentum of 0.937 and a weight decay of 0.0005. The batch size was set to 16, and the initial learning rate was set to 0.01. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduling strategy was adopted to gradually reduce the learning rate during training. The model was trained for a total of 100 epochs.

For data preprocessing, all chest X-ray images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels to match the input size requirements of the YOLO architecture. To improve the generalization ability of the model, several data augmentation techniques were applied during training, including random horizontal flipping, random scaling, and Mosaic data augmentation.

Within the object detection framework, anchor boxes were automatically generated using the k-means clustering method provided by the YOLO framework based on the training dataset, enabling better adaptation to the scale distribution of lung nodules. Model parameters were initialized using YOLOv12 weights pretrained on the COCO dataset, which helps accelerate model convergence and improve detection performance.

During model evaluation, an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 was used to determine the correspondence between predicted bounding boxes and ground-truth annotations.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the overall performance of different object detection algorithms, this study established a multi-angle evaluation metric system. This system incorporates key metrics such as precision (Pre), recall (Rec), Intersection over union (IoU), mean Average precision (mAP), F1 score, parameters (Params), floating point operations (FLOPs), and frames per second (FPS).

Regarding the evaluation of mAP, this study adopted two different IoU judgment standards. The first employs a fixed IoU threshold of 0.5, denoted as mAP50. The second introduces a dynamic IoU threshold mechanism, where the threshold is incrementally adjusted from 0.5 to 0.95 with a step size of 0.05, denoted as mAP50-95. This allows for a more comprehensive assessment of algorithm performance under various levels of matching strictness.

Regarding the matching criteria between predicted boxes and ground truth boxes, this study followed the classic matching strategy based on IoU thresholds. Specifically, the system calculates the IoU value between each predicted box and the corresponding ground truth box and compares this value with a preset IoU threshold. If the IoU value reaches or exceeds the set threshold, the detection is classified as correct; otherwise, it is considered a false detection.

The aforementioned evaluation system not only enables accurate and reliable quantitative analysis of the algorithm’s basic detection performance but also facilitates a deep exploration of the algorithm’s robustness and adaptability under different matching conditions through the use of multi-level IoU thresholds. Furthermore, Params reflects the total number of trainable parameters in the network, serving as a crucial metric for measuring algorithm complexity and video memory requirements. FLOPs is used to assess the computational overhead of the algorithm during inference, while FPS intuitively reflects the real-time inference speed of the algorithm in practical applications.

Results and analysis

Ablation studies

To evaluate the individual and combined contributions of the proposed modules, a series of ablation experiments were conducted. Further improvements were observed when combining two modules. In addition to the accuracy improvements, the model also exhibited favorable computational characteristics. As shown in Table 1, the integration of the three modules reduced both the number of parameters and FLOPs while maintaining a higher inference speed (FPS). These results indicate that the proposed modules not only improve detection performance but also reduce model parameters and computational complexity while maintaining efficient inference speed. Discussion

Detecting lung nodules in chest X-ray images remains challenging because nodules are often small, low in contrast, and easily obscured by overlapping anatomical structures such as ribs and blood vessels. To address these difficulties, this study developed an optimized YOLOv12-based detection framework by integrating three complementary modules: SPDConv, DySample, and VoVGSCSP. Through their coordinated design, the proposed YOLOv12-DSV model enhances the representation of subtle nodules while improving computational efficiency.

The three modules exhibit a tightly coupled synergistic effect, forming a progressive and complementary feature-processing pipeline. SPDConv plays the foundational role of information fidelity. Applied in the early stages of the backbone, it preserves fine-grained pixel-level information of small targets and provides detail-rich inputs for subsequent feature extraction, thereby alleviating early feature loss. DySample then serves as the key component for intelligent enhancement during multi-scale feature fusion. Instead of simple interpolation, it performs content-aware dynamic upsampling to selectively strengthen important regions such as edges and textures, enabling nodule contours and internal structures that might otherwise be blurred or overlooked to be more clearly restored in higher-level feature maps. VoVGSCSP finally achieves efficient distillation in the high-level network structure. Built upon the high-quality features prepared by SPDConv and DySample, it employs a lightweight cross-stage partial connection design and optimized channel interaction mechanism to extract more discriminative fine-grained features while reducing computational redundancy. At the same time, its lightweight structure helps preserve deployment feasibility by preventing excessive memory and computational overhead. The synergy of these three components forms a full-chain optimization from information fidelity to intelligent enhancement and then to efficient distillation, which cannot be achieved by any single module alone and constitutes a key reason for the effectiveness of the proposed method.

The experimental results support the effectiveness of this design. Compared with the baseline YOLOv12 model, YOLOv12-DSV achieved consistent improvements in mAP50, mAP50-95, Precision, Recall, and F1, while also reducing Params and FLOPs and increasing FPS. In addition, comparison with SSD and RT-DETR-L showed that the proposed model maintains a favorable balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency in a broader comparison setting, rather than only within the YOLO family. These results suggest that the proposed architecture is well suited for chest radiograph-based lung nodule detection, where both accuracy and efficiency are important.

Recent studies indicate that YOLO-based architectures for medical image detection continue to evolve. Lightweight YOLO designs have been used to improve the efficiency of X-ray image analysis, as exemplified by G-YOLOv11 for pediatric wrist fracture detection41. Meanwhile, quadratic convolution-based YOLO variants have been proposed to enhance feature extraction and lesion localization in medical imaging, and have shown potential in intracranial hemorrhage localization from head CT images42,43. Earlier YOLOv3-based head CT localization studies further demonstrated the feasibility of extending the YOLO framework to clinically relevant lesion detection tasks44. Overall, these studies suggest that lightweight design, convolutional refinement, and task-oriented architectural optimization remain effective strategies for improving the performance and efficiency of medical image detectors. In this context, the proposed YOLOv12-DSV framework extends this direction to lung nodule detection on chest X-ray images, while jointly emphasizing subtle feature preservation and computational efficiency.

The error analysis further helps explain the remaining limitations of the proposed model. False-positive detections mainly occurred in anatomically complex regions, such as areas with rib overlap, vascular shadows, or dense hilar/parahilar structures, whereas false-negative detections were more frequently associated with small nodules, low-contrast lesions, and nodules located near challenging anatomical backgrounds such as the heart border or diaphragmatic region. These findings indicate that lesion conspicuity, anatomical overlap, and structural complexity remain major factors affecting automatic lung nodule detection on chest X-ray images.

Nevertheless, the current study still has several limitations, and practical application of the proposed method will require further investigation. First, although the datasets used in this study were carefully annotated by experienced radiologists, the overall sample size, particularly the external test set, may still be insufficient to fully represent the diversity of lung nodule appearances encountered in clinical practice. In addition, publicly available datasets often lack detailed metadata regarding patient demographics, imaging devices, acquisition protocols, and pathological subtypes, which may limit generalization in real-world environments. More importantly, the current dataset does not provide explicit subgroup annotations for nodule size, anatomical location, or lesion contrast, which limits the possibility of performing a fully quantitative stratified analysis of failure cases.

Second, the datasets used in this study mainly contained chest X-ray images with annotated lung nodules. As a result, the present study focuses on lesion-level localization and detection rather than normal-abnormal image classification. Although this setting enables the model to concentrate on improving nodule detection, the lack of negative chest X-ray images may limit its generalizability in large-scale screening settings. Previous studies have shown that deep learning-based chest radiograph algorithms can achieve effective performance in more clinically realistic settings that include both normal and abnormal images45, suggesting the value of incorporating more diverse datasets in future work.

Finally, the real-world deployment capability of the proposed model has not yet been fully investigated. Although the improved architecture reduces Params and FLOPs, its inference efficiency on low-power hardware or mobile devices has not been evaluated.

In future work, several additional directions may be explored to further improve the proposed framework. Image preprocessing and region-of-interest extraction strategies, such as lung region segmentation, patch-based training, or image enhancement, may help reduce background interference and improve the visibility of subtle nodules46. In addition, transfer learning from large-scale natural image datasets or related medical imaging tasks may further improve convergence efficiency and generalization ability. Future studies should also include more diverse datasets and further examine model performance on resource-limited platforms, while exploring model compression strategies such as pruning and quantization to facilitate practical clinical deployment.

Conclusion

This study proposed YOLOv12-DSV, an improved YOLOv12-based model for lung nodule detection on chest X-ray images. By integrating SPDConv, DySample, and VoVGSCSP, the proposed model enhanced subtle feature representation while reducing computational cost. Experimental results showed that YOLOv12-DSV outperformed the baseline YOLOv12 in mAP50, mAP50-95, Precision, Recall, and F1, and also showed favorable performance compared with other YOLO variants and representative non-YOLO detectors. At the same time, the proposed model reduced model size and computational cost while increasing inference speed. Overall, YOLOv12-DSV provides an effective and efficient framework for lung nodule localization on chest X-ray images. Further validation on larger and more diverse datasets is still required before clinical application.
