In Vitro, In Vivo, and Clinical Trial Approaches to Investigate the Effects of Medicinal Plants on Burn Wound Healing: A Systematic Review
Authors: Mahdi Bagheri, Seyed Mohammad Zarei, Mostafa Soodmand, Hossein Mardani Nafchi, Mir Hossein Hashemi Poor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUse of synthetic drugs for the treatment of burn wounds has decreased due to adverse effects, and they are gradually being replaced by compounds obtained from natural products, especially medicinal plants. This review focused on pre‐clinical and clinical studies examining the efficacy of medicinal plants in promoting burn wound healing. This systematic review was performed congruent with the guidance delineated in the PRISMA statement. Databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Embase and Web of Science, along with the search engines Google Scholar and Google, were utilised to retrieve published literatures from 2000 to 2023. Articles were screened according to the determined criteria and appraised using primary evaluation checklists. A total of 108 published articles met the eligibility criteria and were selected for discussion. Of these, 72 were in vivo studies, 28 were in vitro studies and 8 were clinical trials. Various medicinal plants discussed in this review improve the burn wound healing process due to the presence of diverse biological compounds with antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory and antibacterial properties. These plants affect the four phases of wound healing. These plant species and their compounds could be potentially used for the development of effective natural remedies against burn wounds.Keywords: biological compound, burn, clinical trial, in vitro, in vivo, medicinal plant, wound healing

Introduction

A cutaneous wound is a disruption in the normal anatomic structure and function of the skin epithelium caused by physical, chemical, thermal, immunological and microbial factors [1]. Burns are one of the most common and destructive forms of wounds worldwide, resulting in substantial healthcare costs [2]. Burn wound severity is categorised into three degrees: first (superficial), second (partial thickness) and third (full thickness), based on the depth of skin damage [2, 3]. In superficial burns, only the epidermal layer of the skin is damaged. In partial‐thickness burns the dermal layer is also affected, while in full‐thickness burns, the damage extends to the subcutaneous fat and muscle layers, in addition to the epidermis and dermis [4].

Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) show that approximately 180 000 deaths are caused by burn wounds especially in low‐ and moderate‐income countries. According to epidemiological research, Iran experiences approximately 100 000 burn injuries annually, with around 14 000 deaths resulting from burn‐related complications [5].

Wound healing is an intricate phenomenon that involves the restoration of anatomical structure and physiological functions in the skin tissue. The wound healing process consists of four greatly integrated and consecutively overlapping phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and tissue remodelling. In healthy skin, the wound healing process begins with vascular contraction and blood coagulation in the wound area. Following this process, the influx of WBCs occurs through chemotaxis, driven by cytokines released as a result of coagulation cascade activation. These inflammatory cells contribute to the decontamination of the wound area by removing various cellular debris and through the phagocytosis of bacteria. A variety of cells migrate into the wound area during the proliferative phase. The interplay between keratinocytes and fibroblasts is critical for this process. Fibroblasts are activated by cytokines secreted by keratinocytes, leading them to migrate to the wound site. After dismantling the fibrin clot, fibroblasts begin producing extracellular matrix (ECM) components, which contribute to wound contraction. During the remodelling phase, the ECM architecture and vascular density of the wound are restored to resemble that of normal tissue [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

If severe burn wounds are not treated promptly, it can result in a delayed wound healing process and may even lead to complications such as hypovolemic shock and wound infection. In order to heal the burn wound, diverse topical preparations such as silver nitrate, mafenide acetate, chlorhexidine and silver sulfadiazine (SSD) are present in the market. While these creams primarily possess antimicrobial activity, they exhibit limited wound‐healing effects. Furthermore, they may cause complications such as multi‐drug resistance, skin irritation, dryness, argyria, leucopenia, methaemoglobinaemia, hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [12, 13, 14, 15, 16].

Although synthetic drugs have therapeutic effects, their use has declined due to their side effects. Conversely, the use of medicines derived from natural products, particularly medicinal plants, has gained prominence as they mitigate side effects due to the presence of various bioactive compounds. Medicinal plants have been used as poultices and ointments to heal various wounds since ancient times. These plants aid in burn wound healing due to their anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, angiogenic and cell proliferative properties, which are attributed to the presence of compounds such as alkaloids, essential oils, terpenoids, saponins, flavonoids, tannins and other phenolic compounds [17, 18]. Natural origin drugs also offer advantages, including relatively low cost, easy accessibility and shorter healing time [13, 14, 19]. Utilising natural products as a reservoir for novel therapeutic agents could open new avenues for treating cutaneous wounds, especially burn wounds. However, despite extensive research into natural product‐based drugs for wound healing over the past three decades, no significant natural remedies have been commercialised.

In this review, an attempt has been made to mention the great and still predominantly untapped potential of medicinal plants to provide new drugs for burn wound healing. This systematic review aimed to discuss the in vitro/in vivo wound healing efficacy of various medicinal plants, as well as to identify the clinical trial studies that described the use of medicinal plants for the treatment of burn wounds in humans.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This systematic review was designed and performed in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement [20]. The PRISMA checklist is provided in the Table S1. Additionally, the review adheres to the population, intervention, comparator and outcome (PICO) framework. The PICO elements of this review are as follows:

Study subjects included cell lines, laboratory animals and patients with burn wounds. Medicinal plants (whole plants or their components) used in the treatment groups were considered interventions. Negative controls (placebo‐treated or untreatment), positive controls (treatment with standard drugs) and sham groups (treatment with vehicles) were considered comparators. The primary outcomes were the effects of medicinal plants on wound contraction, healing time, histopathological results, cell viability and cell migration rate.

Search Strategy

The databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Embase and Web of Science were used to retrieve articles published from 1 January 2000 to 13 December 2023. Additional data missed in the initial electronic search were identified using Google Scholar and Google search engine. This review included only articles published in English. The search strategy aimed to retrieve published literature related to natural products, medicinal plants and bioactive compounds with burn wound healing activity. The search targeted the ‘title’ and ‘abstract’ fields in the structured search of the information sources.

Based on the search strategy, various relevant terms related to the topic were used and combined using Boolean operators (OR/AND) (Table 1).

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

Publications that described the use of medicinal plants for burn wound healing in humans, as well as studies reporting in vitro wound healing activity and in vivo burn wound healing efficacy of these plants, were included in this review. Exclusion criteria encompassed review studies, research letters, reports, studies related to other wounds, studies concerning other diseases, studies focusing on preparations combining natural products and synthetic chemicals and other irrelevant studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria.

Study Selection

After retrieving articles from electronic databases, all records were transferred to EPPI‐Reviewer Web (Beta), web‐based software. Then, the titles and abstracts were independently assessed by two investigators (M.H.H.P. and M.S.) for eligibility criteria, following which the relevant articles were selected. Next, the investigators screened the full texts of the selected articles based on the inclusion criteria. In case of discrepancies among the investigators, a third researcher (H.M.N., S.M.Z. or M.B.) was invited to discuss the articles and reach a consensus. To standardise the synonyms, and families of plant botanical names, two online resources, ‘Plant list’ (http://www.theplantlist.org/) and ‘Tropicos’ (https://www.tropicos.org/) were referenced.

Quality Assessment

Quality assessment of animal intervention studies was performed using the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation Risk of Bias (SYRCLE's RoB) tool. For each parameter in this tool, the risk of bias was classified as low, high or unclear. A ‘yes’ judgement indicated a low risk of bias, while a ‘no’ judgement signified a high risk of bias. The judgement was marked as ‘unclear’ if insufficient details were reported to determine the risk of bias. Total scores were not calculated using the SYRCLE's RoB tool to avoid inappropriate weighting of each individual study and because these weights might vary for each outcome and review [21].

The critical appraisal process for cell culture studies was carried out using criteria outlined in the WCRF/UoB framework. Each study was evaluated based on a set of criteria, with a score of ‘1’ assigned when a criterion was fulfilled and ‘0’ when it was not fulfilled or not reported. The total scores ranged from 0 to 6. Based on this score, studies were categorised as having low (0–2), moderate (3–4) or high quality (5–6) [22, 23].

The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist was employed to assess the quality of the included randomised controlled trials (RCTs). This tool consists of 12 criteria for each evaluated study, with the number of questions answered ‘yes’ indicating the score out of 12 [24]. The quality assessment of included articles was independently conducted by two reviewers (M.H.H.P. and M.S.), and disagreements was resolved by consulting a third investigator (H.M.N., S.M.Z. or M.B.).

Data Extraction

Two researchers (M.H.H.P. and M.S.), with the support of two specialists (S.M.Z. and M.B.), extracted and compiled the main findings of the included publications using standardised data abstraction forms. The following information was abstracted: study type, botanical name of the plant, plant family, life form of plant, plant part used, major phytochemical compound(s), source of bioactive compound(s), extraction technique, solvent for extraction, formulation, administration method and time, dose and length of treatment, standard drug, burn wound degree, animal model, cell line name, culture conditions, population size, age, gender, outcome(s), p‐value, country, year of publication and number of citations.

Results

Through the literature search, a significant number of relevant studies were identified. After removing duplicates, applying inclusion and exclusion criteria and conducting quality assessments, 108 studies were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Studies without authentic methods and results were excluded during the quality assessment process. The quality assessment details are provided in the Tables S2, S3 and S5.

Main databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Scopus were searched using keywords and subject headings related to the topic. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 114 were included in the qualitative synthesis from a total of 4884. However, six studies were deemed to have insufficient quality upon quality assessment.

In Vivo Wound Healing Activity Studies

We identified a total of 86 plant species belonging to 46 families with burn wound healing activity. These families were Arecaceae, Amaryllidaceae, Agaricaceae, Apocynaceae, Basellaceae, Cannabaceae, Campanulaceae, Dilleniaceae, Globulariaceae, Gentianaceae, Iridaceae, Juglandaceae, Leguminosae, Moringaceae, Tamaricaceae, Pedaliaceae, Pinaceae, Phyllanthaceae, Ranunculaceae, Rubiaceae, Saxifragaceae, Urticaceae, Verbenaceae, Zingiberaceae, Zygophyllaceae (n = 1, 1.07% each), Apiaceae, Elaeagnaceae, Hypericaceae, Liliaceae, Linaceae, Myrtaceae, Meliaceae, Malvaceae, Plantaginaceae, Solanaceae, Scrophulariaceae (n = 2, 2.13% each), Amaranthaceae, Boraginaceae, Oleaceae, Rosaceae (n = 3, 3.18% each), Anacardiaceae, Fabaceae, Lythraceae (n = 4, 4.24%), Cucurbitaceae (n = 6, 6.36%), Asteraceae (n = 8, 8.48%) and Lamiaceae (n = 9, 9.54%).

Most of the investigated plants were verified by botanists and assigned a voucher number. The majority of the studies on the wound healing activity of medicinal plants in animal models were conducted in Iran (n = 23, 31.94%), India and Indonesia (n = 7, 9.72% each). Five studies each were conducted in Tunisia and Morocco, four studies each in China and Turkey, and two studies each in Ethiopia and Romania. One study each was reported from Brazil, Trinidad, UAE, Thailand, Vietnam, Peru, Algeria, Japan, Jordan, Togo, Mongolia, Pakistan and the United States.

The most frequently used plant families in Iran were Asteraceae and Boraginaceae. The majority of medicinal plants used were herbs (71.25%), followed by trees (22.5%), shrubs (5%) and mushrooms (1.25%). Leaves were the most commonly used plant part (38.96%), followed by seeds (15.58%) and aerial parts (12.98%). Other parts included flowers, fruits, roots, stems, spores and resin oils. The most commonly used animal model was the rat (86.66%). All medicinal plants were subjected to extraction processes using various parts, and their crude extracts (75.62%) and oil extracts (19.51%) were utilised.

Crude extracts were primarily obtained using the Soxhlet apparatus (27.41%), followed by solvent extraction (e.g., maceration technique) (41.92%). The solvents predominantly used in the extraction process were ethanol (64.51%) and methanol (22.58%). The fixed oil extraction process was mostly performed through mechanical pressing and fire‐heating. The preferred formulation of herbal extracts was ointment (33.82%), while gel (22.05%) was the second most frequently used herbal formulation. While most formulations were made from a single plant species, there were only 10 polyherbal formulations utilised. Nearly all herbal formulations were administered topically.

In general, all selected studies used a positive control, which was primarily SSD. The selected studies commonly employed ANOVA, Student's t‐test and various non‐parametric tests to analyse experimental data. They also considered a 95% confidence interval and p < 0.05 as the statistically significant level. Parameters such as wound closure rate (WCR), collagen deposition, angiogenesis and inflammation were investigated in the selected studies. The main outcomes are presented in the Table S6.

In Vitro Wound Healing Activity Studies

Among the mentioned plant species, the wound healing activity of 34 plant species was also reported in vitro. Plant species belonging to the Asteraceae (n = 10, 31.42%), Lamiaceae and Fabaceae (n = 4, 11.42% each) families were predominantly used for in vitro wound healing activity studies. These in vitro studies were primarily conducted in Italy (17.14%), Brazil (14.28%), Iran, Thailand, Turkey (11.42% each), Malaysia and Romania (5.71% each). Additionally, China, Indonesia, India, Taiwan (China), UK, Germany, Sweden and Hungary each reported only one in vitro study. The majority of the plants under investigation were authenticated by botanists and given a unique voucher number for identification. Among plant life forms, herbs (63.88%) were dominant, while leaves (50%) and aerial parts (30.55%) were the most commonly used plant parts. Ethanol (43.18%) and water (34.09%) were the primary extraction solvents, and solvent extraction method (e.g., maceration method) (27.27%) and soxhlet apparatus (11.36%) were the preferred extraction techniques in in vitro studies.

Fibroblasts (60.41%) and keratinocytes (14.58%) were the primary cell types used to investigate the wound healing activity of plants. With one exception, all in vitro studies utilised a single plant as the treatment; the remaining study involved cells treated with a combination of two plants. The included in vitro studies commonly employed ANOVA and Student's t‐test to analyse experimental data, and considered a 95% confidence interval and p < 0.05 as the statistically significant level. Parameters such as cell proliferation, cell migration, cell viability, antioxidant activity, antibacterial activity and collagen content were investigated in the selected studies. The main outcomes are presented in the Table S7.

Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs)

This review evaluated a total of 11 plant species, each belonging to 11 different families, with documented burn wound healing activity in humans. These families were Apiaceae, Asphodelaceae, Asteraceae, Boraginaceae, Fabaceae, Lamiaceae, Linaceae, Malvaceae, Oleaceae, Rosaceae and Solanaceae. All studies were conducted in Iran. Most of the investigated plants were authenticated by botanists and had voucher numbers. The majority of medicinal plants used were herbs, with the exception of two trees. Various plant parts, including leaves (n = 4), flowers, petals, bark, seeds, roots and aerial parts, were utilised in these studies. Among the investigated plants, three were used for oil extraction, while six were used for preparing crude extracts.

Oil extraction was performed using either heating or a household oil press machine. Crude extracts were obtained through solvent extraction (e.g., maceration) (n = 4) and decoction (n = 2) methods. The solvents used for preparing the extracts included ethanol (n = 3), water (n = 2) and sesame oil (n = 1). Various formulations were employed for medicinal plants, such as ointments (n = 3), gels (n = 2), oils (n = 2), capsule (n = 1) and cream (n = 1). Notably, one of the ointments was prepared using three different medicinal plants. All herbal formulations were administered topically, with the exception of one oral administration. Nitrofurazone and SSD were used as standard drugs in the selected studies. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant across all studies. Parameters such as WCR, healing time, epithelialisation and pain severity were also investigated. The main outcomes are presented in the Table S8.

Discussion

Medicinal plants are one of the essential sources of therapeutic agents for modern herbal and natural drug synthesis, providing a direct supply of bioactive compounds used in the development of chemical drugs. In recent decades, there has been increasing interest in using medicinal plants or their combinations to treat and manage various wounds, particularly due to their potentially lower side effects and shorter healing times [25].

Chromatographic analysis of the mentioned medicinal plants revealed the presence of various phytochemicals in different plant parts. Among these phytochemicals, asiatic acid and asiaticosides in Centella asiatica [26], celosian in Celosia argentea [27], isopinocarveol, (−)‐myrtenol and (−)‐globulol in Cleistocalyx operculatus [28], iridoids and geniposide in Calvatia gigantean [29], curcumin and apigenin in Dittrichia viscosa [30], cinnamic acid in Globularia alypum [31] and hyperforin in Hypericum perforatum [32] are the primary compounds that exhibit different effects in the wound healing process.

The majority of the selected medicinal plants exhibit the ability to accelerate the wound healing process, primarily due to their antioxidant properties. Among these plants, the specific antioxidant mechanisms of some have been considered.

Crocus sativus (saffron) contains antioxidant compounds, particularly crocin, which possess a greater antioxidant capacity compared to other compounds due to the presence of hydroxyl and glucose moieties [33]. This medicinal plant reduces the ROS in burn environments by decreasing neutrophil counts, which results in reduced superoxide anion production. Quercetin and other flavonoids found in Tephrosia purpurea and Cucurbita moschata (pumpkin) extracts exert their antioxidant activity by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase, glutathione reductase, glutathione peroxidase and catalase) and inhibiting lipid peroxidation [3, 34]. Glycyrrhizin, present in Glycyrrhiza glabra , can reduce superoxide anions and inhibit nitric oxide (NO) production [35]. Olea europaea contains antioxidant compounds, such as hydroxytyrosol, verbascoside, luteolin and apigenin, which prevent mitochondrial membrane depolarization and function as oxidation inhibitors [36]. Albizia julibrissin 's flavonoid react with superoxide radical anions produced by neutrophils and macrophages [37]. Pure polyphenolic compounds isolated from the aerial parts of Brocchia cinerea , such as luteolin‐4′‐O‐glucoside, 4,5‐dicaffeoylquinic acid and chlorogenic acid, exhibit antioxidant activity. Additionally, the essential oil of this plant demonstrates antioxidant activity [38]. Arachidic acid, an NADPH oxidase inhibitor, contributes to the antioxidant activity of the lipid extract of Caralluma europaea [39]. Polysaccharides present in Moringa alterniflora display antioxidant activity against DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl, peroxyl, alkyl, H2O2 and superoxide radicals [40].

Numerous medicinal plants and their chemical compounds have been found to exhibit anti‐inflammatory effects, which can expedite the healing process. The mechanism of anti‐inflammatory activity for some of the mentioned plants in this review has been investigated and documented.

Onosma dichroantha , B. cinerea and cinnamic acids in G. alypum demonstrate a strong inhibitory effect on NO production. As a free radical, excessive NO can directly impair normal cell function and cause inflammation. NO binds to superoxide radicals, forming peroxynitrite, an unstable isomer of nitrate that is highly reactive [31, 38, 41]. Saffron can shift inflammation from an acute state to a chronic one by downregulating IL‐1β expression. Additionally, it reduces TGF‐β1 expression, subsequently decreasing the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the wound bed. This plant also inhibits xanthine oxidase activity and decreases neutrophil count. Neutrophils play a crucial role in producing superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide [33]. Pistacia lentiscus fruits and Cytisus triflorus leaves contain abundant unsaturated fatty acids, such as α‐linoleic acid and linolenic acid, which exert anti‐inflammatory effects by playing a major role in the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the site of inflammation. These compounds also have the ability to provide necessary lipids for cell repair and respiration [42, 43].

Ocimum basilicum also decreases the mRNA expression of inflammatory cytokines, including chemokines C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), IL‐6, IL‐1 and TNF‐α [44]. The constituents of Scrophularia striata , such as phenyl propanoid glycosides, prevent the release of inflammatory factors like PGE‐2, IL‐4 and IL‐1B. Additionally, they inhibit macrophage activities and the production of chemical mediators, consequently reducing inflammation and promoting tissue organisation [45]. Geraniin in Phyllanthus muellerianus increases the phagocytosis activity of macrophages and inhibits the production of TNF‐α and NO [46]. Rutin in Plantago sempervirens prevents innate inflammatory responses through the inhibition of several pro‐inflammatory mediators such as NO, TNF‐α, NF‐κB and other cytokines (IL‐6, IL‐8) [47].

Vitamin D present in C. europaea reduces the activity of lipoxygenase by forming a hydrogen bond with the residue VAL 671 in the enzyme's active site, subsequently attenuating inflammatory reactions. Furthermore, α‐linoleic acid and other fatty acids in this plant contribute to its anti‐inflammatory activity. Alpha‐linoleic acid downregulates the expression of iNOS, COX‐2 and TNF‐α genes by inhibiting the NF‐κB and MAPK pathways in macrophages [39]. The anti‐inflammatory activity of Solanum diploconos fruit extract is attributed to the presence of compounds such as caffeic acid, lutein and β‐carotene. These compounds attenuate chemotaxis by reducing cytokine and chemokine release. They also inhibit NO production, a key signalling molecule in inflammatory processes, and impede NF‐ĸB translocation essential for the transcription of inflammatory mediators. Additionally, these compounds prevent leukocyte adhesion to the endothelium by inhibiting l‐selectin cleavage, subsequently impeding leukocyte migration to the wound area [48].

Steroid compounds in Dillenia ochreata reduce inflammation by inhibiting phospholipase A2 [49]. Trichilia dregeana leaves exert their anti‐inflammatory effects by inhibiting COX‐1 and COX‐2, which further inhibit the formation of PGE2 and PGD2. This process prevents granuloma formation and contributes to rapid healing [50].

Pathogenic bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus , Streptococcus pneumonia , Escherichia coli , S. pyogenes and Klebsiella pneumonia , can easily enter wounds, disrupting healing by reducing fibroblast and epithelial cell proliferation and increasing collagen degradation [51]. Multiple compounds in medicinal plants facilitate wound repair processes through their antimicrobial properties.

Monoterpenoid compounds like linalool in henna plant disrupt bacterial membrane structure and function. Linseed oil's unsaturated fatty acids (omega‐3, omega‐6 and omega‐9 fatty acids), lignan and phenolic compounds inhibit bacterial growth by interacting with the bacterial cell wall, inducing DNA disintegration, and inhibiting DNA gyrase [52]. Sea buckthorn and olive oil's phenolic components, such as secoiridoids (oleuropein and its derivatives), impede the growth of pathogenic bacteria [53]. High tannin content in A. julibrissin attaches to the bacterial cell wall, scavenging the wound site [37]. Gallic acid in B. cinerea disrupts bacterial membrane integrity, altering hydrophobicity, permeability and surface charge. Quercetin in this plant exhibits bacteriostatic activity by inhibiting d‐Ala‐d‐Ala ligase in bacterial cells [38]. Triterpenoids in D. ochreata strongly interact with bacterial outer membrane proteins, leading to their destruction. Steroid compounds in this plant exert antibacterial effects by interacting with phospholipids in the cell membrane, reducing membrane integrity and inducing cell lysis [49].

The medicinal plants examined in this review, in addition to aiding burn wound healing through their antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory and antibacterial properties, exert their wound healing activity by modulating the cells and proteins involved in the wound repair process.

Specifically, oleic acid and linolenic acid in C. pepo increase collagen I and III levels, subsequently stimulating fibroblast growth. Other unsaturated fatty acids such as omega‐3 in linseed oil, increase membrane fluidity, leading to increase blood flow to the wound bed and enhanced transport of nutrients needed for tissue repair [52, 54]. Triterpenes from C. asiatica increase collagen matrix remodelling, cell migration, cell proliferation, angiogenesis and stimulate glycosaminoglycan synthesis. Additionally, asiatic acid induces the expression of TNFAIP6, a hyaladherin (hyaluronan‐binding proteins) involved in ECM remodelling and inflammation modulation in human fibroblasts. Asiaticoside, isolated from this plant, enhances collagen content, tensile strength, epithelialisation and myofibroblast activity involved in wound contraction. This compound induces type 1 collagen synthesis in HDF cells via activation of TGF‐β receptor I kinase‐independent Smad signalling pathway. Moreover, β‐sitosterol, a plant sterol, exhibits intense angiogenic activity, anti‐inflammatory effects and accelerates epithelialisation [26, 42, 55, 56].

Oleuropein in O. europaea can increase type 1 collagen production, fibroblast migration, angiogenesis and wound contraction [36]. Danshensu (DSU), salvianolic acid A (SAA), salvianolic acid B (SAB) and other phenolic compounds in Salvia miltiorrhiza enhance fibroblast proliferation and collagens production. Additionally, salvianolic acids exhibit potent angiogenic activity by increasing VEGF and its receptors, thereby reducing skin flap necrosis [57, 58]. Chlorogenic acid in H. androsaemum enhances collagen synthesis via TNF‐α and TGF‐β and acts as an effective inhibitor of collagenolytic activity [59].

Geraniin and furosin in Phyllantus muellerianus enhance collagen formation and fibroblast mitochondrial activity and induce keratinocytes differentiation by increasing the expression of involucrin, cytokeratins (CK1, CK10) and differentiation‐specific proteins [46]. Myricetin and amentoflavone in Lavandula officinalis activate Wnt/beta‐catenin signalling pathway, which plays an important role in accelerating wound healing by increasing casein kinase‐1 activity, while apigenin and catechin activate this signalling pathway by increasing glycogen synthase kinase‐3 beta activity [60].

Saponins in the extracts of Swietenia mahagoni (mahogany) bark and Anredera cordifolia accelerate fibroblast proliferation, keratinocyte migration, collagen formation, fibronectin synthesis and myofibroblast differentiation, improving the wound revitalization process [14, 61, 62]. Three saponins, julibroside J(29), julibroside J(30) and julibroside J(31), isolated from A. julibrissin , play a key role in the healing by increasing fibroblast migration and angiogenesis [37]. Polysaccharides in M. alterniflora increase angiogenesis and fibroblast proliferation and migration by supplying a connective tissue matrix [40]. Tannins and alkaloids in Urtica simensis promote wound healing due to their antibacterial, proliferative and angiogenic properties. These compounds also precipitate proteins in damaged tissues, leading to rapid scab formation [63]. Compounds like omega‐7, vitamin C, A and K in Hippophae rhamnoides promote epithelization in skin and mucosa tissue [64, 65].

Generally, various biological compounds in other medicinal plants, apart from the mentioned mechanisms, significantly increase collagen fibre resistance, bFGF expression, autophagy, hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1α) expression, MMP‐9 and MMP‐2 expression, and the synthesis of tissue factor (coagulation factor III), epidermal growth factor (EGF) and platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF). They also decrease TGF‐β1 expression, promoting the progression of the wound healing process [3, 33, 51, 66, 67, 68].

Clinical trials demonstrate that using medicinal plants for healing various types of burn wounds reduces epithelization time, inflammation and infection risk in the wound area, leading to reduced hospitalisation duration. This is a significant concern for governments due to reduced treatment costs [37]. Aloe vera ‐based hydrogels are promising platforms for healing burn wounds, given their biocompatible, bioavailable and biodegradable matrix advantages. Acemanan, an essential component in A. vera gel, increases collagen I, keratinocyte growth factor‐1 (KGF‐1) and VEGF synthesis. Additionally, β‐sitosterol present in the gel increases von Willebrand factor, VEGF, VEGF receptor and laminin, enhancing angiogenesis at the skin wound site. The most crucial glycoprotein in this gel is the compound G1G1M1DI2, which plays a role in skin cell proliferation and migration. The anti‐inflammatory activity of A. vera occurs through MMP‐9 inhibition, reducing mononuclear white blood cell count at the wound site, and decreasing IL‐8, IL‐6, IL‐1β and TNF‐α levels [69, 70, 71]. In this systematic review, the limited number of clinical studies included may raise concerns about the reliability and generalizability of the findings. While the available evidence has been synthesised, additional clinical studies are essential to validate the results and contribute to a comprehensive understanding of medicinal plants' wound healing potential. Further investigations, employing standardised methodologies and larger sample sizes, will generate higher quality evidence and facilitate the translation of findings into clinical practice. As more clinical studies become available, future systematic reviews will be better equipped to provide robust and conclusive evidence on the efficacy of medicinal plants for burn wound healing.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that various phytochemicals in different medicinal plants improve and facilitate the wound repair process through various mechanisms, such as enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, inhibiting lipid peroxidation, reducing neutrophil count and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, decelerating the arachidonate metabolic pathway, boosting macrophage phagocytosis, decreasing NO production, inhibiting pathogen growth, enhancing collagen and proteoglycans content, promoting fibroblast and keratinocyte proliferation and migration and stimulating angiogenesis and myofibroblast formation. These compounds are found in different parts of medicinal plants, particularly leaves and seeds. This study serves as a foundation for future research and the potential application of several medicinal plants in discovering and developing new treatments for cutaneous burn wounds.
