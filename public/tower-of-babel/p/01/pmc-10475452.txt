Elevated arsenic level in fasting serum via ingestion of fish meat increased the risk of hypertension in humans and mice
Authors: Takumi Kagawa, Nobutaka Ohgami, Tingchao He, Akira Tazaki, Shoko Ohnuma, Hisao Naito, Ichiro Yajima, Dijie Chen, Yuqi Deng, Takashi Tamura, Takaaki Kondo, Kenji Wakai, Masashi Kato
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThere has been a shortage of human studies to elucidate the association between serum arsenic levels and the prevalence of hypertension. This study multidirectionally investigated associations among arsenic exposure, dietary ingestion, and the risk of hypertension by combined human epidemiological and mouse experimental studies.Methods and resultsThis study focused on the total arsenic level in fasting serum, a biomarker of arsenic exposure. Associations among ingestion frequencies of 54 diet items of Japanese food separated into six categories, total arsenic level in fasting serum, and the prevalence of hypertension were investigated in 2709 general people in Japan. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated a dose-dependent association between serum arsenic level and hypertension and a positive association between the ingestion of fish meat and hypertension. Further analysis showed that the latter association was fully mediated by increased fasting serum arsenic levels in humans. Similarly, oral exposure to the putative human-equivalent dose of arsenic species mixture with the same ratios in a common fish meat in Japan increased systolic blood pressure and arsenic levels in fasting serum in mice.ConclusionThis interdisciplinary approach suggests that fish-meat ingestion is a potential risk factor for arsenic-mediated hypertension. Because the increased consumption of fish meat is a recent global trend, health risks of the increased ingestion of arsenic via fish meat should be further investigated.

Introduction

Lifestyle contributes to the development of hypertension, a crucial determinant of healthy life expectancy in humans. Above all, food intake strongly correlates with hypertension.1 Sodium (Na) ingestion via diet is one of the strongest contributors to hypertension.2,3 Conversely, dietary ingestion of potassium (K), a congener of sodium, suppresses hypertension.2,4 Thus, elements ingested from diet strongly dictate the prevalence of hypertension.

Arsenic is a typical element with high toxicities.5–7 Exposure to arsenic causes various health risks, including immunological, carcinogenic, neurogenic, and cutaneous disturbances.8–11 The health effects of arsenic depend on arsenic species. Inorganic arsenic, one of the main forms of arsenic in groundwater and soil,12 is considered highly toxic.5–11 Although a high level of arsenic in ground and river water has been reported in certain areas in Japan,13,14 exposure to arsenic from drinking water is very limited.15 Inorganic arsenic is metabolized by the body to potentially less-toxic organic forms such as monomethylarsonic acid (MMA) and dimethylarsinic acid (DMA).16 Arsenobetaine (AsB) and trimethylarsine oxide (TMAO) are representative forms of organic arsenic in seafood.17 Japanese population with heavy seafood consumption is assumed to be chronically exposed to high levels of organic arsenic, generally considered of low toxicity.18–20 Thus, it is important to consider the exposure source and species to investigate the human health effects of arsenic exposure. Animal interventional studies are useful to assess the effects of arsenic exposure source and species under the strictly controlled genetic and environmental conditions, although inter-species differences, including chemical sensitivity, remain.21

Recent studies have suggested that vascular endothelial disturbances caused by exposure to arsenic could provoke hypertension.22,23 Arsenic exposure has also been suggested to inhibit vasodilation in the aorta owing to the suppression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, resulting in hypertension in animals.24,25 Since these previous studies indicate that the level of intravascular arsenic is a direct effector of vascular endothelial cells, the arsenic level in serum or plasma seems to have a specific biological significance in hypertension. However, evidence on the association between serum arsenic levels and the prevalence of hypertension in humans is insufficient.

Serum arsenic level may be considered inaccurate for assessing the health risk of arsenic exposure because the intravascular arsenic level is largely affected by the last-minute food ingestion in humans.20 However, arsenic levels in fasting serum might overcome this limitation since fasting serum levels of arsenic, iron, cadmium, and mercury are constant.26,27 Furthermore, previous studies have recommended the measurement of total arsenic level for dose–response analysis in populations with a chronic and wide-range arsenic exposure since species of arsenic in plasma and serum are undetectably low in the general population in advanced countries.28,29 Therefore, the total arsenic level in fasting serum was used to assess the latent risk of hypertension in the general population in this study.

The Japanese diet, a United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization’s intangible cultural heritage, has been generally regarded as a healthy diet because of its low calories and low-fat contents. Another aspect of the Japanese diet is the ingestion of seafood, including a good amount of arsenic, as typified by sushi.30 Because seafood consumption is a global trend given the popularity of the Japanese diet, investigation of the health effects of arsenic exposure via the ingestion of the Japanese diet is indispensable to predicting the future of food and health in the world. Nevertheless, whether diets that cause arsenic exposure can affect hypertension remains unclear.

This transdisciplinary study first investigated the association between arsenic levels in the fasting serum and the prevalence of hypertension in 2709 adults in Japan. Subsequently, food analysis of 54 food items divided into six categories newly identified a special food that increased the prevalence of hypertension and the serum level of arsenic. Our hypothesis that the ingestion of special food could cause hypertension via increased arsenic levels in fasting serum obtained in our human study was more directly confirmed by our animal study.

Methods

Study population

Participants (n = 2808) aged 39–75 years and citizens of Nagoya City in Japan participated in the Daiko Study of the second cross-sectional survey of the Japan Multi-Institutional Collaborative Cohort (J-MICC).31 After excluding participants without serum samples (n = 99), 2709 participants were included in this study.

Ethical permission

The Ethics Committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine was established in strict compliance with the relevant guidelines. The main study protocol was reviewed and approved (2008-0618 and 2013-0070) by the Ethics Committee, as reported previously.32,33 All participants provided written informed consent after understanding the protocol.

Human sample collection

The sample collection method from participants has been described previously.31,34 The first-phase clearance of arsenic in human blood is ∼1 h after ingestion.35 In this study, peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning after last food consumption (overnight fasting) to avoid the change of serum arsenic level by last-minute food ingestion. The fasting time [mean ± standard deviation (SD)] was 14.1 ± 2.5 h. All serum samples were stored at −80°C in the Graduate School of Medicine, Nagoya University, until analysis.

Determination of total arsenic levels in fasting serum and sodium level in urine

Total arsenic concentrations in fasting serum samples from humans and mice in addition to sodium concentration in urine were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (7500cx, Agilent Technologies). The operational details are described in Supplementary material online, Methods S1.

Questionnaire

Participants were asked to complete a self-reported questionnaire about general information, disease record, family history of hypertension, physical activity, cigarette smoking, and alcohol consumption. Participants filled out the questionnaires at home to allow them to answer the questions thoroughly, accurately, and consistently. The questionnaire contained a food-frequency questionnaire (FFQ) surveying the dietary patterns of participants, with about 54 diet items and beverages in the last one year as shown in a previous J-MICC study.36 The validity and reliability of the FFQ were demonstrated in previous studies.37,38 On the day of enrolment, trained staff conducted face-to-face interviews with participants to carefully check the questionnaires. Diets were classified into six categories: cereals/beans, livestock/dairy products, seafood, vegetables/fruits, confectioneries, and beverages. Each category was calculated by summing all daily dietary ingestion in the same classification and separated into low, medium, and high ingestion groups by tertiles. The ingestion of specific seafood items (fish meat, bone-edible small fish, seaweed, fish-paste products, canned tuna, squid/octopus/shrimp/crab, shellfish, and fish roe) was also separated into low, medium, and high groups according to the frequency of ingestion. The details of the diet categories and seafood items are described in Supplementary material online, Methods S2.

Information including blood pressure in participants

A blood pressure measurement device (HEM-100, Omron, Japan) was used to measure blood pressure in the sitting position, as reported previously.31 This study followed the criteria used in previous studies.39,40 Participants who had one of the following four conditions were categorized as hypertensive: (i) previous diagnosis of hypertension, (ii) systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, (iii) diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, and (iv) currently taking an antihypertensive drug. Disease states, including diabetes and kidney disease, were defined according to previous studies.41–43 Physical activity during leisure time (METs/W) was estimated as previously described.44 Detailed information and definitions of the covariates are described in Supplementary material online, Methods S3.

Animal study

Male mice (ICR line, Japan SLC, Inc.) were maintained with standard mouse chow and water in a specific pathogen-free condition at an ambient temperature of 23 ± 2°C and a 12:12 h light–dark cycle. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya University (approval number: M220179-001). This study followed the Japanese Government Regulations for Animal Experiments. Male ICR mice at age of 6 weeks were orally exposed to a mixture of chemical arsenic species dissolved in drinking water. The ratio of arsenic species in the mixture followed a previous study showing the composition of arsenic species (µg compound/g dry weight of fish meat) in a common fish meat (Tinu; Acanthopagrus Schlegelii) in Japan, which consisted of 0.07 μg/g of pentavalent arsenic [As(V)], 0.13 μg/g of DMA, 10.6 μg/g of AsB, and 0.10 μg/g of TMAO.17 The reported levels (mean ± SD) of total arsenic and summed arsenic species in the fish meat were 10.8 ± 1.1 and 10.9 ± 1.9, respectively, suggesting the reliability of the concentrations.17 The exposure level of the arsenic species contained in the mixture was determined as follows: human dose (μg/g body weight) of the arsenic species was first calculated, assuming that a person weighing 60 kg consumed 200 g of fish meat daily. Blood pressure measurement technique and serum preparation in mice are described in the Supplementary material online, Methods S4 section.

Statistical analysis

This study categorized human participants with a wide range of fasting serum arsenic levels according to the cut-off value of 1.7 µg/L determined based on the maximum Youden index for predicting the prevalence of hypertension in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.46 To investigate the dose–response relationship between arsenic levels in fasting serum and the prevalence of hypertension, a restricted cubic spline regression model was used following a previous study.47 The concentrations of serum arsenic below the lower limit of detection (LOD) were provided as the value of the LOD divided by the square root of two, as previously described.47 Binary logistic regression was performed to determine the associations between diet ingestion frequency and arsenic levels in fasting serum, as shown in a previous study.48 Estimated 24 h sodium excretion (est24hNa), an important effector of blood pressure, was calculated according to a previous study.49 Certain variables were considered for inclusion in the final covariate-adjusted model: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and smoking and drinking habits (previous and current), as indicated in a previous study.50 This study regarded the lowest group as the reference group in the logistic regression model. We used the rms package of R software (ver. 3.6.3) and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Two-sided P < 0.05 was judged as significant in all statistical tests.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

A total of 697 participants were hypertensive among 2709 total participants. Table 1 indicates that age, BMI, physical activity, the proportion of men, family history of hypertension, and est24hNa in participants with hypertension were significantly (P < 0.001) higher than those in participants without hypertension. As a notable characteristic of the assemblage, the median seafood ingestion frequency in all participants exceeded once daily. Moreover, the frequency of seafood ingestion in participants with hypertension was significantly (P < 0.001) higher than that in participants without hypertension. The medians for ingestion frequency of fish meat and bone-edible small fish in participants with hypertension were greater than two-fold and greater than three-fold, respectively, higher than those in participants without hypertension (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). The intake frequency of confectionery in participants with hypertension was significantly lower than that in participants without hypertension. Meanwhile, no significant difference in the intake of cereal/beans, livestock/dairy products, vegetable/fruits, and beverages was noted between participants without and participants with hypertension (Table 1). The median and mean arsenic levels in the fasting serum were 0.7 μg/L (interquartile range: 2.2 μg/L) and 1.5 μg/L (SD: 2.4 μg/L), respectively. The distribution of serum arsenic levels of participants was shown in Supplementary material online, Table S2. The mean arsenic level in this study was comparable with the mean arsenic levels reported in China (4.10 μg/L)51 and Iran (2.73 μg/L).52

Analytical process for this combined study in humans and mice

The analytical process of this study consisted of an epidemiological investigation in humans and verification experiments in mice which are indicated as a schema (Figure 1). After clarifying the association between arsenic levels in fasting serum and the prevalence of hypertension (Figure 2), the food categories (Figure 3) and items (Figure 4) that contributed to the increased arsenic levels were determined. The effect of an identified food item that increases arsenic levels on the prevalence of hypertension was elucidated (Figure 5). Finally, the hypothesis created by syllogism, based on our epidemiological results in humans, was partially verified by our experimental mouse study (Figure 6).

Dose-dependent positive association between total arsenic level in fasting serum and the prevalence of hypertension

The contribution of arsenic levels in fasting serum to the prevalence of hypertension was first investigated using restricted cubic spline analysis (Figure 2A). A positive dose-dependent relationship between fasting serum arsenic levels and the prevalence of hypertension was found in both the crude and covariate-adjusted models, presented with the reference cut-off values determined as 1.7 and 0.7 μg/L based on the maximum Youden index (Figure 2B) and median values (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1), respectively. In addition, a conventional logistic regression analysis in which arsenic level was treated as categorical variable was performed following the method previously described.53 As shown in Supplementary material online, Table S3, significant associations between fasting serum arsenic level (> 1.7 µg/L) and the prevalence of hypertension were again obtained in our analysis that used the LOD value as a reference. These results indicated that total fasting serum arsenic level increased the risk of hypertension in a dose-dependent manner.

Associations between diet ingestion and total arsenic levels in fasting serum

The contribution of food ingestion to arsenic levels in fasting serum (Figure 3A) was investigated using a covariate-adjusted logistic regression model (Figure 3B) and pseudo-R2 (%) in the logistic regression model (Figure 3C). Our analysis of the associations between dietary ingestion frequencies classified into six categories and arsenic levels in fasting serum showed a positive association between high seafood ingestion and high arsenic levels in fasting serum [odds ratio, OR = 1.909 (1.490, 2.447), P < 0.001] (Figure 3B). In contrast, a negative association was found between high ingestion of livestock/dairy products and high arsenic levels in fasting serum [OR = 0.644 (0.512, 0.811), P < 0.001]. The relative contribution of dietary ingestion frequencies classified into six categories to arsenic levels in fasting serum was also indicated (Figure 3C). Among the six categories, the largest and smallest contributions to arsenic levels in fasting serum were seafood ingestion (25.25%) and confectionery ingestion (0.25%), respectively. Considering that the second largest contribution of livestock/dairy products ingestion was 9.63%, these results suggested a high effect of seafood ingestion on total arsenic levels in fasting serum.

Associations between ingestion frequencies of seafood, total arsenic levels in fasting serum, and the prevalence of hypertension

The positive association and the largest contribution of seafood ingestion to arsenic levels in fasting serum encouraged us to further investigate the contributions of seafood ingestion frequencies to arsenic levels in fasting serum using a covariate-adjusted model (Figure 4A and B). Positive associations between high and/or medium ingestion of fish meat [medium: OR = 1.371 (1.144, 1.643), P < 0.001; high: OR = 1.812 (1.173, 2.801), P = 0.007], bone-edible small fish [high: OR = 1.642 (1.077, 2.503), P = 0.021], seaweed [high: OR = 1.436 (1.068, 1.932), P = 0.017], squid/octopus/shrimp/crab [medium: OR = 1.315 (1.068, 1.618), P = 0.010], and arsenic levels in the fasting serum were determined.

Since the contributions of fish meat, bone-edible small fish, seaweed, and squid/octopus/shrimp/crab to arsenic levels in the fasting serum were determined, the associations of these ingestion frequencies of seafood items and the prevalence of hypertension were clarified (Figure 5A). Our analysis of a covariate-adjusted logistic regression model showed a significant association between the prevalence of hypertension and ingestion frequency of fish meat [OR = 1.704 (1.041, 2.791), P = 0.034], but not with the ingestion of bone-edible small fish, seaweed, or squid/octopus/shrimp/crab (Figure 5B).

The causal mediation analysis further showed a significant positive mediation effect of fish meat intake on the prevalence of hypertension through the fasting serum arsenic level [β (95% confidence interval) = 0.0028 (0.00046–0.010), P = 0.008]. Meanwhile, there was no direct effect between fish meat intake and the prevalence of hypertension after considering the fasting serum level of arsenic as a mediator (Figure 5C).

There was a negative association between high intake of canned tuna [high: OR = 0.391 (0.210, 0.729), P = 0.003] and arsenic levels in fasting serum (Figure 4B) despite no association between the prevalence of hypertension and ingestion frequency of canned tuna (Figure 5B). These results suggest that the effect of high ingestion of canned tuna on hypertension via decreased serum arsenic level is limited.

Effect of oral exposure to arsenic species mimicking their ratios in a common fish meat on blood pressure in mice

In our experimental mouse study (Figure 6A and B), the stability of total arsenic levels in fasting serum was investigated in mice that were orally treated by the putative human-equivalent dose of a mixture of arsenic species originated from a common fish meat in Japan.17 The chemical information of arsenic species in the mixture is provided in Supplementary material online, Table S4. After an overnight fasting period following continuous exposure to the mixture for 5 weeks (first sampling), the total arsenic level in fasting serum from the mice exposed to arsenic species was significantly higher than that in the control mice without exposure to arsenic species (Figure 6B). The total arsenic level in fasting serum in the mice following an overnight fasting period after restarting the continuous exposure to the mixture for an additional 1 week (second sampling) was comparable to that in mice exposed to arsenic species after an overnight fasting period following exposure for 5 weeks (first sampling). The results of our animal study suggested that the total arsenic level in the fasting serum is reproducible and reliable.

Furthermore, the level of systolic blood pressure but not diastolic blood pressure in the mice exposed to arsenic species originating from the fish meat for 5 weeks was significantly higher than that in the control mice without exposure to arsenic species (Figure 6C), while baseline levels of systolic and diastolic blood pressures were the same between these two groups of mice before oral exposure to arsenic species. These results suggested that oral exposure to arsenic species originating from common fish meat increased blood pressure.

Discussion

A dose-dependent increase in the risk of hypertension associated with the levels of fasting serum arsenic was demonstrated in a sample of 2709 middle-aged and older participants in Japan (Figure 2 and Supplementary material online, Figure S1). The positive associations were maintained after controlling for est24hNa, an informative index for ingestion of sodium, a crucial effector for blood pressure.49 The results are partially consistent with those of a previous study indicating a positive association between total arsenic levels in fasting serum and blood pressure levels in 395 Chinese individuals. However, the study presented no information about the prevalence of hypertension.53 In addition to the above stable results in our human epidemiological study, the constant level of total arsenic in fasting serum in our mouse experimental and previous studies54,55 suggests that the arsenic level could be a potential tool for evaluating constitutive arsenic exposure and the risk of hypertension.

Next, this study aimed to clarify the origin of total arsenic in fasting serum. Drinking water provided by wells could be one of the main sources of arsenic exposure in developing countries such as Bangladesh, Afghanistan, and Vietnam.56–58 However, the major (94%) origin of oral arsenic exposure in Japan is food, not drinking water.15 Therefore, this study focused on the effect of each Japanese food item on total arsenic levels in the fasting serum. Seafood was determined to be the major contributor to the arsenic level among the diets separated into six categories, in accordance with a previous study.59 Our analysis using the pseudo-R2 test revealed that the contribution of seafood to arsenic level was the largest (>25%) among all confounders. Further analysis of seafood classified into eight items indicated a positive association between arsenic level in fasting serum and ingestion frequencies of fish meat, bone-edible small fish, seaweed, and squid/octopus/shrimp/crab. This human study further demonstrated an association between the prevalence of hypertension and ingestion frequency of fish meat, but not other seafood items. The positive association was maintained after controlling for confounders such as est24hNa and rice. Therefore, the effects of salty foods such as soy sauce, which is usually taken with raw fish meat (sashimi), and rice on the prevalence of hypertension may be limited. Moreover, our intermediary analysis revealed that the positive association between fish meat intake and the prevalence of hypertension was fully mediated by the increase of serum arsenic level. In addition, the associations with the risk of hypertension in the four sub-groups consisting of low or high levels of fasting serum arsenic levels and low or high frequencies of fish meat intake were analysed by a multivariate logistic regression model (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2). There was a significant increase in the risk of hypertension in the group with high fasting serum arsenic levels and that with high frequency of fish meat intake but not the other two groups. These results, presented in Figure 5C and Supplementary material online, Figure S2, more strongly suggest that increased levels of total arsenic in fasting serum due to high ingestion of fish meat, including high levels of organic arsenic of generally lower toxicity than inorganic arsenic,18 may be a latent risk factor for hypertension in humans.

Owing to ethical issues, it is impossible to provide direct evidence for the risk of hypertension via the high ingestion of arsenic species originating from fish meat in humans. To overcome this limitation and more directly verify our hypothesis obtained in a human study, an animal study was conducted. Our mouse study demonstrated increased systolic blood pressure caused by oral exposure to the putative human-equivalent dose of arsenic species mixture following the ratios of common fish meat in Japan.17 The combined results of our human and animal studies strongly suggest that high exposure to arsenic through high ingestion of fish meat could be a potential risk factor for hypertension. Considering that most of arsenic species in fish meat are organic,17 organic arsenic may affect the blood pressure in animals as well as humans.

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Food Agriculture Organization (FAO), and World Health Organization (WHO) recommend the ingestion of fish at 1–2 servings weekly because of the favourable effects of seafood, including omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals on health.60–62 On the other hand, fish consumption per capita in Japan (around 60 kg/year) is the highest in the world.63 Exceeding half of the hypertensive participants in this study had a high frequency of fish meat ingestion (more than three times weekly). The level of arsenic in fish meat is higher than that in other fish tissues, including the intestine, gills, and mouthpieces.64 Thus, the idiosyncratic dietary habits with a high frequency of fish meat intake exceeding the recommendations of EFSA and FAO/WHO in Japan may overcome the beneficial effects of fish meat ingestion on health, thereby shedding light on arsenic-mediated hypertension in this study.

Several limitations should be considered in this study. The preventive effect of fish ingestion on hypertension may be limited because a recent cohort study showed no association between long-term dietary intake of fish and omega-3 fatty acids and the incidence of hypertension in men of middle and senior ages in the USA.65 However, previous clinical studies also suggested that an adequate dose of omega-3 fatty acids ingestion have a beneficial effect on cardiovascular health.66,67 To carefully address the risk for the ingestion of different types of seafood including both omega-3 fatty acids and arsenic, further research is needed to evaluate the association between arsenic exposure and prevalence of hypertension after considering the ingestion of omega-3 fatty acids as a confounder. Furthermore, the amount of fish consumption per person and arsenic levels in fish were unknown in this study. Arsenic level in serum reflects the shorter time of arsenic exposure compared with arsenic levels in urine and toenail samples.35,68 Determination of arsenic species is also difficult in serum arsenic.28,29 Thus, the total arsenic level in serum remains of limited capacity in assessing the arsenic-mediated health risk as a biomarker despite the relatively stable measurement taken after overnight fasting in this study. Epidemiological study to analyse the arsenic species in urine samples will be needed to more directly identify the arsenic species that affect hypertension. Various animal studies involving treatment with individual arsenic species will also be helpful to appropriately distinguish the effects of inorganic and organic arsenic on blood pressure. The proportion of fish consumed worldwide is increasing, exceeding the proportion of global population growth and the proportion of meat consumed from all other animals over the past 60 years.69 Therefore, the issue of high levels of arsenic exposure caused by fish meat ingestion is no longer limited to Japan. More global studies with a large number of participants are required to evaluate the effects of arsenic exposure by fish meat ingestion on health.

In conclusion, arsenic is a typical toxic element, while fish meat ingestion, including large amounts of arsenic, is recommended worldwide for health benefits. How to interpret the health effects of fish meat ingestion regarding the risk of hypertension remains unknown. First, this epidemiological study demonstrated, for the first time, a dose-dependent association between the risk of hypertension and total arsenic levels in fasting serum. In addition to the positive associations between fish meat ingestion and total arsenic levels in fasting serum and the prevalence of hypertension in humans, the results of our intermediary analysis suggest that elevated total arsenic levels in fasting serum derived from fish meat ingestion could be a latent risk factor for hypertension. Finally, the hypothesis presumed in our human cross-sectional study was partially proved by our mouse experimental study of the increased level of blood pressure by the putative human-equivalent exposure to several arsenic species mimicking arsenic exposure originating from common fish meat. Thus, results from both humans and mice more strongly suggest the increased potential risk of hypertension due to high arsenic exposure via frequent ingestion of fish meat. Because of the precipitous increase in the global consumption of seafood, the effects of exposure to arsenic from seafood ingestion on non-communicable diseases, including hypertension, should be carefully investigated.
