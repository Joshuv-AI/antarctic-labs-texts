Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy and Risk of Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Rasmus Bork Dinesen, Deepthi Rajan, Christian Torp-Pedersen, Niels Risum, Peder Emil Warming, Jacob Tfelt-Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the long-term risk of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) following a first-time diagnosis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)?FindingsThis cohort study included 5901 patients with incident HCM and 23 339 matched controls. The results showed a significantly increased 10-year OHCA risk in patients with HCM compared with matched controls within the corresponding age groups (18-60 years: 2.7% vs 1.5%; 61-85 years: 4.4% vs 3.2%).MeaningThese findings suggest that HCM was associated with an increased risk of OHCA, underscoring the need for improved risk stratification to optimize primary prevention.

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a common, hereditary cardiac disease. The estimated prevalence of HCM is 1 in 500 people. There has been an increase in the prevalence of patients with HCM in the Danish population during the last 2 decades.1,2,3 The clinical course of HCM is unpredictable, ranging from asymptomatic to severe; a severe course may involve progressive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and sudden cardiac death (SCD) due to ventricular arrhythmias.3,4,5,6 Fortunately, contemporary medical and surgical treatment and improved preventive care with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) have resulted in a declining incidence of SCD globally in patients diagnosed with HCM.6,7 Nevertheless, SCD remains a consequence of HCM due to its unpredictable nature. Commonly, SCD is defined as being a sudden and unexpected death of presumed cardiac causes and encompasses both witnessed and unwitnessed cases. Preceding SCD, patients often present with an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), more frequently identified and with resuscitation attempted in witnessed cases. However, the long-term risk of OHCA in patients with HCM has not been formerly investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the long-term risk of OHCA in a nationwide cohort of patients diagnosed with HCM compared with a matched cohort of individuals without HCM sampled from the general Danish population. Additionally, the study aimed to characterize and compare patients with HCM who did or did not experience an OHCA.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This cohort study was designed as a nationwide, observational, register-based study comprising both a cohort and a nested case-control study, both in accordance with the Strengthening the Report of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Regional Data Committee. In Denmark, further ethical approvals and informed consent are not required for deidentified data register studies.

In Denmark, all citizens have a personal civil registration number, enabling cross-linkage between different Danish registers and data comparison for research purposes. For this study, data were collected between June 1, 2001, and December 31, 2022, from multiple nationwide administrative registers (eMethods 1 in Supplement 1). Data on OHCA were collected from the Danish Cardiac Arrest Register, which contain data on all cases of OHCA in which resuscitation has been attempted in Denmark since June 1, 2001.8 Identification of covariates is described in eMethods 2 and eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome of the study is the time from the date of exposure (first-time HCM diagnoses) or the matching date until first OHCA with attempted resuscitation. By definition, the primary outcome excludes all cases in whom resuscitation has not been attempted.

Study Population and Matching

Initially, a nationwide source population was defined, comprising all Danish citizens aged 18 to 85 years residing in Denmark between June 1, 2001, and December 31, 2022. Within in the source population, all individuals with at least 1 registered HCM diagnosis prior to December 31, 2022, were identified by the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes I42.1 or I42.2 and registered in the Danish National Patient Register.9

Cohort Study

The cohort study was conducted to investigate the long-term risk of OHCA in a nationwide population of patients with HCM. The cohort included all patients with a de novo HCM diagnosis (exposed) defined by having a first-time diagnosis of HCM between June 1, 2001, and December 31, 2022. Patients with de novo HCM who had had an OHCA or a diagnosis of an explainable phenocopy (eg, Fabry disease, mitochondrial myopathies, or amyloidosis) prior to the first-time HCM diagnosis were excluded. All patients with de novo HCM were matched using exposure density sampling at the time of HCM diagnosis with individuals without HCM (unexposed) from the source population. A variable matching ratio was used with as many as 4 unexposed matches per patient with exposure. They were matched on sex and birth year (5-year categories), as well as presence of comorbidities (heart failure, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cancer) and medication use (statins and drugs to lower lipid levels) prior to the exposure or matching date. Density-sampling plots were created for matched comorbidities and medication to check for imbalances in the duration of time since diagnosis or treatment until time of exposure or matching between the 2 groups (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). All patients with de novo HCM and matched controls were followed up from the time of diagnosis or matching until fulfilling 1 of 5 criteria: event (OHCA), death, older than 85 years, emigration, or end of observation period (December 31, 2022).

Nested Case-Control Study

A nested case-control study was conducted to characterize and identify covariates associated with OHCA in patients with HCM. All patients with HCM diagnosed before December 31, 2022, who had also experienced an OHCA during the observation period (June 1, 2001, to December 31, 2022) and without a prior diagnosis of an explainable phenocopy were identified (cases with HCM). All cases with HCM were matched with patients with HCM who had not experienced an OHCA at the time of event (controls with HCM) using incidence density sampling. They were matched on sex and birth year (5-year categories). A variable matching ratio with as many as 4 matches per case was used to ensure that all cases were included in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed between September 1, and November 30, 2025, using R, version 4.4.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Continuous data are presented as medians (IQRs) and categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages). Statistical differences were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous data and Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical data. Median follow-up time is reported using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences; values less than 0.10 were considered of adequate balance. Cumulative incidences are presented using the Aalen-Johansen estimator accounting for competing risks. Multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted and are described in eMethods 3 in Supplement 1.

To further examine the association between age at HCM diagnosis and OHCA risk, we used restricted cubic splines with 4 knots and tested for nonlinearity using likelihood ratio testing (eMethods 4 in Supplement 1). A conditional logistic regression model was applied to the nested case-control population to estimate the association between each covariate and OHCA. A detailed description of the model is provided in eMethods 5 in Supplement 1.

Differences in cumulative incidence were assessed using a 2-sided z test on the complementary log-log scale with standard errors derived from the delta method. All other tests were 2 sided. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall, the baseline results showed an even distribution of matched covariates. Most comorbidities were evenly distributed between patients with de novo HCM and matched controls. Only presence of valve disease was unevenly distributed, with a higher occurrence in patients with de novo HCM compared with matched controls (709 [12.0%] vs 1210 [5.2%]). Distribution of medication was uneven, with a higher occurrence in patients with de novo HCM compared with matched controls, regarding β-blockers (3116 [52.8%] vs 10 100 [43.3%]), calcium-channel antagonists (2515 [42.6%] vs 8442 [36.2%]), and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (3277 [55.5%] vs 11 586 [49.6%]).

Cumulative Incidences and Long-Term Risk of OHCA

The cumulative incidence of OHCA as time from exposure for patients with de novo HCM and time from matching for controls stratified by age group is illustrated in Figure 2. The results showed a significantly increased 10-year risk of OHCA in patients with de novo HCM in the group aged 18 to 60 years (2.8% [95% CI, 1.9%-3.7%]) compared with matched controls (1.5% [95% CI, 1.2%-1.8%]). Similarly, in the group aged 61 to 85 years, the 10-year risk of OHCA was significantly increased in patients with de novo HCM (4.3% [95% CI, 3.4%-5.1%]) compared with matched controls (3.3% [95% CI, 3.0%-3.7%]).

Age and OHCA Risk

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed an approximately linear association between age at HCM diagnosis and OHCA risk (P = .61 for nonlinearity). The hazard of OHCA increased with each 10-year increment in age at diagnosis (HR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.23-1.61; P < .001) (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1).

Sensitivity Analyses

A cause-specific Cox proportional hazards regression model adjusting for duration of time-dependent covariates at baseline showed no significant differences between duration categories, except for 2 ischemic heart disease categories (HR for 90-365 vs < 90 days, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.18-0.72; adjusted P = .02) (eFigure 3 and eTable 2 in Supplement 1). This finding suggests that matching on covariate presence was sufficient, as duration adjustment provided no additional discriminative value. In a sensitivity analysis, using a redefined exposure cohort with exposure set to the second HCM registration date yielded similar estimates, although significant differences in 10-year risk were limited to the group aged 18 to 60 years (1.4% for matched controls vs 2.6% for patients with de novo HCM; risk difference, 1.1% [95% CI, −0.1% to 2.4%]; P = .03) (eFigure 4 and eTables 3 and 4 in Supplement 1).

A sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with a pre-exposure or matching ICD and treating ICD implantation during follow-up as a censoring event yielded similar estimates, with retained significance for 10-year risk for those aged 10 to 60 years (1.3% for matched controls vs 3.0% for patients with de novo HCM; risk difference, 1.6% [95% CI, 0.6%-2.7%]; P < .001) and 61 to 85 years (31.% for matched controls vs 4.4% for patients with de novo HCM; risk difference, 1.3% [95% CI, 0.3%-2.3%]; P = .005) and an additionally significant 5-year risk difference in the group aged 18 to 60 years (0.5% for matched controls vs 1.0% for patients with de novo HCM; risk difference, 0.5% [95% CI, −0.1% to 1.0%]; P = .04) (eFigure 5 and eTables 5 and 6 in Supplement 1). Last, a sensitivity analyses excluding those with prior ventricular arrhythmias produced similar estimates but loss of significance for 10-year risk in the group aged 61 to 85 years (eFigure 6 and eTables 7 and 8 in Supplement 1).

Nested Case-Control Study

A total of 1250 patients with HCM, of whom 250 were case patients with HCM and OHCA (median age, 68 [IQR, 59-76] years; 83 female [33.2%] and 167 male [66.8%]) and 1000 were controls with HCM and no OHCA (median age, 68 [IQR, 59-76] years; 332 female [33.2%] and 668 male [66.8%]), were included in the nested case-control study. Baseline characteristics are listed in Table 2. Case patients with HCM and OHCA showed a higher prevalence of all covariates (comorbidities, medications used, and ICD device implantation) compared with controls with HCM. For instance, 154 case patients (61.6%) had heart failure compared with 250 controls (25.0%). Likewise, 120 case patients (48.0%) had atrial fibrillation compared with 215 controls (21.5%).

Association Between Covariates and the Hazard of OHCA in Case Patients and Controls

Conditional logistic regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for the association between covariates and OHCA in patients with HCM (Figure 3). Both recent (<1 year) and longer-term (≥1 year) heart failure were associated with higher hazards of OHCA relative to the reference group without heart failure (HRs, 3.63 [95% CI, 1.55-8.50] and 2.82 [95% CI, 1.88-4.22], respectively). Only longer-term treatment was associated with increased OHCA hazard for atrial fibrillation (HR, 2.24 [95% CI, 1.48-3.41]) and chronic kidney disease (HR, 1.72 [95% CI, 1.01-2.93]). Presence of liver disease was also associated with an increased OHCA hazard (HR, 2.64 [95% CI, 1.15-6.10]) as well as presence of diabetes (HR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.00-2.51]). Regarding medication use, short term β-blocker treatment (<90 days) was associated with an increased hazard of OHCA (HR, 2.39 [95% CI, 1.34-4.28]). Likewise, treatment with both mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (HR, 1.83 [95% CI, 0.99-3.38]) and acetylsalicylic acid (HR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.05-2.40]) were associated with increased hazards of OHCA.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the long-term risk of OHCA in a nationwide cohort of patients with de novo HCM. A secondary aim was to describe the characteristics of case patients and controls with HCM and to determine their association with OHCA. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the long-term risk of OHCA in patients with de novo HCM.

HCM and the Risk of OHCA

OHCA is a critical condition defined by a sudden cessation of cardiac function resulting in SCD, if unsuccessfully resuscitated.10,11,12 It is well established that HCM is associated with an increased risk of SCD. Contemporary studies estimate that the annual risk of SCD in patients with HCM lies between 0.5% to 1.0%.13,14 However, the risk of OHCA in patients with HCM is unclear, and with an increasing prevalence of patients with HCM in Denmark, this warrants investigation.2

In the cohort study, we estimated the 5- and 10-year risk of OHCA in patients with de novo HCM during a 20-year period, demonstrating a significantly increased 10-year risk of OHCA compared with matched controls. The 10-year risk was highest for patients in the group aged 61 to 85 years (4.3%) compared with patients in the group aged 18 to 60 years (2.8%). These estimates are broadly consistent with contemporary SCD risk estimates in patients with HCM.7

Historically, HCM has been considered a common cause of SCD among young adults, although a recent meta-analysis found HCM to be less common than a structurally normal heart.15 Nevertheless, HCM remains associated with increased risk of SCD, with younger age being an incorporated risk marker in both European and US HCM-related SCD risk stratification models.16,17,18 Interestingly, our study showed OHCA risk was highest in the older group and that older age at diagnosis of HCM was associated with an increased OHCA risk. This apparent discrepancy may reflect differences in age distribution across study populations but also the phenotypic heterogeneity of HCM. Genotype-positive HCM typically presents earlier, is characterized by a more pronounced arrhythmogenic substrate and a higher risk of ventricular arrhythmias, and is more often targeted for prophylactic treatment with an ICD device.19,20,21,22 Conversely, patients with genotype-negative HCM present later and more often with by cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension.23 Thus, the 2 age groups likely reflect differing phenotypic compositions with different mechanisms leading to OHCA. Additionally, underestimation of the OHCA risk due to ICD implantation is likely proportionally higher in the younger group compared with the older group. However, in our sensitivity analysis accounting for ICD implantation, risk estimates remained stable.24

Clinical practice in Denmark follows European guidelines, recommending continuous outpatient follow-up of patients with HCM, including repeated risk assessment for SCD to identify high-risk patients (5-year SCD risk >6%), who are offered ICD device implantation.25 Improved preventive care has reduced the annual SCD risk in Europe from 0.52% before 2010 to 0.33% after 2010.7,26 Nevertheless, despite risk prediction models that have proven effective, their relevance in older patients with HCM remains debatable.27 Likewise, patients without conventional SCD risk factors also carry a nonnegligible SCD risk.14 Therefore, the results of this study remain relevant, providing a contemporary, overall estimate of the long-term risk of OHCA in patients with HCM in a clinical setting.

Comorbidities and Medication Use Associated With the Hazard of OHCA

The clinical course of HCM varies from benign to severe, complicated by progressive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and sudden death.3 The nested case-control study demonstrated that patients with HCM who experienced an OHCA had a higher occurrence of HCM-related conditions such as heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and stroke, compared with patients with HCM who had not experienced an OHCA. Likewise, patients with HCM who experienced an OHCA also had a higher occurrence of non–HCM-related comorbidities, medication use, and first-time ICD implantation. All in all, these results insinuate that a higher risk of OHCA in patients with HCM may also be related to the cumulative burden of other comorbidities known to be associated with an increased risk of OHCA.28,29,30

Results of the conditional logistic regression analyses showed that the HCM-related comorbidities heart failure and atrial fibrillation both were associated with increased hazards of OHCA. Interpretation of these associations is complicated, but they may be markers of disease progression toward a more severe disease manifestation rather than primary arrhythmic substrate markers.28 Our results also showed that the short-term β-blocker and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist treatment were associated with significantly increased hazards of OHCA. However, these findings almost certainly reflect confounding by indication, since treatment initiation typically is targeted to patients with disease progression. Therefore, initiation of these treatments is more likely a marker of clinical disease progression acting as proxy for disease severity rather than a cause of arrest or harm from these medications.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. HCM is a relatively rare disease, despite being one of the most common hereditary cardiac diseases. Thus, a nationwide registry study is an ideal way to investigate a large population with this relatively rare disease. Nevertheless, despite being comprehensive, Danish registers lack information on diagnostic tests such as echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, genetic testing, and 12-lead electrocardiography, prohibiting an extensive cohort description. Furthermore, HCM identification solely based on codes from the International Statistical Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision, introduces risk of misclassification—for example hypertensive hypertrophy—with directional bias depending on the OHCA risk associated with the misclassified condition. However, the Danish National Patient Register has formerly been validated, showing a 90% positive predictive value for the HCM diagnosis.31 Additionally, exclusion of certain explainable phenocopies at the index date was used to minimize the bias of misclassification. Our sensitivity analysis using the second HCM registration as the exposure date showed similar estimates, although with loss of significance likely due to loss of power.

Appropriate ICD therapy in high-risk patients prevents events of OHCA, thereby attenuating risk estimates. However, a sensitivity analysis accounting for ICD devices showed stable estimates, which remained significant. Additionally, the primary outcome only captured OHCAs with a resuscitation attempt, representing a subset of all SCD events, predominantly witnessed cases. Given that resuscitation is more frequently attempted in younger individuals, this likely results in a proportionally greater event capture in the younger group, introducing bias toward underestimation of risk overall, with a disproportionately larger effect in the older group.24 Last, we are missing exact data on the cause of OHCA and cannot determine whether an OHCA was due to other causes than HCM, limiting the specificity of the outcome.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of patients aged 18 to 85 years with de novo HCM and matched controls, we demonstrated that the risk of OHCA was higher in patients with de novo HCM. In the nested case-control study of patients with HCM who did or did not experience an OHCA, heart failure was associated with an increased rate of OHCA. Future research should explore the circumstances surrounding OHCA in patients with HCM compared with patients who experienced an OHCA without HCM to optimize OHCA prevention. Additionally, identifying patients who do not have known HCM at the time of OHCA and are subsequently diagnosed with HCM would provide new insights into how often OHCA represents as the first clinical manifestation of HCM.
