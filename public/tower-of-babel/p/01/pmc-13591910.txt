Fgf evolution in vertebrates: insights from cyclostomes
Authors: Liyan He, Zheng Dong, Baichuan Tong, Suhan Liu, Fujiang Liu, Zixiang Wu, Guang Li, Qingming Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibroblast growth factors (FGFs) are crucial for animal development, growth and physiological regulation. Early vertebrate evolution was shaped by complex whole-genome duplications (WGDs); after a shared first event (1RV), jawed vertebrates underwent a second distinct WGD (2RJV), while cyclostomes (lampreys and hagfishes) experienced a different, lineage-specific genome expansion (2RCY). Despite their key evolutionary position, the FGF gene family in cyclostomes has remained largely uncharacterized, contrasting with extensive research in jawed vertebrates and their invertebrate chordate relatives. To illuminate this knowledge gap, we conducted a comprehensive genomic survey of FGF genes across four cyclostome species, five jawed vertebrates, and an amphioxus outgroup, leveraging newly available cyclostome genomes.ResultsOur analysis surprisingly reveals a significantly reduced FGF repertoire in lampreys (17 genes) and hagfishes (12 genes) compared to jawed vertebrates (22–32 genes). This finding suggests extensive FGF gene loss in cyclostomes following their unique genome duplication history. Phylogenetic and synteny analyses confirm that all eight ancestral FGF subfamilies were first established in the common ancestor of vertebrates. Significantly, we report the discovery of a novel FGF gene, Fgf25, within the FGF4/5/6/25 subfamily, uniquely retained in actinopterygians but lost in sarcopterygians. We also propose a new evolutionary model for Fgf3, suggesting its origin via tandem duplication of an unknown Fgf gene after the 1RV but before the second genome duplication, ultimately leading to the conserved Fgf3-Fgf4-Fgf19 gene linkage in jawed vertebrates.ConclusionsThis detailed characterization of the cyclostome FGF repertoire provides insights into early vertebrate FGF evolution and a valuable resource for future investigations into cyclostome evolution and development. Introduction

Gene duplication, including tandem and whole-genome duplication (WGD), is a major force driving organismal diversity and morphological evolution [1, 2]. Genes encoding signaling molecules, particularly those involved in development, are crucial in gene regulatory networks and contribute to morphological changes or innovations [3–5]. Recent advances in sequencing technology have generated vast genomic datasets from diverse animal species [6, 7], enabling detailed comparative studies of gene families. These studies are essential for establishing gene homology across species and investigating how their functional evolution may have shaped specific morphological traits in different lineages [8, 9].

The fibroblast growth factor (FGF) family comprises signaling molecules that mostly act through FGF receptors (FGFRs) [10]. Specific FGFs are indispensable for a wide array of embryonic developmental processes in humans and mice, governing crucial events such as organ morphogenesis, limb outgrowth, and cellular differentiation. Consequently, disruptions to these FGFs, including those affecting FGF1, FGF2, FGF3, FGF4, FGF8, FGF10, and FGF18, often lead to severe congenital abnormalities, underscoring their critical roles in the proper formation of the heart, auditory system, limbs, lungs, kidneys, and skeletal structures [10–15]. The FGF family in jawed vertebrates is categorized into eight subfamilies based on sequence similarity and function [10, 16, 17], and these subfamilies are further classified into paracrine, endocrine, and intracrine groups [18]. While some studies suggest that all eight subfamilies existed in the common ancestor of chordates [17], others propose that the FGF11/12/13/14 subfamily is specific to tunicates and vertebrates [19, 20]. Cyclostomes, including hagfishes and lampreys, constitute a key vertebrate clade that, as the sister group to gnathostomes, provides critical comparative insight for reconstructing ancestral vertebrate states and genome evolution. Cyclostomes are comparatively understudied relative to gnathostomes, although they are particularly informative for inferring ancestral character states. The hypothesis of two WGD events in cyclostomes was previously interpreted as both duplication events having occurred in the vertebrate ancestor, such that both cyclostomes and gnathostomes inherited the same two WGD events [21–23]. Recent studies indicate that while the first round occurred in their common ancestor (1RV), the second round was independent, with jawed vertebrates undergoing allopolyploidization (2RJV) and cyclostomes experiencing a unique hexaploidization event (2RCY) [24–28]. Specifically, the cyclostome genome resulted from a tetraploid ancestor hybridizing with a diploid [27, 28]. This complex evolutionary history has hindered the establishment of clear gene correspondence between cyclostomes and jawed vertebrates, necessitating meticulous, gene-family-specific investigations.

While individual Fgf genes in cyclostomes have been studied for their expressions and potential functions in previous studies [29–35], their homology and phylogenetic relationships with gnathostome Fgf genes have not been examined systematically. Most research has focused on jawed vertebrates and amphioxus, leaving a gap in our understanding of Fgf evolution in vertebrates [36–40]. Therefore, this study aims to address the following questions: What is the complete repertoire of Fgf genes in cyclostome genomes? How do these genes relate phylogenetically to those in jawed vertebrates and amphioxus? And how has the unique genome evolution of cyclostomes influenced their FGF gene family? To answer these questions, we performed a comprehensive analysis of cyclostome Fgf genes using whole-genome identification, phylogenetic analysis, and synteny analysis. We identified 17 and 12 Fgf genes in lampreys and hagfish, respectively. Notably, cyclostomes possess Fgf genes representing all eight jawed vertebrate FGF subfamilies, except for the absence of the FGF3 subfamily in hagfish. By integrating recent findings on cyclostome genome duplication, we reconciled our findings with previous experimental data on cyclostome Fgf genes. Furthermore, we compared Fgf genes across chordates, including an amphioxus Branchiostoma floridae, to propose a refined homology framework and develop a new hypothesis regarding the origin of Fgf3 and Fgf3-Fgf4-Fgf19 cluster in vertebrates.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of FGF genes across 10 species

The genomes of Homo sapiens (GCF_000001405.40), Mus musculus (GCF_000001635.27), Danio rerio (GCF_000002035.5), Lepisosteus oculatus (GCF_000242695.1), Carcharodon carcharias (GCF_017639515.1), and Petromyzon marinus (GCF_010993605.1) were obtained from NCBI GenBank; those of Eptatretus burgeri and Lampetra morii are available at the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC): GWHFHHI00000000.1 and GWHFHHE00000000.1; the genome of B. floridae was retrieved from the Science Data Bank (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.08801); and the genome of Eptatretus atami was obtained from Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10227718). Of the four cyclostome species included in this study, the lampreys are P. marinus and L. morii, and the hagfishes are E. burgeri and E. atami.

Hidden Markov model (HMM) files corresponding to the conserved domains of various gene families were obtained from the PFAM database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) (Supplementary Table 1). We employed BLAST (v2.16.0) and HMMER (v3.3.2) [41] to identify protein members of the FGF gene family along with six other gene families (Ephrin, Wnt, Hedgehog, Jagged, Delta, and TGF-β) across 10 species. First, known sequences from each human gene family were used as queries in BLASTP (E < 1e-5) to identify candidate homologs in other species. Next, HMMER’s hmmsearch (with an E-value threshold set at 1e-5) was used to perform HMM searches on the conserved domains of each gene family, selecting proteins that contain the respective conserved domains and considering these genes as candidate genes. Finally, for the FGF gene family, we used the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Batch CD-Search tools (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) and Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) to further confirm whether the FGF domain was present. ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to analyze the physicochemical properties of FGF proteins from the four cyclostome species, including protein length (in amino acids), molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), and instability index. Subcellular localization predictions were performed using the online tool WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequences of the FGF gene family members from the 10 species were aligned using MAFFT (v7.526) [42] with the FFT-NS-2 strategy. The alignments were then trimmed using ClipKIT (v 2.7.0) [43]. Phylogenetic tree was then inferred from the trimmed alignments using IQ-TREE2 (v2.3.5) [44] with the maximum likelihood (ML) method, employing the ModelFinder Plus (-m MFP) option to automatically select the best-fitting amino acid substitution model. Branch supports were assessed by the ultrafast bootstrap approximation (UFBoot; -B 1000) and the SH-aLRT support (-alrt 1000). Finally, the trees were visualized in Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL v6) [45].

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis

GFF of E. burgeri and L. morii genomes are sourced from the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC): GWHFHHI00000000.1 and GWHFHHE00000000.1, while the GFF file of P. marinus genome (GCF_010993605.1) was obtained from NCBI, and that of E. atami genome (10227719) was downloaded from Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/records/10227719). Using information from GFF annotation files of four cyclostome species, we constructed schematic diagrams of the exon-intron structures of Fgf genes. Conserved motifs within FGF proteins were identified using the MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) with the maximum number of motifs set to 10 and all other parameters kept at their default values; subsequently, the results were visualized using TBtools-II [46].

Protein structure prediction analysis

All FGF amino acid sequences from L. morii and E. burgeri were used as input for structural prediction via the AlphaFold Server [47] (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The predicted protein structures were subsequently visualized using PyMOL (version 3.1.0).

Gene synteny analysis

Broccoli (v1.2) [48] was used to analyze amino acid sequences from 10 species, identifying orthogroups containing sets of homologous genes across these species. GFF files from B. floridae, four cyclostomes, and M. musculus were processed using a custom script. This script first reconstructs chromosomal gene orders from the GFF files. It then extracts all genes within a user-defined genomic range (i.e., number of genes) surrounding each target Fgf gene. Finally, using orthogroups identified by Broccoli, the script matches orthologous genes within these flanking regions across species. This pipeline quantifies syntenic conservation around Fgf genes to infer orthology relationships.

Genome-wide identification of FGF genes in Lethenteron camtschaticum

We first downloaded the genome assembly of L. camtschaticum from NCBI (GCA_018977245.1). The genome was then annotated using the NCBI Eukaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline EGAPx (https://github.com/ncbi/egapx) to produce the corresponding GTF file (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.32075085). Subsequently, FGF protein sequences from four cyclostome species L. morii, P. marinus, E. burgeri, and E. atami were used as queries to perform tBLASTn searches against the L. camtschaticum genome. The resulting hits were mapped to the GTF file to identify candidate FGF genes. Finally, phylogenetic analysis was performed using FGF protein sequences from all five cyclostome species including L. camtschaticum. The phylogenetic methods followed those described above. Based on the phylogenetic tree, the FGF genes in L. camtschaticum were named accordingly.

Transcriptomic expression analysis of Fgf genes in cyclostomes

Embryonic transcriptome data for P. marinus were sourced from NCBI GEO (accession # GSE205436). Corresponding raw data for E. burgeri and L. camtschaticum were obtained from the ENA database (project PRJNA371391). Raw sequencing reads from all species underwent preprocessing using fastp (v0.23.2) [49] to remove adapter sequences, reads with ambiguous bases (Ns), and low-quality reads. These filtered reads were then aligned to their respective reference genomes using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [50] under default settings. Gene expression quantification was performed using featureCounts (v2.0.3) [51] with parameters -t exon -g gene_name to generate read counts. Expression levels were normalized and reported as transcripts per million (TPM). Finally, TPM data were used to generate transcriptomic expression heatmaps employing the R package pheatmap.

Results

Identification of Fgf genes in nine chordates

To investigate the number of Fgf genes in four cyclostome species, we employed BLASTP and HMMER search to identify Fgf genes in ten species, including five representative jawed vertebrates (H. sapiens, M. musculus, D. rerio, L. oculatus, C. carcharias), four cyclostomes (L. morii, P. marinus, E. burgeri, E. atami) and a cephalochordate (B. floridae). Because cyclostomes undergo programmed DNA elimination (PDE) during early development, we further examined whether any of the identified Fgf genes were subject to this process. Among the Fgf genes identified in our analysis, only LOC116937341 in P. marinus was found to be a PDE gene, and we have also included its protein sequence in Supplementary File. Therefore, we excluded this gene and retained only those Fgf genes that remain in the somatic genome after PDE for subsequent analyses [52, 53]. In addition, we analyzed the chromosomal locations, amino acid sequence lengths, molecular weights, theoretical isoelectric points (pI), instability indices, and subcellular localizations of the Fgf family members in the four cyclostome species (Supplementary Table 2). Our gene identification results showed that both lamprey species possess 17 Fgf genes and both hagfish species possess 12, whereas jawed vertebrates harbor between 22 and 32 Fgf genes, indicating that cyclostomes have generally fewer Fgf genes than the five jawed vertebrates (Table 1). It should be noted that the P. marinus Fgf7 gene and the E. atami Fgf9/16/20 gene appear to lack portions of their functional domains (Figs. S13D, S14B), likely due to inaccuracies in genome annotation or gene prediction in the published assemblies. Future studies focusing on these genes should take this limitation into consideration.

To assess whether this lower gene number in cyclostomes is specific to the FGF family or also occurs in other signaling gene families, we identified members of six additional gene families Ephrin, Wnt, Hedgehog, Jagged, Delta, and TGF-β. Our analyses revealed that the gene content of these six families in cyclostomes is largely comparable to that in jawed vertebrates, whereas only the FGF family exhibits significantly reduced gene content in cyclostomes relative to jawed vertebrates (Supplementary Table 3).

Cyclostomes underwent hexaploidization during the second whole genome duplication (2RCY) [27, 28], a process expected to produce more ohnologs of Fgf genes compared to the tetraploidization (2RJV) experienced by jawed vertebrates. Our findings therefore revealed significant gene loss in the FGF family of cyclostomes following their genome duplication events. Additionally, hagfishes have five fewer Fgf genes than lampreys, indicating a greater degree of Fgf gene loss.

Homology of Fgf genes between cyclostomes and jawed vertebrates

Our phylogenetic analysis indicated that, except for B. floridae, each FGF subfamily in the remaining nine species forms a monophyletic group, supporting the established classification of Fgf genes into eight subfamilies: FGF1/2, FGF3, FGF4/5/6/25, FGF7/10/22, FGF8/17/18/24, FGF9/16/20, FGF11/12/13/14, and FGF19/21/23 (Fig. 1; Fig. S1) [17]. Although some nodes exhibit relatively low ultrafast bootstrap support percentages, which is expected for the FGF family given its ancient divergences and high sequence conservation across subfamilies, the overall topology remains robust and consistent with previous classifications. Synteny diagrams were constructed to illustrate the syntenic relationships of flanking genes for these subfamilies between the two cyclostome species (L. morii and E. burgeri) and M. musculus (Figs. 2 and 3). To further validate the structural and functional characteristics of Fgf genes in cyclostomes, we performed a systematic analysis of the gene architecture, conserved protein motifs, and three-dimensional protein structures of cyclostome Fgf genes (Fig. 4; Figs. S10–S12). By integrating phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1; Fig. S1), syntenic relationships (Figs. 2 and 3), and protein motif analysis (Fig. 4), we systematically assigned nomenclature to the Fgf genes in cyclostomes (Supplementary Table 4). We also attempted to clarify how the members of each subfamily are related to each other in jawed vertebrates, reflecting their 1RV and 2RJV histories.

Within the FGF1/2 subfamily, the evolutionary path of jawed vertebrate Fgf1 and Fgf2 genes remains unresolved based on our phylogenetic analysis. One possibility is that an ancestral subfamily gene duplicated during the 1RV, with one copy subsequently lost and the remaining copy duplicated during the 2RJV to produce Fgf1 and Fgf2. Alternatively, two gene losses may have occurred after the 2RJV. Each cyclostome retains a single ohnolog of Fgf1/2. Synteny analysis further supports homology between Fgf1 and Fgf2 in M. musculus and Fgf1/2 in L. morii and E. burgeri (Figs. 2E and 3E). The lamprey Fgf1/2 genes and hagfish Fgf1/2 genes are highly similar in their motif structures, all of which have six shared motifs (Fig. 4).

Within the FGF3 subfamily, jawed vertebrates and lampreys possess a single Fgf3 gene. Interestingly, hagfishes have lost Fgf3, this loss most likely occurred on the hagfish lineage after the split between hagfishes and lampreys (See next section). Our alignment shows that FGF3 proteins share a highly conserved region preceding the FGF domain in all included species (Fig. S13B), and this region is not conserved in other FGF proteins. Most jawed vertebrates have retained a highly conserved three-gene cluster including Fgf3, Fgf4 and Fgf19 [54]. In lampreys, we found the only Fgf3 copy is next to Fgf4/6a, forming a two-gene cluster. In addition, Fgf4/6b and Fgf19/21b, Fgf4/6c and Fgf19/21a form two other Fgf clusters in lampreys (Fig. 2D). Since our phylogenetic tree strongly supports a single origin of Fgf3 in vertebrates, it is likely that multiple losses of Fgf3 paralogs happened during the vertebrate genome duplication events. This evolutionary process is further discussed in the next section regarding the origin and evolution of vertebrate Fgf3.

Within the FGF4/5/6/25 subfamily, Fgf4/6 and Fgf5/25 in jawed vertebrates originated from the 1RV (Fig. 1, FGF4/5/6/25 branch). Although Fgf5 and Fgf25 do not cluster in our phylogenetic tree, the strong bootstrap support (100%) for the Fgf4/6 clade suggests that Fgf5/25 represents the other ohnolog resulting from the 1RV. Notably, our phylogenetic analysis reclassifies Fgf25 as a new member of the subfamily, which was previously annotated as fgf9 in D. rerio and C. carcharias. A previous study identified the four-residue sequence “VGIG” in the beginning of FGF domain as unique to FGF4/5/6/25 proteins [55]. Our alignment further indicates that the “GIG” sequence is conserved across proteins in the FGF4/5/6/25 subfamily (except amphioxus), supporting the classification of Fgf25 within this subfamily (Fig. S13C). This gene is present in D. rerio, L. oculatus, and C. carcharias, but absent from the coelacanth (GCF_037176945.1) and lungfish (GCF_019279795.1) genomes, as determined through BLAST analysis. This suggests that the gene was lost in the common ancestor of extant sarcopterygians. Lampreys have three Fgf4/6 ohnologs and two Fgf5/25 ohnologs, while the two hagfish species retain two Fgf4/6 ohnologs but no Fgf5/25 ohnolog. Synteny analysis also unambiguously supports this homology assessment, with cyclostome Fgf4/6 orthologs sharing many syntenic genes with either Fgf4 or Fgf6 of M. musculus (Figs. 2D and 3D). Fgf5 of M. musculus shares no syntenic genes with either Fgf4 or Fgf6, but shows syntenic similarity to lamprey Fgf5/25 (Fig. 2D), suggesting that ancestral Fgf5/25 experienced genomic rearrangement after the 1RV but before the 2RJV and 2RCY.

Within the FGF7/10/22 subfamily, Fgf7 and Fgf10/22 in jawed vertebrates originated from the 1RV, while the 2RJV ohnolog of Fgf7 was lost (Fig. 1, FGF7/10/22 subfamily branch). Phylogenetic analysis shows that lampreys have retained single orthologs of both Fgf7 and Fgf10/22. In M. musculus and L. morii, Fgf7 is syntenic with Dut, Shf, and Fbn1, which do not show synteny with Fgf10 or Fgf22 in M. musculus (Fig. 2C). On the other hand, L. morii Fgf10/22 is syntenic with Hcn1/2, which is syntenic with both Fgf10 and Fgf22 in M. musculus (Fig. 2C). Hagfishes retain two copies of Fgf10/22 and lack ortholog of Fgf7, based on phylogenetic evidence. One copy, named Fgf10/22a, exhibits syntenic similarities to both Fgf7 and Fgf10/22 in M. musculus (Fig. 2C). However, we retained its nomenclature based on strong bootstrap support (96%) for its clustering with lamprey Fgf10/22 genes (Fig. 1, FGF7/10/22 subfamily branch). The other copy is clustered with the Fgf10/22 genes of lamprey and the Fgf10/22a gene of hagfish, and is positioned as an outgroup. Based on this, we named it Fgf10/22b. This nomenclature is also supported by the shared six motifs between Fgf10/22a and Fgf10/22b in two hagfishes (Figs. 4C, D).

Within the FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily, Fgf8/17 and Fgf18/24 in jawed vertebrates originated from the 1RV. Fgf24 is retained only in ray-finned fish (e.g., D. rerio and L. oculatus) and lost in tetrapods, consistent with a previous study [56]. Each cyclostome species contains two genes in this subfamily: one orthologous to Fgf18/24 and another that does not cluster with either Fgf8/17 or Fgf18/24 in our phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1, FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily branch, Fig. S6). Due to insufficient synteny (Figs. 2B and 3B) and motif (Fig. 4) information, this gene could not be definitively assigned to either Fgf8/17 or Fgf18/24 and was subsequently designated Fgf8/17/18/24. Genes within this subfamily contain five exons, exceeding the exon count of other subfamilies (Fig. 4). Moreover, all FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily proteins harbor motif 9 immediately upstream of the core domain (Fig. 4). Structural modeling (Figs. S11, S12) indicates that this motif adopts an α-helical conformation.

Within the FGF9/16/20 subfamily, Fgf9/20 and Fgf16 in jawed vertebrates originated from the 1RV, whereas the 2RJV ohnolog of Fgf16 was subsequently lost (Fig. 1, FGF9/16/20 subfamily branch, Fig. S7). However, phylogenetic analysis shows that all cyclostome sequences are positioned basal to the gnathostome Fgf9/20 and Fgf16 clades and do not form a well-supported monophyletic group with either lineage. Therefore, a one-to-one orthology relationship between cyclostome genes and gnathostome Fgf9/20 or Fgf16 cannot be confidently established. Following a conservative nomenclature strategy, we thus designate all cyclostome members of this subfamily as Fgf9/16/20. Copy number analysis indicates that lampreys retain two Fgf9/16/20 paralogs, whereas hagfishes retain only a single Fgf9/16/20 paralog.

Within the FGF11/12/13/14 subfamily, phylogenetic analysis could not definitively resolve the relationships between cyclostome and jawed vertebrate genes (Fig. 1, FGF11/12/13/14 branch). Consequently, all cyclostome genes were tentatively designated as Fgf11/12/13/14. Lampreys possess two paralogs of this subfamily, while hagfishes retain three, making this the only subfamily in which hagfishes have more copies than lampreys. Synteny analysis revealed a conserved genomic organization between cyclostomes and mouse across all Fgf11/12/13/14 genes, supporting their homology (Figs. 2F and 3F). Fgf11/12/13/14a genes in cyclostomes have lost two motifs compared to Fgf11/12/13/14b and Fgf11/12/13/14c (Fig. 4), suggesting functional divergence within the subfamily. In each cyclostome FGF11/12/13/14 protein, motif 8 is positioned immediately downstream of the core β-trefoil fold. Structural predictions suggest that motif 8 forms a β-strand contiguous with this fold (Figs. S11, S12) [57], distinguishing this subfamily’s core domain from other FGFs and potentially contributing to its function as an autocrine FGF.

Within the Fgf19/21/23 subfamily, Fgf19/21 and Fgf23 in jawed vertebrates originated from the 1RV, while the 2RJV ohnolog of Fgf23 was lost (Fig. 1, FGF19/21/23 branch). Phylogenetic analysis indicates that cyclostomes lack any Fgf23 ortholog. Lampreys have retained two ohnologs of Fgf19/21, both clustering with an Fgf4/6 ohnolog in synteny (Fig. 2D). In contrast, the two hagfish species possess only one copy of Fgf19/21, which also clusters with an Fgf4/6 ohnolog in synteny (Fig. 3D). These homology assessments are further supported by synteny analyses, which show a greater similarity between cyclostome Fgf19/21 genes and Fgf19 (referred to as Fgf15 in M. musculus) and Fgf21 in M. musculus than with Fgf23 (Figs. 2D and 3D). The Fgf19/21 genes in all four cyclostome species exhibit identical exon organization, each containing three exons (Fig. 4). Additionally, FGF19/21A proteins from both lamprey species share the same conserved motifs, whereas lamprey FGF19/21B proteins and hagfish FGF19/21 proteins possess an identical set of conserved motifs (Fig. 4).

Fgf3 and Fgf11/12/13/14 orthologs are probably absent in amphioxus

Amphioxus is used as an outgroup for vertebrates. It didn’t undergo the two rounds of whole-genome duplication and lacks many of the derived features observed in tunicates. Consequently, it is widely used as a representative lineage in comparative studies of vertebrate genome evolution [58]. It contains eight Fgf genes, which some studies suggest correspond to the eight subfamilies found in vertebrates [17, 19]. Interestingly, previous studies have shown that there is a clear homolog of Fgf11/12/13/14 in tunicates [20], suggesting that this subfamily originated in common ancestors of tunicates and vertebrates. To explore the homology between amphioxus and vertebrate Fgf genes, we conducted a genome-wide analysis using the recently published B. floridae genome [59]. This analysis consistently identified eight Fgf genes in B. floridae (Table 1). Our phylogenetic tree confirms that amphioxus contains homologs of the vertebrate Fgf1/2, Fgf8/17/18/24, and Fgf9/16/20 subfamilies (Fig. 1), consistent with previous phylogeny and synteny analyses [17, 19]. The remaining Fgf genes in amphioxus, designated FgfA–FgfE previously [19], were further investigated to clarify their evolutionary relationships with vertebrate Fgf subfamilies.

FgfB and FgfE are located near each other in amphioxus genomes, an arrangement reminiscent of the tandemly arrayed Fgf3, Fgf4/5/6/25, and Fgf19/21/23 subfamilies in cyclostomes and jawed vertebrates (Figs. 2D and 3D) [54]. Previous studies proposed FgfB and FgfE to be homologous to the vertebrate FGF3 gene and the FGF4/5/6/25 subfamily, respectively [17, 19]. However, the evolutionary origin of the Fgf3-Fgf4-Fgf19 cluster in jawed vertebrates, and thus the precise relationship of FgfB and FgfE to it, has remained unresolved. For instance, our phylogenetic analysis could not definitively assign FgfB and FgfE to specific counterparts within this vertebrate cluster (Fig. 1). Notably, a recent study on chordate Fgf genes reported a similar placement of amphioxus FgfB and FgfE outside the FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily branch [20]. Despite these phylogenetic limitations, our synteny analysis provided a clearer resolution (Fig. 5A). The conserved synteny supports the homology between the amphioxus FgfB–FgfE cluster and the mouse Fgf3–Fgf4–Fgf19 cluster, as indicated by multiple shared neighboring genes, including Ruvbl2, Foxm1, Itfg2, C12orf4, and Gtf2h1 (Fig. 5A).

Based on this cluster homology assessment, we proposed two potential scenarios for the formation of the three-gene cluster in modern jawed vertebrates (Fig. 5B and C). In the first scenario, amphioxus FgfB and FgfE are homologous to any two genes from the Fgf3-Fgf4/5/6/25-Fgf19/21/23 cluster in mouse (Fig. 5B). A tandem duplication event then gave rise to the three-gene cluster before the 1RV, followed by the loss of at least four Fgf genes in jawed vertebrates: one after 1RV and three after the 2RJV (Fig. 5B). In the second scenario, amphioxus FgfB and FgfE are considered homologous to Fgf4/5/6/25 and Fgf19/21/23, respectively. In this scenario, the Fgf4/5/6/25-Fgf19/21/23 cluster first duplicated during the 1RV. Fgf3 then arose through a tandem duplication event within one of these clusters, with only three subsequent Fgf gene losses required following the 2RJV to produce the current pattern in mouse (Fig. 5C). The second scenario is more parsimonious compared to the first one (3 losses vs. 4 losses). In addition, the second scenario is consistent with the absence of Fgf3 in tunicates [17], eliminating the need for additional Fgf3 gene losses as previously postulated [20]. It is worth noting that vertebrate FGF3 proteins possess a highly conserved region preceding the FGF domain (Fig. S13), while this domain is not found in FGFB or FGFE. Therefore, we concluded that Fgf4/5/6/25 (FgfB) and Fgf19/21/23 (FgfE) are present in amphioxus. However, as a vertebrate-specific gene, the precise origin of Fgf3 remains unresolved based on current evidence. It may have arisen through a tandem duplication event involving an ancestral gene from either the FGF19/21/23 or FGF4/5/6/25 subfamily. Nevertheless, phylogenetic analyses consistently place the FGF3 subfamily within the same clade as FGF7/10/22, raising the additional possibility that Fgf3 originated from a tandem duplication of an ancestral gene in the FGF7/10/22 subfamily. In addition, we also examined the tunicate genomes and found no instances where any two FGF genes are arranged in tandem or located in close proximity. Although tunicates possess a clear homolog of the FGF4/5/6 subfamily, no homolog of the FGF19/21/23 subfamily has been identified in tunicates [20], making it difficult to assess whether an ancestral Fgf cluster was present in this lineage. As a result, amphioxus FgfB and FgfE remain the best available candidates for the ancestral homologs of the vertebrate FGF4/5/6/25 and FGF19/21/23 subfamilies. Nevertheless, it should be noted that these two genes do not cluster with the corresponding vertebrate subfamilies in our phylogenetic analysis, and this interpretation should therefore be regarded as the most parsimonious scenario at present.

The presence of Fgf3 in lampreys suggests that Fgf3 originated in the vertebrate ancestor after the 1RV but before the divergence of jawed vertebrates and cyclostomes. Drawing on the post-1RV Fgf gene linkage patterns in vertebrates, the arrangement of Fgf genes in L. morii, and the cyclostome-specific second-round genome triplication (2RCY), we proposed the following scenario to reconstruct the evolutionary events that led to the arrangement of Fgf gene clusters in lampreys. This scenario also followed the principle of parsimony, requiring the fewest gene loss events to account for the observed pattern in extant lampreys. Immediately after the 1RV and the origin of Fgf3, the vertebrate ancestor possessed two distinct gene clusters: one containing Fgf3, Fgf4/5/6/25, and Fgf19/21/23, and another containing only Fgf4/5/6/25 and Fgf19/21/23. Following the divergence of jawed vertebrates and cyclostomes, the cyclostome ancestor retained two paralogous clusters, one of which lost its ancestral Fgf19/21/23 gene, leaving only Fgf4/5/6/25. Before the 2RCY, one of the cyclostome ancestors underwent tetraploidization, doubling the two clusters to four. In one of these clusters, the loss of Fgf3 resulted in a group containing only Fgf4/5/6/25 and Fgf19/21/23 homologs. Subsequently, fusion between tetraploid and diploid genomes produced a hexaploid genome, in which: 1). One cluster lost Fgf3 but retained the linkage between Fgf4/6b and Fgf19/21b; 2). Another cluster lost its Fgf19/21/23 homolog but retained the linkage between Fgf3 and Fgf4/6a; 3). A third cluster lost its sole Fgf4/5/6/25 homolog (Fig. 5D).

Previous synteny analysis has suggested that FgfA is orthologous to the ancestor of the vertebrate FGF7/10/22 subfamily [19]. Using synteny comparisons between amphioxus and hagfish, we found that amphioxus FgfA shares more syntenic genes with hagfish Fgf10/22a compared to other Fgf genes (Fig. S15), thus supporting the previous homology assessment. FgfC and FgfD have been proposed as homologous to the Fgf19/21/23 and Fgf11/12/13/14 subfamilies, respectively [17]. However, our analysis above no longer supports the FgfC homology; instead, it suggests a relationship between amphioxus FgfE and vertebrate Fgf19/21/23. Similarly, no synteny or phylogenetic evidence supports homology between FgfD and vertebrate Fgf11/12/13/14 [20] (Fig. 1). In summary, our findings, summarized in Fig. 6, indicate that the eight Fgf subfamilies in vertebrates were established in their common ancestor after the 1RV, while amphioxus possesses six orthologs corresponding to these subfamilies. Notably, amphioxus FgfC and FgfD exhibit no clear homology with any vertebrate FGF subfamily.

Discussions

Challenges in FGF family phylogenetics

The inherent characteristics of FGF proteins, such as their small size and limited phylogenetic information, pose a significant challenge for constructing robust phylogenetic trees. These issues often result in low bootstrap support, especially within certain subfamilies [39, 60–62]. To overcome this, we employed a maximum likelihood approach to build a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1). We complemented this with synteny and sequence analyses to enhance the accuracy of our findings. Additionally, we performed synteny analysis on the spotted gar genome, which independently validated our homology assessments in the mouse genome and thus was not presented as a separate finding.

The evolutionary history of the FGF family is notably complex. Several fgf genes are arranged in clusters, yet they don’t seem to have originated from simple tandem duplications. This complexity, combined with a weak phylogenetic signal, suggests that the evolutionary hypothesis presented in this study is one of several possibilities. It’s likely that we may never fully resolve the complete evolutionary history of this gene family [17, 18, 62]. To gain a clearer understanding of the FGF family’s evolutionary path, future research should integrate data from a broader range of species and use more comprehensive analytical methods.

Nevertheless, our study revealed several lineages that are robustly supported. For instance, the FGF3 subfamily shows sufficiently high bootstrap support (100%) and a well-resolved branching pattern in our phylogenetic analyses (Fig. 1). In addition, Fgf25, identified here as a novel member, is clearly supported to have been lost in the common ancestor of extant sarcopterygians. However, certain uncertainties remain. For example, although the Fgf4/6 clade exhibits strong bootstrap support, Fgf5 and Fgf25 do not cluster together in our phylogenetic tree. Thus, it remains unclear whether they originated from the same ancestral gene (Fgf5/25), and it cannot be definitively concluded that Fgf5/25 represents the ohnolog of Fgf4/6 generated during the 1RV. The support from synteny analysis is also not definitive, as Fgf5/25 seem to have undergone extensive rearrangements (Fig. 2D). Similarly, the formation of the Fgf3–Fgf4/5/6/25–Fgf19/21/23 cluster should also be considered with caution, since FGF3 subfamily is not clustered with FGF4/5/6/25 subfamily. Our proposed scenario is the most parsimonious based on present data and should be tested when more data is available in the future.

Current Knowledge of Fgf Genes in Cyclostomes

Given the critical role of Fgf genes in vertebrate embryonic development [10, 12, 13, 15, 63, 64], previous studies have examined the expression patterns of various cyclostome Fgf genes to explore their contributions to vertebrate innovations [29–32, 34, 35, 65, 66]. However, the precise homology between these Fgf genes and their vertebrate counterparts remains unclear due to previous conflicting perspectives on the nature of whole genome duplications in ancestral vertebrates. To date, expression patterns in cyclostomes have been examined for only Fgf3 and Fgf18/24, with most studies focusing on Fgf18/24 [29–35]. In hagfish E. burgeri, Fgf18/24 is the only FGF gene whose expression has been characterized by in situ hybridization, localizing to the mid-hindbrain boundary (MHB) and the naso-hypophyseal plate [65, 66]. In lampreys, expression patterns of both Fgf18/24 and Fgf3 have been analyzed across various species and developmental stages. For instance, in Lethenteron camtschaticum, Fgf18/24 transcripts are initially detected in the perioral ectoderm at the early neurula stage [32]. This expression persists ventrally in the forebrain of stage 24 embryos, expands into the ventral anterior telencephalon by stage 26 [34], and later localizes to the MHB by stages 28–30 [66]. Similarly, in Lampetra fluviatilis neurulae, Fgf18/24 expression is observed at the MHB, and by stage 27, strong expression is also evident at the rostral tip of the telencephalon, hypothalamus, and dorsal diencephalon [29]. In P. marinus, Fgf18/24 is expressed at the MHB and presomitic mesoderm in stage 20 embryos. By stage 21, its activity is noted in the pharyngeal region; at stage 23.5, high expression levels are observed in the pharyngeal endoderm, developing pharyngeal pouches, stomodeal ectoderm, and the pharyngeal ectoderm surrounding the first pouch. This expression pattern continues through stage 28 in the oral ectoderm and the ectoderm surrounding the branchial clefts [31, 33]. Regarding Fgf3 in P. marinus, its expression is initially detected in the pharynx of stage 23 embryos. While it disappears from the anterior pharyngeal pouch by stage 26, expression is maintained in the pharyngeal endoderm and posterior pouches [31].

The current study also identifies an additional cyclostome ohnolog, designated Fgf8/17/18/24, which belongs to the FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily. Investigating the expression of this gene in cyclostomes is therefore crucial for further comparative investigation on the FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily in jawed vertebrates. In addition, the preceding summary highlights that the expression patterns of most Fgf genes in cyclostomes remain largely uncharacterized, underscoring the need for future investigations in this area. Furthermore, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis including this PDE‑associated gene (LOC116937341) together with all P. marinus Fgf genes. The analysis indicates that this PDE gene falls within the FGF8/17/18/24 subfamily and clusters robustly with other members of this group; accordingly, we designate it Fgf8/17/18/24PDE (Fig. S17). The expression and potential function of this unusual Fgf gene should also be taken into account in future comparative analyses of cyclostomes and jawed vertebrates.

Potential significance of Fgf3 loss in hagfish

Fgf3, along with some other members of the FGF family, is recognized as a critical factor in inner ear development across all vertebrate groups studied [67]. This established role prompted our investigation into whether Fgf3 loss in hagfish, which possess a distinctive inner ear with only a single semicircular canal [68], contributed to this unique morphology.

In Fgf3−/−/Fgf10+/− mouse mutants, only a single semicircular canal develops, while the other two canals are either absent or fused into an abnormal duct, unlike the three distinct canals observed in wild-type embryos [68]. Similarly, in chick embryos, reducing Fgf3 expression via siRNA-mediated knockdown results in incomplete otic placode invagination [69]. Furthermore, inhibiting FGF receptor (FGFR) signaling blocks semicircular canal formation in chicks: moderate inhibition impairs posterior canal development, while high-level inhibition prevents all three canals from forming [70]. In zebrafish, while single fgf3 or fgf8a mutants show variable otic vesicle sizes, fgf3−/−/fgf8a−/− double mutants completely lack the otic placode [67]. Moreover, overexpressing fgf3 in zebrafish at 72 h post-fertilization (hpf) induces complete double-anterior ear duplications, characterized by posterior otolith mispositioning and loss of the posterior saccular macula [71]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Fgf3 is indispensable for semicircular canal development and morphogenesis in jawed vertebrate.

To investigate the potential function of Fgf3 in cyclostomes, we analyzed its expression using published transcriptome data from early developmental stages of three species [72, 73]. After processing raw counts to transcripts per million (TPM) (Supplementary Table 5), we visualized gene expression profiles using heatmaps (Fig. S18). These data show very low absolute expression of Fgf3 in P. marinus embryos at the T11 (blastula) stage. In contrast, L. camtschaticum embryos show appreciable Fgf3 expression between stages 16 and 22 (Fig. S18A). However, these expression data may not be informative for assessing Fgf3’s role in inner ear development. In P. marinus, the otic vesicle forms between stages 13–17 [74], yet our transcriptomic sampling was limited to the earlier blastula stage (Fig. S18B). Likewise, otic vesicle and semicircular canal morphogenesis in L. camtschaticum mainly occurs later, during the larval 9–14 mm period (stages 30–35) [68, 75], beyond the stages covered by the available transcriptomes. Previous work on P. marinus also reported Fgf3 expression only in the pharyngeal region at later stages (23–26.5) [31], again outside the critical window for otic development. Therefore, existing data are insufficient to determine Fgf3’s role in lamprey otic vesicle and canal formation, leaving its function in these structures unresolved.

Given that hagfish lacks Fgf3 and have only a single semicircular canal [68], and considering the unresolved role of Fgf3 in lamprey inner ear development, we propose two alternative hypotheses. First, if Fgf3 is essential for otic vesicle and semicircular canal development in lampreys, its function likely originated in the vertebrate ancestor and was subsequently lost in hagfish, leading to their simplified inner ear. Second, if Fgf3 is not involved in these processes in lampreys, its crucial role in semicircular canal formation may represent a gnathostome-specific innovation. This hypothesis could potentially be resolved by knocking out Fgf3 in P. marinus [31, 33].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive genomic framework for FGF gene family evolution across key chordate lineages. We have demonstrated a notably reduced Fgf complement in cyclostomes (17 genes in lampreys, 12 in hagfish) compared to jawed vertebrates, a result of substantial gene loss following genome duplications. Despite these losses, our phylogenetic and synteny analyses confirm that the eight Fgf subfamilies were first established in the common ancestor of all vertebrates. The amphioxus genome, which notably lacks clear orthologs for the Fgf3 and Fgf11/12/13/14 subfamilies, further illuminates the pre-vertebrate context for the evolution of this critical gene family.

Building on these findings, we propose a detailed evolutionary model regarding the origin of Fgf3 gene. This model posits that an ancestral Fgf4/5/6/25 and Fgf19/21/23 gene configuration underwent tandem duplication and rearrangement after early vertebrate whole-genome duplications, creating the ancestral Fgf3-Fgf4/5/6/25-Fgf19/21/23 cluster. Subsequent lineage-specific genome duplications and extensive differential gene losses, particularly exemplified by the specific absence of Fgf3 in hagfish following their divergence from lampreys, ultimately shaped the diverse Fgf repertoires observed in extant vertebrates. This comprehensive evolutionary synthesis has enabled us to formulate two specific hypotheses concerning the role of Fgf3 in vertebrate otic vesicle development and evolution. These hypotheses offer clear avenues for future experimental validation, promising deeper insights into the functional consequences of FGF gene family evolution.
