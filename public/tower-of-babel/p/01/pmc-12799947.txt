TMEtyper: A computational method for tumor microenvironment subtyping with applications in immunotherapy
Authors: Yaru Miao, Tong Zhou, Yan Li, Jie Yao, Yanghui Bi, Ruiping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a critical determinant of outcomes in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. However, robust methodological frameworks for systematically characterizing this heterogeneity and identifying causal regulators of treatment response are still lacking.MethodsWe developed TMEtyper, a comprehensive computational framework for TME characterization. This was achieved by constructing a pan-cancer TME signature that integrates cellular compositions, pathway activities, and intercellular communication networks. We employed consensus clustering coupled with topological feature extraction to delineate seven distinct TME subtypes. Key hub genes specific to each subtype were identified through an integrative machine learning approach, and their regulatory mechanisms were elucidated using structural causal modeling.ResultsTMEtyper integrates 231 TME signatures to characterize the TME via network-based clustering, defining seven subtypes with distinct prognostic implications. Its analytical pipeline combines ensemble machine learning with a convolutional neural network for robust subtype classification and employs structural causal modeling to reconstruct underlying regulatory networks. Validation across 11 independent immunotherapy cohorts confirmed its strong predictive power, with the Lymphocyte-Rich Hot subtype being consistently associated with superior clinical outcomes. TMEtyper is implemented as an open-source R package with an interactive web interface, facilitating TME analysis and biomarker discovery for the research community.ConclusionsTMEtyper establishes an integrative framework that advances TME characterization beyond conventional classifications, delivering both biological insights and clinical utility. Its deployment as an accessible analytical resource opens new avenues for personalized immunotherapy strategies and biomarker development.Keywords: Tumor microenvironment, Immunotherapy, Structural causal model, Regulatory networks, Neural network, TMEtyper

Introduction

Alongside genetic changes, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has a crucial impact on tumor cell growth and differentiation during cancer progression [1]. The TME is a complex ecosystem composed of tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells, each contributing distinct functions that collectively give rise to its profound heterogeneity. While cancer immunotherapy has demonstrated significant efficacy across various solid tumors, only a subset of patients benefits from these treatments. In 2015, Allison's conceptual distinction between immunogenic ("hot") and non-immunogenic ("cold") tumors underscored the pivotal impact of TME heterogeneity on immunotherapy outcomes [2]. A growing body of evidence confirms that the response to immunotherapy is strongly associated with the compositional variation of the TME [3], [4]. Furthermore, the strategy of remodeling the TME has been established as a promising approach to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, there is an urgent need to comprehensively decipher the TME and elucidate the molecular characteristics of its constituent subtypes, which is anticipated to inform the development of novel targeted and immunotherapeutic strategies.

Previous studies have established foundational frameworks for TME classification. Bagaev et al. delineated four TME subtypes conserved across 20 cancer types, based on major functional components and cellular populations [5]. Similarly, Thorsson et al. identified six immune subtypes across 33 cancers by integrating published immune gene expression signatures [6]. while Combes et al. defined 12 immune archetypes spanning various cancers, characterized by 10 distinct immune cell compositional features [7]. However, a common limitation across these and many subsequent studies is their predominant focus on immune cells, which risks overlooking the critical contributions of other TME constituents, such as stromal cells, cell-cell communication networks, and tumor-related signaling pathways [8], [9]. This narrow perspective presents several critical shortcomings. First, the TME constitutes an intricate ecosystem wherein non-immune components—particularly stromal cells like cancer-associated fibroblasts and endothelial cells—exert profound influences on tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic efficacy [10]. Second, cell-cell communication signals (e.g., ligand-receptor interactions, cytokine networks) and complex tumor-related signaling pathways govern the dynamic crosstalk within the TME, fundamentally shaping its immunosuppressive or immune-permissive state [11]. Disregarding these multifaceted interactions means missing key drivers of immune resistance and response heterogeneity, ultimately limiting the predictive power and clinical applicability of the resulting TME subtypes.

Recent advances in multi-omics and spatial profiling technologies are now addressing these limitations, paving the way for more integrative TME classifications. For instance, single-cell RNA sequencing enables the systematic decoding of cell–cell interaction networks, revealing how ligand-receptor-mediated crosstalk governs immune cell recruitment and function [12]. Spatially resolved transcriptomics and tissue imaging move beyond mere cell-type densities by capturing the geographical context of immune-stromal interactions; these approaches demonstrate that spatial organization—not just cellular composition—is a critical determinant of immune activity and patient prognosis [13]. Furthermore, the integration of single-cell multi-omics data provides a unified view of transcriptional, epigenetic, and protein-level dynamics. This integration facilitates the discovery of functionally coherent TME archetypes that transcend conventional immune-centric categories, thereby offering novel insights for personalized immunotherapy strategies [14], [15].

Current TME classifications are rapidly evolving toward multidimensional functional annotation. To overcome the limitations of existing frameworks, we developed TMEtyper—an integrative framework that simultaneously incorporates immune and stromal cellular features, cell-cell communication networks, and tumor-related pathway activity (Fig. 1). This comprehensive strategy enables the identification of robust TME subtypes with clear clinical relevance, unveils key mechanistic interactions, and enhances the prediction of therapeutic response. Our analytical pipeline combines ensemble feature selection with a convolutional neural network for precise stratification, and further employs machine learning and structural causal modeling to infer the regulatory networks underlying each subtype. By providing a refined resource for deciphering TME complexity, TMEtyper advances the field toward more effective biomarker development in cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Construction a comprehensive TME gene signature library

To systematically capture the multicellular and functional complexity of the TME, we integrated three complementary categories of gene signatures: TME components (including immune cells and stromal cells), cell communication signaling, and biological pathways. Cellular signatures for 54 immune and stromal cell types were sourced from the EcoTyper [16]. The communication module comprised signaling pathways related to cytokines, interleukins, and chemokines. For pathways related signatures, we first curated a set of 16 TME-relavant keywords (e.g., “Hypoxia”, “Extracellular”, “TNF”). Corresponding gene sets were retrieved from the Human Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) using these keywords. We further incorporated 17 immune response pathways from the ImmPort database and gene sets representing the 7 steps of the cancer-immunity cycle. To enhance specificity, genes within each pathway were refined by extracting the top-ranked contributors using the leading-edge analysis function from the GSEA package. This process yielded a final, integrated library of 231 functionally curated TME gene sets (Table S1), which served as the foundation for all subsequent analyses.

Identification of TME subtypes

Clustering analysis of TME signature gene sets

TME components do not function in isolation. To identify coherent TME communities, we adopted the methodology described by Bogdan et al. [16], which maximizes pairwise co-associations between TME components while minimizing mutual avoidance within clusters. First, we quantified the overlap between each pair of TME components using the Jaccard index, based on the enrichment score matrix E derived from ssGSEA analysis across tumor samples. The gene expression matrix used for ssGSEA calculation was obtained from the pre-processed and batch-corrected Pan-Cancer Atlas cohort available on the UCSC Xena platform [17]. Each TME component was discretized into a binary vector A of length l (the number of tumor samples). These vectors were assembled into a binary matrix T with dimensions 231 (components) × l (samples). For a given sample j, if component s was the most abundant TME component, Ts,w was set to 1; otherwise, it was set to 0. Pairwise Jaccard indices were computed across all rows (components) of matrix E, resulting in a similarity matrix M of size 231 × 231. We employed a hypergeometric test to assess whether each pair of TME components exhibited significant overlap. Pairs with p > 0.01 had their Jaccard index set to zero. The resulting Jaccard matrix M1 was subjected to hierarchical clustering using average linkage with Euclidean distance (hclust in the R stats package) (Figure S1). The optimal number of clusters was determined by maximizing silhouette width (Figure S2). Clusters containing two or fewer TME components were excluded from subsequent analysis, resulting in seven robust clusters.

TME classification and subtype analysis

Samples were assigned to TME subtypes based on the seven clusters identified in the previous step. We first calculated subtype abundance by averaging the enrichment scores of TME components within each subtype, followed by proportional normalization to ensure the sum across all subtypes equaled 1 for each sample. We then applied a Welch's t-test to compare the enrichment score of each TME component within its assigned subtype against the combined enrichment scores of all TME components in other subtypes. Resulting p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. A sample was assigned into a subtype if it met two criteria: (1) the subtype demonstrated the highest enrichment score, and (2) at least one TME component within that subtype showed a q-value < 0.25.

Cellular component analysis across TME subtypes

We integrated multiple established TME classification systems for comparative analysis. Four immune/fibrotic TME subtypes for The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) samples were obtained from Bagaev et al. [5]. The TCGA immune clusters (C1-C6), signature values, and individual tumor subtypes were curated from Thorsson et al. [6]. Additionally, we incorporated data from TabulaTIME, a recently published single-cell-based TME classification framework [15]. Survival differences were assessed using the log rank test implemented in the R survival package. We performed Cox regression modeling for multivariate survival analysis incorporating TME subtypes and clinical features. All continuous variables were median-centered unless otherwise specified. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted to visualize survival differences. Cellular abundance was quantified using the ImmuCellAI R package [18], and immune/stromal scores were calculated with the ESTIMATE R package [19]. Tumor purity data were obtained from the PanCancer Atlas consortium (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas). Pathways activity scores (11 pathways) were calculated using PROGENy [20].

Identification of TME hub genes

Feature gene selection and structural causal model construction

We performed Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) to identify gene modules associated with TME subtypes. Using the blockwiseModules function in the WGCNA R package, we constructed a scale-free co-expression network from pan-cancer transcriptome data. The soft-thresholding power was set to 6 based on scale-free topology fit criteria. Module eigengenes were calculated and correlated with TME subtype labels to identify subtype-relevant modules for downstream analysis.

From the WGCNA-identified modules, we employed six machine learning algorithms (Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Logit Boost, Prediction Analysis for Microarrays (PAM), Partial Least Squares (PLS), Random Forest (RF), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB)) to calculate feature importance scores for each gene in predicting TME subtypes. The final importance for each gene was its average rank (or normalized weight) across all six methods. This ensured that only genes consistently ranked high by multiple, independent criteria were selected. Training parameters for all methods are provided in Table S2.

To investigate the potential regulatory mechanisms within TME subtypes, we employed structural causal model (SCM) as a hypothesis-generating framework. The model represents causal relationships between variables and phenotypes as a directed acyclic graph (DAG), where nodes denote genes and edges represent gene interactions. A prior knowledge network was compiled from TRRUST, String, and CellPhoneDB [21], [22], [23], integrating transcriptional regulation, Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI), and ligand-receptor pairs (13063 interactions in total, Table S3). Using this network as a structural prior, we trained a Bayesian belief network (BBN) via the bnlearn package to infer SCM based on the previously selected feature genes. To evaluate the robustness of inferred causal edges, we performed prior perturbation analysis by randomly adding or removing 20 % of edges in the initial prior graph across 1000 perturbed versions. We recorded the recovery rate of originally reported causal relationships (e.g., PAX8→ MT1F) across all perturbed models (Table S4).

Identification and analysis of hub genes

The inferred DAGs captured causal information flow between genes and TME subtypes. Genes positioned upstream in the network were considered potential regulators as their perturbation may propagate through downstream genes and alter the network state. For each TME subtype, we ranked genes by upstream hierarchy using the “dagitty” R package and imported the top 30 genes and their causal structures into cytoscape. Hub genes were identified via the cytoHubba plugin based on the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm. A list of immunomodulators (Table S5) was previously curated from Thorsson et al. [6]. Hub gene variant allele frequency (VAF) and tumor mutation burden (TMB) were computed using Maftools R package.

Construction of hub gene regulatory networks

We built integrative regulatory networks comprising four key components: (1) hub genes, (2) top five enriched pathways from co-expressed gene modules, (3) correlated immune cell types with hub gene expression (|Pearson’s r| > 0.6), and (4) cytokine ligand/receptor pairs involved in immune cell crosstalk. Gene modules co-expressed with each hub gene were identified with WGCNA, and the most correlated module was subjected to GO/KEGG enrichment analysis, and the top five pathways (ranked by enrichment score) were incorporated into the network. Immune cell types strongly correlated (|Pearson’s r| > 0.6) with hub gene expression (estimated via CIBERSORTx) were retained.

To determine network nodes and edges, we adapted the framework from Thorsson et al. [6]. First, all nodes—including cellular fractions and ligand and receptor expression levels—were discretized across the entire TCGA cohort into ternary values (low, medium, high) based on the tertiles (33.3 % and 66.6 % percentiles) of their respective distributions. A value was considered to be in the ‘mid or high’ category if it fell above the 33.3% percentile. Node specificity for each immune cell type and ligand/receptor within a TME subtype was then quantified as the proportion of samples in that subtype where the node’s value was ‘mid or high’. Subsequently, edge weights were assigned based on interaction concordance scores. The final integrated network was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1)

Development of TMEtyper

We implemented a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) using TensorFlow (version 2.11.0; www.tensorflow.org/) and keras (version 2.3.1; https://keras.io/api/) to classify TME subtypes from TME component enrichment scores. The dataset was first stratified into training (70 %) and independent test (30 %) sets, preserving the distribution of TME subtypes in both partitions. To ensure robust model performance and generalizability, we employed a stratified 5-fold cross-validation strategy during the training process. A single-hidden-layer neural network was implemented using the nnet package in R. The model architecture consisted of 64 units in the hidden layer to capture non-linear patterns in the data. Training was conducted using weight decay regularization (λ = 0.001) to prevent overfitting, equivalent to L2 regularization in deep learning frameworks. The model was trained for a maximum of 100 iterations with early stopping criteria based on both absolute (1.0e-6) and relative (1.0e-8) tolerance thresholds to ensure convergence while avoiding overfitting. The maximum number of weights was set to 50,000 to accommodate the model complexity. The network used entropy-based fitting for the multi-class classification task of TME subtype prediction. The resulting model was applied to predict TME subtypes in external ICB therapy datasets.

Analysis of immune checkpoint blockade therapy datasets

Collection and processing of ICB therapy datasets

The immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy datasets utilized in this study were obtained from previously established resources with detailed information provided in Table S6. Data processing procedures followed the methodology described in our prior publication [24]. Treatment response was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v1.1 [25]. Patients exhibiting complete or partial response were classified as responders (R), and those with progressive or stable disease were categorized as non-responders (NR). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from ICB-treated melanoma patients were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE120575.

Analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data

We conducted an integrative analysis of scRNA-seq data to characterize cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication within the TME. The scRNA-seq expression matrix was processed using Seurat (v5.3.0). Cell clusters were identified through the Leiden algorithm (resolution=0.8) and annotated according to established marker genes (e.g., CD3D for T cells, CD68 for macrophages). Cell annotation was performed using the following approach: initial classification at low resolution distinguished broad immune cell categories, after which specific immune cell types of interest were extracted and subsequently re-analyzed at higher resolution to delineate distinct immune cell subtypes.

We generated gene sets comprising the top 15 characteristic genes for each macrophage subtype. Using ssGSEA, we calculated enrichment scores for these gene sets across samples in the ICB datasets. Based on the TME subtypes predicted for each sample by neural network analysis, we further analyzed the distribution of macrophage subtypes across different TME subtypes. Cellular differentiation dynamics were investigated using Monocle3 (v1.3.7) for pseudotime trajectory analysis, which reconstructed developmental paths from CXCL9+ transitional macrophages to GZMB+ terminally differentiated macrophages. CellChat (v1.6.1) was employed to decode intercellular communication networks. All analyses were performed on high-performance computing clusters with appropriate statistical thresholds (FDR < 0.05 for differential expression; permutation p < 0.01 for ligand-receptor interactions).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the enricher function from the Bioconductor package clusterProfiler. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) for each gene in predicting treatment response were generated and calculated with the pROC R package. Multivariate Cox regression, log-rank tests, and Kaplan-Meier analyses were conducted using the "survival" R package. Basic statistical analyses, including the Kruskal-Wallis test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and Pearson correlation, were performed using R. For normally distributed data, group comparisons were assessed using a Welch's t-test; otherwise, an unpaired or paired two-sided Wilcoxon test was applied as appropriate. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for multi-group comparisons. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Correlations between different factors (such as gene mutation frequency and tumor mutation burden) were evaluated using correlation analysis implemented in the "ppcor" R package. All R packages employed in this study, along with their detailed parameters, are summarized in Table S2.

Results

Pan-cancer analysis identifies seven distinct TME subtypes

Through hierarchical clustering of the pairwise Jaccard index matrix derived from ssGSEA enrichment scores, we classified 10,533 samples across 33 cancer types into seven distinct subtypes (Fig. 2A, Figure S3, Table S7), each exhibiting unique cellular composition and signaling pathway activities (Fig. 2B, Table S8). Integrated analysis of cellular infiltration and pathway activity enabled the definition of seven TME subtypes: Metabolic Stress (MS), Antigen-Presenting Hot (APH), Lymphocyte-Rich Hot (LRH), KRAS-driven Stroma-Rich (KSH), Myeloid and Cytokine Hot (MCH), Fibroblast-Rich Stroma-Rich (FSH), and Mesenchymal Stroma-Rich (MSH). Each subtype demonstrated distinct cellular infiltration patterns, pathway activities, and therapeutic implications, as summarized in Table 1 and visualized in Fig. 2B and Figure S3-S9.

TME subtype characters and definition.

Analysis of subtype distribution across 33 TCGA cancer types revealed significant heterogeneity (Fig. 2C, Figure S10), with immunologically "hot" subtypes (APH, LRH) enriched in melanoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma, while stroma-rich subtypes (KSH, FSH, MSH) predominated in desmoplastic cancers like pancreatic and colon adenocarcinoma. The MS subtype was prominent in liver and kidney cancers, whereas the MCH subtype showed variable presence across tumor types, highlighting the diverse TME architectures across cancers and their implications for developing tailored therapeutic strategies.

To validate the biological and clinical relevance of our TMEtyper classification, we performed comprehensive comparisons with three established pan-cancer TME frameworks (Fig. 2D-2F). The immunologically "hot" subtypes showed consistent alignment with immune-activated phenotypes: LRH correlated strongly with Thorsson's C2 subtype and TabulaTIME's AILS, while APH aligned with Thorsson's C1/C2 and TabulaTIME's AIHS, supporting their classification as promising immunotherapy candidates. Conversely, the stroma-rich subtypes (KSH, FSH, MSH) consistently mapped to immune-cold categories, showing enrichment in Thorsson's C4/C6 and TabulaTIME's DLP/NIHS subtypes, underscoring their likely immunotherapy resistance and the need for stroma-targeting approaches.

The MS subtype exhibited heterogeneous alignment with Bagayev's D subtype, Thorsson's C4/C6, and TabulaTIME's NIHS/DHP, suggesting an inflamed but functionally impaired state. The MCH subtype displayed a unique distribution pattern across classification systems, indicating a distinct microenvironmental state not fully captured by existing frameworks focused on adaptive immunity. Our TMEtyper system offers three principal advancements beyond existing classifications: (1) cellular-resolution specificity enabling identification of mechanistically distinct subtypes; (2) discovery of novel hybrid states, particularly the MCH subtype representing a previously unclassified microenvironment; and (3) a refined stromal taxonomy that categorizes heterogeneous "immune-cold" environments into three biologically distinct subtypes.

Distinct clinical and transcriptional features across TME subtypes

TME heterogeneity significantly influences cancer patient prognosis and therapeutic response [26]. Our analysis of the seven TME subtypes revealed cancer type-specific survival patterns (Fig. 3A). The APH subtype was associated with favorable outcomes in breast cancer and low-grade glioma but with a poorer prognosis in bladder cancer and other malignancies, potentially reflecting context-dependent regulation of tumor progression through epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity [27]. Similarly, the LRH subtype showed divergent prognostic implications across cancer types, possibly due to heterogeneous functional states of immune cell populations within distinct TME contexts [28].

Multivariable Cox regression identified MS, APH, LRH, and FSH as favorable prognostic subtypes, while KSH, MCH, and MSH were associated with significantly elevated mortality risk, indicating more aggressive and therapy-resistant phenotypes (Fig. 3B). Molecular characterization using PROGENy [20] revealed distinct pathway activation patterns across subtypes (Fig. 3C-3D). Notably, KSH subtype exhibited pronounced activation of hypoxia, VEGF, and WNT signaling pathways—all established drivers of tumor progression and poor outcomes [29], [30], [31]—whereas these pathways showed minimal activity in APH subtype.

Further characterization of cellular composition revealed that MS subtype exhibited the highest tumor cell infiltration, indicating a tumor cell-dominant microenvironment, while LRH subtype showed the highest immune cell infiltration, a feature typically associated with improved response to immunotherapy [32]. Interestingly, stromal cell distribution showed no significant variation across subtypes, suggesting that the type and functional state of stromal components, rather than their abundance, may serve as key discriminators among TME subtypes (Fig. 3E).

Integrated profiling of clinically actionable features revealed that APH patients, despite elevated tumor mutational burden and drug-target mutation frequencies, displayed reduced expression of immunotherapy-related genes. This genomic profile suggests heightened susceptibility to targeted therapies rather than immune checkpoint blockade, consistent with the principle of oncogene addiction and the established correlation between non-inflamed TMEs and poor immunotherapy response [33], [34]. Conversely, LRH patients exhibited robust immune infiltration and elevated immune checkpoint expression, representing a classic immune-inflamed phenotype with documented sensitivity to immunotherapy [35]. Collectively, these findings underscore the translational potential of TMEtyper classification for guiding precision oncology.

Structural causal modeling identifies and characterizes hub genes regulating the TME

To identify causal drivers of TME subtypes, we applied structural causal modeling SCM to reconstruct regulatory networks from prior molecular interaction knowledge. Prior studies show SCM of RNA-seq data identifies causal dependency structures representing signal transduction cascades [36]. We first identified 19 co-expression modules (5673 genes) by WGCNA (Fig. 4A), then selected 438 subtype-specific feature genes by integrating six machine learning methods with Wilcoxon testing (Fig. 4B-C). SCM reconstructed a causal regulatory network from these genes, and cytoHubba analysis pinpointed upstream hub genes (Fig. 4D, Figure S11, Table S9). For instance, the model proposed ZEB1 as a potential upstream regulator of LINC01559. This computationally derived link is strongly supported by experimental findings, which demonstrate that ZEB1 induces LINC01559 expression that promotes cell proliferation, migration, and EMT in gastric cancer [37].

Further investigation revealed seven primary hub genes exhibiting distinct expression patterns across subtypes (Fig. 5A-5B). The expression level of each hub gene significantly correlated with the feature gene scores of its corresponding TME subtype. For instance, PAX8—the hub gene for the MS subtype—showed correlation coefficients with MS subtype feature scores ranging from −0.32–0.58. This aligns with previous reports that PAX8 modulates epithelial cell survival through regulating p53 signaling and apoptosis pathways [38], [39], suggesting its potential role in driving TME1 subtype formation. Analysis of hub gene associations with immune modulators demonstrated subtype-specific regulatory roles (Fig. 5C). PLA2G2D, the hub gene for the LRH subtype, showed strong positive correlations with most immune modulator categories, indicating its broad involvement in shaping an immunologically active microenvironment. DSG3, associated with the KRAS-driven Stroma-Rich (TME4) subtype, correlated primarily with immune cell ligands and co-inhibitors. The hub genes CSF3R (MCH subtype) and LRP2 (FSH subtype) both showed significant associations with antigen presentation-related genes, with CSF3R additionally linked to cell adhesion and immune ligand groups.

Integrative network analysis delineated the regulatory architecture of each subtype (Fig. 5D-5F, Figure S12). In MS subtype, high PAX8 expression positively correlated with infiltration of B cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, but negatively correlated with CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. APH subtype exhibited specific cytokine receptor/ligand activity in epithelial cells, potentially mediating immunosuppressive effects [40]. LRH subtype demonstrated the most active immune profile, with specific cytokine expression detected across B cells, NK cells, and T cell subsets, consistent with a "hot tumor" phenotype and indicating that patients with this subtype may derive the greatest benefit from immunotherapy [41].

TMEtyper reveals subtype-specific immunotherapy response patterns

To systematically investigate the association between TME subtypes and response to ICB, we developed a neural network-based classifier for TME subtyping (TMEtyper) that enables standardized and reproducible microenvironment classification. Application of TMEtyper to 11 independent ICB cohorts (Fig. 6A) revealed a predominant distribution of LRH (33.77 %) and MSH (38.58 %) subtypes, while the remaining subtypes accounted for 2.81–15.23 % of cases (Fig. 6B). The absence of MS and MCH subtype samples is consistent with their low prevalence (1 % and 3 %, respectively) in the original TME dataset, potentially explaining their underrepresentation in current ICB cohorts. We further validated the expression patterns of previously identified hub genes across these external datasets. As anticipated, CDX2 showed elevated expression in samples of APH subtype, PLA2G2D in LRH subtype, DSG3 in KSH subtype, LRP2 in FSH subtype, and GPM6B in MSH subtype (Fig. 6C). These results not only confirm the robustness of our subtyping system but also reinforce the biological relevance of the hub genes identified through structural causal modeling. Among these, CDX2 has been previously associated with favorable immunotherapy outcomes in colorectal cancer and reported to promote NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [42], supporting its potential role in modulating ICB response.

We evaluated the distribution of TME subtypes and their association with therapeutic response across 11 independent immunotherapy cohorts. Despite substantial technical and clinical heterogeneity among these datasets, we observed a highly consistent and biologically plausible pattern: the LRH subtype demonstrated robust response rates exceeding 30 % in 7 of the 10 cohorts, with nearly 80 % of LRH subtype patients responding to treatment in melanoma (ERP105482) and non-small cell lung cancer (SRP217040) datasets (Fig. 6D). Conversely, the stroma-rich TME4 subtype showed consistently lower response rates across most cohorts (Fig. 6E). In summary, these findings indicate that TME subtypes can serve as a stable predictor independent of technical and clinical heterogeneity to distinguish patient populations with different response potentials to immunotherapy.

TME subtype-specific molecular mechanisms underpin differential immunotherapy responses

To delineate the molecular basis of TME subtype-specific responses to immunotherapy, we conducted comparative transcriptomic analyses centered on the contrasting LRH and KSH subtypes. In melanoma, this comparison identified 7863 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, Fig. 7A). A broader analysis comparing LRH against three other subtypes revealed 2668 shared DEGs (Fig. 7B–7 C), defining a core LRH-specific gene signature. Ninety-six genes were consistently up-regulated in LRH across both melanoma and NSCLC (Figure S13), underscoring its pan-cancer relevance. Functional enrichment of the LRH-upregulated signature highlighted its profound immune specialization, with significant overrepresentation of cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and Th17 cell differentiation pathways (Fig. 7D, Figure S14)—all critical for immune cell recruitment and activation within the TME. Conversely, genes suppressed in LRH were enriched in pathways like neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (Figure S14), the downregulation of which has been linked to improved immunotherapy outcomes.

We further refined this signature to eight shared DEGs that were significantly upregulated in ICB responders (Fig. 7E). Among these, CXCL9 emerged as the most powerful predictor, achieving the highest median AUC for ICB response (>0.7) and surpassing established biomarkers like IFNG (Fig. 7F–H). Calibration curve analysis further indicated that CXCL9 exhibited lower prediction bias (Figure S15). These results collectively suggest that CXCL9 is a robust predictor and a potential target to enhance immunotherapy sensitivity, a notion supported by recent findings where CXCL9-engineered dendritic cells promoted T cell activation and augmented immune checkpoint blockade efficacy in lung cancer [43]. The clinical relevance of this TME-driven molecular stratification was confirmed in the PRJNA578193 melanoma cohort, where TME subtypes showed significant survival differences (log-rank p = 0.033). Multivariable Cox regression confirmed the LRH subtype as an independent favorable prognostic factor.

To dissect the cellular origin of this LRH-specific signal, we turned to single-cell RNA-seq data from ICB-treated melanoma. This revealed a distinct CXCL9+ M1 macrophage subpopulation (Fig. 8A–C) with a hybrid activation phenotype (Fig. 8D). Crucially, these CXCL9+ macrophages were significantly enriched in ICB responders (Fig. 8E) and were most abundant within the LRH subtype (Fig. 8F), providing a mechanistic link between the TME subtype, a specific chemokine-driven cellular module, and clinical response to immunotherapy. Pseudotime trajectory analysis indicated that CXCL9+ M1 macrophages occupy an intermediate state along the M1–M2 polarization continuum, with CXCL9 expression peaking during this transitional stage (Fig. 8G, Figure S16).

Discussion

In this study, we established a comprehensive taxonomy of the TME by integrating multi-omics data across 33 cancer types, defining seven distinct TME subtypes characterized by unique cellular compositions, pathway activities, and clinical outcomes. This framework transcends conventional classifications based solely on immune cell infiltration, offering a holistic view of the TME's functional states. A pivotal advancement of our work lies in the application of structural causal model to infer core hub genes that potentially drive the formation of these subtypes. The clinical relevance of our classification was robustly validated using independent cohorts, which revealed striking differences in responses to ICB therapy.

Our TME subtypes demonstrate significant concordance with established classifications, such as those by Bagaev, Thorsson, and the TabulaTIME consortium [5], [6], [15], thereby affirming their biological validity. The alignment of our LRH subtype with immune-enriched phenotypes and our stroma-rich subtypes (FSH/MSH) with immune-depleted or fibrotic categories underscores conserved biological themes across different classification systems. However, the identification of subtypes like MS and MCH reveals previously underappreciated TME states. The MS subtype, dominated by hypoxia, p53 signaling, and apoptosis, depicts an ecosystem under profound metabolic duress [44].

A central contribution of this study is the move beyond correlation to infer potential causal drivers of TME states using SCM. The identified hub genes are not merely correlates but are positioned upstream in the regulatory network and exhibit strong functional coherence with their respective subtypes. For instance, PAX8, the hub gene for the MS subtype, is a well-established regulator of epithelial cell survival and p53 pathway activity, directly pointing to a plausible causal role in shaping the metabolic-stress phenotype [39]. Similarly, the broad, positive association of PLA2G2D (hub gene for LRH) with nearly all immune modulators positions it as a potential master regulator of an immunologically active TME [45]. These SCM-derived insights provide high-quality candidates for future functional studies aimed at therapeutically manipulating TME states.

Our comparative analysis identified CXCL9 as a pivotal regulator of immunotherapy response, with single-cell profiling pinpointing a unique CXCL9-expressing macrophage population enriched in the responsive LRH subtype. Notably, TNFSF13B demonstrates context-dependent functions - while upregulated in responders where it potentially enhances immunity through tertiary lymphoid structure formation [46], its association with poor prognosis in certain cancers may reflect regulatory B-cell mediated immunosuppression [47]. This functional duality underscores the necessity of interpreting immune biomarkers within their specific microenvironmental context. These findings not only provide mechanistic insights into TME-driven immunotherapy responses but also highlight the potential of targeting specific macrophage subpopulations while emphasizing the need for contextual interpretation of immune modulators for future therapeutic development.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study delivers a powerful and refined framework for TME classification, complemented by the accessible TMEtyper tool. It provides profound insights into the heterogeneity of the TME and unveils novel mechanisms underlying ICB response and resistance. Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. While our classifier demonstrated robust performance across multiple retrospective cohorts, we acknowledge that this study lacks prospective clinical validation. The true clinical utility of our model must therefore be confirmed in future prospective trials before it can be considered for routine clinical implementation. The precise mechanistic roles of the predicted hub genes and the CXCL9+ macrophage subset require definitive validation through in vitro and in vivo functional experiments. While our classifier demonstrated robust performance across multiple retrospective cohorts, we acknowledge that this study lacks prospective clinical validation. The true clinical utility of our model must therefore be confirmed in future prospective trials before it can be considered for routine clinical implementation.
