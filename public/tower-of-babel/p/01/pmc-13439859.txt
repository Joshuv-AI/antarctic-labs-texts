Presumed parasitic meningoencephalitis following fresh snail exposure: physiotherapy interventions and functional outcomes in two distinct families and one solo case
Authors: Abiola Fafolahan, Abigail Davis, Oladunni Sodipo, Miracle Ogunmola, Usman Bello, Peace Awofiranye, Yusuf Tajudeen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeningoencephalitis can arise from several infections, but those linked to the parasitic infection Angiostrongylus cantonensis are uncommon. This parasite, often transmitted through consuming raw or undercooked snails, can cause severe neurological complications. This case series highlights physiotherapy management of neurological sequelae in children with presumed parasitic exposure associated meningoencephalitis.Case presentationsFive pediatric cases of presumed parasitic meningoencephalitis temporally associated with raw snail consumption are presented. All patients were Yoruba children from Southwest Nigeria. Two familial clusters were observed, involving a 9 year old boy and a 5 year old boy in one family, and a 5 year old boy and an 8 year old boy in a second family, alongside one isolated case involving a 9 year old girl. One patient died within 24 h of admission. All five cases received initial medical management, including anti-parasitic and supportive care. All surviving patients received medical and supportive care, while physiotherapy was initiated early to manage neurological impairments including muscle weakness, mobility limitations, and functional deficits. Interventions included range of motion exercises, strengthening, soft tissue techniques, transfer training, gait re-education, and desensitisation strategies for sensory disturbances.ConclusionsThese cases highlight the importance of preventive public health education on zoonotic exposure risks associated with snail consumption. Physiotherapy may play a supportive role in improving functional outcomes in children with neurological deficits secondary to presumed parasitic meningoencephalitis, particularly in resource limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05034-w.

Introduction

Meningoencephalitis refers to inflammation of the brain parenchyma and meninges, resulting from infectious or immune mediated processes, and remains a significant but often challenging clinical condition due to its varied aetiologies and overlapping clinical features with other neurological disorders [1]. In children, meningoencephalitis is particularly concerning due to higher vulnerability to severe neurological complications, including long term disability and mortality [2]. Its diagnosis is often complex because it may arise from infectious agents such as viruses, bacteria, and parasites, or from non-infectious causes including autoimmune and metabolic conditions [3, 4].

Among parasitic causes, eosinophilic meningoencephalitis is a rare clinical syndrome characterised by eosinophilic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid and has been associated with Angiostrongylus cantonensis infection [5]. This nematode, commonly referred to as rat lungworm, is considered the most frequently reported cause of eosinophilic meningoencephalitis globally [6].

It is endemic in parts of Southeast Asia and the Pacific region, with cases reported in countries such as Thailand, China, and several Pacific islands [7].

Transmission occurs through ingestion of third stage larvae present in intermediate hosts such as snails and slugs or transport hosts including freshwater crustaceans [5, 7]. The parasite life cycle has been well described by Barratt et al. [8] in Fig. 1. Although consumption of snails is a dietary practice in some African and European communities, including parts of Nigeria, Europe, and North Africa, infection in humans may also occur through accidental ingestion of contaminated produce [9–11].

Globally, most reported cases occur in endemic regions; however, sporadic infections have been documented in non-endemic areas including parts of the United States, such as Hawaii and Louisiana [7, 11]. The increasing geographical distribution has been linked to environmental spread of infected intermediate hosts and human mediated transport of rodents and gastropods [11].

After ingestion, larvae penetrate the intestinal wall and migrate hematogenously to the central nervous system, with an incubation period typically ranging from one to three weeks. Clinical manifestations may include headache, meningeal irritation, and sensory disturbances, while severe cases may progress to motor impairment, coma, and death [12, 13].

In Nigeria, meningoencephalitis remains a significant public health concern, compounded by limited diagnostic capacity and healthcare resource constraints. Available estimates suggest a measurable contribution of encephalitis related mortality within the country [14]. In many Nigerian communities, snail consumption remains a culturally accepted dietary practice due to its nutritional value [10, 15]. However, in resource limited settings, definitive identification of parasitic aetiologies such as A. cantonensis is often not feasible, and diagnosis is commonly based on clinical presentation and exposure history. Against this background, there is a need to document clinically presumed cases of meningoencephalitis associated with parasitic exposure and to explore supportive rehabilitation approaches, including physiotherapy, in managing neurological sequelae in affected children. This case series is reported in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines.

Case 1

Patient OMH, a 9-year-old boy, was admitted to the children’s emergency ward alongside his younger brother (Case 2, OMJ) due to complaints of itchy skin, fever, inability to move both lower limbs, and bowel incontinence. His health was normal until 17 days before admission when he began experiencing severe, generalized itchiness without a rash. His mother treated this with bitter leaf extract, which resolved the itchiness after three days. However, he subsequently developed a fever, which was temporarily relieved with paracetamol and oral amoxicillin. Despite these interventions, his condition worsened. Three days later, he lost the ability to move his lower limbs, and six days afterward developed fecal and urinary incontinence. He also reported pain and abnormal sensations in the lower limbs. There was a history of consuming raw snails prior to symptom onset.

There was no history of trauma, similar past symptoms, seizures, photophobia, recent travel, immunization, or blood transfusion. His prenatal, natal, and postnatal histories were uneventful, and he was fully immunized for age. Developmental milestones were appropriate. He is a Primary 5 pupil and the seventh of eight children in a polygamous family. The family resides in a well-ventilated five-bedroom apartment. On admission, vital signs were temperature 39.1 °C, pulse rate 130 bpm, respiratory rate 28 cpm, and SpO₂ 97%. He was conscious and oriented, with nuchal rigidity present. Muscle strength was graded 3/5 in the upper limbs and 2/5 in the lower limbs. Deep tendon reflexes were reduced, plantar responses were flexor, and Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs were positive. Sensory examination showed impaired light touch.

CSF analysis showed glucose 25 mg/dL (low), protein 15 mg/dL (normal), and microscopy revealed no organism growth. CRP was elevated. Differential diagnoses included meningoencephalitis and acute demyelinating encephalomyelitis, with atypical Guillain-Barré syndrome also considered. Herpes screening was negative for HSV-1 and HSV-2 IgM, but HSV-1 IgG was positive. Peripheral blood film suggested mild leukocytosis with neutrophilia and microcytic hypochromic red cells. A presumptive diagnosis of neuroangiostrongyliasis (Angiostrongylus cantonensis infection) was made based on clinical presentation, exposure history, and CSF findings. The patient was subsequently referred for physiotherapy.

Physiotherapy assessment for case 1

The patient was met in supine lying, asleep but easily aroused by his mother’s voice. Nuchal rigidity was evident, as the patient’s head was positioned in left side flexion, with severely limited and painful range of motion in the cervical spine. Upon examination, his skin was warm to touch but showed no obvious signs of respiratory distress. Muscle strength grading revealed a minimum of 3/5 in the majority of upper limbs (ULs) and 2/5 in lower limbs (LLs), with no associated tenderness or stiffness. In contrast, the lower limbs exhibited mixed muscle strength gradings, accompanied by muscle tenderness but no stiffness. Functionally, the patient was unable to roll, sit up, or stand independently, highlighting significant limitations in their mobility and strength.

Case 1 had a total Functional Independence Measure (FIM) of 11 on the first assessment.

Case 2

A 5-year-old boy (OMJ) was brought to the children emergency unit with a 10-day history of fever and a 3-day history of inability to move both lower limbs. The child was in his usual state of health until 10 days before presentation, when he developed a low-grade fever that was temporarily relieved by paracetamol. Seven days after the onset of the fever, he began experiencing difficulty moving his lower limbs, which progressively worsened to the point that he could no longer walk.

This was accompanied by an abnormal peppery sensation and pain in the limbs. There was no history of trauma, and the patient was brought to the facility for expert care. There was no prior history of similar symptoms, no history of blood transfusion, and his genotype is AA. His prenatal, natal, and postnatal periods were uneventful. The child is fully immunized, and developmental milestones were achieved on time. The patient is the last of eight children in a polygamous family where his mother is the first wife. His mother is a 44-year-old secondary school teacher with a Basic School Certificate, and his father is a 49-year-old fashion designer with a Senior School Certificate. The second wife has two children, all of whom are alive and well. The patient is a nursery 2 pupil who resides with three other siblings and his parents in a well-ventilated, five-bedroom apartment. There is a history of a similar symptom in his elder sibling (Case 1), a 9-year-old male.

A review of systems revealed no headache, convulsions, loss of consciousness, photophobia, visual disturbances, behavioral changes, urinary incontinence, frequency, urgency, dysuria, nocturia, or hematuria. On examination, the child was not pale, anicteric, not cyanosed, febrile (temperature 38.2 °C), not dehydrated, and had no pedal edema. His weight was 14 kg. Neurological examination showed the child was conscious, well-oriented to time, place, and permson, right-handed, with normal speech and intact memory. His neck was supple, and muscle tone was normal. Muscle power was 5/5 in the upper limbs and 3/5 in the lower limbs. Deep tendon reflexes were normal in the upper limbs but hyporeflexic in the lower limbs. Hyperesthesia was present in the lower limbs.

Respiratory examination revealed a respiratory rate of 42 breaths per minute, dyspnea with flaring of the alar nasi, vesicular breath sounds, and oxygen saturation of 96% in room air and 98% on intranasal oxygen. Cardiovascular examination showed a pulse rate of 110 beats per minute, full in volume and regular in rhythm, with normal heart sounds (S1, S2). The digestive system examination was unremarkable, with the mouth showing no abnormalities, and the abdomen was full, moving with respiration, soft, and without palpable organomegaly.

CSF analysis showed a glucose level of 32 mg/dL (normal: 40–70 mg/dL) and a protein level of 10 mg/dL (normal: 15–40 mg/dL). The packed cell volume (PCV) was 36%, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 25.71 mg/L (normal: 0-0.4 mg/L). The patient was conscious but exhibited right cranial nerve VI palsy and photophobia. A week later, it was revealed that both siblings had consumed raw snails. An MRI identified a left choroid fissure cyst and periventricular white matter hyperintensities, raising concerns for demyelinating disease or viral encephalitis. Urinalysis was negative for leukocytes, nitrite, urobilinogen, protein, blood, ketones, bilirubin, and glucose, with a pH of 6 and a specific gravity of 1.015. Differential diagnoses included meningoencephalitis and acute demyelinating encephalomyelitis, with plans to rule out atypical Guillain-Barré syndrome and conduct herpes screening. Herpes screening was negative for HSV-2 and HSV-1 IgM but positive for IgG. A presumptive diagnosis of neuroangiostrongyliasis was made based on exposure history, clinical features, and CSF findings.

Physiotherapy assessment for case 2

Segmental examination revealed no abnormalities in the head, neck, tonsils, or adenoids. The upper limbs showed no pain on palpation, with normal muscle tone and bulk on both sides. Both active and passive ranges of motion were full and pain-free in the ULs with a gross motor power of 3. There was hyperalgesia on assessment on almost all muscle groups of ULs and LLs. In the LLs, there was no pain on palpation, and muscle tone and bulk were normal bilaterally. The active and passive ranges of motion were also full and pain-free, with no tightness observed in the Achilles tendons. Functionally, the patient was able to roll and sit independently but could only stand and walk with support. The findings indicated weakness in the lower limbs, with a power of 3 and hyperalgesia. The FIM score was 15.

Case 3

Patient is a 5 years old boy (SA) who was admitted at the children emergency ward of this facility following the complaints of loss of consciousness, convulsion and fever 38 h prior to presentation (Prior history of ingestion of fresh snail before presentation). He was rushed to a private hospital before admission into this facility. There was a history of elder sibling (Case 4) admitted into this facility due to similar symptoms and presentation but died within 24 h of admission. Patient was in usual state of health until two days prior to presentation when noticed to have convulsed and lost consciousness. Upon admission into the private hospital, high grade fever was noticed which was abated with PCM, IV Amikacin, and IV dextrose were also administered to abort the convulsion. Intra nasal oxygen (INO2) was also administered when patient was noticed to be in respiratory distress. The frequency and intensity of the convulsion was reported to have remain the same despite the measures taken. The child was not exclusively breastfed, with water introduced early, followed by pap and water at five months of age. The child transitioned to an adult diet at six months. The child received only one vaccine, as the father believed further vaccinations could be harmful. The patient and their older sibling had a history of consuming raw snails in the neighborhood before being admitted. Developmental milestones were reportedly achieved on time. Birth was term and via SVD. Known seizure disorder Patient and was diagnosed three years prior. Last episode of seizure was reported to have been a year ago before the current episode. Patient is not a known asthmatic and is the 3rd of four children in a polygamous setting.

Mother is a 39 year old teacher with Nigeria Certificate in Education and father is a 50 year old farmer with tertiary level education. They live in a rented apartment (two-rooms).

On examination, the child was unconscious, not pale, anicteric, febrile (37.9 °C), not cyanosed, and had no pedal edema. The child weighed 13 kg, was 111 cm in length, and had a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of 14.5 cm. Neurological examination revealed unconsciousness, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 5/15 (EO-1, VR-1, MR-3). Both Brudzinski’s and Kernig’s signs were positive, tone and reflexes were normal globally, and the neck was supple. The respiratory rate was 38 cpm, the chest was clear, breath sounds were vesicular, and SpO2 was 97% in room air. Cardiovascular examination showed a pulse rate of 96 bpm, full volume, regular rhythm, and heart sounds S1 and S2. The abdomen was normal with no abnormalities detected.

A presumptive diagnosis of neuroangiostrongyliasis was considered based on exposure history and clinical progression; however, confirmation was not possible due to lack of advanced diagnostic testing. Full blood count results included a PCV of 34%, lymphocytes at 20%, monocytes at 4%, neutrophils at 75%, and a white blood cell (WBC) count of 10.7 × 10^9/L. Electrolyte, urea, and creatinine tests showed elevated urea levels at 48 mg/dL, with normal bicarbonate at 21 mmol/L, chloride at 100 mmol/L, creatinine at 0.76 mg/dL, potassium at 3.7 mmol/L, and sodium at 136 mmol/L. CSF analysis revealed CSF glucose at 72 mg/dL, CSF protein at 28 mg/dL, and CSF chloride at 103 mmol/L. The patient was referred for physiotherapy five days after admission and received five physiotherapy sessions while in the ward.

Physiotherapy assessment for case 3

During the objective assessment, GCS was 12/15, there was no swelling observed in the head and neck region, and the patient demonstrated good eye and auditory tracking. There was no evidence of neck stiffness, although neck control was fair, while trunk control was poor. For the upper limbs, no swelling or differential warmth was noted, and sensation was intact. Muscle bulk was preserved, but the muscle tone was hypotonic. Grip strength could not be assessed, although both passive and active range of motion (PROM and AROM) were full and pain-free, with a muscle grading of 3/5.

In the lower limbs, there was also no swelling or differential warmth, with intact sensation and preserved muscle bulk. The muscle grading was 2+/5, while the FIM score was 12. The muscle tone was hypotonic. PROM and AROM were full and pain-free, with no tightness observed in the tendon-Achilles or hamstrings. The patient was able to roll and sit with support but was unable to stand or walk independently.

Case 4 – deceased

A 9-year-old male child, sibling of Case 3, was admitted with a history of fever, altered consciousness and convulsion. On admission, the patient was critically ill, presenting with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 5/15 (E1, V1, M3), temperature 39.4 °C, pulse rate 142 bpm, respiratory rate 36 cpm, and oxygen saturation of 89% in room air. Quick examination revealed marked nuchal rigidity, positive Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs, hypotonia of the lower limbs, absent deep tendon reflexes in the lower limbs, and flaccid paraplegia. During the brief hospital stay, the patient rapidly developed worsening neurological deficits, recurrent seizures, and signs of raised intracranial pressure, which limited the ability to perform further investigations or interventions. Clinical impression was severe meningoencephalitis with status epilepticus in the context of suspected shared exposure (case 3).

Case 5

Patient is a 9 years old female patient (NB) who presented with a fever, initially treated by her mother with paracetamol and other unknown medications. Two days after the onset of the illness, she developed a generalized headache, more severe in the frontal region. Concurrently, she began experiencing dull, aching neck pain, exacerbated by movement and alleviated by keeping the neck still. A day later, she was admitted to a private hospital where she received unknown intravenous medications but showed no significant improvement upon discharge after two days. Two days post-discharge, she was taken to the referring hospital, admitted, and started on intravenous fluids, artesunate, and ceftriaxone. A day before presenting to the current facility, she was noted to be unable to walk and unresponsive to calls while in the hospital, leading to her referral. The patient has no history of seizures, similar presentations, previous hospital admissions, or blood transfusions. The patient was born at term via spontaneous vaginal delivery at a missionary home, cried well at birth, and had no adverse neonatal events. She was exclusively breastfed for six months and is now on a normal adult diet. The mother stated that the patient had eaten a raw snail and mentioned that the family regularly collects snails to eat after cooking them.

On admission at the children emergency unit of this facility, the patient exhibits no irrational behavior or seizures. There was no loss of consciousness, ear, eye, or nasal discharge, sore throat, cough, or dyspnea. She was not pale, anicteric, or cyanosed, and there was no lymphadenopathy or pedal edema. The patient had a fever of 38.0 °C and altered consciousness with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 10/15, with eye-opening at 1, best verbal response at 4, and best motor response at 5. Pupils are equal and reactive, but the right eye is deviated medially. There was neck stiffness with positive Kernig’s and Brudzinski’s signs. The patient is highly irritable and exhibits left-sided abducens nerve palsy (CN-VI).

The electrolyte, urea, and creatinine levels indicate a bicarbonate level of 22 mmol/L (normal: 21–29 mmol/L). Chloride is low at 92 mmol/L (normal: 98–107 mmol/L). Creatinine is 0.67 mg/dL (59 µmol/L), within the normal range of 0.57–1.11 mg/dL. Potassium is slightly low at 3.3 mmol/L (normal: 3.5–5.1 mmol/L), and sodium is low at 129 mmol/L (normal: 136–145 mmol/L). Urea is normal at 30 mg/dL (5 mmol/L, normal: 10–35 mg/dL). The full blood count shows a packed cell volume (PCV) of 30%, platelet count of 319 × 10^9/L, and white blood cell count of 7.5 × 10^9/L, with 80% neutrophils, 14% lymphocytes, and 4% monocytes. Mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration is 37.5%, mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) is 30.8%, and mean corpuscular volume is 82.3 fL. Random blood glucose is high at 222 mg/dL (12.3 mmol/L, normal: 0-199 mg/dL).

CSF analysis revealed glucose at 70 mg/dL (normal: 40–70 mg/dL), protein at 7 mg/dL (low, normal: 15–40 mg/dL), and chloride at 98 mmol/L (low, normal: 110–125 mmol/L). Computed topography (CT) findings suggested meningeal inflammation. Upon admission, the clinical diagnoses included suspected poorly treated meningitis, complicated by CN VI palsy and paraplegia, hemoglobinopathy resulting in a cerebrovascular accident, and a potential space-occupying lesion. Further evaluation the following day revealed that the child had consumed raw snails a week before symptom onset, prompting a re-diagnosis of neuroangiostrongyliasis. A repeat CSF analysis showed 20 white blood cells/µL (80% lymphocytes, 20% neutrophils), with eosinophils present. A follow-up full blood count indicated normal white blood cells but eosinophilia at 558 per cubic mm. The child is the youngest of four in a monogamous family. Her father died two years ago from a febrile illness, and her mother is a 44-year-old teacher. A presumptive diagnosis of neuroangiostrongyliasis was made based on eosinophilia, exposure history, and clinical presentation.

Physiotherapy assessment for case 5

On first assessment, the patient was afebrile, acyanosed, anicteric, and not in any obvious distress. The patient had mild neck stiffness, neck pain on movement, and full range of motion but painful across all planes. Muscle tone was reduced in both upper and lower limbs, with muscle power graded at 0 in the lower limbs and at least 3 in the upper limbs. Deep tendon reflexes were reduced globally, and there was a flexor plantar response with no ankle clonus. The patient’s trunk control was fair, and sensation was intact in the ULs and LLs. There was pain associated on palpation or movement on all muscle groups of ULs and LLs. There was hyperalgesia in almost all muscle groups of ULs and LLs. The patient’s functional assessment revealed that she could roll, sit with minimal support, but could not come up from lying to sitting or standing without support. The patient’s analysis of findings revealed that she was very apprehensive to touch and movement of both ULs and LLs and had neck stiffness. Patient had FIM score of 7.

Treatment plan and therapeutic intervention for all case studies

Case 1 (OMH)

The treatment progression began with the introduction of AROM exercises in the first session, focusing on the neck and upper extremities. Active-assisted exercises were initiated for the upper limbs, while passive movements were applied to the lower limbs to enhance mobility. In the second and third sessions, AROM exercises continued for the neck and upper extremities, and active-assisted exercises were extended to include the lower limbs. Passive movements and light effleurage massage were maintained for the lower limbs during this time. In the fourth session, tactile stimulation, light effleurage, and deep kneading were incorporated into the lower limb treatment. The patient was also guided through positioning exercises, transitioning from supine to side-lying to promote mobility. By the fifth session, the patient showed visible improvement, appearing brighter and afebrile. AROM exercises were continued, and tibialis anterior stretching was introduced bilaterally. Passive movements were applied to the lower limbs, and the patient was positioned in high sitting for approximately 10 min to further encourage mobility. By the sixth session, AROM exercises for the neck and upper extremities continued, and STM with shea butter was applied to the upper and lower back. The patient also engaged in auto-assisted exercises for the upper limbs.

In Session Seven, AROM exercises for the neck and upper limbs continued, while STM was applied to the thighs and calf muscles. Passive movements of the ankle and interphalangeal joints were also included. Session Eight introduced more functional activities, such as bridging and rolling exercises to strengthen the core muscles and help the child practice transitioning between positions, such as moving from lying to sitting or sitting to standing. Transfer retraining, involving moving from supine to sitting and then standing, was initiated, and the session focused on standing reeducation with support from physiotherapists to improve balance and postural control. Session Nine continued with focused active exercises for the lower limbs, including bridging and soft tissue mobilization to the paraspinal muscles. Modified straight leg raises were performed, and resisted active exercises using sand bottles for added resistance were introduced. Standing and walking reeducation was also performed for the patient with the use of a pediatric walker. By Session Ten, progressive mobilization out of bed and standing reeducation with the use of a Zimmer frame were emphasized. The ward program focused on functional ambulation and enhancing mobility. In Session Eleven, the patient was discharged from inpatient physiotherapy care. He was counseled to continue with home-based exercises and was given an outpatient physiotherapy appointment. The patient showed significant improvement over the course of treatment, with the total FIM score increasing from 11 to 46 (Table 2). Self-care abilities and transfers improved notably, while locomotion also progressed. Muscle power in the upper limbs remained steady at 3+, while lower limb strength improved from 2 + to 3, contributing to better mobility and functional independence.

Case 2 (OMJ)

In the first session, the patient was assessed, and clerking was completed to document relevant medical history and current condition. During the second session, free active exercises (FAEXs) for the upper and lower limbs were introduced, alongside manual resisted active exercises (RAExs). Given the patient’s muscle power of 3 in both upper and lower limbs, we initiated standing reeducation using a pediatric walker, as well as walking reeducation, which the patient tolerated well. By the third and fourth week, squatting exercises and one-legged stance exercises were added to enhance strength and balance. By the fifth session, the patient was ambulating well, and STM with shea butter was applied to the knees to alleviate discomfort. Resisted active exercises with a 2 kg dumbbell were also introduced.

In the sixth and final session, the patient was discharged from inpatient physiotherapy care and was advised to continue outpatient physiotherapy for further progress. The patient demonstrated steady progress throughout treatment, with the total FIM score rising from 15 to 43 (Table 2). Self-care, transfers, and locomotion all showed improvement, especially in the later sessions. ULs muscle power remained consistent at 3 until the final sessions, where it improved to 4. LLs strength gradually increased from 3 to 3+, contributing to enhanced mobility and overall functional independence.

Case 3 (SA)

In the first session, the patient was met in a side-lying position, afebrile, acyanosed, anicteric, and not in any painful distress. In the second session, the patient’s general muscle power was graded 3 + for the LLs and 2 + for the right lower limb, with intact sensation on the right side, while the ULs had a power grading of 3. We initiated FAEXs and manual RAEXs for the joints of both ULs and LLs. STM was performed on the lower limbs using a topical ointment.

By the third session, the muscle power for the LLs had improved to 3+. We introduced strengthening exercises with two kg dumbbells for further muscle strengthening, which the patient tolerated well. An attempt at standing was made, but the patient was still unable to bear weight on the right lower limb. The ward program involved advising the patient’s mother to continue sitting him out of bed regularly. During the fourth session, the patient was able to bear weight on both lower limbs, initially with physiotherapist support, and later independently. He managed to stand for three minutes. By the fifth and sixth sessions, the patient could stand for five minutes and ambulate around the hospital bed and to the nurses’ station. One-legged stance exercises and squatting were also incorporated during these sessions. After the seventh session, the patient was discharged with a home exercise program, which was taught and emphasized to the mother. He was given an outpatient appointment to continue his rehabilitation.

The patient made substantial progress during the course of treatment, with the total FIM score increasing significantly from 12 to 57 (Table 2). Self-care, transfers, and locomotion improved markedly across all sessions. ULs muscle power increased from 3 to 4/5, while LLs strength advanced from 2 + to 4+. These gains in muscle strength contributed to improved mobility, transfers, and functional independence. Overall, the patient achieved considerable improvement in both strength and daily functional abilities.

FAEXs for the lower limbs were performed in a high sitting position (five counts, three reps), followed by standing and walking reeducation with minimal support. Discussion

Meningoencephalitis, though relatively uncommon, presents important challenges to public health, particularly when linked to zoonotic vectors such as snails. While rare, its severity, potential for outbreaks in vulnerable populations, and association with emerging infectious agents necessitate attention. Snails, often vectors for parasites like Angiostrongylus cantonensis, have been linked to eosinophilic meningitis, primarily in tropical and subtropical regions [16–18]. The consumption of improperly prepared snails could lead to life-threatening neurological conditions [19], especially in children with underdeveloped immune systems. This case series is important because it documents a rare and clinically presumed etiology of meningoencephalitis in pediatric patients and highlights the role of supportive physiotherapy interventions in neurological recovery and functional independence. This case series illustrates how a seemingly harmless dietary practice consumption of fresh snails can lead to serious neurological infections, requiring hospitalization and intensive rehabilitation.

All patients presented with symptoms including fever, neck stiffness, and lower limb weakness. Additionally, sensory disturbances were observed in Case 2 and Case 5, characterized by tingling (“peppery”) sensations and heightened pain sensitivity (hyperalgesia). These clinical features are consistent with parasitic infiltration of the central nervous system (CNS), which often leads to inflammatory processes and subsequent motor dysfunction [20]. The presence of these neurological symptoms is indicative of CNS involvement, reflecting the damage caused by parasitic migration and inflammation, particularly when motor pathways are compromised [21, 22]. Fever and neck stiffness often arise due to the inflammatory response in the meninges, while the sensory and motor abnormalities reflect the parasites’ direct effects on neural tissue [22]. The sensory disturbances, especially the “peppery” sensation, suggest irritation or damage to peripheral nerves and dorsal root ganglia, often seen in such parasitic infections [23, 24].

Case 3 presented with incomplete immunization, as the father believed that vaccinations could cause harm to his children. He also had background history of seizure disorder. The rapid decline and subsequent death of the sibling in Case 4, occurring under similar circumstances, underscore the critical impact of individual immune responses on the progression of the disease. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in vulnerable populations, including those with underlying neurological conditions, where the body's ability to combat central nervous system infections is significantly compromised [25]. However, in the absence of microbiological or molecular confirmation, these outcomes may reflect a combination of disease severity, host response variability, and healthcare access limitations rather than immunization status alone. Therefore, causal attribution should be interpreted cautiously.

In these case studies, physiotherapy played a supportive role in restoring function and mobility through progressive exercises, manual therapy, and patient and caregiver education. The primary goals of intervention were to enhance functional independence, prevent secondary complications, and support neurorehabilitation. Across all cases, treatment began with passive movements, active range of motion, and active-assisted exercises to maintain joint integrity and prevent contractures. As patients improved, strengthening and functional training were introduced, including resisted exercises (e.g., 2 kg dumbbells in Cases 2 and 3), which contributed to gradual improvements in muscle power and functional mobility [26]. Soft tissue mobilization (STM) and massage techniques were used to reduce pain and improve tissue extensibility. In Case 1, deep kneading was introduced in later sessions, while in Case 5 STM with shea butter was used to assist pain modulation and improve tolerance to movement. These techniques likely contributed to symptom relief and improved participation in rehabilitation rather than acting as direct curative interventions [27, 28].

Physiotherapists incorporated functional activities, such as bridging, rolling, and transfer training, to improve mobility and independence. In Case 1, transfer retraining was initiated in the eighth session, while in Case 5, standing reeducation using a Zimmer frame was attempted in the second session. These activities help patients develop essential skills for daily living and reduce the risk of falls. In Cases 2 and 5, the patients experienced hyperalgesia and a burning sensation, which was managed by applying tactile stimulation to the plantar surface of the foot during the second session. To address pain and reduce inflammation, soft tissue mobilization (STM) with shea butter was administered to the dorsum of the foot, legs, and thighs. The initial assessment and first treatment were challenging as the case 5 was uncooperative due to pain. She exhibited distress, crying, and an inability to bear weight on the lower limbs, due to heightened pain sensitivity associated with hyperalgesia. In response, play therapy was incorporated to enhance engagement and facilitate treatment, as literature suggests that using playful interventions can improve cooperation in pediatric patients experiencing pain [29]. Tactile stimulation and soft tissue mobilization (STM) were applied as part of a desensitization protocol. Research indicates that tactile stimulation can help modulate pain perception by promoting sensory integration and reducing the discomfort associated with hyperalgesia [30]. Additionally, STM using shea butter was employed to alleviate pain and reduce inflammation, which aligns with the findings of studies suggesting that soft tissue techniques can enhance circulation and promote relaxation in cases of heightened sensory responses [31]. Physiotherapists also educated family members on continuing the ward-based rehabilitation program at home, ensuring that exercises and activities were consistently maintained after discharge. By involving family members and stressing the role of outpatient appointments, physiotherapists aimed to promote long-term adherence, optimize functional outcomes, and reduce the likelihood of re-admission [32].

The outcomes in Table 2 demonstrate progressive improvement in functional independence and muscle strength across Cases 1, 2, 3 and 5. Early physiotherapy focused on positioning, passive and active-assisted movements, and prevention of secondary complications. Evidence supports that early mobilization in pediatric neurological conditions helps maintain joint integrity and reduce immobility-related complications [33, 34]. In later sessions, task-specific training such as sitting balance, transfers, and gait re-education contributed to improved functional independence [35, 36]. However, while functional gains were observed, these improvements likely reflect a combination of natural recovery, medical management, and rehabilitation input rather than physiotherapy alone. By the final sessions, increased FIM scores and improved mobility reflected the effectiveness of a structured, progressive rehabilitation approach in supporting recovery in pediatric meningoencephalitis [37].

Limitations

The findings of this case series should be interpreted within several limitations. First, the study involved only five pediatric patients from a single resource limited setting, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Although the report provides important clinical insights into a rarely reported neurological presentation, the small sample size restricts broader epidemiological and therapeutic conclusions. Second, definitive confirmation of neuroangiostrongyliasis could not be established because advanced diagnostic investigations including serologic testing, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays, parasitological identification, and comprehensive neuroimaging were unavailable or financially inaccessible within the study setting. Consequently, diagnosis relied mainly on clinical presentation, exposure history, cerebrospinal fluid findings, eosinophilia in selected cases, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses, consistent with accepted clinical descriptions of eosinophilic meningoencephalitis in resource limited settings.

In addition, original neuroimaging figures could not be retrieved in publication quality; therefore, imaging findings were described narratively and acknowledged as a limitation. Finally, the absence of standardized confirmatory testing means that alternative infectious, inflammatory, or autoimmune neurological conditions could not be completely excluded in all cases. Therefore, the relationship between raw snail exposure and the neurological manifestations observed should be interpreted cautiously within a clinically presumptive framework.

Conclusion

In settings where snails are commonly consumed or handled, fresh snail consumption related pediatric meningoencephalitis remains an important public health concern. This case series describes clinically presumed eosinophilic meningoencephalitis likely associated with parasitic exposure and highlights the diagnostic challenges encountered in resource limited settings. The cases were supported by compatible neurological features, exposure history, cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities, and eosinophilia in selected patients.

This report emphasizes the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management, including physiotherapy. Physiotherapy contributed to functional recovery through early mobilization and structured neurorehabilitation. The findings also reinforce the need for public health education on safe food handling practices and highlight the importance of rehabilitation services for children with neuroinfectious conditions in low resource settings.
