Sample size, model stability, and the interpretation of adjusted effect estimates: correspondence on Moreira et al.
Authors: Alejandro Chapa-Rodriguez, Hafiz Sarwar, Shraddha Narechania
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We comment on the recent retrospective cohort by Moreira and colleagues evaluating intracranial-compliance-guided mean arterial pressure management. The adjusted multivariable logistic regression in the published analysis includes ten estimated coefficients against 22 ICU deaths, producing an adjusted odds ratio reported with a confidence interval spanning more than two orders of magnitude. We outline why these features are consistent with model instability rather than a precise causal estimate, identify a corroborating signal within the same model, and raise two secondary questions about control selection and the headline cost figure. We support the authors’ call for prospective evaluation while suggesting that the inferential claims of the present analysis warrant more cautious presentation.

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the work by Moreira and colleagues evaluating ICC-guided MAP titration using the brain4care system [1]. The clinical question is timely, and the authors are forthright about the study’s exploratory and hypothesis-generating intent. We write to raise a methodological concern that we believe deserves consideration before these findings inform practice or future trial design.

The adjusted multivariable logistic regression in Table 3 of the published article reports an adjusted odds ratio of 0.0093 (95% CI 0.0008–0.1140) for ICC-guided care. With 22 ICU deaths across n = 100 and ten estimated coefficients entered into the model, including primary diagnosis expanded as three indicator terms against a reference category. The model operates at roughly two events per variable, well below the conventional minimum of ten events per variable recommended by Peduzzi and colleagues for stable logistic regression estimates [2]. More recent methodological work has appropriately complicated the EPV = 10 rule of thumb, but the relaxation has come through shrinkage methods and simulation-based sample-size calculation tailored to model complexity and outcome prevalence [3, 4], neither of which appears to have been applied here. Under any of these frameworks, a 22-event model with ten coefficients would be flagged as underpowered for stable estimation. A confidence interval spanning more than two orders of magnitude is the expected signature of this regime, and we respectfully suggest that the point estimate, reported to four decimal places, conveys a precision the underlying data cannot support.

A related signal appears within the same model. The covariate “male sex” is reported with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.045 (95% CI 0.0039–0.52, p = 0.013), implying an approximately 22-fold protective effect of male sex on ICU mortality after adjustment. In a neurocritical cohort with the case mix described, this magnitude is biologically implausible and is more parsimoniously explained as a manifestation of model instability or quasi-separation in sparse strata. When a nuisance covariate produces an effect of this size, the primary exposure estimate from the same model warrants similar caution.

Two additional points are worth addressing. First, the control cohort was selected from 59 eligible non-B4C patients down to 51 based on “contemporaneous admission and completeness of outcome data.” Clarifying the disposition and outcomes of the eight excluded patients would help readers assess whether this selection step affected the unadjusted mortality contrast. Second, Table 4 presents a “total net savings per patient ~$13,760” that combines a readmission-driven estimate ($3,878, anchored to a statistically significant rate difference) with LOS-driven estimates ($9,882) derived from differences with p-values of 0.91 and 0.64. The text appropriately notes this caveat, but readers and policymakers are likely to cite the summed figure. Restricting the headline economic estimate to the readmission component would more faithfully represent what the data support.

None of this is to dismiss the underlying physiological rationale, which is sound, or the feasibility contribution, which is real. The case for a prospective, protocolized, ideally cluster-randomized evaluation, which the authors also advocate for, is strengthened, by acknowledging the limits of what the present analysis can establish. We thank the authors for an open and detailed report that makes this kind of methodological dialogue possible.

Sincerely,

Alejandro Chapa-Rodriguez
