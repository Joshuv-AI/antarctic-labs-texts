Prognostic Value of Resting Left Ventricular Sphericity Indexes in Coronary Artery Disease With Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Ping Wu, Yuting Zhao, Xiaoshan Guo, Xia Liu, Yingqi Hu, Yuxin Xiao, Li Xu, Nan Huang, Yuanyuan Li, Yanhui Wang, Tailin Ren, Qiuyan Wu, Ruonan Wang, Xiaoli Zhang, Zhifang Wu, Sijin Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse left ventricular remodeling is a significant cardiovascular predictor for patients with coronary artery disease and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). However, the remodeling indexes reflecting left ventricular spherization by myocardial perfusion imaging are underexplored.Methods and Results727 patients (mean age 59.8±13.5 years, 329 women) diagnosed or suspected coronary artery disease with preserved LVEF who underwent resting myocardial perfusion imaging were retrospectively enrolled. The myocardial perfusion imaging findings including the total perfusion deficit and sphericity indexes (shape index (SI) and eccentricity index (EI) obtained from gated (QGS) and non‐gated (QPS) images) were collected. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were followed up for 45.1±22.0 months. All patients were divided into 4 subgroups based on total perfusion deficit at 10% and LVEF at 65%. Univariable comparative analyses were performed in 5 cohorts (all patients and 4 subgroups). Patients who experienced MACE displayed higher SI and/or lower EI (all P<0.05). Kaplan–Meier survival analyses suggested significant differences for SIQPS in all 5 cohorts, for EIQPS and EIQGS in 4 cohorts, and for end‐systolic and end‐diastolic SIQGS in 3 cohorts (all P<0.05). Multivariate Cox analysis showed that abnormal SI and EI remained statistically significant predictors for MACE after adjusting for total perfusion deficit, LVEF, and other confounding factors.ConclusionsFor patients diagnosed or suspected of coronary artery disease with preserved or supra‐normal LVEF, resting sphericity indexes by myocardial perfusion imaging displayed incremental long‐term prognostic value. Among these indicators, SIQPS is particularly promising across different perfusion or preserved functional conditions.Keywords: myocardial ischemia, myocardial perfusion imaging, preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, prognosis, sphericity indexesSubject Categories: Coronary Artery Disease, Prognosis

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Although perfusion indicators have been extensively documented and provide critical information for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of coronary artery disease, the incorporation of left ventricular sphericity indicators could further enhance the identification of high‐risk patients, regardless of different perfusion or preserved function conditions.

Although perfusion indicators have been extensively documented and provide critical information for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of coronary artery disease, the incorporation of left ventricular sphericity indicators could further enhance the identification of high‐risk patients, regardless of different perfusion or preserved function conditions.

Fifty percent of patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) may progress to heart failure (HF), of whom, 50% may present with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and exhibit a comparable adverse prognosis to those with reduced LVEF. 1 , 2 About one‐third of patients with HF with preserved LVEF who received hospital treatment died within 1 year, 2 and autopsy reports indicated that the prevalence of CAD is as high as 50% to 65%, with acute or chronic myocardial ischemia and LV remodeling (LVR) being significant pathological changes. 3 Therefore, early detection of myocardial perfusion and remodeling abnormalities in patients with CAD with preserved LVEF is crucial for identifying individuals at risk of developing HF.

Myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) with single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) has been extensively documented as a pivotal tool in clinical diagnosis, risk stratification, treatment evaluation, and prognosis prediction in diagnosed or suspected CAD because of its multidimensional evaluation including perfusion, wall motion, cardiac function, and remodeling. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 As a comprehensive index in evaluating the extent and severity of myocardial perfusion defect, total perfusion deficit (TPD) is included in the imaging guidelines by the American Society of Nuclear Cardiology in 2010. 5 , 8 , 9 For remodeling parameters, volumetric indexes like end‐diastolic volume and end‐systolic volume have been widely used, meanwhile, recent studies indicated sphericity parameters, like shape index (SI) and eccentricity index (EI), could also provide additional prognostic information in CAD, even in those with normal myocardial perfusion or systolic function. A study that enrolled 167 patients with diagnosed or suspected CAD without obvious hyperemic perfusion abnormalities showed that SI calculated by Quantitative Gated SPECT (QGS) was closely related to LV volume and function. 10 In a group of 674 patients with normal stress MPI, SI was reported to have the capability to identify patients with primitive LVR and those at higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). 11 Furthermore, a large‐scale clinical registry recently demonstrated the capacity of post‐stress SI and EI changes in independently predicting MACE and improving risk stratification. 12 Despite the compelling evidence demonstrating the substantial influence of LVR on prognosis, there is still a lack of information on the prognostic value of sphericity indexes assessed by resting MPI.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed data from patients with diagnosed or suspected CAD with preserved LVEF who underwent resting gated SPECT MPI and explored the incremental prognostic value of sphericity indexes beyond TPD and LVEF. Considering the unique physiopathological characteristics and even more unfavorable prognosis associated with supra‐normal LVEF (snLVEF, LVEF ≥65%), subgroup analyses were conducted in individuals with snLVEF. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

A total of 6386 consecutive patients referred for resting gated SPECT MPI between January 2016 and March 2021 at the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University were retrospectively analyzed. Inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed or suspected CAD; (2) presence of echocardiographic LVEF within 3 months; (3) echocardiographic LVEF ≥50%. Exclusion criteria: (1) previous history of percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass graft, and cardio‐cerebrovascular disease; (2) failure of gated imaging acquisition or unreliable perfusion imaging results because of severe arrhythmia or technical reasons, and missing imaging data; (3) presence of structural heart disease, cancer, respiratory failure, severe liver and kidney diseases, severe infections, and other diseases that may affect LVEF significantly; (4) patients censored for non‐response in follow‐up were also excluded for inapplicability of analysis in threshold determination of sphericity indexes, MACE‐related comparison, model prediction and diagnostic performance. The flowchart of patients enrolled is shown in Figure 1. Each patient was informed of the purpose of this study and signed the informed consent form. The investigation was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (ID 2022‐K‐128).

Baseline Characteristics

The demographic and clinical information included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), clinical history, cardiovascular risk factors such as smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia status, family history of CAD, classification of the New York Heart Association, chest pain symptoms, number of acute myocardial infarction, abnormal electrocardiogram changes, coronary artery stenosis grade, and medication. Laboratory indexes included a complete blood count, serological biomarkers of myocardial injury (TnI, creatine kinase myocardial band and B‐type natriuretic peptides (BNP)), inflammation (C‐reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate), and metabolism (fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin, cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein, high‐density lipoprotein, and homocysteine). The aforementioned information was collected within 1 week before or after the MPI. Echocardiography data were obtained within 3 months of MPI, including measurement of LVEF, end‐diastolic volume, and end‐systolic volume.

Acquisition of Resting Gated MPI

All patients underwent gated resting SPECT MPI using a single IQ‐SPECT dual‐probe scanner (Symbia T16, Siemens Medical System, Germany). 99mTc‐methoxyisobutylisonitrile (MIBI) (740–925 MBq) was injected intravenously after fasting for at least 4 hours. Fatty meals were consumed 15 to 20 minutes before imaging. Electrodes were placed on the patient's chest to obtain gated information. Patients stayed in a supine position with their arms raised and fixed in the support groove, and the acquisition started 60 minutes after the injection. The acquisition lasted for 8 minutes. The parameters were as follows: matrix 128×128, step and shoot mode, 208° rotation, 34 frames, and 25 seconds per frame. Images were reconstructed using the ordered subsets expectation maximization method. All acquisitions were conducted in a fully automated mode.

Quantification Analyses of MPI

Quantification parameters were obtained automatically by QGS and Quantitative Perfusion SPECT (QPS) software (Cedars‐Sinai Medical Center, Los Angeles, CA) following SPECT MPI guidelines. 5 The classification and definition of the quantitative parameters are detailed in Table 1. There were a total of 5 sphericity indexes obtained from non‐gated (QPS) and gated (QGS) images. SI is defined as the ratio of the maximum short‐axis diameter to the long‐axis length measured from the endocardial surface of the 3‐dimensional contours. 12 The value ranges from 0 to 1; the higher the value, the more spherical the heart becomes. EDSIQGS and ESSIQGS were obtained at the end‐diastole and end‐systole, respectively. EI is derived from an iterative approach to constructing a 3‐dimensional ellipsoidal model that best fits the mid‐myocardial segment of the LV. Measurements are obtained for the diameter of the short‐axis (x and y) and the long‐axis length (z), calculated as (1−(xy/z2))0.5. 12 EI is an index reflecting LV elongation, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A lower value indicates a more spherical heart shape. Considering the reported potential of kinetic parameters in diagnosing, risk stratification, and prognostic prediction for CAD, 3 , 4 the summed motion score and summed thickening score (STS) were semiquantified to evaluate LV regional wall motion and thickening, respectively. This evaluation was based on the 17‐segment model of the American Heart Association (AHA) using a 5‐point scale. Bandwidth, SD, and entropy were measured to assess LV systolic dyssynchrony.

Follow‐Up

MACE was recorded based on the most severe condition. All follow‐ups were completed between April and August 2022 using various methods, including an electronic medical record system, phone calls to patients or their relatives, or consultations with their referring physician. Follow‐up time refers to the intervals between the date of SPECT imaging and the occurrence of MACE or the follow‐up date. The primary endpoint was a composite MACE including all‐cause death, nonfatal MI, late revascularization (more than 90 days after SPECT MPI), late coronary angiography, and readmission for HF or unstable angina. The secondary endpoint included all‐cause death, nonfatal MI, and late revascularization. All‐cause death was defined as cardiac death and non‐cardiac death, including sudden death or death without a known cause.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD and categorical data were described using frequency and percentage. Patients were categorized into 4 subgroups stratified by TPD and LVEF. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to determine the cutoff values of TPD and sphericity indexes based on the primary endpoint of all patients. The threshold value (65%) of LVEF was referenced from previous literature. 13 , 14 Threshold adjustments of sphericity indexes by subgroup receiver operating characteristic analysis were conducted as appropriate, considering different baseline characteristics. The univariate comparisons in 5 cohorts (all patients and 4 subgroups) were analyzed using Student's independent samples t test and Chi‐square test. The event‐free survival curve was generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared hierarchically with the log‐rank test.

Sphericity indexes were individually included in a multivariate Cox regression model to assess their predictive performance for primary and secondary outcomes. The analysis used the input method and adjusted for age, sex, coronary artery stenosis, duration of hypertension and diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, BNP, summed motion score, ‐peak ejection rate, entropy, as well as snLVEF and TPD (or 1 of the 2). All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software (version 25.0, Corp., New York) and R language (version 4.3.0, http://www.R‐project.org). A P‐value of less than 0.05 (2‐sided) was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of All Patients and 4 Subgroups

The baseline characteristics of the 5 cohorts were summarized in Table 2 and Table S1. Finally, a total of 727 patients (mean age 59.8±13.5 years, 329 women) were enrolled and followed for 45.1±22.0 months (range 3.0–80.6 months). Out of these, 194 (26.7%) patients experienced MACE, including 11 deaths (1.5%), 3 nonfatal MIs (0.4%), 15 cases of late revascularization (2.1%), 122 readmissions (16.8%), and 43 late coronary angiographies (5.9%). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis provided the optimal threshold value of TPD at 10%. A total of 574 (79.0%) patients displayed TPD<10%, and 153 (21.0%) displayed TPD ≥10%. There were 143 (24.9%) and 51 (33.3%) patients who underwent MACE in the 2 subgroups, respectively. 370 (50.9%) patients had normal LVEF (nLVEF), and 357 (49.1%) displayed snLVEF. There were 96 (25.9%) and 98 (27.5%) patients who underwent MACE in the 2 subgroups, respectively.

Multivariate Analyses of the Sphericity‐Related Predictors for Outcomes

For the primary endpoint, after adjusting for age (men ≥45 years or women ≥55 years), sex, coronary artery stenosis, duration of hypertension and diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, BNP, calcium channel blocker, summed motion score, ‐peak ejection rate, entropy, as well as snLVEF and TPD (or 1 of the 2), Cox regression analysis results (Table 4) revealed that all 5 sphericity indexes were statistically significant predictors in all patients, 2 TPD subgroups, and subgroup nLVEF. Abnormal SIQPS, EDSIQGS, and EIQGS were all statistically significant predictors in subgroup snLVEF.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective clinical study explored the incremental prognostic value of resting MPI sphericity indexes in patients diagnosed or suspected of CAD with preserved LVEF. The main findings are as follows: (1) Sphericity is an independent feature of cardiac remodeling distinct from perfusion or mechanical motion; (2) Several crucial sphericity indexes are still linked to MACE in cohorts with varying perfusion or preserved systolic function, even after accounting for confounding factors; (3) SIQPS>0.65 demonstrated consistent incremental stratification and prognostic value in cohorts with varying severity; (4) The incremental prognostic value of sphericity indexes also applies to patients with snLVEF. The quantification of sphericity indexes can be automatically obtained by MPI without requiring additional imaging time or radiation exposure. This makes it feasible for clinical use to provide more prognostic or risk information.

The LV undergoes changes in myocardial blood supply and contractility under stress conditions. Ventricular dysfunction, whether global or regional, can lead to alterations in ventricular loading conditions, subsequently resulting in modifications to the SI or EI. 12 A large multicenter registry displayed that after multivariate adjustment, post‐stress changes, rather than separate rest or stress sphericity indexes, were more strongly associated with MACE. 12 Although stress MPI was recommended by the guidelines, our study focused on resting MPI, considering that some patients could not undergo stress MPI because of certain contraindications in clinical practice. 4 However, simply 30% of patients with suspected CAD were found with abnormal perfusion in resting SPECT MPI, 17 and quite a few patients showed no significant visual or quantitative perfusion abnormalities attributable to balanced ischemia from multivessel coronary lesions or coronary microvascular dysfunction. 12 , 13 , 18 On the other hand, LVR caused by nonischemic factors such as cardiomyopathy and abnormally increased LV pressure or volume load also needs to be considered when assessing dissatisfaction by solely relying on perfusion evaluation. 10 , 11 Therefore, the combination of perfusion and non‐perfusion indicators can further enhance the identification of such patients with CAD and high risk. The current study aimed to explore additional valuable indices that reflect the geometric characteristics of cardiac disease using resting gated SPECT MPI, with a view to address the limitations of perfusion‐dependent evaluation or stress imaging that may be occasionally constrained. Our results indicate that other MPI parameters, such as wall motion, wall thickening, and LV dyssynchrony, could not independently predict MACE. This may be attributed to the normal systolic function observed in our study population. Our data emphasize that SIQPS is the most relevant index for long‐term outcomes, which is consistent with previous findings. 5 , 7 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the incremental long‐term prognostic value of resting sphericity indexes assessed by SPECT MPI in patients diagnosed or suspected of CAD with preserved LVEF.

LVR is one of the typical pathological features of CAD induced by a series of complex molecular and biological alterations. It could occur even before abnormal examinations or typical clinical symptoms. 10 , 19 During physiological LVR, the heart's elliptical shape persists despite adaptive myocardial growth induced by regular physical activity or pregnancy. 20 However, in pathological conditions, myocardial stretch is caused by the loss of myocytes, leading to reduced or abnormal contractility of LV. Therefore, the compensatory over‐functioning of the remaining normal myocardium becomes necessary. 20 , 21 Prominent volume expansion and proliferation of the extracellular matrix after acute myocardial infarction and chronically increased pressure load of LV caused by hypertension, cardiomyopathies, valvulopathies, antineoplastic drugs, arrhythmias, and senescence of cardiac fibroblasts may transform LV from an adaptive and physiological process to a maladaptive and pathological outcome. 10 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 At this point, the SI will increase as the heart changes gradually from an ellipsoid to a sphere. The deterioration of LV structure and function can lead to a decline in heart function, potentially resulting in HF and increasing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. 11 , 24

At present, volumetric parameters are routinely used in LVR evaluation in clinical practice, while the application of SI and EI by SPECT MPI is still not well‐established. 22 Nitta et al demonstrated the effective performance of ESSI in early identification of LVR in patients with normal perfusion. A positive correlation was found between ESSI and early, gradually increased end‐systolic volume (end‐systolic volume ≤40 mL). 10 Furthermore, a previous study involving 93 inpatients who underwent 99mTc‐tetrofosmin stress MPI reported that ESSI might be a clinical indicator for congestive HF. 19 In addition, abnormal EI is also associated with significant LVR and functional abnormalities in a gated cadmium zinc telluride SPECT study. 22 Similarly, in a clinical study involving 14 016 patients, SI and EI were both strongly associated with MACE including death, nonfatal MI, unstable angina, or late revascularization. 12 In the current study, more occurrences of MACE and stable prognostic performance of sphericity indexes were found in patients with TPD<10%, suggesting the limitations of simple perfusion evaluation, and meanwhile, the potential of LVR evaluation in the early detection of cardiac abnormalities.

Our results indicate that SIQPS>0.65 was the most reliable predictor for MACE in patients with varying perfusion or cardiac functional conditions. The preserved systolic function in our study population was hypothesized to be one of the main reasons. In fact, ESSI partly represents the LV systolic function because in a well‐contracted LV, the long‐axis diameter decreases more significantly than the maximum transverse diameter of the short axis. 25 This may also contribute to the limited predictive performance of EI in the present study. Firstly, the calculation methods of SI and EI are different. The SI was measured by using the endocardial surface of the 3‐dimensional contours as the boundary, while the EI was constructed using an iterative method to create the 3‐dimensional ellipsoid model that best fits the middle segment of the LV myocardium. 12 Additionally, these 2 indices were obtained using different computational techniques and image processing software.

Notably, LVR can also be evaluated by other noninvasive tests. The results of a multicenter study revealed that extremely high sphericity measured by magnetic resonance imaging is a predictor of HF. 26 Furthermore, SI by magnetic resonance imaging has been demonstrated capability to reflect LVR and the severity of nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy. 27 Raghava et al 28 have demonstrated that the pattern of LVR is related to future changes in LVEF evaluated by echocardiography. Centripetal hypertrophy is associated with the future incidence of preserved LVEF, while eccentric hypertrophy is likely to progress to reduced LVEF. However, its clinical application is limited because of variations between different software and observers, as well as the lengthy magnetic resonance imaging inspection time. SPECT MPI evaluates sphericity from a 3‐dimensional perspective, with high repeatability. 29 Additionally, the sphericity index values may not be interchangeable across various modalities and software. 4 Moreover, ensuring proper quality control from image acquisition to processing is crucial for precise quantification of perfusion and LVR indexes.

Study Limitations

First, 44 patients with no definite outcomes were excluded from the current study because of their inapplicability in some MACE‐related analyses, which might influence the results of Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. However, the low loss‐to‐follow‐up rate (5.7%) and their almost identical baseline characteristics with the currently enrolled cohort (Table S7) make the conclusions of this study convincing. Second, the incremental prognostic value of sphericity parameters is currently being used in resting SPECT practices and in patients with CAD and preserved LVEF. Further verifications need to be conducted using other imaging modalities and in patients with reduced LVEF. Third, threshold adjustments to the sphericity indexes were made within subgroups (primarily in TPD ≥10% and nLVEF) by considering various baseline characteristics. Additionally, the diagnostic sensitivity of sphericity indexes, as determined by the receiver operating characteristic curve, was unsatisfactory, possibly because of bias in the enrolled cohort. Fourth, although prior studies 30 and our investigation (R=0.727, P<0.001) have proved that gated SPECT MPI has a good correlation with echocardiography for measuring LVEF, echocardiographic LVEF was used to group patients in this study, considering the susceptibleness of the measurement of LVEF by SPECT. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Additionally, echocardiography, as the key technique, determined the current threshold of snLVEF. 13 , 14 , 15

CONCLUSIONS

For patients diagnosed or suspected of CAD with preserved LVEF or supra‐normal LVEF, resting sphericity indexes by SPECT MPI displayed incremental long‐term prognostic value. Among these indicators, SIQPS is more promising because of its stable capacity for prognostic stratification across different perfusion or preserved functional conditions.
