Accounting for Genomic Testing Costs in Cost-Effectiveness Evaluations: Challenges and Lessons Learned from the NICE Single Technology Appraisal of Erdafitinib for Treating Unresectable or Metastatic Urothelial Cancer with FGFR3 Alterations After a PD-1 or PD-L1 Inhibitor
Authors: Andrea Fernández Coves, Mirre Scholte, Jonathan Emerson, Robert Wolff, Teebah Abu-Zahra, Jiongyu Chen, Kevin McDermott, Nigel Armstrong, Manuela Joore, Willem Witlox
Published: 2026

Introduction

Precision medicine incorporates genomic and molecular profiling into clinical decision-making, enabling treatments to be tailored to the unique characteristics of each patient and their disease [1, 2]. For instance, therapies may target epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) mutations, B-Raf proto-oncogene (BRAF) variants, or programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression levels [3]. Over the past decade, the implementation of precision medicine in oncology has resulted in improved survival rates, reduced off-target toxicities, and enhanced quality of life compared with non-targeted therapies [3–7]. However, precision oncology presents new challenges. Alongside the high acquisition costs of precision therapies, influenced by the complex research, development, and smaller treatment populations [8], patients must undergo additional genomic testing to determine eligibility [4]. These diagnostic requirements introduce further costs that can influence cost-effectiveness evaluations, especially when genomic testing is not (yet) part of routine clinical practice, and the prevalence of actionable mutations is low [9, 10]. Applying the full cost of genomic testing to populations with low prevalence can substantially increase incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), which may risk discouraging innovation [11, 12]. In contrast, in larger populations, testing costs are distributed across more patients, thereby reducing their impact. Yet, oncology is increasingly moving towards molecularly defined subgroups as more targeted therapies emerge [3, 8]. This commentary highlights the challenges and key learnings of integrating genomic testing costs in cost-effectiveness evaluations from an External Assessment Group (EAG) perspective, using the example of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Single Technology Appraisal (STA) of erdafitinib for treating unresectable or metastatic urothelial cancer (UC) with fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) alterations after a programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1) or PD-L1 inhibitor in adults.

Case Study: Erdafitinib for Treating Metastatic or Unresectable FGFR-Altered Urothelial Cancer

As part of the NICE’s STA process, NICE invited the manufacturer, Johnson & Johnson, to submit evidence on erdafitinib (Balversa®). The submission assessed the clinical and cost-effectiveness of erdafitinib over a lifetime horizon for the treatment of adults with unresectable or metastatic UC harbouring FGFR3 genetic alterations, using a de novo partitioned survival model. In this appraisal, the target population included patients who had received at least one prior line of therapy for unresectable or metastatic UC that contained a PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor. The main comparators considered were paclitaxel ± carboplatin, implemented as a basket of paclitaxel monotherapy and paclitaxel in combination with carboplatin, weighted 3:1, respectively [13]. A key consideration in the appraisal was determining the estimated per-patient cost of FGFR3 gene alteration testing used to determine eligibility for erdafitinib treatment.

In its initial submission, the company assumed that FGFR3 gene alteration testing could be incorporated into an existing next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel at an additional cost of £37.33 per test. Based on an estimated prevalence of FGFR3 alterations of 16.6%, the cost per identified patient was calculated as the additional cost per test (£37.33) divided by the prevalence, yielding an estimated value of £224.85 [13]. At the first Appraisal Committee Meeting (ACM1), however, the National Health Service (NHS) England Cancer Drugs Fund clinical lead noted that, unlike appraisals in other indications, which used a similar approach [14–16], patients with metastatic UC were not routinely tested for targeted therapies, as erdafitinib represented the first targeted treatment available for this population [17]. Therefore, there was no existing genomic testing in routine practice to which FGFR3 gene alteration testing could be added, as assumed by the company [17]. Following the ACM1, the committee concluded that the full cost of implementing an NGS panel, reported as £1282 per test (£7723 per identified patient), should be applied [17]. In response to the draft guidance [18], the company argued that cheaper polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or reverse transcriptase (RT)-qPCR methods could suffice to identify eligible patients. It further stated that testing for FGFR3 gene alteration is already listed on the National Genomic Test Directory (NGTD) and technically available in NHS laboratories, even in the absence of other targeted therapies for metastatic UC. According to the company, implementing the full cost of an NGS panel could create inequities, with testing expenses representing a much larger fraction of total costs for patients with rare indications and a short life expectancy compared with those with more common diseases with longer survival [18]. In its revised base case, it therefore assumed the cost of adding a test to an existing panel of £74.65 per patient tested (£449.70 per identified patient), reflecting the fact that FGFR3 testing requires both RNA and DNA analysis (two tests per sample).

The EAG acknowledged the uncertainty on the per-patient genomic testing cost and adopted the company’s revised estimate in its base case [18, 19]. Additionally, the EAG modelled the committee’s preferred cost of adding a full testing panel as a scenario analysis, which significantly increased the ICER (25% increase) [19]. At the second committee meeting, the NHS Genomic Medicine Service (GMS) clarified that the National Genomic Test Directory (NGTD) recommends two panels (RNA and DNA) for FGFR3 gene alteration detection, each of which carries a tariff. Nonetheless, the GMS stated that a confidential marginal tariff rate, instead of a full tariff, could be applied in this case. It was unclear to the EAG what the application of the ‘marginal tariff rate’ entailed and how it was calculated or implemented within current NHS genomic testing frameworks. The GMS explained that the company’s lower cost estimate, based on the assumption that the test for FGFR3 gene alteration could be added to an existing panel already in use, was not reflective of current practice for this indication (i.e., there was no existing panel carried out in current practice). However, it also noted that a full tariff would not reflect the availability of the FGFR3 tests on the NGTD, nor likely future reductions in testing costs [20].

Key Learnings

This appraisal highlights several important considerations when estimating diagnostic testing costs, particularly for rare diseases or therapies that are the first to require genomic testing in a disease area. Cost estimates should be carefully tailored to the specific decision context and care pathway, with assumptions supported by guidelines and empirical data, or, in the absence of these, by alternative sources of evidence (e.g., structured expert elicitation) [21]. When genomic testing costs are uncertain, exploring multiple plausible scenarios for testing costs supports robust decision-making. This is particularly critical when the future trajectory of these testing costs remains unclear, as in this case, where broader NGS adoption or new targeted therapies might affect future costs. In practice, anticipating such cost evolution may require dynamic modelling approaches that account for uptake and technology diffusion effects [22]. This may include sensitivity and value of information analyses that reflect the gradual incorporation of new comparators over time.

The appraisal further illustrates the challenges that arise when the existing diagnostic infrastructure is not routinely applied in the patient population of interest. For small populations, allocating full testing costs may substantially inflate ICERs, since high fixed costs are distributed over a few eligible patients. This can make targeted therapies less cost-effective and may pose hurdles for precision medicine in rare diseases or settings where therapies are the first requiring genomic testing in a disease area. To show the impact, alternative approaches, such as applying reduced costs or considering future diffusion and wider adoption of testing technologies, may more realistically and equitably reflect costs. The NICE health technology evaluations manual states that committees should consider a non-reference-case analysis (alongside the reference-case analysis) in which a particular cost is apportioned or adjusted when there is an established plan to change practice or service delivery in the NHS. Testing costs and practices may differ significantly across disease areas, particularly related to the routine use of NGS panels. Early engagement with the NHS GMS is essential to ensure an accurate understanding of the testing landscape, clarify testing requirements, costs, and potential future reductions, and prevent complications in the later stages of the appraisal process. Costs should also align with NGTD specifications rather than assuming cheaper alternatives, such as PCR testing, which may not capture all relevant genomic alterations. From an ethical perspective, limiting access to genomic testing or targeted therapies solely because a disease has low prevalence or short survival raises critical equity questions, as these groups may be disproportionately affected by testing costs. Developing targeted therapies without considering the infrastructure and costs required to identify eligible patients may create inequities. One approach could be to consider the cost of testing across all NHS patients potentially eligible for target therapies and assess whether it is cost-effective when applied universally versus selectively.

As more targeted therapies become available, the fixed costs of testing can be spread across a larger population, reducing the per-patient cost and supporting both equitable access and more sustainable cost-effectiveness. Manufacturers should also consider the additional testing costs in their development of treatments and pricing strategies, rather than relying on healthcare payers to absorb these expenses. From an economic perspective, including these genomic testing costs in cost-effectiveness evaluations reduces the maximum price at which a therapy remains cost-effective, meaning manufacturers effectively bear these costs through a lower permissible price. However, this applies only when the product is priced at the cost-effectiveness threshold; if priced below it, the payer may instead absorb the testing costs. Guidance emphasises the need for coordinated processes to share information promptly, ensuring that cost estimates reflect actual NHS practice and support appropriate commercial discussions and implementation planning [23].

Conclusions

The erdafitinib appraisal highlights the significant impact that assumptions about diagnostic testing costs can have on cost-effectiveness estimates, particularly in rare diseases or therapies in disease areas where testing capacity exists but is not currently deployed in routine practice. The availability, scope, and implementation of genomic testing must be carefully defined, and appraisals should explicitly account for uncertainty in testing costs through scenario analyses. Inequities may arise when targeted therapies are developed without sufficient consideration of the infrastructure and costs required to identify eligible patients. Incorporating cost-reduction strategies or modelling the expected trajectory of testing costs over time can provide a more realistic and equitable reflection of future practice. The question of who should bear the costs of diagnostic testing remains a key policy and ethical debate. Establishing systematic guidance on how to integrate genomic testing costs into health technology appraisals would improve consistency, transparency, and equitable decision-making, helping to ensure that innovative therapies are appropriately evaluated and accessible to patients.
