Spectroscopic Signatures of Doping in Thin Films of Semiconducting Single‐Walled Carbon Nanotubes
Authors: Jana Zaumseil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSemiconducting single‐walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) have unique optical, electronic, and mechanical properties that enable a wide range of applications. Changing and controlling the charge carrier density in thin films of SWCNTs by chemical, electrochemical, or electrostatic doping lies at the heart of many (opto‐)electronic devices, such as thermoelectric generators, electrochromic cells, or field‐effect transistors. The accumulation of charge carriers (holes or electrons) also affects the optical properties of nanotubes, especially their visible to far‐infrared absorption, near‐infrared fluorescence and electroluminescence as well as their Raman modes. The related spectral changes give insights into the interaction of charge carriers with SWCNTs on a fundamental level but also enable quantification and spatial resolution of carrier densities in operating devices. Here we review the current understanding of various spectroscopic signatures of doping in thin films of semiconducting SWCNTs, how they can be used to explore carrier concentration‐dependent properties, and how they are applied in optoelectronic devices.

Introduction

The unique optical, electronic, and mechanical properties of single‐walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) are a direct result of their molecular structure, which can be viewed as a seamless cylinder of a rolled‐up sheet of graphene consisting of hexagonally arranged, sp2‐hybridized carbon atoms (see Figure 1a). The diameter of these tubes ranges from 0.6 to 2 nm and their length can vary from tens of nanometers to many micrometers or even longer. Due to this large aspect ratio, SWCNTs are considered to be 1D semiconductors. Their electronic properties directly depend on the roll‐up vector ( Ch ), which is defined by the indices (n,m) [1]. Based on the possible combinations of indices, about one‐third of SWCNTs are metallic, and two‐thirds are semiconducting. To tune their electronic properties for different applications, such as thermoelectric generators [2], flexible and transparent electrodes [3], or infrared electrochromic devices [4], they must be p‐ or n‐doped to different carrier concentrations in a controllable and reproducible manner.

Previous reviews on doping of carbon nanotubes have focused on various chemical doping methods and possible applications of p‐ or n‐doped nanotubes, while discussing their carrier density‐dependent optical properties only briefly [5, 6, 7]. However, the spectroscopic/optical features of SWCNTs change significantly with doping and the resulting spectral signatures can be used—directly and indirectly—to characterize and track the doping process. For the purpose of this review, we will focus solely on semiconducting SWCNTs as they are more commonly used and investigated in (opto‐)electronic devices. The aim is to provide an overview of the current understanding of the interactions of holes and electrons with the lattice vibrations, electronic ground and excited states of semiconducting SWCNTs, and to demonstrate how the resulting spectroscopic signatures of doping can be utilized to detect and even quantify the overall or local carrier densities in nanotube thin films and devices in situ and in operando.

Following a brief description of the general electronic structure, most important optical transitions (absorption, fluorescence), and Raman modes of semiconducting SWCNTs, we will consider the formation of nanotube thin films and networks with random or aligned orientation by a variety of solution‐based methods. Subsequently, doping strategies for SWCNTs from chemical doping to electrochemical and electrostatic doping will be introduced (Section 2). They enable the observation of carrier density‐dependent spectroscopic and optical signatures such as charge‐induced bleaching, shifts of the SWCNT absorption peaks, fluorescence quenching, and trion emission, as well as doping‐dependent changes of Raman modes, which will be discussed in detail in Section 3. Finally, selected examples of the direct utilization of these spectroscopic signatures to characterize and understand SWCNT‐based devices will be presented in Section 4.

To introduce the electronic properties of semiconducting nanotubes, we start by considering the very often investigated small‐diameter (6,5) SWCNTs (diameter 0.76 nm) in comparison to large‐diameter SWCNTs (typically in the range of 1.1–1.6 nm). The density of states (DOS) for (6,5) SWCNTs is shown in Figure 1b. The sharp van Hove singularities of the valence and conduction bands are the result of their 1D band structure, which can be derived from graphene via the zone‐folding model or through density‐functional theory calculations [9, 10, 11, 12]. In the simplest picture of doping in terms of changing the majority charge carrier density, the valence band is gradually emptied, that is, holes are accumulated, when the nanotubes are p‐doped. When the SWCNTs are n‐doped, the conduction band is increasingly populated with electrons. As the bandgap of semiconducting nanotubes is inversely proportional to their diameter, the ionization potential (for removing electrons from the valence band) and the electron affinity (for accepting electrons into the conduction band) also depend directly on the diameter and (n,m) species of the SWCNT. As shown in Figure 1b, the bandgap of a (13,5) nanotube, which has a much larger diameter (1.28 nm) than (6,5) SWCNTs, is significantly smaller. The (13,5) nanotube and similar SWCNTs with large diameters are both easier to reduce and to oxidize compared to small‐diameter nanotubes. This dependence of the nanotube redox potentials on diameter has also been shown experimentally (see Figure 1c) [8, 13].

Likewise, the optical properties of SWCNTs are determined by their chirality. The energies of the allowed transitions (here labeled E11, E22, … Eii, although, the label Sii is also often used for semiconducting nanotubes) depend on diameter and (n,m) of the nanotube as summarized in the Kataura plot [14, 15]. Figure 1d shows a representative absorption spectrum of a dispersion of (6,5) SWCNTs featuring sharp E11 and E22 absorption peaks in the near‐infrared and visible wavelength range, respectively. The corresponding photoluminescence (PL) spectrum features a narrow E11 emission peak that is only slightly (a few nm) redshifted from the E11 absorption. Importantly, these transitions arise from excitons [16] and their energies are modified from band‐to‐band transitions by electron–electron repulsion (self‐energy) and the exciton binding energy [17, 18]. Both E11 absorption and emission are highly polarized along the nanotube axis [19]. Furthermore, phonon sidebands are visible, which reflect momentum‐forbidden transitions in the exciton manifold [20]. We will see in Sections 3.1. and 3.2. that both absorption and PL spectra of SWCNTs are highly sensitive to charge carriers.

Another common spectroscopic technique for the characterization of SWCNTs is resonance Raman spectroscopy [21, 22]. Figure 1e shows a typical Raman spectrum of a network of (6,5) nanotubes (excitation with 532 nm laser, close to the E22 absorption), highlighting the most important Raman‐active modes. The diameter‐dependent radial breathing mode (RBM) appears between 100 and 350 cm−1. Its wavenumber is inversely proportional to the nanotube diameter (here at 311 cm−1 for the small‐diameter (6,5) nanotubes) and thus enables a direct diameter estimation [23]. The tangential G+ and G− modes represent the C─C bond stretching parallel and perpendicular to the nanotube axis [24]. The additional E2 mode between G+ and G− is only observable for highly purified nanotube samples with small diameters [25, 26]. The D‐mode (1300–1350 cm−1) is activated by defects in the carbon lattice, such that the D/G+ ratio can be utilized as a metric for the defect density [27, 28]. The 2D (also often labeled G′) mode is characterized by the collective breathing motion of the carbon atoms in the hexagons of the graphene lattice [22, 29]. The wavenumber and intensity of both D‐ and 2D modes depend significantly on excitation energy [30, 31]. All Raman modes are sensitive to doping with the G+, D, and 2D modes showing characteristic and significant shifts as discussed in Section 3.3.

Absorption, fluorescence, and Raman spectra are direct reflections of the electronic structure and phonon modes of SWCNTs. Analyzing and quantifying the impact of doping on them can provide deep insights into the distribution (homogeneous/inhomogeneous) and localization/de‐localization of carriers, possible charge traps, formation of quasiparticles (e.g., trions, polarons), and electron‐phonon interactions. A better understanding of these interactions also helps to characterize and optimize SWCNT‐based (opto‐)electronic devices. Before going into the details of the optical and spectroscopic properties of thin films and networks of doped nanotubes and their applications, we will briefly review how SWCNT films with different morphologies can be created and subsequently doped by different methods.

Doping of SWCNT Thin Films

To study the impact of charge carriers on the electronic and optical properties of SWCNTs, we first need well‐controlled and reproducible samples. That is, the nanotubes should ideally be purified and sorted in a way that allows for well‐resolved spectroscopic features to be observed, e.g., only certain nanotube diameters, purely semiconducting, or even monochiral SWCNTs. These samples enable reliable analysis of and conclusions from the recorded spectra. In general, doping of nanotubes can be performed in dispersion or in thin films. Here, we will focus on thin films (both random and aligned) that are processed from type‐sorted aqueous or organic dispersions. A wide range of doping methods for carbon nanotubes has been developed and applied over the past decades, including different types of chemical doping, electrochemical, and electrostatic doping. Each of these methods has specific areas of application and can be employed to investigate the properties of SWCNTs at different, high or low, and ideally precisely tunable hole or electron concentrations.

SWCNT Film Formation

The creation of thin or thick films of SWCNTs depends to a large degree on the preceding processing steps, i.e., dispersion and purification method. While fibers, large area mats, or composites of SWCNTs are usually processed directly from as‐grown nanotubes, thin films of purely semiconducting nanotubes for fundamental studies first require liquid dispersion and sorting [32]. Sorting of SWCNTs not only by type (metallic/semiconducting) but also by (n,m) species is particularly important when investigating small variations (e.g., peak shifts) in the spectroscopic signatures of doped nanotubes. Overlapping absorption and emission peaks of SWCNTs with similar diameters or possibly appearing doping‐induced features would make the assignment and analysis of carrier‐density‐dependent spectral changes very difficult. Ideally, the investigated samples should contain only a single nanotube species. If this is not possible, a limited number of spectrally well‐separated semiconducting SWCNT chiralities or at least purely semiconducting nanotubes are preferred for device applications and to investigate doping‐dependent changes.

Over the past twenty years, a large variety of nanotube dispersion and‐ more importantly ‐ sorting techniques have been developed, optimized, and scaled‐up to various degrees [33, 34, 35]. These include density‐gradient ultracentrifugation [36, 37], gel‐chromatography [38, 39, 40], aqueous two‐phase separation [41, 42, 43], and selective dispersion with conjugated polymers [44]. Only the last method uses organic solvents instead of aqueous surfactant solutions. Selective polymer‐wrapping of SWCNTs was introduced by Nish et al. and other groups [44, 45] and enables easy, reproducible, and scalable dispersion of purely semiconducting and even monochiral nanotubes in solvents such as toluene or xylene [46, 47, 48, 49]. However, there is only a limited choice of conjugated polymers (usually polyfluorene co‐polymers [50], polythiophenes [51], and polycarbazoles [52, 53]) that can be used efficiently to sort specific small‐diameter (mainly (6,5) and (7,5) SWCNTs) [44, 46] or mixed semiconducting large‐diameter nanotubes [54, 55, 56]. The obtained polymer‐wrapped and sorted SWCNTs in organic solvents are easily processed into networks and thin films that can be integrated in electronic devices. Aqueous dispersions of SWCNTs and the corresponding sorting methods usually offer a wider range of extractable (n,m) species, including near‐monochiral large‐diameter nanotubes [57]. They have also been used to create thin films, however, their deposition is accompanied by various problems such as the high surface tension and low vapor pressure of water as well as foaming due to the presence of surfactants [58].

The most common but also most wasteful method for the creation of nanotube networks is spin‐coating, usually of polymer‐wrapped SWCNTs from dispersions in organic solvents. To achieve sufficiently dense films, very high concentrations are required [64], which may lead to aggregation of the nanotubes over a short period of time [59]. Nevertheless, it is possible to create fairly uniform and well‐controlled networks (see Figure 2a) with different densities depending on the nanotube concentration [65]. The deposited nanotubes stick very well to the substrate surface and to each other through strong van‐der‐Waals forces, such that they cannot be detached again by solvent rinsing. Hence, repeated spin‐coating steps are possible to increase the network density. Excess surfactants or wrapping polymer can be partially removed by rinsing with suitable solvents [66] or through post‐processing techniques to cleave the polymers and decompose them [67, 68, 69, 70]. The SWCNTs in spin‐coated networks are oriented randomly but highly in‐plane. Nanotube films of similarly random orientation can be produced by aerosol‐jet printing [60, 71, 72] (see Figure 2b) or inkjet printing [73, 74, 75] as well as ultrasonic spraying [76] and screen‐printing [77] for larger areas. In these cases, the in‐plane orientation is less pronounced, and much thicker films (20–100 nm) can be produced in selected areas by repeated printing steps using less material than spin‐coating [78]. Due to the variability of nanotube orientation and porosity, the effective optical density of thin films may vary, depending on the deposition method.

Hopping of charge carriers over the many junctions between nanotubes in random networks limits the overall charge transport [79] and hence several methods for aligning nanotubes during the deposition process were developed [80]. Recently, large area alignment was accomplished through the use of ordering effects at the interface between the nanotube dispersion and other solvents (e.g., water, 2‐butene‐1,4‐diol or ethylene glycol) during dip‐coating [81, 82] or tangential flow interfacial self‐assembly (TaFISA) [61, 62], which can be optimized to yield monolayers of densely packed and highly parallel nanotubes (see Figure 2c,d). These arrays of nanotubes are mostly employed for high‐performance, short‐channel nanotube field‐effect transistors [83, 84, 85].

Finally, a convenient way to create dense and very thick (>20 nm) films of SWCNTs is filtration onto a dissolvable filter membrane (e.g., mixed cellulose ester) with small pore sizes (usually less than 250 nm) [63, 86, 87]. The thickness of the film (filter cake, see Figure 2e) is determined by the filtered volume and concentration of the SWCNTs dispersion. Subsequently, the filter membranes can be cut into the desired shape, and the nanotube networks can be transferred onto a target substrate by wetting the filter membrane with solvent and pressing the nanotube side onto the receiving substrate surface. The membrane is carefully dissolved with acetone and the obtained film is washed to remove all residues (see Figure 2f). Through optimized filtration flow and the innate grooves of many filter membranes (e.g., polyvinylpyrrolidone‐coated polycarbonate), it is even possible to macroscopically align the nanotubes within these filtered films [88, 89, 90, 91]. Such large‐area alignment is also helpful to reveal the dependence of optical transitions on the polarization of incident light. While the Eii transitions are highly polarized and enhanced along the nanotube axis, Eij transitions (e.g., E12) are only allowed for cross polarization and even then very weak [92].

Overall, the choice of method for the creation of nanotube films depends on the amount of available material as well as the intended application, doping, and characterization methods. Importantly, nanotube films and networks are partially porous (depending on deposition method) but essentially insoluble in common solvents. Thus, they can be treated with different dopant solutions or immersed in electrolytes without significantly changing their network morphology. The alignment of nanotubes within an array or film can be exploited to enhance the detection of certain doping‐dependent spectral features (e.g., intersubband plasmon excitations, see Section 3.1) or to increase Raman signals by aligning the polarization direction of the laser to the nanotubes (see Section 3.3). For most of the described doping‐induced spectroscopic signatures in this review, random networks were used unless noted otherwise.

Chemical Doping

Although, conventional semiconductor doping through substitution of lattice atoms similar to doping of silicon was demonstrated for nanotubes as well (e.g., substitution of carbon with nitrogen or boron) [93, 94, 95], it is very rarely applied and will not be discussed here. We will also not consider the special case of encapsulated dopants inside of nanotubes, as they require very special reaction conditions [96, 97, 98]. Instead, we will focus on molecular doping, where the dopant is added externally and in relatively high concentrations (a few %) because this method is used predominantly for nanotube thin films and practical devices. A large number of molecular dopants were tested on SWCNTs and a variety of doping mechanisms were proposed. In general, chemical doping of carbon nanotubes (in analogy to organic semiconductors) can be described as charge transfer doping, Brønsted acid/base doping, or Lewis acid/base doping.

Charge transfer doping is the most common method and typically involves either full (integer charge transfer, ICT) or partial electron transfer (with the formation of a charge transfer complex) from the nanotube to an acceptor molecule or from a donor to the nanotube; similar to charge transfer doping of organic semiconducting polymers [99, 100]. The efficiency of doping depends largely on the oxidation or reduction potential of the dopant in comparison to the position of the valence or conduction band edge of the nanotubes (see Figure 1c). The ionization potential of SWCNTs ranges roughly between 4.5 and 5 eV depending on diameter, which makes p‐doping with a wide range of common electron acceptors fairly easy and stable. Notably, most semiconducting SWCNTs (especially those with large diameters) are already slightly p‐doped in the presence of oxygen and water, i.e., when processed in air [101].

Commonly applied oxidants for charge‐transfer p‐type doping of SWCNTs include AuCl3 [102, 103, 104], 2,3,5,6‐tetrafluoro‐7,7,8,8‐tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4TCNQ) [105, 106, 107], and triethyl‐oxonium hexachloroantimonate [108, 109, 110] (OA, all molecular structures are shown in Figure 3a). Importantly, the holes in the ‘oxidized’ nanotubes are not completely delocalized but still interact with the remaining counterions (i.e., the anions of the reduced p‐dopant) due to poor screening of the Coulomb attraction [87]. This localization is expected to reduce the carrier mobility. To mitigate this effect, large fluorinated boron clusters (e.g., dodeca(2,3,4,5,6‐pentafluoro‐benzyloxy)‐hyperclosododecaborane, DDB‐F60, see Figure 3a) [111, 112] were introduced recently as effective p‐dopants for SWCNTs. Their bulky anions do not localize the created holes as strongly as smaller anions, resulting in higher carrier mobilities and thus higher electrical conductivities for the same degree of doping. Consequently, higher maximum power factors (as required for thermoelectric generators [2] were reached for DDB‐F60‐doped films of large‐diameter nanotubes compared to OA‐doped films [111].

While some localization of carriers in SWCNTs by counterions takes place, it should be noted that compared to many organic semiconductors, the rigid lattice of carbon nanotubes results in low reorganization energies [113] and no specific charge‐transfer (CT) state absorption is observed in donor/acceptor heterojunctions with SWCNTs as the donor [114]. Both properties indicate that photoinduced electron transfer and subsequent charge separation and transport are fast and efficient in nanotubes, which is advantageous for photovoltaic cells and photodiodes [115].

Beyond p‐doping with strong oxidants, it is also possible to use a dopant with an apparently insufficient electron affinity but strong Lewis acidity, that is, the molecule can accept an electron pair due to an unoccupied orbital. Strong Lewis acids are, for example, BF3 and SbF5 and have been used for p‐doping of nanotubes [116, 117]. A recent example is the Lewis acid tris(penta‐fluorophenyl)borane (BCF, see Figure 3a), which can be applied to dope even small‐diameter semiconducting nanotubes that are wrapped with nitrogen‐containing Lewis basic (i.e., with free electron pairs) conjugated polymers (e.g., a polyfluorene with bipyridine moieties, PFO‐BPy) [118]. The doping mechanism is not entirely clear [119], but it might be assumed that BCF forms a complex with the bipyridine units followed by protonation of the polymer by a Brønsted acidic complex of BCF with residual water (in the organic solvent), and finally electron transfer from the SWCNT to the protonated polymer. Compared to other common dopants for polymer‐wrapped SWCNTs in organic solvents (e.g., AuCl3 or F4TCNQ), BCF enables well‐controlled and stable p‐doping without unwanted aggregation [118].

Another type of p‐doping for carbon nanotubes (mostly applied to mixed semiconducting/metallic samples) is treatment with oxidizing or Brønsted acids (e.g., H2SO4, HNO3, or HCl) [120, 121, 122], which has been extended further to solid acids (e.g., phosphotungstic acid) [123] and polymer acids (PAA—polyacrylic acid, PSS—poly(p‐styrene‐sulfonic acid)), which also enable dispersion of doped SWCNTs in water [124, 125]. The stability of the achieved p‐doping varies depending on the acids and especially on the structure of their anions. Based on the hard and soft acid and base (HSAB) concept, coordination between the soft nanotube cation (delocalized holes) and a soft anion (large, low charge density, high polarizability, e.g., bis(nonafluoro‐butanesulfonyl)imide, NFSI−) leads to a higher stability of the p‐doped state compared to a hard anion (e.g., chloride or nitrate) and the soft nanotube cation [121]. Here, it should be noted that the redox potential of oxygen (e.g., dissolved in water) becomes more positive with decreasing pH (O2 + 4H+ + 4e− ⇆ 2 H2O) and thus also promotes p‐doping in the presence of acids [101].

While p‐doping of carbon nanotubes is easy to realize and reasonably stable, n‐doping of SWCNTs has proven to be much more challenging [7]. The electron affinity of semiconducting nanotubes (especially with large diameters, see Figure 1c) is within an accessible range for typical molecular reducing agents, however, n‐doping of SWCNTs is usually not very stable unless films are encapsulated [126, 127]. Reports on stability under ambient conditions vary widely [128, 129, 130, 131]. Due to these stability issues, detailed studies of the optical properties of chemically n‐doped SWCNTs are also less common than those for p‐doped nanotubes.

A variety of strong reducing agents were applied to create n‐doped SWCNTs networks such as decamethylcobaltocene (DMC, see Figure 3b) [132], a pentamethylrhodocene dimer [133], benzyl viologen [134, 135], and even NaBH4 [126]. Many of these n‐dopants are not air‐stable themselves and require working under inert conditions. When n‐doped SWCNT samples were subsequently exposed to air, they were quickly de‐doped again, which would be a major problem for thermoelectric applications. A common n‐dopant that provides at least some air‐stability is the branched polymer polyethyleneimine (PEI, see Figure 3b) [136, 137, 138, 139]. As a Lewis base, it donates the free electron pairs of the nitrogen to the nanotubes while also covering/passivating them. Indeed, most molecules with free electron pairs (e.g., those containing phosphine, amine, or imine groups) [140, 141] and even the oxygen‐rich polyethylene glycol (PEG) [142, 143, 144] induce a degree of n‐doping at least in large diameter or metallic nanotubes.

Surprisingly strong and stable n‐doping of nanotubes has been observed for a range of anions with weak reducing power (e.g., OH−, Cl−) when their corresponding cations (Na+ or K+) were complexed with crown ethers in aprotic solvents, which resulted in an enhanced nucleophilicity of the now poorly stabilized anions [128]. Similar to the impact of large counterions for p‐doping of SWCNTs, the crown ether‐based cations behave as soft Lewis acids that stabilize the n‐doped nanotubes as soft bases. Nevertheless, sorted semiconducting nanotubes that were n‐doped with benzo‐18‐crown‐6 ether and potassium hydroxide showed significant de‐doping after exposure to air. Smaller diameter nanotubes were de‐doped even faster than larger diameter nanotubes, which reflects their different and diameter‐dependent electron affinities (see Figure 1c) in relation to oxygen‐ and/or water‐related oxidation reactions [145]. Further studies with potassium benzo‐18‐crown‐6 ether complex salts [126] and other n‐dopants (e.g., PEI) found a substantial film thickness dependence of the stability of n‐doping over time, with thicker films being significantly more stable [131].

Finally, derivatives of 2‐aryl‐1,3‐dimethyl‐2,3‐dihydro‐1H‐benzo[d]imidazole (DMBI, see Figure 3b) [129, 146, 147] can be used for fairly stable n‐doping of dense networks of SWCNTs. They are reducing agents that can act via a hydride or a hydrogen and one‐electron transfer mechanism [148]. They can also be used for photo‐induced electron doping [149]. These DMBI derivatives and the resulting cationic forms are stable in air. Nakashima et al. showed that full coverage of SWCNTs by o‐MeO‐DMBI led to protection of the nanotube surface from oxygen and thus substantially increased air‐stability of the n‐doped films [130]. Transferring this approach to vapor‐deposited DMBI derivatives enabled the creation of stable SWCNT‐thermoelectric generators with alternating pn‐junctions [150].

As shown above, the counterions of the charged nanotubes play an important role for doping efficiency and stability, but also for the (de‐)localization of the induced charges. An intriguing variation of chemical doping that replaces the redox‐active counterions of the oxidizing or reducing agent with redox‐inactive and typically more hydrophobic and larger counterions such as bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (TFSI) or tetrabutylammonium (TBA) was originally developed for organic semiconductors by Yamashita et al. [151] and successfully applied to various semiconducting polymers [152, 153] as well as SWCNT networks [63]. This method is termed ion‐exchange doping and relies on the removal of the reactive counterions from the doped film driven by a large excess of electrolyte ions in the doping solution. The concept is shown in Figure 3c for p‐doping of an SWCNT film with AuCl3 as a strong but also very reactive oxidant in an acetonitrile solution with a large excess of [BMP][TFSI] (1‐butyl‐1‐methylpyrrolidinium bis(trifluoro‐methylsulfonyl)imide) as the electrolyte. After rinsing the sample, the nanotubes are p‐doped to a degree that depends mostly on the AuCl3 concentration (as determined by absorption bleaching and conductivity measurements), but there are no traces of gold left, and the TFSI anions compensate the positive charges of the nanotubes. Ion‐exchange doping can improve the stability of chemically doped semiconductors, as was recently shown for n‐doped carbon nanotubes where the counter cation from doping with DMC was replaced by bicyclic guanidinium cations [154]. It also enables testing the impact of different counterion sizes on the localization of charge carriers and hence on charge transport [63, 155].

The concept of ion‐exchange doping can be further combined with proton‐coupled electron transfer (PCET) for oxidants whose redox potential depends on the presence and concentration of protons (H+), i.e., on the pH of the doping solution [156]. A prominent example is benzoquinone, which can accept two electrons from a donating semiconductor (e.g., a semiconducting polymer or SWCNT) and two protons (H+) from a buffered aqueous solution to form a neutral hydroquinone. To stabilize the p‐doped semiconductor, large and redox‐inactive counterions are introduced such as TFSI [157]. The degree of doping is directly related to the redox potential of benzoquinone, which is determined by the pH of the buffered solution. Although nanotubes can already be p‐doped by strong acids (see above), the combination of benzoquinone as a mild oxidant, excess electrolyte (e.g., lithium bis(trifluoromethyl‐sulfonyl)imide, LiTFSI) and pH‐buffered aqueous solutions (pH 7 to 1) enables very controlled, reversible and long‐term stable p‐doping of SWCNT networks [158]. The precise tunability of the doping levels by adjusting the pH of the solution, the use of water instead of organic solvents and the excellent stability of the doped films due to a lack of reactive counterions make this an interesting and versatile doping method. However, really high doping levels and high electrical conductivities – as required for thermoelectrics – are only achievable for large diameter nanotubes under these conditions.

Electrochemical and Electrostatic Doping

While the different methods for chemical doping are highly effective and necessary to obtain SWCNT films with a fixed doping level (e.g., for thermoelectrics), it is often difficult to tune the carrier concentration precisely or even change it continuously and reversibly for one sample. Alternatively, the doping levels of thin‐film semiconductors can be adjusted by electrochemical or electrostatic doping. Here, the charge carriers are injected from an electrode (working electrode or source electrode) in response to a potential that is applied to a counter or gate electrode across an electrolyte or an insulating dielectric, respectively. Sweeping this potential (i.e., voltage) moves the Fermi level and hence adjusts the number of free holes or electrons in a semiconducting material (here SWCNTs) in a controlled manner, which enables detailed studies of the impact of carriers (holes or electrons) on its electrical and optical properties.

Electrochemical doping of SWCNTs or SWCNT networks essentially relies on their partial oxidation or reduction. [13, 159] It can be performed in a typical electrochemical setup with the nanotubes deposited directly on or just in lateral contact with a working electrode that is immersed in an electrolyte together with a large counter electrode (often platinum) and a reference electrode (e.g., Ag/AgCl) with a fixed and known potential. The electrolyte can be an aqueous or organic (e.g., acetonitrile) salt solution or an ionic liquid; containing highly mobile anions and cations. The electrolyte should not react with the electrodes or the nanotubes within a certain electrochemical window. When a voltage is applied between the working electrode and the counter electrode, the mobile ions will move toward the respective electrodes to form electric double layers or even penetrate a porous semiconductor. Depending on the applied voltage, holes or electrons are simultaneously injected from the working electrode into the nanotube film to compensate the charges of the ions. The SWCNTs are partially and ideally reversibly oxidized or reduced as their corresponding redox potential (a function of their diameter, see Figure 1c) [13, 160] is exceeded. Note that while the bias is applied between the working electrode and the counter electrode, the actually acting potential should be measured between the working electrode and the reference electrode (three‐electrode setup). This setup avoids systematic errors of the measured potentials due to current flow and the related voltage drop.

Such electrochemical cells have been used often to study SWCNTs as they enable very precise and reversible tuning of carrier concentrations [13, 159, 160, 161, 162, 163]. Increasing the carrier density also increases the conductivity of a semiconducting nanotube network, and thus the concept of electrochemical transistors (ECTs) or electrolyte‐gated transistors (EGTs) was introduced initially for fundamental studies [164, 165, 166] but later also applied in devices, e.g., as a sensing platform [167, 168, 169, 170]. Here, the nanotube network (or single nanotube) acts as the channel between a source and drain electrode (with a certain channel length L, i.e., the distance between the electrodes, and a channel width W, i.e., the lateral length of the electrodes). The whole structure is covered with an electrolyte, which can be liquid (salt solution or ionic liquid) or a solid electrolyte, which still enables ion transport (e.g., an iongel—a mix of an ionic liquid and a polymer matrix) [171]. The channel together with the electrodes can be viewed as the working electrode in an electrochemical cell, but now it is possible to apply a small bias (V DS) between the source and the drain electrode and measure the current flow (drain current, I D), which is proportional to the induced carrier density and the carrier mobility. The carrier density is again modulated by the potential applied to a counter electrode, which is now the gate electrode (V G – gate voltage). In an ECT, this gate electrode does not have to be on top of the channel as in a conventional field‐effect transistor (see below) but can be on the side as shown in the schematic in Figure 4a. A reference electrode (usually a AgCl‐coated silver wire) can be used in addition to the gate electrode or even replace the gate electrode entirely. Importantly, the movement of ions takes some time (depending on the ionic mobility in the electrolyte and the electric field), especially inside a polymer/iongel or for a laterally removed side‐gate. Thus, it is important to make sure that the ECT is fully equilibrated before starting any measurements [172].

ECTs are a very convenient platform to investigate both charge transport and doping‐dependent optical properties of SWCNT networks, especially since both holes and electrons can be accumulated by simply changing the sign of the applied bias (see Figure 4b). However, note that the sign convention for electrochemistry is opposite to that for electronic circuits. Positive bias in an electrochemical cell indicates hole accumulation in a nanotube film on the working electrode (anodic charging), while a positive gate voltage in an electrochemical transistor leads to electron accumulation in the channel.

The limiting parameters for the maximum charge carrier concentration by electrochemical doping are the electrochemical window of the electrolyte and the redox potentials of the employed electrodes [173]. For example, the electrochemical window of aqueous electrolytes is very limited due to the electrolysis of water and reduction of dissolved oxygen at potentials above ±0.8 V (depending on electrode material) [173]. Electrolytes based on organic solvents or ionic liquids usually have much wider electrochemical windows (e.g., −2.2 to 2.5 V for [EMIM][TFSI]) [174, 175]. Decreasing the temperature, further slows down undesired Faradaic reactions and thus extends the voltage range, but also reduces the mobility of ions. Notably, using LiClO4 in polyethylene glycol as the electrolyte in combination with a fast cool‐down allowed for the application of up to 15 V to a graphene device and electron accumulation of up to 4·1014 cm−2 [176]. Cooling down an electrolyte‐gated transistor while a constant gate voltage is applied fixes the ions in place and thus also the carrier distribution, which can enable a bias‐independent p‐n junction in ambipolar devices [177, 178] or a further increase of the carrier density by a second electrostatic gate [179].

For networks of carbon nanotubes such high carrier concentrations lead to complete filling of the first subband (van Hove singularity) and partial filling of the second subband as demonstrated by Shimotani et al. [163]. This is also shown in Figure 4b for two iongel‐gated transistors with either small‐diameter (6,5) SWCNTs (left) or a mix of large‐diameter nanotubes (right). In both cases, the drain current increases for positive (electron accumulation) and negative (hole accumulation) gate voltages when the source‐drain voltage is constant and small (linear regime). The gap between the onset voltages for charge transport clearly scales with the bandgap of the nanotubes. At very high positive or negative gate voltages, the drain current does not increase linearly anymore and even appears to saturate. This change is best visualized by the first derivative of the transfer characteristics, which is the transconductance (g m = dI D/dV G).

The transconductance is indeed the most important parameter of an ECT as it quantifies the amplification factor (e.g., as a transducer for a sensor). It is determined by the width, length, and thickness of the channel as well as the carrier mobility and volumetric capacitance of the channel. Drift and diffusion of ions must be considered to model the current–voltage characteristics of ECTs [181, 182]. For electrochemical transistors (including organic ECTs) the transconductance first increases and then decreases with V G, thus always showing a distinct peak (in contrast to conventional field‐effect transistors). This peak is very pronounced for SWCNT‐ECTs (see Figure 4b) as it also includes the drop in capacitance and carrier mobility of the nanotubes when their first subband is filled. For large‐diameter nanotubes with smaller bandgaps, even a second transconductance peak can be observed representing the second subband (see DOS of (13,5) SWCNTs in Figure 1b for comparison). In spectro‐electrochemical experiments with ECTs, the channel conductivity and transconductance peaks can be correlated directly with the bleaching of the main SWCNT absorption peaks (see Section 3.1) [163].

Field‐effect transistors (FETs, see Figure 4c) with random networks or aligned arrays of semiconducting SWCNTs are usually fabricated to create very fast and/or conformal electronic circuits, thus utilizing their high charge carrier mobilities, their mechanical flexibility, and robustness even against ionizing radiation [183, 184, 185, 186]. Here, the semiconducting channel is separated from the gate electrode by a thin dielectric layer, e.g., an insulating oxide (e.g., SiO2, HfO2), a polymer (e.g., PMMA), or a bilayer of both (e.g., PMMA/ HfO2) [187, 188, 189]. The charge carriers are injected from the source electrode in response to the gate voltage. Applying a negative voltage to the gate leads to the accumulation of holes at the interface between the semiconductor and the dielectric to compensate the electric field (as in a MIS capacitor). A positive gate voltage results in electron accumulation. The number of induced carriers depends directly on the applied voltage and the areal capacitance of the dielectric layer, which is proportional to its dielectric constant (e.g., 2.6 for PMMA and 15–20 for HfO2 depending on deposition method) [189] and inversely proportional to its thickness. The increased charge carrier density enables current flow through the channel, i.e., the transistor changes from the off‐state to the on‐state. The carrier density in FETs can be tuned continuously and precisely by the gate voltage. However, it is important to note that not all carriers are mobile as they can be trapped in deep or shallow trap states within the semiconductor or at the interface with the dielectric. The tunability of the carrier density and thus channel conductance (or drain current) in FETs over many orders of magnitude by the applied gate voltage is the basis for many electronic circuits [185, 190]. It also enables detailed analysis of charge transport processes [79] and the impact of charge carriers on the optical properties of the semiconductor, here SWCNTs [191].

Typical transfer characteristics of FETs based on random networks of polymer‐sorted (6,5) SWCNTs and large‐diameter SWCNTs are shown in Figure 4d [180]. Here a thin hybrid dielectric of PMMA/HfOx and a top gate electrode were employed, which enabled maximum gate voltages of ±10 V and an effective areal capacitance of ∼100 nF/cm2. Similar to ECTs, the gap between the onset voltages for hole and electron transport depends strongly on the bandgap of the nanotubes. Although the hole and electron mobilities of carbon nanotubes should be roughly the same, a lower mobility and more positive onset voltages for electron transport are usually observed due to ubiquitous electron trap states [192].

Importantly, the 1D nature of SWCNTs and the DOS with sharp van Hove singularities result in a quantum capacitance (CQ = 4.0 · 10−10 Fm−1 for a single nanotube [164, 193]) that is small enough to limit the overall capacitance of the device even for nanotube networks. Due to the electrostatic coupling between 1D SWCNTs and a planar gate electrode, a low network density in combination with a thin dielectric layer has a large impact on the actual device capacitance and must be taken into account [194]. Hence, the channel capacitance should be measured directly on the FET to be able to calculate carrier mobilities correctly [65, 195].

Due to intrinsic limits to the breakdown voltages of common dielectric materials, the maximum carrier densities in FETs are typically in the range of 1012–1013 cm− 2 and thus significantly lower than for electrochemical doping (up to 1014 cm−2, see above) [196, 197] This is also reflected in the gate voltage‐dependent transconductances of the FETs presented in Figure 4d. For a constant carrier mobility and fixed capacitance, we would expect a constant plateau of g m for a conventional FET, however for large diameter SWCNT we find broad transconductance peaks associated with filling the first subband [198]. For the larger bandgap (6,5) SWCNTs these peaks are often not yet reached within the applicable gate voltage range, while they are fairly clear for large‐diameter (small bandgap) nanotubes [79, 199]. Due to electrostatic screening, only the first couple of nanometers next to the dielectric interface are actually charged in FETs even with thick SWCNT films (>10 nm) compared to the entire film in an ECT. Hence, changes in optical features, especially in absorption, are more difficult to detect. Likewise, variations in photoluminescence or Raman shifts may represent a superposition of doped and undoped layers of a nanotube network in an FET.

Spectroscopic Signatures of Doped SWCNTs

The accumulation of positive (p‐doping) or negative (n‐doping) charge carriers has a direct impact on the spectroscopic properties of semiconducting SWCNTs: from changes in absorption, fluorescence quenching, new charge‐induced spectral features to characteristic shifts of vibrational modes in their Raman spectra. These characteristic optical signatures can be applied, for example, in electrochromic devices (see section 4), but more importantly, they can be used to quantify the overall or image the local charge carrier density in a film or network of nanotubes. Thus, they can provide in situ information about doping efficiency and carrier distribution in different SWCNT samples and devices. In the following sections, we will discuss these spectroscopic signatures of nanotube doping but will focus solely on semiconducting SWCNTs.

Charge‐Induced Bleaching and Absorption

The effect of charge carriers on the absorption spectra of dense networks of SWCNTs became instantly apparent in early doping experiments, long before nanotubes could be separated into semiconducting and metallic and even before the excitonic nature of the excited state was confirmed and photoluminescence was observed. The earliest measurements were performed on mixed networks of nanotubes with relatively large diameters and hence the recorded absorption peaks were broad with a significant scattering and plasmon background. Nevertheless, a clear bleaching of absorption with chemical doping for both metallic and semiconducting nanotubes was found, which was rationalized with the depletion of the valence band (p‐doping) or filling of the conduction band (n‐doping) [200, 201]. Subsequent studies using electrochemical doping confirmed these observations [86, 161, 202] and led to the first electrochromic cells based on SWCNTs (see Section 4.1). Around the same time, free‐carrier absorption in the mid‐ to far‐infrared spectral range was observed for higher doping levels [203].

Progress in the separation of metallic and semiconducting SWCNTs and especially the selection of monochiral nanotubes allowed for a much more detailed investigation of doping‐induced bleaching and also helped to reveal charge‐induced absorption features. Shimotani et al. directly correlated the bleaching of the E11 and E22 transitions of purified semiconducting SWCNT films with the filling/emptying of their subbands and electrical conductivity for electrochemical doping [163]. Moreover, for semiconducting nanotubes with small diameters (chemically doped in dispersion), a narrow and redshifted charge‐induced absorption feature was observed and associated with the generation of charged excitons, i.e., trions [106]. A more detailed analysis of the absorption spectra of nearly monochiral (7,5) and later (6,5) nanotubes further revealed significant shifts and broadening of the E11 transition with increasing carrier density [87, 204].

Figure 5a shows absorption spectra of thin films of polymer‐sorted (6,5) SWCNTs that were chemically p‐doped with different concentrations of AuCl3 in acetonitrile. Bleaching of the E11 (at 993 nm) and E22 (at 578 nm) transitions is evident with the E11 absorption peak shifting to higher energies and broadening before almost completely disappearing at very high doping levels [63]. At this point, the E22 absorption is still visible, although at about half its initial value and slightly redshifted. At even higher doping levels than shown in Figure 5a, the E22 absorption peak appears to split into two features with one shifting to higher energies and one increasing in oscillator strength at lower energies [205]. The latter might be associated with increasing intersubband plasmon excitations (see below), however, this has not been confirmed yet. Note, that the E33 absorption (at ∼350 nm, not shown) is also bleached and redshifts slightly at very high doping levels [206].

While the main (6,5) SWCNT transitions are bleached, a new absorption peak appears at 1155 nm in Figure 5a (i.e., at lower energies than E11, ΔE = 175 meV). It initially increases in oscillator strength and blueshifts with doping before it also vanishes at higher doping concentrations. This peak is usually attributed to a trion (charged exciton) absorption and often labeled as T+ or T− (also X+, X−), depending on the polarity of doping [87]. However, transient absorption measurements indicate that this charge‐induced absorption feature could also be a polaron absorption, similar to those observed in doped semiconducting polymers, and not a direct transition from the ground state into a trion state (see section 3.2.) [207, 208, 209, 210]. Nevertheless, the energy offset (ΔE) between this charge‐induced absorption peak and the main E11 absorption decreases with increasing SWCNT diameter in the same way as expected for trions [106, 211]. Furthermore, the decrease and eventual disappearance of the trion/polaron absorption at higher doping levels (unlike polarons in polymers) is not yet fully understood.

These general changes of the absorption features of SWCNTs are found for all types of p‐ and n‐doping (chemical, electrochemical, electrostatic) of nanotubes in dispersion and in thin films [78, 110, 118, 211]. For example, Figure 5b shows the differential change in transmittance (ΔT/T) of a random network of (6,5) nanotubes in a field‐effect transistor where the applied gate voltage and thus carrier density is modulated around an offset gate bias VOS (charge modulation spectra, CMS) [212]. The bleach (red), blueshift, and broadening of the E11 absorption, the appearance of a charge‐induced absorption peak (trion or polaron, blue), and its disappearance at higher carrier densities are all present. As ambipolar FETs with SWCNT networks enable both electron and hole accumulation in the same device, it is possible to compare the corresponding CMS directly. While the shift of E11 is the same for p‐ and n‐doping, there is a small (∼5 meV) energy difference between the charge‐induced absorption peaks, which was also observed for electrochemical doping of (6,5) SWCNTs [211].

The blueshift and asymmetric broadening of the E11 transition with increasing carrier density (by up to 80 meV for (6,5) SWCNTs) was explained by Eckstein et al. as the result of inhomogeneous doping and the segmentation of nanotubes by localized charge carriers that form “charge puddles” due to their Coulomb interaction with counterions [87]. This segmentation of the nanotubes into intrinsic undoped segments and randomly distributed charge puddles leads to axial confinement of excitons between neighboring sites of localized charges, which causes an increase of the transition energy. It should be noted that the blueshift of E11 with carrier density can also be observed for suspended SWCNTs that are gated through free space [215]. Possibly, localization of charges can occur by reorientation of surrounding dipoles (e.g., adsorbed water molecules in the case of free‐standing nanotubes) instead of Coulomb interactions with counterions. An alternative explanation for the blueshift of E11 would be bandgap renormalization and doping‐induced changes of the exciton binding energy when assuming homogeneous doping [215, 216, 217].

Under the assumption of homogeneous carrier distribution, hole or electron doping reduces the number of available k‐states (i.e., phase‐space filling) and thus oscillator strength depending on the effective carrier mass and exciton size [112, 213]. Larger excitons are more affected by doping than smaller excitons. The confinement model, on the other hand, assumes that excess carriers are inhomogeneously distributed. The charge carriers are localized in small sections of the nanotubes (charge puddles, see above) [87] and the absorption bleach is simply attributed to shorter segments of intrinsic (undoped) nanotube. In this case, the bleach of the exciton band should be mostly independent of exciton size. Both assumptions lead to a reduction of exciton oscillator strength, i.e., E11 absorption.

Eckstein et al. derived a direct correlation between the experimentally obtained E11 absorption bleaching of (6,5) nanotubes, the E11 blueshift and the carrier density (in charges per nm SWCNT length, see Figure 5c) based on carrier concentrations that were obtained using either the homogeneous band filling model (blue squares, electrochemical doping) or the inhomogeneous confinement model (open squares, chemical doping) [213]. Although there is a fairly large margin of uncertainty, the data show very similar exciton bleaching depending on the calculated carrier density (between 0.005 and 0.5 nm−1), which was attributed to the fact that in both models electronic states at the edge of the valence or conduction band were removed/blocked and thus were not available anymore for the formation of excitons.

The plot in Figure 5c is extremely helpful for rough carrier density estimations in doping experiments. A similar correlation of E11 bleaching of large‐diameter plasma‐torch nanotubes in dispersion with increasing carrier density (based on phase‐space filling) was independently corroborated by 19F‐NMR measurements and the quantification of DDB‐F72 clusters as the dopant (see Figure 5d,e) [112]. Note that for equal doping levels (e.g., for 0.1 nm−1) the E11 bleaching of large‐diameter nanotubes is much weaker than for small‐diameter (6,5) SWCNTs. Unfortunately, while the correlation of the E11 bleaching with carrier density is very useful for studies where absorption spectra can be recorded and compared to an initially undoped state, it does not provide an independent metric for the doping level of unknown SWCNT samples, e.g., as a measure for unintentional doping by oxygen in air.

Aside from absorption features in the visible and near‐infrared, doped SWCNTs also show broad absorption bands in the mid‐infrared region (500–5000 cm−1) that are commonly attributed to axial plasmon resonances of mobile charge carriers, which depend on the length of the nanotubes [218, 219] but also on the carrier density [220]. The broad free carrier absorption (Drude and intraband plasmon responses) in highly doped SWCNTs becomes even stronger in the far‐infrared (i.e., in the THz range, 10–300 cm−1), which enables an estimation of the local conductivity and carrier mobility [221, 222, 223] as well as their application as effective and tunable THz absorbers and detectors [224, 225, 226, 227].

Finally, another distinct charge‐induced absorption peak was discovered for aligned films of SWCNTs using perpendicularly polarized near‐infrared light. These intersubband plasmon (ISBP) excitations occur within the conduction or valence band of strongly doped SWCNTs (usually through electrochemical gating) when the interband transitions (E11, E22, with polarization along the axis) are almost completely suppressed [206, 228, 229]. ISBPs can be described as collective intersubband excitations of charge carriers in doped SWNTs (metallic or semiconducting) [230]. For semiconducting nanotubes, they appear between the E11 and E22 transition energies (see Figure 5f). With decreasing diameter, they blueshift and become weaker to the point of being nearly unobservable [214].

Overall, the absorption spectra of semiconducting nanotubes show significant and characteristic changes over a very broad wavelength range from the far‐ and near‐infrared to the visible and even ultraviolet. While small differences exist, these general carrier density‐dependent features are essentially the same for all types of doping and can be used as direct and indirect indicators for the charge carrier density in SWCNT films as well as for practical applications (e.g., tunable electrochromic filters, see Section 4.1).

Fluorescence Quenching and Trion Emission

Similar to other bulk, organic, or nanoscale semiconductors, the fluorescence of semiconducting SWCNTs is severely quenched by the presence of excess charge carriers. This phenomenon was observed very early on for various samples of carbon nanotubes and under different measurement conditions [105, 122, 231, 232, 233, 234]. While reduced absorption at the excitation wavelength due to bleaching (see above) plays a role for the detected emission intensity at constant illumination, the main reduction of PL is caused by non‐radiative exciton decay. The quenching of excitons by free carriers is usually (especially in solid‐state devices and when multiple excitons are present) assigned to Auger recombination, i.e., the transfer of the exciton's energy and momentum to a free charge carrier [232, 235, 236, 237]. The possibility of phonon‐assisted indirect exciton ionization (i.e., exciton decay via phonon emission and creation of an intraband electron–hole pair for energy and momentum conservation) [238] was also proposed for nanotubes and could account for the temperature‐dependent quenching dynamics in F4TCNQ‐doped nanotube dispersions [233]. However, it was estimated to only become relevant at relatively high dopant densities [104].

In both cases, the 1D confinement of excitons in nanotubes increases the interaction probability of charges and excitons significantly (similar to other low‐dimensional semiconductors such as quantum dots [239, 240] or monolayered transition metal dichalcogenides [241, 242, 243]). Consequently, the exciton population is quickly depleted and PL is quenched strongly. Significant PL quenching in SWCNTs is already observable at very low carrier densities and long before bleaching of the E11 transition becomes apparent as shown by Eckstein et al. [205]. For chemically doped SWCNTs, nonradiative decay at localized charge puddles caused by interaction with adsorbed counterions was suggested to explain the observed exciton dynamics [87]. Single‐nanotube PL imaging demonstrated that even very localized solitary charges can lead to quenching of emission for tens to hundreds of nanometers along the nanotube due to the fast exciton diffusion [104, 244].

Overall, the PL efficiency of SWCNTs is so sensitive to charge carriers that even very small concentrations (one hole or electron per 300–500 nm of nanotube) can be easily detected through fluorescence quenching. Furthermore, a blueshift of the E11 emission is detectable, although it is smaller compared to that of the E11 absorption. For chemically doped SWCNTs, this difference might be explained by the notion that the detected emission mainly originates from excitons that are less confined by the charge puddles created by the dopants [205]. Also, the PL signal of doped SWCNTs is usually completely quenched before the E11 blueshift exceeds a few nm [211, 215].

Charge‐induced quenching severely limits the applicability of SWCNTs as near‐infrared emitters, especially in electroluminescent devices (light‐emitting diodes or transistors) [245, 246, 247, 248], where high carrier densities are difficult to avoid (see section 4.2.). Even in photovoltaic cells based on SWCNTs as the absorbing and donor layer, quenching of excitons by excess charges may take place before charge separation can occur, which reduces the achievable external quantum efficiency [114, 249, 250]. Furthermore, the ubiquitous unintentional p‐doping of nanotubes, e.g., by oxygen in aqueous environments, limits the brightness of SWCNTs as near‐infrared emitters for bio‐imaging and bio‐sensing [251]. Mild reducing agents such as dithiothreitol or β‐mercaptoethanol have been shown to brighten SWCNTs under these conditions (i.e., reducing the p‐doping‐induced quenching) [251, 252].

Like other low‐dimensional semiconductors [253, 254], doped semiconducting SWCNTs show significant trion emission as a narrow and redshifted peak in the PL spectrum. In contrast to the charge‐induced absorption at similar wavelengths, it is widely accepted that this transition directly originates from quasiparticles consisting of a bound electron–hole pair with an additional hole (positive trion, T+ or X+) or electron (negative trion, T− or X−). Trion emission was first observed by Matsunga et al. for polymer‐sorted CoMoCat nanotubes in toluene that were p‐doped by the addition of F4TCNQ (see Figure 6a) [106]. This seminal study was followed‐up by the demonstration of both positive and negative trions for electrochemically doped SWCNTs by Park et al. [255]. They could show that the binding energies for negative and positive trions were almost equal. They also confirmed and adjusted the parameters for the previously found empirical equation for the dependence of the energy gap between exciton and trion emission (ΔE E‐T) on nanotube diameter (d) as ΔE E‐T = 49/d + 65/d 2, which combines the diameter‐dependence of the trion binding energy and singlet‐triplet exciton splitting, respectively. This relation is also consistent with theoretical calculations for trions in SWCNTs [256, 257] and has been corroborated by several experimental studies on doped SWCNT thin‐films [211] as well as suspended single nanotubes [215].

Figure 6b shows a schematic energy diagram for excitons and negative trions, indicating the lower energy of the bound trion state compared to the exciton and a free electron. This lower energy is reflected in the different exciton and trion emission wavelengths. While ΔE E‐T is very large for small‐diameter (6,5) nanotubes (approx. 175–180 meV) it is reduced to around 100 meV for large‐diameter nanotubes (1.2–1.3 nm). Due to this relatively large energy difference, nanotube trions are fairly easy to distinguish from excitons even for nanotube networks at room temperature. In contrast to that, trions in 2D semiconductors (e.g., monolayered MoS2) exhibit only small energy offsets (about 20 meV) to their excitons, which necessitates measurements at cryogenic temperatures to clearly resolve the trion peak [254]. Nonetheless, monochiral SWCNT samples are usually preferred for the reliable observation of trion emission to avoid the superposition of quenching the PL of SWCNTs with larger diameters and trion emission from less‐doped smaller‐diameter nanotubes [258].

Figure 6c shows PL spectra of polymer‐sorted (6,5) SWCNTs with well‐resolved exciton and trion emission for different concentrations of AuCl3 as the p‐dopant [205]. The trion peak width is very similar to that of the exciton and also shows a blueshift of a few nm with increasing carrier density [211, 259]. As shown further in Figure 6d, the increase of trion emission (blue, I/I max) is directly correlated with the quenching of the E11 emission (red, I/I 0). The trion peak starts to dominate the PL spectrum at higher doping levels when the E11 emission is nearly completely quenched. However, trion emission is always very weak and never reaches more than a fraction of the intensity of the original exciton emission. Hence, corresponding spectra are usually normalized to the E11 emission to highlight the trion peak. At even higher doping levels, the trion emission also disappears completely. Notably, the decrease of trion emission starts at lower carrier densities than the decrease of the trion (polaron) absorption (shown as normalized oscillator strength f/f max in Figure 6d) [205].

The decay dynamics of excitons and trions in doped SWCNTs were investigated by several groups using pump‐probe transient absorption spectroscopy and different methods of time‐resolved PL spectroscopy [205, 209, 233, 260, 261, 262] Overall, the PL lifetime of excitons in doped SWCNTs is severely shorted (to less than 1 ps) by their interaction with free charge carriers or charge puddles due to either Auger quenching, phonon‐assisted indirect exciton ionization or the formation of trions [233, 261, 263]. The formation of trions from a possibly dark exciton and a hole (or electron) is very fast, as is their subsequent decay (< 2 ps) [260]. The trion decay is probably governed by both trion‐hole Auger recombination and an internal Auger recombination process that dominate over the radiative decay and result in very low trion emission efficiencies [261]. Indeed, the very short PL lifetime of trions can be used to distinguish their emission from that of luminescent quantum defects (sp3‐defects, oxygen‐defects), which occurs at very similar wavelengths but whose PL lifetimes are in the range of 100 – 300 ps [264, 265, 266].

Despite these kinetic studies (usually in dispersion), it is not completely clear, which sample parameters (beyond doping concentration) influence the observable intensity of trion emission and its ratio to the corresponding exciton emission and how. A recent PL study on ambipolar FETs with (6,5) SWCNT networks and different gate dielectrics (see PL spectra at different gate voltages in Figure 6e) suggests that the quenching of excitons and the formation/radiative decay of positive and negative trions depends on the type of charge traps in the surrounding gate dielectrics [259]. For example, electron traps in fluorinated polymers (e.g., AF2400) lead to lower trion‐to‐exciton ratios for electron doping in comparison to hole doping. Likewise, hole traps in metal oxides (e.g., HfOx) result in lower trion‐to‐exciton ratios for hole doping vs. electron doping. The localization of charge carriers, which should lead to the formation of charge puddles with trion emission, and their separation from neutral nanotube segments with exciton emission may counteract the interaction (quenching and trion formation) of fully mobile charge carriers with highly diffusive excitons. Notably, the dielectric constant of the surrounding medium appears to have no significant impact on trion‐to‐exciton emission in these devices.

The presence of trions in the emission spectra of electroluminescent devices such as light‐emitting transistors [178, 211, 267], organic light‐emitting diodes with SWCNTs [246], or single SWCNT devices [268, 269] is a clear sign of excess carriers (holes or electrons). Consequently, they also indicate undesired exciton quenching, which results in lower external quantum efficiencies (see Section 4.2). As mentioned above, noticeable quenching of nanotube PL by charge carriers occurs at much lower doping concentrations than bleaching of the main absorption peaks [270]. Thus, it should be avoided as much as possible for applications that require high emission efficiencies. On the other hand, PL quenching can also be utilized as a highly sensitive optical probe for doping in SWCNT films and devices (see Section 4.3).

Doping‐Dependent Raman Spectra

Resonant Raman spectroscopy is a highly versatile tool for characterizing and investigating a variety of carbon‐based materials, including graphene, carbon nanotubes, and carbon fibers [271]. It can provide information about strain, defects, interactions with the environment, and doping with high spatial resolution (by confocal Raman microscopy) and without interfering with or damaging the sample or device [21, 22, 272]. The sensitive and complex dependence of Raman spectra on the doping state of SWCNTs became apparent almost immediately with the first in‐depth resonant Raman spectroscopy studies on bundled and single SWCNTs [161, 273]. The most important spectral trends for metallic and semiconducting nanotubes were observed using electrochemical and in some cases electrostatic doping, although mostly for large‐diameter nanotubes and initially without precise assignment of the nanotube (n,m) species [274, 275, 276, 277, 278, 279, 280]. With the progress of SWCNT sorting techniques, the dependence of charge‐induced Raman mode shifts on nanotube diameter (especially for small‐diameter nanotubes) were also established [26].

On a fundamental level, Raman spectroscopy probes the electron‐phonon coupling in doped SWCNTs and the resulting renormalization of phonon energy depending on charge carrier density. While there are many similarities between the Raman responses of doped graphene [29, 281], metallic [282, 283, 284], and semiconducting nanotubes, there are also significant differences. For example, Kohn anomalies do not exist in semiconducting nanotubes, while they are strongly enhanced in metallic nanotubes [285, 286]. Here, we will focus exclusively on semiconducting SWCNTs. Most of the systematic Raman studies have been performed with electrochemically doped nanotubes in different experimental setups; only a few used electrostatic or chemical doping. Overall, the following changes of the main features in resonance Raman spectra of semiconducting nanotubes have been observed with increasing hole or electron concentration:

(I) Decrease of intensity of all Raman modes. For all types of nanotubes and for all of the common SWCNT Raman modes a significant decrease in intensity is observed with increasing hole or electron doping [26]. This effect is mainly attributed to the absorption bleaching of doped SWCNTs (see Section 3.1). The reduced oscillator strength of the main optical transitions directly results in less efficient resonant excitation [232], which is typically required for recording Raman spectra of carbon nanotubes. This Raman intensity drop can pose a challenge to acquiring meaningful spectra at very high doping levels, as some of the weaker Raman peaks may not be detectable anymore. The intensity loss is shown in Figure 7a for the RBMs of different resonantly excited small‐diameter nanotubes [287]. For RBMs, this decrease in intensity is the only significant impact of doping as their spectral positions and linewidths remain essentially unchanged [287, 288, 289]. Note that for samples of mixed nanotubes with a range of diameters and bandgaps, the intensity ratios of the corresponding RBM peaks to each other can vary differently with doping, depending on whether the laser excitation wavelength is resonant with the larger or smaller diameter nanotubes [158].

(II) Shift and broadening of the G+ mode (approx. 1589 cm−1). Different from metallic nanotubes [284], the shift of the G+ mode with doping is rather complex for semiconducting SWCNTs. The magnitude and even direction of the peak shift depend significantly on the nanotube diameter as was shown for a range of small‐ to large‐diameter SWCNTs by Grimm et al. (see Figure 7b,c) [26]. For SWCNTs with very small diameters (<1 nm), the G+ mode shift for electron doping is small and negative (redshift), while for large diameter nanotubes it is larger and positive (blueshift). For hole doping, a change from redshift to blueshift can be observed with increasing hole density and nanotube diameter. For all types and levels of doping, a slight broadening of the G+ peak occurs, which is stronger for larger diameter nanotubes. Interestingly, almost no shifts are found for the G− mode of small‐diameter nanotubes compared to noticeable shifts for large‐diameter nanotubes (see Figure 7d). Das et al. proposed a combination of the renormalization of the phonon energy based on nonadiabatic (dynamic) effects and lattice relaxation (static) effects (i.e., doping‐induced changes in the equilibrium lattice parameters) to model the experimental trends (solid lines in Figure 7d). However, a full theoretical description that also takes into account small‐diameter (<1 nm) nanotubes with strong curvature effects and the corresponding diameter‐dependence is still lacking.

(III) Blueshift of the D‐mode (defect‐activated mode, approx. 1320–1350 cm−1). While the D‐mode (more precisely the D/G+ mode ratio) mainly reflects the defect density in the sp2‐carbon lattice, it also changes in intensity and position depending on doping. A decrease in intensity (as all other Raman modes), a substantial blueshift (by 5–20 cm−1) and broadening have been observed for small‐ as well as large‐diameter nanotubes (see Figure 7e) [26, 277, 278, 289, 290]. However, as the D‐mode also depends in position and intensity on the Raman laser wavelength [30] and ideally should be very weak for unfunctionalized pristine nanotubes, it is not commonly used to characterize the doping state of SWCNTs.

(IV) Blueshift and broadening of 2D (G′) mode (approx. 2600–2700 cm−1). The 2D band of semiconducting SWCNTs shows a very strong (up to 30 cm−1), continuous, and nearly equal blueshift for hole and electron doping (see Figure 7f,g) as well as some broadening (more pronounced for small‐diameter nanotubes) [26, 289]. These characteristic shifts of the 2D‐mode can be used to map the carrier density in thin films or networks of semiconducting nanotubes. This concept was successfully applied to investigate the in situ carrier density profiles along the channel of electrolyte‐gated unipolar and ambipolar transistors based on sorted (7,5) and (6,5) SWCNTs under different biasing conditions [289, 291]. In‐operando Raman microscopy even enabled imaging the recombination zone and its changing position with applied voltage in the channel of an ambipolar transistor with micrometer spatial resolution (see Section 4.3).

Some or all of the described doping‐induced Raman mode shifts, linewidth, and intensity changes have been observed for different samples of semiconducting nanotubes: single SWCNTs, mixed networks, randomly oriented or aligned nanotubes, with different degrees of bundling and with different dielectric surroundings. All of these experimental parameters also have an effect on Raman modes and their response to holes or electrons. Hence, their precise or quantitative dependence on carrier density may vary to some degree. So far, there is no unifying equation, for example, for the 2D‐mode shift at a specific doping level for semiconducting nanotubes depending on their diameter or laser excitation wavelength. At the moment, the observable Raman mode changes must be measured and correlated to hole or electron concentrations for the corresponding nanotube samples under the same experimental conditions. In all cases, an undoped sample is necessary as a reference point. Furthermore, it should be noted that significant mode shifts only occur at fairly high carrier concentrations (above 0.1 nm−1). These are easily reached with intentional chemical or electrochemical doping, however, the detection of very low carrier densities by Raman spectroscopy is usually not feasible.

Applications

As shown in the previous sections, several spectroscopic features (absorption, fluorescence, Raman modes) of semiconducting SWCNTs vary characteristically and quantitatively with increasing charge carrier densities. In addition to reflecting the interactions of holes and electrons with the nanotubes on a fundamental level, these correlations can be utilized directly, e.g., modulation of absorption, or indirectly, e.g., spatial mapping of doping levels, in a variety of SWCNT‐based optoelectronic devices. Tuning and quantifying the carrier concentration in intentionally (or unintentionally) doped films, networks or arrays of semiconducting SWCNTs play an important role in field‐effect transistors for complementary circuits [135], diodes [246, 292], transparent electrodes for flexible displays [3], sensors in bioelectronics [293], thermoelectric generators [2], and tunable near‐infrared optical filters [4]. Here, we will focus on a few selected applications that involve the spectral response of nanotubes to charge carriers for their functionality, i.e., electrochromic filters (Section 4.1) and electroluminescent devices (Section 4.2). We will also highlight two examples of optoelectronic devices, for which the spatial and temporal resolution of the carrier distribution through spectroscopic imaging has helped to understand their operating mechanism (Section 4.3).

Tunable Filters and Electrochromic Devices

The idea to use the tunable transmittance of carbon nanotube films (see Section 3.1) for electrochromic filters in the near‐infrared wavelength range was first implemented for unsorted SWCNT networks with an ionic liquid as the electrolyte [86]. Good modulation of absorption was achieved for 50–150 nm thick films created by filtration with the absorption peaks of the semiconducting nanotubes showing the most pronounced changes. However, the response times were still long (a few minutes). This device concept was later optimized by using sorted semiconducting or metallic nanotubes and improving the layout of the electrochromic cells (e.g. by counter electrode impedance matching, optimized network morphologies), which helped to increase the modulation depth (>90%) and reduced response times to a few ms [294, 295, 296, 297, 298]. Aside from the strong absorption of SWCNTs over a broad wavelength range in the near‐infrared and their resistance to photobleaching, the high conductivity of doped carbon nanotubes is a significant advantage compared to other electrochromic materials [4]. Large areas can be switched that are only in lateral contact with an electrode (e.g., transparent ITO or printed silver lines), thus enabling more versatile and flexible electrochromic cell geometries [299].

With the improved sorting methods for semiconducting and monochiral SWCNTs, it is even possible to create electrochromic filters not for a broad wavelength range but for a specific wavelength and with very high optical densities, i.e., a type of notch filter. Figure 8a shows the bias‐dependent absorption spectra of such an electrochromic notch filter (main absorption at 1000 nm) based on a thick (35 nm) aerosol‐jet printed film of polymer‐sorted (6,5) nanotubes (purple square in the photograph) and spray‐coated mixed large‐diameter nanotubes as the counter electrode (gray, horseshoe‐shaped area). An iongel based on an ionic liquid and a fluorinated polymer was used as a solid electrolyte. The depth of modulation at the E11 transition was large (>90%) with response times of a few seconds [78]. Thicker films (up to 360 nm) with absorbances of 4 to 5 remained fully bleachable and excellent cycling stability was demonstrated. Due to the lateral structure of this electrochromic cell and the thickness of the nanotube film, it was even possible to program different levels of transparency/absorption through voltage pulses of different length or frequency (see Figure 8b), which remained stable for hours under open circuit conditions.

Other nanotube‐based electrochromic devices have used the modulation of ISBP absorption in the infrared [214, 300] and the color changes of doped metallic SWCNTs in the visible range [301]. Charge‐tunable plasmon resonances [218] as well as doping‐induced strong plasmon‐exciton coupling and trion‐polaritons were also explored [302, 303]. Recently, tunable Fresnel zone plate (FZP) lenses based on patterned thin films of SWCNTs were demonstrated, which provided subwavelength lateral focusing at first‐ and second‐order focal positions and 72% modulation of the focus intensity [304]. Furthermore, the ability to tune the near‐ and mid‐infrared absorption and thus also the thermal emissivity of carbon nanotube films (usually unsorted) through electrochemical doping has been applied for thermal radiation shielding, thermal camouflage, and infrared displays [299, 305, 306]. Finally, the broadband absorption of doped nanotubes in the THz range depending on chemical and electrochemical doping in combination with their high charge carrier mobilities has been exploited for THz detectors and modulators [224, 225, 226], including the creation of nanotube‐based metamaterials for sensing of small molecules [307, 308].

Another interesting application of electrochemically doped SWCNT layers utilizes the variation of their nonlinear optical responses to control pulse generation in fiber lasers [309]. Electrolyte‐gated networks of mixed SWCNTs show saturable absorption in the near‐infrared, where the depth and dynamics of transient photobleaching can be tuned by the applied voltages (i.e., electrochemical doping). To exploit this effect, a dense SWCNTs film was transferred onto a side‐polished optical fiber, which provided interaction with the evanescent field of the fiber core and covered with an ionic liquid that was in contact with a counter electrode. As a result, the laser pulse generation of a polarization‐maintaining erbium‐doped fiber system could be switched between a femtosecond mode‐locking regime and microsecond Q‐switching by the applied gate voltages [309, 310].

Electroluminescent Devices

Nanotube‐based electroluminescent devices such as light‐emitting diodes or transistors rely on the electrical creation of excitons via hole–electron recombination or impact excitation. For these devices, the observation of trion emission (see Section 3.2) can indicate undesired excess charge carriers and possible charge‐induced emission quenching. Hence, analyzing electroluminescence (EL) spectra can provide useful insights into the balance of injected carriers and help to optimize operating conditions.

The earliest demonstration of near‐infrared EL from semiconducting SWCNTs was accomplished with a single carbon nanotube FET at IBM [247, 311]. This experiment introduced a new approach to generating a mobile pn‐junction in a semiconductor without chemical doping but purely by the applied gate and drain voltages, resulting in electron–hole recombination and light‐emission. For that, the semiconductor must be capable of conducting holes and electrons (i.e., being ambipolar) and injection of both should be possible without high barriers. Indeed, semiconducting SWCNTs are ideally suited for such ambipolar transistors due their nearly symmetric band structures with equal hole and electron mobilities as well as small bandgaps for efficient hole and electron injection. The concept of ambipolar light‐emitting transistors was later expanded to a large variety of luminescent materials such as semiconducting polymers [312, 313], organic single crystals [314, 315], colloidal quantum dots [316, 317], and monolayers of transition metal dichalcogenides [318, 319].

In an ambipolar transistor, holes are injected from one electrode (e.g., the source) and electrons are injected from the other electrode (drain, see schematic in Figure 8c) under the appropriate biasing conditions [320]. Depending on the applied gate and drain voltages, the oppositely charged accumulation layers meet somewhere along the channel where holes and electrons recombine to form excitons, which then relax either radiatively or non‐radiatively. This recombination and emission zone can be shifted through the entire channel by adjusting the biasing conditions. For light‐emitting transistors with long channel lengths, the moving position of the emission spot (for a single nanotube) [311, 321] or line (for a thin film) [211, 322] can be observed directly by near‐infrared imaging. For a simple calculation of the location of the emission zone and the carrier density profiles for a set of applied voltages, it is usually assumed that all holes and electrons recombine within the recombination zone and thus the carrier densities are set to zero at this point [323].

For a fixed set of voltages the emission zone will stay in one place and EL spectra can be collected, which should predominantly show excitonic emission similar to the PL spectrum of the nanotubes [258]. This is indeed the case. However, especially electrolyte‐gated transistors with small‐diameter semiconducting SWCNTs show EL spectra with strong and sometimes even dominant trion emission [178, 211], while the EL spectra of optimized ambipolar FETs usually exhibit very little trion emission [324, 325]. As discussed above, the observation of trion emission is a clear indicator for excess charge carriers in the recombination zone, which is in contrast to the assumption of complete electron–hole recombination that was made for calculating the emission zone position. The presence of charge carriers in the recombination zone and thus charge‐induced Auger quenching may also explain the very low (below 0.02%) EL efficiencies of SWCNT light‐emitting transistors compared to the PL efficiencies of similar thin films (∼0.1%) [322, 325].

For short‐channel (<1 µm) nanotube transistors, the position of light emission cannot be resolved, and the question of balanced charge injection and recombination becomes even more complex. Thus, EL spectra and the ratio of trion‐to‐exciton emission can provide helpful information. Figure 8d shows a short‐channel, back‐gated FET with a single (9,8) nanotube between the source and drain electrodes (channel length < 50 nm). When performing current biasing on this FET at 4.2 K, ambipolarity and gate‐voltage‐only‐controlled operation is enforced, which allow for stable EL to be observed [269]. The cryogenic temperatures ensure very narrow emission linewidths (3‐5 meV) and thus a clear distinction of all emission features. Excitonic emission at 1460 nm is evident in the recorded EL spectra (see Figure 8e) for a constant positive drain current and negative gate voltage (V G). However, for positive V G, a new peak emerges at ∼1585 nm, which was identified as trion emission. The energy difference to the exciton (70–90 meV) follows the scaling law for trion energies by Park et al. (see Section 3.2) [255]. Figure 8f visualizes the increasing trion emission with more positive drain currents, which indicates the presence of excess electrons and thus a lack of balanced charge injection. Short‐channel light‐emitting transistors based on (7,5) SWCNTs with luminescent defects under similar conditions showed even further redshifted trionic emission from charged defects [268, 326]. Since strong trion emission indicates the presence of excess carriers (either holes or electrons) in a device during operation, it is also a common feature in unipolar light‐emitting SWCNT FETs, where exciton generation is often assumed to occur via localized impact excitation at high drain bias [267, 327].

While ambipolar electrolyte‐gated or field‐effect transistors with SWCNTs induce mobile pn‐junctions for electron–hole recombination, it is also possible to create lateral [245, 328] or conventional vertically stacked light‐emitting diodes with semiconducting SWCNTs as the emitter. For example, organic multilayer light‐emitting diodes (OLEDs) were created with polymer‐sorted (6,5) SWCNTs embedded in the emission layer between an organic hole and electron transport layer to achieve near‐infrared OLEDs [246, 329]. Interestingly, these OLEDs predominantly showed trion emission from hot‐spots and very low external quantum efficiencies (< 0.014%), which again indicates an imbalance in the charge carrier distribution and highlights the need for further device optimization.

The common observation of trion emission in electroluminescent SWCNT devices in combination with their low emission efficiencies reveals the undesired presence of excess charge carriers, which leads to strong charge‐induced quenching as discussed in section 3.2. This limitation on the external quantum efficiency presents a serious obstacle for the real‐world application of nanotubes as electrically‐driven near‐infrared emitters. Unfortunately, not even the introduction of luminescent quantum defects to boost emission efficiency can solve this fundamental problem [325, 330].

Spatial Resolution of Carrier Densities in Devices

While a strong sensitivity of spectroscopic features to charge carriers might be an issue for the efficiency of SWCNT‐based electroluminescent devices, it can be employed successfully as a non‐invasive optical probe for the local state of doping. Combined with (confocal scanning) microscopy techniques, it enables imaging the uniformity or non‐uniformity of doping in thin films or the visualization of gradual variations of carrier density in SWCNT network devices such as unipolar or ambipolar transistors.

For example, confocal charge modulation microscopy (CMM, that is, spatially resolved differential changes of absorption with gate voltage modulation, see Figure 5b), which was previously applied to polymer thin‐film transistors [331], can be used to map the hole and electron distribution in top‐gate ambipolar FETs with dense networks of polymer‐sorted (6,5) or HiPco nanotubes [332]. A spatial resolution of about 0.8 µm was achieved and preferential charge percolation paths depending on nanotube species and gate voltage could be identified. However, CMM is a fairly complex and time‐consuming technique, which is not easy to implement. More accessible experimental methods are thus highly desirable.

An intriguing question for FETs based on carbon nanotube networks is the precise distribution of holes and electrons in the ambipolar regime, that is, when both types of carriers are injected from opposite electrodes, form accumulation layers and recombine somewhere within the channel. As described above for light‐emitting transistors (see Section 4.2), we should observe electroluminescence from a narrow recombination zone that can be moved through the channel. However, the spatial resolution of the recombination zone is limited by the wavelength of the emitted light (here 1–2 µm) and does not provide any information about the carrier density distribution along the channel [248]. The hole (electron) concentration should of course be highest at the injecting electrodes and gradually drop to a minimum or zero in the recombination zone.

One option to observe the local concentration of holes and electrons is to map PL intensity and thus local PL quenching during device operation. That is, suitable gate and drain voltages are applied while the channel is illuminated at a wavelength that optically excites the semiconductor and the resulting light emission is imaged. This approach was previously applied to polymer‐based light‐emitting FETs and planar light‐emitting electrochemical cells [333, 334, 335]. However, as the recombination zone itself emits light, the EL image must be subtracted from the PL image to obtain an indirect map of the carrier distribution. Furthermore, this method is restricted to materials that show sufficiently bright luminescence and the spatial resolution is limited by the emission wavelength. Applying such in situ PL mapping to electrolyte‐gated (6,5) SWCNT network transistors with a frozen pn‐junction at 200 K indicated differences in charge carrier excess depending on the position of the recombination zone, which also correlated with the ratio of trion to exciton emission in the EL spectra [178].

Alternatively, the shift of SWCNT Raman modes with doping can be used to non‐invasively map the carrier density in operating devices. This approach was first demonstrated for unipolar electrolyte‐gated networks of (7,5) SWCNTs [289]. The gradual decrease of the hole concentration from the injecting source to the drain electrode along a 20 µm channel was visualized by mapping the corresponding shift of the 2D mode (as discussed in Section 3.3). Here, the spatial resolution of approx. 300 nm was largely determined by the wavelength of the Raman laser (633 nm) and the confocal imaging system instead of the emission wavelength of the nanotubes.

A very intuitive example for carrier density mapping by Raman microscopy is shown in Figure 9a,b for an ambipolar electrolyte‐gated transistor with an aligned network of (6,5) SWCNTs. As the 2D Raman mode shifts to higher wavenumbers for both hole and electron accumulation (see Figure 7g in Section 3.3), mapping the 2D mode shift for the entire channel in the ambipolar regime not only visualizes the position of the recombination zone (see arrow in Figure 9b) for different gate voltages but also shows the gradual increase of hole and electron densities on either side of the recombination zone [291]. A closer inspection of the cross‐sections of the 2D mode shifts along the channel (Figure 9c) also confirms that the carrier density within the recombination zone is at a minimum (i.e., smallest shift compared to undoped state) but not at zero as expected from the model, which again explains the observation of trions in the EL spectra of electrolyte‐gated light‐emitting transistors [211].

Importantly, Raman mapping of doping in SWCNT networks can also be applied to semiconducting nanotubes for which PL is not easily or not at all detectable (e.g., SWCNTs with large diameters and thus emission wavelengths beyond typical InGaAs detectors). The main drawback of this method is the need for point‐by‐point collection of Raman spectra and the necessary post‐processing (peak fitting) to extract precise Raman shifts. However, modern Raman microscopes can be optimized to record spectral maps of large areas very fast and have become increasingly common in many labs [336, 337].

Using Raman shifts for in‐operando carrier density mapping is easy and very reliable at higher hole or electron densities, but the observable shifts for low to moderate doping levels (e.g., in FETs) are close to the spectral resolution limit (< 1 cm−1). In contrast to that, quenching of SWCNT fluorescence by charge carriers is highly sensitive, and even very small doping concentrations lead to noticeable emission intensity reductions that can be detected in near‐infrared PL images. This high sensitivity was recently exploited to investigate the tunable tactile synapse properties of pressure sensors based on a nanotube ECT with a structured iongel electrolyte [338]. The employed device layout is shown schematically in Figure 9d. The pressure sensor consists of a (6,5) SWCNTs network as the active channel material, which is barely touched by the tips of a pyramid array of a 3D‐patterned iongel that is connected to a side‐gate electrode. Even when a suitable gate voltage is applied, the accumulation of charge carriers (holes) in the nanotube network is limited by the minimal contact with the iongel. However, under applied pressure, the contact area between iongel and nanotube network increases, ions move, and hole doping can occur. Consequently, a higher drain current is measured (see Figure 9e). Importantly, the current flow persists after the pressure is released again and only slowly decreases. Subsequent pressure pulses increase the drain current further, thus showing synaptic plasticity in excitatory mode [338].

In situ PL imaging of a section of the channel, as shown in Figure 9f, revealed the underlying mechanism. Before pressure is applied, the PL from the (6,5) nanotubes in the channel is uniform without any signs of quenching or doping (Frame 1). As the pressure is increased, PL quenching becomes evident where the tip of one of the iongel pyramids is in contact with the nanotube network (blue square) indicating accumulation of holes (Frames 2 and 3, within about 20 s of initial pressure), which is enabled by anions from the iongel penetrating the network under the applied negative gate bias. The PL quenching is strongest where the iongel is in direct contact with the network, but the surrounding areas also show reduced emission and thus increased doping over time. Upon release of the applied pressure, the iongel recovers its initial shape quickly but de‐doping of the previously contacted area and the channel is very slow as shown in Frames 5 to 7 over a time period of about 60 s. Full recovery of the initial PL only occurs after roughly an hour. These time‐resolved images of PL quenching clearly show that slow de‐doping of the channel due to limited movement of the residual anions leads to the long retention time of the output signal of these pressure sensors and thus their functionality as tactile synapses [338]. The high sensitivity of SWCNT fluorescence to even very low carrier concentrations enables these direct insights into the temporal and spatial changes of doping profiles that would not be obtainable by Raman mapping.

As shown with these two examples, Raman and PL mapping of carrier distributions in nanotube networks are two relatively simple and non‐invasive optical techniques to characterize SWCNT devices during operation. Both could also be employed for checking the uniformity of doping in nanotube films over large areas (e.g., chemically doped films for thermoelectric generators) or for identifying (un‐)desired charge transport paths. The two methods are somewhat complementary, with PL mapping being particularly useful for detecting very low doping concentrations (locally or globally) within a fairly short time period (i.e., integration time of the camera or imaging detector). However, the investigated nanotubes must emit efficiently in a detectable wavelength range (i.e., below 1600 nm for typical InGaAs detectors). This restriction excludes large‐diameter nanotubes (e.g., plasma torch or arc‐discharge produced nanotubes), which are often applied in electronic devices where fast charge transport is important. Furthermore, the spatial resolution of PL imaging is limited by the emission wavelength (i.e., around 1 µm). In contrast to that, mapping the carrier density in SWCNT networks through Raman mode shifts only becomes reliable at relatively high carrier densities. Although, it can be applied for both small‐ and large‐diameter nanotubes. The spatial resolution of confocal Raman microscopy is determined primarily by the objective, the laser excitation wavelength, and the step intervals of the sample stage. Thus, it can reach less than 500 nm. However, depending on integration times and scan speed, the recording times for larger maps might become prohibitively long for in‐operando measurements.

Concluding Remarks and Open Questions

The impact of doping on the absorption, emission, and Raman spectra of semiconducting nanotubes has been investigated and indeed employed for various optoelectronic devices over the past two decades. Most importantly, the bleaching of the absorption of semiconducting SWCNTs can serve as a reliable metric for the carrier density in thin films and enables stable electrochromic devices in the near‐ to far‐infrared. The efficient quenching of near‐infrared emission from SWCNTs by excess charge carriers is a highly sensitive indicator even for very low doping levels. However, it also limits the efficiency of SWCNTs as near‐infrared emitters especially in electroluminescent devices.

Despite these obvious and well‐understood effects, there are still open questions. For example, the existence of trions as stable quasiparticles (charged excitons) in doped nanotubes has been clearly established, but further research is required to understand their precise formation pathways and properties especially in relation to the nanotube environment, mode of excitation, carrier density, and temperature. The bleaching of absorption, fluorescence quenching, and the shifts of exciton and trion peaks with doping were studied repeatedly and in much detail for (6,5) nanotubes, however, the dependence of the observed changes on nanotube diameter and chirality have not yet been explored. In general, larger diameter nanotubes show a weaker dependence of their excitonic features on carrier density and smaller energy differences, e.g., between exciton and trion. However, a more generalized structure‐property relationship for the impact of doping on different types of nanotubes would be helpful. With the increasing availability of monochiral small‐ and large‐diameter nanotubes in sufficient quantities, establishing at least empirical correlations and equations should just be a matter of performing these experiments systematically.

Much of the observed absorption peak shifts was explained with the concept of charge puddles that are generated by counterions in chemically or electrochemically doped SWCNTs and the corresponding inhomogeneous carrier distribution. However, the general impact of the surrounding medium (its dielectric constant or polarizability as well as charge traps) on the localization of carriers and the formation of trions is still unclear and the exploration of different types of counterions is still in progress. In addition to these questions, the complex shifts of the E22 and E33 transitions with doping require more attention and a theoretical framework. Likewise, a suitable theoretical description of nanotube absorption at very high doping levels (>1 nm−1) is still lacking. Indeed, doped SWCNTs with their narrow and well‐defined absorption and emission peaks even at room temperature could be a good test system for exploring effective many‐body physics of fermions in low‐dimensional materials in comparison to and beyond 2D semiconductors [339, 340]. One still underexplored area is the impact of temperature on all of the discussed doping‐induced spectral changes of semiconducting nanotubes. Almost all of the presented studies were performed at or around room temperature, thus possible temperature dependencies, especially at cryogenic temperatures, are still unknown.

The characteristic shifts of semiconducting SWCNT Raman modes with hole and electron doping have been applied to map charge carrier distributions in thin films and devices with high spatial resolution and even while the device was in operation. It would be very valuable to derive reliable quantitative correlations between carrier densities and the observed Raman shifts (similar to absorption bleaching) that also take into account the sample composition, e.g., different SWCNT diameters, bundles in networks, lattice defects, excitation wavelength, etc. A possible correlation of characteristic Raman shifts with PL quenching for the intermediate doping regime may also close the gap between these two mostly complementary carrier density mapping techniques. Moreover, there are still SWCNT Raman modes that were not yet studied with respect to doping, such as the intermediate frequency modes (IFM, 400 to 1000 cm−1) [341] or collective modes in nanotube bundles [342], which may show a different dependence on doping (in particular chemical doping). A closer look at the impact of doping on Raman excitation maps [343] may also give additional insights into the decrease of Raman intensities in relation to absorption bleaching and the possible impact of doping on the energy dispersion of the D‐ and 2D‐mode.

Overall, the spectral changes of semiconducting SWCNTs with hole and electron doping enable detailed insights into the interaction of charge carriers with their unique and highly diameter‐ and chirality‐dependent phonons and electronic structure. Various spectroscopic signatures of doping can be applied directly in devices for the detection of excess carriers and for mapping the charge carrier distribution with very simple and straightforward microscopy techniques that are available in most laboratories working with SWCNTs.

Future practical applications of the doping‐dependent optical properties of semiconducting nanotubes will most likely take advantage of the electrochemical modulation of absorption from the visible to the far‐infrared and THz range. The mechanical flexibility, environmental robustness, and photo‐stability of SWCNTs in combination with their high charge carrier mobilities and large modulation depth over such a broad wavelength range are truly unique and open a wide range of possibilities for novel devices. However, for their tailored application in large area films, in switchable metamaterials or 3D structures, the dispersion and sorting of SWCNTs must be scaled up further. In addition to that, new device concepts should take into account the sustainability of the employed materials. Thus, bio‐derived dispersants for SWCNTs (e.g., cellulose derivatives [344]) and device substrates (e.g., nanocellulose [345, 346, 347] or silk [348]) as well as more biocompatible and less harmful dopants and electrolytes [349] and their impact on electrochromic or thermoelectric device performance should be considered and explored. To achieve this, monitoring and mapping the optical signatures of doping in these novel SWCNT films and devices will again be very important.
