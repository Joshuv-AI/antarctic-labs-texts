Subsidy Schemes to Alleviate the Burden of Travelling for Healthcare: A Brief Report on Users' Experiences in Queensland, Australia
Authors: Leah Zajdlewicz, Belinda C Goodwin, Emma Pearse, Fiona Crawford‐Williams, Elizabeth A Johnston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIssue AddressedFor people in rural and remote areas, government‐funded travel subsidy schemes provide vital financial assistance and equitable access to specialist healthcare in city centres. However, few studies have examined users' experiences of these schemes.MethodsAdults travelling for healthcare in Queensland, Australia, completed an online survey capturing their most recent experience applying for the Patient Travel Subsidy Scheme (PTSS). Participants' responses were analysed using descriptive statistics and thematic analysis.ResultsFrom 69 respondents (aged 29–80 years, 60% female, 88% most recent application for cancer‐related care), 57% agreed information about the scheme was easy to access, although only 39% found the initial registration process easy. Most indicated that local hospital staff and PTSS officers were knowledgeable and helpful (65%–70%). Less agreed that staff were available to assist (48%) or that processes were well‐coordinated across hospitals (42%). Of those who sought reimbursement for travel costs (75%), almost two‐thirds (63%) were out‐of‐pocket post‐reimbursement (mean = AU$725; range = AU$20–10 000). Delayed reimbursement was relatively common (60%) and contributed to financial hardship (36%). For some (13%), challenges accessing the scheme impacted treatment decisions. User feedback included improved visibility of online application and reimbursement status, and fast‐tracking of high‐risk, urgent and ongoing cases.ConclusionsWhile information about the scheme and interactions with frontline staff were viewed positively by most, several system‐level challenges remain.So What?Substantial out‐of‐pocket costs and reimbursement delays contribute to financial hardship and can affect treatment decisions. Increased subsidy reflecting actual travel costs, timely reimbursement, fast‐tracking, and greater application assistance and coordination would improve user experiences.Keywords: financial support, health equity, health services, regional and remote, rural health

Introduction

People living outside of major cities with serious health conditions, such as cancer, often need to travel to metropolitan centres for specialist medical procedures and treatments. In many cases they are accompanied by family or friends, who provide vital practical, medical and emotional support. For patients and support people living in rural and remote areas, travelling for healthcare not only increases the physical and social burden associated with ill‐health, but can lead to financial strain due to expenses such as accommodation, transport, parking and food [1, 2]. To address this, Australian state and territory governments offer distinct reimbursement schemes for healthcare‐related travel expenses that vary across jurisdictions [3]. In Australia, ~27% of the population reside in regional and remote areas [4], making such schemes key for equitable health care access; however, research continues to identify financial toxicity, access barriers and administrative challenges associated with treatment‐related travel [5, 6, 7].

Policy discussion and advocacy efforts have focused on eligibility, subsidy amounts, and funding allocation, with less attention given to patient experiences with accessing information and applying for financial support via these schemes [7, 8]. This latter focus is important for ensuring travel subsidy schemes in Australia are optimised for supporting the physical, emotional, and financial health of regional Australians. In the state of Queensland, the Patient Travel Subsidy Scheme (PTSS) provides partial financial reimbursement for eligible travel and accommodation costs and is administered by Hospital and Health Services, which receive funding based on historical demand [9]. In this study, we examine patients' experiences with the PTSS with a focus on information provision, administrative processes, service quality and responsiveness to individual needs.

Methods

Setting and Participants

Queensland's large geographic area and highly decentralised population, with over 5.4 million people and approximately 75% living outside the Greater Brisbane Area, contribute to the need for many patients to travel considerable distances to access specialist healthcare [10]. To support this, the PTSS provides financial assistance to subsidise (but not fully cover) transport and short‐term accommodation costs for patients located more than 50 km from a treating hospital and, where clinically indicated, a support person [11]. Assistance is provided at set subsidy rates but varies according to eligibility and approved travel arrangements. This is arranged either through direct booking by health services or reimbursement to patients, subject to eligibility criteria and approved travel arrangements [11].

Eligible participants were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) who could provide informed consent, understand English, and who had previously applied for the PTSS. Recruitment occurred between June 2022 and April 2023 via social media and posters and flyers at community‐based organisations frequented by people accessing the PTSS (e.g., Cancer Council Queensland). Ethical approval was from the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (EC00172).

Procedure and Measures

An online anonymous survey collected information on sociodemographic characteristics, medical and treatment information, and outcomes relating to participants' most recent PTSS application. Participants answered a series of author‐developed statements to capture experiences on their most recent PTSS application. Responses reflected the degree to which participants agreed with each statement on a 5‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Additionally, open text items were included to examine participants' experiences in their own words.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics, medical condition and treatment, and PTSS application details. Geographical remoteness was coded according to the Australian Statistical Geography Standard 2021 [12] using residential postcodes reported at the time of survey completion. Frequencies and proportions of scale responses were calculated and responses to open‐ended questions were analysed using codebook thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns [13]. Coding and theme generation was completed by one researcher, reviewed by a second researcher for consistency, and refined through team discussion.

Results

Sixty‐nine eligible participants completed the survey. Most were female (N = 41, 60%), aged 59 years on average (range 29–80 years), not employed (N = 38, 61%). Participants mostly resided outside of metropolitan areas (41% outer regional, 25% remote and very remote areas, 24% inner regional).

Most participants had recently applied for the PTSS to cover cancer‐related travel (N = 59, 88%). Participants were mostly referred to the PTSS via their local hospital (N = 24, 36%), specialist (N = 21, 31%), family or friends (N = 15, 22%), and word of mouth (N = 13, 19%). Most had applied for more than one reimbursement (N = 51, 74%) and in the past 12 months (N = 54, 78%).

User Experiences

Table 1 summarises responses regarding the information received, ease of applying, service received, and sensitivity to needs. Over half of participants agreed that sufficient and clear information was provided to them about the PTSS, although 23% reported receiving conflicting information. In terms of completing and submitting paperwork, responses were generally positive, with 60%–70% agreeing that paperwork was easy to access and process, although only 39% found the initial registration process (Form A) easy. Responses regarding the service received indicated high agreement with the knowledge, support, and helpfulness of individual local hospital staff and PTSS officers. However, less than half agreed that PTSS officers were readily available to assist (48%) or that the processing of paperwork was well coordinated across multiple doctors and hospitals (42%). Results indicated that PTSS staff and processes accommodated practical and personal needs in most cases, while less than half of respondents felt that safety was a key consideration in their application outcome. Most participants responded neutrally (79%) to the item regarding sensitivity to cultural needs, potentially implying this was not a concern for them.

Application Outcomes

Most participants (N = 49/65, 75%) received or applied for reimbursement rather than having their travel directly booked and paid for by the PTSS office (N = 13/65, 20%), with three participants reporting other outcomes (e.g., refusals or support person decisions). When asked about their experiences, 60% (N = 32/53) reported experiencing reimbursement delays. Most received funds within 4 to 9 weeks (median = 6 weeks; IQR = 4 weeks; range = 2–52 weeks). Two participants had not been reimbursed at the time of the survey with one waiting more than 5 months. More than one third (N = 19/53, 36%) indicated experiencing financial hardship from having to wait for the reimbursement after paying costs upfront. Seven people (13%) indicated that challenges accessing the PTSS impacted their treatment decisions. For example, inadequate reimbursement for travel costs influenced decisions to cancel appointments, avoid travel, or consider relocating to access care more easily. Many participants (N = 39/62; 63%) reported remaining out‐of‐pocket expenses after reimbursement (mean = AU$725; SD = AU$1688; range = AU$20–10 000).

Recommendations for Improvement

Open‐text responses highlighted several positive aspects of the PTSS, including reduced financial stress, reduced worry associated with travel for care, ease of access, and supportive interactions with staff. Respondents expressed strong appreciation and highlighted the scheme's importance in enabling access to specialist care that may otherwise have been difficult to afford. Recommendations for improvement included integrating trauma‐informed support, clarifying eligibility criteria and guidelines, greater availability of staff to assist with completing applications, particularly for those unwell or applying for the first time, reduced reimbursement wait time, fast‐tracking of high‐risk, urgent and ongoing cases, increased reimbursements to reflect full travel costs, improved online tracking of application status and payments, and initiatives to increase community awareness of the support available via the PTSS.

Discussion

Our findings highlight key factors that facilitate or hinder travel subsidy scheme use, with implications for improving patient‐centred delivery. Interactions with frontline staff and application processes were viewed positively by most. Interpersonal support in navigating health system processes is crucial, particularly in decentralised systems like in Queensland where treatment often occurs in a different hospital and health service (HHS) to their local provider [15]. While individual staff were viewed as knowledgeable and supportive, increasing their availability to assist with applications and improved communication and coordination across HHSs may improve subsidy access. Encouragingly, positive reports of application processes in this survey may in part reflect the revised guidelines and protocols introduced in 2017 and the roll out of an electronic application system [16, 17]. However, further refinements could streamline initial registration for the PTSS as difficulty at this stage could be a barrier to first‐time users.

Despite positive experiences, participants reported poor coordination across hospitals, delayed reimbursements, and financial strain from upfront costs, and insufficient subsidy for actual travel costs. On occasion, these challenges influenced suboptimal treatment decisions, such as cancelling or delaying appointments. These negative experiences underscore the importance of system‐level reforms to improve access to and receipt of financial support. Other recommendations include clarifying eligibility criteria, coordinating appointment schedules across providers to reduce travel requirements, and improving online tracking of application status and payments [18]. Beyond these recommendations, expanded use of alternatives to patient travel, such as clinically appropriate virtual care models, may reduce the need for travel and reliance on the PTSS [19].

Delays in reimbursement and out‐of‐pocket costs highlight the financial vulnerability associated with treatment‐related travel, with implications for equity of access. Two‐thirds of participants who received reimbursement through the PTSS reported substantial remaining out‐of‐pocket expenses, with an average shortfall of $725. Shortly after data collection, subsidy rates increased modestly in Queensland [11]; however, these changes are unlikely to reduce financial burden, given average accommodation costs in Brisbane remain two‐to‐three times higher than the revised subsidy. Advocacy efforts in other states and territories have increased subsidy allowances. For example, in January 2026, the South Australian Government committed to a substantial increase to the accommodation subsidy in their Patient Assistance Transport Scheme [20]. Adequate subsidy rates and timely reimbursement are especially important given that travel and accommodation costs represent only one component of the broader financial burden associated with accessing healthcare.

Overall, most participants reported the PTSS was responsive to their personal circumstances. However, perceptions of safety as a central consideration were less evident and feedback regarding scheme improvements highlighted the need to strengthen trauma‐informed, risk‐sensitive, and flexible support mechanisms within the scheme to support unwell or vulnerable patients. This suggests that existing processes could be improved to better accommodate users' needs, including fast‐tracking of higher‐risk, urgent, or ongoing cases.

While the PTSS is used by people with various conditions, this sample predominantly comprised people with cancer. This reflects the typical users of the PTSS, given that cancer impacts one in two people by the age of 85 years [21] and specialist treatment centres are located in major cities, requiring frequent or prolonged travel for people in rural and remote areas. Given the small, self‐selected sample, and the potential over‐representation of financially disadvantaged participants, findings should be interpreted as exploratory and indicative rather than representative. Although the study reflects perspectives from a single state‐based scheme, it nonetheless contributes to ongoing efforts for subsidy reform nationally. It also provides valuable insights into patients' experiences of navigating travel subsidy schemes and opportunities to improve patient‐centred delivery of these schemes.
