Ultrasonography and Particle Image Velocimetry of Gliding Characteristics of Kager’s Fat Pad and Surrounding Tissues
Authors: Takeyuki Kato, Yasushi Fukuda, Hiroto Kobune, Takeshi Asano, Takumi Uchida, Koki Takahashi, Koji Koyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Kager’s fat pad (KFP), located at the posterior ankle joint, is divided into three regions: The Achilles-associated part (AT part), flexor hallucis longus - associated part (FHL part), and calcaneal wedge (Wedge part). Although the Wedge part has been studied several times, the gliding characteristics of the AT and FHL parts remain unclear. The purpose of this study was to clarify the gliding characteristics of KFP and the surrounding tissues using ultrasonography.Methods:20 healthy adult patients performed active motion in the prone position with knee extension and an ankle neutral position with a metronome at 60 BPM. The gliding velocity and direction of movement of the AT and FHL parts of the KFP, Achilles tendon, and flexor hallucis longus muscles were calculated using ultrasonography and particle image velocimetry. Gliding velocity comparisons were made using one-way repeated measures ANOVA and multiple comparison tests.Results:In terms of gliding velocity, the AT part of the KFP was significantly lower than the FHL part (1.03 ± 0.15 vs 1.18 ± 0.15, P < 0.05) and the Achilles tendon (1.03 ± 0.15 vs 1.19 ± 0.20, P < 0.01). Regarding the direction of movement, the FHL part of the KFP moved distally and showed movement opposite to that of the AT part.Conclusion:The differences in gliding velocity between the AT part and Achilles tendon shown in the present study suggest that the AT part may be involved in damage around the Achilles tendon. The differences in gliding velocity and direction of movement between the AT and FHL parts, which have the same KFP, suggest that they may have movements according to their respective roles.

INTRODUCTION

Kager’s fat pad (KFP), also known as the pre-Achilles fat pad, is located in the Kager’s triangle, with the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) muscle and tendon anterior to the triangle, the Achilles tendon posterior to the triangle, and the calcaneus bone inferior to the triangle.[1] KFP has been reported to glide during ankle plantar flexion,[2] and it has been suggested that the KFP may assist in lubrication between the calcaneus and Achilles tendon and distribute stress by gliding in response to the load placed on the Achilles tendon during ankle plantar flexion.[3] Malagelada et al. reported a significant increase in pressure on the KFP during maximal dorsiflexion and plantar flexion compared to the ankle-neutral position.[4] Overall, it has been reported that gliding and functional deformation associated with joint motion are important for KFP, and that a reduction in these functions may contribute to retrocalcaneal bursitis and Achilles tendon disorders, as it does not act as a lubricant or buffer for surrounding tissues.[45678] In addition, the basic function of the KFP is closely associated with its characteristic anatomical structure.

Other features of the KFP include the presence of three regions closely associated with its surrounding tissues, mainly classified as the “Achilles-associated part” (AT part), the “FHL-associated part” (FHL part), and the “calcaneal wedge” (wedge part).[23] The AT part protects the blood vessels entering the Achilles tendon, the FHL part supports the gliding of the wedge during plantar flexion of the ankle, and the wedge part regulates pressure changes against the posterior calcaneal bursa. The importance of each of these KFP regions is attributed to their interlocking gliding properties in response to joint motion.[2] Several studies have been conducted on the wedge part. Ricci et al. reported that the wedge moves distally within the retrocalcaneal bursa during plantar flexion of the ankle and glides between the calcaneus and the Achilles tendon.[7] Gupta et al. reported that the wedge glides proximally during heel strike and distally during toe-off.[9] Ghazzawi et al. also reported a significant increase in the wedge gliding distance during active plantar flexion of the ankle compared to passive plantar flexion.[3] They also found greater gliding of the wedge during plantar flexion under load while standing on one leg compared to both passive and active plantar flexion. However, there are few reports on the AT and FHL parts of the KFP, and their gliding characteristics have not been clarified. These two parts are considered to be associated with the Achilles tendon and the FHL, respectively. Watanabe et al. reported ankle joint dysfunction due to adhesion between the Achilles tendon and the KFP, pointing out the importance of inter-tissue gliding and dynamic evaluation.[8] Therefore, for a comprehensive understanding of the gliding characteristics of KFP, the evaluation of gliding, including the associated surrounding tissues, may be important for its clinical significance. Although there have been many studies on the wedge part, the AT and FHL parts have not been adequately studied despite their clinical importance.

US Ultrasonography

Ultrasonography (US) is used to evaluate tissues such as the infrapatellar fat pad (IFP) and KFP. Because US is noninvasive to the human body and can observe images in real time, it is considered one of the most important tools for the early detection and follow-up of inflammation and trauma.[310111213141516] Although these studies used muscle fiber and bone landmark measurements, it is difficult to evaluate the AT and FHL parts of the KFP objectively for detailed movement because of the inability to obtain appropriate landmark points. As fat pads functionally deform and glide in response to joint motion, objective methods for evaluating these dynamic properties are important.

We hypothesized that the AT and FHL parts of the KFP exhibit different gliding characteristics because they are adjacent to the Achilles tendon and FHL, respectively.

Therefore, we hypothesized that the gliding characteristics of the KFP would exhibit different movements during ankle plantar flexion. Thus, in this study, we aimed to determine the gliding characteristics of KFP and its surrounding tissues using US.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

The participants were recruited directly and verbally by the researchers from among university students and related persons, asking for their cooperation. The purpose and content of the study were explained to each participant, and those who met the selection criteria were selected from among those willing to participate. The selection criteria were as follows: healthy patients with no history of ankle joint injury (fracture, dislocation, sprain, Achilles tendinopathy, or Achilles tendon rupture) or exercise habits; thus, 20 patients with 20 limbs were included in the study (age: 19.8 ± 1.2 years, height: 171.3 ± 0.1 cm, weight: 65.7 ± 8.1 kg, body mass index (BMI): 22.4 ± 2.7 kg/m2, and range of motion in plantar flexion: 45.2° ± 0.8° (mean ± standard deviation [SD]). Besides, the dominant foot in all patients was the right foot, and in this study, the dominant leg was defined as the leg used primarily for kicking. In addition, the sample size was calculated a priori using the G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). With an effect size of 0.25, a significance level of 5%, and a power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated to be 18 cases. Patients with a history of ankle trauma, joint laxity, or limited range of motion were excluded. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Tokyo Ariake University School of Medicine (Approval Date, July 22. Ariake University School of Medicine Ethics Approval No. 460). The patients were informed about the study via writing as well as verbally, and written informed consent was obtained subsequently.

Ultrasonography protocol

US was performed using B-mode imaging with LOGIQe BT12 and an 8–12 MHz linear probe (GE Healthcare, Japan). The AT and FHL parts of the KFP as well as the surrounding tissues of the Achilles tendon and FHL were visualized in the long-axis image to fit within the screen by keeping the probe perpendicular to the Achilles tendon [Figure 1]. In this position, the patient performed active motion from ankle neutral to maximum plantar flexion at a constant pace of 70 BPM [Figure 2] (intraclass correlation coefficients [ICC]) = 0.63, 95% confidence interval: 0.38–0.81). To avoid shaking of the dynamic images during plantar flexion due to the collision of the probe with the calcaneus, the probe was recorded with it being positioned 1 cm proximal to the calcaneus based on previous studies.[1718]

Three dynamic images were acquired for the gliding of four tissues (AT part/FHL part/Achilles tendon/FHL muscle) and used for analysis. The US pixel count was set to 532 × 432 pixels, and the frame rate was set to 50 fps. The settings were as follows: frequency, 8 MHz; gain, 98 Gn; dynamic range, 72 dB; depth, 4.5 cm; and focus/near the FHL muscle.

Particle image velocimetry protocol

The resulting dynamic US images were analyzed using particle image velocimetry (PIV). The PIV technique has been used in a wide range of fields, including engineering, science, and medicine, where flow is involved. The visualization of tissue flow and motion has been reported, including vector flow mapping and the measurement of blood flow using echo-PIV.[192021] It has also been used to evaluate gliding for musculoskeletal tissues such as muscles and intertissues.[2223] For the PIV analysis, we utilized the previously validated method described by Kato et al.,[24] who demonstrated high reliability in measuring soft-tissue gliding (ICC = 0.60–0.75). This study was conducted using a cross-correlation coefficient processing algorithm with Flow PIV (Library Co., Ltd., Japan). The PIV was set up with a measurement point spacing of 7 pixels × 7 pixels, an interrogation window of 15 pixels × 15 pixels, and a search window of 25 pixels × 25 pixels, and the gliding velocities and directions of movement in the four tissues (AT part/FHL part/Achilles tendon/FHL muscle) were calculated.[24] The display format of the calculated angle vectors was 360°, with vectors based on 0° or 360° in the distal direction, and 180° in the proximal direction. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 software for Windows (IBM, USA). The average angle vector for each of the four tissues was calculated to determine the direction of movement. The gliding velocities of the four measured tissues were compared using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality, and parametric tests were used for statistical analysis as they were normally distributed. Because the results of the Mauchly test did not assume sphericity, a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. A post hoc multiple comparison procedure (paired t-test with Bonferroni adjustment) was performed when significant differences were observed. All measurements are expressed as the mean ± SD, and the statistical significance level was set at 0.05. Moreover, effect size and post hoc power analyses were performed using the G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany).[2526]

RESULTS

The physical characteristics of the 20 patients who participated in the experiment were as follows: age: 19.8 ± 1.2 years, height: 171.3 ± 0.1 cm, weight: 65.7 ± 8.1 kg, BMI: 22.4 ± 2.7 kg/m2, and range of motion in plantar flexion: 45.2° ± 0.8° (mean ± standard deviation).

The AT part of the KFP showed a proximal direction of movement similar to that of the Achilles tendon. In addition, the FHL part migrated in a distal direction, with the polar opposite direction of the AT part [Figure 4 and Table 1]. The results of the one-way repeated-measures ANOVA (Greenhouse–Geisser ε correction method) showed a significant difference in the gliding velocities of the four tissues (P < 0.05, η2 = 0.167).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the gliding characteristics of KFP and the surrounding tissues using US and PIV. The AT part of the KFP showed a significantly lower gliding velocity than the Achilles tendon, and the direction of movement was proximal, similar to that of the Achilles tendon. Furthermore, it exhibited a significantly lower gliding velocity than the FHL part. The AT and FHL parts showed opposite polar movements in the proximal and distal directions, respectively.

Stecco et al. and Naňka et al. reported that Achilles tendons do not have a synovial sheath and are enveloped throughout their length by paratenons.[2728] Stecco et al. observed that the Achilles tendon glides freely inside the paratenon because of the loose connective tissue.[27] Furthermore, several studies showed that this paratenon also envelops KFP.[2729] Theobald et al. suggested that the AT part is encased by paratenon, which minimally restrains the movement of the AT part and adopts a structure that stabilizes the blood vessels.[2] Szaro et al. noted that the connection between the KFP and paratenon may reduce the mutual gliding between the paratenon and Achilles tendons, and that this may be due to Achilles tendon disorders such as overuse changes.[29] These findings suggest that differences in the binding styles of the AT part and paratenon and the Achilles tendon and paratenon may have influenced the respective gliding velocities. Additionally, the differences in velocities observed in this study may be related to injuries around the Achilles tendon, similar to the findings of Szaro et al.[29]

The AT part of the KFP exhibited a significantly lower gliding velocity than the FHL part. Moreover, the AT and FHL parts showed opposite polar movements in the proximal and distal directions, respectively. Differences in gliding and deformation between fat pad parts, as shown in the present results, were also evaluated in a similar fat pad, the IFP. Vera-Pérez et al. reported differences in the intensities and tissue elasticities between the superior and inferior portions of the IFP.[30] Macchi et al. reported that the superior layer of the IFP had a higher rate and amount of change in thickness than the inferior layer.[31] Furthermore, Nakanishi et al. reported that the inferior layer of the IFP was significantly faster than the superior layer during knee joint extension.[32] Fontanella et al. showed that the superior and inferior layers of the IFP have higher strain and minimum principal stress values, respectively, suggesting that the IFP may adapt to the load applied to the knee joint by forming different mechanical properties between the parts.[33] On the other hand, regarding the function of KFP, Kinugasa et al. reported that the Achilles tendon buckled when KFP was removed, suggesting that KFP may inhibit excessive bending of the Achilles tendon and act as a buffer.[34] Presumably, the AT part is adjacent to the Achilles tendon and may be subjected to greater bending stress than the FHL part. In contrast, the FHL part has been suggested to assist in the gliding of the bursal wedge and may have a lubricating function.[2] The results of this study showed differences in the gliding velocities and directions of movement between the AT and the FHL parts. Therefore, it is possible that the AT and the FHL parts may differ in the type of stress load to which they respond and may exhibit gliding characteristics according to their respective roles.

The results of this study may be useful in several clinical applications. First, it included healthy adults. Abnormal fat pad dynamics have been suggested to contribute to pain, impingement, and inflammation in surrounding tissues, and case reports have described impaired gliding of the Achilles tendon and KFP.[46893536] Therefore, understanding the gliding characteristics of the KFP and surrounding tissues will aid in the evaluation and clinical examination of Achilles tendon disorders and KFP injuries. In addition, the results obtained in this study may contribute to our understanding of the biomechanical role of the KFP during plantar flexion of the ankle joint.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, vorticity measurements of tissue motion could not be performed and evaluated. Second, because ankle plantar flexion is a nonload-bearing motion, the effects of loading on the gliding velocity and direction of motion of the KFP were not examined. Third, the ultrasonography imaging system provides a two-dimensional image; hence, it was not possible to capture complex three-dimensional gliding characteristics during measurement.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this study investigated the gliding characteristics of KFP and the surrounding tissues using the US. As a result, differences in gliding velocities and directions of movement were observed between the AT and FHL parts of KFPs. These results may indicate role-specific gliding properties and highlight the importance of evaluating KFP as functionally distinct parts rather than as a single structure, as in IFP,[30] which may lead to the establishment of a more effective clinical evaluation and treatment for the disorder.
