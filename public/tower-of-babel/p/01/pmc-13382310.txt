Protocol for the isolation of inducible GαsR201C explant cultures for ex vivo modeling of fibrous dysplasia of bone and pathologic osteoclastogenesis
Authors: Morteza Abyadeh, Jarred M Whitlock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFibrous dysplasia (FD) is caused by activating mutations in GNAS that disrupt skeletal progenitor differentiation and promote osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Here, we describe a protocol to isolate bone marrow stromal cells from doxycycline-inducible Gαs∧R201C mice and assess osteoclast formation using tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining. We also detail procedures for confirming transgene induction by X-gal staining to detect β-galactosidase activity. This workflow provides a reproducible ex vivo platform to evaluate stromal-driven osteoclastogenesis in a controlled setting.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Whitlock et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cell culture, Cell isolation, Developmental biology, Health Sciences, Model Organisms

Before you begin

Fibrous dysplasia of bone (FD) results from gain-of-function mutations in GNAS that cause constitutive Gαs signaling in the skeletal stem cell lineage. To model FD, we use a previously described quadruple transgenic murine model of FD - Prrx1-Cre/LSL-rtTA-IRES-GFP/TetO-GαsR201C/TetO-βgal.1,2,3 In this animal model, both GαsR201C and β-gal expression are under control of Tet-On promotors, which require tetracycline activation of the reverse tetracycline-transactivator (rtTA) for expression. rtTA expression is restricted by a floxed stop cassette and is therefore induced only in cells where Prrx1-Cre excises the stop sequence, specifically within skeletal stem/progenitor cell populations of the limbs and craniofacial bones. It has been shown that Prrx1 is expressed in skeletal stem/progenitor cells within craniofacial tissues.1 Hence, doxycycline administration selectively activates GαsR201C and β-gal expression in skeletal stem cell progeny in the cultures described (i.e., osteoprogenitors), but not in osteoclast progenitors or other cell types. This protocol describes the isolation of bone marrow cultures from these mice and the evaluation of osteoclast formation by TRAP staining. In addition, X-gal staining confirms β-gal reporter activation as evidence of successful transgene induction in osteoprogenitors.

Innovation

This protocol establishes a primary bone marrow derived culture system that recapitulates some of the intracellular signaling perturbations driving FD pathology. By combining stromal cell–derived osteoclastogenic signaling with inducible activation of the GαsR201C mutation, the model captures the functional interaction between the key cellular components responsible for FD-associated bone remodeling abnormalities. By modeling both stromal-driven signaling alterations and osteoclast formation in a primary culture setting, this workflow provides a reproducible platform for studying the cellular mechanisms underlying FD and for evaluating factors that regulate osteoclastogenesis in this disease context. In addition, this model can be applied broadly to study osteoclastogenesis and pre-osteoblast–osteoclast signaling, processes that play critical roles in many bone diseases, including osteoporosis. Importantly, this culture system also provides a relatively simple and rapid platform for screening therapeutic compounds that target disrupted interactions between bone cell populations in pathological bone conditions.

Sample preparation

Before starting the protocol, freshly euthanized mice are needed for bone marrow isolation. Primary marrow cultures are generated from tibias and femurs. Doxycycline induction is performed on marrow cultures to activate GαsR201C, the production of osteoclastogenic factors, and ultimately, to stimulate osteoclast differentiation. TRAP and X-gal staining are performed to detect multinucleated osteoclasts and β-gal transgene expression in skeletal stem cell progeny, respectively.

Workflow for hindlimb dissection, bone isolation, and bone marrow extraction

(A) Removal of the carcass from 70% ethanol followed by drying with a paper towel prior to dissection.

(B) Skin removal to expose the hindlimbs, including incision around the hip and peeling of the skin toward the head.

(C) Dissection of hindlimbs by cutting connective tissue at the hip joint and dislocating the femoral head to detach the limb.

(D) Isolation and cleaning of femur and tibia by removal of muscle, tendons, and connective tissue, and separation at the knee joint.

(E) Preparation of bones and flushing of bone marrow using a syringe and needle to expel marrow into a sterile dish.

(F) Dissociation of flushed marrow by gentle trituration to obtain a homogeneous cell suspension, followed by plating in culture medium for expansion.

4.Bone Marrow Flushing (Figure 1E).a.Prepare the bones.i.Using a 25–27G hypodermic needle, puncture a small hole at both the proximal and distal ends of each femur, and only at the proximal end of the tibia, to open the marrow cavities.ii.Perform this step for each bone.Note: Be gentle with the needle and twist it in a drilling motion rather than trying to force it through the epiphysis. The goal is to drill into the cavity, not stab through it.Note: Do not use the same needle used to drill the bone ends to also flush out the bone marrow, as it will likely become clogged with bone fragments.b.Flush the bone marrow.i.Fill a 1 mL syringe with αMEM (no additives).ii.Insert the needle into one end of the bone and flush the marrow into a sterile 35mm dish.iii.For the tibia, cut the distal end to allow the bone marrow to flush out.iv.Begin with a few short pulses to loosen the marrow and then apply steady pressure until the marrow plug is expelled from the bone shaft.c.Repeat flushing.

Expected outcomes

Primary marrow cultures derived from murine bone marrow form mixed, adherent cultures that initially resemble fibroblast-like colonies within 5–7 days of plating. These cultures can be passaged at a 1:6 dilution (i.e., 1-part passaged culture + 5-parts fresh media = 6 total parts) for at least 4 passages provided they are not allowed to overgrow. Upon subculturing, doxycycline induction at 30–40% confluency elicits the expression of GαsR201C and β-gal (dependent on genotype) specifically in osteoprogenitors of the skeletal stem cell linage, which are detectable via X-gal staining. X-gal staining serves as a lineage tracer, demonstrating rtTA activation and transgene induction (Figure 2). Alternatively, GαsR201C possesses a C-terminal EE tag that others have used for verification.2 TRAP staining reliably identifies multinucleated osteoclasts after 3-4 days (fresh) or 5–8 days (cryopreserved) of induction (Figure 2). Formation of osteoclasts is dependent on RANKL produced by induced osteoprogenitors, as addition of OPG or anti-RANKL monoclonal antibodies blocks the formation of multinucleated, TRAP+ osteoclasts without affecting β-gal expression in osteoprogenitors.1 Both 10% and 20% FBS support induction, though initial culture attachment is optimized with 20% FBS.

Limitations

Osteoclast formation and fusion efficiency vary between mice and isolation batches (Figure 2). Over-confluent cultures during induction can detach or form aggregates, obfuscating visualization of osteoclast formation and osteoprogenitor proliferation/lesional formation. This protocol measures osteoclast formation and lineage-specific transgene expression but does not directly assess resorptive activity. This model has been employed to assess mineral resorption previously1,3 and likely exhibits bone resorption activity if assessed via classical bone slice assay. Marrow isolation delays following mouse euthanasia can reduce yield, impair cell attachment, and alter induction capacity.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Bone marrow is difficult to flush (step 4).

Potential solution

This may occur if there is a delay between euthanasia and bone marrow isolation, leading to coagulation, or if the needle becomes clogged with bone fragments or connective tissue. Isolate bones immediately after euthanasia. If processing multiple mice, isolate marrow sequentially, complete the first mouse before starting the second to avoid necrosis of the marrow and coagulation. If flushing remains difficult, repeat the procedure using a fresh needle.

Problem 2

Slow marrow culture adherence and growth (step 5c).

Potential solution

Suboptimal attachment or low initial cell yield may delay culture growth. If primary isolated cells are less than 75% confluent after 7 days, replace the medium and extend the culture for an additional 2–3 days before passaging. If the culture still fails to expand adequately, discard the sample and repeat the isolation.

Problem 3

Cells detach or form large, three-dimensional lesions during induction (step 7).

Potential solution

Induction should be started when cultures reach approximately 30–40% confluency. Avoid inducing over-confluent or prolonged cultures, as this may lead to cell detachment or large, difficult to visualize lesions to form.

Problem 4

Few large osteoclasts after 5 days of induction (step 7).

Potential solution

Extend doxycycline induction to 7–8 days to allow sufficient time for osteoclast multinucleation, as osteoclast formation may vary depending on the mouse line and osteoclast progenitor density.

Problem 5

Weak or uneven TRAP staining (step 8).

Potential solution

Ensure that the TRAP staining solution completely covers the entire well, including the center and edges, to allow uniform staining.

Problem 6

Over- or under-staining with TRAP (step 8).

Potential solution

Monitor TRAP color development every 5 min and stop the reaction when multinucleated osteoclasts are clearly visible (typically 5–20 min). Save the staining solution and reuse it to stain additional plates if needed. Err on the side of weaker staining, as plates can be restained if necessary.
