Association Between Neighborhood‐Level Income and the Incidence of Cardiovascular Events Varies by Immigration Status: A Population‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: Manav V. Vyas, Hibo Rijal, Amy Y. X. Yu, Peter C. Austin, Anna Chu, Maria Santiago‐Jimenez, Jiming Fang, Nadia A. Khan, Husam M. Abdel‐Qadir, Moira K. Kapral
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeighborhood‐level income is inversely associated with cardiovascular events; however, it is uncertain whether this association varies with immigration status.Methods and ResultsWe conducted a population‐based cohort study of 5.2 million (53% women, 19% immigrants) urban‐dwelling people aged ≥40 years without a prior history of cardiovascular disease in Ontario, Canada. Neighborhood‐level income was measured in quintiles from quintile 1 (lowest) to quintile 5 (highest), and immigrants were defined as those born outside of Canada who moved to Canada after 1985. We estimated the association between neighborhood‐level income and the rate of incident cardiovascular events (hospitalization for stroke or myocardial infarction, or cardiovascular death) using multivariable cause‐specific hazards models and added an interaction term to see if the association varies by immigration status. The absolute difference in the rate of cardiovascular events across income quintiles was less pronounced in immigrants than in long‐term residents: age‐ and sex‐adjusted rate per 1000 person‐years in quintile 1 versus quintile 5: 5.69 versus 4.10 in immigrants and 8.37 versus 5.87 in long‐term residents. In adjusted models, the interaction between immigration status and neighborhoodl evel was significant (P interaction <0.001). The hazard of cardiovascular events declined with increasing income among long‐term residents (hazard ratio [HR]Q1vsQ5, 1.46 to HRQ4vsQ5, 1.10) and immigrants, albeit with a smaller gradient (HRQ1vsQ5, 1.43 to HRQ4vsQ5, 1.20).ConclusionsThe association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular disease incidence varies by immigration status. Understanding the social and structural factors associated with residing in low‐income neighborhoods can help with the development of prevention programs that improve health for all.

The association between neighborhood‐level measures of socioeconomic status (SES) and cardiovascular disease incidence and outcomes has been well established, with residence in lower SES neighborhoods conferring a higher disease incidence and worse outcomes compared with higher SES neighborhoods. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 In addition to a strong correlation between neighborhood‐level SES with individual SES, 5 high neighborhood‐level SES may also be associated with better access to healthier foods, potentially lower exposure to smoking, 6 and better health care access, leading to better screening, health behaviors, and treatment compared with low neighborhood‐level SES. 7 However, the association between neighborhood‐level SES and cardiovascular disease can be modified by other social determinants of health. 2 , 4

The number of immigrants in the world rose to a total 253 million in 2022, 8 and prior work suggests that recent immigrants in high‐income countries have a lower risk of cardiovascular disease due to a selection bias known as the healthy immigrant effect. 9 , 10 Thus, immigration status is recognized as an important social determinant of health. About 10 million people (≈23% of the total population) are considered immigrants in Canada, where immigration occurs through a points‐based system on the basis of education and work experience. Most recent immigrants in Canada come from India, China, and the Philippines, but Canada has also accepted a number of refugees from many conflict zones. An immigrant's decision to live in a particular neighborhood may be driven by the proximity to ethnic foods or concordant ethnic enclaves or ethnic density, and this may not be the case for long‐term residents. 11 , 12 Residing in neighborhoods with ethnic homogeneity may buffer stressors that new immigrants experience and raise social cohesion, a factor associated with improved health. 13 Also, as foreign education and work experience may not be recognized, there may be a dissociation between individual education level and individual or neighborhood income. Further, visible ethnic minorities living in Canada are less likely to smoke relative to other nonvisible minority Canadians, but some ethnic groups have a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. 14 Thus, the relationship between neighborhood SES and cardiovascular disease may differ between recent immigrants and long‐term residents. However, there are limited data on whether the association between neighborhood‐level SES and cardiovascular disease incidence differs between immigrants and long‐term residents.

Our objective was to evaluate if the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular disease incidence varies by immigration status in Ontario, Canada. We also evaluated if any observed association was attenuated after accounting for neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations, a proxy of neighborhood selection by immigrants. We hypothesized that the effects of neighborhood‐level income will be less pronounced in recent immigrants than in long‐term residents and potentially will be eliminated after accounting for neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations.

Methods

Setting

We conducted a population‐based cohort study in Ontario, which is Canada's most populous province and the province where up to 40% of all new immigrants to Canada reside. We excluded people residing in rural Ontario, defined as a population size <10 000 people, because >95% of immigrants in Ontario reside in urban areas. Ontario has a publicly funded, single‐payer health insurance plan (Ontario Health Insurance Plan) for all its residents, which covers essential health services, including physician and in‐hospital care. Temporary residents and visitors are not covered under this plan and thus are not included in our study. Some indigenous people residing on Federal Reserve lands were also not included as they are covered under a separate federally funded health care system.

The study was conducted using administrative databases that were linked using unique encoded patient identifiers and analyzed at ICES (previously the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences). The use of data in this project was authorized under section 45 of Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a research ethics board. Data‐sharing agreements prohibit ICES from making the data set publicly available, but access may be granted to those who meet prespecified criteria for confidential access (available at www.ices.on.ca/DAS).

Cohort Creation

We used the CANHEART (Cardiovascular Health in Ambulatory Care Research Team) cohort, a population‐based cohort derived from linkage of various databases. 15 We used the Registered Persons Database, which includes information on all people registered with the province and its health insurance plan (almost all people residing in Ontario have provincial health insurance). We identified people aged ≥40 years who were registered with Registered Persons Database and had a valid Ontario Health Insurance Plan number in the 5 years before the cohort start date of January 1, 2011. We excluded those with a prior history of major cardiovascular disease events, defined as hospitalization for stroke 16 or myocardial infarction 17 (MI) before the start date (look‐back window of 20 years) using a validated algorithm relying on hospitalization records. We obtained information on age and sex from the Registered Persons Database. We also obtained information on the following prevalent risk factors on the cohort start date: hypertension, 18 diabetes, 19 hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, 20 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, 21 and Charlson comorbidity index (based on hospitalizations in the prior 5 years) using validated algorithms (Table S1).

Exposures

Our primary exposure of interest was neighborhood‐level income, which was obtained from 2011 Census‐derived data on dissemination areas (DAs) and census tracts. A DA is the smallest area at which population characteristics from the Canadian Census are reported; it typically consists of 400 to 700 people. To calculate the neighborhood‐level income quintile, total income is first calculated for each DA by multiplying household incomes from the Census and total number of households in a DA. The quintiles are generated by ranking all DAs from lowest to highest income and then dividing them into appropriate fifths (ie, 20% of DAs in each quintile). Ontario neighborhoods were categorized by quintile of income from the lowest (quintile 1: lowest income) to the highest quintile (quintile 5: highest income).

We also included a measure called “proportion of racialized and minority populations.” This measure is derived on the basis of the proportion of people in a DA who reported being a racialized minority (this was self‐reported) and the proportion of people who are immigrants who arrived in the past 5 years. The information is collected as part of the Canadian Census 2011. Neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations is operationalized in the form of quintiles from the lowest (quintile 1 lowest concentration, reference group) to highest (quintile 5 highest concentration), where the proportion of racialized minorities and the proportion of recent immigrants are equally weighted when estimating the neighborhood‐level quintile. Because the metric includes 2 parts, immigration status and visible minority status (non‐White ethnicity or non‐White race), individuals in each quintile can potentially be discordant; for example, a Chinese Canadian‐born individual could reside in a predominantly South Asian immigrant neighborhood. 22

Immigration status was obtained by linkage with immigration records from the Immigration, Refugee, and Citizenship Canada's Permanent Residents Database. People born outside of Canada who arrived in Canada after 1985 were classified as immigrants and the remaining Ontario residents as long‐term residents. The Immigration, Refugee, and Citizenship Canada data also include information on region of origin of immigrants on the basis of their prior citizenship, immigration class (economic, refugee, or family/other), age at the time of immigration, time since immigration, and education before arrival in Canada (secondary or less; trade, diploma, or some university; bachelor's degree; and postgraduate degree) (see Table S1 for details).

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was hospitalization for a major cardiovascular event, which was a composite of incident stroke, MI, or cardiovascular death. Incidence of stroke and MI was a secondary outcome. We ascertained stroke or MI as a hospitalization with the most responsible diagnosis of stroke (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] code H34.1, I63.x, I64.x, or I61.x) or MI (ICD‐10 code I21 or I22) between cohort start date and December 31, 2018 (last date of follow‐up). This definition has been shown to have 85% to 95% sensitivity for both stroke and MI. 16 , 17 Information on cardiovascular death was obtained from the Office of the Registrar General's Vital Statistics–Death registry, where cause of death is listed in the death certificate.

Statistical Analysis

We compared the baseline characteristics of people in different quintiles of neighborhood‐level income, stratified by immigration status (recent immigrants versus long‐term resident). We used the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables.

We reported unadjusted and age‐ and sex‐adjusted rates of cardiovascular events per 1000 person‐years of follow‐up. We did the same for the component events of stroke, MI, and cardiovascular death. Standardization for age and sex was performed using the direct method with the 2011 Census population. We also estimated unadjusted cumulative incidence curves of cardiovascular events for each income quintile stratified by immigration status, accounting for the competing risk of noncardiovascular death.

We then performed a sequence of regression analyses. First, we constructed a multivariable cause‐specific Cox hazards model, a semiparametric model with unspecified survival, where the outcome of interest was the time to an incident cardiovascular event. Both neighborhood‐level income and immigration status were added to the model along with an interaction term between immigration status and neighborhood‐level income quintile (model 1). We reported hazard ratios (HRs) of cardiovascular events for each quintile, with quintile 5 (highest) as the reference category, stratified by immigration status. We adjusted for age, sex, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and Charlson comorbidity index). We accounted for death from noncardiovascular causes as a competing risk in our analyses and used robust variance estimator to account for the clustering of patients within the neighborhoods on which SES was measured. We constructed the same analyses for the secondary outcomes of stroke, MI, and cardiovascular death, separately. Second, we added a measure of proportion of racialized and minority populations to the multivariable models to evaluate if the addition of neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations altered the neighborhood‐level income–outcome association (model 2).

Sensitivity Analyses Performed Among Immigrants Only

Certain characteristics such as time since immigration, region of origin, and immigration class apply only to the immigrants, as this information is not defined for long‐term residents. To evaluate how these immigration‐specific characteristics influence the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular events, we created a subcohort consisting only of immigrants who arrived in Canada after the age of 40 years (ie, in their adulthood). In this cohort, we used the same covariates in model 1 and further adjusted for immigration‐specific characteristics, particularly region of origin (Africa, Caribbean, Africa, East Asia, Latin America, Middle East, South Asia, stateless, and Western countries), immigration class (economic, refugee, and family/other), and time from immigration to the cohort entry date of 2011 (continuous) (model 3). Finally, we added level of education before moving to Canada to model 3 to evaluate if education before arrival in Canada would change the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular events (model 4).

All analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). We set P<0.01 as the threshold for significance for interaction terms. 23

Results

We included 5.3 million urban Ontario residents aged ≥40 years without a prior history of cardiovascular events, of whom 981 158 (18.4%) were recent immigrants (Figure 1). Note that the quintiles of neighborhood‐level income are based on the DA and not the overall population; hence, the proportion of the cohort in each quintile is not 20% (Tables S2 and S3). Immigrants were more likely to reside in the lowest‐income neighborhoods (quintile 1) than long‐term residents (25.5% versus 16.0%; P<0.001), were younger (median age, 50 versus 55 years; P<0.001), and were less likely to have comorbid conditions (Table; Tables S2 and S3). About 10% of the immigrants in our cohort arrived before the age of 25 years, 47% of immigrants were economic immigrants, and 56% of immigrants had higher than secondary education before arrival in Canada (Table). Over a quarter of immigrants were from high‐income Western countries.

We observed 20 903 (2.1%) cardiovascular events in immigrants and 181 168 (4.2%) cardiovascular events in long‐term residents during a median follow‐up of 8 years. Overall, immigrants had a lower age‐ and sex‐standardized rate of cardiovascular events than long‐term residents: rate per 1000 person‐years, 5.09 (95% CI, 5.01–5.17) versus 6.84 (95% CI, 6.81–6.87) in long‐term residents. This was also true for the component cardiovascular events (Figure 2). In both immigrants and long‐term residents, the age‐ and sex‐standardized rates of cardiovascular events decreased with increasing neighborhood‐level income quintile; however, the difference in the absolute rates across different neighborhood‐level income was smaller in immigrants than in long‐term residents (Figure 2). The same was observed when evaluating unadjusted incidence curves representing the rate of cardiovascular events over time across income quintiles (Figure 3).

In multivariable adjusted cause‐specific hazard models (model 1), neighborhood‐level income was associated with the rate of incident cardiovascular events in both immigrants and long‐term residents such that those in the lowest quintile (quintile 1) had a higher hazard of cardiovascular events compared with those in the highest quintile (quintile 5), with a gradient from lowest to highest quintile (Figure 4). Immigration status modified the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular event incidence (P interaction<0.001) such that the gradient of change over quintiles was linear in long‐term residents but not immigrants; that is, the hazard of cardiovascular events declined with increasing income among long‐term residents (HRQ1vsQ5, 1.46 [95% CI, 1.44–1.48] to HRQ4vsQ5, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.08–1.11]), and this was also observed among immigrants, albeit with smaller difference (HRQ1vsQ5, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.34–1.47] to HR Q4vsQ5, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.13–1.26]) (Figure 4).

When examining results after accounting for neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations (model 2), residing in an ethnically diverse neighborhood was associated with a lower hazard of cardiovascular events than residence in an ethnically homogeneous neighborhood (highest versus lowest proportion of racialized and minority populations; HR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.73–0.75]). However, accounting for neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations resulted in minimal change in the HRs of cardiovascular events for the different quintiles of neighborhood‐level income, in both immigrants and long‐term residents, with immigration status still modifying the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular events (P interaction<0.001) (Figure 4).

Immigration status modified the association for the outcomes of myocardial infarction (P interaction<0.001) and cardiovascular death (P interaction=0.006) such that the gradient of decline in hazard of outcomes with increasing neighborhood‐level income quintile did not manifest in immigrants but was present in long‐term residents (Figure 5). This was not the case for the outcome of incident stroke where the interaction between immigration status and neighborhood‐level income did not reach significance threshold (P interaction=0.048) (Figure 5).

Sensitivity Analyses Performed Among Immigrants Only

In a subgroup of immigrants only who arrived after the age of 40 years, the addition of immigration‐specific characteristics of time since immigration, immigration class, and region of origin (model 3) marginally attenuated the gradient of association between neighborhood‐level income and incidence cardiovascular events (HRQ1vsQ5, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.18–1.35] to HR Q4vsQ5, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.07–1.23]). However, the addition of the highest level of education (model 4) did not further attenuate the point estimates for the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular events (Table S4).

Discussion

In this population‐based study in a single‐payer, publicly funded health care system, low neighborhood‐level income was associated with higher cardiovascular disease incidence. This association varied by immigration status such that the associations between neighborhood SES and cardiovascular disease incidence were less pronounced in immigrants than long‐term residents, especially the absolute rates. The observed associations did not change after accounting for neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations. The addition of immigration‐specific characteristics and education before arrival among immigrants also did not alter the effect of neighborhood‐level income.

Previous reports on neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular disease in high‐income countries have consistently shown an association between area‐level SES and a higher risk of cardiovascular disease. 2 , 3 , 4 Few studies, however, have explored the modifying effect of other social factors on this association. In the REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) cohort in the United States, race did not modify the association between neighborhood‐level SES and stroke outcomes. 2 In the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) study, accounting for neighborhood‐level and individual‐level SES attenuated the differences in the all‐cause death and cardiovascular disease death between White and Black participants and exacerbated the differences in Chinese and Hispanic participants compared with White participants. 24 However, immigration status was not recorded in these studies. We found that the age‐ and sex‐adjusted incidence rates of cardiovascular events were lower in immigrants when compared with long‐term residents, which is consistent with the “healthy immigrant effect” observed in immigrants in different jurisdictions. 10 , 25 We further found that the rates were less variable across different neighborhood‐level quintiles in immigrants compared with long‐term residents, and, in the adjusted models, the interaction term was statistically significant, supporting that the association between neighborhood‐level income and incidence of cardiovascular events varies by immigration status. One possible reason for our finding could be that immigrants' factors in choice of neighborhood residence may differ from than those of long‐term residents. In previous studies, immigrants have been shown to reside in particular neighborhoods on the basis of ethnic diversity, access to religious places of worship, and family ties. 26 , 27 , 28 However, even though the neighborhood‐level proportion of racialized and minority populations was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular events, its addition to our models did not attenuate the association between neighborhood‐level income and cardiovascular events for both immigrants and long‐term residents, suggesting that neighborhood selection based on proportion of racialized and minority populations in immigrants may not be the only explanation of the observed findings.

Reasons for a link between residence in a low‐income neighborhood and increased incidence of cardiovascular disease include poorer access to health care, 29 healthy foods, 30 and recreational facilities. 31 Another factor through which neighborhoods may exert their effects on cardiovascular health is the development of neighborhood social norms. 32 The social norms theory suggests that our actions can be influenced by our perceptions, or misperceptions, about how our peers think and act. 33 Because social norms in immigrants are rooted in long‐standing cultures, 34 immigrants in the lowest‐income neighborhoods may be less likely to be influenced by neighborhood social norms.

An acquired social determinant of health that may be more important than income for cardiovascular disease risk is education. 35 In the cohort of immigrants who arrived in Canada in adult life, adjusting for highest education before arriving in Canada did not alter the observed associations. It is possible that some immigrants may obtain additional training after arriving in Canada. Alternatively, education obtained in the source country before moving to Canada may not be valued, 36 potentially diminishing the protective effect of education in immigrants. Other protective effects in immigrants that were not studied may include factors such as being less lonely, 37 having higher social cohesion, 38 and community participation. 39 Finally, while a relationship between unemployment and coronary heart disease has been observed, 40 the effect of underemployment, 41 whereby one is overeducated for a role compared with others in the same role, on the risk of cardiovascular disease remains to be studied.

From a policy perspective, efforts to reduce cardiovascular disease in low‐income neighborhoods may include dedicated investments to improve access to better health care. Improving walkability in a neighborhood could be one such investment, as it is associated with lower rates of cardiometabolic disease in both immigrants and long‐terms residents. 42 Community‐based primary prevention programs in certain neighborhoods may be another avenue to improve cardiovascular health for all. 43 While the at‐risk or vulnerable population public health approaches would suggest targeting long‐term residents in low‐income neighborhoods, our findings call for the need to gain a better understanding why some Canadians are affected by neighborhood‐level SES more than others. This may help inform population approaches for public health policies that address structural determinants of health associated with living in a low‐income neighborhood. 44

This study is strengthened by the use of health administrative data that captured all hospitalizations for cardiovascular events in Ontario. Further, we were able to account for various individual‐level risk factors for cardiovascular disease. However, some limitations merit attention. First, our study cohort included only residents of urban areas, and it is conceivable that effects of neighborhood‐level SES on cardiovascular events could differ in rural areas, limiting our generalizability. Because of the availability of immigration records only after 1985, we had to classify the cohort into immigrants and long‐term residents on the basis of this artificial cutoff, which meant that long‐term residents included both Canadian‐born residents and immigrants who were in Canada before 1985, further limiting our results to recent immigrants. Second, neighborhood‐level SES is not equivalent to individual‐level SES, despite their high congruity. We did not have information on individual‐level SES such as income or employment, and we did not account for change in neighborhood over time. Also, we were not able to adjust for education level in long‐term residents, or for the education attained by immigrants after moving to Canada, as our data sets do not include this information. Future work could consider accounting for education attained in host nations to identify its potential protective effect on cardiovascular disease risk in immigrants. In addition, as the follow‐up period was relatively short to detect cardiovascular changes, we were not able to determine other nonhospitalized cardiovascular diseases such as the development of atherosclerosis. There is also a possibility that hospitalizations for stroke or MI may underrepresent true incidence in immigrant groups due to differential health care use pattern. Finally, we could not account for residual confounding due to diet, smoking status, or level of acculturation in immigrants (beyond time since immigration) in our analyses.

In summary, low neighborhood‐level income is associated with a higher cardiovascular disease incidence in both immigrants and long‐term residents, but the magnitude of its effects may be higher in long‐term residents than in immigrants. Hence, primary preventive strategies could benefit from improving their understanding of the composition of neighborhoods and existing social norms. Future work should focus on uncovering the interplay between structural and social determinants of health associated with the poor outcomes in residents of low‐income neighborhoods.
