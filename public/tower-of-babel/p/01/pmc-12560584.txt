Genome-wide identification, expression analysis of taars (trace amine-associated receptors) and response to cadaverine and putrescine in Mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi)
Authors: Xiaoru Dong, Maolin Lv, Qiyao Luo, Xiao Jiang, Xu-fang Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe unique feeding behavior of Siniperca chuatsi (S. chuatsi), which exclusively consumes live fry and rejects dead fry, severely limits the development of its aquaculture industry. This rejection of dead fry likely stems from an aversion to necromones (death-associated odors). Although a subset of trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs) containing key conserved residues (Asp3.32 and/or Asp5.42) are implicated in detecting amine-related odors, the taar gene members in S. chuatsi and their responsiveness to necromones remain unexplored.ResultsIn this study, we found that the aversion of S. chuatsi to dead prey is associated with necromones. Furthermore, we identified 68 taar family members in the S. chuatsi genome, distributed across six chromosomes, with 36 tandem duplication events generating 54 duplicated genes. Phylogenetic analysis classified taars into two classes, Class I and Class III, with a total of eight subfamilies. Synteny analysis revealed low synteny between the taars of S. chuatsi and Dicentrarchus labrax, with only 12 pairs of genes showing syntenic relationships. Protein sequence analysis demonstrated that taars contain amine recognition sites, N-glycosylation, and conserved cysteine structures, with high conservation and purifying selection observed within subfamilies. Subcellular localization revealed that TAARs are localized to the cell membrane and nucleus. Transcriptome data indicated that 61 taars are expressed in the olfactory epithelium, while only 19 are expressed in the brain. The response of representative taars to putrescine and cadaverine was divergent when comparing olfactory and brain tissues.ConclusionsThis study systematically identified the taar gene family in S. chuatsi, analyzing their evolutionary relationships, protein structures, subcellular localization, expression profiles in olfactory and brain tissues, and the response of selected TAARs to necromones (cadaverine and putrescine). Background

The sustainable development of the aquaculture industry is highly dependent on the research and application of formulated feeds. Currently, most major aquaculture species are fully fed with artificial feeds [1]. However, obligate carnivorous species, such as Siniperca chuatsi, continue to exhibit a strong preference for live prey. These species not only reject conventional pellet feeds but also show significant feeding reluctance toward dead bait, requiring the continuous provision of live bait fish during farming, which leads to increased production costs [2]. Therefore, shifting S. chuatsi feeding habits from live prey to dead prey consumption, or even ultimately to artificial diets, represents a critical issue for reducing aquaculture costs. In recent years, although artificial domestication strategies have allowed S. chuatsi to accept artificial feed during the juvenile stage, the long domestication period and high difficulty have significantly limited the widespread use of this technology [3]. Despite some insights into the neurobiological basis for S. chuatsi’s preference for live prey, based on whole-genome analysis and liver and brain transcriptome analysis, the mechanisms behind their aversion to dead food sources remain unclear [4, 5]. Compared to live bait fish, dead bait fish significantly accumulate biogenic amines (such as cadaverine and putrescine) due to tissue autolysis and microbial metabolic processes [6]. These nitrogen-containing organic compounds are characterized by strong volatile foul odors, which can trigger innate aversive responses in vertebrates [7, 8]. In formulated feeds, although biogenic amines (e.g., putrescine, cadaverine, histamine) are not intentionally added, raw materials inherently contain substantial amine residues that carry over into the final product [9]. These amines likely exacerbate S. chuatsi rejection of artificial diets, thereby increasing domestication challenges. Consequently, developing low-amine-sensitivity strains through selective breeding or editing amine-sensing genes via genetic engineering represents a potential strategy to mitigate critical bottlenecks in S. chuatsi aquaculture [10].

The recognition of amine chemical signals primarily relies on trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs), a receptor family that belongs to a specialized subclass within the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily and is highly expressed in the olfactory epithelium [11]. The initial characteristic of TAARs was their ability to detect low concentrations (0.1 to 10 nM) of endogenous biogenic amines, such as tyramine and 2-phenylethylamine, while they were unresponsive to traditional biogenic amines [12–14]. Subsequent studies revealed that TAARs are highly abundant in olfactory epithelial cells, where they act as receptors for odor compounds, particularly volatile amines, with a very high affinity for these molecules [15, 16]. The origin of taars can be traced back to ancient times, with taar-like (tarl) genes found in jawless vertebrates such as Petromyzon marinus, despite lacking some classical taar motifs [16]. Within the taar family, olfactory functionality has independently emerged twice: once in jawless fish and once in jawed vertebrates [17]. The taar genes first appeared in cartilaginous fishes and have been retained across teleost, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals [18, 19].A recent study suggests that the origin of taar and tarl genes can be attributed to the duplication of serotonergic receptor 4 (htr4) in vertebrates [17]. In tetrapods, taars are categorized into two clades, denoted as clades I and II, while a unique clade III is identified in fish [16]. The initial duplication of primitive taar genes seemingly occurred prior to the divergence of cartilaginous and teleost, giving rise to class I and II taar branches, respectively [20]. The earliest Clade II taar gene duplication in the teleost lineage generated ancestral taar12 and taar13 genes, with ancestral taar12 and taar13 genes giving rise to the complete set of mammalian taar genes and teleost taars of class II. Among these, only class II taars exhibit olfactory functionality across tetrapods and teleosts, although taar12 within class II seems to be lost in more modern fish species (neoteleosts). The taar13 appears as a single gene in early-derived finfish and has undergone expansion in certain teleosts [16, 17, 21].

Each type of TAAR detects different amines, some of which can trigger innate olfactory behaviors [20]. Clade I TAARs primarily detect primary amines produced by amino acid decarboxylation, while Clade II TAARs exhibit greater affinity for tertiary amines [22]. Both Clade I and Clade II TAARs contain the classic amine recognition motif — an aspartate residue (Asp3.32) located in the third transmembrane α-helix, which forms a salt bridge with the ligand’s amine group [23, 24]. In contrast, most clade III TAARs in teleost lack Asp3.32 and are suspected to be incapable of detecting amines [16]. Currently, no ligands have been identified for any of teleost TAARs, except for a few TAARs in Danio rerio, which have been shown to recognize amine substances. These D. rerio TAARs contain not only Asp3.32 but also Asp5.42, together forming a common binary recognition motif. In most TAARs lacking Asp3.32, Asp5.42 is preserved, which increases the likelihood that these receptors recognize amines located in non-classical inverted orientations [21].

S. chuatsi is an important economic fish in China, with an annual production of nearly 500,000 tons, reflecting its significant role in Chinese aquaculture [25]. However, its aversion to dead prey sources poses a serious limitation to its farming development. In this study, we found that the aversion of S. chuatsi to dead prey is associated with necromones and identified the members of the amine-sensing taar gene family based on the genome of S. chuatsi, extensively characterized the features of the taar family, and examined the expression of taars in the olfactory epithelium and brain through transcriptomic analysis. We also determined the subcellular localization of TAARs and explored their responsiveness to compounds such as cadaverine and putrescine.

Results

Necromones trigger feeding rejection responses in S. chuatsi

To investigate the impact of death-related odors on the feeding behavior of S. chuatsi, we observed their predatory responses toward four types of prey fish (live prey without necromones, live prey treated with necromones (cadaverine and putrescine), dead prey with necromones and dead prey without necromones) (Fig. 1A). The results indicated that S. chuatsi actively preyed on untreated live prey and freshly dead prey, but consistently refused to consume dead prey carrying death-related odors (Fig. 1B). Notably, when live prey fish were exposed to death-related odors, S. chuatsi lost interest in feeding, significantly reduced its attacks, or even ceased feeding entirely (Fig. 1C). These findings suggest that the presence of death-related odors (cadaverine and putrescine) played a key role in triggering the rejection of carcass prey by S. chuatsi.

Identification and physicochemical characterization of the taar genes

To identify the members of the taar family within S. chuatsi, we conducted a comparative analysis, aligning 431 taar sequences from 11 different species (Mus musculus, Rattus norvegicus, Gallus gallus, Xenopus tropicalis, Danio rerio, Oryzias latipes, Takifugu rubripes, Salmo salar, Gasterosteus aculeatus, Lethenteron camtschaticum and Dicentrarchus labrax) with S. chuatsi genome. Through a combination of sequence similarity and conserved domain analysis, we identified a total of 68 taar genes in S. chuatsi (GenBank accession numbers: PX277156-PX277218). To gain insights into the physicochemical characteristics of TAAR proteins, we performed bioinformatics evaluations. The findings revealed a variability in the number of amino acids within TAAR proteins, ranging from 272 to 383 amino acids. Additionally, these proteins exhibited molecular weights spanning from 30.20 to 43.44 kDa, with isoelectric points (pI) falling within the range of 6.853 to 8.536 (Supplementary Table S1).

Chromosome location and phylogenetic analysis of TAAR proteins

TBtools II was used to visualize the chromosomal distribution of the taar gene family. The analysis revealed that taar genes are located on six chromosomes (Chr03, Chr06, Chr10, Chr12, Chr16, and Chr24) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2). Among them, Chr03 and Chr24 each harbor only one taar gene. Chr10 and Chr12 exhibit a higher density of taar genes, forming three distinct gene clusters. Employing the MCScanX tool, we further investigated tandemly duplicated genes within the taar gene family (delineated by red arcs). The results indicate a significant occurrence of tandem duplication events within the taar gene family, encompassing a total of 54 tandemly duplicated taar genes, constituting 36 tandem duplication events.

Collinearity analysis and selection pressure analysis of the taar gene family

To explore evolutionary clues regarding taar members between S. chuatsi and other species, genomic collinearity analysis was performed between S. chuatsi and D. labrax. The taar gene family is distributed across six chromosomes in both S. chuatsi and D. labrax, yet only 10 orthologous taar gene pairs exhibit syntenic conservation (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table S2). This differential synteny likely results from distinct evolutionary timing of tandem duplication events in their respective taar families. We compared the amino acid sequence similarity of 68 taar genes in S. chuatsi. The results showed differences in the sequence similarity between the pairs of taar genes (27.33%−99.90%) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table S3). Specifically, the sequence conservation was lower between different classes of taar genes, while members within the same subfamily exhibited higher homologous conservation. Positive selection analysis revealed that, among the 2276 gene pairs examined, 93.14% had a Ka/Ks ratio less than 1 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Table S4). Under BUSTED’s unconstrained model, we identified six positive selection sites (103, 104, 112, 238, 309, and 310) (ω > 1, Epost[α] > 0.95, p < 0.05) (Table 1, Supplementary Table S5). This result indicates that while the taar gene family in S. chuatsi has undergone strong purifying selection acting on the overall sequence during evolution, specific sites have experienced positive selection.

The structural analysis of TAAR proteins

We analyzed the protein structures of TAARs and performed 3D structural homology modeling of one member of the S. chuatsi TAARs using AlphaFold 3 (Fig. 5A). The results indicate that all 68 TAAR proteins exhibit 7 transmembrane domains. Moreover, we identified a conserved N-glycosylation site in TAAR, near the N-terminus of the first transmembrane region, which is present in nearly all TAAR proteins, and two conserved cysteines located in the second intracellular domain and the second extracellular domain (Supplementary File 1). A previous report highlights the critical role of Asp3.32 in the third transmembrane domain and Asp5.42 in the fifth transmembrane domain of TAAR for recognizing cadaverine [21]. Thus, we analyzed these specific positions across TAAR proteins. Our findings revealed that 6 TAAR proteins exclusively contain Asp3.32, 45 TAAR proteins exclusively contain Asp5.42, while 15 TAAR proteins contain both Asp3.32 and Asp5.42 (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, 2 TAAR proteins lack both residues. Using the MEME Suite database for search and comparison, we identified 6 conserved motifs in the S. chuatsi taar gene family (Fig. 5C). Based on the above analysis, the structural features of TAAR proteins were summarized and schematically represented to provide an intuitive understanding of the protein architecture and the distribution of conserved sites (Fig. 5D).

Analysis of the expression profiles of taars

To gain insights into the expression levels of taar genes, we analyzed the transcriptome profiles of taars in the olfactory epithelium and brain of S. chuatsi (The raw transcriptome data have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject ID: PRJNA1292722.). The analysis revealed that out of the 68 taars examined, 61 were found to be expressed in the olfactory epithelium of S. chuatsi, while only 19 were detected in the brain (Fig. 6A). Notably, among these, 5 taars (taar20a1, taar20b1, taar15g1, taar15f1, and taar15i1) exhibited TPM (Transcripts Per Million) values exceeding 10 in the olfactory epithelial cells, 46 taars showed TPM values ranging from 1 to 10, and 10 taars had TPM values between 0 and 1. Conversely, all 19 taars expressed in the brain had TPM values below 1. Further comparative analysis of taar20a1, taar20b1, taar15g1, taar15f1, and taar15i1 between olfactory epithelium and brain tissues highlighted significant expression differences (Fig. 6B). Additionally, qPCR validation corroborated these findings, demonstrating consistent expression patterns of taar20a1, taar20b1, taar15g1, taar15f1, and taar15i1 across olfactory epithelium and brain tissues. Notably, these 5 taars exhibited notably higher expression levels in the olfactory epithelial cells compared to the brain tissues (Fig. 6C).

Localization of TAARs in the cells

To validate the subcellular localization of TAAR proteins, we successfully cloned the full-length coding sequences of 5 taar genes (taar20a1, taar20b1, taar15g1, taar15f1 and taar15i1) and fused them with a CMV-GFP vector to generate TAAR-GFP fusion protein constructs. These plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells to observe the subcellular localization of the TAAR proteins [26, 27]. The results indicated that while GFP protein without the inserted gene was expressed in various cellular organelles of the 293 T cells, the TAAR-GFP fusion proteins were specifically localized to the cell membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus. This observation confirms that taar genes are expressed and functional in these specific cellular compartments (Fig. 7A). To prevent potential inaccuracies arising from differences between human and fish cell systems, we also transfected these genes into GCO cells and examined their subcellular localization. Similar to observations in 293 T cells, TAARs were localized in the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus (Fig. 7B).

Response of TAARs to cadaverine and putrescine

TAARs are known as receptors for amine odors. To investigate their response to two specific amines, putrescine and cadaverine, we studied the expression levels of taar20a1, taar20b1, taar15g1, taar15f1, and taar15i1, which showed the highest expression levels in the olfactory epithelium transcriptome. Our findings indicated significant responses among these taars in the olfactory epithelium: taar20a1, taar20b1, and taar15g1 responded significantly to cadaverine (p < 0.01), while taar15f1 and taar15i1 exhibited increased expression in response to putrescine (p < 0.01). Conversely, in the brain, taar15g1, taar15f1, and taar15i1 showed no significant response to either amine, whereas taar20b1 was significantly upregulated in response to both putrescine and cadaverine (p < 0.01). Notably, taar20a1 was inhibited in response to both putrescine and cadaverine (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8).

Discussion

The taar family originated in vertebrates through duplication events of ancient serotonin receptors (5-HT4) [17]. In teleost fishes, unlike the highly conserved vomeronasal 1 receptor family, the taar family has undergone significant expansion, likely linked to adaptations for sensing complex and diverse aquatic chemical environments [18, 28]. Here, we determined that S. chuatsi aversion to dead prey is mediated by necromones and identified a total of 68 protein-coding taar members, designated as taar1- taar68 based on their chromosomal locations. We systematically analyzed the evolutionary characteristics, conserved structures, expression patterns in the olfactory epithelium and brain, and subcellular localization of these 68 TAARs. Additionally, we assessed the responsiveness of several taars to cadaverine and putrescine.

Cadaverine and putrescine derived from decaying matter trigger innate aversion responses in vertebrates. As observed in our studies on S. chuatsi, this aversion manifests specifically as rejection of dead prey capture, while dead prey without such odors is still consumed. Notably, S. chuatsi even refuses to attack live bait emitting necromone cues, indicating that cadaverine and putrescine are critical factors inhibiting feeding on dead prey. Recent advances have identified the taar gene family as key chemosensory receptors for necromone detection in fish (Fig. 1). Therefore, TAARs represent a promising target for addressing the rejection of dead bait and formulated feeds in S. chuatsi, potentially through selective breeding for low amine sensitivity or gene editing of amine-detecting receptors. In S. chuatsi genome contains 68 taar members, 54 of which originated from 36 tandem duplication events, forming an expanded receptor repertoire that enhances molecular diversity and potentially enables dramatically expanded detection capabilities for amine odorants (Fig. 2). Notably, the large-scale amplification of the taar gene family is a relatively common phenomenon in the olfactory systems of fish species—similar expansion patterns are also observed in the olfactory receptor (OR) gene family, which belongs to the same olfactory receptor category [18, 29]. Previous studies have shown that the dynamic evolution of the OR gene family (including expansion and contraction) is closely linked to adaptive changes in species’ habitats and ecological behaviors [30]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the large-scale duplication of S. chuatsi’s taar genes may be evolutionarily related to its specialized predatory strategies. The taar family is divided into three subfamilies (Class I, II, and III) in vertebrates. We found that only Class I and III members are present in the genome of S. chuatsi, while the Class II subfamily is systematically absent (Fig. 3). This discovery is consistent with the evolutionary traits of the Neoteleostei (which includes Percopsiformes, Gadiformes, and Percomorpha) that Class II taar members are generally absent in the Neoteleostei (S. chuatsi belongs to Percomorpha) [16]. Notably, in the Class I subfamily, S. chuatsi retains the conserved TAAR10 receptor. This receptor, similar to its homologs in various teleosts, has molecular characteristics that allow it to recognize serotonin [11]. Considering that the taar family originated from the ancestral gene of the serotonin receptor htr4 subtype, we hypothesize that taar10 subfamily members may have preserved its ancestral ligand-binding characteristics through evolutionary conservation. We did not detect taar13 subfamily members in the S. chuatsi genome, which have been shown in D. rerio to specifically recognize polyamines and putrescine-related amines. This absence has also been observed in several other neoteleost species. Class II taars, including taar13 subfamily, are known to exhibit frequent gene loss as well as evolutionarily recent gene gains across teleosts, and the loss of taar13 subfamily has been associated with multiple independent gene loss events [17]. Nevertheless, we identified two putatively specialized taars in S. chuatsi: taar14 subfamily and taar16 subfamily. In D. rerio, taar14 subfamily shows putrescine-response characteristics similar to those of taar13d, while taar16 specifically responds to isoamylamine—both of which are characteristic odor molecules produced during protein degradation [21, 31]. Given the high sequence similarity between S. chuatsi taar14/16 subfamily and their zebrafish orthologs, we hypothesize that they may perform similar functions.

The extent of taar expansion varies significantly across different fish species, with notable differences in their numbers [18, 32]. For instance, D. labrax has 109 taars, which is much higher than that of S. chuatsi. Moreover, taar gene families of these two species do not exhibit clear synteny, suggesting that this gene family may have undergone divergent amplification and evolution after species differentiation. Despite the numerical differences, fish taars maintain highly conserved molecular features, such as a high degree of amino acid similarity and strong purifying selection pressures, along with six highly conserved motifs (Figs. 4 and 5). However, we also identified six specific codons (103, 104, 112, 238, 309, and 310) under significant positive selection. Notably, most of these sites are spatially clustered near the residues Asp3.32 and Asp5.42, which serve as key structural elements for amine recognition. This strongly suggests an adaptive evolutionary process that has fine-tuned the ligand-binding pocket. Positive selection at these specific positions may have altered the physicochemical properties of the binding site, potentially leading to changes in ligand specificity, binding affinity, or the ability to recognize amine compounds. This indicates that TAARs are conserved in their role in detecting amine-based odors. Asp3.32 and Asp5.42 are the key structural elements responsible for recognizing amine compounds. In S. chuatsi, six TAARs retain Asp3.32, 45 TAARs preserve Asp5.42, and 15 TAARs have both Asp3.32 and Asp5.42. The presence of different conserved sites leads to differences in receptor preferences. TAARs with only Asp3.32 or Asp5.42 mainly respond to monoamine substances (e.g., serotonin, isoamylamine), while receptors with both sites tend to recognize diamines or polyamines (e.g., putrescine, agmatine) [21]. This “coexistence of single- and double-site receptors” evolution strategy in S. chuatsi allows them to broadly perceive various amine chemical signals with a limited number of taar genes, providing a molecular basis for their complex ecological behaviors. Furthermore, heterologous expression of TAARs in two distinct cell lines yielded consistent results. The TAARs were correctly localized to the cell membrane, consistent with the characteristic of TAARs as G protein-coupled receptors (Fig. 6). Similar findings were also confirmed in lamprey [26], demonstrating that this heterologous expression approach holds great potential for future ligand deorphanization and mechanistic studies of TAARs.

Similar to olfactory receptors, the expression of taars follows the “one olfactory sensory neuron (OSN) chooses to express one taar” rule, but not every taar is expressed in the olfactory epithelium [33–35]. Transcriptome sequencing analysis revealed that 61 taars were effectively expressed in the olfactory epithelium of S. chuatsi, while 7 taars showed no detectable expression signals, suggesting that these receptors may play ectopic roles in other tissues. Notably, the brain, an organ rich in monoamine neurotransmitters (known or potential ligands of taars), displayed a significantly different expression profile of taars compared to the olfactory epithelium [36]. The number and expression levels of taars in the brain were much lower than in the olfactory epithelium, which may be related to the olfactory system’s need to detect complex amine-based signals for environmental adaptation (Fig. 7). By examining the expression dynamics under stimulation by putrescine/cadaverine, we found that five representative taars exhibited a biphasic expression pattern in response to ligand concentrations, which is similar to that reported in D. rerio [37]. At low concentrations, the expression of each taar increased with rising ligand levels, but once a certain threshold was surpassed, expression was suppressed. This shift in expression dynamics suggests that taars may facilitate the adjustment of olfactory responses across a range of ligand concentrations, though the exact mechanism—whether it involves adaptation, feedback inhibition, or another regulatory process—requires further investigation (Fig. 8). Interestingly, the response patterns of taars in brain tissue were significantly different from those in the olfactory epithelium when exposed to the same stimuli: three taars did not show any response, only taar20b1 exhibited an upregulation trend, and another, taar20a1, demonstrated an inhibitory effect. This tissue-specific response difference suggests that the same taar may have ligand recognition preferences depending on the expression microenvironment.

Conclusion

This study presents the first systematic identification and functional characterization of the taar gene family in S. chuatsi. Through comprehensive analyses including chromosomal localization, genomic collinearity, and evolutionary reconstruction, we revealed a remarkable expansion of the taar family in this species, which may be an adaptation to its unique feeding habits. Conserved domain architecture analysis combined with selection pressure assessment confirmed evolutionary conservation across taar members, suggesting strong functional constraints underlying their critical biological roles. Transcriptomic expression profiling unveiled tissue-specific patterns, with olfactory epithelium exhibiting significantly higher taar expression diversity and abundance than cerebral tissues. Functional investigations through cadaverine/putrescine stimulation experiments further demonstrated organ-specific response patterns among five representative taars: Olfactory taars displayed biphasic regulation (activation at low concentrations followed by suppression at higher doses), while brain tissue taars primarily maintained expression homeostasis. Our findings provide a foundational molecular framework for TAAR-mediated amine sensing in S. chuatsi, suggesting a potential mechanism for dead-prey rejection. However, the in vivo functional importance of these specific TAARs awaits further validation through gene knockout or behavioral assays. This work paves the way for developing testable hypotheses for scientifically informed feeding modulation strategies.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of taar genes

We collected 499 taar (trace amine-associated receptor) gene sequences and 12 htr4 sequences from M. musculus, R. norvegicus, G. gallus, X. tropicalis, D. rerio, O. latipes, T. rubripes, S. salar, G. aculeatus, L. camtschaticum, D. labrax and S. chuatsi [16, 17, 38–41]. Using these protein sequences as query sequences, we conducted TBLASTN searches in S. chuatsi genome (PRJNA513951) using TBtools II (v1.120) software, with an e-value cutoff of 1 × 10−5 [4, 42]. Intron size prediction was performed using default parameters. All candidate taar genes were further screened and validated using the SMART (http://smart.embl.de), HMMER and Pfam [43].

Sequence and phylogenetic analysis of taar genes

The conserved motif of S. chuatsi taar proteins was identified utilizing MEME (https://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) [44]. The parameters for the MEME analysis were as follows: a p-value threshold of < 0.05 was used for significance, with a maximum of 50 sites tested. Prediction of transmembrane domains was conducted employing TMHMM Server v.2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?TMHMM-2.0) [45]. Potential glycosylation sites were predicted using NetNGlyc − 1.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc-1.0/). The pattern diagram of taar proteins was visualized utilizing AI software. AlphaFold 3 was used to 3D model the proteins of TAAR [46].

The amino acid sequences of the identified S. chuatsi taar were aligned with partial taar sequences from various representative vertebrates. We employed the Nei-Gojobori method in MEGA-X to calculate pairwise Ka/Ks ratios of taar genes, assessing overall selection pressure on the taar family [47]. To generate the alignment, we used MAFFT v7. For algorithm selection, the auto option was applied, allowing MAFFT to automatically determine the most suitable alignment strategy based on sequence data size and heterogeneity. Subsequently, we used the branch-site unrestricted statistical test (BUSTED) on the Datamonkey platform to detect positive selection signals in the S. chuatsi taar gene family. First, taar coding sequences from S. chuatsi and D. rerio were aligned using MAFFT v7. The alignment was then used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogeny with IQ-TREE v2 under the optimal substitution model (GTR + F + I + G4) selected by ModelFinder, with branch support assessed via 1,000 ultra-fast bootstrap replicates. Nodes with bootstrap support >70% were designated as background branches, while clades derived from tandem duplications were defined as test branches. Positive selection sites on test branches were analyzed under BUSTED’s unconstrained model [48]. We further evaluated the posterior probability (Epost[α]) for each site belonging to the positive selection class (ω >1). To ensure the reliability of identification, stringent criteria were adopted: a codon site was considered to exhibit statistically significant positive selection only if it simultaneously satisfied a significant likelihood ratio test (p < 0.05) and a posterior probability greater than 0.95. Simultaneously, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA-X, with the Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT) model selected, and 1000 bootstrap replicates were performed (The amino acid sequences in the evolutionary tree are listed in Supplementary File 1). The Multiple Collinear Scanning Toolkit (MCScanX) with default parameters were employed to analyze collinear patterns among homologous gene blocks in both S. chuatsi and D. labrax genomes (PRJEB43042) [49, 50]. The syntenic relationships between orthologous gene pairs from these genomes were then determined using the dual synteny graph in TBtools II [42]. The visualization of these results was achieved through the utilization of the Advanced Circle program.

Behavioral observation

One-year-old S. chuatsi were divided into four groups (6 fish per group) and fed live prey (D. rerio), live prey with necromones (Live D. rerio immersed in a 1:1 mixture of cadaverine (1,5-Diaminopentane; Macklin, Shanghai, China, D766684) and putrescine (1,4-Diaminobutane; Macklin, Shanghai, China, D807415) to simulate necromonals from decomposing prey), dead prey with necromones (D. rerio carcasses, postmortem interval of 24 h) and dead prey without necromones (Fresh cadaver of anesthesia-induced death), respectively. Ambient and water temperatures were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C, respectively. S. chuatsi were acclimated in a 40 L water tank for 1 h before the test. Six prey items corresponding to each group were introduced, and predation behavior was recorded for 15 min. Feeding responses (swim and ingestions) were quantified from video recordings using EthoVision XT 14 software (Noldus Information Technology, Beijing, China).

Sample collections

S. chuatsi utilized in this research were bred at the Chinese Perch Research Center, Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan, China) (Fig. 9A). This study was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan, Hubei, China) (approval no.: HZAUFI-2025-0037). Prior to commencing the experiment, one-year-old S. chuatsi underwent a temporary housing period of 12 h for acclimation. Prior to sampling, all S. chuatsi were anesthetized using MS222 (100 mg/L) (Tricaine methanesulfonate, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). After dissection and sample collection (Fig. 9B), the samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA-seq analysis and a portion of the samples was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin-eosin (H.E.) staining (Fig. 9C and D).

Total RNA extraction, cDNA library construction and RNA-seq

Total RNA from the olfactory epithelium and brain tissue (three biological replicates each) was extracted using the RNAiso Plus Kit (Takara Bio, Beijing, China, code: 9109) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following assessment of RNA quality via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, the total RNA was further evaluated and quantified using the 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technology, CA, USA). Samples meeting the quality criteria from both groups were employed for the preparation of RNA-Seq libraries.

A total of 6 samples meeting the quality standards (including olfactory epithelium and brain tissues with three samples each) were utilized for the preparation of RNA-Seq libraries using the Illumina® TruSeq™ RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego). mRNA was enriched and purified from the total RNA using Oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation into 150 bp fragments. The first-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers, followed by synthesis of the second-strand cDNA. Subsequently, the cDNA libraries were obtained through PCR amplification to enrich the fragments. Sequencing was then performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, PE150).

Data processing, mapping and expression analysis of taars

After conducting quality control checks on the raw sequencing reads to ensure data integrity, we proceeded to align them with the reference genome of S. chuatsi (PRJNA513951) using the HISAT2 tool [4, 51, 52]. Next, employing StringTie software, we generated transcriptome profiles and quantified the gene expression levels (TPM) for each sample using RSEM software [53, 54]. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software, which is based on the negative binomial distribution [55]. Genes exhibiting a fold change greater than 2 and a p-adjust value below 0.05 were identified as differentially expressed genes.

Cell culture, plasmid construction and transfection

The 293 T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, NY, USA, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099–141 C), 100 units/mL penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich, P4333), and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P4333). Grass carp ovarian (GCO) cells were cultured in M199 medium (Gibco, 11150059) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099–141 C), 100 units/mL penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich, P4333), and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich,S1277). Both cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, with 293 T cells at 37 °C and GCO cells at 28 °C.

The original plasmid for constructing expression vectors was pCMV-eGFP. For taar localization studies, the full-length open reading frames of taar20a1, taar20b1, taar15g1, taar15f1, and taar15i1 were amplified from cDNA extracted from S. chuatsi olfactory epithelium using corresponding primers (Supplementary Table S6). Subsequently, the full-length taar constructs were fused to pCMV-eGFP via homologous recombination by ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C112-01, Nanjing, China), resulting in the generation of pCMV-taar20a1/24/45/47/54-eGFP fusion vectors. Based on a study concerning P. marinus taars, we constructed an expression plasmid for mouse receptor transporter protein 1 (rtp1) [26, 27].

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, all vectors were transfected into 293 T cells using FuGENE®6 transfection reagent (Promega, E2691, WI, USA), using GFP as a control. The culture media of the cells were replaced with Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium (OPTI, Gibco, 31985070). OPTI and FuGENE®6 were mixed in a ratio of 84 µL: 6 µL and incubated for 5 min, followed by the addition of 2000 ng plasmid (taar and rtp1 were mixed at a 1:1 ratio) and further incubation for 15–30 min with thorough mixing. Subsequently, all reagents were added to the OPTI medium cultured cells for transfection. After 24 h of transfection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, MBD0015) for nuclear staining. Subsequently, observation was conducted under a confocal fluorescence microscopy.

Stimulation of S. chuatsi with different odors

One-year-old S. chuatsi with a body length of 22 ± 1 cm served as experimental subjects. Individual fish were adapted to 40 L recirculating aquaculture system water for 12 h. Stimulation trials, designed with reference to dosages reported in relevant literature [37], comprised five groups (n = 6 per group): a Control group marked as “Control” exposed to aquaculture water and four stimulation groups exposed to final concentrations of 10 µM cadaverine, 100 µM cadaverine, 10 µM putrescine, and 100 µM putrescine. Stimuli were administered during the experiment by slowly adding reagents via a soft tube, with the negative control group receiving an equal volume of aquaculture water identical to the treatment groups. During stimulation, fish were maintained in a quiet, undisturbed environment to preclude external influences. Forty-five minutes post-stimulation, fish were deeply anesthetized using MS222 (100 mg/L), after which olfactory epithelial tissue and brain tissue samples were rapidly dissected and collected using sterilized scissors and forceps. The collected samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C for long-term storage pending RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis.

qRT–PCR

Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, code No. RR047A). Gene expression analysis for each sample was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) reagent kit (TaKaRa, RR820A). Primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S7, and the raw Ct values from the qRT-PCR analysis conducted in this study are provided in Supplementary Table S8. The relative expression levels of different samples were compared using the 2-ΔΔCt method, and data visualization was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 Significance levels were set at p < 0.05 (indicating significance) and p < 0.01 (indicating high significance) to determine the statistical significance.
