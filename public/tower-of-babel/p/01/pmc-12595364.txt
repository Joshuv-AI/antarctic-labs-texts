A Rare Case of Anti-glutamic Acid Decarboxylase 65 Autoimmune Encephalitis Followed by Acute Graft-Versus-Host Disease in Simultaneous Kidney and Pancreas Transplant
Authors: Leigh-Anne Dale, Alexander Prete, Alexis Puyleart, Xia Qian, Omar Siddiqi, Audrey Le-Mahajan, Amtul Aala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimultaneous kidney and pancreas transplant is a treatment option for diabetic patients with kidney failure. Posttransplant complications are common and include surgical complications, and infections, as well as immunosuppression toxicity. Some complications like autoimmune encephalitis and graft-versus-host disease have been reported after hematopoietic stem cell transplant but very few reports are published in solid organ transplants. We present a case of a 32-year-old woman who underwent simultaneous kidney and pancreas transplants and presented 2 weeks after transplant with subacute altered mental status as well as persistent fevers. Extensive infectious, rheumatologic, hematologic, and neurologic workups revealed multiple hyperintense brain lesions with the presence of glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 autoantibodies in the serum and cerebrospinal fluid. She initially improved with steroids and immunoglobulin, but symptoms quickly returned. Subsequently, she developed rapid pancytopenia along with transaminitis. The repeat bone marrow biopsy was consistent with aplastic anemia, and the liver biopsy showed subtle biliary ductal changes consistent with graft-versus-host disease. She was evaluated for a bone marrow transplant but because of persistent infections was deemed not to be a candidate. She died 6 months posttransplant.Index Words: Graft-versus-host disease, autoimmune encephalitis, simultaneous pancreas and kidney transplantation, GAD65 autoantibodies, hyperintense brain lesions

Autoimmune encephalitis and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) are well described complications of hematopoietic stem cell transplant. These are rare and fatal in solid organ transplant (SOT), with only a handful of cases separately reported for both these conditions in the SOT literature.1, 2, 3 Glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) is an essential enzyme in the conversion of glutamate to gamma-aminobutyric acid, a key inhibitory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system.4 The presence of autoantibodies against the glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65) isoform has been implicated with autoimmune encephalitis. On the other hand, GVHD can be triggered by an inflammatory microenvironment, leading to donor T cells attacking various target tissues, classically bone marrow, liver, intestine, and skin. There are a few case reports of kidney, pancreas, heart, lung, and multivisceral transplants.1, 2, 3 Here we present a case of subacute anti-GAD65 autoimmune encephalitis followed by GVHD in a patient with simultaneous kidney and pancreas transplants.

Case Report

The patient is a 32-year-old woman with a history of type 1 diabetes mellitus and end-stage renal disease. She underwent a simultaneous kidney–pancreas transplant from a deceased donor with excellent graft function. For immunosuppressive induction, she received antithymocyte globulin and steroid taper per protocol. She was cytomegalovirus (CMV) high risk, Ebstein-Barr Virus not high risk. She was discharged on a standard immunosuppression regimen of tacrolimus and mycophenolic acid.

She presented 17 days after transplant with nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, periods of confusion, and fever. Both the pancreas and kidney were working well; complete blood count was normal besides leukocytosis to 17 K/uL. The initial workup was largely unremarkable except a urine culture growing > 100,000 CFU/mL of pan-sensitive Escherichia coli. She completed a 10-day course of antibiotics (ceftriaxone transitioned to levofloxacin). Infectious disease consultants expressed concern for superinfected ureteral stent. Despite stent removal, she had fevers, reaching a maximum of 103 °F. Extensive infectious testing for fungal, viral, atypical bacterial, and parasite infections was explored, which was unremarkable except a quantitative CMV viral load resulted at low positive (less than 1.5 log) and was not thought to be clinically significant.

Her mental status continued to deteriorate, displaying signs of somnolence, dysmetria, and headaches. A lumbar puncture was obtained, with initial cell count and cultures inconsistent with infectious meningoencephalitis. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies were sent for viral encephalitides (herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, Epstein-Barr virus, human polyomavirus 2, arboviruses, and enterovirus) and toxoplasma, which were negative. Antibiotics were empirically broadened to vancomycin, ampicillin, and acyclovir. Because of a positive lyme Ab screen confirmed by Western blot, doxycycline was started, transitioned to ceftriaxone for possible neuroborreliosis but the CSF lyme studies ultimately resulted negative.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of her brain was obtained (Fig 1), which revealed multiple T2 hyperintense lesions involving the corpus callosum, periventricular white matter, right middle cerebellar peduncle, and right cerebellum. A multidisciplinary review of her imaging offered a broad differential, but ultimately a brain biopsy was deferred because it was too high risk. As part of the hematologic workup, she underwent a bone marrow biopsy, which was unrevealing. Additionally, because of concern for calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, she was transitioned to belatacept without improvement in her mental status. Ultimately, her CSF autoimmune encephalitis panel returned with elevated GAD65 autoantibody (0.11 nmol/L, reference < 0.02) alongside an elevated serum titer (95.4 nmol/L, reference < 0.02). Given concern for GAD65 autoimmune encephalitis, she received a 5-day course of 1 gm intravenous (IV) methylprednisolone followed by 5 days of intravenous immunoglobulin with improvement in her fever curve and overall mental status. A repeat MRI brain on hospital day 45 revealed interval improvement (Fig 2).

She was discharged to rehabilitation after 2 months of hospitalization. She was readmitted to the hospital 10 days later with a return of fevers. Pancreas and kidney function were normal; complete blood count was normal besides mild anemia with hemoglobin 8.2 g/dL. She was found to have an Enterobacter cloacae urinary tract infection that was treated with a 10-day course of cefepime. Despite appropriate antibiotic therapy she continued to have fevers, prompting repeat MRI and lumbar puncture. The MRI showed significant improvement in T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery hyperintense lesions in the right cerebellar peduncle and bilateral periventricular white matter without any new lesions. Lumbar puncture studies were negative. She was started on a 5-day course of intravenous immunoglobulin because of concern for continued encephalitis. Fevers persisted with new liver function test abnormalities. She developed diarrhea, and flexible sigmoidoscopy was performed with biopsy, which ruled out CMV. Imaging was notable for numerous small lymph nodes with fatty stranding in the left supraclavicular region. She underwent excisional lymph node biopsy that was nondiagnostic. Cultures from the lymph node biopsy were positive for Enterococcus faecium and Staphylococcus epidermidis for which she was treated with meropenem and daptomycin.

She developed significant pancytopenia and underwent a second bone marrow biopsy that was consistent with marrow aplasia; methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg twice a day was started. A positron emission tomography scan showed nonspecific marrow flourodeoxyglucose in the femoral metadiaphyseal, sternum, bilateral clavicular heads, and proximal left tibia. A liver biopsy was done (Fig 3) showing subtle biliary ductal changes consistent with GVHD. Blood chimerism analysis showed 18.1% donor cells supporting GVHD. Treatment with steroids, ruxolitinib, and tacrolimus was initiated but ultimately, she was deemed not to be a candidate for bone marrow transplantation and transitioned to hospice (Fig 4).

Discussion

Here we demonstrate a case of a young female who developed subacute altered mental status as well as persistent fever approximately two weeks following simultaneous pancreas-kidney transplant. Broad infectious, rheumatologic, hematologic, and neurologic workup revealed multiple hyperintense brain lesions. Serologies were notable for the presence of GAD65 autoantibodies in both peripheral serum and CSF with a working diagnosis of anti-GAD65 autoimmune encephalitis and a later presentation of severe pancytopenia and liver function abnormalities with diagnosis of GVHD.

Autoimmune encephalitis has recently begun to be recognized in scientific literature, constituting a range of closely related disease entities unified by the presence of immune-mediated inflammation of brain parenchyma.5 The presence of autoantibodies against the GAD65 isoform has been implicated in several autoimmune diseases, including type 1 diabetes mellitus, pernicious anemia, and autoimmune thyroid disease.6,7 Neurologic sequelae of autoimmune encephalitis include epilepsy, psychiatric symptoms such as psychosis, changes in cognition, and movement disorders.8 If untreated, autoimmune encephalitis can lead to serious complications, including cognitive impairment, autonomic dysfunction, coma, and seizures.9 Though rare, there have been cases of autoimmune encephalitis posttransplantation.10 Interestingly, there are no preexisting cases reported of anti-GAD65 antibody autoimmune encephalitis occurring in patients who have undergone simultaneous pancreas and kidney transplants.

The pathogenesis of autoimmune encephalitis following organ transplantation is poorly understood. One theory is that administration of immunosuppressants leads to dysregulation of the immune system.11 Patients with immunosuppression also have a higher risk of developing or reactivating infections such as human herpesvirus-6, which subsequently predispose to autoimmune disorders by mechanisms such as molecular mimicry. Another possible etiology is cytokine release syndrome (CRS), thought to be secondary to systemic inflammation with associated increased release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor α, and interferon-γ early posttransplant. The CRS has been strongly linked with both autoimmune neurologic diseases and GVHD. Although it is difficult to make conclusions, whether the two events are related, in this patient’s case, symptoms started rapidly after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. Given her history of type 1 diabetes, she likely had anti-GAD65 antibodies that lead to encephalitis in an immunosuppressed state. On the contrary, her brain lesions could also represent a nonclassical manifestation of GVHD, but this theory seems less plausible as she had a negative first bone marrow biopsy, plus she did not fit the diagnosis of central nervous system-GVHD per the 2010 consensus conference criteria.

Inflammatory cytokines also play a pathogenic role in GVHD, which is an underrecognized and fatal complication of SOT. Main triggers for GVHD include human leukocyte antigen mismatch, immunosuppressive therapy, and specific posttransplant factors, including infections and tissue ischemia. Classic clinical manifestations of GVHD include fever and skin rash with progression to a multisystemic disease affecting the bone marrow. Diagnosis is based on clinical manifestations, histopathologic findings on biopsy, and evidence of systemic lymphoid chimerism. Although GVHD traditionally presents acutely after transplantation, late GVHD has been described by Singh et al12 occurring months to years posttransplant and resembles an autoimmune disease with inflammation affecting multiple organs.

The pathogenesis of GVHD after SOTs remains unclear. It has been speculated that there are different phases. Phase 1 consists of tissue injury, which releases proinflammatory cytokines. Phase 2 occurs when donor T cells are activated and migrate to the recipient. Phase 3 occurs with increase in natural killer cells and anti-host cytotoxic T-lymphocyte activity, which leads to induced apoptosis in target cells.

Few cases of autoimmune encephalitis and GVHD occurring simultaneously have been identified in hematopoietic stem cell transplant and CRS was thought to be strongly linked.13 This has not been reported in SOT; hence, ours will be the first case report to describe these two rare conditions in the same patient. Ultimately, our patient had persistent fevers with central nervous system findings. Although these brain lesions could represent nonclassical GVHD, they most likely represent autoimmune encephalitis from GAD65 autoantibodies followed by development of GVHD.
