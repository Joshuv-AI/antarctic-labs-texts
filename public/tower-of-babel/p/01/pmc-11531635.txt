Mechanical Failure of Modular Rotating Hinge Femoral Component
Authors: Justin Leal, Rahul K. Goel, Michael P. Bolognesi, Samuel S. Wellman, Thorsten M. Seyler, Sean P. Ryan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hinge constructs can be used in revision total knee arthroplasty in cases of major collateral ligament deficiency, global knee instability, repeated failure of constrained implants, or large bone defects. Although the success of this construct in revision total knee arthroplasty varies depending on the type of surgery and bone quality, hinged implants can solve a wide array of complex problems while preserving stable flexion-extension motion for ambulation. The most common reasons for re-revision include patellofemoral complications, aseptic loosening, and infection. Most cases of mechanical failure for these hinged constructs occur at the hinge component. Here, we report the first instance of catastrophic femoral component failure in a modular rotating hinge via a low-energy mechanism.

Introduction

Although modern implant designs for primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) have excellent success regarding outcomes and survivorship, the demand for revision TKA (rTKA) is expected to continue increasing [1]. Each rTKA is unique depending on the indication for surgery, the implant being revised, patient bone quality, and ligamentous stability [2]. Common indications for utilization of a rotating or fixed hinge implant in rTKA are repeated failure of constrained implants, collateral ligament and/or global knee instability, extensor mechanism insufficiency, and significant bone loss [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. The benefit of hinge implants includes coronal stability and preservation of flexion-extension motion for walking [7,8,10]. Initial iterations of the hinge prosthesis exhibited a high incidence of early loosening and required long cemented stems to accommodate the significant stress exerted on the bone-implant interface [8,10]. However, contemporary designs have incorporated a rotating platform, which confers more physiologic knee motion and decreases stress transmission through the bone-implant interface [8,10].

Although these implants are an improvement from previous designs, survivorship and complication rates vary widely depending on surgery type and patient-related factors [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Overall, the most common complications include infection and aseptic loosening [8,10]. There have been several reported complications involving disengagement of the hinge mechanism; however, there have been no reported mechanical failures of the femoral component to our knowledge [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Here, we report the case of a patient with catastrophic femoral component failure in a modular rotating hinge via a low-energy mechanism.

Case history

A 73-year-old Jehovah’s Witness female patient with a history of hypertension, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and a previous cerebral vascular accident (without residual effects) presented with pain and instability in her right knee. These symptoms appeared 11 years after her initial TKA. Her primary concerns were significant pain and instability. At this visit, the patient was found to have a range of motion of 0-90 degrees with gross instability and no endpoint on varus stress. Anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs were suggestive of lateral tibial subluxation but no obvious evidence of component loosening, polyethylene insert failure, malpositioning, or fracture (Fig. 1). Intraoperatively, it was noted that her lateral condyle lacked soft tissue attachments and her medial collateral ligament also showed significant laxity; thus, she was revised to a modular rotating hinge (MRH) (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI). At the time of revision to a MRH, minimal bone loss was noted with removal of the femoral and tibial components. However, poor bone quality resulted in needing a tibial cone for additional stability and adequate fixation. Femoral bone loss was less than 5 mm, thus cement was sufficient without the need for augments. Over the course of her first year of follow-up, she made improvements in pain and function returning to her baseline. However, at her 1-year postoperative visit, she presented with new-onset pain and instability after a caregiver fell onto her flexed knee. After this incident, she continued to have pain that localized anteriorly and caused her increased difficulty with walking. AP and lateral radiograph of the knee did not demonstrate any obvious complications; femoral and tibial components appeared well-fixed with no progression of radiolucency (Fig. 3). Consequently, she was instructed to use a cane as needed and to wrap her leg with an ace wrap for gentle compression. She initially improved after this visit until she fell, resulting in significant worsening of her condition. She returned to clinic in a wheelchair, as she now had trouble ambulating. On examination, her straight leg raise was intact with a range of motion of 0-115 degrees. Updated radiographs were unchanged. However, given her worsening pain, infectious workup and computed tomography scan were done. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (12 mm/hr) was within normal limits, C-reactive protein (1.56 mg/dL) was elevated, and aspiration was benign (cell count: 859 cells/μL; neutrophils: 48%; culture: no growth).Figure 3(a) Anteroposterior (AP) and (b) lateral radiographic view of right knee after onset of pain and instability 1-year postoperatively.

At her subsequent visit, her knee was found to have gross multidirectional instability. She did not report any additional specific events leading to this new instability. Repeat AP and lateral radiographs as well as stress radiographs showed catastrophic failure of the MRH femoral component (Fig. 4) with motion through the lateral femoral condyle. Considering that the tibial component appeared to be well fixed, we planned for expeditious revision of her femoral component only.Figure 4(a) Anteroposterior (AP) and (b) lateral radiograph of right knee as well as (c) valgus and (d) varus stress view showing catastrophic femoral component failure.

Intraoperatively, upon entering the knee joint, the femoral component was found to be completely fragmented across the distal lateral condyle (Fig. 5). There was considerable wear on the extension block poly; however, there was no evidence of damage at the hinge mechanism. The femoral component was easily removed with only mild bone loss on the lateral distal femur likely associated with the component failure (Fig. 6). She was reconstructed with a new MRH femoral component. Postoperatively, the patient was doing well, and her AP and lateral radiographs showed appropriately aligned and well-fixed components with no sign of early complications (Fig. 7). Discussion

Generally, hinge constructs are a valuable option for patients with significant instability and bone loss, especially on repeat revisions [8,10]. While MRH constructs have been an improvement on pure hinge constructs regarding aseptic loosening and periprosthetic fracture, aseptic loosening remains one of the most common mechanisms of failure [3,4,6,7,10]. Additionally, other mechanisms of failure have been reported in the literature such as failure of the bushing [11], fracture of tibial post [12], fracture of the tibial yoke [13], disengagement of the antidislocation mechanism [16], and tibiofemoral disengagement of the hinge-post extension [12,14]. However, there are no reports in the literature of lateral condyle fracture of an MRH construct.

To our knowledge, this was the first report of catastrophic lateral femoral condyle failure in an MRH. In this case, there were several instances of trauma to the knee. The initial trauma was poorly described by the patient; however, there appeared to be significant force applied to the knee while in deep flexion. This resulted in pain and instability, but there was no radiographic evidence of problems with the components at this time. The patient subsequently had another fall, likely due to the instability and progressively worsening function of her knee. Still, at this point there was no radiographic evidence of femoral component failure. It was not until her next follow-up that there was global instability on examination and radiographic evidence of femoral component failure.

The true cause for failure at this junction of the prosthesis is ultimately unknown. Failure secondary to fatigue of cobalt-chromium femoral component was possible given the combination of the initial trauma suffered by the patient and insufficient deep lateral condyle support producing a stress riser. Most fatigue fractures reported in the literature occurred 4-10 years after implantation and were often related to material properties, component design, high loading conditions, and/or poor fixation [17,18]. It is possible that additional cement, metal augments, rebar, and/or a femoral cone could have improved deep support for the lateral femoral condyle and decreased metal fatigue; however, considering the trauma was low energy and the time to failure was less than 2 years, this would be an unusual presentation of this mechanism, thus there was likely an additional contributing factor.

For example, the bumper could have been mal-seated, allowing for increased forces across the axle and lateral condyle; however, at the time of revision surgery, this did not appear to be the case, and the entirety of the hinge mechanism traveled with the lateral aspect of the implant where the fracture occurred. This device is predicated on previous MRH designs such as the Modular Rotating Hinge Knee (Howmedica Osteonics; Allendale, NJ), which was predicated from the Kinematic Rotating Hinge Total Knee Pr. (Howmedica Osteonics; Walker, MI) before that [[19], [20], [21]]. Although for approval by the Food and Drug Administration, this MRH passed magnetically induced displacement force tests and magnetically induced torque tests [21], there may have been an issue with casting of the component at the time of its manufacturing leading to an area of weakness within the lateral aspect of the implant, leaving it susceptible to fracture. Ultimately, the cause of this catastrophic failure of the lateral femoral condyle was likely multifactorial.

Summary

Re-revision of an MRH is most commonly due to patellofemoral complications, aseptic loosening, and infection. Most cases of mechanical failure for these hinged constructs occur at the hinge component. This is the first report describing a femoral component failure in a MRH via a low-energy mechanism.
