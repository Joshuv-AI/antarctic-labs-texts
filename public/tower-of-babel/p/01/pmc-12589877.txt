Nerve ultrasound abnormality in RFC1 positive isolated sensory neuropathy is milder than in the full RFC1 canvas phenotype
Authors: L Pelosi, A Garvey, J Kao, R Roxburgh
Published: 2025

Dear Editor

Patients with inherited sensory neuronopathy have a unique ultrasound feature of abnormally ‘small’ cross sectional areas (CSAs) in mixed nerves of the upper limbs. This distinctive ultrasound pattern, first discovered in cerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) (Pelosi et al, 2018) and subsequently confirmed in other inherited sensory neuronopathies (Pelosi et al., 2019, Pelosi et al., 2022, Watanabe et al., 2022), has high sensitivity and specificity, and a high predictive diagnostic value for inherited sensory neuronopathy. It is detectable in individual patients and has been proposed as a marker for inherited sensory neuronopathy (Pelosi and van Alfen, 2023).

Biallelic pentanucleotide expansions in intron 2 of the RFC1 gene were first discovered in ataxic patients with fullblown CANVAS, but subsequently found to be associated with a spectrum of clinical disorders, ranging from a late-onset pure sensory neuropathy to the full CANVAS phenotype (Cortese et al, 2020).

It is unclear if the ultrasound abnormality typical of patients with CANVAS is to be found throughout the RFC1 spectrum disorder. It is also unclear whether this is a developmental feature that is present in all people with the RFC1 expansion from birth, or whether it develops and progresses in the course of the disease.

In this exploratory study, we compared the nerve ultrasound findings of five patients with clinically isolated sensory neuropathy who were positive for RFC1 expansion, with those of 17 patients with the fullblown CANVAS phenotype and with those of our historical 38 healthy controls.

In all participants, the ultrasound nerve cross-sectional areas (CSAs) were measured, on the dominant arm, on the median and ulnar nerves at mid-forearm and mid-upper arm. The mean upper limb nerve CSA was also obtained by averaging the values at the four measurement sites. After testing for normality of distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test of normality), one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey's HSD (honestly significant difference) was used for group comparisons. The individual nerve CSAs of the patients at each measurement site were also compared with our control reference values, to detect ultrasound abnormalities in each patient (values were considered abnormal if >2 SDs from the reference mean value) and to compare them with abnormalities on nerve conduction study (NCS).

As a group, the CSAs of the patients with RFC1 expansion were significantly reduced at all measurement sites in comparison with the healthy controls. However, the CSAs of the five patients with isolated sensory neuropathy were significantly larger at three out of four sites (Table 1) than those of the 17 patients with the full CANVAS spectrum.

On individual analyses, the ultrasound pattern of neuronopathy was found in four of five patients with isolated sensory neuropathy and in 16 of 17 patients with CANVAS. The one patient with normal nerves in the neuropathy group had nerve size at the lower end of the normal range but also had glucose intolerance / diabetes which may have explained why their nerves were a little larger.

NCS showed a non-length dependent sensory axonal neuropathy (NLDSN) consistent with sensory neuronopathy in four of five patients with isolated sensory neuropathy and, a length dependent sensory neuropathy (LDSN) in the remaining patient. Interestingly, the patient with normal nerve CSAs had a NLDSN on NCS and, the patient with a LDSN on NCS had abnormally small nerve CSAs.

The nerve CSAs of the patients with isolated neuropathy, were intermediate between the controls’ and those of patients with the full CANVAS spectrum. This suggests that the sensory neuronopathy of the RFC1 positive patients with isolated sensory neuropathy, who are at the mild end of the RFC1 spectrum disorder, is less severe. This could reflect an early stage of the same disease which would then progress to a more severe axonal loss with time or, a different milder phenotype. A longitudinal study on a larger cohort of patients may answer this question.

These findings provide further evidence for a role of ultrasound in the diagnostic workup of late-onset sensory chronic idiopathic axonal polyneuropathy (CIAP) as a sensitive screening tool for RFC1 testing. It should be noted that reduced upper limb nerve CSAs, while typical of pure inherited sensory neuronopathy, are not unique to RFC1 expansion. However, in the clinical context of a late-onset sensory CIAP, RFC1 expansion is the most likely genetic association.

The nerve ultrasound protocol used for this purpose is fast and easy to conduct, requiring only the unilateral scanning of two upper limb nerves at two non-compressive sites. In this study, diagnostic accuracy for RFC1 positivity increased to 100 % when both nerve ultrasound and NCS were used, suggesting that the two methods can complement each other when inherited sensory neuronopathy is clinically suspected but either test is negative. This should be further defined in studies on larger patient populations.
