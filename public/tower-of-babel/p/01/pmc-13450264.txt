Multi-stakeholder perspectives of palliative care needs in advanced COPD: a qualitative study
Authors: Yuying Dong, Mengzhen Qin, Xi Guo, Mengyuan Qiao, Haiyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(COPD) endure severe physical and psychological suffering, face heavier socioeconomic burdens, and experience long-term unmet needs for palliative care. This study aims to explore the multidimensional needs of patients at an advanced stage of disease, with the goal of developing customised palliative care services tailored to their specific requirements.MethodsBetween October 2024 and April 2025, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 38 participants recruited from a tertiary general hospital in Urumqi, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China. The participants included patients with advanced COPD, their corresponding family caregivers, and healthcare professionals. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach.ResultsThe analysis revealed that the palliative care needs of patients with advanced COPD arise from a dynamic framework comprising two interrelated dimensions, rather than a simple checklist. The first dimension encompasses two core needs that run throughout a patient’s entire life journey: the challenges in maintaining identity and the empowerment and support system requirements. The second dimension is the time-based disease progression pillar, representing ‘Needs across the disease trajectory’, which encompasses needs during stable periods, acute exacerbations and the end-of-life stage.ConclusionThe palliative care needs of patients with advanced COPD must be addressed, as they directly impact quality of life and dignity during the final stages of life. Efforts should focus on developing systematic interventions that are culturally appropriate and policy-driven.

Background

According to current guidelines, palliative care for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) integrates palliative treatment, supportive care, and advance care planning (ACP) [1]. Its core lies in implementing personalised interventions based on a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s condition and individual needs. It is also defined as “supportive care,” aiming to address patients’ physical and psychological needs, including life-prolonging treatments when necessary. Therefore, integrating supportive care into long-term management and accurately assessing needs is crucial [2, 3]. Patients with advanced COPD face complex health issues, commonly experiencing a high burden of symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, pain, and anxiety. The severity of these symptoms can be comparable to lung cancer, and patients often have multiple comorbidities, leading to frequent acute exacerbations and increased mortality risk [4–7]. Studies indicate that patients in this stage often enter a “liminal state,” marked by profound loss and existential distress [8, 9]. Consequently, integrating comprehensive palliative care into management is essential to alleviate suffering and provide care aligned with patient values.

However, despite the diverse palliative care needs of patients with COPD, the actual provision of such services remains significantly inadequate. Across the entire UK cohort, only 7.8% of patients had received palliative care; the uptake rate among patients with advanced COPD was also only 21.4% [10]. Although their symptom burden is comparable to that of patients with lung cancer, the proportion receiving specialist palliative care is less than a third of that seen in the latter group [10]. Although there are currently no nationwide survey data in China reporting on the uptake of palliative care among patients with COPD, existing studies indicate that the need for palliative care among patients with severe COPD is moderate, suggesting a heavy symptom burden and an urgent need for intervention [11]. In resource-limited settings, achieving a shared understanding of needs among patients, families, and healthcare professionals becomes critical. Stakeholder theory provides a useful analytical framework, suggesting that any goal achievement depends on identifying and aligning the interests of all groups affected by or able to influence the goal [12]. In the context of COPD palliative care, healthcare professionals, as medical experts, are responsible for determining the advanced status and initiating palliative care. Patients, as the individuals directly experiencing the illness, define the core orientation of palliative care through their priorities regarding quality of life. Family members, undertaking daily care responsibilities, can keenly observe the patient’s actual experiences and act as crucial bridges for communicating needs [13]. Only by systematically identifying, analysing, and balancing the concerns of all three parties can cognitive barriers be overcome, establishing effective pathways to improve the quality of palliative care.

Therefore, this study conducted in-depth interviews with patients with advanced COPD, their families, and healthcare professionals to systematically explore their differing perceptions and actual needs regarding palliative care. The aim is to provide an evidence base for developing targeted intervention strategies, thereby alleviating patient suffering and enhancing their quality of life at the end of life.

Methods

Aim

To explore the diverse care needs of patients with advanced COPD, thereby providing guidance for the development of personalised palliative care services.

Participants and procedures

This descriptive phenomenological study employed in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted between October 2024 and April 2025 at Urumqi Municipal Public Health Clinical Centre. Participant recruitment utilised combined purposive and maximum variation sampling strategies through health professionals from tertiary hospitals. The sampling process continued until data saturation was achieved while aiming to capture broad variation in demographic characteristics, including age, gender, and educational background [14].

Inclusion criteria

Interviews were conducted with patients with advanced COPD, their family caregivers, and healthcare professionals to capture diverse, real-world perspectives. Inclusion criteria comprised patients with incurable, advanced COPD, nominated by individuals with COPD and aged over 18 years, together with their family caregivers. Healthcare professionals from various disciplines with experience in providing palliative care to this patient population and their families were also recruited. Definition of advanced COPD: advanced COPD was defined per the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines utilising a comprehensive assessment strategy [15]. The definition required patients to have persistent airflow limitation (FEV₁/FVC < 0.70) with lung function severity of GOLD grade 3 (30% ≤ FEV₁ < 50%) or grade 4 (FEV₁ < 30% ), plus at least one of the following: an mMRC dyspnea score ≥ 3, a history of frequent exacerbations in the past year (≥ 1 hospitalization or ≥ 2 moderate exacerbations), or the presence of severe complications such as chronic respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension, or cor pulmonale. Interview guide

Data collection was carried out by two researchers trained in qualitative research methods. All interviews were conducted face-to-face in a semi-structured format. Before the formal interview begins, the researcher first introduces themselves and explains the background, objectives and methodology of the study to the participant in detail, whilst also collecting the participant’s demographic information. Once the researcher had confirmed that the participant fully understood the nature of the study, they asked them to sign a written informed consent form. The researcher then collected the participants’ demographic information. Before beginning the recording, the researcher once again informed the participants that the entire interview would be recorded; after obtaining their verbal consent, the recording equipment was switched on, and the interview commenced. Each interview lasted between 20 and 60 min. During the interviews, the researchers paid close attention to and recorded the participants’ non-verbal cues, such as intonation, tone of voice, facial expressions and body language. The interview continued until data saturation was reached, that is, until no new information emerged [17]. The writing of this paper followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) standards (Please refer to the supplementary materials for further details) [18].

Given the generally severe condition of patients with advanced COPD enrolled in this study, interviews were conducted in their hospital rooms. For patients unable to complete the interview in a single session, we adopted a strategy of multiple consecutive interviews within a three-day period. The interview was immediately paused if a patient showed any signs of discomfort, at which point medical assistance was sought. The decision to resume or terminate the interview was then made based on the patient’s recovered condition. In contrast, interviews with family caregivers and healthcare professionals were conducted in a quiet office within the department.

Data analysis

All interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and anonymised prior to analysis to protect participants’ privacy. Data analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis method, following their six-stage framework: (1) familiarisation with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) identifying themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) writing the report [19]. An iterative analysis strategy was employed, whereby preliminary analysis was conducted upon completion of each interview transcription. The insights gained were then used to guide subsequent data collection, allowing for deeper exploration of emerging themes until the data reached saturation. Data management was carried out using NVivo 11 software. Two coders carried out manual coding independently, resolving discrepancies through comparison and consolidating related codes into higher-level categories and themes. Whilst preserving the original meaning, text fragments reflecting patient needs were extracted and clustered, and ultimately merged into sub-themes.

Rigour and reflexivity

To ensure trustworthiness, the standards of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability were applied. Credibility was ensured through continuous in-depth data analysis and independent coding. Detailed audit trails were maintained throughout the research process, documenting methodological and analytical decisions to support confirmability and dependability. The process was overseen by a senior researcher proficient in qualitative research methods who was not directly involved in data collection.

Qualitative findings

Through an in-depth analysis of interview data, this study reveals that the palliative care needs of patients with advanced COPD are not a simple checklist but rather a dynamic framework composed of two interrelated dimensions. The first dimension consists of two persistent core needs: (1) challenges in maintaining identity, and (2) the need for empowerment and support systems. These pillars persist throughout the entire disease course, from diagnosis to the end of life. The second dimension is defined by the disease trajectory, encompassing needs specific to the stable phase, acute exacerbation phase, and end-of-life care phase. In the Results section, we will first elaborate on the two core needs, then characterize the distinct phases of the disease trajectory, illustrating how these elements collectively shape patients’ unique experiences and prioritized needs at different points in their journey. Challenges in maintaining identity

Maintain self-identity

In early COPD, individuals frequently alternate between “patient” and “healthy” identities. As the disease progresses to the advanced stage, persistent airway obstruction leads to respiratory muscle fatigue and weakened drive, causing dyspnea at rest, increased secretions, and ineffective cough. This marked functional decline reinforces a fixed patient identity, provoking strong resistance that reflects not only physical limitation but also a psychological struggle against illness-defined self-worth, characterising these patients’ distinct experience.

“I used to go dancing often… (sighs), what’s the point in mentioning that now. Never mind dancing, even gasping for breath while lying down is a struggle now. I need my daughter’s help just to get dressed every day (shook his head helplessly). I can’t do anything right.”(N3,Patient).

Maintaining family roles

The disease leads to a severe limitation of patients’ physical function, making it difficult for them to fulfil their original family responsibilities. This state of functional impairment first creates strong psychological distress for the patients themselves, who feel frustrated by their inability to perform daily tasks or support their families. As disengagement from family life increases, they develop growing feelings of alienation and shame. Simultaneously, the inevitable shift of responsibilities and household duties to the caregiver imposes its own discomfort and pressure.

“To be honest, when my son sees me suffering, it’s hard for him to bear. It makes me feel terrible too — not being able to help and just causing trouble all the time.”(N6,Patient).

Maintaining social connections

The progression of the disease leads patients with advanced COPD to shift from being a “contributor” to perceiving themselves as a “burden.” They often describe this shift as an “invisible wall” that isolates them from society, accompanied by a profound longing to feel needed. Disruption in the patient’s social role is identified as a central factor triggering spiritual distress, manifesting as loss of self-worth and meaning in life.

“I used to be a doctor myself, and now I’m lying here as a patient. It’s a bitter feeling — I can no longer save lives like before. I feel completely useless now.”(N13,Patient).

Empowerment and support system requirements

Strengthen social support networks

Most patients recognise the need for a social support network to access consolidated medication information and a reliable primary-level drug supply. However, an underdeveloped primary care system necessitates frequent visits to large hospitals. Similarly, despite needs for home-based care and telemedicine, fragmented resources sustain reliance on in-person visits, hindering the transition of care to home and community.

I truly hope the government could find a solution to make these drugs accessible in our community, even when hospitalisation isn’t strictly necessary.”(N10,Patient).

Clear disease and care information

Most patients lack tailored palliative care guidelines, as available information focuses on stable-phase management. This leaves caregivers unprepared, especially during acute events like respiratory failure. Healthcare providers recognize this need, yet cannot provide structured guidance due to heavy workloads and limited resources. The absence of practical tools leads to fragmented post-discharge support, creating a disconnect that challenges patients, families, and clinicians alike.

Needs across the disease trajectory

Stable-phase needs

During the stable phase of advanced COPD, patients and families shift focus from immediate symptoms to future medical and psychological challenges. Through daily discussions about disease progression, they actively seek support and demonstrate a clear willingness to establish advance care planning, while expressing a need for psychological support to address future-related anxiety.

Acute exacerbation phase needs

During acute exacerbations in advanced COPD, patients often develop severe dyspnea that may progress to altered mental status. End-of-life care phase needs

In the end-of-life care phase, some patients may be in a state of impaired consciousness or even coma. For such patients, the primary focus of care should be on maintaining physical comfort. However, this does not mean that psychological and spiritual needs can be overlooked; in fact, these needs are more often reflected in adjustments to care objectives. They are sustained through the efforts of family members and healthcare professionals, primarily by respecting the patient’s previously expressed wishes and assisting family members in completing any unresolved emotional communication (such as saying goodbye or seeking reconciliation), thereby helping the patient pass away with dignity and without suffering. At the same time, family members often experience profound anticipatory grief, yet their own psychological support needs are frequently unmet.

Discussion

In patients with advanced COPD, reluctance to adopt the patient role originates from progressive physical deterioration that triggers a self-worth crisis and functions as psychological self-protection [20]. This manifests initially through declining daily function, including self-care and mobility, where repeated inability to perform routine tasks gradually reduces self-efficacy and ultimately creates a sense of lost control over one’s life [21]. Subsequently, shame associated with illness emerges following the disruption of the individual’s healthy identity and their transition from family contributor to care recipient. Patients often hide symptoms to maintain dignity, but this behaviour intensifies a harmful pattern of worsening symptoms, self-denial, and social withdrawal [22]. Within China’s socio-cultural context, which is grounded in filial piety values that prioritise autonomy and not burdening one’s children, this psychological distress becomes particularly severe [23]. The dependency required by advanced COPD directly contradicts these cultural values, especially when patients can no longer fulfil family responsibilities such as household chores or grandchild care, leading to strong self-blame and perceived loss of family value. Research indicates that over 90% of elderly Chinese citizens depend on their children for care [24], with patients with COPD being especially susceptible to feelings of worthlessness when they can no longer contribute to domestic tasks. Family members’ well-intentioned care frequently extends to making decisions on behalf of patients, which may unintentionally reduce patient autonomy and strengthen dependency [25]. The loss of social and professional roles further diminishes a sense of purpose in life, and when family support is affected by relationship strains, patients’ psychological distress becomes significantly more severe [26, 27].

This study identifies significant unmet needs in support, empowerment, and information among patients with advanced COPD and their families. Patients encounter a dual financial burden, including direct costs from frequent hospitalisations, cross-regional medical visits, and long-term medications, as well as indirect costs such as income loss due to disability and opportunity costs for family caregivers who forgo career development. Data from a 2023 US study indicates that the average annual total healthcare expenditure for patients with COPD is $19,449 (with direct medical costs amounting to $4,322) [28]; the median annual direct medical costs per patient with COPD in China range from $150 to $2,014 [29]. The imbalanced allocation of medical resources intensifies this issue; rural patients bear higher expenses for non-local care, and primary care facilities frequently lack essential equipment, resulting in treatment delays, whereas urban patients benefit from convenient access to quality care. Information emerges as another critical gap. Both patients and caregivers generally lack essential knowledge on disease management, medication use, and emergency response [30]. In contrast to patients in other countries who often utilise community resources, Chinese patients place greater trust in healthcare professionals as their primary information source [31, 32]. However, the guidance provided by the healthcare system is typically fragmented and inconsistent, failing to supply structured and practical knowledge.

Family-centred decision-making introduces ethical challenges. Influenced by filial piety, adult children commonly act as surrogate decision-makers for parents [25]. A South Korean study reported that only 12.9% of non-cancer patients participated in their key medical decisions [33]. Despite good intentions, this approach often neglects patient preferences regarding quality of life and dignity, leading to misalignment between treatment plans and personal values. To bridge these gaps, we propose a patient-centred, multi-level support system. This entails optimising medical resource distribution through regional healthcare networks and telemedicine, facilitating mutual recognition of diagnostic results across institutions, and implementing tiered diagnosis and treatment to reduce financial strain. Furthermore, structured health education programs should be developed, integrating multimedia tools and scenario-based training to improve self-management capabilities. Healthcare providers should actively offer decision aids, such as advance care planning guides and treatment option tables, to promote patient participation and empowerment while ensuring privacy protection.

During the stable phase, clinical decision-making focuses on optimising quality of life. Although symptoms are relatively controlled and patients maintain basic daily activities, progressive airflow limitation results in persistently declining activity tolerance, reinforcing fears of autonomy loss and underlying death anxiety [34]. Furthermore, limited awareness of advance care planning means many patients miss opportunities to document their treatment preferences in advance [35]. When patients transition to acute exacerbation, their decision-making needs shift abruptly toward balancing life extension against suffering alleviation. Those experiencing sudden, severe dyspnea move from relative stability into a survival crisis, where choices centre on accepting potentially burdensome invasive treatments. Families frequently encounter emotional and ethical conflicts, torn between fearing prolonged suffering from aggressive interventions and experiencing guilt over treatment withdrawal [36]. These dilemmas often result in decision delays and missed therapeutic windows.

In the end-of-life care phase, decision-making priorities evolve toward preserving end-of-life dignity. Key considerations include foregoing non-beneficial resuscitative efforts and selecting the preferred place of death. Patients endure refractory physical symptoms alongside profound death anxiety, while families experience anticipatory grief, collectively creating a multidimensional burden encompassing physical, psychological, and emotional distress. Unlike advanced cancer’s predictable decline, COPD follows an unstable trajectory characterised by frequent fluctuations and sudden deteriorations [37, 38]. Recurrent, unpredictable exacerbations can disrupt previously established care plans. A dual-track palliative care model integrating “routine preparation” and “crisis response” is essential. This includes systematic personalised assessments and advance care planning during stability, combined with rapid adaptive support during crises, supplemented by proactive symptom management, family decision-making support, and grief counselling to form an integrated bio-psycho-social approach across the disease continuum [39].

Implications for practice and future research

In clinical practice, it is essential to promote a shift from ‘disease management’ to ‘holistic care’. Against a cultural backdrop that places great emphasis on filial piety, a family-centred communication model should be adopted, with information shared gradually to ensure that the patient’s wishes are reflected in treatment decisions [40]. In response to the widespread difficulty patients and their families face in managing acute episodes, there is a need to develop multimedia educational tools covering medication, symptom recognition and emergency strategies, supplemented by scenario-based simulation training to enhance home care capabilities. Palliative care should be incorporated as a standard component of treatment for patients with advanced COPD. Resource allocation should be optimised through healthcare consortia, telemedicine, and training at the primary care level, thereby reducing frequent hospital visits due to drug shortages in primary care settings. At the same time, the medical insurance reimbursement system will be improved to include home care, psychological counselling and advance care planning consultations, with a particular focus on rural and low-income groups [41]. Promote the institutionalisation of the advance care planning scheme and raise awareness and participation rates through public outreach and training for professionals. Given that only 20% of healthcare staff hold palliative care accreditation in this study, relevant courses should be introduced in medical schools and a specialist accreditation system established, with ethics training, communication skills and burnout prevention incorporated into the training curriculum to ensure the quality of care and staff retention [42].

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size and recruitment from a single institution may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions or populations. The use of purposive sampling, while valuable for in-depth exploration, may introduce selection bias, as patients who are more willing to participate might not fully represent the diversity of the broader advanced COPD population. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data carries a potential risk of recall bias, particularly for sensitive topics such as disease-related stigmatisation.

Future research should involve multi-centre studies with larger sample sizes to verify these findings and provide a more comprehensive evidence base for palliative care services in this patient population.

Conclusion

This study employed interviews with patients, family caregivers, and healthcare professionals to identify the core needs of patients with advanced COPD concerning psychosocial integrity, empowerment, and support systems, and to describe the evolution of these needs throughout the disease trajectory. Despite the established needs for palliative care in this population, there is a general lack of effective response and service provision. To address these challenges, there is an urgent need for systematically supported, culturally congruent interventions. Key priorities include integrating palliative care into standard medical frameworks and enhancing policy and financial support, particularly by improving insurance reimbursement mechanisms, to increase the accessibility and affordability of services for rural and underserved populations.
