Impact of knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management of urinary incontinence on the quality of life in middle-aged women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yoon Hee Cho, Mi-Kyoung Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the effects of urinary incontinence (UI)–related knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors on the condition-specific quality of life among middle-aged women. A cross-sectional survey was conducted with 183 women aged 50–64 years experiencing UI symptoms. Participants completed validated instruments measuring UI-related knowledge, attitudes, symptom severity, self-management behaviors, and the Incontinence Quality of Life (I-QOL) scale. Data was analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson’s correlation, and hierarchical multiple regression analysis. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with I-QOL. Model 1 included general health and UI-related characteristics, explaining 18.3% of the variance. Among these, higher perceived health status was significantly associated with lower I-QOL scores (β = − .28, p = .011). In Model 2, four main study variables—UI-related knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors—were added. Positive attitudes were associated with higher I-QOL scores (β = .19, p = .041), whereas greater symptom severity (β = − .22, p = .022) and more frequent self-management behaviors (β = − .19, p = .037) were negatively associated with I-QOL. Model 2 accounted for 29.6% of the variance. Multicollinearity and autocorrelation diagnostics indicated no violations of assumptions. UI symptoms and attitudes toward UI are key factors influencing the quality of life of middle-aged women. Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a common health problem among middle-aged women1. Previous studies conducted in various countries have reported that the prevalence of UI in women ranges from approximately 25% to 45%, with some studies reporting figures as high as 70%2. The prevalence tends to increase, particularly among middle-aged and older individuals3. A cross-national survey conducted in China, Taiwan, and Korea found that 26.4% of women aged 40 and older experienced UI, which was associated with reduced health-related quality of life (QOL), anxiety, and depression4. In addition, a global meta-analysis of women aged 55 years and older reported that Asian women exhibited the highest prevalence of UI, at 45.1%5. These results emphasize the importance of in-depth research on UI in middle-aged women in Asia, including Korea.

According to the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) and the International Continence Society (ICS), UI is defined as “the complaint of any type of involuntary loss of urine”6. This condition can cause discomfort and hygiene-related difficulties7, and may further lead to psychosocial consequences such as social withdrawal, embarrassment, depression, and anxiety8. Nevertheless, many women perceive UI as a natural part of aging or avoid seeking treatment due to embarrassment9. As a result, medical help is often delayed, and symptoms may worsen over time9. A large community-based survey in the United States reported that only 38% of women with UI symptoms had discussed the condition with a physician8. Factors associated with treatment seeking included longer symptom duration, greater impairment in quality of life, and reduced embarrassment about discussing urinary symptoms with others8.

In general, QOL refers to an individual’s subjective evaluation of their well-being across physical, mental, socioeconomic, and spiritual domains10. In particular, health-related QOL refers to the aspects of life affected by health conditions, and various diseases have been shown to negatively influence this domain11. UI has been reported to adversely affect QOL through its physical, psychological, and social consequences3,12. Thus, the QOL of middle-aged women with UI is expected to be lower than that of women without UI, and investigating the factors that contribute to this difference may help in improving their well-being.

The role of knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) in disease and health management is widely recognized13. An individual’s knowledge, attitude, symptom awareness, and self-care behaviors play a crucial role in the management of UI14. Women with adequate knowledge of UI are more likely to identify symptoms early and initiate appropriate management strategies15. Moreover, women with positive attitudes actively participate in managing UI, contributing to symptom relief and improved QOL15. Self-care strategies such as lifestyle modifications have also been reported to alleviate UI symptoms16. A systematic review demonstrated that pelvic floor muscle training had significant therapeutic effects across all types of UI, improving both symptoms and QOL16. In addition, weight loss has been shown to reduce symptoms in overweight or obese women17, and conservative approaches such as timed voiding and fluid intake reduction have also been recommended18. However, the effectiveness of these strategies largely depends on the individual’s level of knowledge and attitude toward UI13. In other words, women with accurate knowledge and positive attitudes toward UI tend to cope more actively with symptoms and consistently engage in self-care practices15. Accordingly, these psychosocial and behavioral factors are anticipated to significantly influence both UI management and the health-related QOL of affected individuals.

Although many studies have addressed the prevalence and consequences of UI, comprehensive analyses of how psychosocial and behavioral factors—such as knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management—affect health-related QOL among middle-aged women remain scarce. The KAP provides a useful framework for understanding these relationships, yet its application to UI remains limited in current literature. Given the physiological and psychosocial transitions that middle-aged women often undergo, a deeper understanding of how these factors contribute to their management of UI is warranted.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate how knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors related to UI affect UI-specific QOL among middle-aged women. The findings are expected to inform future strategies to improve symptom management and promote a better QOL in this population.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey to investigate the relationships between UI–related knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors and UI-specific quality of life (I-QOL) among middle-aged women.

Participants

This study employed a convenience sampling method, targeting a finite population of middle-aged women aged 50 to 64 years who voluntarily agreed to participate in an online survey. Participants were recruited through an online platform and were eligible if they reported symptoms of UI, accessed the survey URL, understood the study purpose, provided informed consent, and independently completed the questionnaire using their own smartphone or personal computer. Exclusion criteria included the absence of UI symptoms, severe pelvic organ prolapse, gynecologic or urologic surgery within the past six months, a history of pelvic radiation therapy following cervical cancer surgery, or limited literacy that could interfere with understanding the informed consent or completing the survey. The required sample size for this study was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.6 for multiple linear regression analysis with a fixed model and R2 increase, based on the hierarchical regression method. Parameters included a significance level of 0.05, a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), and a statistical power of 0.9019. A total of 16 predictors were considered, including 12 general health and UI-related characteristics (age, perceived health status, body mass index [BMI], perceived stress level, pregnancy experience, childbirth history, parity, mode of delivery, menopausal status, type of UI, and treatment history [surgery or medication use]) and four main variables (UI-related knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors). The minimum required sample size was estimated to be 109. To account for a potential dropout rate of approximately 10%, the target sample size was set at 122 participants. However, due to the online survey company’s active recruitment efforts, a total of 183 individuals were enrolled. Of these, 62 participants who did not report any UI symptoms were excluded based on the inclusion criteria, resulting in a final analytic sample of 122 participants.

Measurements

Permission to use all instruments in this study was obtained from the original developers and the authors of the Korean versions.

Incontinence quality of life questionnaire (I-QOL)

QOL is a multidimensional concept that encompasses physical functioning, social functioning, role functioning, mental health, and perceived general health, and refers to an individual’s subjective sense of well-being in daily life20.

The Incontinence Quality of Life questionnaire (I-QOL) was originally developed by Wagner et al.21,23 and later revised by Patrick et al.22,24. In this study, the Korean version of the I-QOL, adapted from Nojomi et al.23 and linguistically validated by Oh et al.24, was used. The I-QOL consists of 22 items grouped into three subscales: Avoidance and Limiting Behavior (8 items), Psychosocial Impacts (9 items), and Social Embarrassment (5 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Extremely”) to 5 (“Not at all”), based on the perceived severity of symptoms. Total raw scores range from 22 to 110 and were subsequently converted to a standardized 0–100 scale, with higher I-QOL scores indicating better condition-specific QOL and fewer problems associated with UI. The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) was reported as 0.94 in the original study by Patrick et al.23, 0.78 in the Korean validation study by Oh et al.24, and 0.97 in the present study.

Urinary incontinence knowledge scale (UIKS)

The Urinary Incontinence Knowledge Scale (UIKS) was developed by Yuan and Williams25 to assess knowledge of UI among healthcare professionals and the public. The tool was translated, adapted, and validated into Korean by Jo and Park26 and was used in the present study. UIKS comprises 30 items across six subdomains: knowledge of risk factors, symptoms, effects, prevention, treatment, and management of UI. Each item is answered with “True,” “False,” or “I don’t know.” Correct responses are scored as 1 point, while incorrect or “I don’t know” responses are scored as 0. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge of UI. The internal consistency of the original version was KR-20 = 0.6925. For the Korean version, Cronbach’s α was 0.8426, and in the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.86.

Urinary incontinence attitude scale (UIAS)

The Urinary Incontinence Attitude Scale (UIAS) is designed to assess individuals’ perceptions and attitudes toward UI. In this study, the Korean version, originally developed by Yuan, Williams, and Liu27 and subsequently translated, adapted, and validated by Jo and Park26, was utilized. The scale consists of 15 items across four domains: lower urinary tract symptoms (4 items), prevention (1 item), treatment (5 items), and management (5 items). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 4 (“Strongly Agree”). The scale includes 7 positively worded items and 8 negatively worded items. To calculate the total score, negatively worded items were reverse-coded. Total scores range from 15 to 60, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward UI. The internal consistency reliability of the original tool was reported as Cronbach’s α = 0.7027. In the Korean validation study by Jo and Park26, Cronbach’s α was 0.54, and in the present study, it was 0.67.

International consultation on incontinence questionnaire-urinary incontinence short form (ICIQ-UI-SF)

The International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF) is a validated instrument used to assess the severity of UI symptoms and their impact on health-related QOL28. It comprises four items that evaluate symptoms experienced over the past four weeks: frequency of urinary leakage (Q1), amount of leakage (Q2), the impact of incontinence on daily life (Q3), and the timing or situations in which leakage occurs (Q4). The frequency of UI (Q1) is rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 5 (“all the time”). The amount of leakage (Q2) is rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (“none”) to 6 (“a lot”). The impact on daily life (Q3) is assessed on an 11-point scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 10 (“a great deal”). The fourth item (Q4) consists of eight multiple-choice options describing specific situations in which incontinence occurs, such as during physical activity or while sleeping. Although not included in the total score, Q4 is used to help classify the type of UI. In the original scoring system, the total score is obtained by summing Q1 through Q3 (range 0–21), with higher scores reflecting greater severity28. However, given the conceptual overlap between Q3 and I-QOL, in the present study, UI severity was calculated using only Q1 and Q2 (frequency and amount), yielding a possible range from 0 to 11, with higher scores indicating greater severity of UI symptoms. The internal consistency reliability of the original instrument was Cronbach’s α = 0.9529; in the Korean validation study by Kim et al.30, Cronbach’s α was 0.83; and in the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.59.

Urinary incontinence self-management scale (UISMS)

In this study, the Urinary Incontinence Self-Management Scale (UISMS) originally developed by Chang et al.31 with 25 items to assess self-management in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia and later adapted by Lee32 for individuals with UI was used. The scale was further refined and validated through expert review to ensure content validity. In Lee’s adaptation, three items with factor loadings below 0.5 were removed, resulting in a 22-item version. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“Not at all”) to 5 (“Very much so”). Total scores therefore range from 22 to 110, with higher scores indicating better self-management. The internal consistency reliability of the original version was Cronbach’s α = 0.8131; in Lee’s study32, Cronbach’s α was 0.89; and in the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.93.

General health and UI-related characteristics

General health and incontinence-related characteristics were assessed, including age, perceived health status, stress level, body mass index (BMI), pregnancy experience, childbirth history, parity, mode of delivery, menopausal status, type of UI, and experience with UI-related treatments such as surgery or medication. Age was recorded as chronological age in years, and parity was reported as the number of births. BMI was calculated from self-reported height and weight (kg/m2). Perceived health status and stress level were each measured using a single-item question rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 5 = very much). A higher score indicated better perceived health and greater stress, respectively. Pregnancy experience, childbirth history, menopausal status, and surgical treatment for UI were assessed using binary (yes/no) responses. Mode of delivery was assessed by asking participants to indicate their primary method of childbirth, with response options including vaginal delivery and cesarean section. Experience with medication for UI was measured using a categorical item with three response options: “never used,” “currently using,” and “used in the past.” The type of UI was identified using item Q4 of ICIQ-UI SF.

Data collection procedure

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Chungbuk National University. To recruit participants, Korea Research, an online survey company, was commissioned to distribute the questionnaire. Prior to distribution, participants were asked to provide consent for the disclosure of information to a third party. A web-based survey link (http://b22.hrcglobal.com/?PN=pk392) was then sent to those who agreed. The first page of the survey provided a detailed explanation of the study, enabling participants to make an informed and voluntary decision regarding participation. Informed consent was obtained electronically, with options labeled “I agree” and “I do not agree.” Clicking “I agree” was considered equivalent to a signed consent. The second page included screening questions regarding age, sex, diagnosis or treatment of severe pelvic organ prolapse, history of gynecologic or urologic surgery within the past six months, and prior radiation therapy for cervical cancer. If a participant met any exclusion criteria, the survey was automatically terminated. Those who passed the screening proceeded to complete the main questionnaire. Data collection was conducted from April 11 to 16, 2025. Responses from 183 participants were anonymized, verified, and coded through Korea Research’s internal data validation process before being delivered to the researchers. Of these, 62 participants who did not report any UI symptoms were excluded from the final analysis, resulting in a final analytic sample of 122 participants.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level set at p < 0.05. The internal consistency of the instruments was assessed using Cronbach’s α. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize participants’ general and UI-related characteristics, as well as levels of UI-related knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, self-management behaviors, and I-QOL. Differences in I-QOL scores (standardized to a 0–100 scale) by participant characteristics were examined using independent t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant group differences were identified via ANOVA, Scheffé’s post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons. The number and proportion of correct responses for each item on UIKS were analyzed using frequency and percentage. In addition to the total score, item-level correct response rates were also analyzed to identify knowledge gaps across UIKS subdomains, including risk factors, symptoms, impacts, prevention, treatment, and management. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships among knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, self-management behaviors, and I-QOL. To identify factors influencing I-QOL, hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted. Variables found to be significant in univariate analyses were sequentially entered into the regression models: Model 1 included participant characteristics, and Model 2 added the main study variables.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungbuk National University (IRB No. CBNU-2025-A-0035). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. During the recruitment process, a detailed explanation of the study and an informed consent form were provided in clear and easy-to-understand language. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, and were assured that their participation was entirely voluntary. Contact information for both the principal investigator and the institutional review board (IRB) was included in the consent materials. Only individuals who passed the screening questions were allowed to participate in the survey. It was explained that participants could withdraw from the study at any point, and that if they withdrew after completing the survey, their data would be excluded from analysis. Although there were minimal risks associated with participation, respondents were informed that they could discontinue the survey at any time if they felt discomfort, without penalty or disadvantage. Participants were assured that all responses would be used solely for research purposes and that personal data would remain confidential. All data will be securely stored and permanently deleted three years after the study concludes.

Results

General health, UI-related characteristics, and I-QOL differences by participant characteristics

The general characteristics of the participants were as follows (Table 1): the mean age was 57.00 ± 3.87 years (range: 50.00–64.00); 88 participants (72.1%) reported a perceived health status of 2.89 or higher; and 43 participants (35.2%) reported a perceived stress level of 3.30 or higher. Regarding UI-related characteristics, 114 participants (93.4%) had a history of pregnancy, and 88 (72.1%) were classified as having stress UI (SUI). Surgical treatment for UI was received by 5 participants (4.1%), and 2 participants (1.6%) were currently taking medication for UI. I-QOL scores significantly differed according to participants’ characteristics. Notably, participants with higher perceived health status reported significantly lower I-QOL scores compared to those with average or lower perceived health (t = 2.54, p = 0.013), suggesting that perceived general health may not directly reflect condition-specific QOL related to UI. Conversely, participants with lower perceived stress levels had significantly higher I-QOL scores (t = 2.45, p = 0.017). Furthermore, I-QOL scores were significantly lower among participants who had experienced pregnancy (t = –2.98, p = 0.010). Those currently on medication reported lower I-QOL scores than those who had taken medication in the past or had never taken it (F = 4.73, p = 0.011; Table 1).

Descriptive statistics of UI-related variables

The mean total score for I-QOL was 72.73 ± 20.53 (range: 7.95 ~ 97.73). The mean total score for knowledge related to UI was 18.16 ± 4.79, with an overall correct answer rate of 61%, indicating a moderate level of knowledge. Among the subdomains of knowledge, the highest correct response rate was observed for the impact of UI (85%), while the lowest was for knowledge of risk factors (41%). The mean total attitude score toward UI was 43.29 ± 4.23. The mean total symptom score was 3.42 ± 0.75, and the mean total score for self-management behaviors was 74.19 ± 13.80 (Table 2).

Number of correct responses and correct answer rates on the UIKS

Among the UIKS items, the highest correct response rates were observed for “Kegel exercises can also help treat UI” (Item 21, Treatment domain, 95.1%), “UI means leaking urine even when you don’t want to” (Item 6, Symptom domain, 94.3%), “Urine can leak during heavy exercise, coughing, or sneezing” (Item 7, Symptom domain, 94.3%), “Pelvic floor exercises (like Kegel exercises) can help prevent UI” (Item 20, Prevention domain, 94.3%), and “UI can change your lifestyle and lower your quality of life” (Item 15, Impacts domain, 93.4%). Correlations among UI-related variables and I-QOL

The correlations between incontinence-related variables and I-QOL are presented in Table 4. I-QOL was significantly and weakly positively correlated with incontinence-related attitudes (r = 0.20, p = 0.030) and showed a strong negative association with incontinence symptoms (r = − 0.39, p < 0.001). Incontinence-related knowledge demonstrated a weak positive correlation with incontinence-related attitudes (r = 0.30, p = 0.001) and a weak negative correlation with incontinence symptoms (r = − 0.31, p < 0.001). Incontinence-related attitudes were also weakly positively correlated with self-care behaviors (r = 0.19, p = 0.038).

Factors associated with I-QOL

To identify factors influencing I-QOL, hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed. The entry criterion for variables was set at a significance level of p < 0.05, and the removal criterion at p > 0.10. Model 1 included only participants’ general health and UI related characteristics that were associated with I-QOL. Model 2 added the main study variables—knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors related to UI—to those included in Model 1 (Table 5). Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed no concerns, with tolerance values above 0.1 and variance inflation factors (VIFs) below 10 for all predictors. The Durbin-Watson statistics were close to 2 in both models, indicating no significant autocorrelation in the residuals. In Model 1, higher perceived health status (β = − 0.28, t = − 2.61, p = 0.011) was significantly associated with lower I-QOL scores. Notably, variables such as pregnancy experience and childbirth experience were not included in the final model, likely due to high collinearity with childbirth frequency, which was retained. These predictors collectively explained 18.3% of the variance in I-QOL (F = 2.89, p = 0.001). In Model 2, more positive attitudes toward UI were significantly associated with higher I-QOL scores (β = 0.19, t = 2.07, p = 0.041), whereas greater symptom severity (β = − 0.22, t = − 2.33, p = 0.022) and higher self-management behaviors (β = − 0.19, t = − 2.11, p = 0.037) related to UI remained significant negative predictors. With the inclusion of these additional predictors, Model 2 accounted for 29.6% of the variance in I-QOL (F = 3.72, p < 0.001).

Discussion

UI is a prevalent health issue among middle-aged women1 and is one of the major conditions that significantly impact QOL3,12. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the effects of UI-related knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors on condition-specific QOL (I-QOL) among middle-aged women.

Previous research suggests that not only disease symptoms, but also individual knowledge, attitudes, and self-management related to health conditions play a crucial role in influencing health-related QOL through various mechanisms. Although comprehensive studies on the impact of symptoms and KAP on QOL in women with UI are limited, several studies have demonstrated that KAP-based education or nursing interventions for patients with coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, or benign prostatic hyperplasia improved psychological well-being, treatment adherence, and QOL3,33. Furthermore, self-management capacity in chronic illness has been shown to have a direct influence on QOL34. Therefore, symptoms and KAP related to chronic conditions such as UI may serve as important determinants of individual QOL.

The present study demonstrated that UI symptoms exerted the strongest negative influence on QOL among middle-aged women, while attitudes were positively associated with QOL. In addition, self-management behaviors were negatively related to QOL, whereas knowledge did not emerge as a significant predictor.

The strong negative association between symptom severity and I-QOL is consistent with previous studies reporting that the physical and psychosocial burden of UI is a key contributor to diminished QOL35,36. UI symptoms were found to adversely affect daily functioning, emotional well-being, and social engagement, aligning with previous studies reporting that even mild symptoms negatively impact women’s lives37,38. These findings underscore the need for active treatment and interventions to manage UI symptoms in this population.

The finding that more positive attitudes toward UI are associated with better QOL suggests that psychosocial resources may buffer the negative impacts of UI symptoms and help improve QOL. Prior studies have also shown that psychological factors, such as disease acceptance and coping strategies, significantly influence QOL in individuals with UI39. A recent systematic review on barriers to help-seeking among women with pelvic health symptoms identified embarrassment, shame, and perceptions that healthcare professionals dismiss the symptoms as key reasons for avoiding treatment40. Similarly, a systematic review of women’s KAP regarding UI reported that women often hold negative attitudes toward UI and have low rates of healthcare utilization41. These findings suggest that negative attitudes toward UI are common and may contribute to lower QOL in affected women. It is therefore essential for nurses to assess patients’ attitudes and make efforts to foster more positive perceptions. However, since women with negative attitudes toward UI may be less likely to seek medical care40, it is important to provide active public education to raise awareness of UI and available treatments. Concurrently, educational programs aimed at improving perceptions and reducing stigma should be implemented. Because knowledge alone is insufficient to induce behavior change or improve QOL, integrated interventions that include motivational strategies and practical skills are needed.

Of particular note, self-management behaviors were negatively associated with QOL. This may indicate that self-management alone does not guarantee symptom relief and may even heighten awareness of symptoms, thereby exacerbating psychological distress. The absence of structured guidance or continuous professional feedback could also limit the effectiveness of self-management practices. Prior evidence suggests that education alone is insufficient to achieve sustained behavioral change and that repeated, structured support is required42,43. Thus, self-management should be strengthened through comprehensive interventions that combine practical skills training with emotional support.

In this study, UI-related knowledge was not a significant predictor in the regression analysis. While knowledge may indirectly influence attitudes, it does not directly translate into behavioral change, symptom improvement, or enhanced QOL. These findings suggest that interventions should move beyond the provision of information alone and instead incorporate multidimensional strategies that enhance motivation and facilitate long-term behavioral adherence.

An intriguing finding was that participants who rated their overall health more positively had lower I-QOL scores. Contrary to the expectation that better perceived health would be associated with higher QOL in individuals with chronic conditions such as UI44, those who generally perceive themselves as healthy may experience greater subjective burden, regarding UI symptoms as more intrusive or abnormal. Another possible explanation is that individuals with high health expectations may perceive even mild UI symptoms as disproportionately detrimental, resulting in lower reported QOL.

Although not a major predictor in this study, the domain with the lowest knowledge scores within the KAP model was related to risk factors. Several incorrect beliefs were observed regarding medication use, aging, and childbirth as risk factors for UI. This is consistent with previous systematic reviews, which also noted misinformation and knowledge gaps among women regarding UI41. Shearon et al.35 similarly reported that women with UI had limited understanding of its causes and treatments. However, providing accurate information on disease-related risk factors may serve as an important foundation for prevention and management. Thus, health education focused on UI-related risk factors is needed.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation. Second, all data were collected via self-report, which may introduce social desirability bias or recall bias. Third, self-management behaviors were measured based on subjective evaluations rather than objective performance, which may reduce measurement accuracy. Lastly, the reliability of the attitude and symptom instruments was relatively low (Cronbach’s α = 0.67 and 0.59, respectively), warranting cautious interpretation of those variables. Finally, sociodemographic characteristics such as education level, marital or partner status, and employment were not measured. Although the study focused on general health and UI-related characteristics, the absence of these factors limits the ability to account for potential confounding effects on QOL.

Future research should employ longitudinal or intervention-based designs to explore the causal pathways through which knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors affect QOL. In addition, qualitative research may be useful in identifying psychosocial barriers that hinder the translation of knowledge and attitudes into actual behavioral practices. Including a broader range of sociodemographic variables in future studies may also provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of QOL in women with UI.

Conclusion

This study investigated the impact of knowledge, attitudes, symptoms, and self-management behaviors related to UI on I-QOL among middle-aged women. The findings revealed that UI symptoms had the most significant negative effect on I-QOL, while more positive attitudes toward UI were associated with better QOL. In contrast, knowledge and self-management behaviors were not significant predictors in the regression analysis. These results suggest that psychosocial and behavioral factors, particularly attitudes and symptom severity, play a critical role in shaping QOL in women with UI. Moreover, the findings highlight the need for active symptom management, attitude-focused interventions, and structured educational programs that go beyond knowledge delivery to include motivation and skill-building components. Although the study had limitations such as its cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data, it provides insights for developing targeted strategies to support UI management and enhance the QOL of middle-aged women.
