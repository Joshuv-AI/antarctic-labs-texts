Parasternal Pericardiocentesis for Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus Pericarditis Complicated by Abscess and Tamponade
Authors: Sagar S Prabhu, Sankalp Malhotra, Richard Hajjar, Ernest L Mazzaferri, Konstantinos D Boudoulas, Katarzyna Gil, Ellen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pericarditis is a rare infection, with high morbidity and mortality rates.Case SummaryA 70-year-old woman with a past medical history of multiple myeloma, atrial fibrillation, cirrhosis, and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction presented with chest pain and dyspnea. MRSA bacteremia from an infected central venous port led to septic shock and an anterior pericardial abscess, causing cardiac tamponade. Urgent parasternal pericardiocentesis was performed, with fluid cultures positive for MRSA. Despite tamponade resolution, the patient's condition deteriorated, and she was transitioned to comfort care.DiscussionThis is to our knowledge the first reported use of parasternal pericardiocentesis for MRSA pericarditis. The case underscores the complexity of managing rare infectious pericardial complications.Take-Home MessagesMRSA pericarditis may cause abscess and cardiac tamponade, requiring a nontraditional pericardiocentesis approach. Despite interventions, outcomes remain poor owing to infectious sequelae.Key words: constrictive, echocardiography, imaging, pericardial effusion, right ventricle, tamponade, x-ray fluoroscopy

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old woman presented with positional, pleuritic chest pain, described as a heavy sensation exacerbated by sitting, deep inspiration, and exertion, accompanied by dyspnea. On admission she was in sinus tachycardia to 120 beats/min, but was normotensive and afebrile. Physical examination displayed reproducible left costal margin tenderness.

Past Medical History

Past medical history included multiple myeloma (treated with daratumumab administered via a right internal jugular vein port), atrial fibrillation, cirrhosis, and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction: 38%) due to nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy after implantation of a biventricular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) with an epicardial lead.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included skin and subcutaneous infection, costochondritis, pneumonia, pericarditis, pulmonary embolism, acute-on-chronic heart failure exacerbation, and acute coronary syndrome.

Investigations

Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia with no ischemic changes. Initial laboratory tests found high-sensitivity troponin of 29 mg/L (reference: <34 ng/L) and B-type natriuretic peptide of 370 pg/mL (reference: 0-100 pg/mL). Chest x-ray displayed no acute findings. Computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiogram ruled out pulmonary embolism but identified a small left pleural effusion, cardiomegaly, and biatrial enlargement.

Initial transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a thickened pericardium with small to moderate pericardial effusion adjacent to the right ventricle, without echocardiographic or clinical evidence of cardiac tamponade. Her hospitalization was complicated by community-acquired pneumonia requiring 7 days of amoxicillin-clavulanate and doxycycline, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate necessitating direct current cardioversion, and hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring 7 L of supplemental oxygen. On hospital day 7, chest CT demonstrated a moderate pericardial effusion with suggested proteinaceous or hemorrhagic components and an enlarged left pleural effusion (Figures 1A to 1C).

On hospital day 15, an episode of ventricular tachycardia, hypoxia, fever with rigors, and elevated lactate level (8.5 mmol/L; reference: 0.5-1.6 mmol/L) prompted treatment for presumed septic versus cardiogenic shock entailing intravenous metoprolol tartrate, intravenous fluid resuscitation, empiric antibiotics (vancomycin and ertapenem), and intermittent norepinephrine infusion. Cultures from the central venous port grew Enterobacter cloacae, while peripheral blood cultures grew E. cloacae along with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The central venous port was removed on day 16, and blood cultures cleared E. cloacae by day 17. Chest CT on day 17 revealed multifocal pneumonia and an enlarged loculated anterior pericardial effusion with right ventricular compression (Figures 1D to 1F). Subsequent TTE demonstrated a large, anterior pericardial effusion with right ventricular free wall and outflow tract diastolic collapse and a plethoric inferior vena cava (Figures 2A to 2D). Physical examination was notable for hypotension (mean arterial pressure: 53 mm Hg), poor peripheral perfusion with cool extremities, jugular venous distension, and tachypnea (respiratory rate: 28 breaths/min), though the patient remained in a paced rhythm at 97 beats/min.

Management

Given clinical evidence of tamponade, emergent pericardiocentesis was performed. The anterior location of the effusion precluded standard subxiphoid or apical approaches, necessitating an ultrasound- and fluoroscopy-guided left parasternal approach. A phased-array probe was used to mark the access site at the left fourth intercostal space, with micropuncture needle entry superior to the rib. A 0.018-inch wire was advanced through the needle, and its placement within the pericardium was confirmed with fluoroscopy. Using the standard Seldinger technique, a pericardial drain was placed, yielding 400 mL of purulent, turbid, rust-colored fluid (Figure 3). Fluid analysis was negative for malignant cells, but abundant neutrophils and necrotic debris were demonstrated (Table 1). Fluid cultures grew MRSA, confirming purulent pericarditis. A pericardial drain was left in place, and antibiotics were switched to ceftaroline and daptomycin.

Outcome

Postprocedurally, the patient's ICD generator site appeared erythematous, with tenderness to palpation suggesting device infection. Chest CT on day 18 displayed decreased size of the pericardial effusion and decompression of the right ventricle (Figures 1G to 1I). Given the presence of an epicardial lead, electrophysiology and cardiac surgery were consulted for ICD removal but ultimately deferred procedural recourse given risks associated with her acute illness and comorbidities. TTE on day 20 showed trivial pericardial effusion, septal bounce, plethoric inferior vena cava, and >25% decrease in mitral inflow velocity during inspiration, suggesting pericardial constriction. At this point, the patient elected for inpatient hospice, where she was switched to oral linezolid for palliation and ultimately died.

Discussion

Purulent pericarditis comprises <1% of pericarditis cases.1 Globally, Mycobacterium tuberculosis is the most common bacterial etiology, but in developed countries, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Pneumococcus species predominate. Less commonly, anaerobic species are associated with oropharyngeal or mediastinal infections.1,2 Spread of organisms into the pericardium can be hematogenous or via contiguous spread from cardiac tissue, subdiaphragmatic tissues, or the retropharyngeal space.2,3 Common sources of contiguous spread include empyema and pneumonia.2 Reported sources of MRSA pericarditis include endocarditis, musculoskeletal abscesses, osteomyelitis, thrombophlebitis, and skin infections.1,3,4

The initial suspected source of infection was the central venous port, as evidenced by positive port cultures, though only blood cultures grew MRSA. Alternatively, purulent pericarditis may have stemmed from multifocal pneumonia, with MRSA reaching the pericardial space via hematogenous dissemination (given bacteremia) or contiguous spread from the pulmonary parenchyma or a likely parapneumonic pleural effusion. Thoracentesis was not performed to compare pleural and pericardial fluid cultures. The ICD was a less likely primary source, as no clinical signs of pocket infection were observed prior to pericardiocentesis.

Risk factors for purulent pericarditis include diabetes, chronic kidney disease (including end-stage renal disease), alcohol use, immunosuppression, malignancy, and prior thoracic surgery.4 Our patient was likely susceptible to infection owing to advanced age, multiple myeloma, and daratumumab treatment. Untreated MRSA pericarditis carries a mortality rate approaching 100%, driven by sepsis and resultant multiorgan failure. Even with antibiotic therapy and invasive interventions, mortality remains high, ranging from 20.5% to 40%.1,3, 4, 5 Although antibiotics are typically administered for 2 to 4 weeks, specific guidelines on treatment duration are lacking, and outcomes may be affected by concomitant infection(s) and effective source control.1,4

In our case, the anterior location and complex nature of the pericardial effusion necessitated parasternal pericardiocentesis. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of MRSA pericardial abscess drainage using this approach. Parasternal pericardiocentesis has been described for managing pericardial effusion of different etiologies, including non-MRSA infectious pericarditis, postoperative effusions, malignancy, post–myocardial infarction effusions, traumatic injuries, inflammatory diseases, and uremic pericarditis.6,7 Both right and left parasternal approaches, typically performed with real-time ultrasound guidance using a linear probe for enhanced resolution, have been documented.6,8 Needle entry is typically at a 45° angle to the skin in the fourth or fifth intercostal space, within 1 cm of the sternal border, to optimize visualization and access the pericardium at the cardiac notch of the left lung while avoiding injury to the internal mammary arteries and lungs.7,9 The Seldinger technique facilitates drainage catheter placement after successful access. In our procedure, we used a phased-array probe for ultrasound-guided site marking, followed by fluoroscopy to confirm pericardial access upon guidewire advancement.

Osman et al7 reported a 100% complication-free success rate in a retrospective analysis of 11 patients who underwent parasternal pericardiocentesis for cardiac tamponade in an emergency department setting, performed by emergency medicine physicians without fluoroscopy. A parasternal approach is particularly valuable when subxiphoid or apical approaches are not feasible, such as in patients with obesity or anteriorly located effusions. Ultrasound confirmation of needle access is crucial but may be challenging in mechanically ventilated patients or those with emphysematous lung architecture.6,8,10 Deep effusions may necessitate a phased-array probe, as linear probes may inadequately visualize surrounding structures. However, a parasternal approach may be impractical during tamponade-induced cardiac arrest, as it can impede active resuscitation efforts.8,10

Conclusions

MRSA pericarditis is rare, with significant sequalae including pericardial abscess and constrictive pericarditis, and a high mortality rate despite optimal management. A high index of suspicion and prompt recognition of this uncommon entity is crucial in potentially reducing morbidity and mortality. In our case, an anterior pericardial abscess necessitated nontraditional parasternal pericardiocentesis. Source control is challenging in patients with comorbidities as well as venous or intracardiac devices. Future studies should compare the efficacy and complication rates of various pericardiocentesis approaches for purulent pericarditis and their impact on sequelae development.
