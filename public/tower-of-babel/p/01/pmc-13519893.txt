Clinical impact of amyloid PET in memory clinic: A 2‐year longitudinal real‐world study in Taiwan
Authors: Chia‐Ju Chou, Yi‐Fang Chuang, Yen‐Ling Chiu, Shu‐Ching Wu, Yu‐Ming Fan, Yi‐Chien Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLongitudinal evidence on amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) for Alzheimer's disease (AD) diagnosis in Taiwan and Asian memory clinics remains limited. This study evaluated the diagnostic and prognostic impact of florbetaben PET in a Taiwanese cohort.METHODSThis prospective cohort study enrolled 206 memory clinic participants who underwent florbetaben amyloid PET, volumetric magnetic resonance imaging, apolipoprotein E genotyping, and neuropsychological assessment, with 2‐year longitudinal follow‐up.RESULTSAmyloid positivity was present in 48% of participants and drove diagnostic reclassification in 49.5% of the cohort. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified an optimal Centiloid (CL) threshold of 21.60 (sensitivity 91.18%, specificity 99.04%, area under the curve [AUC] = 0.98). Amygdala, inferior parietal, and superior temporal atrophy were strong structural predictors of amyloid positivity (AUC = 0.79).DISCUSSIONThese findings establish a locally validated amyloid threshold and provide actionable diagnostic and prognostic stratification in a real‐world Asian memory clinic.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid positron emission tomography, Centiloid, memory clinic, magnetic resonance imaging volumetry

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of late‐life pathological cognitive decline, with fibrillar amyloid beta (Aβ) pathology as a defining hallmark. 1 Within the amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration (ATN) classification framework, 2 , 3 amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) serves as a pivotal in vivo tool for confirming AD pathology across the disease continuum. Its clinical importance has grown substantially following the recent approval of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies, which mandate accurate amyloid identification as a prerequisite for treatment eligibility. 4 , 5 Large longitudinal cohorts, such as the Imaging Dementia–Evidence for Amyloid Scanning (IDEAS) and Amyloid Imaging to Prevent Alzheimer's Disease (AMYPAD) studies, have demonstrated that incorporating amyloid PET into routine evaluation significantly modifies diagnosis and patient management. 6 , 7 Furthermore, randomized evidence from European memory clinics shows that early amyloid PET accelerates high‐confidence etiologic diagnosis across the clinical spectrum, from subjective cognitive decline to dementia. 8 , 9 Similarly, multi‐center longitudinal studies in China and Japan confirm that amyloid PET enhances diagnostic accuracy within Asian memory clinic settings. 10 , 11

Despite these advances, the clinical use of amyloid PET in routine memory clinics remains largely binary: Scans are classified as positive or negative, and quantitative amyloid burden is treated mainly as a threshold variable rather than a continuous clinical measure. Interpreting a CL value involves reconciling multiple discordant thresholds. Values above 30 CL are commonly used to define established amyloid pathology 4 , 5 ; however, the optimal threshold for agreement with expert visual reads is notably lower at 21 to 26 CL, creating a diagnostically ambiguous gray zone that confounds clinical management decisions. 5 , 12 , 13 Additionally, amyloid positivity rates and quantitative cutoffs vary across racial and ethnic groups. 14 , 15 Evidence from Korean memory clinics shows that both prevalence and threshold values diverge from Western estimates despite utilizing the same tracer. 14 For Taiwan specifically, a local CL threshold has been established but not yet prospectively validated, a gap that directly impacts disease‐modifying therapy eligibility in regional practice.

To address this gap, this study evaluated the clinical impact of amyloid PET in a real‐world memory clinic over a 2‐year longitudinal follow‐up. In this study, we established and prospectively validated an optimal florbetaben CL threshold in a consecutively recruited Taiwanese cohort. Utilizing this threshold, we evaluated the impact of amyloid PET on diagnostic reclassification and assessed its capacity to predict the longitudinal course of cognitive decline.

METHODOLOGY

Participants

This prospective longitudinal cohort study enrolled 206 participants with cognitive complaints or objective cognitive impairment who were evaluated at the Memory Clinic of Cardinal Tien Hospital in Taipei, Taiwan, between 2021 and 2023. Inclusion criteria included age 60 to 90 years, at least 6 years of formal education, absence of confounding neurological or psychiatric conditions, and regular clinic follow‐up for a minimum of 2 years. At baseline, the clinical spectrum included 14 cognitively normal older adults, 47 participants with subjective cognitive decline (SCD), 140 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI; 127 amnestic, 13 non‐amnestic), and five with dementia. Final clinical diagnoses were established after integration of amyloid PET findings with 2‐year longitudinal clinical follow‐up, incorporating interval cognitive assessments and functional status to confirm or revise the initial clinical impression. The longitudinal cohort demonstrated a high retention rate, with 82% of participants completing the 2‐year follow‐up. Attrition rates were comparable across baseline diagnostic groups (p = 0.12), minimizing potential selection bias.

Cognitive and clinical assessments

Cognitive function was comprehensively evaluated across multiple domains. Global cognition and disease severity were assessed using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) scale, Specific cognitive domains were measured as follows: episodic memory via the Logical Memory immediate and delayed recall subtests (WMS‐III); semantic fluency using the animal category verbal fluency task; executive function and processing speed through the Color Trails Test Parts 1 and 2 (CTT‐1, CTT‐2); and attention and working memory via the Digit Span and Digit Symbol subtests (WAIS‐IV). Clinical progression during follow‐up was defined as an increase of ≥1 point on the CDR‐SB within a year. 16 Conversion to dementia during the study was determined by comprehensive clinical evaluation by the senior neurologist (Y.C.L.), based on the temporal course of diagnostic transition.

Imaging and biomarker quantification

All participants underwent dynamic florbetaben amyloid PET acquisition. Spatial normalization and regional assignment utilized T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) co‐registration to the Hammers atlas, generating regional time–activity curves across the frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, and cingulate cortices. Non‐displaceable binding potential (BP_ND) images were modeled using cerebellar gray matter as the reference region. The dynamic acquisition protocol comprised an early‐phase scan at 10 min after injection and a late‐phase 20‐min scan initiated 50 min after the injection. Cortical amyloid beta burden was derived from the volume‐weighted mean late‐phase standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) and converted to CL units via standardized algorithms.

Quantification was executed via the Amyloid PET Quantification (AmPQ) 17 automated pipeline and converted to CL units using published standardization algorithms. To ensure the technical accuracy and reliability of this novel tool, regional SUVRs were cross‐validated using the industry‐standard PMOD software (version 4.1, PMOD Technologies, Zurich, Switzerland). The high degree of consistency between the two platforms confirmed the robust performance of the AmPQ pipeline in our study cohort (r = 0.96, p < 0.001). Binary amyloid positivity was established through a joint consensus visual read conducted by a senior neurologist and three nuclear medicine specialists, with quantitative CL values serving as an interpretive reference. For borderline cases or scans with suspected artifacts from elevated cerebellar uptake, the final classification relied strictly on established florbetaben visual read criteria.

Regional brain volumes were quantified from 3T structural MRI using the Heuron AD deep learning segmentation tool (version 1.0.4.0, Heuron Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea). 18 The volumetric analysis targeted 17 pre‐specified regions of interest (ROIs) vulnerable to AD pathology. These included the bilateral hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and the inferior, middle, and superior temporal regions, alongside global measures for the whole parietal lobe, temporal lobe, and cerebral white matter. All anatomical ROI volumes were normalized to individual brain size by dividing each volume by the total intracranial volume (ICV) and multiplying by 100, thereby expressing the final normalized values as a percentage of ICV (%ICV). APOE ε4 genotyping classified participants into carriers and non‐carriers.

Diagnostic classification and criteria

Diagnostic classification followed the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) research framework, integrating clinical assessments with biomarker evidence. “Cognitively unimpaired” was defined as preserved cognitive function (MMSE > 26, CDR = 0) accompanied by negative amyloid PET findings. “Preclinical AD” required objective evidence of amyloid PET positivity in the absence of clinical cognitive impairment (MMSE > 26, CDR = 0). “AD dementia” was established based on the combination of amyloid PET positivity and objective cognitive‐functional decline (CDR ≥ 0.5). Participants with clinical profiles or biomarker combinations inconsistent with typical AD pathways were classified under “non‐AD etiologies,” which encompassed conditions such as vascular cognitive impairment, frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson's disease dementia, primary progressive aphasia, or depression‐associated cognitive impairment.

Diagnostic reclassification was defined as a change in etiological classification between the baseline clinical impression and the final diagnosis established after integration of amyloid PET findings and 2‐year follow‐up. Specifically, reclassification was operationalized as a transition across the AD versus non‐AD boundary: Participants whose initial clinical impression suggested an AD etiology but whose final diagnosis was non‐AD, or vice versa, were counted as reclassified. The reclassification rate was calculated as the proportion of reclassified participants among all individuals.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1. 19 Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on normality, and compared using independent samples t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages and analyzed using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with significance defined at α = 0.05. The optimal CL threshold for binary amyloid classification was identified via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis utilizing the Youden index, with the overall diagnostic accuracy quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). 20 Linear regression models, adjusted for age, sex, and education, examined the associations between CL values and cognitive outcomes. Interaction terms were integrated to test whether these relationships varied by amyloid status. For volumetric analyses, Lasso regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation was implemented to address multicollinearity and select the most robust predictor regions from the 17 pre‐specified ROIs. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined based on the minimum criteria (λmin) to minimize cross‐validation error. Brain regions with non‐zero coefficients at this optimized λ were selected as key predictors, which were subsequently entered into the multivariate logistic and linear regression models. These selected features were then entered into multivariate logistic regression (Model 1, binary amyloid status) and linear regression (Model 2, continuous CL), both adjusted for age, sex, and education.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics and centiloid threshold validation

To validate the quantitative CL distribution against clinical adjudication, ROC curve analysis was performed using the consensus visual read classification as the gold standard. This mathematical validation yielded an optimal cohort‐derived threshold of 21.60, yielding a sensitivity of 91.18%, a specificity of 99.04%, and an AUC of 0.98. This cutoff demonstrated robust separation between amyloid‐negative (mean CL −4.10 ± 9.26) and amyloid‐positive (61.91 ± 32.30) populations. Figure 1 displays the CL density distributions by amyloid status, with the optimal threshold indicated by a vertical dashed line. To evaluate the stability of our cohort‐derived cutoff, we performed a sensitivity analysis applying alternative literature‐derived thresholds (21.0, 24.1, 25.0, and 26.0 CL). 5 , 12 , 13 While specificity remained stable at a ceiling of 98.1% to 100% across all models, sensitivity dropped monotonically from 91.2% at 21.0 CL to 84.3% at 26.0 CL.

Among the 206 participants (mean age 74.87 ± 7.03 years; 56% female), 99 (48.05%) were amyloid‐positive and 107 (51.94%) were amyloid‐negative based on consensus visual read. While age and education levels were comparable between groups, the amyloid‐positive cohort exhibited a higher proportion of female participants (66% vs 47%, p = 0.006). Conversely, hyperlipidemia was more prevalent in the amyloid‐negative group (51% vs 36%, p = 0.03; Table 1). For genetic comparisons, 15 of the 206 enrolled participants lacked available APOE genotyping data and were excluded, leaving 191 participants for the final analysis. A striking enrichment of the APOE ε4 allele was observed in the amyloid‐positive group, where 48% of participants (45 of 95) were carriers, compared to only 15% (15 of 96) in the amyloid‐negative group (p < 0.001).

Diagnostic reclassification and 2‐year clinical progression

After integrating amyloid PET and 2‐year follow‐up, 102 of the 206 participants (49.5%) underwent diagnostic reclassification. Specifically, 99 of the 192 participants initially classified on the AD continuum (51.6%) were reclassified to a non‐AD category (CU or non‐AD etiologies), while three participants initially classified as non‐AD were moved onto the AD continuum (Table S1). Among the 47 participants with SCD at baseline, nine confirmed amyloid pathology and were reclassified onto the AD continuum; seven were classified as preclinical AD, and two progressed to a more advanced AD‐spectrum stage over the 2‐year follow‐up. Over the 2‐year follow‐up, conversion to dementia was significantly higher in the amyloid‐positive group than in the amyloid‐negative group (43% vs 13%, p < 0.001), representing a 3.3‐fold elevation in relative risk.

Among amyloid‐positive participants, linear regression analyses revealed that higher continuous CL burden was associated with lower MMSE (β = −0.43, p < 0.001) and poorer episodic memory on both immediate (β = −0.38, p < 0.001) and delayed (β = −0.36, p < 0.001) Logical Memory recalls. Higher CL values also correlated with increased disease severity (baseline CDR‐SB: β = 0.31, p < 0.001; 2‐year CDR‐SB change: β = 0.30, p < 0.001) and prolonged completion times on executive function tasks (CTT‐1, β = 0.35, p < 0.001; CTT‐2, β = 0.31, p < 0.001).

To examine whether the prognostic impact of amyloid burden differed by established pathology status, and to distinguish it from the effect of general neurodegeneration represented by hippocampal atrophy, we fitted linear regression models with interaction terms between continuous CL values and binary amyloid PET status. Interaction analyses showed that these relationships were specific to the amyloid‐positive range (Figure 2). The negative association between CL and MMSE was markedly steeper in the positive group (β = −1.83) than in the negative group (β = 0.54, interaction p < 0.001), with a similar divergent trajectory observed for CDR‐SB change (positive: β = 2.63; negative: β = −0.34; interaction p = 0.019). By contrast, hippocampal volume correlated consistently with both MMSE and CDR‐SB progression regardless of amyloid status (interaction p > 0.2), confirming its role as a generalized rather than amyloid‐specific marker of neurodegeneration.

Low‐amyloid‐burden subgroup analysis

Within the amyloid‐positive cohort (n = 99), 49 participants met the screening criteria for amyloid‐targeting treatment (ATT) based on current appropriate use recommendations in Taiwan. These operational criteria required a MMSE score above 20, the absence of significant microbleeds or superficial siderosis on MRI, a non‐homozygous APOE ε4 genotype, and no concurrent anticoagulant use. Among these eligible individuals, nine participants (18.4% of the eligible subgroup; 9.1% of the total amyloid‐positive cohort) initiated ATT at our clinic.

To characterize clinical features at the lower end of the amyloid pathology spectrum, we defined an exploratory low‐burden subgroup as amyloid‐positive participants in the lowest CL quartile (≤37 CL, n = 25). While derived from our study cohort, this quartile‐based cutoff closely aligns with the 40 CL benchmark established in prior staging and clinical trials, 5 , 21 supporting its utility as a descriptive threshold. At baseline, this subgroup demonstrated a mean MMSE score of 25.04 ± 5.23, with 21 participants (84.0%) scoring above 20. Over the 2‐year follow‐up, zero participants progressed to formal dementia, and the overall cohort exhibited a mean CDR‐SB change of 1.38 ± 2.47 (compared to 2.29 in overall amyloid‐positive participants). Clinically meaningful functional decline was concentrated in a specific subset, with six participants (24.0%) exhibiting a rapid CDR‐SB increase exceeding two points and an isolated mean progression of 5.00 ± 2.24.

Structural brain imaging findings

Amyloid‐positive participants demonstrated significant volumetric reduction in nine of 17 pre‐specified anatomical ROIs, primarily clustered in the medial temporal lobe, limbic system, and temporoparietal cortices (p < 0.001). Regional brain volumes by amyloid PET status are shown in Table 3. In Lasso‐selected multivariate models, the most pronounced independent predictor of amyloid positivity was amygdala volume (β = −1.3, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −2.0 to −0.75, p < 0.001), followed by inferior parietal (β = −0.69, p = 0.002) and superior temporal (β = 0.96, p = 0.002), with the final logistic model achieving an AUC of 0.795. For continuous CL, the linear model explained 29.8% of variance (adjusted R 2 = 0.298), driven by significant contributions from the superior temporal (β = 0.42, p < 0.001), amygdala (β = −0.31, p = 0.015), inferior parietal (β = −0.30, p < 0.001), and inferior temporal (β = −0.23, p = 0.043) volumes (Table 4). While the hippocampus was retained by the Lasso variable selection procedure, it failed to reach statistical significance in the final multivariate model, establishing a clear contrast to its role as a broader neurodegenerative marker. To further confirm the regional specificity of these associations, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed for each ROI against binary amyloid status and continuous CL. Amygdala volume was independently associated with amyloid positivity (odds ratio [OR] = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.72, p = 0.004) and with higher continuous CL (β = −9.61, 95% CI: −18.83 to −0.40, p = 0.04), as was inferior parietal volume (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.44 to 0.88, p = 0.01; β = −9.82, 95% CI −15.22 to −4.42, p < 0.001). Superior temporal volume did not reach significance in either model (p = 0.62 and p = 0.43, respectively), suggesting its contribution in the multivariate setting may reflect shared variance with other retained regions rather than an independent effect.

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world memory‐clinic cohort, stratification by amyloid PET status using florbetaben and a CL threshold of 21.6 anchored to visual interpretation revealed a clear clinical–biological separation across the AD continuum. The observed 48.05% prevalence of amyloid positivity aligns closely with larger multicenter symptomatic cohorts reporting similar positivity rates in memory clinic populations. 8 Clinically, amyloid PET positivity was associated with a higher proportion of AD‐spectrum diagnoses and a higher rate of conversion to dementia during follow‐up. This trajectory supports prior evidence that amyloid positivity in symptomatic individuals confers increased risk of progression, thereby offering meaningful diagnostic and prognostic stratification. 22 The amyloid‐positive group also demonstrated higher amyloid burden on the CL scale and a strong enrichment of APOE ε4 carriers, reinforcing the biological validity of PET‐based classification. 1 By contrast, the amyloid‐negative group included a large fraction of non‐AD or etiologically heterogeneous cases, highlighting the practical value of amyloid PET in differential diagnosis and in guiding subsequent testing and management decisions in routine clinical settings. 23 Notably, incorporating amyloid PET altered clinical diagnosis in 49.5% of participants. This high reclassification rate underscores the substantial gap between conventional clinical assessment alone and biomarker‐verified pathology, supporting the broader integration of amyloid biomarkers into routine cognitive evaluation pathways. 5

The neuropsychological profiles complement this biological stratification. Beyond lower baseline global cognition and greater clinical severity, amyloid‐positive individuals exhibited significantly accelerated functional decline over 2 years, quantified by 2‐year CDR‐SB progression. 24 The specific domain‐level deficits, characterized by pronounced episodic memory impairment on Logical Memory testing alongside prolonged processing times on CTT, 25 present a distinct cognitive phenotype In clinical practice, recognizing that this specific biomarker‐associated profile can directly refine risk stratification, optimize follow‐up intensity, and guide patient counseling within memory‐clinic settings. 6 , 7 , 14 , 15

Structural MRI findings revealed an AD‐typical pattern of regional neurodegeneration. Atrophy was concentrated in the medial temporal lobe, posterior midline, and temporoparietal cortices, while total brain volume and cerebral white matter were preserved. 26 , 27 In multivariate modeling, amygdala volume emerged as the strongest independent predictor of binary amyloid positivity in Model 1, followed closely by the inferior parietal lobule. For continuous CL in Model 2, significant independent contributions were distributed across the superior temporal, amygdala, and inferior parietal volumes (Table 4). Notably, the amygdala and inferior parietal regions demonstrated the most robust translational consistency. Univariate models further confirmed this regional topographic specificity. Amygdala and inferior parietal volumes each independently predicted binary amyloid positivity and continuous CL burden.

Crucially, although the hippocampus was selected by the initial Lasso variable selection procedure, it failed to maintain statistical significance in either final multivariate model. This striking contrast indicates that while hippocampal volume is a marker of generalized neurodegeneration, it lacks the specificity required to predict precise amyloid burden when other early‐stage vulnerable regions are adjusted. This premise is further strengthened by the longitudinal findings in Figure 2, where the associations between hippocampal volume and cognitive decline persisted irrespective of baseline amyloid status. Nonetheless, the structural MRI‐based logistic model achieved an AUC of 0.795, suggesting that regional volumetric quantification, particularly of the amygdala and inferior parietal lobule, could serve as a scalable screening tool to prioritize high‐risk patients for expensive biomarker testing in resource‐limited environments. 28 , 29 , 30

Beyond binary amyloid PET classification, baseline amyloid burden on the CL scale provided a continuous and clinically prognostic signal within the amyloid‐positive population. As shown in Figure 2, the association between quantitative CL and cognitive measures was strongly modified by amyloid status. In amyloid‐positive individuals, higher CL correlated with lower concurrent MMSE scores and with greater CDR‐SB change over follow‐up, whereas amyloid‐negative individuals showed no significant association and clustered tightly at low CL values with limited dispersion. 31 This interaction pattern suggests that quantitative amyloid load becomes clinically informative primarily once a pathological threshold is breached, 32 , 33 consistent with prior evidence linking CL to future CDR‐SB trajectory 34 in preclinical cohorts. Our real‐world data align closely with post‐treatment evidence indicating that residual amyloid burden remains significantly associated with subsequent clinical and cognitive outcomes even after anti‐amyloid therapy. 35 By contrast, hippocampal volume correlated with cognitive measures regardless of amyloid status, reflecting its role as a marker of generalized neurodegeneration rather than amyloid‐specific pathology. CL–cognition associations are restricted entirely to the amyloid‐positive spectrum, confirming that quantitative burden yields prognostic utility only once a threshold is breached. The absence of diagnostic ceiling effects within this relationship reflects the early‐stage disease (CDR 0.5 to 1), where amyloid accumulation has not yet achieved the saturation plateau typical of advanced dementia.

These findings carry practical implications for therapeutic stratification, particularly in the context of current clinical guidelines in Taiwan that support early intervention in borderline or low‐burden individuals. While the rationale for early treatment is well established, real‐world data to guide decision‐making in gray‐zone cases remain limited. Our subgroup analysis of low‐burden amyloid‐positive participants is necessarily exploratory given the small sample size, but it illustrates the clinical heterogeneity within this range. Although none converted to dementia and 84.0% maintained a MMSE score above 20 at baseline, 24.0% experienced rapid functional decline over 2 years. Clinically, this argues against regarding low‐burden positivity as an entirely benign status, since these individuals sit at the critical transitional threshold where localized pathology begins to drive downstream clinical progression. Closer clinical surveillance rather than routine follow‐up is therefore warranted.

To navigate this clinical heterogeneity, our findings suggest that a single CL value may be insufficient to characterize prognosis in borderline cases. Instead, incorporating structural MRI metrics, particularly the amygdala and inferior parietal volumes identified as independent predictors in our model, may help identify individuals at higher risk for rapid progression and thereby refine treatment prioritization before advanced cognitive decline limits the therapeutic window. Importantly, these clinical inferences remain constrained by the small, exploratory sample size, the lack of a non‐AD comparison group, and the possibility that functional decline may reflect underlying neuropathological factors beyond amyloid burden, underscoring the need for larger confirmatory investigations. Ultimately, this study contributes a prospectively validated CL threshold and longitudinal real‐world evidence from a Taiwanese memory clinic cohort, offering a replicable framework for other Asian healthcare systems seeking to incorporate amyloid biomarker assessment into routine clinical practice.

Several limitations merit consideration. This was an observational, single‐center memory‐clinic cohort, which may introduce referral or selection bias and limit generalizability. Group differences and CL–outcome associations may be affected by baseline disease stage, etiologic heterogeneity in the amyloid‐negative group, and residual confounding. Methodologically, the determination of binary amyloid positivity relied on a joint consensus visual read panel rather than independent individual reads, which precludes the calculation of formal inter‐rater reliability statistics such as Kappa values. Furthermore, the florbetaben CL threshold anchored to visual reads, and the cross‐sectional, multi‐region MRI analyses may be sensitive to analytic pipelines and multiple comparison effects. Finally, the subgroup analysis of participants with low amyloid burden (CL ≤ 37) is exploratory given the small sample size (n = 25) and the absence of dementia progression during follow‐up; these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in larger cohorts. Follow‐up was limited for some participants; longer‐term outcomes with additional biomarkers (e.g., tau) are needed to validate and refine prognostic inferences.
