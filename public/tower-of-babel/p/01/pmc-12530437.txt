Macrophages expressing chimeric cytokine receptors have an inflammatory phenotype and antitumoral activity upon IL-10 or TGFβ stimulation
Authors: Sabrina Traxel, Florian Schmidt, Corina Beerli, Dinh-Van Vuong, Roberto F Speck, Simon Bredl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a highly aggressive subtype of breast cancer that lacks hormone receptors and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) amplification, making it unresponsive to standard hormone or HER2-targeted therapies. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown promise in tumors with high lymphocyte infiltration, their efficacy remains limited in tumors with minimal lymphocyte infiltration. Inflammatory macrophages are associated with better prognosis and response to ICIs. The tumor microenvironment (TME), however, is rich in interleukin (IL)-10 and transforming growth factor β (TGF β) inducing immunosuppressive, protumoral macrophages. Therefore, we engineer macrophages with a chimeric cytokine receptor (ChCR), designed to bind IL-10 or TGFβ and promote the generation of inflammatory and antitumoral active macrophages. Such genetically modified macrophages could be used as adoptive cell therapy (ACT).MethodsThe ChCRs consist of the extracellular domains of the IL-10 or TGFβ receptor fused to the intracellular domains of interferon (IFN)-γ receptor. Upon binding IL-10 or TGFβ, the ChCRs shall trigger STAT1 signaling, eventually rendering the macrophages pro-inflammatory. We transduced human primary macrophages with a lentiviral vector expressing the ChCR and analyzed their phenotype, secretome, and transcriptome following stimulation. Moreover, we assessed their antitumoral activity in a three-dimensional (3D) co-culture assay with TNBC spheroids.ResultsChCR macrophages showed robust STAT1 activation in response to IL-10 or TGFβ stimulation, resulting in an inflammatory phenotype similar to IFN-γ-activated macrophages, as confirmed by phenotypic markers, and transcriptomic profiling. These stimulated ChCR macrophages demonstrated significant antitumoral effects in 3D TNBC spheroids. Moreover, ChCR stimulation led to the upregulation of CXCL9 and CXCL10, chemokines essential for lymphocyte recruitment, and genes associated with good response to ICIs.ConclusionWe successfully engineered macrophages to express ChCRs that induce macrophages with an inflammatory phenotype and antitumoral activity within an IL-10-rich and TGFβ-rich environment. The induction of CXCL9 and CXCL10 expression via ChCRs stimulation could further support lymphocyte recruitment, potentially facilitating lymphocyte infiltration and disruption of the immunosuppressive TME. A future application of ChCR macrophages as an ACT might improve outcomes in patients with TNBC, particularly those with low immune cell infiltration, thereby addressing a critical unmet clinical need.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Low T-cell infiltration in triple-negative breast cancer is associated with bad prognosis and non-responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitor in combination with chemotherapy. The presence of macrophages with an inflammatory phenotype is associated with T-cell infiltration and better prognosis. The tumor microenvironment (TME), however, is rich in immunosuppressive cytokines like interleukin (IL)-10 and transforming growth factor β (TGFβ), rendering macrophages immunosuppressive. Thus, targeting macrophages to acquire an inflammatory phenotype within the TME is of great interest in the field of cancer immunotherapies.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We here evaluated macrophages expressing a chimeric cytokine receptor (ChCR) in vitro, which might be used as an adoptive cell therapy (ACT) in the future. The ChCRs are designed to bind IL-10 or TGFβ and induce an interferon-γ-like signaling and thus use the local presence of immunosuppressive cytokines to induce inflammatory macrophages. Indeed, ChCR-expressing macrophages show an inflammatory phenotype in the presence of immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 or TGFβ and have an antitumoral activity in vitro.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype of breast cancer that lacks both hormone receptor expression and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) amplification and is therefore not amenable to hormone or HER2 targeted therapies (reviewed1). Current first-line treatment for advanced stage, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) positive (combined positive score ≥10) and high-risk early-stage TNBC is the immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy. Early-stage TNBC has a good prognosis with 86.6% overall survival at 60 months when treated neoadjuvant with pembrolizumab and chemotherapy.2 In patients with advanced stage TNBC, the combination of pembrolizumab and chemotherapy minimally improves the median overall survival to 23 months, compared with 16.1 months in the placebo and chemotherapy group.3 Thus, there is clearly an unmet need for patients with advanced TNBC.

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) using chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells has achieved remarkable success in hematological malignancies (reviewed4) and there was great hope that CAR T cell therapy would also change the therapeutic landscape for solid tumors. To date, CAR T cells have only limited success in solid tumors (reviewed5). Several phase I clinical trials of CAR-based immunotherapies for TNBC are currently underway (reviewed6). While we eagerly await the first results of adoptive CAR T cell therapies in TNBC, alternative innovative treatments must be sought.

A critical determinant of disease progression of solid tumors including TNBCs is their immunophenotype, which is based on the number and localization of CD8+T cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME).7 8 Three main immunophenotypes have been defined: hot (high number of intratumoral T cells), excluded (T cells restricted to the tumor margin) or cold (lack of T cells) (reviewed9). Patients with a hot TME, including patients suffering from TNBC, have a better prognosis and show better response to ICI treatment than patients with an excluded or cold TME.7 8 10 11 In TNBC, 42–54% of patients have a tumor with excluded or cold immunophenotype.7 8 12 Therefore, shifting a cold into a hot TME is of great therapeutic interest.

Monocytes are actively recruited into the TME13 and there, differentiate to tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which, together with other myeloid cells, constitute up to 30% of the tumor mass.14 Macrophages can adopt a wide range of phenotypes depending on the stimuli received from the microenvironment (reviewed15). Consequently, their activities include immunosuppression, tissue repair, phagocytosis, inflammation, and cytotoxicity. In vitro, we can model extreme macrophage phenotypes: inflammatory macrophages are induced by pro-inflammatory signals like interferon (IFN)-γ and/or lipopolysaccharides, whereas immunosuppressive macrophages are induced by immunosuppressive cytokines like interleukin (IL)-4, IL-13, IL-10, or transforming growth factor β (TGFβ). Notably, these extreme phenotypes cannot describe the complexity of phenotypes in macrophages found in vivo, where features of both extremes can be found within the same cell (reviewed16). The presence of IL-10 and TGFβ in the TME of solid tumors, such as in TNBC,1719 however, pushes TAMs towards an immunosuppressive phenotype. These protumoral TAMs inhibit the immune system, support tumor growth, angiogenesis, and tumor cell migration (reviewed20). In contrast, inflammatory macrophages have antitumoral activity by attracting T cells, by activating adaptive immune response, and by their direct tumoricidal effects.2123 In fact, tumors with a high number of inflammatory macrophages are more likely to have a hot TME, a better prognosis and better response to ICIs.212427 Thus, macrophage-targeting cancer immunotherapies are gaining significant attention (reviewed16 28). We are focusing here on macrophage-based ACTs: early attempts in the 1990s using ex vivo polarized inflammatory macrophages showed limited clinical response, possibly due to a reversion of the inflammatory activation by the TME (reviewed29 30). Recent advances in cell engineering have reignited the interest in macrophage ACTs. Importantly, a phase I clinical trial with CAR-expressing macrophages has shown the technical feasibility of this approach.31 Furthermore, the engineered macrophages were recruited into the TME.31

Here, we evaluate genetically engineered macrophages, expressing a chimeric cytokine receptor (ChCR) (ChCR macrophages), which might be used for ACT. The ChCR is designed to use immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 or TGFβ, prevalent in the TME, to induce IFN-γ signaling. Thus, ChCR macrophages are intended to be activated inflammatory even in the highly immunosuppressive milieu of the TME. We expect this activation of ChCR macrophages to be localized to the TME, potentially minimizing systemic off-target effect.

Methods

Cell lines

HEK293T (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose, Sigma-Aldrich, D6429) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10270–106) (hereafter called D10). HEK-Blue IFN-γ cells (Invivogen, hkb-ifng) were maintained in D10 with 100 µg/mL Zeocin (Invivogen, ant-zn-1) and 30 µg/mL Blasticidin (Invivogen, ant-bl-1). MDA-MB-231 (ATCC, HTB-26) were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, R8758) with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (hereafter called R10). BT-549 (ATCC, HTB-122) were maintained in R10 with 10 µg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, I9278). THP-1 (ATCC) were maintained in R10 with 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 31350010). MRC-5 (ATCC, CCL-171) was maintained in Eagle’s minimal essential medium (EMEM, ATCC, 30–2003) with 10% heat-inactivated FBS. All cells were maintained at 5% CO2 and 37°C in a humidified incubator.

Monocyte-derived macrophages

Monocytes were obtained from buffy coat of healthy donors, anonymously provided by the Blood Donation Service Zurich, Swiss Red Cross, Schlieren. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated using density gradient centrifugation on lymphoprep (Axon Lab, 12053230). Monocytes were isolated using CD14 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-050-201) and plated at 150,000 cells/cm2 in F+high attachment plates (Sarstedt) in RPMI-1640 with penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, 15140–122). 2 hours after plating, medium was replaced with RPMI-1640 with P/S and 10% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich, H6914), hereafter called RH10. Monocytes were differentiated for 6–11 days with medium change every 2–3 days. In some experiments, macrophages were stimulated for an additional 48 hours with IFN-γ (BioLegend, 570204), IL-10 (BioLegend, 571004) or TGFβ1 (PeproTech, 100–21) at indicated concentrations.

Plasmid construction

To generate ChCR plasmids, amino acid sequences of the human IL10RA, IL10RB, TGFβR1, TGFβR2, IFNGR1 and IFNGR2 receptor were obtained from UniProt (online supplemental table 1). The ChCR were constructed as follows: the extracellular domains of IL10RA, IL10RB, TGFβR1, or TGFβR2 were fused to the transmembrane and extracellular domain of the IFNGR1 or IFNGR2, including a glycine-serine linker for flexibility.32 As an N-terminal signal peptide, we used the one from the corresponding intracellular IFNGR. All variants were analyzed with DeepTMHMM to verify in silico signal peptide cleavage, transmembrane domains and surface expression.33 Sequences were ordered codon-optimized at GenScript Biotech Corporation. For the expression of single chains, ChCR sequences were subcloned into a pCWX Dest lentiviral vector (Gift from Patrick Salmon) under the control of an elongation factor 1 alpha core promoter (EF1α). To simultaneously express both chains, ChCR sequences were subcloned into a pCWX Dest lentiviral vector separated by a P2A sequence and under the control of a spleen focus-forming virus promoter. All lentiviral vectors also encode for a truncated nerve growth factor receptor (tNGFR) driven by a human phosphoglycerate kinase promoter as a reporter for transduction. Mock lentiviral vector only encodes for tNGFR.

To generate the plasmid for luciferase and enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) expression, luciferase and EGFP sequences, separated by a T2A sequence, were ordered as gene blocks and subcloned into a pCWX Dest lentiviral vector under the control of EF1α promoter.

Lentivirus production and titration

Lentiviral vectors were produced in HEK293T. Briefly, 13 million cells were plated in T150 flasks. The day after, cells were co-transfected using PEI (Polysciences, 23966) with pMD2.G, p8.9NDSB-DPAVDLL34 (gift from Jeremy Luban), pcDNA3.1 Vpx23934 (gift from Jeremy Luban) and pCWX Dest transfer vector at a molar ratio of 251:107.5:756.1:45. 4 hours after transfection, medium was changed and supplemented with 5 mM sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich, B5887). The virus was harvested 52 hours after transfection and clarified by centrifugation. Lentiviral supernatants were concentrated by ultrafiltration using 100 kDa cut-off Amicon filters (Merck Milipore, UFC910024). Titration of lentiviruses was done by transducing HEK293T. 3 days after transduction, transduced cells were stained for tNGFR and analyzed by flow cytometry on an LSRII Fortessa (BD Biosciences).

Stable cell line generation

THP-1 were transduced with lentiviruses at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 by spinoculation (800 g, 45 min, 32°C) in the presence of protamine sulfate (100 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, P4020). Transduced cells (tNGFR+) were sorted using MACSelect LNGFR microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-330).

MDA-MB-231 cell line was transduced with the lentiviral construct to express luciferase and EGFP. Cells were sorted for high EGFP expression on a BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences).

Monocyte transduction

To transduce monocytes, lentivirus was added 2 hours after plating of monocytes in RPMI with P/S and 5% human AB serum at an MOI of 10 or 15 in presence of protamine sulfate (100 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, P4020). After overnight incubation, the medium was changed to RH10. Transduction efficiency was assessed between day 6 and day 12 of differentiation and was defined as %tNGFR+cells. In general, macrophages with transduction rates <65% were excluded from experiments.

IFN-γ signaling reporter cells

HEK-Blue IFN-γ were transduced with lentiviruses by spinoculation in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Santa Cruz, sc-134220). To measure secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase (SEAP), transduced cells were detached mechanically and seeded in a 96-well plate. Cells were incubated with cytokines as indicated for 24 hours. Thereafter, 30 µL of the cell supernatant was incubated with 170 µL Quanti-Blue solution (Invivogen, rep-qbs) and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. Optical density (OD) was measured at 620 nm using SpectraMax iD3 plate reader (Molecular Devices).

Flow cytometry analysis

To assess transduction rate and polarization marker expression, macrophages were harvested for flow cytometry by incubation with 5 mM EDTA in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on ice for 45 min, followed by gentle pipetting. Detached macrophages were first treated with True-Stain FcX (BioLegend, 422301) and thereafter stained with antibodies (table 1) and Zombie NIR (BioLegend, 423106). Stained samples were acquired on LSRII Fortessa (BD Life Sciences). Untransduced or mock-transduced cells were used as gating control for tNGFR and IL-10Rα, IL-10Rβ, and TGFβR2, respectively. Data were analyzed using FlowJo V.10.10 software (BD Life Sciences). Corresponding isotypes were used to normalize median fluorescence intensities by subtraction or as a gating control.

Flow cytometry antibodies

STAT1 phosphoflow

THP-1 or human primary macrophages were used for STAT1 phosphoflow experiments. THP-1 were plated in round bottom 96-well plates in R10. Macrophages were detached as described for flow cytometry analysis and plated in round bottom ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (Corning, 7007) in RH10. Cells were rested for 1 hour at 37°C and thereafter stimulated for 30 min as indicated. Cells were fixed by adding one volume of 4% paraformaldehyde(Santa Cruz, sc-281692) and 15 min incubation at 37°C. Next, cell surface markers were stained before cells were permeabilized with True-Phos Perm Buffer (BioLegend, 425401). Cells were stained for intracellular pSTAT1 (Tyr701) and analyzed on LSRII Fortessa (BD Biosciences).

IL-10 and TGFB1 expression in TNBC

Gene expression data from 195 patients with TNBC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Patients with TNBC in the TCGA dataset were previously identified by Lehmann et al.35 Raw counts were retrieved, preprocessed and normalized using the R package TCGABiolinks (V.2.33.0) without filtration.36 Percentile expression of IL-10 and TGFΒ1 across all genes was calculated by dividing the rank expression with the total analyzed genes.

RNA sequencing

RNA-seq and preprocessing

For bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), monocytes were transduced and differentiated into macrophages. Macrophages were stimulated for 48 hours as indicated. Total RNA was extracted using QIAshredder (Qiagen, 79654) and RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104) with on-column DNA digestion (Qiagen, 79254). RNA-seq was performed at the Functional Genomics Center Zurich (FGCZ). The NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina, California, USA) was used for cluster generation and sequencing according to standard protocol. Sequencing was paired-end 150 bp. Between 28 and 86 million reads were generated per sample. RNA-seq analysis was performed using the SUSHI framework,37 which encompassed the following steps: read quality was inspected using FastQC,38 and sequencing adaptors removed using fastp,39 alignment of the RNA-seq reads using the STAR aligner,40 and with the GENCODE human genome build GRCh38.p13 (Release 42) as the reference,41 the counting of gene-level expression values using the featureCounts function of the R package Rsubread.42 Unless stated otherwise, all R functions were executed on R V.4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2020) and Bioconductor V.3.17.

RNA-seq analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the generalized linear model as implemented by the DESeq2 Bioconductor R package.43 Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prcomp function from stats R package. For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), differentially expressed genes were ranked by log2 fold change (FC). Gene Ontology (GO) GSEA tests were performed using the gseGO function, respectively, of the clusterProfiler Bioconductor R package.44 Hallmark and custom pathways GSEA was performed using the GSEA function of the Bioconductor R package clusterProfiler (V.4.13.0)44 and the hallmark gene sets (MSigDb V.2023.2)45 on R V.4.4.0.

Analysis of previously published scRNA-seq data

We compared the transcriptome of ChCR macrophages with previously described single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data of macrophages from patients with TNBC with or without response to anti-PD-L1 atezolizumab.24 Using the R package VennDiagram (V.1.7.3), we determined the overlap of significantly upregulated genes in ChCR macrophages (log2 FC>0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR)<0.05) and genes upregulated in baseline tumor macrophages of anti-PD-L1 responders previously published. Moreover, we performed GSEA with custom pathways generated from genes upregulated in baseline tumor macrophages from anti-PD-L1 Rs and non-responders, respectively. GSEA was performed using the GSEA function of the Bioconductor clusterProfiler (V.4.13.0)44 on R V.4.4.0.

CXCL10 ELISA

To analyze CXCL10 secretion, differentiated macrophages were stimulated for 48 hours as indicated. Supernatants were collected, cell debris removed by centrifugation, and stored at −80°C. CXCL10 in supernatant was measured using the ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Human CXCL10 (IP-10) kit (BioLegend, 439904). OD450nm and OD570 as background were measured using Multiskan SkyHigh plate reader (Thermo Scientific).

Three-dimensional spheroid assay

Heterotypic spheroids were generated by mixing 1,000 MDA-MB-231, expressing EGFP and luciferase, and 1,500 MRC-5 cells in Akura 96 Spheroid Microplates (InSphero, CS-PB15) in R10. Plates were centrifuged at 100 g for 5 min to remove air bubbles and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 overnight. To support the aggregation and maturation of the spheroids, the plates were placed on a tilting stand at a 30° angle. The following day, macrophages were detached as described for flow cytometry analysis, added at a 10:1 ratio (macrophages:MDA-MB-231 at seeding), and stimulated as indicated. In some experiments, z-VAD (Selleckchem, S7023) was added to inhibit apoptosis at 20 µM. Medium, stimuli and inhibitors were replaced every 2–3 days. Fluorescent (EGFP) images were captured using the Cytation 3 (BioTek) with a 4× objective. Images were processed and analyzed with the Gen5 (BioTek) software. The mean fluorescence intensity of the full image was determined and the background of a medium control well subtracted.

Luciferase assay

As an endpoint quantification of tumor cells in the spheroids, luciferase activity of the remaining MDA-MB-231 was determined at day 6. Spheroids were washed two times with PBS to remove luciferase in the supernatant and left with 50 µL residual PBS per well. 50 µL of BrightGlo substrate (Promega, E2610) or ONE-Step Luciferase Assay System (BPS Bioscience, #60690–2) was added per well to lyse remaining cells. The lysate was transferred into a 96-well white plate (Pierce, 15042) and incubated for a minimum of 2 min. Luciferase activity was determined using GloMax Explorer luminometer (Promega, GM3500) and background signal from medium control wells was subtracted.

LEGENDplex

Supernatants from spheroid co-cultures were collected on day 6, centrifuged to remove cell debris and stored at −80°C. Supernatants were analyzed using the LEGENDplex HU Essential Immune Response Panel Standard (BioLegend, 740932) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

WST1 assay

To assess the ability of genetically-engineered macrophages to affect TNBC proliferation, macrophages were stimulated for 2 days with IFN-γ, IL-10 or TGFβ at indicated concentrations. Supernatants were harvested, clarified by centrifugation and stored at −80°C. For WST1 assay, 5,000 BT-549 or 10,000 MDA-MB-231 cells were plated in 96-well plates. The next day, medium was replaced with supernatants from stimulated macrophages or RH10 as untreated control. 72 hours after addition of supernatants, viability was measured by incubating cells for 1 hour with cell proliferation reagent WST-1 (Roche, 5015944001). OD440nm and OD650nm as background were measured using Multiskan SkyHigh plate reader (Thermo Scientific).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of TCGA and previously published single-cell RNA-seq data was performed in R (V.4.4.0). The statistical analysis of bulk RNA-seq data was performed using the Sushi framework of the FGCZ.37 The remaining statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.2.0 using statistical tests specified in the figure legends.

Data availability

RNA-seq data are deposited on the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE280810.

Results

IL-10 and TGFB1 are expressed in TNBC

We found that IL-10 and TGFB1 were expressed within the 73rd (median) and 90th (median) percentile of all genes, respectively (figure 1A). Thus, TCGA gene expression analysis confirms previously published results of IL-10 and TGFβ expression in patients with TNBC.

IL-10 and TGFβ ChCR induce STAT1 signaling

To convert IL-10 or TGFβ signals into IFN-γ signals, we designed two ChCR that consist of the extracellular domains of IL-10 receptor or TGFβ receptor, respectively, fused to the intracellular domains of the IFN-γ receptor (figure 1B,C). To control for transduction efficacy, we also incorporated the tNGFR in the lentiviral vector.

In the first instance, we verified IFN-γ signaling through the IL-10 ChCR in HEK-Blue IFN-γ reporter cell line. In the HEK-Blue IFN-γ reporter cell line, IFN-γ-induced STAT1 signaling leads to expression of SEAP, which can be measured in the supernatant using a colorimetric assay. We stably expressed either the full IL-10 ChCR or the individual components, that is, IL-10RA fused to IFNGR1 and IL-10RB fused to IFNGR2 in the HEK-Blue IFN-γ reporter cells (online supplemental figure 1A). All cell lines were transduced at >90% efficiency (data not shown) and ChCR expression was confirmed by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure 1B). We found that IL-10 induced IFN-γ signaling in a dose-dependent manner when the full IL-10 ChCR was expressed (online supplemental figure 1C). Surprisingly, IL-10RA-IFNGR1 alone triggered moderate IFN-γ signaling, but both chains of the ChCR were necessary to achieve maximum STAT1 signaling. We also verified that the expression of the full TGFβ ChCR in HEK-Blue IFN-γ reporter cell line induced STAT1 signaling (data not shown). Next, we proceeded to the characterization of both ChCRs in the monocyte cell line THP-1, which more closely approximates the properties of primary human macrophages. We found a prominent upregulation of IL-10Rα and TGFβR2 and a moderate one of IL-10Rβ using flow cytometry (figure 1D). We tested several TGFβR1 antibodies, all suffered from non-specificity, and therefore, we do not have expression data for TGFβR1. Since TGFβR1 is on the first position of the polycistronic vector, and TGFβR2 expression is detectable, we assume that TGFβR1 is expressed as well. Importantly, ChCR stimulation induced a dose-dependent activation of STAT1 in IL-10 and TGFβ ChCR-expressing THP-1 cells, with the TGFβ inducing maximum activation at 10 ng/mL and IL-10 at 100 ng/mL (figure 1E). As expected, untransduced and mock-transduced THP-1 cells only showed pSTAT1 signaling upon IFN-γ stimulation. To investigate the ChCRs in primary macrophages, we first optimized the protocol for transducing monocytes. We achieved a transduction efficiency of 70.2±14.5% (mean±SD) across all vectors (figure 1F). Similar to ChCR expression in THP-1, we saw a very prominent upregulation of IL-10Rα and TGFβR2 and a lesser one of IL-10Rβ (figure 1G). In ChCR macrophages, IL-10 induced STAT1 activation to a similar level as IFN-γ, whereas TGFβ showed tendentially an increase in STAT1 activation compared with mock macrophages (p=0.1058, mean fold change 1.6±0.67 SD) (figure 1H). Thus, both IL-10 and TGFβ ChCR variants trigger the IFN-γ signaling pathway.

ChCR expression induces an inflammatory phenotype upon IL-10/ TGFβ stimulation

We next assessed whether triggering the ChCRs induces an inflammatory phenotype in primary human macrophages. We focused on polarization markers typically upregulated on macrophages either upon IFN-γ (ie, HLA-DR, CD38, CD86) or IL-10 stimulation (ie, CD163) and analyzed their expression in unstimulated, IL-10, TGFβ or IFN-γ stimulated macrophages. The main comparison is between ChCR and mock macrophages upon IL-10 or TGFβ stimulation, in order to assess the effect of ChCR expression on macrophage function in an IL-10-rich or TGFβ-rich environment. Importantly, HLA-DR and CD38 were upregulated in IL-10 ChCR macrophages in response to IL-10 compared with IL-10 stimulated mock macrophages and unstimulated ChCR macrophages (figure 2A). Additionally, the IL-10 ChCR inhibited CD86 downregulation by IL-10 (figure 2A). Similarly, HLA-DR, CD38 and CD86 were upregulated in TGFβ ChCR macrophages in response to TGFβ both in comparison to TGFβ-stimulated mock macrophages and unstimulated ChCR macrophages (figure 2B). Moreover, IL-10 blocked the upregulation of CD163 in IL-10 ChCR transduced macrophages, while it increased its expression in mock macrophages (figure 2A). Notably, CD163 is upregulated in response to IL-10 but not to TGFβ, and therefore, we did not measure CD163 expression in TGFβ ChCR macrophages. The transduction itself did not affect the phenotype, except for reduced IL-10-induced CD163 expression in mock macrophages (figure 2A). Overall, we found that ChCR macrophages indeed show a phenotype similar to IFN-γ stimulated macrophages upon stimulation with IL-10 or TGFβ.

ChCR macrophages have an IFN-γ-like transcriptome

Next, we performed RNA-seq to extend the characterization of ChCR macrophages. PCA showed that ChCR macrophages stimulated with their corresponding ligand clustered together with IFN-γ stimulated macrophages, indicating an IFN-γ-like transcriptome profile induced by stimulation of the ChCRs (figure 3A). As expected, differential gene expression analysis of ChCR macrophages showed the upregulation of many IFN-γ stimulated genes upon IL-10 or TGFβ stimulation when compared with mock-transduced macrophages stimulated equally (figure 3B). Similarly, GSEA of hallmark gene sets showed that IFN-γ stimulated genes, among other CXCL9, CXCL10, and CIITA, were significantly enriched in ChCR macrophages upon stimulation (figure 3C). Moreover, GSEA of GO biological processes showed enrichment of genes associated with antigen presentation (major histocompatibility complex-II human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes) and several immune-related pathways (figure 3D). Thus, we could confirm that ChCR macrophages have an IFN-γ-like transcriptome.

ChCR macrophages upregulate genes associated with anti-PD-L1 response in patients with TNBC

CXCL9 and CXCL10 have been associated with better response to immunotherapy and a hot TME in TNBC and other tumors.21 24 26 27 46 Interestingly, both CXCL9 and CXCL10 were upregulated in ChCR macrophages in RNA-seq, which we confirmed for CXCL10 by ELISA (figure 4A,B). We further correlated the in vitro transcriptome of ChCR macrophages with clinical data from patients with TNBC. Zhang et al, have performed single-cell RNA-seq of biopsies from patients with TNBC treated with chemotherapy and anti-PD-L1 and have identified a TAM transcriptome profile associated with anti-PD-L1 response.24 Thus, we assessed the expression of other genes associated with good response to anti-PD-L1 treatment in our ChCR macrophages upon stimulation. Indeed, 46 (IL-10 ChCR) and 55 (TGFβ ChCR) of 195 response-associated genes were also significantly upregulated in ChCR macrophages upon stimulation with the respective ligand in comparison to mock transduced macrophages (onlinesupplemental tables 2 3). Moreover, the heatmap analysis of response-associated genes revealed two distinct clusters, which were separated based on the presence of IFN-γ or ChCR-induced IFN-γ-like signaling (figure 4C). Note that 14 (IL-10 ChCR) and 22 (TGFβ ChCR) response-associated genes were downregulated in samples with ChCR-induced IFN-γ-like signaling. Response-associated genes that were upregulated in ChCR macrophages upon stimulation belong, for example, to hallmark pathways of IFN-γ response and allograft rejection (ie, CD74, CXCL9, STAT1, SERPING1, B2M…) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α signaling via NF-κB (ie, TNF, SAT1…). In addition, we have performed GSEA of custom responder (upregulated in anti-PD-L1 responders) and non-responder (downregulated in anti-PD-L1 responders) gene sets. We found that the responder gene set was indeed enriched in ChCR macrophages upon stimulation with the respective ligand (figure 4D). In contrast, the non-responder gene set was not enriched in ChCR macrophages. Thus, according to the transcriptome data, our genetically engineered macrophages could be beneficial for the response to anti-PD-L1 therapy.

ChCR macrophages have antitumoral activity

Inflammatory macrophages have long been known to have antitumoral effects.22 47 Therefore, we explored whether ChCR macrophages have an antitumoral effect in a heterotypic TNBC spheroid model. This spheroid model consisted of the TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 and MRC-5 fibroblasts. The presence of fibroblasts was essential as MDA-MB-231 cells alone did not form spheroids. One day after formation of MDA-MB-231 and MRC-5 spheroids, we added the genetically engineered macrophages at an effector:target ratio of 10:1. MDA-MB-231 can induce a TAM-like phenotype48 which could revert the ChCR-induced inflammatory phenotype. Importantly, CXCL10 secretion in spheroids co-cultured with ChCR macrophages suggests that the inflammatory phenotype is maintained (figure 5A). To assess the effect of ChCR macrophages on the viability of MDA-MB-231 cells, we engineered the cell line to express both EGFP and luciferase to determine cell number by microscopy over time and as endpoint, respectively. Indeed, IL-10 and TGFβ ChCR macrophages limited the expansion of MDA-MB-231 spheroids when added together with the corresponding cytokines, as assessed by mean EGFP intensity and luciferase activity on day 6 (figure 5B–D). Previously, inflammatory macrophages were shown to induce apoptosis in tumor cells.49 50 Apoptosis, however, does not seem to be the main antitumoral mechanism of ChCR macrophages as the pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD did not prevent their antitumoral effect in the spheroid assay (online supplemental figure S2). Next, we were wondering whether the antitumoral effect of ChCR macrophages was mediated by soluble factors or cell contact. We found that supernatant from stimulated ChCR macrophages alone led to reduced MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cell number in two-dimensional (2D) culture (online supplemental figure S3), indicating that secreted factors, are, at least in part, responsible for the antitumoral effect observed in the spheroid model.

Discussion

The presence of inflammatory macrophages in the TME is a favorable prognostic marker21, and therefore, macrophages represent a therapeutic target. Here, we evaluated ChCR macrophages, designed to switch to inflammatory macrophages when exposed to IL-10 or TGFβ abundantly found in the TME. We show that ChCR macrophages indeed have a phenotype similar to IFN-γ-induced inflammatory macrophages and direct antitumoral activity in vitro.

The TME is rich in immunosuppressive cytokines, among other IL-10 and TGFβ. In TCGA RNA-seq data, we confirmed that the transcripts of these cytokines are found among the top 70% of transcripts in TNBC. IFN-γ signaling is involved in inducing macrophages that are associated with better prognosis.21 Therefore, we constructed ChCRs by combining the extracellular domains of the IL-10 and TGFβ receptors with the intracellular signaling domains of the IFN-γ receptor to activate macrophages in the TME, leveraging the local enrichment of IL-10 and TGFβ to induce antitumoral macrophages. Recently, similar switch receptors have been described for macrophages: comparable to our IL-10 ChCR, Sloas et al described macrophages engineered to express an IL-10/IFN-λ switch receptor for cancer therapy.51 Furthermore, the switch receptor concept has also been adapted to achieve the opposite switch from an inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory activity for the treatment of chronic inflammation, with macrophages engineered with an anti-TNF-α/IL4R CAR.52

Our results indicate that ChCR macrophages become inflammatory in response to IL-10 or TGFβ. These ChCR macrophages exhibit several attributes that could enable them to disrupt an immunosuppressive TME. First, RNA-seq and ELISA showed that ChCR macrophages upregulated the expression of the chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10/CXCL10 upon IL-10 or TGFβ stimulation. This suggests that ChCR macrophages might attract T cells into the TME as both CXCL9 and CXCL10 are associated with a hot TME and better prognosis.21 26 Similarly, IL-10/IFN-λ switch receptor macrophages lead to increased T-cell infiltration and reduced tumor growth in a syngeneic tumor mouse model.51

Second, HLA-DR and CD86 were also upregulated as determined by flow cytometry. These surface proteins are crucial for T-cell activation and antigen presentation. RNA-seq analysis also showed the enrichment of pathways associated with antigen presentation. Therefore, ChCR macrophages might attract and activate T cells.23 53

The presence of inflammatory macrophages is associated with better response to ICIs in TNBC and beyond.212427 The RNA-seq data we obtained from ChCR-expressing macrophages in response to the corresponding cytokines showed upregulation of genes previously associated with response to anti-PD-L1 ICI in TNBC.24 Thus, future studies should explore whether PD-L1 ICI treatment in combination with ChCR macrophage ACT could result in an additive or synergistic antitumoral activity.

Ultimately, we show that ChCR macrophages have a direct antitumoral effect when adding to a TNBC spheroid composed of MDA-MB-231 cells and MRC-5 fibroblasts, though they did not completely eradicate tumor cells. While macrophages are known to be tumoricidal upon both toll-like receptor (TLR) and IFN-γ stimulation,22 not a lot is known about their antitumoral activity upon IFN-γ stimulation. In the case of ChCR macrophages, apoptosis does not seem to be involved in their antitumoral activity, since treatment of spheroids with pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD did not prevent the antitumoral activity of ChCR macrophages. The ability of inflammatory macrophages to induce tumor cell apoptosis, as previously reported,49 50 seems to be dependent on the target cells, macrophage source and additional TLR4 stimulation. In fact, murine bone marrow macrophages require both stimulation with IFN-γ and TLR agonists to act antitumoral.54 Additionally, our data suggest the antitumoral activity is partially mediated by soluble factors, as supernatant obtained from ChCR macrophages reduced the viability of the TNBC cells MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 in 2D. TNF-α and nitric oxide (NO) are the main soluble factors reported to mediate the antitumoral effect of TLR and IFN-γ stimulated macrophages.54 55 Yet, we did not detect TNF-α in the spheroid co-culture supernatant (data not shown). Regarding NO, it is controversial whether human macrophages have the ability to secrete it and under which conditions they do so (reviewed56). In fact, the NO synthetase NOS2 was neither upregulated upon IFN-γ nor upon ChCR stimulation in our RNA-seq data (data not shown). The mechanism by which our ChCR macrophages act antitumoral remains to be determined.

Besides IL-10 and TGFβ, the TME is rich in factors such as IL-4, low pH, hypoxia and high adenosine levels that might affect the inflammatory activation of ChCR macrophages. The complexity of the TME cannot be fully recapitulated in vitro. Nevertheless, ChCR macrophages retained their inflammatory phenotype, at least in the spheroid assay used here, as demonstrated by CXCL10 secretion, despite the reported ability of MDA-MB-231 to induce a TAM phenotype.48 In line with that, IFN-γ was previously shown to reverse the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs isolated from the ascites of patients with cancer.57

We observed differences in the performance of the two ChCR variants: the TGFβ ChCR responded to lower cytokine dose than the IL-10 ChCR, as shown by the STAT1 activation in THP-1 and CXCL10 secretion in primary macrophages. This may reflect differences in cytokine affinity or activation thresholds of the ChCRs. The latter may be influenced by the specific extracellular domain that is fused to the IFNGR2 signaling domain, which is the limiting subunit.58 Specifically, for the IL-10 ChCR, we fused the low affinity IL-10Rb to IFNGR2 (reviewed59), whereas for the TGFβ ChCR, we fused the high affinity TGFβR2 to the IFNGR2.60 Importantly, in the spheroid assay, macrophages expressing IL-10 ChCR or TGFβ ChCR performed similarly when saturating concentrations were used. Ultimately, in vivo experiments will be needed to assess and compare activity and toxicity of both ChCR variants.

Numerous cancer therapies that target the macrophage function are currently under investigation (reviewed16 28). For example, CSF1R and CCR2 blocking agents are investigated to reduce recruitment of TAMs or to deplete them. Additionally, STAT3 and PI3K inhibitors and TLR or STING agonists may be used to reprogram TAMs. Currently, none of these are approved for the treatment of TNBC. Ultimately, clinical trials are needed to evaluate the potency and toxicity of the different approaches.

In conclusion, we show that ChCR macrophages present several attributes that may enable them to disrupt an immunosuppressive TME and render them antitumor active. This study is a starting point, and we will further investigate the use of ChCR macrophages as an ACT for TNBC in vivo in a humanized mouse model.
