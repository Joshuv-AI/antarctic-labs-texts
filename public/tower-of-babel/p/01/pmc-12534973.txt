Pleiotrophin modulates cell proliferation, prostate smooth muscle contraction and fibrosis in hyperplastic prostate
Authors: Jiang Liu, Jiuming Fan, Huan Liu, Mingzhe Chen, Xun Fu, Yiqiao Zhao, Muyang Cheng, Yuming Guo, Yan Li, Jianmin Liu, Zhonghua Wu, Yong Li, Michael E. DiSanto, Xinhua Zhang, Ping Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBenign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common disease afflicting elderly males all over the world. The existing scientific research still has not completely clarified its etiology. Pleiotrophin (PTN) is a developmental regulatory protein involved in various biological processes. This study aimed to elucidate the expression, biological function, and underlying mechanism of PTN in the onset and progression of BPH.MethodsHuman prostate tissues, cell lines, rat models and PTN-knockout mice models were utilized. PTN knockdown, PTN overexpression, and estradiol cell models were established. The qRT-PCR, Western Blot, flow cytometry, CCK-8 assay, collagen gel contraction assay, co-immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence, H&E, Masson’s trichrome, immunohistochemical staining, TUNEL assay and tissue micro-array analysis were performed during in vivo and in vitro experiments.ResultsOur current data validated that PTN is localized in stroma and epithelium of the prostate, with stronger expression in BPH tissues. Functionally, silenced PTN promoted cell apoptosis while it inhibited cell proliferation, cell contraction and fibrosis. Consistently, overexpression of PTN suppressed cell apoptosis, and facilitated cell proliferation, cell contraction, as well as fibrosis. More importantly, AKT phosphorylation and RhoA/ROCK1/2 axis were confirmed to be involved in the regulation of prostate biological processes by PTN. Moreover, estradiol treatment could enhance PTN expression and modulate downstream biological process. Also, PTN expression was correlated with prostate volume (PV), fPSA and the ratio of fPSA/tPSA. Finally, recombinant PTN induced prostatic hyperplasia in rats, PTN knockout suppressed BPH in mice, and estradiol treatment upregulated PTN expression. These results further confirm the crucial role of PTN in BPH onset and progression.ConclusionOur current study demonstrates that PTN is of significance in the onset and development of BPH and may be a new target for the treatment of BPH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07172-0.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common disease that afflict the majority of elderly men [1, 2]. The onset and progression of BPH is attributed to both static factors (enlarged prostate) and dynamic factors which include increased prostatic tension (active and passive) [3]. The static factors include cell proliferation and growth factors. In terms of dynamic factors, changes in cytoskeleton and myosin regulate prostatic tension.

Myosin II is a protein that can induce motion and force generation after coupling with actin [4]. In prostate tissue, smooth muscle myosin II (SMM II) and non-muscle myosin II (NMM II) are abundantly expressed [5]. Functionally, SMM II can regulate prostate SM tone [6]. Furthermore, in the absence of SMM heavy chains (SMMHC), NMM heavy chains A, B and C (NMMHC-A, NMMHC-B, NMMHC-C) can generate force and shorten in SM [7]. Moreover, prostate fibrosis, which contributes to passive tension, is now recognized as another etiology of BPH [8].

Additionally, estrogen is also involved in the regulation of BPH. Animal models of BPH suggest that estrogen and androgens can synergistically induce BPH [9]. In our recent study, the increase of estrogen/androgen ratio resulted in rat prostate stroma hyperplasia [3]. Also, estrogen can act on prostate cells to cause prostate stromal cell proliferation and prostate epithelial cell apoptosis [10]. Additionally, epithelial-mesenchymal interaction, inflammation, and cytokines also participate in the development of BPH [11]. However, despite decades of investigations, the driving factors leading to BPH remain unclear.

In our recent study, five BPH tissues and three normal BPH tissues were collected for cDNA microarray analysis and 198 differentially expressed genes, including PTN, were identified [12]. Pleiotrophin (PTN), the protein encoded by PTN, is a developmental regulatory factor related to mitosis and nerve growth activity [13]. It also has been shown to interact with a variety of cell surface receptors, including PTPRZ, integrin αMβ2, and nucleolin. Among them, PTPRZ is thought to be related to important biological functions of PTN [14, 15]. Publications revealed PTN is involved in cell proliferation and differentiation [16–21]. Indeed, PTN is highly upregulated during embryonic development and early cell differentiation. In adults, PTN is mainly expressed in the central nervous system, but also in the prostate, testes, bladder, and stomach [15]. Furthermore, it was confirmed estrogen could stimulate PTN expression in osteoblasts [22]. However, whether estrogen can modulate PTN production in the prostate remains to be elucidated.

The biological processes regulated by PTN involve multiple signaling pathways, including AKT signaling and the RhoA/ROCK axis [15, 23]. AKT signaling mainly regulates cell proliferation, survival and mitosis [24, 25]. Regarding the RhoA/ROCK axis, it is thought to be associated with cell contraction and force production [3, 26]. Indeed, the activation of RhoA/ROCK could amplify contraction of the prostate stromal cells and induce prostatic fibrosis, which further regulate the development of BPH [11].

However, the functional mechanisms of PTN in BPH pathogenesis remain unclear. Current study characterized PTN expression patterns in human prostate tissues. Functional assessments were then performed in human prostate cells via PTN knockdown and overexpression, evaluating effects on cell proliferation and apoptosis, cell contractility, and fibrosis prosses. In addition, we confirmed the effect of estrogen on PTN expression and its subsequent biological processes. The underlying molecular mechanisms regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis, cell contraction and fibrosis in BPH were also identified.

Methods and materials

Cell lines (WPMY-1, BPH-1, RWPE-1) obtained and cultured, total RNA extraction, RNA reverse transcription, qRT-PCR analysis, Western blot analysis, Flow cytometry analysis, CCK-8 assay, Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay were performed as we previously described [25, 27, 28]. The primary and secondary antibodies were summarized in Table S1 and Table S2. Primer sequences were summarized in Table S3.

Animals and tissues

A total of 24 specific-pathogen-free (SPF) grade male Sprague–Dawley rats (6 weeks) weighing 160–200 g were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 6 per group): (a) control group, corn oil (MedChemExpress, CHN) injection (subcutaneously (s.c.)); (b) T-E2 (100:1) group [29], T (testosterone propionate, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) (10 mg/kg/day) - E2 (estradiol, MedChemExpress, CHN) (0.1 mg/kg/day)/corn oil injection (s.c.) ; (c) T-E2 (100:10) group, T (10 mg/kg/day) - E2 (1 mg/kg/day) /corn oil injection (s.c.) ; (d) recombinant PTN (rPTN) group, rPTN (Cloud-Clone Ltd, in Wuhan, CHN) / normal saline (intraprostatic injection). A small incision is made in the midline of the lower abdomen above the rat’s penis, exposing the ventral prostate. rPTN (5 µg) in a final volume of 50µL sterile normal saline was injected into both right and left ventral lobes of the prostate with a 30-gauge needle. For the control, T-E2 (100:1), and T-E2 (100:10) groups, 50µL sterile normal saline was injected. On day 28, ventral prostates and seminal vesicles were harvested and weighed. All procedures were performed under anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (35 mg/kg; Abbott Laboratory, USA).

PTN-knockout and wild-type C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China). The PTN gene comprises five exons encoding an 18-kDa protein and a 32-amino acid signal peptide. Exons PTN2–3 of the PTN gene were deleted using gRNA. In this study, 8-week-old male PTN−/− and wild-type (PTN+/+) mice were utilized. The genotype of PTN−/− mice was validated through PCR by amplifying cDNA fragments of 0.441, 0.608, and 2.456 kb using Primer 1 and Primer 2 (Table S3 ) [27]. These fragments were visualized through agarose gel electrophoresis of genomic DNA extracted from the tails of PTN−/− and PTN+/+ mice. A total of 48 specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade male mice weighing 18.0–30.0 g were included, comprising 24 PTN−/− and 24 PTN+/+ mice. Six mice from each group were randomly selected for sample collection. The remaining PTN−/− and PTN+/+ mice were divided into three subgroups (n = 6 per subgroup), forming six experimental groups: (a) PTN+/+ Control group, corn oil (s.c.); (b) PTN+/+ T-E2(100:1) group, T (10 mg/kg/day) - E2 (0.1 mg/kg/day) /corn oil injection (s.c.); (c) PTN+/+ T-E2(100:10) group, T (10 mg/kg/day) - E2 (1 mg/kg/day) /corn oil injection (s.c.); (d) PTN−/− Control group, corn oil (s.c.); (e) PTN−/− T-E2(100:1) group, T (10 mg/kg/day) - E2 (0.1 mg/kg/day) /corn oil injection (s.c.); (f) PTN−/− T-E2(100:10) group, T (10 mg/kg/day) - E2 (1 mg/kg/day) /corn oil injection (s.c.). On day 28, the ventral prostate was excised and weighed. All animal protocols were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee for Experiments at Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University before the experiments were conducte.

A total of Eight prostate samples were obtained as controls from young brain-dead men (mean age 25.2 ± 3.4 years) that underwent organ donation, with pathological examination revealing no hyperplasia. Moreover, 104 BPH samples with clinical data were obtained from patients (mean age 69.4 ± 5.7 years) who underwent transurethral resection of the prostate in the Department of Urology, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, with prostatic hyperplasia verified by post-operative pathologic examination. All prostate samples were collected with written informed consent from all patients or their relatives and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committees of Zhongnan Hospital. Our human studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture

Human breast epithelial cell line MCF10A was originated from ATCC and was maintained in DMEM/F12 medium (Procell, CHN) supplemented with 20 ng/ml human epidermal growth factor (MedChemExpress, CHN), 5% horse serum (Gibco, CHN), 10 µg/ml human recombinant insulin (Novoprotein, CHN), 0.5 µg/ml hydrocortisone (APExBIO, CHN), and penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, CHN) at 37 °C, in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Immunofluorescence staining

Prostate tissue was cut into 5-µm-thick sections and mounted on glass slides using a cryostat (Leica cm 1850, Wetzlar, Germany). After air drying, the tissue-loaded slides were fixed in ice-cold acetone for 30 min. Slides were washed three times (5 min each) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The slides were subsequently incubated in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 and 0.1% bovine serum albumin for 2 h, followed by incubation with the primary antibody overnight (Table S1). Prostate cells were cultured in 6-well plates on 12-mm coverslips. After the cells were completely attached, the coverslips were washed three times with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Next, the coverslips were incubated with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature (RT) for 5 min, followed by blocking with 10% bovine serum albumin for 1 h (RT). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the primary antibodies (Table S1) overnight at 4 °C. After overnight incubation, tissues and cells were washed with PBS and incubated with Cy3-labeled or FITC-labeled secondary antibodies (Table S2). Coverslips loaded with cells and slides loaded with tissues were incubated in humidified air for 1 h at RT and then washed with 2 µg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for nuclear staining. Laser Scanning Confocal Fluorescence Microscopy (cat. no. IX73; Olympus, Japan) was performed for visualization.

Cell transfection

PTN-targeting small interfering RNAs(siRNAs) were purchased from Genepharma Ltd., Suzhou, China. PTN- and estrogen receptor α (ERα)- specific plasmids were acquired from TsingkeBiotechnology Co. Ltd. in Wuhan, China. The cDNA of PTN was cloned into a 3×FLAG-pcDNA3.1 vector (FLAG-PTN plasmid). The cDNA of ERα was cloned into a HA-pcDNA3.1 vector (HA- ERα plasmid). Three different siRNAs (named si1, si2, and si3) were tested for knockdown efficiency and si2 and si3 were selected for subsequent experiments (Table S4). In addition, RhoA siRNA (si-RhoA) was also purchased from Genepharma Ltd., Suzhou, China and its knockdown efficiency had been validated in a previous study [11]. Negative control siRNA (NC) and empty plasmid (vector) were used as controls, respectively. siRNAs (or plasmid) and negative control siRNA (or vector) were diluted in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium and mixed with Lipofectamine®2000 (Invitrogen, USA) as directed. The mixture was added to 6-well plates. After 48 h of siRNA or plasmid transfection, qRT-PCR and Western blotting were performed to detect the changes in PTN at the mRNA and protein levels.

Collagen gel contraction assay

The contractile ability of the cells was assessed by collagen gel contraction assay (Cell Biolabs, USA). Cells with plasmid/siRNA transfection were harvested and re-suspended in medium. Collagen, 5 × DMEM, and neutralization solution were mixed according to the instructions. The cell suspension was mixed 2:8 with the working solution. Then 0.5 mL of the mixture was immediately transferred to 24-well plates (final cell density: 2 × 106 cells/well) and incubated for 1 h. After gel polymerization, 1 mL of medium was added to the top of the gel and incubation continued. After 48 h, the gel was separated from the Wells using a sterile spatula. Photographs were taken 48 h after separation and gel shrinkage was analyzed using Image J software (United States National Institutes of Health).

Drug treatment

To explore the effect of estrogen on PTN, estradiol (MedChemExpress, CHN) was diluted to 10− 4M, 10− 3M, and 10− 2M in DMSO and the estradiol diluent was added to the medium to make the medium estradiol final concentration 10− 7M, 10− 6M, and 10− 5M. An equal volume of 0.1% DMSO was added to the control group (0 M). Cells were collected at 24 h after estradiol treatment and used for subsequent experiments. With regard to AKT activator SC79, it was added to the culture medium after siRNAs transfection to activate AKT (at a concentration of 4 µg/ml) [25]. Cells in the untreated group were incubated with an equivalent amount of 0.1% DMSO. Cell viability and apoptosis were detected using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) and flow cytometry, respectively. The extracellular PTN protein levels were measured by ELISA Kit (ELK1873, ELK Biotechnology CO., Ltd, Wuhan, CHN) according to the standard protocols.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Forty-eight hours after plasmid transfection, cells were harvested to the EP tube containing cell lysis buffer and proteasome inhibitor and then lysed on ice for a half-hour. The mixture was next centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. A small amount of supernatant was used as an input for Western blot analysis. Magnetic beads bound to the corresponding antibody were added to the remaining supernatant and slowly shaken overnight at 4 °C. Antigen-antibody magnetic beads were conjugated. After the immunoprecipitation reaction, the magnetic bead conjugate was washed with buffer. Then 15 µL of 1 × SDS buffer was added, boiled at 100 °C for 10 min, and proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. Finally, Western blotting was performed to identify the interacting proteins.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Human, rat and mice prostate tissues were embedded in paraffin after being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 to 36 h and then cut into 5 μm sections. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and visualized by light microscope (Leica, Germany). In each sample, we analyzed three regions (×200) at random under magnification.

Masson’s trichrome staining

The rat and mice prostate tissue sections were also stained using Masson’s trichrome staining. Staining was detected by light microscope. Prostate smooth muscle (SM) cells, collagen fibers, and epithelial cells were stained red, blue, and orange, respectively. In each sample, we analyzed three regions (×200) at random under magnification.

Tissue microarrays (TMA) construction

TMA was constructed according to the method previously reported by our group [25, 30]. Briefly, paraffin-embedded specimens were sectioned and stained with H&E. Representative areas of these sections were then evaluated and confirmed by a senior pathologist. One core (1.5 mm diameter) was removed from each paraffin-embedded sample and inserted into a blank paraffin block. The resultant TMA was cut into 4 μm sections.

Tissue immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and clinical correlation analysis

The rat and mice prostate tissue sections or TMA sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated using descending gradient ethanol concentrations. Antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked according to the instructions. Sections were then incubated with PTN primary antibody, following secondary antibody incubation. Finally, sections were observed with a light microscope. PTN expression was quantified by two pathologists in a blinded manner. The percentage of target protein positive area was then measured using Image J software. In addition, we performed Pearson correlation analysis between the relative expression of PTN and various clinical parameters.

Data analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments. Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA were used for statistical analysis using SPSS statistical software (IBM, 23.0), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The expression pattern and localization of PTN in prostate tissues and prostate cells

As shown in Fig. 1, our previous cDNA microarray data (GSE119195) were analyzed and PTN was discovered as one of the upregulated genes in BPH (Fig. 1A, Table S5). Also, Western Blot and qRT-PCR revealed that PTN mRNA and protein levels were up-regulated in BPH tissues (Fig. 1B, C). The histomorphology of BPH and normal prostate tissue was confirmed by H&E staining (Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence showed that PTN was localized in both prostatic stroma and prostatic epithelium. Consistently, the BPH tissue possessed stronger fluorescence intensity than that in normal prostate tissue (Fig. 1D). As a control, immunofluorescence staining showed that PTN was abundantly expressed in normal mammary cell line MCF10A (Fig. S1). Also, immunofluorescence staining identified the expression of PTN in WPMY-1, RWPE-1, and BPH-1 cells (Fig. 1E).

PTN modulates cell apoptosis and cell proliferation in cultured human prostate cells

PTN-knockdown and PTN-overexpression cell models were established with siRNAs and plasmids, respectively. The knockdown and overexpression efficiency of si-1, si-2, si-3 and plasmids was assessed by qPCR (Fig. 2A, B). Due to high knockdown efficiency, si2 and si3 were employed for the subsequent experiments. Moreover, Western Blot validated the efficiency of PTN silencing and overexpression at protein level (Fig. 2C, D). Furthermore, flow cytometry revealed increased apoptotic rates in PTN-knockdown cells (Fig. 2E) but decreased rates in PTN-overexpressing cells (Fig. 2F). Consistently, PTN knockdown upregulated the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2 (Fig. 2G), whereas PTN overexpression produced opposite effects (Fig. 2H). Moreover, CCK-8 assay validated that PTN deficiency inhibited the cell proliferation (Fig. 2I), while its overproduction promoted cell survival (Fig. 2J). Also, flow cytometry analysis showed no obvious changes of cell cycle after PTN overexpression or knockdown (Fig. 2K, L).

PTN regulates cell proliferation and apoptosis via AKT phosphorylation

To explore how PTN modulates cell apoptosis and proliferation, the classical receptor of PTN (PTPRZ1) and its downstream AKT level were next detected. As shown in Fig. 3A, PTPRZ1 expression and the AKT phosphorylation level were attenuated with PTN-knockdown (Fig. 3A) while amplified with plasmid transfection (Fig. 3B) in both cell lines. Moreover, the AKT agonist SC79 rescued increased cell apoptosis and decreased cell proliferation triggered by PTN deficiency (Fig. 3C, D). Also, the alterations of apoptosis-associated proteins and AKT phosphorylation level were recovered by SC79 (Fig. 3E).

PTN enhances stromal cell contraction and promotes fibrosis process

WPMY-1 cell contractility was detected with a collagen gel contraction assay. It was determined that PTN-knockdown attenuates while PTN-overexpression enhances contraction significantly (Fig. 4A, B). Also, myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK) expression and myosin light chain 20 (MLC20) phosphorylation level declined after PTN disruption (Fig. 4C). In contrast, they were increased after PTN overproduction (Fig. 4D). Additionally, SMMHC, NMMHC-A, and NMMHC- B expression decreased significantly in PTN-knockdown WPMY-1 cells, as well as the fibrosis markers (α-SMA and collagen-Ⅰ) (Fig. 4E). Parallelly, increase of these proteins was observed after PTN overproduction (Fig. 4F). However, no changes were shown in the NMMHC-C expression (Fig. 4E, F).

PTN modulates cell contraction and fibrosis through RhoA/ROCK1/2 axis

We further investigated the expression of RhoA and ROCK/2 in PTN-knockdown and PTN-overexpression WPMY-1 cells. PTN deficiency decreased, but PTN-plasmid enhanced, the expression of RhoA and ROCK1/2 (Fig. 5A, B). Also, RhoA-siRNA could reverse the enhanced cell contraction induced by PTN overproduction (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the amplified MLC20 phosphorylation level, MLCK, and RhoA/ROCK1/2 signaling in our PTN-overexpression cell model were recovered by RhoA silencing (Fig. 5D). Meanwhile, the alteration of myosin isoforms (SMMHC, NMMHC-A, NMMHC-B) and fibrosis markers were restored by RhoA-siRNA (Fig. 5E).

Estradiol augments PTN expression and modulates cell biological processes via α Erα in WPMY-1 cells

To validate the stimulatory effect of estrogen on PTN, WPMY-1 and BPH-1 cells were pretreated with different doses of estradiol. As shown in Fig. 6A, the expression of PTN in WPMY-1 cells was increased at doses of 10− 7M, 10− 6M, and 10− 5M, especially at 10− 6M (Fig. 6A). However, estradiol showed no effect on PTN in BPH-1 cells (Fig. 6A). Hence, the WPMY-1 cell line and the estradiol dose of 10− 6M were employed. Western Blot further validated the increase of PTN with 10− 6M estradiol treatment (Fig. 6B). Also, due to difference in PTN transcript and translation levels, we used time-course experiments to explore whether temporal kinetic and secretory effects contributed to the inconsistency. Results showed that the differences in the temporal kinetics of transcription and translation after estrogen treatment of WPMY-1 are mainly concentrated between 0 and 24 h. ELISA indicated that PTN secretion mainly occurs between 24 and 48 h (Fig. S2). Immunofluorescence co-localization showed that PTN co-localized with ERα in prostate tissue and WPMY-1cells (Fig. 6C). Also, with FLAG-PTN and HA-ERα overexpression in 293T cells, the interaction between PTN and ERα was detected via co-immunoprecipitation analyses (Fig. 6D). Similarly, endogenous PTN efficiently interacted with ERα in WPMY-1 cells. Additionally, a decrease in apoptosis, an increase in cell proliferation, and no changes in cell cycle were found with estradiol treatment (Fig. 6E-G). The apoptosis-associated proteins were also altered accordingly (Fig. S3). Furthermore, the collagen gel contraction assay and Western Blot revealed enhanced cell contraction and increased fibrosis by 10− 6M estradiol (Fig. 6H, I). We then examined contraction related proteins and found MLCK expression and MLC20 phosphorylation increased with estradiol pretreatment (Fig. S4A). Meanwhile, SMMHC, NMMHC-A, and NMMHC-B also increased after estradiol pretreatment (Fig. S4B).

The BPH-models induced by T plus E2 and rPTN were validated by the increased ventral prostate volume and by H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 7A-C; Table 2). The weight of ventral prostate was obviously increased in all these BPH animals when compared with controls (Fig. 7A; Table 2). However, there was no difference between T-E2 (100:10) group and T-E2 (100:1) group in the ventral prostate weight or prostate index (Fig. 7A; Table 2). H&E staining showed enlarged prostate gland lumen and increased epithelial cell layers in T-E2 (100:10) group, T-E2 (100:1), and rPTN group, accompanied with papillary processes in the lumen (Fig. 7B). As shown in Fig. 7C, Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated epithelium, collagen fibers and SM all increased with epithelium more significantly increased (Fig. 7C). Moreover, the T-E2 (100:10) group had more collagen and SM components compared with the T-E2 (100:1) group (Fig. 7C). Also, immunofluorescence indicated that the positive rate of Ki-67 was significantly increased in the other three groups compared with the control (Fig. 7D). Consistent with the ventral prostate weight, the Ki-67 positive rate in T-E2 (100:10) group presented no difference to T-E2 (100:1) group (Fig. 7D). Additionally, the IHC staining validated that the expression of PTN was amplified in the T-E2 (100:1), T-E2 (100:10), and rPTN groups. Moreover, the PTN expression in T-E2 (100:10) group was higher than that in T-E2 (100:1) group (Fig. 7E). Also, immunoblots showed that PTN expression was increased in the T-E2 (100:1), T-E2 (100:10), and rPTN groups, and the expression T-E2 (100:10) group was higher than that in T-E2 (100:1) group (Fig. 7F). As shown in Fig. 7G, comparison with the control group, the antiapoptotic protein Bcl2 increased and the proapoptotic protein Bax declined in other three groups (Fig. 7G). We further found no changes in apoptosis-associated protein level between T-E2 (100:10) and T-E2 (100:1) group (Fig. 7G). Regarding the contraction and fibrosis related proteins, there were slight elevation in the T-E2 (100:1) group and apparent increases in the T-E2 (100:10) and rPTN groups (Fig. 7H), with no alteration of NMMHC-C observed between each group (Fig. 7H).

Discussion

Our current study showed that PTN is located in both prostate stroma and epithelium and is up-regulated in the hyperplastic prostate. We further demonstrated that PTN modulates cell apoptosis and proliferation via AKT phosphorylation and regulates cell contractility and fibrosis through RhoA/ROCK1/2 signaling. Additionally, our novel data shows estrogen mediates the effect of PTN on stromal cell fibrosis and contractility.

PTN is expressed in a variety of organs such as prostate, testis, and bladder [15]. In the current study, immunofluorescence staining showed that PTN is abundantly present in the epithelium and stroma of the human prostate. Similarly, PTN is strongly expressed in cultured human BPH-1and WPMY-1. As a potential oncogene, this molecule is also upregulated in many neoplastic diseases [31, 32]. Orr et al. observed that PTN was most abundant in the newborn and perinatal period then decreasing until adulthood, suggesting its involvement in embryonic development and senility [33]. Nevertheless, the function of PTN in prostatic hyperplasia has not been fully elucidated. BPH is regarded to be a “reawakening” of embryonic processes with aging being one of the main pathogenic factors [34]. We thus hypothesized that PTN might drive the onset and progression of BPH. Indeed, the elevation of PTN in BPH tissues was validated both at mRNA and protein levels in the ccurrent study. Also, upregulation of PTN in BPH tissues was confirmed in our previous cDNA microarray (GSE119195) [12].

To investigate whether PTN regulates the biological behavior of prostate cells, PTN-knockdown and PTN-overexpression cell models were established. Interestingly, we observed the discrepancy between transcriptional and protein expression levels following PTN overexpression. Indeed, previous study also revealed the GRP78, BMP5, and S100A4 that regulate BPH progression were increased by hundreds of folds in transcriptional levels after overexpression, while the protein levels were only increased by a few folds [25, 30, 35]. This may be attributed to post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms or changes in protein stability, which will be intriguing to be further investigated. PTN is regarded as a key regulator in cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis in a series of tissues [36, 37]. Specifically, upregulation of PTN in hepatocellular carcinoma amplified cell proliferation and metastasis [38]. Also, inhibition of PTN in multiple myeloma (MM) decreased the survival and expanded apoptosis in MM cell lines [39]. Consistent with the above findings, our current data shows an increase of cell apoptosis ratio and inhibition of cell growth in PTN-knockdown cell models. On the other hand, the PTN-overexpression cell models possessed greater proliferative capacity and a lower apoptosis ratio compared with the controls. Meanwhile, the apoptosis-associated proteins were highly altered, specifically, the proapoptotic protein Bax was augmented and the antiapoptotic protein Bcl2 was reduced in PTN-knockdown cell models. These proteins control the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [40]. Again, there was a decrease of Bax and an increase of Bcl2 in the PTN-overexpression models. It is well established that the cell cycle is a key regulator of cell proliferation and apoptosis imbalance [41]. In retinal pigment epithelium cells and human umbilical vascular endothelia cells, silencing PTN could inhibit cell proliferation and promote cell apoptosis via cell cycle arrest [42]. Interestingly, no cell cycle changes were found in cells with either PTN silenced or overexpressed, suggesting that the pro-proliferation effect of PTN on human prostate cells did not involve cell cycle arrest. This differential effect of PTN on cell cycle arrest may be attributed to the different cell species. PTPRZ1 is one of the first reported PTN receptors and is most often implicated in some of the important biological functions of PTN [15, 43]. PTN binding to PTPRZ1 induces dimerization of active D1 domain, thereby inhibiting PTPRZ1 phosphatase activity. This further enhances phosphorylation of downstream targets including AKT [15]. In glioblastoma, there is a significant positive correlation between PTN and PTPRZ1 [44]. Tumor-associated macrophages secrete abundant PTN which stimulates glioma stem cells proliferation through PTPRZ1 and thereby promotes glioblastoma growth [44]. Moreover, PTN binding to PTPRZ1 could subsequently enhance cell differentiation by activating the AKT pathway in oligodendrocyte precursor cells [45]. Consistently, we found that PTN augmented PTPRZ1 expression and AKT phosphorylation in prostate cells. Indeed, AKT phosphorylation was involved in prostate cell proliferation and ultimately resulted in BPH [3, 25, 28]. Previous publications confirmed that phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K), the upstream protein of AKT, was phosphorylated to p-PI3K and then induced AKT phosphorylation, which finally upregulated a number of transcription factors to facilitate protein synthesis and strengthen cell proliferation and cell survival [46]. We further used the AKT agonist SC79 [25], which could enhance AKT phosphorylation via binding to the PH domain of AKT, to treat PTN silenced prostate cells. It was found that the decreased AKT phosphorylation level, decreased cell proliferation, and increased cell apoptosis induced by PTN knockdown were entirely reversed by SC79, as well as the expression of altered apoptosis-related proteins. Therefore, we deduced that PTN could induce cell proliferation/apoptosis through binding to PTPRZ1 and phosphorylating AKT in prostate.

Prostatic tension is another critical factor in the progression of BPH [3, 47]. To determine whether PTN regulates prostate tension alterations, a collagen gel contraction assay was utilized. It revealed a reduced contractile capacity of human prostate stromal cells after PTN silencing while an elevated contractility was observed in the PTN-overexpression model. Furthermore, enhanced and attenuated MLCK levels and MLC20 phosphorylation levels were confirmed in the PTN-overexpression and PTN-knockdown models, respectively. Mechanistically, PTN-induced tension alterations might involve MLCK activation and MLC20 phosphorylation. Generally, prostatic tension is regulated by α1-adrenoceptors, and up to 50% of the total urethral pressure in BPH patients is attributed to α-adrenoceptor mediated muscle tone [48, 49]. Adrenergic activation increases intracellular Ca²⁺, which binds calmodulin to activate MLCK. This triggers MLC20 phosphorylation, inducing actin-myosin filament sliding and contraction [3, 50]. Adrenergic activation enhanced calcium sensitivity, subsequently triggering the Rho/ROCK pathway to amplify MLC20 phosphorylation and sustain contractile force [3]. It was reported that PTN colocalized with SMMHC [51]. In keloid formation, SMMHC significantly increases and then greatly promotes tissue fibrosis, thus increasing tissue tension and reducing tissue compliance [52]. Indeed, we have observed the alterations of Collagen-Ⅰ and α-SMA, as well the contraction related biomarkers after PTN disruption or overexpression. However, it is interesting to note that NMMHC-C was not changed after PTN interference. Actually, SMMHC, NMMHC-A and NMMHC-B are essential for SM contraction (active tension) in eukaryotes and are highly expressed in BPH tissues [5, 7, 53]. With regard to the fibrosis, it is associated with the passive tension of the prostate. We therefore speculated that PTN could modulate cell contraction and fibrosis, which subsequently regulate the prostatic tension and ultimately drive the progression of BPH.

In addition, it is reported that the Rho/ROCK pathway participates in PTN-PTPRZ signaling and prostate contraction [11, 23, 54, 55]. Publication showed PTN formed a complex with PTPRZ1 and then activated RhoA/ROCK signaling to promote glioma cell migration and invasion [23]. Moreover, a recent study implicated the activation of RhoA/ROCK signaling in enhancing the contraction of prostate cells [11]. Currently, our study found that PTN overexpression greatly amplifies PTPRZ1 expression, as well as proteins associated with RhoA/ROCK pathway. In contrast, silencing RhoA counteracted the cell contraction induced by PTN as did the MLCK expression, MLC20 phosphorylation, contraction related proteins, and fibrosis-related proteins. In line with our findings, the ROCK inhibitor Y-27,632 suppressed electrical field stimulation-induced prostatic strips contraction, demonstrating RhoA/ROCK signaling mediates prostatic contraction [56]. Furthermore, our previous studies discovered that the amplification of the Rho/ROCK system resulted in increased fibrosis, enhanced myosin expression, and elevated MLC20 phosphorylation in the prostate [3, 11]. Therefore, we supposed that PTN binding to PTPRZ1 activated RhoA/ROCK signaling and further enhanced myosin accumulation and prostate fibrosis, which ultimately triggered BPH through dynamic factors. Meanwhile, RhoA/ROCK activation upregulated MLCK expression and MLC20 phosphorylation, promoting contractility generation and maintenance .

Subsequently, estrogen was found to promote PTN production in WPMY-1cells in present research. Similar to the PTN overexpression cell model, there were also inconsistency in the levels of transcription and translation at 24 h after estradiol treatment. In addition, we excluded the influence of temporal dynamics and secretory effects through time course experiments. Therefore, we believe that the inconsistency between the level of transcription and translation at 24 h may be due to post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms or changes in protein stability, which needs to be further investigated. With increasing age, serum androgen levels in men decrease gradually while estrogen levels remain constant or decrease slightly, resulting in increasing estrogen/androgen ratio in plasma [10]. In addition, a positive association between plasma estrogen levels and the risk of BPH has been demonstrated [57, 58]. In previous investigation, the increase of stroma in rat prostate was determined after castration, which was attributed to the elevation of the estrogen/androgen ratio [3]. Indeed, estrogen exerts its biological function via binding to ERα and ERβ. Previous publication have indicated that ERα is mainly expressed in prostate stromal cells, while ERβ is mainly expressed in prostate epithelial cells [10]. Therefore, ERα was explored and the interaction between PTN and ERα was determined in current study. We suggested that the stimulatory effect of estrogen is mediated by ERα. Consistent with our finding, Xi et al. demonstrated that estrogen promotes PTN expression in mice osteoblasts [22]. We further detected the biological functions that changed after estradiol treatment in WPMY-1 cells. Consistent with the aforementioned alterations induced by PTN overproduction, we found that the apoptosis rate was decreased, the survival was enhanced, and the contractility was increased after estradiol treatment. Meanwhile, prostate fibrosis biomarkers and contraction related proteins were amplified. Similarly, previous studies have reported that estrogen promotes stromal cell proliferation by binding to ERα [10, 59]. Moreover, estrogen is believed to enhance prostatic fibrosis and thereby accelerate the BPH progression [60]. Although the biological regulation of estrogen and the associated signaling pathways are extensive [10], our original data implied that estrogen modulates the biological phenotype changes of prostate stromal cells at least in part through ERα/PTN signaling.

In order to better integrate with clinical practice, TMA was employed. Results showed that IPSS score, PV, fPSA, fPSA /tPSA were correlated with PTN expression levels. Actually, with PTN expression increasing, the proliferation of prostate stroma and epithelium is active, and the deposition of collagen in the stroma is accumulated, which triggers the dynamic and static factors of BPH. Consequently, an increase in PV and IPSS score occurred.

Finally, in vitro studies were translated into in vivo experiments. rPTN was used to mimic PTN overexpression [61]. Consistent with previous studies [29], prostatic hyperplasia were observed in the T-E2(100:1) group. In addition, we found a slight elevation of the SM component in the T-E2(100:1) group, which could be caused by the increased PTN expression resulting from estradiol treatment (29, 66). Compared with the T-E2(100:1) group, the T-E2(100:10) group had a stronger increase in SM components and collagen deposition, which may be attributed to the amplified expression of PTN. So did the contraction related proteins and fibrosis biomarkers. As aforementioned, our in vitro experiments confirmed that PTN could promote the production of myosin and fibrosis proteins in prostate stromal cells. Consistently, we observed the SM components elevation, collagen deposition, myosin and fibrosis biomarker increase in the rPTN group. Moreover, we found that SM components paralleled PTN expression. So did the contraction related proteins and fibrosis biomarkers. Regarding the proliferation level, there was enhanced proliferation capacity in the T-E2(100:1) group, T-E2(100:10) group, and rPTN group. Interestingly, unparallel with the PTN level, there is no different proliferation capability between the T-E2(100:1) group and T-E2(100:10) group. In our previous study, androgen treatment could significantly increase the hyperplasia of epithelial components in rats [35]. Unlike androgens, estrogen promotes stromal cell proliferation by binding to ERα while inhibiting epithelial cell proliferation via binding to ERβ [10]. We speculated that androgen induced significant epithelial components proliferation in T-E2(100:1) group and T-E2(100:10) group. Thus, despite that the stromal components were partially altered due to different estrogen levels, the overall level of proliferation was not significantly changed nor were the apoptosis related proteins. In addition, PTN knockout mice were employed. Consistently, depletion of PTN resulted in decreased endothelial components, SM and collagen fibers, further suggesting that PTN regulates the development and progression of BPH. Consistent with the results in rats, significant prostatic hyperplasia was found in both T plus E2 administrated PTN+/+ mice. But interestingly, our PTN−/− mice with T plus E2 treatment also showed prostatic hyperplasia. Indeed, it is widely accepted that androgen and estrogen could induce prostatic hyperplasia. Hormones play a wide range of roles in vivo. Hormonal induction of BPH is a very complex process. In addition to the increase of PTN, there are also other proteins that promote BPH such as S100A4, SMO, GRP78, and GPX3. Thus, even following PTN depletion, the development of hormone-induced benign prostatic hyperplasia could not be fully prevented [25, 35, 62, 63].

Current study revealed PTN was up-regulated in BPH tissues and it could modulate cell apoptosis and proliferation via AKT phosphorylation and regulate cell contraction and fibrosis process by RhoA/ROCK signaling. Therefore, PTN could be rediscovered as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for BPH. Previous study revealed high levels of PTN in Alzheimer’s disease patients, suggesting that PTN may be a novel marker for Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis [64]. In addition, Chang et al. uncovered that PTN may be a major factor in the malignant progression of breast cancer, and the overexpression of PTN leads to increased and rapid tumor growth [65]. However, the existing studies on PTN are all preclinical studies, and potential drugs related to PTN are limited. Thus, it would be intriguing to explore PTN system associated therapeutic agents for the treatment of BPH in the future.

Conclusion

Collectively, our current data establishes PTN as a novel pathogenic regulator in BPH. Our original findings demonstrate that PTN is significantly upregulated in human BPH tissues and exhibits distinct localization within both the prostatic epithelium and stroma. Crucially, PTN exhibits dual pro-hyperplastic mechanisms: driving dynamic factors via the RhoA/ROCK1/2 axis and regulating static factors through AKT phosphorylation. Furthermore, we identify estrogen as a key upstream regulator of these pathways. These findings add a new layer to our understanding of BPH etiology. PTN and its associated signaling pathways (RhoA/ROCK and AKT) emerge as highly promising and previously unexplored therapeutic targets for the development of novel, mechanism-based interventions aimed at halting BPH progression and improving clinical management.
