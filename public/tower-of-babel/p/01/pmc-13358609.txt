Structural and functional brain changes following diverse exercise interventions in Parkinson’s disease: an MRI-based narrative review
Authors: Hunyoung Ha, Heehyun Shin, Kiwon Park, Ryul Kim, Kyeongho Byun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]This narrative review synthesizes magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence from exercise intervention studies in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD), focusing on structural and functional brain changes associated with motor and cognitive outcomes. We also propose an interpretative framework in which exercise-related neuroplasticity in PD is understood as region- and task-dependent reorganization across cognitive–motor networks.[Methods]PubMed and Web of Science were searched for interventional studies in patients with PD that examined the effects of exercise-based training on motor and/or cognitive outcomes and included MRI-based assessments of brain structure or function. Studies were eligible if they used a randomized controlled or crossover design and were published in English.[Results]Eighteen interventional trials met the inclusion criteria. Structural MRI findings were limited but suggested subtle region-specific changes in the cerebellum, putamen, and hippocampus rather than a uniform global structural response. Resting-state fMRI studies indicated distributed reorganization across both motor and extramotor networks, including the sensorimotor, salience, default mode, frontoparietal, attentional, and medial temporal systems. Task-based fMRI studies further suggested that exercise-related neural adaptation in PD is strongly shaped by task demand, with reduced prefrontal recruitment observed during complex gait-related conditions and increased recruitment of control-related regions during motor imagery or internally simulated tasks.[Conclusion]Exercise interventions in PD may influence motor and cognitive outcomes through neuroplastic changes that are better characterized by circuit-specific structural remodeling, context-dependent resting-state network retuning, and task-dependent functional reallocation rather than by a single uniform neural response.

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cardinal motor symptoms, such as tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability [1]. Among these manifestations, gait impairment is particularly disabling because it directly increases fall risk and compromises functional independence [2]. Importantly, gait dysfunction in patients with PD cannot be understood solely on the basis of motor deficits. Increasing evidence indicates that gait control in PD is strongly influenced by cognitive processes, particularly under attention-demanding conditions, such as dual-task walking, and that freezing of gait (FoG) may emerge when cognitive control mechanisms fail to compensate for impaired automatic motor control [3,4]. Accordingly, PD-related gait dysfunction is increasingly viewed as a manifestation of disrupted cognitive–motor integration rather than a purely motor disturbance [5].

Cognitive impairment is a common and clinically important nonmotor symptom of PD that affects attention, executive function, memory, and visuospatial ability, even in the early stages of the disease [6-8]. These deficits are relevant because they can exacerbate gait instability and mobility limitations [6,7]. Neurobiological studies further suggest that PD-related cognitive dysfunction reflects not only nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration but also broader pathological processes involving cholinergic dysfunction, Lewy-related pathology, and structural and functional brain alterations across distributed cortical–subcortical systems [9-11]. Therefore, these observations support the need to understand motor and cognitive dysfunction in PD within a shared neural framework.

Exercise has emerged as a promising nonpharmacological strategy for improving both motor and cognitive outcomes in patients with PD [12]. Exercise and habitual physical activity may influence neural efficiency and cognitive–motor performance, including reduced prefrontal demand during walking and more favorable brain network organization [13,14]. In addition, observational neuroimaging studies have shown that higher cardiovascular fitness and physical activity are associated with preserved thalamic volume, stronger intrinsic functional connectivity in cognitive–motor networks, altered striatal dopaminergic function, and better cognitive performance in patients with PD [15-18]. These findings raise the possibility that the behavioral benefits of exercise may be mediated, at least in part, by neuroplastic changes in the brain systems that support cognitive and motor control.

However, the available neuroimaging literature remains fragmented. Numerous studies have focused on observational associations between physical activity or fitness and brain markers or on wearable neuroimaging studies centered on prefrontal cortical responses during gait [13–18]. By contrast, less attention has been paid to whether exercise interventions induce measurable structural (i.e., changes in brain morphology, volume, or structural organization) and functional (i.e., changes in neural activity, functional connectivity, or network-level dynamics) brain changes as assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in patients with PD. In this review, these complementary dimensions are considered distinct but related expressions of exercise-related neuroplasticity. This question is important because emerging intervention studies using structural MRI, resting-state fMRI, and task-based fMRI suggest that exercise may induce region-specific and task-dependent neuroplastic adaptations involving the cerebellum, basal ganglia, hippocampus, salience, default mode, frontoparietal, and cognitive–motor control networks [19-24].

Therefore, this narrative review aims to synthesize MRI findings from exercise intervention studies in patients with PD and examine how exercise-related brain changes may be related to motor and cognitive outcomes. We hypothesize that exercise does not induce a single uniform neural response but rather promotes region- and task-dependent neuroplastic reorganization across cognitive–motor networks relevant to gait, balance, executive function, and other cognitive–motor behaviors.

METHODS

A structured literature search was conducted using PubMed and Web of Science on February 23, 2026. Search terms related to Parkinson’s disease, exercise, cognition, and neuroimaging were used. Studies were included if they (1) used a randomized controlled or crossover design, (2) recruited patients with PD, (3) included exercise-based interventions targeting motor and/or nonmotor outcomes, particularly cognitive outcomes, (4) used MRI-based neuroimaging to assess structural and/or functional brain outcomes, and (5) were published in English.

The initial search yielded 119 records (PubMed, 69; Web of Science, 50). After removing 27 duplicates, 92 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 34 were excluded (31 because of irrelevant study design, two because of irrelevant topics, such as COVID-19, and one was not written in English), leaving 58 records for full-text assessment. During full-text screening, 40 records were excluded according to the predefined exclusion criteria: 19 involved irrelevant populations (including patients with Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, or Huntington’s disease), 15 did not include an exercise intervention, and six did not use neuroimaging methods (including structural and/or functional MRI). Consequently, 18 interventional trials met all inclusion criteria and were included in this review. RESULTS

Structural MRI Evidence for Exercise-Induced Neuroplasticity in PD

Structural MRI studies of exercise interventions in PD remain limited, but the available evidence suggests that exercise may induce region-specific neuroplastic changes rather than a single uniform structural response. A proof-of-concept study by Sehm et al. (2014) demonstrated that 6 weeks (1 session/week, 45 min/session) of dynamic balance training improved balance performance in patients with PD and was accompanied by time-dependent gray matter changes in the right cerebellum (p < 0.05, corrected). Additionally, performance improvements were positively correlated with gray matter alterations in regions implicated in sensorimotor processing, motor learning, and compensatory control. By contrast, Albrecht et al. (2021), using a randomized controlled design with a 10-week highly challenging balance and gait program (HiBalance), found no significant group-by-time effects on balance, gait speed, or whole-brain gray matter volume (FWE < 0.05). However, the HiBalance group showed increased left putamen volume after training, together with changes in the structural covariance network topology, including stronger thalamic–cerebellar connectivity. Together, these findings indicate that balance- and gait-oriented interventions may influence cerebellar, sensorimotor, and basal ganglia-related systems in patients with PD, although such changes may be subtle and not always detectable in conventional whole-brain pre–post volumetric analyses.

A complementary pattern was reported by Schaeffer et al. (2022), who examined a 6-week moderate-intensity exergaming program (three sessions/week; aerobic and coordinative). In that study, patients with PD showed significant increases in left hippocampal volume and selected subfields (CA1, CA4/DG, and subiculum), together with a significant increase in serum BDNF levels over time. These findings extend the structural MRI literature beyond motor control networks and indicate the involvement of memory-related structures implicated in cognitive vulnerability in PD. However, current evidence does not demonstrate a consistent association between structural brain changes and measurable behavioral improvement across studies, and no correlation was observed between BDNF changes and hippocampal volume in the exergaming study. Resting-State Network Reorganization Following Exercise and Training Interventions in PD

Resting-state fMRI intervention studies in PD suggest that exercise or training may induce functional reorganization not only within motor-related networks but also across networks involved in cognitive control and attention. One recurring pattern is that gait- and motor-oriented interventions are accompanied by a reorganization of the sensorimotor and motor control systems. Droby et al. (2020) reported that both treadmill training (TT) alone and TT combined with virtual reality (VR) were associated with increased connectivity within the sensorimotor network, together with decreased connectivity within the basal ganglia network, executive control network, and frontostriatal network; in the TT + VR group, additional changes were observed in the supplementary motor area (SMA), right precentral gyrus, and a cerebellar network region. Similarly, Lench et al. (2021) reported that gait training combined with paired inhibitory stimulation reduced SMA connectivity with regions related to executive and attentional processing in patients with FoG. In a related study, a cognitively challenging agility-based intervention for patients with FoG was associated with changes in the cortical–brainstem locomotor axis. A randomized crossover trial by King et al. (2020) reported improvements in FoG-related outcomes, including reduced dual-task cost on gait speed, together with reduced right SMA–pedunculopontine nucleus resting-state functional connectivity after exercise, but not after education. By contrast, Messa et al. (2019) reported that a specialized upper limb exercise intervention was associated with improved motor symptoms, balance, and postural control, together with increased connectivity involving the sensorimotor cortex, M1–superior frontal gyrus, and left cerebellar crus II. Long-term Tai Chi training was also associated with changes in motor-relevant resting-state networks in PD. Li et al. (2022) demonstrated that Tai Chi training was associated with better balance, gait, and motor outcomes and that improvements in Berg Balance Scale scores were related to enhanced visual network function, whereas improvements in UPDRS scores were associated with enhanced default mode network function. As illustrated in Figure 2, motor- and gait-focused interventions were more commonly associated with reorganization of the locomotor and sensorimotor systems, whereas cognitively enriched or multimodal interventions were more frequently associated with reorganization of higher-order cognitive–motor networks. However, these patterns were not strictly domain-specific, and the direction and extent of connectivity changes appeared to vary according to the intervention type and behavioral target. A complementary pattern was observed in studies examining cognition-related resting-state networks. Leocadi et al. (2024) found that dual-task gait/balance training combined with action observation and motor imagery (AOT-MI) was associated with increased connectivity within the anterior salience network, particularly in the left anterior prefrontal cortex (BA10), together with reduced connectivity within the anterior default mode network, right executive control network, and precuneus network. Compared with the dual-task training alone group, the dual-task + AOT-MI group showed reduced anterior default mode network connectivity, which was associated with better set-shifting performance. Hajebrahimi et al. (2022) reported that VR-based exergaming produced greater improvements in general cognition, delayed visual recall, and naming performance than conventional exercise, together with increased activity in the precuneus, a region closely related to the posterior default mode network and memory-related processing. Kim et al. (2022) revealed that robot-assisted gait training was associated with greater reductions in coupling between the visual and dorsal attention networks, bilateral frontoparietal networks, and auditory and medial temporal networks, whereas improvements in cognitive dual-task interference were related to changes in visual–medial temporal coupling. Finally, Johansson et al. (2022) demonstrated that aerobic exercise improved cardiorespiratory fitness and oculomotor cognitive control, with increased connectivity between the right frontoparietal network and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. A related pattern was observed in a 1-year randomized study of Tai Chi that focused on nonmotor symptoms. Li et al. (2024) reported that Tai Chi improved cognition and daytime sleepiness and that improved performance on the Parkinson’s Disease Cognitive Rating Scale, particularly frontal cortical scores, was associated with enhanced somatomotor network function. Collectively, these findings indicate that exercise-related resting-state plasticity in PD extends beyond the motor circuitry alone and involves extramotor networks, including the salience, default mode, frontoparietal, attentional, and medial temporal systems, which are relevant to cognitive function and cognitive–motor control. These patterns were not strictly domain-specific, suggesting that exercise-related resting-state plasticity in Parkinson’s disease is better characterized by distributed and flexible reallocation across cognitive–motor systems than by a single recovery pathway.

Task-fMRI Signatures of Exercise- and Training-Induced Functional Reallocation in Cognitive–Motor Control of Gait in PD

Task-based fMRI intervention studies have primarily used paradigms that operationalize the cognitive–motor control demands relevant to gait, including (i) dual-task interference during lower limb motor execution, (ii) obstacle negotiation via gait motor imagery, and (iii) anticipatory postural adjustment (APA; preparatory postural control before stepping) control as a proxy for step initiation and turning. In patients with PD with postural instability and gait disorder, Sarasso et al. (2021) randomized participants to dual-task gait/balance training with or without AOT-MI, and fMRI was performed during a motor task and dual task, with the study reporting robust clinical improvement in mobility and executive function, with additional gains in gait and balance (notably turning) in the AOT-MI arm. In a separate RCT using a gait/balance motor imagery fMRI task, Sarasso et al. (2023) examined training-related changes in motor imagery ability and associated activation patterns. Complementing these approaches, Maidan et al. (2017) compared TT alone with TT + VR, using an fMRI imagery paradigm of clear-path versus obstacle-path walking to examine training-specific changes in activation under complex walking demands. Finally, in patients with FoG, Silva-Batista et al. (2020) evaluated adapted resistance training with instability versus traditional motor rehabilitation and measured BOLD changes during a simulated APA task, directly targeting FoG-linked deficits in postural preparation and inhibition.

Across exercise- and training-based interventions, a convergent neural pattern consistent with greater efficiency/automaticity restoration is the reduction in prefrontal engagement during cognitively demanding motor control. In patients with PD with postural instability and gait disorder, Sarasso et al. (2021) reported that, relative to dual-task training alone, adding AOT-MI was associated with reduced frontal recruitment and increased cerebellar engagement during task fMRI after training. In Maidan et al. (2017), training-related activation differed by mode; after TT + VR, participants showed lower activation than those in the TT group in Brodmann area 10 and the inferior frontal gyrus during obstacle negotiation imagery, and the authors concluded that motor cognitive training decreased reliance on frontal regions, consistent with increased efficiency. Collectively, interventions that embed explicit cognitive–motor challenges (dual-task practice and VR obstacle negotiation) appear to shift task-evoked recruitment away from frontal executive regions toward a more efficient task-evoked configuration during complex gait-relevant demands.

Second, complementary patterns reflect optimized cognitive control/compensatory engagement when the task emphasizes internal simulation, monitoring, and imagery rather than overt gait execution. In the motor-imagery sub-study, Sarasso et al. (2021) reported that increased recruitment was observed after training within the anterior cingulate and other frontal/temporal territories and motor cerebellar regions during the MI fMRI task. This pattern is most parsimoniously interpreted as task-demand-dependent “up-regulation” of control/monitoring nodes supporting improved internal motor representation, rather than as a universal shift toward reduced frontal engagement. Importantly, this provides a mechanistic basis for why increases in anterior cingulate cortex recruitment can coexist with the efficiency-like pattern described previously, depending on whether the fMRI task primarily probes performance efficiency under interference (dual-task/obstacle context) or internal simulation and monitoring (MI context).

When mapped to behavioral outcomes, reduced frontal recruitment was most consistently observed in studies that reported improvements in complex mobility or gait-related performance. In the Maidan RCT, TT + VR targeted both the motor and cognitive aspects of ambulation, and post-training differences in frontal recruitment emerged during obstacle-negotiation imagery [21]. In patients with FoG, clinically meaningful improvements extend beyond gait measures to inhibition and APAs. Silva-Batista et al. (2020) reported that both groups trained for 12 weeks with matched session frequency and duration, but only adapted resistance training with instability incorporated unstable devices with progressive increases in motor complexity. By contrast, Myers et al. (2018) used treadmill, tango, and stretching interventions without a motor imagery component, and training-related changes in gait or task-evoked activation were not the primary findings of the analysis. Together, these studies suggest that task-fMRI responses may vary according to both the intervention content and patient phenotype.

Collectively, these task-fMRI intervention studies indicate that gait-related improvements in PD are not associated with a single uniform pattern of cortical changes. Instead, the direction of functional reallocation appears to vary according to task demands, intervention content, and patient phenotype. Reduced prefrontal recruitment was observed in studies emphasizing complex gait-related performance under interference [21], whereas increased recruitment of cognitive control regions, such as the anterior cingulate cortex, was reported when interventions targeted motor imagery and internal simulations [34]. In addition, FoG-focused interventions that progressively increased motor complexity under unstable conditions were associated with changes in APA-related task processing [35]. Overall, these findings support the presence of heterogeneous, task-dependent functional reorganization following exercise and training interventions in patients with PD. Interventions involving complex gait performance under interference were associated with reduced frontal recruitment, whereas interventions using motor imagery or internal simulation were associated with increased frontal, temporal, and cerebellar recruitment. DISCUSSION

This narrative review suggests that exercise interventions in PD are associated with neuroplastic adaptations across multiple levels of brain organization rather than a single uniform neural response. Although the available evidence remains heterogeneous, the overall pattern indicates that exercise may influence both brain structure and function in ways relevant to motor and cognitive outcomes. Structural MRI findings indicate subtle but potentially meaningful region-specific changes in motor- and memory-related areas, whereas resting-state fMRI studies demonstrate reorganization across both motor and extramotor networks implicated in cognitive control, attention, and cognitive–motor integration. Task-based fMRI studies have further suggested that exercise-related brain adaptation is highly task-dependent, with some interventions associated with reduced reliance on prefrontal executive regions during complex gait-related demands and others associated with increased recruitment of control-related regions during imagery or monitoring tasks. Collectively, these findings support the view that exercise in PD may promote adaptive reorganization of cognitive–motor neural systems, but that the direction and expression of these changes depend on the imaging modality, intervention content, and behavioral context.

Structural MRI findings do not indicate a robust global restorative effect of exercise in PD but do suggest subtle region-specific plasticity in circuits relevant to motor adaptation and cognitive–motor function [19,22,25]. In the intervention studies reviewed here, exercise-related structural changes were observed primarily in the cerebellum, putamen, and hippocampus rather than as uniform whole-brain volumetric alterations, suggesting that the structural response to training in PD may be anatomically selective rather than widespread [19,22,25]. This pattern is not entirely unexpected, as exercise-related structural remodeling is often modest and regionally specific, particularly over relatively short intervention periods. In healthy older adults, higher habitual physical activity and aerobic exercise have been linked most consistently to hippocampal preservation or enlargement rather than to diffuse global brain changes [37,38]. In PD, however, the anatomical expression of exercise-related structural plasticity may be shaped by disease-specific vulnerability within the motor and cognitive–motor systems [39,40]. From this perspective, cerebellar and putaminal changes after balance- and gait-oriented training are neurobiologically plausible because these regions contribute to sensorimotor integration, postural adaptation, and automatic motor regulation, all of which are compromised in gait dysfunction in PD [19,25,41]. Similarly, hippocampal enlargement after exergaming may reflect the engagement of memory- and learning-related systems, extending the structural effects of exercise beyond motor circuitry alone [22,37]. However, the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain because direct associations between structural brain changes and behavioral improvements were inconsistent across the included studies [19,22,25]. Therefore, the current structural MRI literature is best interpreted not as evidence of widespread structural recovery but as an early indication that exercise may induce circuit-specific remodeling in training-relevant regions in PD.

Resting-state fMRI findings suggest that exercise in PD induces context-dependent retuning of large-scale cognitive–motor networks rather than a single canonical recovery mechanism. Motor- and gait-oriented interventions were more often associated with reorganization of the sensorimotor, supplementary motor, cerebellar, and locomotor-control systems, consistent with the idea that exercise may support partial restoration or redistribution of neural processes related to gait automaticity and motor regulation in PD [4,5,26-30,41]. Moreover, cognitively enriched or multimodal interventions more frequently modulated extramotor networks, including the salience, default mode, frontoparietal, attentional, and medial temporal systems, indicating that exercise-related resting-state plasticity in PD extends beyond motor circuitry alone [16,18,20,24,31-33]. Importantly, the direction of connectivity change was not uniform across studies. Some interventions were associated with strengthened coupling within task-relevant networks, whereas others were associated with reduced connectivity between networks, which may reflect less efficient or potentially maladaptive coordination. Collectively, these findings suggest that exercise does not simply increase functional connectivity in a nonspecific manner but may instead optimize intrinsic brain organization by strengthening relevant interactions while reducing unnecessary or maladaptive coupling [16,18,20]. In this sense, resting-state plasticity after exercise in PD may reflect flexible reallocation across cognitive–motor systems, with some interventions favoring more efficient locomotor control and others enhancing the coordination of higher-order control networks relevant to cognition and dual-task performance. Accordingly, the current rs-fMRI literature argues against a strict one-to-one correspondence between a behavioral domain and single network signature and instead supports the view that exercise-related functional plasticity in PD emerges through distributed, task-relevant reorganization of large-scale brain networks [20,24,30,33].

Task-based fMRI findings suggest that exercise-related neural adaptation in PD is highly task-dependent and is best interpreted as functional reallocation according to task demand rather than as a single uniform direction of cortical change. In studies using complex gait-related performance under interference, exercise and training have been associated with reduced recruitment of prefrontal executive regions, which is consistent with greater neural efficiency and reduced executive burden during cognitively demanding motor control [21,23]. By contrast, when the task involved motor imagery, internal simulation, or monitoring, training was associated with increased recruitment of control-related regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex and frontal, temporal, and cerebellar areas, suggesting engagement of neural resources supporting internal motor representation and task-specific control [23,34]. Therefore, these patterns are more plausibly viewed as different forms of task-relevant adaptation than as conflicting results. In addition, FoG-focused training delivered under progressively unstable and complex conditions was associated with altered activation during APA processing, indicating that task-fMRI responses in PD are influenced not only by the intervention type but also by the behavioral process being tested [35]. By contrast, interventions without clear motor imagery or cognitively enriched components showed less distinctive task-evoked changes, suggesting that the expression of task-fMRI plasticity may vary according to intervention specificity and patient phenotype [36]. Collectively, the available studies indicate that exercise alters cognitive–motor control in PD through a context-sensitive redistribution of activity across task-relevant neural systems rather than through a single canonical cortical response.

The current literature remains limited by small sample sizes, substantial heterogeneity in intervention content and duration, variability in patient phenotypes, and differences in MRI modalities and task paradigms, all of which constrain direct comparisons across studies and limit mechanistic inference. Future research will require larger and more systematically designed trials that integrate structural MRI, resting-state fMRI, and task-based fMRI within the same intervention framework, along with well-characterized motor and cognitive outcomes. Greater attention should also be paid to phenotype- and intervention-specific effects, as the available evidence suggests that neural responses may differ according to gait phenotype, cognitive status, and cognitive–motor demands embedded within the training program. In addition, studies designed to test brain–behavior relationships more directly are necessary to determine whether exercise-related neural changes are reliably linked to clinically meaningful improvements. Such work will be important not only for clarifying the mechanisms of exercise-induced neuroplasticity in PD but also for guiding more precise and individualized exercise prescriptions targeting motor and cognitive–motor dysfunction.
