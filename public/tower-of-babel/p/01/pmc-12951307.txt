Factors affecting clinical trial participation in people with interstitial lung disease: a qualitative study
Authors: Mariana Hoffman, Simone Dal Corso, Jean Bremner, Anne E. Holland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical trials are the best method for evaluating new treatments for people with interstitial lung disease (ILD). However, recruitment in ILD clinical trials is often challenging. Certain trial characteristics may influence willingness to participate but this has not been investigated in people with ILD. This study aims to identify the factors that influence participation in clinical trials in people with ILD.MethodsThis is a qualitative study using thematic analysis. Participants with ILD were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Melbourne, Australia between 2023 and 2024. Interviews were conducted over the phone, recorded and transcribed. The study was approved by the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (598/22).Results17 participants with mild-to-moderate ILD were included. Overarching themes were: 1) reasons for participation, including development of new treatments, acceptable burden of participation and positive relationship with the research team; 2) reasons for refusing participation, consisting of disruption to daily life including financial burden, impact on health and uncertainty of benefits; and 3) experience when participating, including lack of communication and poor relationship with the research team. Participants described positive experiences (care and support from the research team) and negative experiences (discontinuation of treatment, insufficient information on trial outcomes) of trial participation.DiscussionPeople with ILD prefer trials that offer clear information and minimise disruption to their daily routines. A unique finding in this population is the importance placed on communication and support from the research team, as well as the desire to receive feedback about the trial's outcomes.

Background

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) refers to a group of lung conditions characterised by inflammation and/or fibrosis of the lung tissue. People with ILD typically experience a progressive decline in lung function, impaired gas exchange and reduced functional and exercise capacity [1]. Treatment strategies for ILD are complex and usually depend on the underlying cause [2]. In the case of fibrotic ILD, antifibrotic therapies can slow the progression of lung disease, but do not stabilise or reverse the condition. In addition, these medications are often associated with significant side effects and adverse events justifying the need for ongoing research for more effective and better-tolerated treatment options [3]. Non-pharmacological interventions such as pulmonary rehabilitation, self-management practices, patient education and end-of-life planning are also part of the management of ILD. These strategies should be evaluated trough clinical trials to ensure they effectively improve health-related quality of life for people living with ILD [4].

Clinical trials generate best evidence to guide best practice and improve care for patients with chronic diseases [5]. Clinical trials in ILD are essential to confirm treatment efficacy and safety, support drug approval and funding, and strengthen evidence to guide clinical decisions. However, recruitment to ILD clinical trials is challenging, like in many other rare diseases [6]. There has been limited research investigating the drivers of clinical trial participation in ILD. One small study using trial design simulations examined potential barriers to participation in a pharmacotherapy trial of pulmonary fibrosis (PF), demonstrated that patient-specific factors such as fatigue and breathlessness may influence willingness to participate [7]. However, factors that act as barriers or facilitators to participation in a broader range of ILD trials have not been investigated.

Reasons for participating in clinical trials have been explored in the oncology and cystic fibrosis fields. Personal factors such as age, sex and disease stage, trial-related factors like the amount of trial information provided, fear of being in an experiment, distance of the trial centre and financial resources influence the decision to participate in an oncology clinical trial[8]. In cystic fibrosis, patients refuse trial participation due to study centre location, the stipend they might receive or features of the trial design, such as inclusion of a placebo [9].

Reasons for participating in clinical trials in people with ILD may differ from people with cancer or cystic fibrosis due to differences in demographic characteristics, disease experience and the nature of their treatments; however, to date this has not been explored [9]. Thus, the aim of this study was to identify factors that influence participation in clinical trials in people with ILD.

Methods

Study design

This was a qualitative descriptive study involving a purposive sample of participants with ILD identified from a specialist ILD clinic at a tertiary hospital in Melbourne, Australia between January 2023 and April 2024 [10]. Participants were selected using purposive sampling based on their confirmed diagnosis of ILD and their attendance in a centre where patients are routinely offered the opportunity to participate in clinical trials, ensuring potential exposure to trial invitations regardless of prior participation.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited from a respiratory outpatient clinic of a tertiary hospital. Once eligibility was confirmed, clinicians provided initial information about participants. On the day of their scheduled appointment, eligible patients were approached in person by the ILD nurse while waiting to see their respiratory clinician. Inclusion criteria were adults with confirmed diagnosis of ILD who could read and speak English, with or without prior participation in clinical trials. The study was approved by the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (598/22). Verbal consent was obtained prior to all interviews.

Data collection

Interview-based methods were used in this study to explore individual experiences and attitudes. This approach allows in-depth exploration of participants’ views, helping uncover challenges and benefits of ILD research participation. Given the study's exploratory nature, and the complexity of personal experiences, qualitative interviews captured diverse and nuanced perspectives.

Individual interviews were conducted once via telephone. Participants completed the interviews from a location of their choice, on a day and time that was most convenient for them. The interviewer was based in a private office at the research centre to ensure confidentiality and minimise interruptions. All interviews were conducted on a one-to-one basis between the participant and the researcher. While we could not guarantee that participants were physically alone, they had confirmed during screening that they were able to respond independently without assistance from others. Telephone interviews were conducted and audio-recorded using a professional digital recorder by one of the research team members (MH). MH is a female research fellow with experience in conducting qualitative interviews with patients with chronic lung conditions. She was not part of the participant's treating clinical team and had no prior relationship with participants before the study commenced. Participants were informed about MH's role as a researcher and the purpose of the research. Demographic data were collected from participant's medical records to characterise the study sample.

A semi-structured interview with open-ended and probing questions was developed by the first author and used to understand the reasons why people with ILD accept or refuse participation in clinical trials (supplementary file S1). Interviews were iterative, with follow-up questions used to confirm or explore participant responses where needed. All participants were asked to reflect on their views about clinical trial participation regardless of whether they had accepted or refused participation in a clinical trial. For those with prior trial experience, questions focused on their actual experiences as well as factors that might have influenced or changed their decision to participate. For those without such experience, questions explored their willingness to participate and the factors that might influence that decision. Data analysis incorporated both perspectives, and all participants contributed to the development of at least one theme.

Electronic recordings were transcribed verbatim. Participants were offered to receive the transcriptions via mail or email. Data saturation was reached when interviewer observed no new themes during the final interviews.

Data analysis

We conducted an inductive, reflexive thematic analysis guided by the interview guide and following the approach described by and Nowell et al. [11] and Braun & Clarke [12]. This approach enabled a detailed and flexible exploration of participant's motivations, experiences and attitudes and allowing themes to be emerged naturally from the data rather than by pre-existing frameworks. The analysis followed six steps and supported an in-depth understanding of participant's views and experiences (supplementary file S2). Results are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist [13] (supplementary file S3).

Results

Participant characteristics

20 participants agreed to be contacted by the researcher and 17 consented to participate. Three participants declined to take part in the interviews. The average interview time was 15 min and ranged up to 33 min. Participants had mild-to-moderate ILD according to their lung function (mild: forced vital capacity (FVC) >75% and transfer factor for carbon monoxide (TLCO) >55% of predicted value; moderate: FVC 50–75% and TLCO 36–55% of predicted value) [14], and the majority were male and diagnosed with idiopathic PF (table 1). Four participants lived in regional or rural areas. 11 participants were married, two were single, one widowed, one divorced and two did not provide their marital status. Only one participant requested a copy of their interview transcript. However, no feedback or corrections were received from this participant.

Theme 1: reasons to participate in a clinical trial

A few interviewees had a genetic factor related to their condition, so they were keen to participate in clinical trials to improve future treatment for their children or relatives. The chance of improving their own symptoms while enrolled in a clinical trial was also mentioned by some participants. Some highlighted factors such as manageable visit schedules and proximity to the study centre to reduce travel time.

Some participants valued education and support from the research team, which improved their understanding and self-management, while many were more willing to join when details about the trial schedule, procedures and potential benefits and risks were clearly explained.

Theme 2: reasons to refuse clinical trial participation

Participants identified key reasons for likely refusal, grouped into three sub-themes (disruption to daily life and expected financial burden; impact on health and uncertainty of benefits; lack of communication and poor relationship with the research team) (table 3). Many participants found trials that disrupt daily life unattractive, while a few cited long assessment durations and time commitments as reasons to refuse participation. A few mentioned transportation costs, parking and work absences as other reasons to refuse trial participation.

Theme 2: reasons to refuse clinical trial participation

Some participants refused participation due to concerns that interventions might worsen their disease, cause illness, interfere with their current treatments or negatively impact their health, especially the ones stable on their medication. A few participants cited uncertain benefits as a reason to refuse participation.

Many participants cited lack of communication and insufficient trial information as reasons to refuse participation. Some participants wanted more details about assessment schedules, trial duration, potential benefits and possible side effects.

Theme 3: experiences of clinical trial participation

Many participants valued the support and communication with researchers, as well as treatment benefits that improved their symptoms, as positive aspects of trial participation.

Participants also agreed that a better relationship and communication with the research team could have improved their experience. A few felt they should have received more information before enrolment, and many were frustrated by the lack of feedback on their participation progress and assessment results. Some participants highlighted the importance of continuing trial medication after the study and receiving results, including whether the intervention was effective and how it might inform future research. Some also mentioned the lack of acknowledgement in the community about their participation.

Discussion

This is the first qualitative study to address reasons for participation in clinical trials in people with ILD. Our findings explored participant's motivations, experiences and attitudes. Results of the thematic analysis allowed themes to emerge directly from the data and showed that people with ILD are likely to participate in clinical trials if enough information about the trial is provided at the time of recruitment, and the research participation has lower interference with their daily routine. Participants preferred trials with fewer number of research assessments, shorter travel distance to the research centre and minor financial cost. They also appreciated the support and communication with the research team but were also disappointed by the lack of information on trial results and post-trial access to the clinical trial drug.

Our results highlight the importance of the ILD research team in facilitating trial participation. The interaction between the investigator and participant and the communication about the study beyond the time of consent is extremely important to patients. It impacts participants beyond the clinical trial, by increasing their understanding of the condition and improving their care and support. In a study of patients who had a stroke or myocardial infarction [15] this communication included content beyond what was written in the patient-facing study documentation, such as casual discussion about the risks and benefits of the trial [15]. As shown by our results, people with ILD valued this relationship and interaction when considering participation in clinical trials. Future studies should consider expanding study information delivery and improving investigator–participant interaction. Kraft et al. [16] suggested that research participants decisions rely on the interactions they have before, during and after the review of the informed consent. In this case researchers should pay attention to early interactions involving discussions with a participant's family, friends, physician and availability of other study materials to build the critical trustworthy relationship between participant and the research team [16].

Most participants described their accessibility to the research centre as a very important factor guiding their decision to enrol in clinical trials. This was a common opinion regardless of participants’ postcode and is consistent with a previous study where physicians reported reduced opportunity for clinical trial participation in people with PF who lived further away from major PF trials centres [17]. Transportation, time commitment and costs involved in clinical trials have been raised as a negative factor in adherence and recruitment success rate in other research groups such as people with positive HIV and cancer [18]. This result is consistent with a study by Nguyen et al. [19] that showed that in a large group of people with different chronic diseases (such as asthma, cardiovascular disease, diabetes and osteoporosis), 80% of participants prefer to have all or some research visits performed at their home. Decentralised clinical trials are not a novel concept, but they have expanded since the pandemic and use remote consent and electronic consent forms, as well as participant's electronic healthcare record data from sources such as registries or local general practitioners without having to do in-person trial assessment. It also supports the use of patient-reported outcomes via internet access, phones and wearables, allowing participants to provide data when it suits their schedule better, without the need to come to the research centre [20]. A recent study in a smaller sample of people with PF showed a preference for virtual visits and how it would alleviate the burden of travel to the research centre and the length of research assessments [7].

Unlike in cancer studies, people with ILD in this study did not fear participation in clinical trials [21]; however, some mentioned being unwilling to participate if side effects were expected or if they would have to stop taking their own medication. In cancer studies it is recommended that risks and benefits of the drug trial should be taken into consideration for eligibility and concomitant medications should only be a reason for exclusion if relevant known overlapping toxicity or drug interactions are present [22]. Antifibrotic therapy is standard care in ILD and it should neither exclude patients from ILD clinical trials nor require discontinuation for trial participation. Some participants in this study reported negative experiences with clinical trials, mainly related to the lack of feedback from the research team, the frustration of either not being informed about the trial result or losing access to the study drug after the trial ended. Some of these experiences can be modifiable with better research staff training and development of a plan to share the results and acknowledge participation of patients in the end of the study.

This study highlights some unique factors that may influence clinical trial participation for people with ILD that have not been reported in other lung diseases, particularly the role of the research team in providing clear study information and emotional support. This aspect appears especially important for individuals coping with uncertainty around disease progression and treatment options, which are factors not reported widely in other chronic diseases. A key strength of our study is the inclusion of participants from both rural and metropolitan areas, representing variation in access to care and demographic backgrounds, including differences in marital status. Although only a small number of participants were from rural areas, their perspectives provide some insight into geographical considerations relevant to clinical trial participation in the context of a metropolitan tertiary centre. The negative experience reported about clinical trial involvement is an important finding and should lead to better management of participants at study completion. Participants valued acknowledgement of their contribution, being informed of trial results, and in some cases, continuing access to the clinical trial treatment when it had a perceived benefit. Those are findings that seem to be novel in this population. This study has several limitations, including the absence of patients and patient organisation involvement in the study design, the single-centre recruitment, which may not reflect experiences of participants from other ILD centres limiting its external validity, and the relatively short duration of some telephone interviews, which may have constrained the breadth of discussion and participant openness. However, this approach improved accessibility for participants.

Additionally, participants were recruited from a tertiary hospital ILD clinic with a strong research culture and infrastructure, which may not reflect the experiences of individuals from other settings, particularly those with limited access to clinical trials due to geographical, functional or socioeconomic barriers. Other factors previously identified in other studies, such as socioeconomic status and access to treatment, were not reported in this study and could potentially have contributed to a better understanding of participation in clinical trials [23]. Moreover, thematic analysis is naturally interpretive, and findings derived from this approach are shaped by how researchers engage with the data. In this study, we adopted strategies such as independent coding, team discussions and consensus during theme development to enhance rigour and credibility.

Conclusion

Enhancing our understanding of the factors that facilitate clinical trial participation in ILD may provide new opportunities for researchers to optimise patient-centred trial design and improve recruitment and retention. Consideration of alternative clinical trial designs, including remote consent and assessments could potentially increase recruitment of participants with ILD to clinical trials. Future studies should consider refining research communication and patient awareness when recruiting and conducting clinical trials. It is important to have a plan on how participants will access the results of the study and whether they will have the possibility to keep using the intervention offered during the trial period.
