Current Practices and Expectations on the Environmental Impact of Interventional Cardiology: The GREEN‐CCF Study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile interventional cardiology has a significant environmental footprint, the level of engagement of professionals in mitigating their ecological impact remains unexplored. The GREEN‐CCF (Global Review of Environmental Expectations in Cardiac Catheterization in France) study aimed to assess current practices, perceptions, and expectations of interventional cardiology professionals in France about their environmental impact.MethodsAn online survey was developed and distributed to health care professionals involved in cardiology across France. The target population included senior cardiologists, cardiologists in training, nurses, medical imaging technicians, and research staff. The questionnaire explored participants' knowledge and motivation on environmental issues, current waste management practices in catheterization laboratories, and perceived barriers and potential solutions to reducing their professional environmental footprint.ResultsAmong 859 participants who submitted fully completed responses, 367 (43%) were involved in interventional cardiology. Despite a high level of motivation on the environmental issues, the participants reported low levels of knowledge and perceived motivation of their centers. More than 70% of participants reported discarding materials used during procedures, including packaging, plastics, and specific materials. The most frequently cited barrier to reduce the environmental impact of interventional cardiology practices was the difficulty in changing professional behaviors (47%). The most frequently proposed actions to reduce the environmental impact of interventional cardiology were improving the durability of materials (42%), implementing standardized recommendations (33%), reducing product packaging (48%), and adopting incentives or regulatory requirements (34%).ConclusionsThese findings reinforce the need of addressing environmental sustainability in interventional cardiology. While professionals are motivated and aware of the challenges, structural and institutional changes are needed to support future interventions.Keywords: catheterization laboratory, environment, health care, interventional cardiology, sustainabilitySubject Categories: Health Services, Quality and Outcomes

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In this nationwide survey, interventional cardiology professionals in France report high motivation to reduce the environmental footprint of catheterization laboratory activities, contrasting with limited knowledge on this topic, perception of low institutional engagement, and substantial waste generation during routine procedures.

Health care is one of the largest economic sectors in Western societies, contributing up to 10% of greenhouse gas emissions. 1 Hospitals are major consumers of vital resources and generate large amounts of waste through their various activities, thus directly contributing to air pollution. 2 While the evaluation of health care system performance is traditionally based on outcomes such as care quality, number of procedures performed, hospital stays, and financial costs, it is equally essential to quantify the environmental impact of health care activities due to their effect on health. 3 Analyzing the environmental footprint of health care activities is thus an undeniable challenge in the operation of our health care system.

Although operating rooms and interventional catheterization suites account for <6% of the hospital budget, they are responsible for nearly a third of total hospital waste. 4 Several medical specialties have begun assessing the carbon footprint of their activities, including cardiac, 5 orthopedic, 6 and digestive surgeons 7 ; nephrologists 8 ; and ophthalmologists. 9 In the field of cardiology, a recent study focused on evaluating the practices and expectations of practitioners to reduce the environmental impact of electrophysiology procedures in Europe. 10 This study revealed the significant motivation of European electrophysiologists to improve the sustainability of their products and reduce their professional environmental impact. Interventional cardiology, with its high volume of coronary and structural procedures, faces similar challenges, even though data on its environmental footprint remain scarce.

Thus, the GREEN‐CCF (Global Review of Environmental Expectations in Cardiac Catheterization in France) study aimed to analyze the current practices and expectations of cardiology health care professionals in France on the environmental impact of their activities, with a specific focus on those involved in interventional cardiology.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

The GREEN‐CCF study was conducted using an online survey specifically designed for health care professionals involved in cardiology in France. Target population included senior cardiologists, cardiologists in training, nurses, medical imaging technicians, and research professionals. Cardiologist in training refers to a physician who has completed general medical education and is undergoing specialized postgraduate training in cardiology, typically within a residency or fellowship program, under the supervision of certified cardiologists. Senior cardiologist refers to a fully licensed cardiology specialist, often holding a consultant or attending position, responsible for independent patient management and for supervising and mentoring junior cardiologists and trainees. To ensure a comprehensive perspective, a stratified sampling approach was applied on the basis of interventional cardiology practice, professional role (physician, nurse, other), type of institution (academic, public nonacademic, private), and geographic region (grouped by administrative area). The recruitment target was to achieve at least 50 responses per key stratum to ensure sufficient representation and enable subgroup analysis. We therefore aim for at least 600 completed surveys, of which at least 300 should come from interventional cardiologists.

All participants were invited to complete a comprehensive survey assessing the current practices and expectations on the environmental impact of cardiology. All participants gave their informed consent to participate in the study. The participant's details and responses remained strictly anonymous from the investigators. Informed consent from all participants was obtained at the beginning of the survey, after providing information on the study's objective, confidentiality, and data anonymization. The survey was designed to ensure that participants' personal data cannot be directly traced (no identification of participants via email, professional number, or other specific information). Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before survey completion. Institutional review board approval was not required for this study, as it consisted of a survey of health care professionals about their practices and opinions, did not involve patients or patient‐identifiable data, and was conducted in compliance with applicable General Data Protection Regulation requirements.

The questionnaire was distributed via the networks (mailing lists, social media) of the French working group of young cardiologists, the French Society of Cardiology, the Interventional Cardiology working group of the French Society of Cardiology, and other key national cardiology working groups of physicians and nurses (Collège National des Cardiologues Hospitaliers, Collège National des Cardiologues Français, Syndicat National de Cardiologie, Groupe Paramédical de la Société Française de Cardiologie).

Data Collection

Data were collected using the online survey platform SurveyMonkey (https://www.surveymonkey.com). This secure platform ensures participant confidentiality and data traceability. Data are hosted on certified servers as compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation. The survey remained open from November 21 to December 31, 2024.

Survey Development

A version of the questionnaire is provided in the Supplemental Methods. The questionnaire was initially drafted by a representative board from the French working group of young cardiologists, the French Society of Cardiology, and the Interventional Cardiology group of the French Society of Cardiology. It underwent extensive revision by a scientific committee composed of 17 experts involved in general cardiology care and interventional cardiology and representative of distinct geographic areas in France, different functions (cardiologists in training, senior cardiologists, and paramedical professionals), and various work environments (public academic hospital, public nonacademic hospital, private practice, and liberal practice). As no validated questionnaire was available to specifically address environmental sustainability practices in interventional cardiology, the survey instrument was specifically developed for the purpose of this study on the basis of previously published surveys in cardiovascular care sustainability and refined by the multidisciplinary expert panel to ensure relevance to catheterization laboratory–specific practices. This comprehensive writing and review process spanned 6 months, ensuring that the questionnaire met the highest methodological standards and accurately captured the diverse perspectives within the cardiology community. Multiple‐choice questions were prioritized to allow an appropriate statistical analysis. The survey was designed to take a maximum of 10 minutes to complete. A feasibility test was conducted on a pilot sample including 25 cardiologists before full deployment.

The survey was divided into 2 parts. The first part comprised 11 questions directed at health care professionals involved in various fields of cardiovascular medicine. The aim was to assess their level of knowledge on environmental issues, their current practices related to environmental sustainability, and their overall expectations on the environmental impact of their medical activities. The second part consisted of 15 questions specifically targeting professionals involved in interventional cardiology. This section aimed to thoroughly investigate current sustainability practices in catheterization laboratories and to explore the motivations and obstacles to reducing environmental impact.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Between‐group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test for samples with n>30, and Fisher's exact test for samples with n<30. A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were analyzed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

A total of 367 respondents (43%) reported being actively involved in interventional cardiology. The proportion of women in this subgroup did not significantly differ from those not involved in interventional cardiology (45% versus 51%; P=0.172). The most represented age group was 30 to 39 years (41%), and the predominant practice setting was public academic hospitals (52%). Knowledge and Motivation on Environmental Issues

Survey results concerning environmental knowledge and motivation are presented in Figure 1. While a majority of participants reported an intermediate or high level of knowledge about general environmental issues without difference between interventional and noninterventional professionals (P=0.93), less than half of respondents reported such a level of knowledge about the cardiovascular effects of air pollution, the carbon footprint of cardiology activities, and the possibility of reusing and recycling cardiology materials. One third of interventional cardiology participants reported intermediate or high levels of knowledge on the carbon footprint of cardiology activities (33% versus 26% respectively; P=0.04), and the possibility of reusing and recycling cardiology materials (37% versus 20%; P<0.001).

More than 80% of interventional cardiology respondents reported a high or very high level of motivation to reduce their environmental impact in both their personal lives and professional practice, with no significant differences from their noninterventional counterparts (P>0.05 for both comparisons). Furthermore, 84% of respondents in this group stated that they would be influenced in their choice of medical materials if manufacturers demonstrated strong environmental performance, without difference between interventional and noninterventional professionals (P=0.13). Despite these high levels of individual motivation, only 45% of interventional cardiology professionals and 36% of noninterventional professionals (P=0.01) perceived their institutions as being motivated to reduce environmental impact.

Fate of the Materials Used in Interventional Cardiology

The results of the survey on the fate of the materials used in interventional cardiology are reported in Figure 2. Participants predominantly reported discarding materials used during procedures. A large majority indicated that specific materials (85%), plastic items (79%), and packaging (71%) are discarded rather than recycled. Chemicals were discarded slightly less frequently, though still predominantly (66%), with 34% reportedly being recycled. Recycling rates were highest for plastic (21%) and packaging (17%). Notably, a small proportion of respondents reported not knowing the fate of specific materials (8%) and packaging (12%).

Perceived Obstacles and Proposed Solutions to Reduce Environmental Impact in the Catheterization Laboratory

Barriers to reducing the environmental impact of interventional cardiology practices, as reported by participants, are summarized in Figure 3. The most frequently cited obstacle was the difficulty in changing professional behaviors (47%), followed by procedural complexity (40%), and limited training (39%). Concerns related to infectious risk were reported by 37% of participants, while 36% pointed to a lack of support from hospital administration. Economic cost was the least commonly reported barrier, while still identified by 20% of respondents.

Figure 4 illustrates participants' perceived usefulness of various strategies to reduce the environmental impact of interventional cardiology. To limit their carbon footprint in the catheterization laboratory, respondents most frequently supported enhancing the sustainability of materials (42%) and the establishment of clear national or international environmental guidelines (35%). Regarding material sustainability, the most suggested actions included the implementation of standardized recommendations (33%) and the reuse of materials following appropriate sterilization procedures (32%). The most frequently proposed solutions to reduce waste volume were minimizing product packaging (48%) and optimizing shipping packaging (22%). Finally, to encourage the adoption of more environmentally friendly practices, participants highlighted the importance of regulatory incentives or requirements (34%), alongside education on best practices (33%).

Need for Climate Action to Protect Health

In addition to being a major political and societal issue, climate change is a public health problem due to its direct impact on human health and represents one of the greatest global health challenges of the current era. Rising global temperatures, extreme weather events, and air pollution are already contributing to increased cardiovascular morbidity and death, particularly through heat stress and pollution‐related disease. 11 Global warming and air pollution have well‐documented harmful effects on the cardiovascular system, particularly by amplifying inflammatory and thrombotic processes, increasing oxidative stress, and causing endothelial dysfunction. 12 , 13 These effects are associated with a significant reduction in life expectancy and account for nearly 9 million premature deaths worldwide each year. 12 The European Commission estimates the cost of premature deaths and diseases caused by air pollution at 1 trillion euros annually, which exceeds the costs attributed to tobacco and alcohol. 14 Air pollution is thus recognized as the world's largest environmental risk factor for cardiovascular disease and premature death.

Cardiologists stand at the crossroads of both the causes and consequences of climate change. While the health care sector contributes significantly to global greenhouse gas emissions, cardiology professionals also witness first‐hand the health impacts of environmental degradation. In this context, interventional cardiology, which is an energy‐ and material‐consumer specialty, has a particular responsibility. Reducing its environmental footprint is not only a matter of operational efficiency but also a commitment to public health. Addressing sustainability in interventional cardiology is thus essential to align the practice of cardiovascular medicine with its overall goal: to reduce the burden of cardiovascular diseases. 15 , 16

Current Practices and Expectations on the Environmental Impact of Interventional Cardiology

In line with previous studies in electrophysiology and cardiac surgery, 5 , 10 our results confirm a high level of personal motivation among health care professionals involved in interventional cardiology to reduce the environmental footprint of their activities. It is important to acknowledge that survey participation was voluntary, nonresponse bias being possible, and respondents may represent the most environmentally sensitized professionals. The high level of reported motivation should be extrapolated cautiously to the entire interventional cardiology community. This individual engagement contrasted with a relatively low institutional commitment reported by the respondents, highlighting a critical gap between awareness and actionable support within clinical environments. The relatively low perception of institutional engagement in environmental impact reduction is noteworthy. While numerous sustainability initiatives are currently promoted in French hospitals, these actions may not be directly perceived at the level of interventional cardiology practice, where regulatory constraints, device‐related requirements, and limited local autonomy may reduce the visibility of institutional policies.

A large majority of participants reported that packaging, procedure‐specific materials, plastics, and chemicals used in interventional cardiology are routinely discarded, while only a minority indicated that these materials are subjected to recycling. These findings echo those of Boussuge‐Roze et al, 10 who reported that the majority of electrophysiology catheters and their packaging are systematically discarded, with minimal reuse or recycling practices implemented across European centers. Notably, a nonnegligible proportion of respondents reported not knowing the fate of specific materials and packaging, highlighting a potential gap in awareness or documentation of waste management practices. In addition, the interpretation of waste management practices may be limited by the self‐reported nature of the survey. In particular, the reported recycling of chemical products may reflect confusion between regulated disposal pathways, waste segregation, and true recycling processes, as respondents may not always have precise knowledge of the final treatment of waste at the institutional level.

Altogether, these findings underscore the high degree of motivation of interventional cardiology professionals to reduce their personal and professional environmental impact and suggest opportunities to improve sustainability practices in interventional cardiology.

Barriers and Solutions to Reduce the Environmental Impact of the Catheterization Laboratories

Resistance to behavioral change, procedural complexity, and lack of training were identified as the 3 main barriers to reducing environmental impact in the catheterization laboratory. These systemic obstacles align with one of the most frequently proposed solutions: education on best practices, a strategy that could be relatively easy to implement within cardiovascular care pathways. However, more than one third of participants also cited the lack of prioritization of environmental sustainability by hospital administrations as a major obstacle in the reduction of environmental impact of interventional cardiology. These findings underscore the need for a coordinated, multilevel effort involving health care professionals, institutions, and policymakers to collectively reduce the environmental footprint of the health care system. Several initiatives aimed at reducing the environmental impact of cardiovascular care are already underway, including pilot reprocessing programs in electrophysiology, actions led by professional societies, and institutional policies promoted by national organizations such as the French Society of Cardiology. 17 These efforts indicate that the field has already begun to address sustainability issues, although they are not always perceived by involved practitioners.

Participants also expressed strong support for the reuse of medical materials when appropriate sterilization is feasible, as well as for reducing the volume of packaging. These actions align with the widely recognized “5R concept” (reduce, reuse, recycle, rethink, and research), 16 , 18 which provides a comprehensive framework for improving environmental sustainability in health care settings. Reducing waste by eliminating nonessential items from procedural kits appears to be an essential and immediately actionable step. In interventional cardiology, devices such as coronary angioplasty balloons are frequently packaged in bulky cardboard boxes and hard plastic shells, often accompanied by printed paper instructions, all contributing to excessive waste. Reuse of devices deemed safe for sterilization, following rigorous infection control protocols, also represents a possible strategy, particularly as infectious risk was not identified as a major fear in our study. Finally, recycling uncontaminated materials, including paper, plastics, and cardboard, could be implemented through clear and structured waste segregation protocols, provided appropriate recycling infrastructure is available. Collectively, these strategies offer tangible and effective ways to reduce the environmental footprint of catheterization laboratories.

Future Directions: A Call for Collective Actions on Environmental Sustainability in Interventional Cardiology

The growing awareness among interventional cardiology professionals of their environmental impact should now translate into coordinated, system‐wide action. Individual motivation, while essential, is insufficient in the absence of institutional leadership, regulatory support, and industrial accountability. The respondents of our survey highlighted the need for educational practice on this question. Structured training in eco‐responsible practices should be included in cardiology curricula and continuous medical education, empowering current and future operators to act as agents of change.

In addition, >80% of the participants reported that reducing environmental impact would influence their choice of materials used in clinical practice if manufacturers demonstrated strong performance in this area. These findings underscore a significant opportunity for the medical device industry to act as a catalyst for change, by integrating sustainability into product design, packaging, and life‐cycle transparency. The high level of responsiveness to environmental claims from manufacturers also suggests that clear labeling, third‐party certifications, or eco‐performance metrics could meaningfully influence procurement decisions in interventional cardiology. Ultimately, fostering an environmentally responsible supply chain will require stronger collaboration between clinicians and manufacturers, ensuring that sustainability becomes a competitive and ethical standard in the medical device market. Beyond individual motivation, many determinants of the environmental impact of interventional cardiology depend on industrial, logistical, and regulatory constraints, including device design, packaging, sterilization requirements, and supply chain organization. These structural factors may limit the ability of individual operators to implement meaningful changes in daily practice and should be accounted for. The results of the present study suggest that, despite increasing awareness, reduction of the environmental impact of interventional cardiology will likely require coordinated action involving health care professionals, scientific societies, industry, and regulatory bodies. Surveys may help identify priorities and barriers, thereby informing future recommendations and structural changes.

Future efforts should prioritize the integration of sustainability into clinical guidelines, procurement policies, and device development. Collaboration between health care providers, hospital administrators, manufacturers, and policymakers will be crucial to redesign supply chains, reduce reliance on single‐use items, invest in recyclable or reusable, and simplify regulations on the recycling process. Furthermore, implementing metrics to monitor environmental performance in catheterization laboratories could foster accountability and guide quality improvement. 16 As climate change continues to threaten global health, interventional cardiology must commit to aligning technological advancement with ecological responsibility. Sustainability should no longer be seen as optional but as a fundamental component of safe, high‐quality cardiovascular care.

Overall, the paradox between high individual motivation and the expectation of externally driven change may represent a central barrier to the transition toward more sustainable interventional cardiology. Meaningful progress will likely require coordinated action involving health care professionals, scientific societies, industry, and regulatory authorities rather than individual initiatives alone. It will also be necessary to associate repeated communication of coordinated actions to practitioners.

Study Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the survey was conducted exclusively in France, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other health care systems with different organizational structures, regulatory frameworks, and levels of environmental engagement. However, the findings provided offer novel perspectives that may inform broader reflections on sustainability in interventional cardiology. Second, the sample size remains relatively limited, and the results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than definitive. However, the respondent pool included a diverse range of professionals involved in interventional cardiology across various practice settings, suggesting reasonable representativeness for a developed health care system. Third, the use of a self‐administered online questionnaire introduces the possibility of sampling and response bias, with likely overrepresentation of individuals already sensitized to environmental issues, and underrepresentation of those less engaged with the topic. The open dissemination of the survey through professional networks and social media prevented determination of the exact number of individuals reached and precluded calculation of a response rate. However, France currently counts ≈1000 physicians involved in catheterization laboratory activity, according to national statistics. 19 The number of respondents represents a nonnegligible proportion (>20%) of the national interventional cardiology community. Overall, the potential response bias should be considered when interpreting the reported motivations and perceived barriers reducing the environmental impact of interventional cardiology. Finally, the use of predefined response options in the questions on the solutions to reduce the environmental impact of interventional cardiology allowed quantitative analysis but may have limited the identification of all relevant strategies. Open‐ended questions might have allowed the emergence of additional concepts such as reprocessing, reconditioning, or alternative sterilization pathways for interventional devices, and should be considered for future works.

Conclusions

The findings of the present study reinforce the urgency of addressing environmental sustainability in interventional cardiology. While professionals are motivated and aware of the challenges, this contrasts with perception of low institutional engagement. Structural and institutional changes are necessary to support meaningful, scalable interventions and should be associated with improved communication toward practitioners. Priorities should include implementation of standardized eco‐guidelines, investment in education and training, and stronger engagement from health care leadership. Sustainability in the catheterization laboratory should no longer be a secondary concern but an integrated component of quality care.
