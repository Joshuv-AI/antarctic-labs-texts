Presacral meningocele presenting after rupture into the retroperitoneal space with acute debilitating intracranial hypotension: illustrative case
Authors: Mahnoor Shafi, Ryan Gensler, Yuanxuan Xia, Timothy F. Witham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPresacral meningoceles are rare congenital cystic lesions caused by herniation of the thecal sac through an anterior sacral defect. Presentations range from asymptomatic to rupture and meningitis. Treatment aims to eliminate subarachnoid communication, recurrence, and complications.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report the case of a 21-year-old female who developed acute, debilitating, positional headaches requiring flat bed rest. MRI revealed a ruptured presacral meningocele with intra-abdominal fluid accumulation. She underwent surgical repair via a transsacral approach with sacroplasty and CSF diversion. The cyst was fenestrated, nerve roots were dissected, and a large dural defect was reconstructed with intra- and extradural repair. A tethered cord with a fatty filum terminale was released; the repair was reinforced with autologous fat graft, fibrin sealant, and synthetic hydrogel; and the sacroplasty was stabilized with titanium mini-plates. Postoperatively, the patient had immediate resolution of headaches and was able to ambulate upright. Five-month follow-up MRI confirmed cyst resolution with complete symptom relief.LESSONSThis case demonstrates successful management of a ruptured congenital presacral meningocele, a rare but debilitating condition. Rupture may cause intracranial hypotension or meningitis with severe symptoms. Surgical intervention provides definitive resolution. Meningoceles should be distinguished from perineural or Tarlov cysts, which differ in clinical behavior and surgical management. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26114

Presacral, or anterior sacral, meningoceles are rare congenital cystic lesions formed when the thecal sac protrudes through a developmental defect in the anterior sacrum.1 Since their first description in 1837, approximately 150 cases have been reported worldwide.2 They are commonly associated with congenital sacral dysraphism, including Currarino syndrome, and may also occur in hereditary sacral agenesis.2 Presacral meningoceles are primarily caused by congenital defects in sacral bone formation that allow anterior protrusion of the meningeal sac. Familial associations have been described, including neurofibromatosis, Marfan syndrome, and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.3 Acquired cases are rare but may occur following trauma or prior surgery.4

Clinical presentation is variable, with many cases discovered incidentally. Symptomatic patients may present with pelvic or low back pain, urinary obstruction, constipation, radiculopathy, or headaches.5 Neurological symptoms occur with nerve root involvement or tethered cord syndrome.6 Rarely, spontaneous rupture may occur.1 At present, only 6 cases of ruptured presacral meningocele have been reported.7–12 Rupture may create direct communication between the subarachnoid and presacral spaces, increasing the risk of bacterial meningitis.13 This risk is particularly high if rupture involves the rectum or vagina or follows transrectal or transvaginal aspiration or biopsy.13

Management depends on symptoms and risk of complications. Symptomatic, enlarging, or complicated meningoceles (e.g., infection, rupture, and neurological deficits) may require surgical intervention. The primary surgical goal is closure of the communication between the meningocele and the subarachnoid space, typically via a posterior approach.14 In a posterior transsacral approach, a partial sacrectomy or laminectomy creates access to the presacral space. A posterior sagittal approach, involving midline division of the sphincter complex, is more commonly used in pediatric cases or patients with Currarino syndrome.14

In this report, we describe a unique case of a large presacral meningocele that ruptured following sexual intercourse, resulting in debilitating positional low-pressure headaches. We discuss the radiographic findings, surgical approach, and clinical outcomes along with a relevant review of the literature.

Illustrative Case

Patient History

A 21-year-old female with a medical history notable for recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), urinary retention (not previously evaluated with formal urological testing), and constipation was evaluated as an outpatient with an MRI study, which demonstrated a large 16-cm pelvic cyst consistent with a meningocele or giant perineural/Tarlov cyst. Two months later, while arranging for outpatient neurosurgical consultation, she presented to the emergency department with intractable, positional low-pressure headaches (debilitating when upright, tolerable when lying supine/flat). The symptoms had an abrupt onset 30 minutes following sexual intercourse; she presented to the emergency department 2 days after the onset of the headache when symptoms became debilitating. She was treated with intravenous (IV) ketorolac, morphine, methylprednisolone sodium succinate, and ondansetron with improvement of her symptoms. The patient was also suspected to have a urinary tract infection due to burning micturition and was treated with IV ceftriaxone. A noncontrast head CT study was ordered that revealed no abnormalities. Following symptom improvement, the patient was sent home with pain medication as needed. Seven days later, her symptoms recurred and she presented to our institution’s emergency department. On physical examination, she did not appear to be in acute distress, and her neurological examination was unremarkable, without concerning findings such as loss of rectal tone or perineal anesthesia. However, she was unable to function in the upright position and was essentially bedbound.

Workup

A pelvic MRI study (Fig. 1A and B) demonstrated a new fluid collection anterior to the cyst that was not present on her initial outpatient MRI. A CT myelogram (Fig. 1C) showed that the lesion directly communicated with the thecal sac and suggested that the cyst had ruptured, with MRI findings also showing some fluid in the abdomen/retroperitoneal space with concomitant enlargement of the pelvic meningocele. Given her low-pressure headaches, CT venography and brain MRI with and without contrast were also performed. These did not show venous sinus thrombosis or hydrocephalus. However, there were findings of intracranial hypotension, including diffuse pachymeningeal thickening, engorged pituitary gland, distended right transverse sinus, and brainstem sag. The Bern score was 7, further supporting the diagnosis of intracranial hypotension.

Her neurological examination remained stable with continued positional headaches. Because of cyst rupture and intractable headaches, surgical management was recommended with a midline posterior surgical approach with S1–3 osteoplastic laminectomy/sacroplasty for fenestration of the cyst, and closure of any potential communication to the thecal sac.

It should be noted that urodynamic testing was not performed on this patient to distinguish between a neurogenic bladder and obstruction from the meningocele because the operating surgeon felt that surgery was the only option for the patient, and this needed to be done fairly expeditiously.

Operative Procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone, and EMG and sphincter monitoring electrodes were placed. The posterior lumbosacral region was prepped, and a lumbar drain was inserted at the L2–3 interspace to allow for CSF diversion postoperatively to protect the repair. During exposure, a defect of the posterior sacral elements at S3 consistent with spina bifida occulta was identified (Video 1), where a large cyst-like structure rose posteriorly and had eroded through the caudal posterior sacral elements. An S1–3 laminoplasty was performed with a bone scalpel, and the bone flap was lifted to expose the thecal sac and underlying sacral nerve roots.

A large anterior sacral bony defect was identified, revealing a giant cyst-like structure. The operating microscope was brought in, and the cyst was fenestrated, releasing clear CSF and exposing what appeared to be the outline of pelvic and/or abdominal organs through a large anterior bony defect. Careful dissection of the involved nerve roots was performed, including S2–4 bilaterally, with extension to S5 on the left. At the proximal sacral edge, a suspected CSF leak from the distal thecal sac prompted repair with interrupted 4-0 Nurolon sutures. However, since a leak was still suspected, an intradural exploration was also undertaken at the S1–2 level. A fatty filum terminale was identified that did not stimulate with bipolar interrogation and was coagulated and transected at S2.

Further inspection confirmed a defect at the distal thecal sac, continuous with the cyst. Reinforcing sutures were placed intradurally near the distal thecal sac to ensure watertight closure of this communication. An autologous fat graft was also placed on the inside of the caudal thecal sac and sealed in place with fibrin glue. A running 5-0 Prolene suture was used to close the midline durotomy. A bioabsorbable fibrin sealant patch was applied over both the cyst repair and dural closure. Laminoplasty was completed using titanium mini-plates to reconstruct the posterior sacral bony elements.

Postoperative Course

The immediate postoperative course was uneventful, and the lumbar drain was maintained with a drainage goal of 10 ml/hr. On postoperative day (POD) 2, however, the patient developed fever, leukocytosis, and hypotension. She was not meninigitic. Empiric IV vancomycin and cefepime were initiated. CSF fluid analysis was noninfectious, urinalysis was negative, and blood cultures remained without growth. Abdominal CT revealed pancolitis, and stool testing was positive for Clostridioides difficile. The patient continued therapy for the infection with antibiotics Metronidazole, cefipime, and oral vancomycin. Resolution of symptoms was achieved over the following 4 days. Her lumbar drain was clamped on POD 5 and removed on POD 6. She did not report any positional headaches. She was subsequently able to assume an upright posture and ambulate without difficulty.

Immediate postoperative noncontrast MRI (Fig. 2) revealed expected surgical changes, including a significant interval reduction in the size of the presacral meningocele, now measuring 6.1 × 10.9 × 4.7 cm. By 1.5 months postoperatively, she reported baseline bladder/bowel and sexual function with resolution of surgical pain. She required no postoperative analgesics and was able to return to school as a college student.

At her 5-month postoperative follow-up, MRI (Fig. 3) revealed resolution of the cyst and she returned to her baseline functional status. Literature Review

Online databases including PubMed, Ovid Medline, and Science Direct were used to conduct a comprehensive literature search to identify relevant studies and case reports pertaining to presacral meningoceles, Tarlov cysts, and associated complications. The initial phase of the search involved broad search terms such as “sacral meningocele” and “presacral meningocele” to gather general background information. This approach yielded a variety of studies, including general anatomy papers, incidental findings, and pediatric congenital cases, many of which were not directly relevant to the specific clinical context of our case.

A refined search targeted Boolean combinations to identify case reports and surgical series. The primary Boolean search terms included “Tarlov cyst” AND “presacral meningocele,” “meningocele rupture” AND “orthostatic headache,” “Tarlov cyst” AND “intracranial hypotension,” and “posterior approach” AND “dural repair” AND “sacroplasty.” This focused strategy helped identify literature directly addressing our case, including reports on meningocele rupture, associated headaches, and surgical management.

The search yielded 18 studies consisting of 72 patients (17 case reports and 1 case series of 55 individuals) (Table 1).10–12,15–29 Of the 72 total patients, 72.2% (n = 52) were female. Six studies described patients with presacral meningoceles, while the remaining detailed Tarlov cysts. All cases underwent surgical intervention and experienced partial to complete resolution of symptoms. Discussion

Observations

Overview of Case and Clinical Significance

Here, we describe the case of a patient with a large ruptured presacral meningocele that caused debilitating, positional low-pressure headaches. Since these lesions are rare, therapeutic options are controversial. In most cases, these lesions only become symptomatic when the cyst grows large enough to cause urinary obstruction, constipation, or radiculopathy. However, feared complications like meningitis and CSF leakage may occur after cyst rupture. Although 6 cases7 –12 of ruptured presacral meningoceles have been previously reported, the majority involved pediatric patients or individuals with underlying congenital syndromes and were largely descriptive in nature, or focused on imaging, with limited operative detail. Furthermore, intracranial hypotension has not clearly been documented in prior reports. In contrast, our case involves a young adult who experienced acute rupture following sexual intercourse, with radiographic confirmation of intracranial hypotension. In this video case report, a posterior transsacral approach for S1–3 sacroplasty was performed to fenestrate the cystic lesion, seal the communication between the thecal sac and cyst extradurally, and perform an intradural tethered cord/fatty filum release with additional repair of the dural defect. This approach was successful, with the patient’s cyst resolving over the 5-month follow-up course, and she returned to baseline functional status. To our knowledge, this is the first detailed report to combine confirmed intracranial hypotension with comprehensive operative management and video documentation of definitive repair in this setting.

Clinical Manifestations and Diagnostic Evaluation

Clinical manifestations of presacral meningoceles vary with symptoms, ranging from bowel/bladder and neurological symptoms to life-threatening meningitis and death.1 Indications for treatment vary based on the symptoms and risk of complications. For patients with asymptomatic or incidentally discovered meningoceles, observation with or without serial imaging is sufficient.30 Initial diagnostic evaluation relies on MR and CT imaging to delineate the anatomy, define the sacral defect, and assess the relationship with neural elements. In their studies, Ma et al., Yang et al., and Cunha et al. described the use of multimodal neural reconstruction MRI sequencing to precisely delineate the nerve roots, contents, and subtypes of sacral cysts, thus allowing for an optimized surgical approach in these patients and reinforcing the crucial role that MR-guided techniques play in the initial diagnostic workup and surgical planning of sacral cysts.31–33

Differentiating Presacral Meningoceles From Tarlov Cysts

Presacral meningoceles and Tarlov cysts are both cystic sacral lesions, making them prone to misidentification. However, it is crucial to distinguish between the two as their treatment approaches may differ. Presacral and sacral meningoceles are herniations of the meningeal sac (dura and arachnoid) through a dural defect and possibly sacral defect into the presacral space to form a CSF-filled cavity that does not contain nerve fibers.2 This is in contrast to Tarlov cysts/perineural cysts, which are dilations of the nerve root sheath (typically at the dorsal root ganglion) and contain CSF with nerve fibers in their wall.34 Diagnosis of Tarlov cysts is primarily based on MRI, where they appear as well-defined, fluid-filled lesions isointense to CSF on T2-weighted imaging, typically located along the dorsal nerve roots within the sacral canal. These cysts are frequently multiple and small, closely associated with nerve roots, and may occasionally be seen extending along or traversing the nerve roots.26,33,34 Presacral/sacral meningoceles, on the other hand, present as large solitary unilocular or multilocular cystic masses in or anterior to the sacrum, sometimes extending into the presacral space, and are not associated with a distinct nerve root.2 CT myelography can confirm the diagnosis of the cyst when communication with the subarachnoid space is unclear.33,34

Tarlov cysts can be managed conservatively unless symptomatic, with a stepwise escalation from observation to minimally invasive procedures (aspiration and fibrin glue) and surgery, if needed.35 Presacral meningoceles, on the other hand, are primarily managed surgically when symptomatic or enlarging, as conservative management is rarely effective and there is a risk of rupture, which can be life threatening. The goal of surgery is to obliterate the communication with the subarachnoid space, with techniques tailored to anatomy and nerve involvement.36

Complications of Presacral Meningoceles

Although few studies—6 in this systematic review—have reported rupture of presacral meningoceles, this remains a severe complication.7–12 Rupture of the cyst may be spontaneous, iatrogenic, or caused by increased intra-abdominal pressure. Cyst rupture has been reported in patients as young as 10 months.7 A case of a 45-year-old female who presented with spontaneous rupture of a giant presacral meningocele with signs and symptoms of intracranial hypotension has been reported, similar to the case presented herein.10 That patient had a history of Marfan syndrome and dural ectasia in the sacrum and presented with postural headaches, nausea, vomiting, and decreased abdominal fullness; however, she did not exhibit signs of meningitis.10 Yeh et al., on the other hand, described a case of iatrogenic rupture of a presacral meningocele that had initially been diagnosed as an ovarian cyst.11 The lesion was partially resected without complete dural repair, which resulted in postoperative CSF leakage and subsequent symptoms of intracranial hypotension.11 This misrepresentation reflects the care that must be taken to distinguish presacral meningoceles from similar masses in the region, such as ovarian and Tarlov cysts, to avoid possible rupture.

Surgical Intervention and Approach Selection

The main surgical approaches used in patients with presacral meningoceles include a posterior transsacral approach, posterior sagittal approach, anterior approach, and combined posterior and anterior approach.14 The most commonly used approach is the transsacral approach that was first published by Adson in 1938.37 This technique was used in our case, as it allows for direct visualization of the neural elements, is suitable for cases associated with tethered cord or nerve root involvement, and is considered low-risk. After selecting the approach, the connection between the meningocele and thecal sac is visualized by creating a pathway through the dura or cyst.

Complex and Multistage Surgical Scenarios

However, different approaches may be used depending on the circumstances. One study described a 46-year-old female with Marfan’s syndrome who experienced a ruptured presacral meningocele and bowel herniation into the thecal sac and spinal canal.10 Owing to concern for bowel injury and communication with the thecal sac, she underwent urgent surgery. This patient was managed with a multistage procedure: a posterior sacroplasty with titanium mesh reconstruction of the anterior sacral wall, followed by an anterior transabdominal resection of the meningocele.10

Mankotia and colleagues reported a case of a presacral meningocele with a CSF-containing fistulous tract between the meningocele and rectum.22 Progressive enlargement exerted pressure on the rectum, resulting in erosion and fistula formation.22 Six similar studies have reported presacral meningoceles associated with rectothecal fistulas.25,38–42 This highlights the risk associated with enlarging meningoceles, where outcomes can range from CSF leakage from the rectum to mucopurulent discharge and meningitis. In these cases, management included posterior approaches or laparotomy. Laparotomy was favored due to the friable nature of the dural sac and the ability to transpose omentum to cover the sacral defect. Phillips and colleagues similarly described the dura as friable, likely secondary to chronic CSF leakage and underlying connective tissue disorders, making CSF-tight closure challenging.39 Additional reinforcement with dural coverage has been described, as dura possesses immunomodulatory properties, stem cells, and growth factors that may aid healing where soft tissue support is limited.43 In our case, no meningocele–rectal fistula was present, and CSF-tight closure was achievable; therefore, a transsacral approach was used with placement of an intradural fat graft combined with a fibrin sealant and extradural synthetic gel. Such grafts have been shown to remain viable and remodel over time, reducing the risk of postoperative CSF leakage.44

Anatomical Considerations

Surgical strategies for presacral meningoceles are also tailored to the anatomical classification and nerve root involvement.4,14 For lesions without nerve root involvement, intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring is used to confirm lack of nerve root presence, and then meningoceles can be ligated. When functional roots are present, the nerve sleeve can be reconstructed or imbricated to preserve function.14 Overall, precise identification and ligation or closure of the communicating ostium between the thecal sac and meningocele is critical. This is often achieved with a muscle graft or nonpenetrating clips, and the cyst wall is generally left untreated (not imbricated or resected), as resection is unnecessary if the communication is securely closed, like in our illustrated case.31,45

A surgical series of 50 patients with sacral meningoceles published in 2011 noted that 28% of patients were found to have spinal cord tethering and associated thickened fatty filum.6 Histopathologically, the fatty infiltration and abnormal connective tissue composition increase traction on the conus medullaris and caudal spinal cord, contributing to tethering. The presence of a fatty filum is important in the context of a presacral meningocele as the coexistence of this lesion with a meningocele influences surgical planning.6

Lessons

We report the case of a patient with debilitating low-pressure headaches due to rupture of a large presacral meningocele. This case illustrates the clinical features and complications of giant presacral meningoceles, outlines the surgical strategy leading to symptom resolution, and includes a focused literature review. These lesions should be distinguished from Tarlov or perineural cysts, which require different management.
