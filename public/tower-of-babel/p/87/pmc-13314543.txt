Development of a Biphasic Modified‐Release Ibuprofen Tablet Formulation: Single‐Dose and Multiple‐Dose Pharmacokinetics and Food‐Effect Studies
Authors: Ludmila Kvapilová, Jiří Hofmann, Josef Černý, Tomáš Hauser, Mohammed Bouhajib, Martin Šíma, Ondřej Slanař
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIbuprofen is the most commonly used NSAID; however, its short biological half‐life, reflected in the short duration of pain relief, limits the comfortableness of its usage. This study evaluated the pharmacokinetic properties of a newly developed biphasic ibuprofen 400 mg immediate‐release/sustained‐release (IR/SR) modified‐release tablets (test formulation), which was developed with an aim to overcome a need for frequent dosing. Both single‐dose and multiple‐dose, open‐label, randomized, crossover studies compared the bioavailability of the test formulation with two Nurofen 200 mg, immediate‐release coated tablets (reference formulation) administered within 4 h intervals. In addition, the effect of food on the test formulation performance was evaluated in a single‐dose, open‐label, randomized, crossover, bioavailability study. Based on the 95% confidence intervals of the geometric mean ratios, the new test formulation exhibited equivalent (80.00–125.00%) total (AUCt,ss), peak (Cmax,ss), and minimum (Cmin,ss) steady‐state exposure compared to the reference product after repeated dosing. After a single dose of the test formulation, total (AUCt and AUC∞) and peak (Cmax0‐4) exposure were bioequivalent to the reference. Food intake did not significantly alter the rate or extent of drug absorption from this product. The study medication was well‐tolerated. This ibuprofen test formulation may represent a potentially advantageous therapeutic alternative, providing extended relief of mild to moderate pain. While these considerations are supported by pharmacokinetic data, further clinical evaluation may help to better characterize their relevance in practice. The formulation may be particularly suitable for conditions where less frequent dosing is desirable, such as dental and back pain, or nighttime pain.

Introduction

Ibuprofen, like other non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), is a medication that reduces pain, fever, and inflammation. It is indicated for the relief of migraine headache, back pain, dental pain, neuralgia, period pain, rheumatic and muscular pain, cold and flu symptoms, and to reduce inflammation and fever [1]. Introduced to the market in the late 1960s, ibuprofen was approved for over‐the‐counter (OTC) use in the 1980s and, since then, it became the most commonly used and prescribed NSAID [2]. Ibuprofen is a propionic acid derivative NSAID that has shown its action via inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis. It reduces inflammatory pain, swellings, and fever and also reversibly inhibits platelet aggregation [1].

Following oral administration, ibuprofen is rapidly and almost completely absorbed. Maximum plasma concentrations are observed 45 min after ingestion if taken on an empty stomach, while peak levels occur 1–2 h after administration with food [1]. Ibuprofen is rapidly distributed with a low apparent volume of distribution of 0.1–0.2 L/kg and is extensively bound to plasma albumin (> 98%) [3]. It is used as a racemic mixture of R‐(−) and S‐(+) enantiomers, and the latter, S‐(+) ibuprofen, is considered to be a more pharmacologically active substance. Cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2C9 has been identified as the major enzyme responsible for the formation of all oxidized metabolites of both enantiomers [3]. More than 80% of the dose is recovered in urine, mainly as carboxymetabolites and conjugated hydroxymetabolites. Bile and feces represent minor elimination routes [3]. The elimination half‐life of ibuprofen is approximately 2 h [1].

Relatively short plasma half‐life of ibuprofen requiring more frequent administration to maintain therapeutic plasma concentration over 24 h is one of the most clinically relevant shortcomings of this drug that makes its use less convenient, resulting in potential pain breakthroughs and a necessity of a more frequent dosing regimen.

Considering the above, the objective of this research was to develop a new biphasic drug formulation in the form of a bilayer tablet combining immediate‐release (IR) and sustained‐release (SR) properties and to describe its pharmacokinetic profile. The IR layer was designed to mimic the pharmacokinetic profile of a standard (200 mg) ibuprofen IR tablets, ensuring rapid absorption and onset of action. The SR layer was formulated to maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations over an extended dosing interval of 8 h, thereby reducing the frequency of administration compared to conventional IR products.

The pharmacokinetic profile of the novel formulation was evaluated in comparative bioavailability studies with Nurofen 200 mg immediate‐release coated tablets (Reckitt Benckiser NL Brands, the Netherlands), following both single‐ and multiple‐dose administration under fasted conditions. Additionally, the effect of food on the bioavailability of ibuprofen 400 mg IR/SR modified‐release tablets was examined after a single‐dose in healthy subjects. The safety and tolerability of the study treatments were also evaluated.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Test Formulation

The manufacturing of test formulation involved standard manufacturing processes. In brief, separate IR and SR granules were produced by wet granulation, a commonly used granulation process for ibuprofen formulations. The IR layer included excipients such as hydroxypropylcellulose, silicified microcrystalline cellulose, croscarmellose sodium, colloidal silica, and iron oxide red. The IR granules were blended with a mixture of lubricants (glycerol dibehenate and sodium stearyl fumarate) to form the IR part (red colored part of the product) of the mixture ready for compression. The SR layer included excipients such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, hydroxypropylcellulose, silicified microcrystalline cellulose, and colloidal silica. The SR granules were blended with a mixture of lubricants (glycerol dibehenate and sodium stearyl fumarate) to form the IR part (white colored part of the product) of the mixture ready for compression. The SR layer utilized hydroxypropyl methylcellulose as the release‐controlling polymer, along with similar excipients to ensure consistent matrix integrity and drug release kinetics. The blends were then compressed on a bilayer tablet press to obtain tablet cores. The cores were coated using a transparent film composed of hypromellose, macrogol 300, and talc to produce the final drug product.

Dissolution Experiment

Dissolution testing of the test and reference formulations was performed in media representing physiological pH conditions (pH 1.2, 4.5 and 6.8). The dissolution profiles obtained in phosphate buffer at pH 6.8 (prepared according to Ph.Eur. 5.17.1) were conducted in 900 mL medium using a standard USP II paddle apparatus (Sotax AG, Aesch, Switzerland) with sinker. Rotation speed was set to 50 rpm for 720 min (Ibuprofen IR + SR, modified‐release tablet) and 60 min (Nurofen 200 mg, coated tablet), respectively. Samples were automatically withdrawn, filtered through a 2.7 μm filter (Whatman glass microfiber grade GF/D) and analyzed by UV spectroscopy (Analytik Jena GmbH+Co. KG, Jena, Germany) at 221 nm in a 1 mm cuvette.

Human Subjects

All clinical studies (single‐dose and multiple‐dose comparative bioavailability study and single‐dose food effect study) were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Pharma Medica Research Inc. (Ontario, Canada). Studies were conducted between December 2021 and May 2022 at Pharma Medica Research Inc. (PMRI), in accordance with Good Clinical Practice, applicable regulatory requirements and the ethical principles that originate in the Declaration of Helsinki. All trial applications (file number HC6‐24‐c261942/control number 261942, file number HC6‐24‐c261943/control number 261943 and file number HC6‐24‐c257797/control number 257797) were reviewed by Health Canada's Therapeutic Products Directorate and studies did not commence until appropriate no objection letters were received. All subjects gave informed consent prior to study participation.

The eligibility of the subjects was based on demographics, medical history, vital signs, physical examination, and laboratory tests. Healthy, non‐smoking males and females, aged ≥ 18 years, with a body mass index of 18.5–30 kg/m2 and willing to use acceptable, effective methods of contraception were eligible to participate in these studies. Subjects were ineligible if they had a history of hypersensitivity and/or a contraindication to the study medication, positive test results for hepatitis B/C or human immunodeficiency virus, history or presence of chronic diseases. Subject's kidney and liver function tests, and coagulation tests had to be within normal range. Subject did not have a history of or concurrent abuse of alcohol or illicit drugs and did not take regular prescription and/or OTC medication 14 days prior to study participation.

Study Designs and Procedures

The single‐dose comparative bioavailability study was conducted in an open‐label, randomized, 2‐period, 2‐treatment, 2‐sequence crossover design. In each period, subjects received either a single dose of treatment A, consisting of 1 tablet of the test product (ibuprofen 400 mg IR/SR, modified‐release tablets), after an overnight fasting of at least 10 h, or a single dose of the treatment B, consisting of 2 tablets of the reference product (Nurofen 200 mg, coated tablets), 1 tablet administered after an overnight fasting of at least 10 h (0‐h) and 1 tablet (200 mg ibuprofen) administered at 4‐h after the 0‐h dose (total dose = 400 mg). The subject fasted for at least 1.5 h after the 4‐h drug administration. Subjects were randomly assigned to two treatment sequences (AB or BA) according to a randomization scheme [generated by procedure PLAN in SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, NC, USA)]. The washout between the 0‐h drug administrations for each subject was at least 7 days. Blood samples were collected in pre‐chilled 3 mL K2EDTA tubes prior to dosing (0‐h) and at 0.33, 0.67, 1.00, 1.33, 1.67, 2.00, 3.00, 4.00, 4.33, 4.67, 5.00, 5.33, 5.67, 6.00, 7.00, 8.00, 10.00, 12.00, 16.00 and 24.00 h after dosing. The blood samples were placed in an ice bath until centrifugation at 3000 RPM for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatant plasma was transferred to polypropylene tubes and stored at −80°C.

The bioavailability of the test product (treatment C) and an early formulation prototype (treatment D) in comparison to reference (treatment E) following repeated dosing was evaluated in an open‐label, multiple oral dose, randomized, 3‐period, 3‐treatment, 6‐sequence crossover study. In each period, repeated doses were administered as follows: treatment D or C: one tablet administered at 0, 8, 16, 24, and 32 h, treatment E: one tablet administered at 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, and 36 h. For all treatments, subjects fasted for at least 2 h prior to each drug administration until at least 1 h post‐dose, except the 0‐h dose, which was administered after an overnight fasting of at least 10 h. Subjects were randomly assigned to six sequences (DCE, DEC, CDE, CED, EDC and ECD) using the SAS 9.4 software. The washout between the last drug administration in a period and the 0‐h drug administration in the subsequent period for each subject was 7 days. Blood samples were collected into pre‐chilled 3 mL K2EDTA tubes pre‐dosing (zero time) and at 16.00, 24.00, 28.00, 32.00, 32.33, 32.67, 33.00, 33.33, 33.67, 34.00, 35.00, 36.00, 36.33, 36.67, 37.00, 37.33, 37.67, 38.00, 39.00 and 40.00 h post‐dosing. The blood samples were processed as in single‐dose comparative bioavailability study.

The food effect study was performed in an open‐label, single oral dose, randomized, 2‐period, 2‐treatment, 2‐sequence, crossover design. In each period, subjects received either a single dose of test product administered 30 min after the start of a standardized high‐fat, high‐calorie breakfast (907 kcal in total; 57% fat, 28% carbohydrates, 16% protein) (treatment F) or a single dose of the same treatment administered after an overnight fasting of at least 10 h, which was maintained until at least 4 h post‐dose (treatment A). Subjects were randomly assigned to two sequences (FA and AF) using the SAS 9.4 software. The study periods were separated by a washout of at least 7 days between drug administration. In each period, blood samples were collected prior to dosing (0‐h), and at 0.33, 0.67, 1.00, 1.33, 1.67, 2.00, 2.33, 2.67, 3.00, 3.33, 3.67, 4.00, 4.50, 5.00, 6.00, 7.00, 8.00, 10.00, 12.00, 16.00 and 24.00 h after each drug administration. The blood samples were processed similarly as in the single‐dose fasting study.

Bioanalytical Methods

Ibuprofen in plasma was determined by a liquid chromatographic tandem mass spectrometric (LC–MS/MS) method developed and validated at the Bioanalytical Laboratory of PMRI. The method involved a liquid–liquid extraction. The standard calibration range was from 0.0500 to 50.0 μg/mL using a plasma sample volume of 0.100 mL. Acidified plasma samples were extracted under acidic conditions with a mixture of organic solvents; the organic phase was dried, reconstituted and transferred for LC–MS/MS analysis. Sample analysis was conducted using reversed phase chromatography. Ibuprofen was analyzed by the Sciex API 4000 (AB Sciex, Canada) mass spectrometer using negative ion scan mode. Data acquisition and analysis was performed in Analyst software (AB Sciex, Canada, version 1.6.3). Ibuprofen‐13CD3 (supplied by PMRI's internal Organic Synthesis Laboratory) was used as internal standard. For analyte and the internal standard, the parent‐daughter mass to charge ion transitions were 205–161 and 209–165, respectively. The expected retention time for ibuprofen and the internal standard was approximately 1.0 min. The bioanalytical method was validated in line with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidelines and applicable Good Laboratory Practices (GLP) [4, 5]. Method validation was completed prior to the entry into force of ICH M10 (Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis); therefore, the FDA and EMA guidance documents cited herein were the applicable regulatory standards at the time of study conduct.

Data Analysis and Statistics

Arithmetic mean ± SD concentration‐time profiles were calculated in GraphPad Prism version 9.1.0. (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Phoenix WinNonlin 8.2 (Certara, Princeton, USA) and SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, NC, USA) software were used for pharmacokinetic and statistical analysis, respectively. Pharmacokinetic parameters—the area under the concentration‐time curve at steady‐state (AUCt,ss), minimum measured concentration at steady‐state (Cmin,ss), maximum measured concentration at steady‐state (Cmax,ss) – were evaluated in multiple‐dose study, while the area under the concentration‐time curve from zero to last measurable concentration (AUCt), the area under the concentration‐time curve from zero to infinity (AUC∞), maximum plasma concentration over the sampling period (Cmax) and maximum plasma concentration over 0 to 4 h (Cmax0‐4) were evaluated in single‐dose comparative bioavailability study. Finally, AUCt, AUC∞, Cmax and time to maximum plasma concentration (tmax) were evaluated in single‐dose food‐effect study. The natural logarithmic transformation of AUCt,ss, AUCt, AUC∞ and Cmax,ss, Cmin,ss, Cmax0‐4, Cmax was used for statistical inference. AUCt,ss and AUCt were calculated using the linear up/log down trapezoidal rule, while Cmax,ss, Cmin,ss, Cmax and tmax were taken directly from the observed data. AUC∞ was calculated from the sum of AUCt and extrapolated AUC from last measured concentration to infinity (Ct), calculated as Ct/terminal elimination rate constant, where terminal elimination rate constant was estimated for each subject and for each treatment via linear regression of at least three last points at the terminal phase of the ln‐concentration versus time curve. The ln‐transformed pharmacokinetic parameters were analyzed by means of general linear model in SAS 9.4 software, where the effects of subject(sequence), treatment, period, and sequence were included as the fixed factors. The 90% or 95% confidence interval for the ratio of geometric least‐square means between each treatment was calculated. Descriptive statistics included arithmetic mean, standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variability (CV), geometric mean, median, maximum and minimum values. The non‐parametric analysis of tmax in the food effect study was performed with the crossover procedure (rank‐based approach) in Phoenix WinNonlin 8.2 software.

Safety Assessments

Safety was evaluated through assessment of adverse events, clinical laboratory test results of hematology, general biochemistry, and urinalysis, vital signs measurements, ECG findings, and physical examination findings.

Discussion

The main purpose of the biphasic modified‐release product is to provide rapid onset of action followed by an extended analgetic efficacy over a prolonged period. This effect is achieved via rapid release of the active ingredient from the IR component of the biphasic tablet as well as maintenance of therapeutic levels originating from the SR portion of the formulation. As a result, dosing frequency is reduced while dosing interval is being prolonged. A similar concept of biphasic ibuprofen formulation that allows prolongation of dosing interval has already been utilized by Pfizer Consumer Healthcare, who developed tablets containing 200 mg of IR ibuprofen and 400 mg of extended‐release ibuprofen [6]. Since our aim was to develop an easily accessible, OTC product for a wider EU population, the total dose of our biphasic system could not exceed 400 mg, the limiting single‐dose for OTC use in most EU countries. Therefore, the biphasic formulation system containing in total 400 mg of ibuprofen was chosen for further research and was evaluated in this clinical research. The rate and extent of ibuprofen absorption from the IR/SR modified‐release tablets was compared with standard immediate‐release formulation (Nurofen 200 mg), following both single‐dose and repeated dosing.

Frequent dosing of ibuprofen is required to maintain therapeutic concentrations due to the short elimination half‐life of the compound. There are situations when an extended duration of analgesia is desired (e.g., night pain). In such instances, a formulation which provides initial immediate release from one part of the dosage form and sustained release from the second part would allow for less frequent dosing. The currently marketed OTC IR ibuprofen formulations are indicated typically with a dosing interval of 4–6 h. Bilayer tablets were developed as a suitable dosage form to combine the two parts differing in release characteristics. The IR part is formulated to achieve a similar rise of ibuprofen plasma concentrations to the IR reference product containing ibuprofen 200 mg. The SR part is responsible for the sustained release over a prolonged dosing interval (8 h). The combined amount of 400 mg for the IR/SR formulation was selected to achieve similar steady‐state concentrations of IR reference given with twice the higher frequency (1 dose of 200 mg every 4 h). Importantly, the dose and dosing intervals were set to respect the 400 mg single‐dose and 1200 mg daily‐dose limits imposed for OTC products containing ibuprofen.

In the single‐dose study, the ibuprofen 400 mg IR/SR modified‐release tablet was compared with two sequential administrations of the immediate‐release reference product at 0 and 4 h, where similar extent of absorption needed to be demonstrated. The AUCt was therefore used as a measure of total exposure. For formulation combining IR and MR characteristics, the rate of absorption is of primary importance, particularly with respect to onset of action. To ensure comparable early exposure, it is essential to achieve plasma concentrations similar to those observed after the first administration of the reference product. Accordingly, Cmax0–4, defined as the maximal plasma concentration within the first dosing interval of the reference product, was selected as the parameter for assessing the rate of absorption. The test/reference ratio for log‐transformed Cmax0–4 was 97.81% (90% CI: 83.27–114.88%), indicating comparable early exposure and suggesting a similar onset of analgesic effect. In contrast, the overall Cmax across the full dosing interval was lower for the test product (T/R ratio 76.42%; 90% CI: 70.93–114.88%), falling outside the conventional bioequivalence limits. However, this difference is not considered clinically meaningful. The overall Cmax reflects the entire concentration‐time profile and is more closely related to safety than to efficacy for this type of formulation. Importantly, a lower peak concentration does not compromise efficacy, which is primarily driven by early exposure and overall exposure (AUC). Therefore, despite the observed reduction in overall Cmax, the pharmacokinetic profile of the test product is not expected to negatively impact clinical efficacy or safety.

In multiple‐dose study, the test product was administered with an 8‐h interval as compared to the 4‐h dosing interval for the reference product. A sufficient number of administrations was given to ensure steady state concentrations for the profiling. A sampling schedule in this study was designed to ensure that the AUC and Cmax parameters of ibuprofen were adequately assessed. The test to reference ratio for AUCt,ss within the acceptance limits 80.00–125.00% was proposed to ensure similar exposure during the 8‐h dosing interval under steady‐state conditions. While the rate of input was critical in a single‐dose setting, it is known not to influence the safety and efficacy profile of ibuprofen provided plasma concentrations remain within the Cmax,ss and Cmin,ss of the reference product. Accordingly, the acceptance criteria for 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were proposed as ≥ 80.00% for Cmin,ss and ≤ 125.00% for Cmax,ss [7]. The interval between the last drug administration in a period and the 0‐h drug administration in the subsequent period for each subject was 7 days, corresponding to more than five times the expected half‐life of ibuprofen to prevent any possible carry‐over effect.

We further assessed the effect of food on the bioavailability of the newly developed test formulation after a single dose in healthy subjects. This single‐dose study was designed with the aim of characterizing the effect of food on the bioavailability of ibuprofen [7]. The effect of food on the bioavailability of the drug product was determined by a statistical comparison of the AUCt, AUC∞, and Cmax parameters for ibuprofen. The high‐fat high‐calorie meal was served before dosing to investigate the worst‐case scenario. The fed state did not affect the bioavailability of the tested product (the 90% CIs for the ratios of AUCt, AUC∞, and Cmax were within the range of 80.00–125.00%), it only prolonged median tmax by 1.33 h, which is fully consistent with prior reports describing slower ibuprofen absorption in the presence of food [6, 8, 9].

In some drug development settings, study designs including more than two formulations are desirable. In such studies, multiple comparisons arise, which may lead to an inflated rate of false‐positive conclusions (i.e., an increased patient risk due to type I error [α]). In brief, if there is an option to select one of several bioequivalent test formulations, the familywise type I error rate must be controlled; this is sometimes referred to as a “multiple‐decision rule” [10]. In (bio)equivalence studies including a single test and a single reference product, equivalence is typically assessed using the two one‐sided t‐tests procedure at the 5% significance level (α), which is equivalent to requiring that the 90% two‐sided confidence interval for the test/reference ratio of log‐transformed pharmacokinetic metrics falls within the prespecified acceptance limits. This approach was applied in the single‐dose study with formulation C (as well as in the food‐effect study), where standard 90% confidence intervals were calculated for the ratios of log‐transformed pharmacokinetic metrics. However, in the multiple‐dose study, two test formulations were included (final formulation C and early formulation D). Therefore, a conservative Bonferroni adjustment was applied (i.e., α was divided by the number of comparisons, 5%/2 = 2.5%) to control the familywise type I error rate. Accordingly, 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the ratios of log‐transformed AUCt,ss, Cmax,ss, and Cmin,ss.

The early formulation prototype (treatment D) showed promising results in a pilot pharmacokinetic study and thus was included as an alternative formulation in the multiple‐dose study (data not shown). This prototype contained different type of releasing excipient (hypromellose) and a slightly different ratio of active ingredient in the immediate‐ vs. modified‐release layer compared to the ratio in the final formulation (treatment C). Despite these differences, the pharmacokinetic and statistical results for prototype D were similar to the results obtained for the final formulation C (following multiple administrations in the study) (data not shown). The decision was taken to continue with further development of the formulation that has shown better scalability of manufacturing process (and that was the formulation C).

In order to assess whether the observed trough concentrations are sufficient to maintain analgesic efficacy throughout the dosing interval, it is important to relate them to published concentration‐effect data. Previous studies have shown that both 200 mg and 400 mg ibuprofen provide significantly greater pain relief compared to placebo as early as 20 min post‐dose [11]. Based on pooled pharmacokinetic data from 17 studies, the estimated mean plasma concentration at 20 min after a 400 mg dose was approximately 8.4 μg/mL (95% CI: 6.8–10.1) [11]. Furthermore, PK/PD modeling studies have estimated the EC50 for ibuprofen analgesic effects to range between 6.4 μg/mL and 10.2 μg/mL [12], which provides a useful approximation of the concentration range associated with clinically meaningful analgesia. In the present study, the observed steady‐state Cmin,ss of approximately 7 μg/mL fall within this reported EC50 range. This suggests that plasma concentrations at the end of the dosing interval are likely to remain within or close to the minimum effective concentration required for analgesic efficacy.

Ibuprofen is a racemic drug that contains equal quantities of two enantiomers, S‐(+)‐ibuprofen and R‐(−)‐ibuprofen. Over the years, there have been continuous discussions about whether chiral or achiral bioanalytical methods should be preferred for the assessment of bioavailability of ibuprofen. We used achiral methodology as this has been accepted as fully appropriate by the EMA for the evaluation of bioequivalence of generic formulations containing ibuprofen [13]. In addition, the achiral method was more sensitive to detect formulation differences in the rate of absorption (Cmax) as compared to the eutomer [14].

We acknowledge that none of the studies presented in the publication were registered in a public clinical trial registry due to patent protection and commercial confidentiality reasons. However, for all presented human trials, clinical trial applications were reviewed by Health Canada's Therapeutic Products Directorate (national competent authority) and the studies did not commence until an appropriate permission (no objection letter) was received.

The product development aimed to prolong the dosing interval and to lower the frequency of tablet administration, which is considered more convenient for the patients. This target has been achieved since the continuous release of the drug, as evidenced by the appropriate steady state pharmacokinetics, allows an 8‐h dosing interval. Accordingly, this was reflected in the proposed posology for the developed product, where a minimum interval of 8 h must be maintained between two doses. Considering the extended dosing interval, only 3 tablets administered within a single day ensure that a sufficient amount of ibuprofen will be absorbed to provide adequate pain relief throughout the entire 24 h. It is important to note that such a dosing regimen still falls within the OTC category, where the maximum daily dose of ibuprofen should not exceed 1200 mg.

Conclusion

A novel biphasic IR/SR modified‐release formulation of ibuprofen has been developed. This product exhibited equivalent total and early peak exposure when compared to the reference product (Nurofen 200 mg, coated tablets) following a single dose in healthy volunteers under fasted conditions. At twice the usual dosing interval of standard ibuprofen, the developed formulation showed equivalent total, peak and minimum steady‐state exposures when compared to the reference product after repeated dosing. Food did not significantly alter the peak or total exposures of this product, and the formulation was well‐tolerated. Based on its pharmacokinetic profile, this product may represent a potentially advantageous ibuprofen‐containing alternative intended to provide prolonged relief in mild to moderate pain, e.g., headache, dental pain or nighttime pain.
