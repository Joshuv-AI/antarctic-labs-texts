Neuroimaging epicenters and the related neurobiological signatures in patients with postherpetic neuralgia

Background

Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), a typical neuropathic pain syndrome, is caused by the reactivation of latent varicella zoster virus within sensory ganglia [21, 24]. It frequently involves central and peripheral neuropathic signs (e.g., tactile allodynia) [39], which compromise patients’ quality of life and escalate socioeconomic burdens [14]. Considering the long-term pain conditions, patients with PHN are at high risks for developing sleep disturbance and mental disorders [3, 45]. On the other hand, sleep disturbance could result in hyperalgesia (i.e., an increased sensitivity to pain) and the development or exacerbation of spontaneous pain symptoms [11], thus forming a vicious cycle. Due to the limited understanding of its complex pathology, conservative interventional treatments cannot relieve all patients’ pain and may lead to various drug-related side effects [6, 30]. Therefore, it is imperative to explore the neurobiological alterations linked with PHN to improve the diagnostic and therapeutic effectiveness.

Animal models and human studies suggest that PHN was not only associated with peripheral neuropathy, but also related with abnormal cortical and subcortical changes [46]. Advanced neuroimaging approaches showed that patients with PHN exhibited both abnormal gray matter volume in brain regions (thalamus, bilateral insula, middle frontal gurus, etc.) [31] and dysfunctional connections involving these regions [22, 29]. Importantly, alterations in brain morphology and functions observed in brain diseases appear to follow network-like patterns constricted by underlying connectome [13, 34]. Based on the nodal stress hypothesis, densely connected regions exhibit increased susceptibility to pathological processes resulting from similar metabolic alterations, spread of pathogens, or shared gene transcriptional profiles [8, 13]. Additionally, regional dysfunction could act as ‘disease epicenters’ by promoting pathological processes in brain regions they connect to, thus serving as pivotal hubs in network reorganization [27]. Although the effects of PHN on the structure and function of pain modulation pathways have been widely explored, it remains unclear how PHN-related morphological alterations are embedded in network organization, and whether the identified disease epicenters are linked to underlying neurobiology.

Neurobiological mechanisms of pain implicate multisystem interactions that encompass neurotransmitter systems (e.g., opioid, monoaminergic), the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, adenosine and nitric oxide signaling [18, 51], etc. In addition, pain burden may also be linked to gene expression involved in pain conduction, modulation, and neurotransmission [35]. In this study, structural epicenters of PHN, quantified by gray matter morphometry, will be mapped onto multi-scale brain blueprints spanning from functional connectivity maps, gene expression, and neurotransmitters receptors and transporters, to pain-related activity. Our goal is to identify potential neuroimaging epicenters in PHN and then explore the underlying neurobiological mechanism.

In pursuit of this goal, we identify areas of probable origin of neuroanatomical pathology of PHN by utilizing neuroimaging-derived connectivity-based epicenter mapping. We then systematically investigated the associations of neuroimaging epicenters with clinical symptoms, gene transcriptional profiles, neurotransmitter distributions, and expected functional activation for pain. Our results suggest MRI-based epicenter mapping as biomarkers that may provide novel targets for the clinical treatment of PHN.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study and recruited 34 patients with PHN from Chongqing Xinqiao Hospital and 29 healthy controls (HCs) from patients’ families or nearby communities. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of the Army Military Medical University of China (Approval No. 2021-Research-045-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. PHN diagnosis was established according to the S2k guidelines for herpes zoster and postherpetic neuralgia (2019 edition). Diagnostic criteria included: (1) documented history of acute herpes zoster infection; (2) Persistence of neuropathic pain (distinct from inflammatory pain) after rash resolution; (3) Characteristic pain descriptors reported by patients, including lancinating, shooting, electrical-like, or stabbing sensations. Diagnoses were confirmed by board-certified neurologists. All included patients reported pain that lasted for at least one month after the resolution of the resolution of the herpes zoster eruption [26].

Study participants meeting the diagnostic criteria for PHN and all protocol-defined inclusion criteria were enrolled. Inclusion criteria comprised: a baseline Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NRS) score ≥ 4; right-handedness; age between 18 and 70 years; ongoing pain managed at other institutions; absence of serious underlying disease; and no history of chronic insomnia prior to PHN onset (defined as ongoing difficulties initiating or maintaining sleep occurring ≥ 3 times per week for ≥ 3 months). Exclusion criteria included: severe cardiac or pulmonary disease; hematological disorders; coagulation abnormalities; poorly controlled diabetes; malignancy; immunosuppressant use; and contraindications for MRI. In total, 5 controls and 7 patients were excluded due to incomplete questionnaire. For MRI analysis, 5 controls and 10 patients were excluded because of abnormal data, excessive head movement (mean frame-wise displacement > 0.2), or incomplete MRI acquisition. Additionally, 2 controls and 10 patients were excluded from polysomnography (PSG) analysis due to incomplete or poor-quality data.

All participants underwent comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological evaluations. Sleep quality was assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). Pain level was evaluated with the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ), visual analog scale (VAS), the 6-point behavioral rating scale (BRS6), and Neuropathic Pain ID Pain Scale (ID Pain). Mental health was assessed with using self-rating anxiety scale (SAS), and self-rating depression scale (SDS). All neuropsychological assessments were administered by clinical physicians trained in standardized neuropsychological scale administration. Characteristics of the study participants and sleep metrics differences between patients and HCs were provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Table 2, respectively.

PSG data collection, preprocessing, and analysis

All participants visited the PSG laboratory twice, arriving at approximately 8 p.m. on both occasions. They spent the first night acclimatizing to the laboratory environment; only data from the second night was analyzed. Participants were instructed to refrain from consuming any beverages, food, or medications containing caffeine, tea, alcohol, or other substances that might affect sleep. PSG recordings commenced between 9 p.m. and 7 a.m. following successful electrode application with low impedance (< 5 kΩ). Subsequently, participants filled out a sleep log to record their sleep onset and wake times.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) signals were acquired using Ag/AgCl active electrodes embedded in an elastic cap according to the international 10–20 electrode placement system. The signals were segmented into non-overlapping 30-second epochs. Experienced sleep technicians manually scored these epochs following the standards established by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM). EEG recordings comprised 16 channels referenced to the contralateral mastoid. In addition, electrooculogram (EOG) and electromyogram (EMG) signals were also recorded.

EEG signals underwent standardized preprocessing using Automagic [41]. Specific steps included: bad channel detection via the PREP pipeline; artefact correction (e.g., muscle, eye, heart) using independent component analysis (ICA); line noise removal using the ZapLine method; high-pass filtering (cut-off frequency: 0.1 Hz); interpolation of bad channels using the ‘spherical’ interpolation method. Subsequently, spectral power was computed using EEGLAB (https://eeglab.org/). Phase-locking values (PLV), quantifying synchronization between each pair of EEG channels, were calculated using HERMES with default parameters [38]. These analyses were performed across distinct sleep stages (wake, N1, N2, N3, REM) and frequency bands: slow oscillation (0.5–1 Hz), delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–15 Hz), and gamma (15–30 Hz). Additionally, sleep spindles were detected using Spindler with default parameters [28]. We evaluated spindle characteristics including length, density, and number. Finally, the number of two-stage sleep-stage transitions was counted following established methods [43].

MRI acquisition

MRI data was acquired using a Siemens Magnetom Terra 7-Tesla scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) at the 7-Tesla Magnetic Resonance Imaging Translational Medical Center of Southwest Hospital. The scanner was equipped with a 1Tx/32Rx Nova Head Coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, MA, USA). Participants were instructed to stay awake but relaxed, keeping their eyes closed during the scan. Foam pads were used to minimize head movement, and earplugs were provided to reduce noise exposure.

Initially, Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) sequence scans were performed to exclude potential intracranial lesions, using the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 9,000 ms, inversion time (TI) = 2,600 ms, echo time (TE) = 96 ms, slice thickness = 3 mm, and scan time = 3 min 9 s. Subsequently, Magnetization Prepared with 2 Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echoes (MP2RAGE) sequence data were acquired with the parameters: TR = 4,300 ms, TE = 2.27 ms, slice thickness = 0.65 mm, TI1 = 1,000 ms, TI2 = 3,200 ms, flip angle 1 = 4°, flip angle 2 = 4°, bandwidth = 200 Hz/pixel, acceleration factor = 3, and scan time = 10 min 16 s. Finally, resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data were acquired using an echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the parameters: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 21.0 ms, slice thickness = 1.5 mm, flip angle (FA) = 90°, bandwidth = 1698 Hz/pixel, acceleration factor = 2, voxel size = 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm³, number of slices = 90, number of volumes = 240, and scan time = 8 min 16 s.

MRI preprocessing

Structural MRI data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) with default settings. Preprocessing included high-dimensional spatial normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using a DARTEL template, followed by nonlinear modulation and correction for individual head size. Gray matter (GM) density maps (voxel size: 1 × 1 × 1 mm³) were generated for group-level analyses. Participants exhibiting an interquartile range (IQR) (Specify metric, e.g., of normalized GM intensity) below 0.8 were excluded.

Functional MRI preprocessing comprised the following steps: (1) Removal of the first four time points to allow for signal stabilization; (2) Realignment to correct for head motion; (3) Spatial normalization to the MNI echo-planar imaging (EPI) template (resampled voxel size: 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm³); (4) Spatial smoothing using an isotropic Gaussian kernel (full width at half maximum [FWHM] = 3 mm); (5) Nuisance covariate regression, including the time series from white matter (WM; 99% probability using SPM tissue priors), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF 90% probability SPM map), and the six rigid-body head motion parameters derived from realignment; (6) Temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). Participants with a mean frame-wise displacement (FD) exceeding 0.5 mm were excluded.

Identification of individual patient GMV patterns

We obtained voxel-wise GM volume (GMV) maps from HCs. We further performed multiple regression for each voxel to estimate GMV as a function of age, sex, educational attainment, and total intracranial volume. This set of covariates were chosen because all participants were right-handed, and MRI data were acquired using the same scanner protocol. For each participant, voxel-level GMV estimate was derived by inputting their individual demographic values into the corresponding regression model. Voxel-wise W-scores were computed to quantify individualized GM atrophy patterns. For each voxel, the W-score was defined as the standardized residual between the observed GMV and the volume predicted by a control-derived regression model (adjusted for age, sex, educational attainment, and total intracranial volume), divided by the standard error of the model fit in HCs. This approach generated personalized atrophy maps while controlling for confounding demographic and anatomical factors.

Identification of ‘disease epicenter map’ in PHN

Connectivity-based epicenter mapping tailored to individual patient was applied to detect potential neuroanatomical abnormality epicenters in PHN. We hypothesized that an epicenter would exhibit both severe GMV reduction and an intrinsic functional connectivity (FC) pattern strongly resembling the patient’s GMV reduction profile [7]. First, we derived a normative set of functional connectomes from HCs. Then we applied the Brainnetome atlas [10] (http://www.brainnetome.org/) parcellating the brain into 208 cortical and 38 subcortical regions (246 total regions). Using resting-state fMRI data from HCs, we performed seed-based FC analysis. For each of the 246 predefined regions, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between the seed region’s time series and all other GM voxels in the brain, generating 246 whole-brain intrinsic FC maps. To achieve normally distributed data, correlation coefficients underwent Fisher-Z transformation, generating FC z-maps. Subsequently, one sample t tests were performed across all HCs’ FC z-maps to derive group-level t-maps of FC.

To identify the disease epicenters, we conducted Spearman correlation analysis between voxel-wise GM atrophy W-scores and voxel-wise FC t maps. This procedure generated goodness-of-fit (GOF) scores for each of the 246 candidate epicenter regions, where higher GOF scores indicated a greater probability of the region being an epicenter. Subsequently, one sample t test was performed on the GOF score to determine the statistical significance of candidate epicenters. The resulting statistical t map quantified the inferred epicenter degree for 246 regions at the group level. And false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to multiple comparison corrections. Finally, given the potential influence of medications on GMV, we performed partial correlation analysis between ventroanterior thalamic GMV and total dosage of pregabalin before our experiment with adjustment for total intracranial volume.

Associations between the regional GOF score and clinical symptoms in PHN

We explored the relationships between GOF scores of epicenter regions and pain level, sleep disturbance, and mental health using partial correlation analysis, controlling for age and sex.

Correlation analysis between epicenter and gene expressions

We acquired gene expression profiles from Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) [19] (https://human.brain-map.org/). Only left hemisphere regions were extracted. Brain-wide microarray expression data were processed using the toolbox-abagen [4, 19, 33]. Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression [1] was performed to explore the spatial relationship between epicenter t map and gene expression levels for all genes across 104 cortical regions in the left hemisphere. We further conducted 1,000 permutations to test the null hypothesis that PLS components explained no more covariance between the epicenter t map and brain-wide gene expression than expected by chance. Spatial autocorrelation was addressed using spin test corrections. Z scores and corresponding P values estimated by Bootstrapping were used to rank genes according to their contribution to each significant PLS component [37]. Genes significantly associated with fourth PLS component (PLS4, FDR-corrected q < 0.05) [42] were selected for enrichment analysis via Metascape [57] (https://metascape.org).

Correlation analysis between epicenter and neurotransmitter distribution

We explored the relationships between epicenter t map and neurotransmitter distribution maps via JuSpace [9](Vision 1.5; https://github.com/juryxy/JuSpace). This toolbox facilitates cross-modal correlation between MRI-derived measures and nuclear imaging-based estimates encompassing multiple neurotransmitter systems, including dopaminergic, serotonergic, noradrenergic, GABAergic, glutamatergic, endocannabinoid, and opioid neurotransmission, as well as the physiological metric of cerebral blood flow (CBF). Spearman’s correlation coefficients were computed between epicenter t map and the selected neurotransmitter maps. Statistical significance was assessed using 1000 permutation tests and was corrected by FDR.

Functional decoding

We examined the relationships among functional activity maps with pain, sleep disturbance, mental health, and the epicenter t map using the “decoder” function within the Neurosynth platform [55](https://neurosynth.org/). We used permutation tests to identify the significance of the derived correlation coefficients to ensure robust statistical inference, which accounted for spatial autocorrelation. Multiple comparisons were corrected using FDR [53] (FDR-corrected q < 0.05). The analysis was implemented in Python (version 3.13).

Results

Neuroimaging epicenters in PHN

34 patients and 29 HCs were finally included in the mapping of neuroimaging epicenters, with no significant differences in sex, age, and educational attainment between groups. For structural MRI data, we firstly identified unique GM atrophy patterns in patients with PHN. Using these maps, we generated a t map of epicenters for each patient, quantifying the GOF scores for 246 regions across whole brain. Higher GOF score indicated a greater likelihood that a region could serve as a candidate epicenter at individual level. This statistical t map of epicenter provided a group-level degree of each region becoming a notable epicenter in 34 patients with PHN (Fig. 1A). After mapping brain regions to seven cortical networks and one subcortical network, we found that subcortical network showed the highest GOF score (Supplementary Fig. 1). Following FDR correction (q < 0.05), we identified 37 regions as epicenter regions, mainly including inferior frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, orbital gyrus, precentral gyrus, cingulate gyrus and thalamus, and the others as non-epicenter regions (Fig. 1B). Detailed results were provided in Supplementary Table 3. Additionally, we explored the differences in GMV between HCs and patients (Fig. 1C). Among the epicenter regions, rostral temporal thalamus (mainly ventroanterior) with the highest GOF score was identified as the top epicenter (Fig. 1D). No statistically significant correlation was observed between ventroanterior thalamic GMV and total dosage of pregabalin (r = -0.288, p = 0.246) before our experiment with adjusting for total intracranial volume, indicating that ventroanterior thalamic atrophy was not caused by medications. Then we took top epicenter as the seed region and derived a normative set of FC maps (Fig. 1E), with MRI data from HCs. Furthermore, we examined the associations among epicenter t map, cross-sectional difference in GMV, and the FC pattern anchored by the top epicenter. A moderate correlation was observed between epicenter t map and cross-sectional difference (r = 0.508, p = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁷; Fig. 1F), indicating that regions exhibiting higher GOF score manifest more pronounced neurophysiological alterations in neuropathic-pain patients relative to HCs. Similarly, cross-sectional difference in GMV was correlated with top epicenter’s FC pattern (r = 0.338, p = 5.4 × 10⁻⁸; Fig. 1G), underscoring that regions with stronger connectivity to the top epicenter exhibit more difference in GMV between patients and HCs. Finally, epicenter t map was correlated with top epicenter’s FC pattern (r = 0.128, p = 0.045; Fig. 1H), suggesting that regions more connected to the top epicenter tend to display higher GOF score. However, this relatively weak correlation needs to be validated in a larger sample or longitudinal study.

Associations between regional GOF score and symptoms in PHN

We explored the associations among pain level and mental health with the GOF score of epicenter regions. We observed that regions significantly linked to IDpain (p < 0.05) were primarily concentrated in thalamus (Fig. 2A). For example, the rostral temporal thalamus demonstrated a moderate correlation with IDpain scores (r = 0.405, p = 2.1 × 10⁻2; Fig. 2B). More information was provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Additionally, regions associated with depression symptom were located in the frontal lobe (Fig. 2C), with a significant correlation observed in the inferior frontal gyrus (specifically opercular area 44) (r = 0.405, p = 2.2 × 10− 2; Fig. 2D). The associations between anxiety and GOF score of epicenter regions were shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Furthermore, we investigated the relationships between sleep disturbance and GOF score of epicenter regions. For sleep disturbance, we comprehensively analyzed both sleep scale (ISI) and sleep metrics based on PSG, including macro-structures, transitions to stages and characteristic waveforms. Among these, we identified that ISI and most of sleep metrics (including density of spindle, length of spindle, wake time after sleep onset, sleep efficiency, n2-so-tp, n3-so-tp, etc.), were significantly linked to GOF score of epicenter regions (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table 4). For example, Fig. 2F showed a significant correlation between wake time after sleep onset and GOF score of caudal temporal thalamus (r = 0.434, p = 2.6 × 10⁻2). Regarding correlations between GOF and clinical/PSG, the statistical power ranges from 0.5 to 0.8, which show moderate effects and indicate that these associations should be validated in larger cohorts. More details please refer to Supplementary Fig. 4.

Association between epicenter t map and cortical gene expression profiles

We derived gene expression profiles for the left hemisphere from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). Then we performed PLS regression to investigate the spatial association between gene expression and the epicenter t map. PLS4 explained 19.9% of the variance (Fig. 3A), indicating that the regional patterns of the PHN-related epicenter t map mirror the cortical transcriptional architecture. And PLS4 showed a significant spatially correlation with epicenter t map (r = 0.446, Pspin = 2.6 × 10− 2; Fig. 3B). Genes were ranked by their weights (i.e., Z scores of PLS4 loadings). For genes meeting significance thresholds (FDR q < 0.05), we conducted enrichment analysis for upregulated and downregulated genes separately using Metascape. Top 20 results of biological pathways for upregulated genes and downregulated genes were provided in Supplementary Tables 5 and Supplementary Table 6 respectively. For upregulated genes, the top significant gene ontology (GO) biological process included “ABC transporters”, “carboxylic acid metabolic process”, etc. Relationships among these terms were visualized as network using Cytoscape [44] (Fig. 3C). For results of downregulated genes, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 5.

Association between epicenter t map and cortical neurotransmitter receptors/transporters distribution

To determine the associations between epicenter t map and cortical neurotransmitter distribution, we assessed spatial relationships between epicenter t map and density maps of 18 neurotransmitter receptors and transporters (including µ-opioid receptor (MU), kappa opioid receptor (KappaOp), vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT), Metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5), etc.) (Fig. 3D). Among these, we observed epicenter degree was significantly associated with KappaOp (r = 0.221, Pspin =1.4 × 10− 3; Fig. 3E), MU (r = 0.216, Pspin =6.5 × 10− 3; Fig. 3F), and VAChT (r = 0.200, Pspin =6.5 × 10− 3; Fig. 3G). More details were shown in Supplementary Table 7.

Functional decoding

We examined the correlations between epicenter t map and functional activation related with pain, sleep disturbance and mental health. As shown in Fig. 3H, the epicenter t map was associated with functional activity related to pain after FDR correction (FDR q < 0.05). More details could be found in Supplementary Table 8.

Discussion

In this study, we first identified the candidate onset site of neuroanatomical pathology in PHN, using a patient-tailored connectivity-based neuroimaging epicenter mapping method. Second, we validated the associations of identified neuroimaging epicenters with clinical symptoms including pain, sleep disturbance, and anxiety and depression levels. Third, we detected a neurobiological basis for the epicenters by demonstrating their correlations with downstream pain activity, upstream neurotransmitter receptors/transporters distribution mainly including opioid and acetylcholine, and gene expression maps. Taken together, our findings systematically demonstrated that neuroimaging epicenters are credible biomarkers that are grounded on important genetic, neurochemical, functional activation, and psychopathological foundations of PHN.

As far as we know, this is the first time that neuroimaging epicenter approach is used to identify the possible onset region of neuroanatomical alterations in PHN. Previous studies have successfully applied this method to identify pathological origin in neuropsychiatric disorders [16, 23] and neurodegenerative diseases [2, 32]. Here we utilized a patient-tailored approach that evaluate the epicenters with maximum likelihood of being the origin region showing the most active pathophysiological process in patients with PHN. It allows for anatomical heterogeneity within PHN [46], thus enabling the assessment of epicenter probability at individual level by comparing individualized atrophy pattern to group-level functional connectivity pattern. We found that brain regions with higher probability being epicenter showed prominent atrophy and regions with stronger connectivity to the top epicenters exhibited more severe atrophy in PHN, which supports our hypothesis that pathological process may spread from epicenters through the intrinsic connectome organization. Overall, these results indicate that neuroimaging epicenter mapping approach is effective in detecting candidate sites and neuroanatomical pathways of pathological process in PHN.

In spite of individual variation, we found the top epicenters are located at the ventroanterior thalamus. Thalamus has long been thought as a key brain region in the ascending pain pathway [36, 56], which is an important information integration hub of pain processing and plays a key role in regulating nociceptive stimulation and cortical excitability [6]. Previous studies suggest that chronic pain could lead to thalamic atrophy through the overuse caused by excitotoxicity and inflammatory agents [17], which provides evidence for thalamus being the candidate region of onset of the neuropathophysiology of PHN. Moving forward, our results suggest the ventroanterior part of thalamus as a more refined pathological target, while previous studies proposed it as a potential brain target for improving motor function [47]. Our findings need to be further validated in future longitudinal study and clinical practice. Moreover, thalamus plays an essential role in sleep regulation [15], and its alternation is both related with pain and sleep disturbance in our study, thus indicating it as a potential treatment target for relieving pain through improving sleep health. A number of studies have demonstrated that deep brain stimulation (DBS) of ventroanterior thalamus is safe and effective for treating neurological disorders. For example, it has proven to be an effective target for treating Tourette syndrome [25]and refractory epilepsy [12]. This is a novel DBS target for pain that needs to be validated in clinical treatment. And targeting these epicenters may develop novel therapeutic approaches, potentially extending beyond PHN to inform treatments for other neuropathic pain conditions.

Furthermore, neurobiological interpretation of the epicenters of PHN may provide important knowledge for uncovering the complex etiology of PHN. First, we found a robust spatial correlation between epicenter distribution and gene transcriptional profiles of the cortical cortex. These significantly up-regulated/down-regulated genes are enriched in biological processes with respect to the metabolic processes of multiple organic compounds (e.g., carboxylic acid). Animal researches suggest that hydroxyl carboxylic acid receptor plays a possible role in the maladaptive neuroimmune processes related with neuropathic pain [5]. ATP-binding cassette transporters that are endogenously at the blood-brain barrier are critical determinants of central nervous system opioid penetration [54], which plays an important role in pain modulation. And previous works also provide 4-arylthiophene-3-carboxylic acid derivatives as inhibitors of ANO1 to relieve pain [50], which may offer evidence for the participation of these compounds in PHN and imply that these compounds could be molecular target in the future development of analgesic agent. Since samples within left hemisphere come from six subjects while samples within right hemisphere are from two subjects, restricting analyses to left hemisphere as before [40, 53] could minimize variability across brain regions [4]. Considering that brain alterations in PHN are usually bilateral [46], future studies including more samples across the whole brain are needed to investigate the associations of gene expression profiles with structural changes across the whole brain. Second, we detected a significant association of epicenter distribution and the distribution patterns of opioid receptors and acetylcholine transporter. Opioid system plays a central role in pain modulation and opioid receptors are broadly expressed throughout the central nervous system [20]. And PET imaging studies show that endogenous opioid peptides are released in multiple brain regions in response to experimentally-induced sustained pain, the degree of mu-opioid receptor activation is linked with decreased unpleasantness and pain level [58], supporting the positive correlation in our study. Previous study also suggest that the upregulation of acetylcholine transporter may contribute to the cumulative analgesic effects of repeated electroacupuncture [48]. Third, we found that the epicenter distribution is spatially correlated with a low-dimensional representation of pain, which supports our identification of epicenters from the respect of functional activity. Collectively, our neurobiologically exploration of the epicenters not only offers a comprehensive insight on the anatomy of PHN from various levels of the neurobiology, but also provides potential targets for developing novel effective analgesic agent.

There are some methodological issues should be considered for the present work. First, we used cross-sectional MRI data that could help identify the potential epicenters in this study. The future longitudinal study is essential for validating the candidate sites of PHN and depict the whole course of its pathophysiology processes. Second, considering that not all patients with PHN respond well to the treatment, our sample size is small that could not help solve the heterogeneity of PHN. A big sample size could help identify subgroups with distinct psychopathology processes, which may be suitable for different forms of treatment. Third, patients with PHN received clinical treatment, such as Pregabalin [49]. Thus, the atrophy in neuroimaging may not be solely attributed to the pathophysiology processes. Fourth, the associations of gene expression and neurotransmitter receptors/transporters depend on public dataset. Although it could provide neurobiological implications, the findings should be validated with patients’ data in the future.

Conclusions

In summary, we used a novel method to identify the ventroanterior thalamus as the potential epicenter of PHN, supported by the neurobiological interpretations. The presence of epicenters highlights that there may exist a reliable neuroimaging biomarker, which offers potential treatment targets.
