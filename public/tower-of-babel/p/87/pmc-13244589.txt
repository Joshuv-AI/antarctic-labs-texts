Contrast‐enhanced ultrasonography vs MRI for indeterminate testicular lesions: a systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Muhammed Arif Ibis, Maria Satchi, Nicholas Raison, Hakan Bahadır Haberal, Araz Musaev, Tet Yap, Angelo Territo, Cagri Akpinar, Giuseppe Fallara, Dean Y. Huang, Murat Gul, Asif Muneer, Antonio Ruffo, Omer Onur Cakir, Fabio Castiglione
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo conduct a systematic review and meta‐analysis to indirectly compare the diagnostic performance of contrast‐enhanced ultrasonography (CEUS) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the evaluation of indeterminate testicular lesions and to explore their potential roles in guiding clinical decision‐making.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched in August 2025. Eligible studies were prospective or retrospective cohorts evaluating CEUS or MRI in small, impalpable, or incidentally detected testicular lesions, with histopathology or clinical/radiological follow‐up as reference standard. Two reviewers independently performed selection, data extraction, and Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies‐2 tool assessment. Random‐effects meta‐analyses and meta‐regression were conducted in R (R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). This review was registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251113920).ResultsA total of 12 studies including 912 patients with 920 lesions (279 malignant, 641 benign) were analysed. CEUS showed a sensitivity of 90% (95% confidence interval [CI] 80–95%) and specificity of 74% (95% CI 47–91%), while MRI achieved a sensitivity of 94% (95% CI 88–97%) and specificity of 84% (95% CI 72–91%). CEUS provided a higher negative predictive value (NPV; 96% vs 88%), whereas MRI demonstrated superior positive predictive value (PPV; 83% vs 65%) and accuracy (91% vs 80%). Differences in PPV and accuracy significantly favoured MRI (P = 0.025 and P = 0.031). Limitations included protocol heterogeneity, operator dependence, and small sample sizes.ConclusionBoth CEUS and MRI demonstrated a high sensitivity and NPV, supporting their role in excluding malignancy. CEUS, with accessibility, low cost, and particularly high NPV, may serve as the first‐line adjunct, while MRI is best reserved for equivocal or higher‐risk cases. No funding was received for this study.

Introduction

Testicular cancer is the most common malignancy in men aged 15–40 years, with a steadily rising incidence, particularly in Europe [1, 2, 3]. Scrotal ultrasonography (US) is the first‐line imaging modality for evaluating testicular pathology because it is widely available, inexpensive, non‐invasive, and free of ionising radiation [4]. The growing use of scrotal imaging, often prompted by infertility investigation, scrotal discomfort, or incidental findings, has led to an increased detection of small or impalpable testicular lesions during routine or unrelated examinations [5].

However, many of these incidentally detected lesions remain radiologically indeterminate, as conventional US lacks sufficient specificity to reliably differentiate benign from malignant pathology, particularly in small, impalpable lesions [6]. This diagnostic uncertainty can result in overtreatment with radical orchidectomy, exposing patients to the risks of infertility, hypogonadism, psychological distress, and altered body image. Importantly, systematic reviews indicate that across studies with varying cut‐off definitions for small lesion size, >40% of testicular lesions excised by orchidectomy are ultimately found to be benign [5], and in one large single‐centre cohort including men with ≤20‐mm lesions, the overall malignancy rate was 23.8% [7]. Conversely, underestimating a malignant lesion may delay curative treatment and, in rare cases, allow metastatic progression [8].

These diagnostic challenges have led to increasing interest in advanced imaging modalities, notably contrast‐enhanced US (CEUS) and multiparametric MRI. MRI provides excellent soft tissue characterisation, while CEUS offers real‐time assessment of microvascular perfusion, both contributing valuable information for lesion evaluation [9, 10]. Over the past decade, both techniques have demonstrated promising diagnostic performance in characterising indeterminate testicular lesions, using histopathology or longitudinal follow‐up as the reference standard [11, 12].

Although CEUS and MRI have been independently evaluated in multiple studies, head‐to‐head comparisons remain scarce, and reported diagnostic metrics are heterogeneous. As a result, the optimal imaging strategy for reliably distinguishing benign from malignant indeterminate testicular lesions remains undefined. We hypothesised that MRI would demonstrate higher specificity and overall diagnostic accuracy compared to CEUS in the evaluation of indeterminate testicular lesions. The present systematic review and meta‐analysis therefore aimed to indirectly compare the diagnostic performance of CEUS and MRI in the evaluation of indeterminate testicular lesions and to explore their potential roles in guiding clinical decision‐making.

Methods

Search Strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA‐DTA) statement and was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251113920).

A comprehensive literature search was performed in August 2025 across PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. In PubMed and Web of Science, searches were performed across all searchable fields, whereas in Scopus the search was restricted to titles, abstracts, and keywords. The search strategy combined terms related to the index tests (MRI and CEUS), target condition (malignant testicular lesions), and population (patients with indeterminate or incidentally detected testicular lesions) using Boolean operators: (‘Small Testicular Mass’ OR ‘Small Testicular Tumour’ OR ‘Small Testicular Tumor’ OR ‘Testicular Incidentaloma’ OR ‘Small Testicular Lesions’ OR ‘Testicular Tumor’ OR ‘Testicular Tumour” OR ‘Testicular Lesions’) AND (‘MRI’ OR ‘CEUS’ OR ‘Contrast Enhanced Ultrasound’ OR ‘Magnetic Resonance Imaging’).

No restrictions were applied on publication year; however, only studies published in English were included.

Eligibility Criteria and Study Selection

We included prospective or retrospective diagnostic accuracy studies and case series with five or more patients [13] that evaluated MRI or CEUS as index tests in patients with small, impalpable, or incidentally detected testicular lesions, with histopathology and/or clinical follow‐up used as the reference standard. The target condition was histologically confirmed malignant testicular lesions.

Studies were required to provide sufficient data to allow reconstruction of 2 × 2 contingency tables (true positive [TP], false positive [FP], true negative [TN], false negative [FN]) or report sensitivity, specificity, or predictive values from which these could be derived.

For the purpose of this review, an indeterminate testicular lesion was defined as an intratesticular abnormality detected on conventional US that could not be confidently classified as benign or malignant using grey‐scale and colour Doppler criteria alone. In nine of the 12 included studies, lesions were identified on US as indeterminate or not characterisable, while in three studies inclusion was based on clinically or sonographically indeterminate testicular masses. Operational definitions of ‘indeterminate’ therefore varied across studies, encompassing explicitly indeterminate US findings, incidentally‐detected small or impalpable lesions undergoing further evaluation, or clinically/sonographically uncertain cases.

Exclusion criteria were: reviews, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, book chapters, case series with less than five patients, and studies without adequate diagnostic accuracy data. Studies were excluded if the diagnostic performance of CEUS could not be evaluated independently, such as when CEUS findings were reported only as part of a combined multiparametric US assessment without separable data.

Two reviewers (M.A.I., A.M.) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full‐text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (F.C.).

Data Extraction

Using a standardised form, two reviewers (M.A.I., A.M.) independently extracted data on study characteristics, including the first author, year of publication, journal, study design, recruitment period, sample size, and number of lesions, as well as patient demographics such as age and lesion size. Details of the index test (CEUS or MRI, including imaging protocol when available) and the reference standard (histopathology or follow‐up) were also collected. For each study, the number of benign and malignant lesions and diagnostic outcomes (TP, TN, FP, FN, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value [PPV], negative predictive value [NPV], and overall accuracy) were extracted. The target condition was histologically confirmed malignant testicular lesions, and the unit of analysis was per lesion. Where such data were not explicitly reported, 2 × 2 contingency tables were reconstructed wherever possible. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with two additional reviewers (F.C., H.B.H.).

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies‐2 (QUADAS‐2) tool [14], covering four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Signalling questions were answered as ‘yes’, ‘no’, or ‘unclear’, and judgements were categorised as low, moderate, or high risk of bias. Two reviewers (A.M., C.A.) performed the assessment independently; disagreements were resolved through consensus with two additional reviewers (M.A.I., H.B.H.). A ‘traffic light’ plot was generated to visually summarise risk of bias across studies. We did not perform formal publication bias assessment (e.g., Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test) because the limited number of studies reduces statistical power for such analyses.

Evidence Synthesis

All analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within the RStudio interface. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and diagnostic accuracy were either directly extracted or calculated from TP, TN, FP, and FN values. Random‐effects meta‐analyses were conducted using the ‘metafor’ package with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to account for between‐study heterogeneity.

Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the I 2 statistic, between‐study variance (Tau2), and Cochran's Q test. I 2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively, while values >75% were interpreted as considerable heterogeneity. A Q test P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistically significant heterogeneity.

Comparative diagnostic performance between CEUS and MRI was assessed via meta‐regression. Pooled estimates were presented with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all two‐tailed tests.

Results

Search Results and Study Characteristics

The initial search retrieved 822 records, of which 514 unique articles remained after duplicate removal. Following title and abstract screening, 377 studies were excluded, leaving 137 articles for full‐text review. After applying the eligibility criteria, 12 retrospective or prospective cohort studies were included in the final meta‐analysis (Fig. 1).

The PRISMA‐DTA flow diagram illustrating the process of study identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion.

Across these 12 studies, 912 patients with 920 testicular lesions were analysed, comprising 279 (30.3%) malignant and 641 (69.7%) benign lesions. Malignant cases formed the study group, while benign lesions served as controls, including both non‐neoplastic entities (e.g., epidermoid cysts, haematomas, testicular atrophy, necrosis) and benign neoplasms such as Leydig cell tumours (LCTs).

In nine of the 12 included studies, the presence of indeterminate testicular masses was identified using conventional grey‐scale scrotal US, whereas in three studies the presence of clinically or US indeterminate testicular masses was reported [9, 11, 15]. The principal characteristics of the included studies are summarised in Table 1 [9, 10, 11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Notably, the prevalence of malignancy differed between modalities, with MRI cohorts including a higher proportion of malignant lesions (42.8%) compared to CEUS cohorts (25.7%) (Table 1). Among the included CEUS studies, two used histopathology alone as the reference standard, while the remaining studies incorporated histopathology combined with clinical follow‐up. In contrast, most MRI studies relied exclusively on histopathological confirmation, with only one study additionally incorporating follow‐up. No study reported test failures, adverse events, or inconclusive index test results.

Meta‐Analysis

Pooled diagnostic performance metrics for CEUS and MRI in differentiating benign from malignant small or impalpable testicular lesions are presented in Table 2. CEUS demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 90% (95% CI 80–95%) and specificity of 74% (95% CI 47–91%), whereas MRI achieved higher sensitivity at 94% (95% CI 88–97%) and specificity at 84% (95% CI 72–91%). CEUS yielded a notably high NPV of 96% (95% CI 87–99%), while MRI demonstrated a superior PPV of 83% (95% CI 76–88%). Regarding overall diagnostic accuracy, MRI achieved a pooled accuracy of 91% (95% CI 87–94%), compared to 80% (95% CI 67–89%) for CEUS.

Risk of Bias and Study Quality

The QUADAS‐2 tool was used in accordance with PRISMA‐DTA recommendations for quality assessment of diagnostic accuracy studies, and the results are summarised in Fig. S1a,b.

Among the four domains assessed for risk of bias, patient selection was rated as low risk in most studies (nine of 12 [75.0%]), with high risk observed in the remaining three studies (25.0%). For the index test domain, seven studies (58.3%) were judged to have low risk of bias, while three (25.0%) had unclear risk and two (16.7%) had high risk. In the reference standard domain, eight studies (66.7%) were rated as low risk, one (8.3%) as unclear, and three (25.0%) as high risk. The flow and timing domain showed low risk in seven studies (58.3%), unclear risk in one (8.3%), and high risk in four (33.3%). The most frequent concerns were suboptimal/insufficiently reported blinding of the index test and variability in the interval between the index test and the reference standard, the high risk in patient selection largely reflected retrospective, non‐consecutive cohorts.

Applicability concerns were generally low across all three domains. For patient selection and index test, the majority of studies (10 of 12 [83.3%]) were rated as low concern, with only one study (8.3%) in each domain judged as unclear and one (8.3%) as high concern. The reference standard domain had low concern in nine studies (75.0%) and high concern in three studies (25.0%). These findings indicate that, overall, the included studies were broadly representative of real‐world clinical practice in terms of their populations, index tests, and reference standards. Additionally, no study was excluded from the meta‐analysis based on the risk‐of‐bias assessment.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta‐analysis of 12 studies, we compared the diagnostic performance of CEUS and MRI in differentiating benign from malignant indeterminate testicular lesions. Both modalities demonstrated high sensitivity (CEUS: 90%, MRI: 94%) and high NPV (CEUS: 96%, MRI: 88%), supporting their potential utility in ruling out malignancy. While MRI demonstrated higher specificity (84% vs 74%) and PPV (83% vs 65%) than CEUS, only the difference in PPV was statistically significant. Meta‐regression analysis confirmed a statistically significant advantage for MRI in terms of PPV (β = 1.06; OR = 2.88; P = 0.025) and overall diagnostic accuracy (β = 1.02; OR = 2.78; P = 0.031). These findings suggest that both modalities may be clinically useful in the evaluation of indeterminate testicular lesions. Although the difference was not statistically significant, CEUS demonstrated a numerically higher NPV, whereas MRI showed a statistically significant advantage in PPV.

It should be emphasised that our indirect comparison of CEUS and MRI was based on meta‐regression across independent study cohorts rather than true head‐to‐head evaluations of the same lesions. As such, observed differences in PPV may partly reflect variation in the prevalence of malignancy across included studies rather than intrinsic superiority of one imaging modality. This is particularly relevant given the prevalence dependence of PPV and NPV. Readers should therefore interpret comparative diagnostic performance estimates with appropriate caution.

In this context, the imaging pathway illustrated in Fig. 3 should not be interpreted as a validated diagnostic algorithm, but rather as a conceptual, hypothesis‐generating framework derived from pooled diagnostic performance estimates. Contrast‐enhanced US in the Evaluation of Indeterminate Testicular Lesions

Conventional US remains the first‐line imaging method for testicular tumours, but its performance is strongly influenced by lesion size [16]. By enabling real‐time assessment of microvascular perfusion and enhancement kinetics, CEUS may provide additional functional information for the characterisation of indeterminate intratesticular lesions.

Malignant lesions often demonstrate strong enhancement; differences in wash‐in/wash‐out patterns may help distinguish histological subtypes. For example, germ cell tumours (GCTs) often show heterogeneous enhancement, disorganised vascular morphology, and rapid washout, whereas lymphomas tend to display intense enhancement, slow washout, and non‐branching linear vessels [12]. However, some reports describe lymphomas with rapid washout, highlighting variability in enhancement patterns across studies. Moreover, it has also been reported that embryonal carcinoma may show no enhancement at all [10]. Importantly, CEUS can detect very low‐velocity intratumoral blood flow that may be missed on Doppler imaging, reducing the likelihood of FNs [12]. This is clinically relevant because some benign but vascular lesions such as adenomatoid tumours, stromal tumours, and fibrous proliferations can mimic malignancy on Doppler imaging. Furthermore, sex cord‐stromal tumours often exhibit marked early enhancement and remain hyper‐enhanced relative to the testicular parenchyma on delayed imaging [24].

Differentiation between LCTs and seminomas represents a particular diagnostic challenge on CEUS, as both typically exhibit homogeneous hyperenhancement and rapid wash‐in. Although some studies have suggested a tendency toward slower wash‐out in LCTs, others have reported no significant kinetic differences, underscoring the limited reliability of wash‐out behaviour as a standalone discriminator [10, 25, 26, 27].

Recent studies have demonstrated that CEUS shows high sensitivity NPV for the detection of neoplastic testicular lesions, supporting its potential role in excluding neoplasia in selected clinical contexts. However, studies directly addressing benign–malignant discrimination remain limited, and reported diagnostic performance varies according to study design, lesion spectrum, and diagnostic thresholds [28, 29, 30].

Moreover, combining CEUS with elastography has been shown to further improve diagnostic confidence, with multiparametric US features matching histopathological findings in up to 98% of cases [31]. Pozza et al. [32] also demonstrated that a multiparametric US approach improves lesion characterisation compared with any single US modality, potentially aiding benign–malignant differentiation and suggesting a specific histological subtype. Nonetheless, even with multiparametric US, determining the precise benign or malignant nature of a lesion can remain challenging.

Emerging quantitative and semi‐quantitative CEUS techniques, including time–intensity curve analysis and perfusion‐derived metrics, may further improve objectivity and reduce operator dependence. However, at present these approaches lack standardised acquisition and analysis protocols and were inconsistently reported across the included studies, precluding quantitative synthesis and limiting inter‐centre reproducibility.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging in the Evaluation of Indeterminate Testicular Lesions

Magnetic resonance imaging provides excellent soft tissue contrast and, with the addition of dynamic contrast‐enhanced (DCE) sequences, offers detailed assessment of vascularity and enhancement kinetics. Its ability to combine anatomical, functional, and perfusion data may make MRI particularly valuable for characterising indeterminate testicular lesions. However, its clinical utility is tempered by higher cost, longer acquisition times, the need for specialised interpretation, and lower accessibility compared with US‐based modalities.

On conventional T1‐ and T2‐weighted imaging (WI) sequences, malignant GCTs and benign lesions exhibit overlapping but distinguishable patterns. Seminomas are typically isointense on T1WI and homogeneous, hypointense on T2WI without a capsule [33]. Fibrous septa appear as hypointense bands on T2WI and enhance more prominently after contrast. Non‐seminomatous GCTs (NSGCTs) may appear iso‐ to hyperintense on T1WI and hypointense but heterogeneous on T2WI due to cystic change, haemorrhage, or necrosis, often with adjacent tissue invasion. Lymphomas present with low signal intensity on both T1WI and T2WI, often in a more diffuse pattern [33, 34]. LCTs are well‐circumscribed, markedly hypointense on T2WI, and isointense on T1WI [35]. LCTs typically appear T2 hypointense with rapid wash‐in and delayed washout; in one series, MRI achieved 89.5% sensitivity and 95.6% specificity for their diagnosis [17].

Diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) is useful for detecting hypercellularity, which is typically more pronounced in malignant tumours. Both seminomas and NSGCTs usually demonstrate restricted diffusion with low apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values [36], whereas benign lesions generally show no diffusion restriction and have higher ADC values; however, exceptions may occur in hypercellular benign tumours. In this context, Manganaro et al. [37] reported that LCTs exhibited less restricted diffusion compared to seminomas, although the difference was not statistically significant. It has also been suggested that the small size of LCTs at diagnosis may limit the evaluation of diffusion sequences and ADC mapping [35]. Feliciani et al. [38] were able to determine the histology of testicular tumours with an overall accuracy of 86–89% via MRI radiomics signature.

A key practical issue is heterogeneity in scrotal MRI acquisition across centres. ‘MRI scrotum’ is often performed with non‐dedicated protocols, with variation in DWI b‐values, field of view (FOV) and spatial resolution, which may contribute to variability in reported performance. Where scrotal MRI is undertaken, a dedicated small‐FOV testicular protocol is recommended, including high‐resolution multiplanar T2, optimised DWI with specified b‐values, and DCE when clinically indicated [39]. Clear communication between referrers and radiology teams helps ensure examinations meet required clinical standards [40].

Despite its ability to provide these multiparametric insights, MRI is limited by cost, scan time, the need for expert interpretation, and limited availability compared to US‐based modalities [41].

Implementation and Quality Considerations

The clinical applicability of advanced imaging techniques for indeterminate testicular lesions is strongly influenced by image acquisition quality, operator expertise, and protocol standardisation. Both CEUS and MRI are subject to substantial inter‐centre variability, which may limit the generalisability of diagnostic performance estimates. Future prospective observational studies should incorporate standardised CEUS and MRI acquisition protocols, potentially supported by pre‐defined quality criteria or a quality assessment framework analogous to the Prostate Imaging Quality (PI‐QUAL) system used in prostate MRI.

Guideline Recommendations and Clinical Implications

Current clinical guidelines do not recommend the routine use of either CEUS or MRI in the evaluation of testicular lesions. The AUA advises against routine scrotal MRI due to high cost and limited additional benefit, suggesting its use only in select benign‐appearing cases where US is inconclusive [42]. Similarly, the European Association of Urology (EAU) recommends MRI only when conventional US is insufficient for local staging or lesion characterisation, particularly in cases where testis‐sparing surgery is considered [1]. CEUS is not yet included in these guidelines despite an expanding evidence base and lower cost compared to MRI. A recently published Delphi consensus also concluded that CEUS and MRI should not be routinely recommended, a position largely attributed to limitations in accessibility, operator expertise, and cost [43].

Proposed Diagnostic Strategy

Recent retrospective evidence has shown that a conservative approach with US alone may be effective in reducing unnecessary surgery for incidentally detected testicular masses <1 cm. However, fewer than half of the patients who proceeded to surgery were found to have malignant tumours [44]. Considering the clinical and psychological consequences of orchidectomy or testis‐sparing surgery, this proportion remains unacceptably high. These findings underscore the need for diagnostic algorithms with higher accuracy during conservative management or active surveillance in order to minimise unnecessary morbidity.

Given their complementary strengths, a tiered imaging approach may represent a plausible strategy to support clinical decision‐making, pending prospective validation. In selected low‐risk scenarios, CEUS may be considered as an initial adjunct to conventional US, whereas MRI may be used as a second‐line problem‐solving modality when CEUS findings are suspicious or equivocal, or when baseline clinical suspicion is high. Such a sequential approach could potentially improve risk stratification and help reduce unnecessary surgery; however, its generalisability is contingent on standardised protocols and imaging quality, and it should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than prescriptive guidance. Importantly, neither CEUS nor MRI can establish a definitive diagnosis, and histopathological confirmation (e.g., frozen section where appropriate) remains the reference standard.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The number of eligible studies directly comparing CEUS and MRI was limited, and some analyses relied on relatively small cohorts, which may have reduced statistical power. Considerable heterogeneity was observed for specificity, PPV, and diagnostic accuracy, likely reflecting variation in malignancy prevalence, the spectrum of benign lesions included, and differences in diagnostic thresholds across studies. This heterogeneity limits the certainty of the pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates, and the summary values should therefore be interpreted with caution. The reference standard was not uniform, with some studies relying on follow‐up rather than histopathology, introducing potential misclassification bias. Furthermore, CEUS performance is inherently operator‐dependent, and MRI protocols varied in field strength, sequences, and use of CEUS, which may have contributed to interstudy variability. Finally, although meta‐regression identified statistically significant differences in PPV and overall diagnostic accuracy favouring MRI, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and wide CIs.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta‐analysis provides the first direct, pooled comparison of CEUS and MRI for the evaluation of indeterminate testicular lesions detected on conventional US. Both techniques demonstrated high sensitivity and NPV, underscoring their utility in excluding malignancy. CEUS, with its accessibility, lower cost, and particularly high NPV, may be considered the preferred first‐line adjunct in patients with low clinical risk lesions on conventional US. If CEUS indicates a low likelihood of malignancy, active surveillance may be appropriate; however, if CEUS findings suggest higher malignancy risk, MRI can be employed as a second‐line modality to refine diagnostic confidence. In patients with higher baseline clinical suspicion, or when both CEUS and MRI indicate malignancy, timely surgical management—either orchidectomy or testis‐sparing surgery—could be considered. This tiered, imaging‐guided diagnostic approach leverages the complementary strengths of CEUS and MRI, with the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce overtreatment, and enhance cost‐effectiveness. Standardised, multicentre prospective observational studies incorporating harmonised CEUS and MRI protocols and pre‐defined quality criteria are needed to validate this conceptual framework and establish reproducible imaging‐based risk stratification strategies.
