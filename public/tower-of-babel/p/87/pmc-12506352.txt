ApoA1/HDL and sepsis-associated vascular endothelial injury: a narrative review
Authors: Kailin Guo, Chang Hu, Le Li, Xiao Liu, Yaohui Liu, Dongsu Zhang, Yujie Fang, Yiming Li, Bo Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis remains a leading cause of global mortality, primarily driven by microvascular collapse stemming from severe vascular endothelial injury. Within this pathology, the dramatic depletion of Apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) and its carrier, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), is a hallmark feature. Despite being recognized as a prognostic biomarker, a comprehensive understanding of the ApoA1/HDL axis as an active participant and therapeutic target in endothelial protection remains fragmented. This review systematically synthesizes current evidence to reframe the ApoA1/HDL axis from a passive indicator to a master regulator of endothelial homeostasis in sepsis. We first delineate the strong correlation between reduced ApoA1/HDL levels and endothelial damage across various infections. Subsequently, we dissect its protective triad of mechanisms: potent anti-inflammatory actions, preservation of endothelial barrier integrity, and restoration of microvascular flow. Furthermore, we explore the emerging interplay between ApoA1 and exosomes, a novel aspect of its regulatory function. Finally, the translational potential of ApoA1-based therapies, including mimetic peptides and recombinant HDL, is evaluated. By providing an integrated mechanistic framework, this review highlights the ApoA1 as a critical therapeutic target and offers a roadmap for developing novel, endothelium-centric treatments for sepsis.

Background

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction condition caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Globally, millions of people are affected by sepsis annually, with a mortality rate ranging from one-sixth to one-third of patients [2]. In China, the 90-day mortality rate for sepsis patients reached 35.5% between 2015 and 2016 [3]. Reducing the incidence and mortality of sepsis remains a critical global priority. Persistent microcirculatory dysfunction in sepsis significantly contributes to multiorgan failure, with vascular endothelial injury being the primary pathogenic mechanism underlying this microcirculatory impairment [4]. The vascular endothelium, a monolayer of cells lining the inner vascular wall, performs essential physiological functions, including maintaining vascular tone and permeability, repairing damage, regulating coagulation, and stabilizing immune responses [3, 5]. However, during sepsis, the endothelium transitions to a pro-inflammatory state, triggering a cascade of pathophysiological alterations including: reduced vascular tone, degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx, increased vascular permeability, enhanced endothelial adhesion, microthrombus formation, and exacerbated apoptosis [3, 6]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at attenuating or reversing vascular endothelial injury and subsequent endothelial dysfunction constitute a critical intervention for sepsis management.

High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is the smallest and densest class of lipoproteins in plasma [7]. Apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), the primary structural and functional protein component of HDL, accounts for approximately 70% of its total protein content [8]. Recent studies have revealed that plasma levels of HDL and ApoA1 are significantly decreased during the initial phase of sepsis, with the magnitude of ApoA1 reduction showing a significant correlation with patient mortality rates [9–13]. Notably, ApoA1 serves as an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in sepsis [14]. However, whether the reduction of ApoA1/HDL contributes to vascular endothelial injury and the potential mechanism in sepsis, or whether exogenous supplementation of ApoA1 or HDL can ameliorate such injury, remains unclear. This review synthesizes the evidence linking ApoA1/HDL depletion with the cascade of endothelial injury in sepsis and evaluates the therapeutic potential of ApoA1-based interventions to reverse this pathology.

Decreased HDL/ApoA1 levels are associated with vascular endothelial injury in sepsis

Bacterial infection

In a murine model of sepsis induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP), reduced serum HDL levels were observed alongside increased serum E-selectin levels, decreased expression of vascular permeability regulatory proteins (Slit2, Robo4), and reduced endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) levels. These changes correlated with increased vascular permeability and aggravated edema in the mice [15]. Kilickap et al.‘s clinical study demonstrated that patients with acute severe infections exhibited significant reductions in serum HDL-c and ApoA1 levels, accompanied by impaired flow-mediated dilation (FMD). Additionally, correlation analysis identified ApoA1 as the specific factor associated with changes in FMD, underscoring its critical role in maintaining vascular regulation in critically ill patients [16].

Viral infections

Multiple studies have demonstrated that COVID-19 patients exhibit reduced serum levels of HDL and ApoA1, with lower ApoA1 levels correlating with increased disease severity [17–21]. Concurrently, decreased HDL levels in COVID-19 patients may weaken its protective effects on the vascular endothelium, manifesting as increased vascular permeability, disruption of intercellular junctions (primarily through redistribution of VE-cadherin), and enhanced apoptosis [19].

Infections by flaviviruses, such as dengue virus, are characterized by a marked reduction in serum ApoA1 levels [22]. Hepatocytes infected with flaviviruses in vitro also downregulate ApoA1 expression [22]. The flavivirus NS1 protein, a multifunctional viral glycoprotein and the sole non-structural protein secreted by infected cells, directly induces vascular endothelial cell dysfunction and vascular leakage [23, 24]. Reduced ApoA1 levels further exacerbate NS1 binding to endothelial cells, aggravating glycocalyx degradation and vascular hyperpermeability [24].

Parasitic infections

Malaria, a parasitic infection characterized by systemic immune activation and hyperinflammation, is a significant cause of sepsis in endemic regions [25]. Malaria infection leads to marked reductions in ApoA1 levels, which correlate with disease severity [26–28]. A study on pediatric malaria revealed heightened endothelial activation in patients, accompanied by decreased serum levels of ApoA1, Angpt-1 (a protective endothelial regulator), and platelets, alongside elevated endothelial markers (vWF antigen, VCAM-1) and glycocalyx damage markers (Syndecan-1) [29]. Notably, ApoA1 levels were positively correlated with platelet counts and inversely correlated with serum Syndecan-1, suggesting that low ApoA1 contributes to microvascular thrombosis and endothelial injury in malaria [29].

Bacterial sepsis or endotoxemia

rHDL is a formulation of ApoA1 and phospholipid or phosphatidylcholine. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that rHDL protects endothelial cells. For example, rHDL reduces IL-1β-induced E-selectin expression in porcine microvascular endothelial cells, thereby suppressing endothelial activation [33]. In a mouse model injected with LPS, Tso et al. discovered that LPS stimulation markedly increased apoptosis of aortic endothelial cells, triggering compensatory recruitment of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) for vascular repair. Notably, rHDL administration enhanced EPC-mediated endothelial regeneration [34]. Another study confirmed that P-selectin and ICAM-1 levels were elevated in glomerular endothelial cells of rats with septic shock, but significantly reduced after rHDL administration [35]. In a large-animal porcine model of endotoxemia, rHDL supplementation increased serum ApoA1 levels while decreasing inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1) and endothelial dysfunction markers (sICAM-1, sVCAM-1) [36]. These findings demonstrate that rHDL mitigates organ and endothelial injury through direct anti-inflammatory action, namely the suppression of inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules.

Lipid-free ApoA1 has rarely been reported to exert endothelial protective effects [37]. Most experiments instead utilize ApoA1 mimetic peptides, such as the 18-amino-acid chain peptide L-4 F, to replace lipid-free ApoA1 [38]. L-4 F binds directly to LPS, neutralizing its activity and inhibiting its interaction with leukocytes [12]. Additionally, L-4 F prevents and reverses LPS-induced HDL-c depletion and reduces IL-6 levels in CLP rats [39]. In a murine model of LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI), L-4 F lowered inflammatory cytokine levels, attenuated alveolar-capillary endothelial damage, reduced ALI severity and mortality [12]. Yang et al. demonstrated that administering the ApoA1 mimetic peptide Reverse-D-4 F to LPS-induced ALI mice decreased pulmonary expression of endothelial dysfunction markers (ICAM-1, VCAM-1, iNOS) while increasing endothelial markers (eNOS, vWF). Reverse-D-4 F also increased the number of EPCs, restoring their LPS-impaired abilities, such as proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and nitric oxide (NO) release, thereby promoting repair of LPS-damaged pulmonary capillary endothelium [40].

Viral infections

In COVID-19 patients, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) unleashes a pathological cascade, triggering cytokine storms and induces endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in microvascular endothelial cells [41, 42]. Previous studies have hypothesized EndMT was one of the potential pathophysiological mechanisms in both COVID-19 and long COVID [43, 44]. In vitro studies demonstrate that the ApoA1 mimetic peptide L-4 F can partially or completely suppress EndMT during COVID-19 by inhibiting the expression of key regulatory genes (SNAIL, SLUG) [44].

For flavivirus-induced dengue fever, research confirms that L-4 F inhibits viral attachment to host cells by depleting membrane lipid rafts [22]. L-4 F also directly binds to the flavivirus NS1 protein, blocking its interaction with and internalization by vascular endothelial cells [24]. Furthermore, L-4 F counteracts NS1-mediated vascular endothelial dysfunction, reducing hyperpermeability and glycocalyx degradation in endothelial cells [24].

In the initial phase of influenza virus infection, an acute inflammatory state emerges, characterized by elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and its induced adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, ICAM-1) [45], along with excessive superoxide production. Concurrently, HDL level declines, eNOS expression weakens, and NO bioactivity and bioavailability decreases, collectively impairing vascular endothelial function [46]. Studies show that the ApoA1 mimetic peptide D-4 F increases HDL-c concentrations and suppresses acute inflammatory responses triggered by influenza infection [47]. Protective mechanisms of HDL/ApoA1 on vascular endothelial structure and function in sepsis

Suppression of endothelial inflammatory responses

Direct neutralization of LPS and reduction of LPS-induced cytokine levels

ApoA1 can directly neutralize endotoxins [48]. Studies demonstrate that the N-terminal region of ApoA1 (aa: 52–74) binds to LPS, reducing its toxicity [52], and significantly decreases LPS-induced cytokine levels (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) while inhibiting LPS-triggered cell death in a dose-dependent manner [53].

Improvement of lipid metabolism and reduction of oxidized lipid generation

The ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), a transmembrane transporter that mediates the crucial process intracellular cholesterol and phospholipids to apolipoproteins bound at the cell surface [54]. Expressed in hepatocytes, macrophages [55], and endothelial cells (ECs) [56], ABCA1 interacts with lipid-free ApoA1 to promote cholesterol efflux and protect endothelial function [57–59]. In macrophages, this interaction directs cholesterol toward ApoA1 [60]. Additionally, ApoA1-binding protein (AIBP) enhances macrophage cholesterol efflux [61]. Cholesterol efflux reduces the abundance of TLR4 in macrophage lipid rafts, preventing TLR4 activation by LPS and suppressing downstream effectors (MyD88, MAPKs, NF-κB), thereby attenuating inflammation [62] (Fig. 1①). Concurrently, it activates the JAK2-STAT3 pathway, inhibiting apoptosis and pro-inflammatory cytokine release [60] (Fig. 1 ②). ApoA1 in mature HDL also can promote cholesterol efflux through binding to the scavenger receptor class B type 1 (SR-B1) [56].

Inhibition of inflammation-related signaling pathways

Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a sphingolipid within HDL that exerts pleiotropic biological effects by interacting with five receptors [63]. Among these, S1PR1 serves as the primary endothelial barrier-protective factor, suppressing inflammation, reducing endothelial permeability, minimizing leakage, and maintaining endothelial barrier integrity [64, 65]. In contrast, as an endothelial barrier-disruptive factor, S1PR2 can be agonized by LPS and TNF-α [66], while simultaneously stimulating TNF-α and adhesion molecules production to exert pro-inflammatory effects [67]. ApoA1 binds to SR-B1, activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and suppressing NF-κB nuclear translocation, thereby reducing S1P/S1PR2-mediated TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels and attenuating EC inflammation [68] (Fig. 1 ③). Additionally, ApoA1 inhibits TNF-α-induced VCAM-1 gene transcription, reducing adhesion molecule expression, cytokine release, and monocyte adhesion to ECs [69, 70]. Recent studies have innovatively engineered a fusion protein combining APOA1 with APOM (the chaperone protein of S1P), namely A1M which preferentially activates S1PR1 upon binding S1P, enhancing endothelial function and suppressing inflammatory factors [71].

The NLRP3 inflammasome represents another critical inflammatory pathway. LPS activates the SIRT1/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis, driving excessive secretion of vascular adhesion molecules [72] (Fig. 1 ④). rHDL directly suppresses IL-1β and NLRP3 inflammasome expression in macrophages and downregulates inflammasome activity via NF-κB inhibition [73].

In addition, early studies have shown that APOA1 and rHDL can inhibit the polymerization of complement C9 and the deposition of polymerized C9 on the endothelial cell membrane, protecting endothelial cells from complement-induced damage [74].

Maintenance of endothelial barrier integrity

Prevention of glycocalyx degradation

The glycocalyx (GC), a layer of glycoprotein polymers on the endothelial surface, serves as both a mechanical and biological barrier for the vascular endothelium [75]. It prevents vascular fluid leakage into the interstitial space [76] and limits leukocyte-endothelial interactions by “masking” adhesion molecules⁶. Inflammatory stimuli, such as sepsis, induce glycocalyx degradation or shedding in murine models, significantly thinning its thickness [77]. Clinical observations in septic patients also reveal sublingual microcirculatory glycocalyx damage and microvascular dysfunction [78]. Glycocalyx degradation exposes previously hidden adhesion molecules, recruiting inflammatory cells and amplifying local inflammation [79, 80].

Angiopoietin-2 (Angpt-2) is an antagonist of Angpt-1/Tie-2 (a tyrosine kinase and endothelial stabilizing receptor) axis [81], which induces endothelial glycocalyx degradation and increases vascular permeability [82]. Angpt-2 expression is mainly induced by Forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) [83]. ApoA1 protects the endothelial glycocalyx by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and inhibiting FOXO1 activity, thereby inducing the upregulation of angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4) [84]. ANGPTL4, a cytoprotective factor produced by brain microvascular ECs, binds to syndecan-4 in the glycocalyx [85]. A recent study demonstrated that ANGPTL4 preserves glycocalyx integrity, stabilizes endothelial junctions, prevents EC apoptosis, which collectively translates into reduce vascular inflammation, and ultimately mitigates vascular dysfunction, organ failure, and mortality [86].

Additionally, circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) are novel biomarkers in diseases and can function as bioactive signaling molecules [87]. miR-223 is a key regulator of inflammation [88]. Mice lacking miR-223 exhibit enhanced systemic inflammation upon LPS treatment [88]. Furthermore, miR-223 can suppress LPS-induced cellular autophagy by downregulating FOXO1 expression [89]. Besides this, nHDL, but not lipid-free ApoA1, can transfer circulating miR-223 into endothelial cells, where it directly targets and inhibits the expression of inflammatory genes (such as ICAM-1) [90].

Inhibition of endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT)

EndMT, a reversible process driving endothelial dysfunction, is characterized by reduced expression of EC markers such as VE-cadherin and CD31 [91]. Studies show that LPS and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during oxidative stress induce EndMT in pulmonary microvascular ECs during acute lung injury [92]. In vitro experiments confirm that recombinant human ApoA1 and the ApoA1 mimetic peptide D-4 F suppress EndMT by inhibiting SMAD signaling in murine arterial ECs [93, 94] Fig. 2.

Restoration of microvascular flow

Prevention of endothelial thrombosis and inhibition of platelet activation

During sepsis, endothelial apoptosis generates membrane-derived microparticles that promote thrombosis [95, 96]. Damaged endothelium exposes adhesion molecules, and activated ECs secrete von Willebrand factor (vWF), enhancing platelet adhesion to injured vessels [97]. Sepsis disrupts three physiological anticoagulant pathways—tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) [98, 99], activated protein C (APC), and antithrombin systems [100, 101]—exacerbating thrombosis. A study involving 20 healthy individuals demonstrated that plasma ApoA1 levels were positively correlated with the anticoagulant response of APC/protein S [102], particularly for large HDL subspecies. This activity can be neutralized by ApoA1 antibodies, indicating ApoA1 mediates HDL’s antithrombotic effects [103]. Studies show that ApoA1 levels inversely correlate with hyperadhesive vWF in sepsis patients. ApoA1 reduces vWF production via SR-BI/eNOS-dependent mechanisms [104] (Fig. 3 ①). Both lipid-free ApoA1 and native HDL can interfere with vWF self-assembly to shorten fiber length and thickness, thereby inhibiting platelet adhesion and microvascular thrombosis [105].

Maintains microvascular tone

In sepsis, blood vessels are in a vasoplegic state. Activation of iNOS leads to NO overproduction, causing systemic vasodilation [106]. However, excessive NO suppresses eNOS in microvessels, reducing localized NO production [107, 108]. As a result, NO-dependent microvascular dilation is restricted [109], and the number of perfused small blood vessels is significantly reduced [110, 111], causing microcirculatory dysfunction. Additionally, excessive production of NO and superoxide radicals generate when endothelial cells are exposed to LPS [112]. Thus, balancing eNOS/iNOS activity is critical for endothelial recovery.

Produce NO

ApoA1 binds to SR-B1, activating eNOS in intact blood vessels to NO. This process occurs in endothelial cell lipid raft domains —— caveolae, where SR-B1 and eNOS are co-localized with caveolin [113]. Signaling through the SR-B1/PDZK1-Src-PI3K-AKT/ERK1/2-eNOS pathway enhances NO generation [114] (Fig. 3 ①).

Additionally, ApoA1 or mature HDL interacts with endothelial ecto-F1-ATPase, converting ATP to ADP to activate P2Y1 receptors, triggering PI3Kβ/AKT signaling and eNOS activation [115] (Fig. 3 ②). The ApoA1 mimetic D-4 F inhibits eNOS overactivation and uncoupling, reducing superoxide production, while preserving endothelial barrier function [116]. Therefore, ApoA1/HDL-mediated eNOS activation helps normalize vascular tone by counteracting iNOS-driven vasoplegia, while the NO produced simultaneously exerts a powerful antithrombotic effect by reducing endothelial tissue factor expression [117].

Increase COX-2 and PGI-2

In a complementary pathway, ApoA1 also maintains microcirculatory flow by upregulating cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and prostacyclin (PGI-2). This is initiated when ApoA1 binds to SR-B1 to activate eNOS; the subsequent phosphorylation of eNOS then triggers a downstream signaling cascade involving p38 MAPK and CREB to drive COX-2 expression [118, 119] (Fig. 3 ③). Second, the interaction between ApoA1 and ABCA1 activates the JAK2/STAT3, p38 MAPK and ERK1/2 pathways [120] (Fig. 3 ④).

Potential mechanism: ApoA1 interaction with plasma exosomes

Exosomes broadly refer to any type of extracellular vesicles (EVs) [121], and are narrowly defined as small EVs (sEVs) derived from endosomes with diameters of 40–150 nm [122]. The cellular origin of exosomes determines their concentration and cargo content. In sepsis, the pathophysiological impact of exosomes is source-dependent, exhibiting both deleterious and protective effects [123, 124]. EVs can promote inflammation, enhance coagulation, induce lymphocyte apoptosis, contribute to organ dysfunction, and cause endothelial cell damage in sepsis [124–126]. Both animal and clinical studies confirm that during sepsis the concentration of plasma exosomes increases [127–129]. And elevated exosome levels are positively correlated with the severity of sepsis-associated organ failure and can predict mortality [130]. Conversely, some studies indicate that exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells can act as protective agents by suppressing inflammatory responses [131], exerting antibacterial activity [126], mitigating organ damage [132], and improving survival rates in sepsis [133].

Septic exosomes exert cell-origin-dependent effects on endothelial pathophysiology [134]. Exosomes trigger pulmonary ECs apoptosis and disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier [135]. Platelet-derived exosomes account for the highest proportion of plasma exosomes [136], it can exacerbate endothelial and smooth muscle cell apoptosis via mechanisms such as oxidative stress or generation of ROS, NO and other superoxides [137–139]. Polymorphonuclear leukocyte (PMN)-derived EVs act as inflammatory mediators, activating ECs and stimulating increased cytokine production and leukocyte adhesion molecule expression [140]. However, opposing research demonstrates that PMN-derived exosomes enriched in functional annexin A1 (AnxA1) can inhibit PMN adhesion to endothelial cells, thereby attenuating inflammatory responses and endothelial injury [141]. Neutrophils contain inflammatory mediators (such as myeloperoxidase, MPO) that activate ECs, inducing vasculitis [142]. Endothelial cell-derived EVs can also stimulate the ECs themselves to release pro-inflammatory factors, inducing inflammatory responses that lead to endothelial damage [143]. Following LPS stimulation, monocyte-macrophages can either release caspase-1-containing EVs that induce death in pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells [135], or secrete EVs carrying miR-155 and miR-223 which enhance IL-6 and ICAM-1 production through the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway [144].

EVs undergo cell-type-specific internalization through distinct mechanisms, including endocytosis (clathrin-, caveolin-, or lipid raft-mediated), macropinocytosis, or phagocytosis [145]. Some studies suggest that septic plasma exosomes mitigate sepsis-induced vascular inflammation, endothelial injury, and organ dysfunction upon uptake [146]. Zhang et al. found that septic cardiomyopathy generates dysfunctional mitochondria-containing dedicated larger vesicles (namely exophers) from cardiomyocytes, which are phagocytosed by macrophages to restore cardiac homeostasis and function [147].

Exosomes with high surface free energy adsorb proteins from biofluids (e.g., plasma) [148]. And then proteins with the highest affinity are anchored on the exosomal surface, forming a “protein corona“ [149]. ApoA1 being the most abundant apolipoprotein in this corona [150]. As described above, ApoA1 binds to ABCA1 in non-raft membrane domains of macrophages [151], and AIBP enhances ABCA1-ApoA1 interaction [152–154]. Thus, exosomal corona-associated ApoA1 may promote exosome-macrophage binding and subsequent phagocytosis. This mechanism is implicated in cellular defense against dengue virus (DENV): DENV replication requires cholesterol-rich membrane domains, and cholesterol depletion inhibits viral replication [155]. DENV NS1 protein increases lipid raft density via TLR4 activation, facilitating viral infection and replication. In contrast, ApoA1 mimetic peptides deplete lipid rafts, reducing TLR4 abundance, and inhibiting NS1-mediated immune activation and viral attachment, preventing DENV exacerbation [22] (Fig. 1).

Potential therapeutic clinical effect of ApoA1/HDL

Initial clinical evaluation of rHDL (CSL-111) in LPS-challenged healthy volunteers demonstrated significant attenuation of endotoxin effects, including reduction in inflammatory cytokines during endotoxemia and decreased leukocyte adhesion [156].

The recent clinical trial applied to sepsis is CER-001(which was also a type of rHDL). A randomized controlled phase 2a clinical trial of CER-001 enrolled 20 sepsis patients, testing different dosing regimens (0/5/10 mg/kg, administered twice daily on days 1, 2, 3, and 6). The results showed that compared to the control group, the treatment group had significantly reduced plasma LPS concentration, increased ApoA1, a rapid and significant decrease in markers of inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, inhibition of the progression of hepatic and renal dysfunction, shorter ICU stays, and lower mortality (Control group: 50% vs. Treatment group: 14.2%) [36]. However, this study did not identify the optimal dose or dosing frequency of CER-001 for sepsis and its long-term safety. Future phase 2b/3 trials of CER-001 are planned to determine a superior dose and clearer clinical benefits for sepsis/severe infection treatment, as well as to explore its cost implications.

Additionally, a study reporting the injection of CER-001 in a critically ill COVID-19 patient observed that during rHDL infusion, ApoA1 concentration increased, while HDL-c and inflammatory proteins like Serum Amyloid A-1 (SAA-1), CRP, and cytokines decreased. This suggests rHDL injection may promote the clearance of pro-inflammatory HDL particles [51]. A prospective clinical trial studied four SARS-CoV-2 infected patients treated with CER-001. IL-8 decreased in three patients, and ApoA1 levels normalized. The study indicated that CER-001 had good feasibility and tolerability in COVID-19 patients.

Limitations and future directions

This review has explored the multifaceted role of ApoA1/HDL in sepsis-associated endothelial injury. However, several limitations should be addressed to guide future research. First, our study incorporates limited clinical trial data. The mechanisms we propose are primarily based on animal models and in vitro experiments. These findings can only offer reference points and insights into the role of ApoA1/HDL in sepsis treatment and cannot substitute for its effects in clinical applications. Therefore, future research should design and conduct more clinical trials. Notably, ApoA1/HDL-related drugs or reagents are currently only used at the experimental stage, meaning that investigating the clinical role and mechanisms of ApoA1/HDL remains a long and challenging task. Nevertheless, CER-001 shows promise as a viable therapeutic approach.

Second, besides exogenous supplementation of ApoA1 mimetic peptides or rHDL, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) might be another approach to alter the state of low ApoA1/HDL levels during sepsis or severe infection, thereby improving endothelial injury. TPE is an extracorporeal adjunctive therapy for sepsis that requires further investigation [157]. It works by removing harmful substances from the plasma (such as toxins, inflammatory mediators, vWF and other antifibrinolytic factors, endothelial damaging substances, etc.) and replenishing essential normal plasma components (such as immunoglobulins, coagulation factors, and procogulant proteins, etc.) [158–160]. Studies have demonstrated that TPE can improve endothelial barrier function and reduce permeability in septic shock [161]. This protective effect maybe not only relies on removing harmful inflammation factors such as heparan sulfate (HS) and glucuronidase Heparanase-1 (Hpa-1) [162], but also on replenishing protective factors such as glucuronidase Heparanase-2 (Hpa-2) and HDL/ApoA1. However, it has not been conclusively demonstrated that TPE directly acts by restoring ApoA1/HDL concentrations, and this hypothesis requires further research for validation.

Third, our research only summarizes the effects of ApoA1/HDL on some endothelial changes during acute inflammation (such as increased inflammatory factors, impaired endothelial barrier, microthrombosis, and reduced microvascular tone). Further summarization and organization are needed regarding ApoA1/HDL’s impact on other aspects, such as angiogenesis, endothelial cell differentiation, and proliferation. It is important to note that our summary is not exhaustive, and some mechanisms remain unclear. For instance, in studies of endothelial dysfunction caused by chronic inflammation, ApoA1/HDL can exert anti-inflammatory and endothelial – protective effects by increasing AnxA1 to inhibit adhesion molecule expression [163], however, it is unclear whether ApoA1/HDL has this effect in acute inflammation like sepsis.

Fourth, while our study has identified multiple protective roles of ApoA1/HDL on the endothelium during sepsis/severe infection, and these mechanisms may exhibit overlapping and interactive effects to some extent, no study has fully elucidated the precise relationships and interactions between these mechanisms. Therefore, future studies need to further investigate the crosstalk between these pathways to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted roles of ApoA1/HDL in severe acute infections like sepsis.

It is worth noting that while HDL is often termed the “vascular scavenger”, excess can be detrimental. Studies have shown that very high HDL levels (>80 mg/dL) are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality [164, 165], reduced cholesterol efflux capacity [166], and diminished anti-inflammatory function, among other issues. Therefore, focus should not solely be on HDL concentration, but rather on its functionality.

Conclusion

The evidence compellingly reframes the ApoA1/HDL axis from a passive biomarker to a master regulator of endothelial homeostasis, whose depletion is a fundamental driver of microvascular collapse in sepsis. We have charted its protective triad of anti-inflammatory, barrier-protective, and vaso-regulatory actions. The next frontier, however, demands a shift from the systemic to the specific. Future progress will hinge on elucidating its organ-specific roles, deciphering its complex interplay with signaling platforms like exosomes, and developing next-generation therapeutics that can overcome current translational hurdles. Successfully addressing these challenges will pave the way for a new era of precision medicine, transforming this lipoprotein into a powerful therapy against sepsis.
