First applications of ultrasound technology in solid rocket propellant combustion promotion
Authors: Zhan Wen, Heng Jing, Feng Hao, Yuheng Wang, Bicheng Kuang, Peijin Liu, Wen Ao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe regulation of propellant combustion using ultrasonic waves is proposed. Under ultrasonic frequencies of 25–40 kHz, we systematically examined the combustion characteristics of Al particles, ammonium perchlorate (AP)/hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene (HTPB) propellants, and Al-containing Al/AP/HTPB propellants. Ultrasonic treatment increased the ignition delay time of the Al particles by 48.3 %. However, it increased the burning rate of the AP/HTPB propellants by up to 26.1 % and decreased their ignition delay by 39.3 %. As the ultrasonic frequency increased, the burning rate of the solid Al/AP/HTPB propellants increased by 22.5 %, and the degree of Al agglomeration decreased, resulting in a 24 % decrease in the size of the condensed-phase combustion products. The action mechanism was analyzed in terms of the effects of ultrasonic waves on Al droplets and combustion flames. The introduction of ultrasonic waves split the Al droplets near the burning surface into smaller particles, which affected combustion efficiency. Bringing the diffusion flame closer to the burning surface affected the burning rate. These findings demonstrate that the combustion and agglomeration characteristics of solid propellants can be modified using ultrasonic techniques, providing a new method for controlling the thrust of solid rocket motors.Keywords: Solid composite propellants, Ultrasound, Aluminum particles, Solid rocket motor, Combustion

Introduction

The variable-thrust technology for solid rocket motors (SRMs) has excellent potential in the field of propulsion [1]. Currently, thrust regulation in SRMs primarily involves temperature and pressure control within the combustion chamber [2]. One approach requires modifying the properties of the solid propellant by adjusting its formulation [3], [4], [5], [6]. This modification influences the temperature, pressure, and composition of combustion products. Another method involves altering the structure or external mechanical components of the motor. For example, changing the nozzle throat area using a pivot mechanism is a common technique [7]. However, this approach is complex and can negatively affect the SRM weight and cost. Another option is to use multiple propellant units within a single combustion chamber and share nozzles to achieve thrust control [8]. This technology is still nascent and is characterized by high complexity and low reliability.

Controlling the combustion of propellants is crucial for achieving optimal thrust control. In general, when a solid propellant is ignited, it burns autonomously until it is extinguished, rendering real-time thrust modulation in the SRMs almost impossible. To address this problem, we recently proposed a special technique that used an electric field to adjust the burning rate dynamically [9], enabling online adjustment of the burning rate by over 20 %. However, electrodes are associated with the challenge of high-temperature failure: the high temperatures (approximately 3000 K or higher) generated during solid propellant combustion limit the application of this technique to SRMs. Therefore, new approaches in the variable thrust technology are urgently required.

The development and application of ultrasound have provided new insights into thrust control in SRM [10], [11]. The combustion of composite solid propellants is widely regarded as a chemical reaction involving hydrocarbon fuels, oxidizers, and metallic additives [12], [13], [14]. Ultrasound can cause the fragmentation of particles such as asphaltene [15], and coal dust[16]. This fragmentation also enhances the combustion performance of fuel particles [17]. Therefore, ultrasound may also cause the rupture of Al powder during the combustion of propellants, thereby improving their combustion efficiency. The combustion of ammonium perchlorate (AP)/hydroxyl-terminated polybutadiene (HTPB) involves the combustion of hydrocarbon fuels. Ultrasound significantly influences the gaseous flames of hydrocarbon fuels. In addition, ultrasound facilitates the evaporation of hydrocarbon fuels [18], reduces their ignition delay time [19], and changes the height and color of their flame [20]. The high-temperature region of the flame gradually expands [21], and excitation enhances the combustion of the flame [22], [23], thereby improving the combustion efficiency of hydrocarbon fuels [24]. Lee [25] found that ultrasound changes the flame structure and propagation speed, causing a distortion of the flame front and changes in the combustion zone. Standing ultrasound waves can effectively improve flame stability and extend flame extinction limits, and flame stability is closely related to standing wave position [26].

The combustion of solid propellants under the influence of ultrasound represents a significant research gap. The mechanisms of the effect of ultrasound on propellant flames are unclear and have not been effectively integrated with practical combustion experiments. However, ultrasound has been hypothesized to beneficially influence the combustion process by altering flame morphology on the surfaces of solid propellants [27]. In general, the sound pressure generated by an ultrasonic wave acts on the propellant burning surface, which increases the ambient pressure near the burning surface. This increases the propellant diffusion mixing and chemical reaction rate, reducing the distance between the diffusion flame and the propellant burning surface, and improving the thermal feedback of the burning surface, thereby increasing the burning rate of the propellant. Furthermore, ultrasound may increase combustion efficiency by optimizing the agglomeration characteristics of Al particles within propellants [28], presenting considerable potential for practical implementation.

Therefore, investigating the combustion of composite solid propellants under ultrasound from the perspective of metal particles and AP/HTPB propellants is necessary. This paper proposes a technique for regulating propellant combustion using ultrasound and presents experiments in which ultrasound is applied to Al particles, AP/HTPB propellants, and composite solid propellants. In this study, different ultrasound frequencies were employed to modify the ignition and combustion processes of Al particles and propellants. The effects of ultrasound frequency on the ignition delay, combustion flame, propellant burning rate, and condensed-phase combustion products (CCPs) were analyzed. The influence mechanisms of ultrasound on gas reactions, Al agglomeration, and the evolution of CCPs are discussed, providing insights for optimizing ultrasound in the combustion of composite solid propellants and highlighting new approaches to controlling the combustion and thrust of SRMs.

Experimental setup and methodology

Al particles, AP/HTPB propellants, and Al-based composite propellants were used in the experiments. The Al particles have a diameter of 1 mm and purity of 99.99 %. The formulation of the AP/HTPB propellant consist of 80 wt% AP (200 μm) and 20 wt% HTPB. The composition of the Al-based composite propellant includes 70 wt% AP (200 μm) as an oxidizer, 12 wt% HTPB as a adhesive, and 18 wt% Al (13 μm) as a metal additive.

Fig. 1(a) presents a schematic of the experimental setup used for laser ignition under ultrasound. The equipment consisted of a carbon dioxide laser, ultrasound control system (generator and oscillator), high-speed camera, and data acquisition system. The ultrasound generator had a power of 200 W, and the effective power of a transducer applied on the powder or propellant grain was 60 W. The distance from the ultrasonication nozzle to the powder or propellant grain was 30 mm. The effective area of the transducer was approximately 8000 mm2. The ultrasound oscillator was operated at frequencies of 25, 33, and 40 kHz. We chose 25, 33, and 40 kHz because these frequencies are close to the resonant frequencies of the aluminum particle size of our experimental propellant, and this is the minimum ultrasonic frequency interval available from the experimental instrument supplier. During the Al particle laser ignition experiment, the Al particles were securely held onto a base using four top needles. The base was then moved to align the Al particles with the laser position. Various ultrasound experiments were conducted by varying the frequencies of the ultrasonic oscillators. The high-speed camera (Mirage M340, USA) was used to record the combustion process at a rate of 2000 fps, and the laser and high-speed camera were synchronized. The ignition delay time and flame intensities of individual Al particles under different experimental conditions were determined by processing and analyzing the experimental images captured by the high-speed camera. Because of the low intensity of the light emitted during the combustion of Al particles, the method for measuring ignition delay using a spectrometer was not applicable in this case [29]. To address this limitation, we incorporated a light-emitting diode (LED) into the experimental setup. When the laser was activated, a voltage signal triggered the LED to emit light, which was captured by the high-speed camera. The time between this moment and the ignition of the Al particles is defined as the ignition delay time. In addition, the captured images of the burning Al particles were processed using the ImageJ software to obtain grayscale images. This software calculates the sum of grayscale values to evaluate the flame intensity during combustion.

(a) Schematic of the system used to examine the effect of ultrasound on the laser ignition of Al particles. (b) System used for investigating the effect of ultrasound on propellant combustion.

Experiments were conducted to investigate the influence of ultrasound on the combustion of AP/HTPB propellants and Al-containing composite solid propellants. Fig. 1(b) depicts a schematic of the experimental system used for propellant combustion under ultrasound irradiation. The system consisted of a propellant ignition system, ultrasound control system (generator and oscillator), high-speed camera, spectrometer, data acquisition system, and product collection system. Due to the limitation of experimental conditions, the space of our tester can only be satisfied to provide the ultrasonic transducer on one side. The propellant ignition system comprised a DC power supply and nichrome wire. The Joule heating effect of the nichrome wire under DC power was used to generate high temperatures for propellant ignition. The product collection system consisted of an alumina crucible filled with distilled water placed beneath the propellant to collect, filter, and dry the CCPs. The spectrometer was used to measure the ignition delays of the propellants. Initially, the spectral intensity data were obtained at different wavelengths over time. Spectral wavelengths of 586 and 486 nm were selected as the characteristic spectra of the AP/HTPB propellant [29], and Al-based solid propellant [30], respectively, to represent the combustion process. The ignition delay is defined as the time at which the combustion intensity first reaches 10 % of the maximum intensity [28]. The ignition delay time of Al by the LED light was measured. The LED light was connected to the laser igniter. When the laser igniter started functioning, the LED light began to emit light from this moment until the Al particles were ignited, and the timer was stopped. At this time, the recorded time was the ignition delay time of the Al particles. The images captured by the camera were processed using the ImageJ software to obtain the pixel coordinates of the parallel regression of the propellant burning surface. The parallel regression distance of the propellant burning surface were determined by converting the pixel points into the actual length of the propellant. Dividing this distance by the parallel regression time yielded the propellant burning rate. The ultrasound frequencies for the propellant combustion experiments were set to 0, 25, 33, and 40 kHz. Three repeated experiments were conducted at each ultrasound frequency to obtain the average values and minimize experimental uncertainties.

Results and discussion

Combustion of single Al particle

The four sets of images in Fig. 2(a) present typical Al particle ignition experiments using a laser ignition apparatus at ultrasound frequencies of 0, 25, 33, and 40 kHz. The combustion images can be divided into four stages: heating, ignition, combustion, and extinction. During the first two stages, the combustion images show minimal changes. However, during the intense combustion stage, without the influence of ultrasound, the burning flame of the Al particles remains stable [31]. When ultrasound is introduced with the ultrasound oscillator placed on the right side of the Al particles, the structure of the combustion flame bends noticeably to the left. The higher the frequency of the ultrasound oscillator, the more pronounced the deviation in the flame structure of the Al particle. This finding is consistent with the observations of Kim [32], who reported that when a flame passes through a locally affected ultrasound field, the flame front undergoes distortion. The location and characteristics of distortion are closely related to the properties of the acoustic field. Therefore, ultrasound can induce oscillations in flame structures. At the same ultrasound frequency, the angle of flame deviation oscillates back and forth over time. This indicates that the oscillation frequency of the burning flame of the Al particles is influenced by the frequency of the ultrasound oscillator. Although the excitation frequency of the ultrasound oscillator is considerably higher than the natural resonance frequency of the Al particle flame, high-frequency ultrasound still significantly influences the flame structure owing to the substantial energy generated [33]. The temperature and viscosity coefficient outside and inside the flame are different, and the effect of high-frequency ultrasonic field on the inside and outside the flame is different [34]. At an ultrasound frequency of 25 kHz, an explosion was observed during the combustion of the Al particles (Fig. 2a). At this ultrasound frequency, the molten Al droplets produced during the melting of the Al particles may experience explosions because of the nonuniform heat transfer caused by changes in the flame structure. When the ultrasound frequency is 33 kHz, small fragmented Al droplets appear at the tail of the burning flame of the Al particles. At this ultrasound frequency, the force exerted by the ultrasound on the Al droplets exceeds the surface tension of the droplets, leading to the fragmentation of their surfaces and generation of smaller droplets [35].

(a) Comparison of ignition and combustion processes of Al particles under various ultrasonic frequencies. (b) Combustion intensities of Al particles. (c) Ignition delay times of Al particles.

Fig. 2(b) shows the combustion intensities of the combustion flame and ignition delay times of the Al particles subjected to ultrasound. The flame intensity of the Al particles decreases significantly when ultrasound is applied, and the combustion flame intensity of the Al particles decreases with an increase in ultrasonic frequency. The application of ultrasound compresses the flame of the Al particles, and the bright area captured by the camera decreases (Fig. 2a). The flame images were converted into gray scale using ImageJ, the average pixel count was extracted, and this parameter was used as an indicator of the combustion intensity. The grayscale values representing the combustion intensity of the Al particles decreases significantly, and this compression becomes more pronounced at higher ultrasonic frequencies. In addition, the thermoacoustic coupling effect during the combustion process leads to instability and a decrease in combustion intensity [36]. In the upper-right corner of the Al particle laser ignition image in Fig. 2(a), a faint spot generated by the LED light can be observed. We measure the ignition delay time of aluminum by LED light. The LED light is connected to the laser igniter, when the laser igniter begins to work, the LED light begins to emit light, from this moment on, until the aluminum particles are ignited when the timing stops, at this time the recorded time is the ignition delay time of the aluminum particles. Fig. 2(c) shows the ignition delay times of the Al particles under the influence of ultrasound. The ignition delay time of the Al particles is the shortest without ultrasound. When ultrasound is applied, the ignition delay time of the Al particles increases. However, the increase in ignition delay time is the smallest at 33 kHz. The formation of a crystalline oxide layer on the surfaces of the Al particles is caused by the polycrystalline phase transition of Al and the competition between the growth and removal rates of the Al particle oxide layer. For particles more than 100 μm, time scales of polymorphic phase transformations are smaller than those of melting [37]. The introduction of ultrasound promotes the reaction between Al and oxygen in the internal liquid, facilitating the formation of an oxide layer on the Al particles surface. However, compression of the gas phase by ultrasound hinders the diffusion of gaseous oxides and removal of the oxide layer [38]. Consequently, the time required for the melting and removal of the oxide layer on the Al particle surface increases, leading to an increase in the ignition delay time of the Al particles. Although the compression effect is the most pronounced and least conducive to gaseous oxide diffusion at an ultrasonic frequency of 33 kHz, the time required for the oxide layer to break owing to Rayleigh fragmentation decreases, so compared to 25 kHz and 40 kHz, the ignition delay at 33 kHz is the further decrease [35].

Combustion of non − aluminum propellant

Fig. 3(a) shows the burning images of the AP/HTPB propellant captured by the high-speed camera. Similar to the Al particle ignition experiment, the effect of the ultrasound on the flame structure is visible. Without ultrasound application, the propellant combustion flames are perpendicular to the burning surface, and the burning flame is stable. However, after applying the ultrasound, we can visualize the flame deflecting perpendicular to the burning surface of the propellant, the AP/HTPB propellant combustion flame exhibits a noticeable tendency to deviate toward the right. The ultrasound transducer was placed on the left of the propellant. The higher the frequency of the ultrasound transducer, the more pronounced the rightward deviation of the propellant combustion flame [25].

(a) Images of AP/HTPB propellants under ultrasound at various frequencies. (b) Ignition delay times and burning rates; (c) Maximum spectra under ultrasound at various frequencies.

The experimental results for the ignition delay time and burning rate of the AP/HTPB propellant under the influence of ultrasound are presented in Fig. 3(b). The ignition delay time of the AP/HTPB propellant initially increases and then decreases with increasing ultrasound frequency. The maximum ignition delay occurs at an ultrasound frequency of 25 kHz. The ignition delay times at ultrasound frequencies of 33 and 40 kHz are shorter than that in the case without ultrasound. For the AP/HTPB propellant, the ignition delay is characterized by the combined thermal decomposition time of the propellant components, mixing time of the decomposition products, and chemical reaction time of the thermal decomposition products, which decreases with increasing pressure [39]. As the frequency of ultrasound loading increases, the pressure variations in the gas near the burning surface increase, resulting in an increase in sound pressure and a corresponding decrease in the ignition delay time. Furthermore, the propellant burning rate increases with the ultrasound frequency. The burning image of the propellant indicates that combustion follows the parallel-layer combustion trend [40]. The emission spectra were collected during the combustion of the AP/HTPB propellant, as shown in Fig. 3(c). The spectral intensity of the propellant is the highest without ultrasound. When ultrasound is applied, the spectral intensity of the propellant gradually decreases with increasing ultrasound frequency. This decrease in spectral intensity indicates weakening of the combustion intensity of the propellant. When ultrasound is applied, the oscillation of the sound is coupled with the fluctuation of the heat release rate of the flame, causing expansion or contraction of the surrounding gas and energy transfer between the flame and the acoustic field. This energy transfer process can lead to combustion instability and a reduction in combustion intensity [42].

Combustion of aluminized propellant

Fig. 4(a) shows the combustion images of the propellant. The application of ultrasound affects the flame structure of the propellant, causing fluctuations in the flame. The higher the frequency of the ultrasound oscillator, the more pronounced the fluctuations in the combustion flame of the propellant. At an ultrasound frequency of 33 kHz, more small Al droplets appear in the combustion image of the composite propellant, suggesting that the 33 kHz ultrasound promotes their generation. Ultrasound causes the surfaces of Al droplets to break, decreasing the sizes of the droplets. Fig. 4(b) shows the ignition delay time and burning rate of the propellant. Without ultrasound, the propellant exhibits the shortest ignition delay. With the application of ultrasound, the ignition delay time of the Al particles increases. However, the increase in ignition delay at 33 kHz is the smallest among the ultrasound frequencies. The ignition delay of a composite propellant is closely related to its composition. Ultrasound can facilitate the ignition of the AP/HTPB propellant. However, when Al particles ignite, a large area of Al oxide shell is formed on the surface of the Al droplets, requiring more heat and thermal expansion stress to melt and remove the Al oxide layer [43]. This eventually results in an increase in ignition delay time. Regarding the burning rate, the propellant without ultrasound exhibits the lowest burning rate. With the application of ultrasound, the burning rate of the propellant initially increases and then decreases with increasing ultrasound frequency. The thermal decomposition of AP and HTPB significantly affects the composite propellant burning rate. The higher the acoustic pressure generated by ultrasound, the more significant the gas-phase reaction and diffusion rates of the propellant [44]. As the diffusion flame approaches the burning surface of the propellant, it enhances the thermal feedback to the burning surface, thereby increasing the burning rate. Moreover, the addition of ultrasound promotes the fragmentation of Al particles, enhancing their combustion and improving the burning rate. This beneficial effect is particularly strong at an ultrasound frequency of 33 kHz [45]. With the combination of both factors, the propellant burning rate increases, and the increase is the highest at an ultrasound frequency of 33 kHz. Fig. 4(c) shows the spectral intensity of propellant combustion. The combustion intensity of the propellant is the highest when ultrasound is not applied. However, when ultrasound is applied, the combustion intensity of the propellant decreases with an increase in ultrasound frequency. The application of ultrasound may cause a thermal–acoustic coupling effect during the propellant combustion process [46], resulting in combustion instability and, ultimately, a decrease in the combustion intensity of the propellant.

(a) Images of combustion under the influence of ultrasound at various frequencies. (b) Ignition delay times and burning rates; (c) Maximum spectra under ultrasound at various frequencies. (d) Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of CCPs; (e) Particle size distribution of the CCPs.

The combustion product morphology of the propellant was examined by analyzing the collected samples. Fig. 4(d) shows SEM images of the propellant combustion products under the influence of ultrasound at various frequencies. The combustion product particles of the propellant without ultrasound are significantly larger than those of the propellant with ultrasound. When the ultrasound frequency is 33 kHz, the combustion product particles are the smallest. Fig. 4(e) shows the particle size distributions of the propellant combustion products. The particle sizes of the combustion products are concentrated in the range of 10 to 1000 μm. Without ultrasound, a significant portion of the propellant combustion products has particle sizes exceeding 200 μm, with a D43 particle size of 354 μm. With the application of ultrasound, the proportion of combustion product particles with sizes larger than 200 μm decreases at all frequencies. When the frequency is 33 kHz, the D43 particle size of the combustion products decreases to 267 μm, representing a reduction of 24 %. The application of ultrasound effectively reduces the particle size of the CCPs and causes the Al droplets formed during the propellant combustion process to split into smaller droplets, thus leading to the formation of smaller condensed-phase combustion products. This is consistent with the results of combustion experiments involving Al particles.

Discussions

Ultrasound significantly influences the combustion and agglomeration of solid composite propellants. The influence of ultrasonic waves on propellant combustion can be explained from two perspectives, that is, flame structure and Al particle behavior, which are reflected by an increase in the propellant burning rate and a decrease in the CCPs particle size of the propellant, respectively.

When ultrasound is applied to the burning propellant, the Al particles are controlled by the balance between ultrasound and surface tension (Fig. 5a). Ultrasound causes vibration of Al droplets, whereas surface tension stabilizes the Al droplets. When the force exerted by the ultrasound on the Al droplets exceeds the surface tension, the surfaces of the Al droplets break, reducing the sizes of the droplets. This process increases the relative contact area between the Al particles and the oxidizer, thereby increasing the burning rate of the Al particles, improving the heat feedback, and increasing the propellant burning rate. The vibration frequency of Al droplets with diameters of 10–18 μm ranges between 25 and 48 kHz, which is close to the ultrasound frequency used in our experiments. The resonant frequency of particles with a diameter of 13 μm is 33 kHz. Under the influence of ultrasound, Al droplets with diameters of 10–18 μm resonate, inhibiting the aggregation of small Al droplets into larger droplets, thereby reducing the CCPs particle size of the propellant [47]. This effect is the strongest when the ultrasonic frequency is 33 kHz.

The fragmentation behavior of the Al droplets during the combustion of the propellant is depicted in Fig. 5(c). The droplets are within the same diffusion flame until they complete the splitting process. When the droplet initiates splitting, both the bright oxidation cap and the darker aluminum droplet start to deform. As the droplet deforms, the diffusion flame also changes. Ultimately, the droplet divides into two separate droplets. This verifies the influence of ultrasonic waves on the behavior of Al particles in the propellant. Ultrasonic waves cause Al particles to fragment during combustion, which increases their burning rate. The fragmentation behavior of Al droplets during combustion further reduces the CCPs particle size and improves combustion efficiency.

In composite solid propellants, the AP/HTPB diffusion flame significantly influences the propellant burning rate [48]. The AP/HTPB diffusion flame primarily affects the AP/HTPB thermal decomposition rate. The higher the diffusion mixing and chemical reaction rates of the propellant, the closer the diffusion flame moves to the burning surface of the propellant. Within a specific range, an increase in environmental pressures results in increased diffusion and chemical reaction rates. As shown in Fig. 5(b), the acoustic pressure of the ultrasound acts on the burning surface of the propellant, which increases the environmental pressure near the burning surface. This results in an increase in the diffusion mixing and chemical reaction rates of the propellant, decreasing the distance between the diffusion flame and the burning surface of the propellant. This phenomenon also improves the thermal feedback of the burning surface, consequently improving the propellant burning rate.

This mechanism was validated via numerical calculations based on the AP/HTPB propellant, for which we established a sandwich combustion model [49]. The AP/HTPB propellant combustion is controlled by the diffusion of gases produced by the decomposition of AP and HTPB. The thermal decomposition of AP is calculated in a single global reaction step [50]. The gas-phase dynamics are governed by the Navier–Stokes equation. The effect of ultrasonic waves on the AP/HTPB propellant was investigated by varying the pressure near the burning surface. To simulate the combustion of the propellant under the influence of ultrasonic waves, we simulated the pressure changes near the burning surface by varying the outlet pressure. The frequency of the outlet pressure variation was 33 kHz, and the magnitude of the outlet pressure was the atmospheric pressure plus the sound pressure, P, induced by the ultrasonic waves. We extracted the combustion results of the propellant over half a cycle (Fig. 5d). When the ultrasonic waves are in a trough, the diffusion flame of the propellant decreases, and the distance between the flame and the burning surface increases. When the ultrasonic waves are at a peak, the diffusion flame of the propellant increases, and the distance decreases. The calculation results indicate that the average propellant burning rate over half a cycle increases by 8 % without the application of ultrasonic waves. Overall, the application of ultrasonic waves increases the propellant burning rate.

Conclusions

We investigated the influence of ultrasound on the combustion characteristics of Al particles, AP/HTPB propellants, and Al-containing composite solid propellants. The combustion of Al-containing composite propellants results from the combined effects of Al particles and AP/HTPB combustion. As the ultrasonic frequency increased, the ignition delay time of the Al particles increased. Ultrasonic treatment promoted the fragmentation of Al droplets, preventing them from coalescing into larger droplets. When ultrasonic vibration was applied to the AP/HTPB propellant, it brought the diffusion flame closer to the burning surface, thereby decreasing the ignition delay time of the propellant and increasing the thermal feedback and burning rate. Under the dual effects of ultrasound on Al droplets and combustion flames, the Al/AP/HTPB propellant burning rate increased, the degree of agglomeration of the Al particles decreased, and thus, the CCP size decreased.

However, the operating pressure of solid propellants often exceeds 10 MPa. Therefore, a next step is to develop a high-pressure apparatus to investigate the combustion mechanism of the propellant subjected to ultrasound under high-pressure conditions and consider the application of ultrasonic waves in the motor. Over time, this can potentially clarify how ultrasound can be used to regulate the combustion and thrust of propellants in SRMs. Additionally, we will further analyze the factors that influence ultrasound and establish a more detailed mechanistic model.
