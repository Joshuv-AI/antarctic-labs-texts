Simulation-Based Education (SBE) in paediatric undergraduate medical education: a scoping review
Authors: Farida Eid, Rida Maryum, Lucy Waugh, Paddy Kilian, William Atiomo, Suleiman Al-Hammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintaining a workforce capable of providing safe and effective care is a critical priority of healthcare systems worldwide, with growing concerns about paediatric workforce and increasing demand on acute services. Ensuring that graduating doctors can recognise and initiate management of acutely ill children is therefore an essential competency in medical education. However, opportunities for medical students to gain experience in paediatric emergencies during undergraduate clinical placements are often limited, unpredictable and ethically constrained. Consequently, students may graduate with little exposure to rare but high-stakes paediatric scenarios despite being expected to recognise clinical deterioration and initiate early management. Paediatric simulation-based education (SBE) therefore provides a safe environment that allows deliberate practice of complex clinical situations without risk to patients and has been shown to improve clinical performance, knowledge, technical skills and communication abilities. Despite these advantages, integration of paediatric SBE into undergraduate curricula remains variable. This scoping review aims to map the current literature on SBE in paediatric undergraduate training, examining educational modalities, outcomes and barriers to implementation.MethodsThis scoping review followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane) were searched for studies published between January 2010 and July 2025. Inclusion criteria focused on undergraduate medical students, paediatric simulation training, and empirical study designs. Data were extracted on study characteristics, modalities, outcomes, and barriers to implementation.ResultsThirty studies met the inclusion criteria. The United States (n = 10), France (n = 3), and Brazil (n = 3) were the most represented countries. Randomized controlled trials (n = 11) and quasi-experimental designs (n = 10) predominated. High-fidelity manikins and virtual reality were the most common modalities. Outcomes included improvements in clinical reasoning, procedural skills, and student confidence, measured using OSCEs and validated tools. Reported barriers to implementation included financial cost, faculty training, and maintenance of technical equipment. No studies from the Middle East/North Africa region were identified in the specified timeframe.ConclusionsSimulation-based education enhances paediatric undergraduate training and prepares learners to manage rare but critical scenarios, supporting its integration into medical curricula. The limited number of studies from the Middle East/North Africa region highlights an important geographical gap in literature. Future research should explore long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and strategies for sustainable, context-specific implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09730-6.Keywords: Simulation-based education, Paediatric medical training, Undergraduate medical education, High-fidelity simulation

Introduction

Over the past two decades, there has been an exponential growth in the use of simulation-based education (SBE), driven by advances in technology, evolving educational theory, and the need for safer and more effective learning environments. Within undergraduate medical education (UME), SBE is widely recognised as an effective modality to improve clinical performance, knowledge acquisition, technical skills, and communication abilities. It provides a safe and controlled environment for deliberate practice, reduces risk to patients and supports mastery learning through repeated exposure to clinical scenarios [1–6].

Alongside these educational developments, healthcare systems worldwide face increasing pressure to maintain a workforce capable of delivering safe and effective pediatric care. In many countries, concerns are growing regarding declining interest in pediatric careers and the increasing demands on acute pediatric services. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) health workforce strategy, these issues are part of a broader global trend characterized by significant workforce shortages and an uneven distribution of health specialists, necessitating more robust global governance and strategic planning [7]. Similar workforce challenges have been reported across multiple regions including the United States, Europe, South America, Asia, Australia and the Middle East [8–15].

Preparing medical graduates to manage acutely unwell children presents challenges within undergraduate education. The complexity of paediatric pathophysiology, the need for specialized communication skills, and the inherently limited opportunities for real-life clinical exposure to acute and critical events create a significant gap in training. Simulation-based education (SBE) has become a vital pedagogical tool to bridge this gap, with evidence supporting its use for practicing procedural and decision-making skills in a risk-free environment [16, 17]. Specifically, high-fidelity simulation (HFS) has been shown to offer superior knowledge retention and clinical performance compared to traditional case-based discussions. Additionally, these studies highlight how interprofessional education (IPE) within pediatric simulation fosters the communication skills essential for clinical practice. Despite these advantages, inconsistencies in its integration across curricula persist, with barriers such as resource limitations, faculty training requirements, and institutional support affecting its implementation [18].While studies highlight the effectiveness of simulation in improving knowledge acquisition, clinical skills, and learner confidence, significant evidence gaps remain regarding its optimal use, standardization, and long-term impact in paediatric undergraduate medical education (UME). The current literature demonstrates considerable heterogeneity in simulation modalities, assessment methods, and implementation strategies, highlighting the need for a comprehensive mapping of this evolving field. Furthermore, the geographical distribution of existing research remains largely unexplored, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings across diverse educational contexts. This scoping review aims to address these gaps by examining the current landscape of paediatric SBE in UME through a systematic analysis of its efficacy, modalities, learning outcomes, and implementation challenges. Specifically, it seeks to evaluate the evidence supporting paediatric SBE integration into standard medical curricula while identifying critical research gaps and geographical disparities that may inform future educational initiatives and research priorities.

Materials and methods

Study design, search strategy, and selection criteria

A scoping review of the literature was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines [19]. To ensure a comprehensive exploration of the literature, this review included all study designs (randomized controlled trials, observational studies, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews) published over the last 15 years (1st January 2010–July 15th, 2025) from four primary databases: Scopus, Cochrane, Embase, and PubMed. Restricting the search to the past 15 years ensured the inclusion of the most recent and relevant data on SBE in paediatric UME, while maintaining a focused and manageable scope. The decision to exclude studies beyond this timeframe was based on the rapid advancements in medical simulation technologies and pedagogical approaches, ensuring that only contemporary and applicable evidence was reviewed.

The search terms were developed informed by the Population–Concept–Outcome (PCO) framework in Table 1., in consultation with the Al Maktoum Medical Library team, at the Mohammed Bin Rashid University in Dubai, United Arab Emirates, who assisted in refining the search strategy and Boolean combinations. The Population included undergraduate medical education students, with MeSH terms such as Education, Medical, Undergraduate, Pediatrics / Education, Students, Medical, and Clerkship, Clinical, and relevant keywords including Education, Undergraduate Medical, Simulation-Based Medical Education, High Fidelity Simulation Training/Trends, Clerkship, and Clinical Rotation. The Concept centered on simulation-based education, with MeSH terms including Education, Medical / Methods, High Fidelity Simulation Training / Methods, Simulation Training, Patient Simulation, and High Fidelity Simulation Training/Methods, and keywords such as Simulation in Healthcare, Pediatric/Paediatric Simulation, Simulation, Augmented Reality Systems, Simulation Training / Methods, and Healthcare Simulation. The Outcome focused on educational effectiveness and skill development, with MeSH terms including Clinical Competence, Qualitative Research, Program Evaluation, Educational Measurement, and Academic Performance, and keywords such as Assessment, Quality and Patient Safety, Qualitative Assessment, Educational Output, Results, and Academic Success. A Boolean search strategy was developed and tailored to each database to maximize sensitivity and relevance.

Data extraction

Following the initial literature search, two reviewers (FFE and RM) then independently accessed the databases and screened the title and abstracts of all identified articles, exporting pertinent articles into an Excel File Spreadsheet. Any discrepancies in screening decisions were resolved through discussion and consensus between the two reviewers, with involvement of a third reviewer (LW) available for arbitration if needed. Upon the completion of data collection, a collective reassessment of all articles was conducted resulting in the exclusion of any articles that focused on postgraduate medical education research, general medical simulation without paediatric applications, and studies involving non-medical students (nursing/allied health). Non-empirical publications like commentaries and editorials were omitted, and only English-language studies were considered. Duplicate records across databases were systematically removed during screening. Upon applying our exclusion criteria and removing the duplicates, eligible full-text articles were identified for review and data extraction.

The eligible full-text articles were fully reviewed to extract the following variables: year of publication, country of study, study design as defined by the authors, simulation modality utilized, sample size of medical student participants, and target learner group. Studies were categorized into the six official World Health Organization (WHO) regions: the African Region (AFRO), the Region of the Americas (AMRO), the South-East Asia Region (SEARO), the European Region (EURO), the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMRO), and the Western Pacific Region (WPRO) [20]. To characterize the educational scope and outcomes, data were collected on study aims, specific learning outcomes assessed, and the assessment tools used for measurement. Furthermore, key findings regarding educational efficacy and reported barriers to implementation were systematically documented (Fig. 1).

Results

Description of the studies

Table 1 shows the PRISMA flowchart of the selection process. A total of 531 relevant articles were identified across four electronic databases (Scopus, Cochrene, Embase, and Pubmed) using the search terms outlined in the methodology section. Following the application of the exclusion criteria, 95 articles remained for abstract screening. After further applying the inclusion criteria to these studies, 30 eligible full-text articles were found to fulfill the requirements and were identified for final review and data extraction.

Geographically, the United States of America was the most represented country, accounting for ten studies [21–30], followed by Brazil [31–33] and France [34–36] with three studies each. Germany [37, 38], the United Kingdom [39, 40], India [41, 42], China [43, 44], and Australia [45, 46] each contributed two studies. Single studies were conducted in Austria [47], Pakistan [48], Japan [49], and Sri Lanka [50].

Study designs were predominantly interventional and comparative. Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) were the most common [21–23, 27, 28, 37, 38, 44, 47], followed by various quasi-experimental designs [32–35, 49], including non-randomized controlled [31], comparative [35], and prospective studies [33]. Other designs included cohort studies [25, 30], mixed-methods studies [46, 48], and single instances of cluster-randomized trials [39, 40], qualitative studies [24], prospective observational studies [42], controlled studies [43], educational interventions [26], prospective sequential trials [34], and a review article [41]. Sample sizes varied considerably, ranging from small, focused studies (n = 17) [28] to large-scale interventions (n = 806) [36].

The target learners were uniformly undergraduate medical students, with several studies focusing on specific cohorts such as clerkship students [22], final-year students [50], and early-phase students [46]. One study specifically targeted program directors rather than students [41] (Table 2).

Characteristics of Included Studies

Simulation modalities and educational applications

The scoping review identified numerous simulation modalities implemented across paediatric medical education, as detailed in Table 3, which highlights a diverse technological landscape. High-fidelity manikins were frequently utilized for teaching paediatric emergencies [31, 32], respiratory diseases [33], therapeutics [50], and OSCE preparation [49]. Virtual reality emerged as a prominent modality for emergency response training [47], congenital heart disease education [22], clinical assessment of respiratory distress [23, 29], airway management [27], and user experience evaluation [24]. Other technologies included virtual patients for BLS training [37], serious games for clinical reasoning [21], simulation games for asthma management [34], and telesimulation for remote training [48]. Hybrid approaches combined manikins with standardized patients for bronchopneumonia management [43] and integrated flipped classrooms with simulation for clinical readiness [46].

Simulation modalities, educational outcomes, assessment methods, implementation barriers, and key findings from included studies

Learning outcomes and assessment methods

As summarized in Table 3, studies assessed diverse learning outcomes using various measurement tools. Clinical performance was evaluated through OSCEs [31, 38, 42, 49] and structured clinical examinations. Knowledge acquisition was measured using pre-post-tests [32, 50], retention tests [32], and validated assessment tools [22]. Technical skills were assessed using performance checklists [33], algorithm adherence forms [37], and procedure-specific metrics [26, 28]. Affective domains including confidence [39], satisfaction [32, 45], stress levels [48], and user experience [24] were evaluated through validated questionnaires and rating scales. Clinical reasoning was assessed through clinical management scenarios [21], reasoning scores [44], and comprehensive evaluations [43], demonstrating a multidimensional approach to competency assessment in simulation-based education.

Implementation, considerations and effectiveness

Analysis of implementation factors revealed consistent barriers across studies, as detailed in Table 3. Resource limitations represented the primary challenge, with cost constraints affecting high-fidelity manikins [31, 33], virtual reality [22, 45, 47], surgical simulators [28], and comprehensive curricula [30]. Technical hurdles included equipment maintenance [32], software requirements [37], platform stability [34], internet reliability [48], and motion sickness [22]. Time constraints further hindered scenario development [35, 40], faculty preparation [39, 50], and session coordination [36, 43]. Despite these barriers, simulation-based interventions consistently improved outcomes. High-fidelity modalities enhanced clinical reasoning, leadership, and confidence in recognizing clinical severity [31, 33], while virtual reality proved effective for knowledge retention and skill acquisition [22, 27, 49]. Hybrid and combined approaches demonstrated multi-domain learning gains [39, 43, 46], and telesimulation achieved outcomes comparable to in-person training [25, 48]. Overall, the integration of these curricula led to superior knowledge scores and clinical performance [30], supporting their strategic use in paediatric education.

Discussion

In this scoping review, we mapped 30 primary studies spanning January 2010 to July 2025 that address simulation-based education (SBE) in undergraduate pediatric training. Research was concentrated across 13 countries, predominantly in high- and upper-middle-income regions. The United States led with ten studies, followed by Brazil and France with three each. Conversely, the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMRO) was represented by a single study from Pakistan, the African Region (AFRO) had none, and a stark research gap was identified across the broader Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Our findings demonstrate that SBE significantly enhances clinical reasoning, procedural skills, learner confidence, and knowledge retention in undergraduate pediatric education, aligning with established literature [18, 51]. We identified modality-specific advantages across simulation approaches. High-fidelity simulation excelled at supporting complex clinical reasoning [31, 33], while virtual reality was particularly effective for procedural skill development [22, 27, 29, 47]. Hybrid models successfully combined these approaches to foster broader competency development [36, 38, 43]. Crucially, emerging strategies like tele-simulation [25, 48] and low-fidelity models [38, 42] present scalable, cost-effective solutions to mitigate access and equity challenges in resource-limited settings.

While this review comprehensively maps the current landscape of undergraduate pediatric SBE, several limitations apply. First, our search was restricted to four major medical databases; omitting education-specific databases (e.g., ERIC) or broader indices (e.g., Web of Science) may have excluded relevant papers. Second, our English-language restriction likely exacerbated geographic bias, missing localized research from non-Anglophone regions. This concentration of data from high-income nations and the paucity of research from the MENA and AFRO regions limit the generalizability of these findings to resource-constrained global contexts. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity in study designs, sample sizes (n = 17 to n = 806), and assessment tools across the included studies complicated direct comparisons between interventions. Finally, in line with scoping review methodology, we focused strictly on undergraduate medical education without aggregating statistical outcomes or exploring interprofessional simulation literature, which may have provided further insights into broader training approaches.

Moving from these findings to practical implementation requires balancing modality selection against institutional infrastructure, faculty development, and cost barriers, which are well-documented in prior literature [52]. To optimize pediatric curricula, future research should investigate the optimal timing for SBE integration, whether prior to clinical exposure to establish foundational skills, during early clinical rotations to reinforce learning, or later in training to refine complex clinical reasoning. Longitudinal follow-up studies are also needed to assess long-term knowledge retention and the direct translation of simulation-acquired skills into clinical practice. Furthermore, expanding high-quality research within underrepresented regions remains essential to establish contextually appropriate implementation models and ensure global applicability.

In conclusion, this scoping review supports a clear role for simulation-based education in undergraduate pediatric medical training, with evidence demonstrating improvements in clinical competencies, reasoning skills, and learner confidence across diverse educational contexts. Expanding the evidence base through inclusive and contextually relevant research will be essential to support the sustainable integration of simulation into medical curricula. Ultimately, strengthening collaborative research efforts across institutions and regions will help address existing disparities in access to simulation training and contribute to improving the quality of pediatric medical education globally.
