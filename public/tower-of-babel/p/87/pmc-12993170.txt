Comprehensive evaluation of thermal processing on the edible quality, flavor and starch properties of Chinese yam
Authors: Lin Chen, Jingling Bi, Honglin Li, Liu Yang, Min Zhang, Zelin Li, Huan Kan, Yun Liu, Guijuan Zhao, Changwei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically evaluated the effects of 3 thermal processing (baked, steamed, and boiled) on the overall quality of yam. Results showed that thermal processing significantly affected the appearance, color, sensory acceptability, and nutritional components of yams, especially the soluble sugar and amylose contents, and texture. HS-SPME-GC-MS identified 10 characteristic volatile flavor compounds (VOCs), and 5 contributing most to the overall flavor of yam. LC-MS detected 199 significantly different and 26 unique non-VOCs. These non-VOCs mainly involve the synthesis and metabolism of amino acids, fatty acids, starch, and bioactive compounds. Moreover, thermal processing promoted starch crystalline region disintegration, double helix disassembly, and intermolecular force weakening, thereby enhancing starch hydration (maximum increase nearly 150%) and digestibility (maximum increase to 51.63%). Overall, baking yam has a better flavor, while steaming and boiling yam are easier to digest. These findings provide a theoretical basis for yam processing and consumption.

Introduction

Yam (Dioscorea spp.) is the tuber of a herbaceous plant of the Dioscoreaceae family and is regarded as the fourth largest tuber crop after potatoes, cassava, and sweet potatoes. As an important staple crop and vegetable, it is widely consumed worldwide, especially in Asia, Africa, and Caribbean, ensuring food security for millions of people (Gong et al., 2022; Oliveira et al., 2021; Sukal et al., 2018). The rich nutritional components of yam, such as starch, dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals, as well as bioactive substances like steroid saponins and mucopolysaccharides, are the main manifestations of its edible value (Sahoo et al., 2023). Edible quality, flavor, and starch characteristics of yam are key factors influencing consumer acceptance. These attributes are influenced by various factors, including yam variety and processing methods, with the latter being particularly crucial. At present, there are very limited reports on the systematic evaluation of the impact of different processing methods on the overall qualities of yam.

Flavor is an important component of yam's edible quality and is influenced by various factors such as yam variety, growing conditions, and processing methods (Gong et al., 2021). Chen et al. (2020) identified 84 volatile flavor compounds (VOCs) from 11 Chinese yam and found that variety has a greater impact on the flavor of yam than origin. Thermal reactions during processing generate a variety of VOCs and non-VOCs, which together constitute the unique flavor characteristics of yam. It has been widely reported that different processing methods significantly alter the types and concentrations of VOCs in foods, and starchy foods are no exception (Wang et al., 2023). Research has demonstrated that processing alters the polysaccharide and other bioactive contents of yam (Gong et al., 2021; Gong et al., 2022). Polysaccharides can influence food flavor, texture, and overall sensory experience (mouthfeel) in multiple ways. The also, polysaccharides interact with proteins, lipids, and phenolic substances in food, further affecting the formation and perception of flavor (Shahidi & Oh, 2020). These findings demonstrate that cooking significantly influences yam's flavor attributes. However, research on the flavor differences of yams processed in different methods is rarely reported.

Additionally, starch is a primary component of yam, and alterations in its structure affect yam’s texture, flavor, and overall edible quality. Different processing methods have varying effects on starch, for instance, boiling, steaming, and baking exert distinct effects on the short-range and long-range molecular orderliness of rice starch, resulting in differences in starch digestion rates (Yu et al., 2023); Adding natural ingredients such as inulin and quercetin can also alter the physical and chemical properties as well as the digestibility of starch (Ji et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020). Other studies have reported that the eating quality of yam is also closely associated with the structure and digestibility of its starch and the digestion rate of starch directly affects the postprandial glycemic response (Zou et al., 2023). For processing methods, different processing methods alter the crystalline types and molecular structure of starch in yam, thereby influencing its digestibility (He et al., 2024). Therefore, in-depth investigation of how various cooking methods influence yam starch characteristics will facilitate healthier dietary choices for consumers.

Current studies on the effects of different processing methods on yam have primarily focused on the physicochemical properties, in vitro digestibility of starch, biological characteristics, prebiotic performance and active components (Gong et al., 2021; Gong et al., 2022; He et al., 2024). However, studies on the impact of different processing methods on the overall quality of yam remain scarce. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of 3 processing methods (steaming, boiling and baking) on the eating quality, flavor, taste, and starch characteristics of yam, and to reveal the intrinsic connection between processing methods and yam quality. These investigations will provide more scientific guidance for the production, processing, and consumption of Chinese yam, thereby better meeting consumer’s demands for health and deliciousness.

Materials and methods

Materials

A batch of fresh, mature, uniformly sized and free from mechanical damage iron yam (Dioscorea opposita Thunb.) tubers were procured in May 2024 from Wangqiying Farmers’ Market, Panlong District, Kunming, Yunnan, China (Fig. S1A). After purchase, the yams were stored in foam boxes at 4 °C for future use. Thermostable α-amylase (40,000 U/g), amyloglucosidase (70 U/mg), glucose oxidase/peroxidase (GOPOD) assay kit, amylose content detection kit were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), Beijing Lanjieke Technology Co., Ltd., and Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., respectively. All chemical reagents used were of analytical grade.

Thermal treatments

The thermal treatment of yam was carried out following the method described by Gong et al. (2021) with minor modifications. Fresh yam was washed, peeled, and sliced into circular pieces with a thickness of 5 mm. Subsequently, the yam slices were randomly divided into 4 groups, with 40 slices in each group. Each group was subjected to the following treatments: steaming at 101 kPa for 10 min (steamed yam, SY), baking at 160 °C for 15 min (baked yam, KY), boiling at 94 °C for 10 min (boiled yam, BY), and unprocessed raw yam (raw yam, RY). Following thermal treatment, selected samples were lyophilized at -60 °C for 48 h (SCIENTZ-12N/C freeze-dryer) and stored until analysis. The other samples were immediately used for sensory evaluation, determination of VOCs, non-VOCs, moisture content, and texture, etc.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation was conducted according to the method previously reported by Wang et al. (2024a). A panel of 20 professionally trained master’s students majoring in food science (10 males and 10 females, aged 23–30 years) evaluated yam samples for mouthfeel (X1), taste (X2), color (X3), smell (X4), and glutinous (X5). The evaluation criteria for yam subjected to different processing methods are presented in Table S1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Biology and Food Engineering, Southwest Forestry University. All participants were fully informed of the experimental protocol and provided written informed consent prior to the sensory assessment. and the overall score of samples(X) was calculated according to the following formula:(1)X=0.2X1+0.2X2+0.1X3+0.4X4+0.1X5

Nutrition ingredient analysis

Soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone-H₂SO₄ method described by Jiang et al. (2023). Moisture content, crude protein, and crude fat were determined based on the National Standard of the People's Republic of China GB5009.3-2016, GB5009.5-2016, and GB5009.6-2016, respectively. Total starch and amylose contents were quantified following Jiang et al. (2010) and the manufacturer's protocol of the commercial assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), respectively.

Amylopectin proportion is the total starch proportion minus amylose proportion. All nutritional components are expressed as a percentage of dry weight.

Color and texture analysis

The color of yam was measured using a colorimeter (CM-5, Konica Minolta, China). Before measurement, the colorimeter was calibrated using a white calibration plate. Texture was determined as described by Cheng et al. (2022). In short, the test was conducted using a cylindrical probe with a diameter of 5 millimeters (P/5), at test speed of 1 mm s-1, 30 % compression, and 5 s relaxation, 6 replicates per sample were averaged.

Headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) analysis

The VOCs in yam under different treatment conditions were determined by HS-SPME-GC-MS in accordance with the method of Han et al. (2021). 5 g of non-freeze-dried yam were placed into a headspace vial and equilibrated at 60 °C for 20 min, then inserted into the aged extraction fiber for headspace adsorption for 50 min. Subsequently, the extraction fiber was inserted into the GC injection port for desorption at 250 °C for 5 min and then analyzed.

GC-MS conditions: DB-WAX capillary column was used for GC separation, with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The injection was performed in spitless mode. The temperature program was as follows: The initial temperature was maintained at 40 °C for 3 min, then increased to 200 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained for 3 min, followed by an increase to 240 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and maintained for 3 min. For MS detection, an electron ionization (EI) source with an electron energy of 70 eV was used. The temperatures of the transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole were set at 250 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. Data acquisition was performed in full-scan mode with a mass scan range of 35-550 u. Computer retrieval and comparison of mass spectral information of substance peaks were conducted using the NIST 14.0 standard library. VOCs with a match degree greater than 80 were retained. According to the method described by Cha et al. (2024), the relative odor activity values (ROAV) of VOCs were calculated to analyze the contribution of flavor compounds. A larger ROAV value indicates a greater contribution to the overall flavor profile of the sample. Components with 1 ≤ ROAV ≤ 100 are considered to be the major odor compounds of the sample; components with 0.1 ≤ ROAV < 1 are considered to be important flavor compounds that have an impact on the overall flavor of the sample.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis

The yam sample was weighed and placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, add 600 μL of methanol containing 2-amino-3-(2-chlorophenyl)-propionic acid (4 ppm) was added to correct for extraction and instrumental variability, enable relative quantification, and monitor method stability. After vortex-mixing for 30 s, steel beads were added for grinding (55 Hz, 60 s). The mixture was then sonicated at room temperature for 15 min and subsequently centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C and 12000 rpm. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and analyzed by LC-MS.

LC-MS conditions: The LC analysis was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC System. Chromatography was carried out with an ACQUITY UPLC ® HSS T3 (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) (Waters, USA). The metabolites were analyzed using a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an ESI ion source. The column temperature was 40 °C, the flow rate was 0.30 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2.00 μL. Simultaneous MS1 and MS/MS acquisitions were performed in Full MS-ddMS2 mode (data-dependent MS/MS). The spray voltage was set at 3.50 kV for positive ions and-2.50 kV for negative ions. The sheath gas was 40 arb, the auxiliary gas was set at 10 arb, and the capillary temperature was 325 °C. The primary and secondary resolutions were 70,000 and 17,500, respectively. Dynamic exclusion was used to eliminate redundant MS/MS information. Metabolites were annotated by matching accurate mass and fragmentation patterns against public databases (HMDB, MassBank, LipidMaps) using a mass tolerance of < 30 ppm.

Characterization of yam starch properties and digestibility

Preparation of yam starch

The yam starch was extracted according to the method reported by Liu et al. (2019). Mix yam powder with distilled water at a ratio of 1:8 (w/v), stir for 1 hour, then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 minutes to remove the supernatant, repeat the above operation more than 5 times until the powder appeared white. Then suspend the white granules in a 75% ethanol solution at 4 °C for 12 hours, centrifuge and discard the supernatant. Repeat this operation 5 to 6 times. The precipitate was freeze-dried and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to obtain the yam starch sample.

Water binding capacity (WBC) analysis

The WBC of yam starch was determined by the method described by He et al. (2024). Approximately 5 g of yam starch was suspended in 75 mL distilled water and stirred at 1000 rpm for 1 h at 25 °C. The slurry was then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The wet starch was recentrifuged under identical conditions, the supernatant was again removed, and the pellet was weighed. The WBC was calculated according to the formula :(2)WBC%=MW−MdMd×100%

Mw represents the weight of wet flour (g), and Md represents the weight of dry flour (g).

Thermal properties analysis

The thermal properties of yam starch were analyzed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Mettler DSC3). In short, yam starch was sealed in an aluminum pan, equilibrated at 4 °C for 1 h, then heated from 40 °C to 120 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The onset temperatures (T0), peak temperatures (TP), end temperatures (Tc), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) were determined.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

Yam starch was mixed with dry KBr at a mass ratio of 1:100, then ground and pressed into thin sheets. The infrared spectral information of starch was recorded using an FTIR spectrometer (SHZ-AIII) over the range of 4000-400 cm-1 with a resolution of 8 cm-1 and 64 scans.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

X-ray diffraction patterns (Rigaku Miniflex 600, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å) were recorded to characterize the crystalline structure of yam starch under different treatments. Measurements were performed at 40 kV and 15 mA, scanning 5°-40° (2θ) at 4° min-1 with a 0.02° step. The crystallinity (Xc) was calculated from the XRD patterns using the peak fitting. All data processing and peak area integration were performed with Origin software.

Morphological analysis

The morphology and particle characteristics of yam starch were analyzed using scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 300) after gold-spraying. The acceleration voltage was 3 kV for imaging and 15 kV for EDS mapping, with an SE2 detector.

In vitro digestibility analysis

The in vitro digestibility of yam starch was analyzed according to the method described by He et al. (2024). The contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) were calculated using the following formulas described by Chen et al. (2019).(3)RDS%=G20−FG/TS×0.9×100(4)SDS%=G120−G20/TS×0.9×100(5)RS%=TG−FG/TS×0.9×100−RDS+SDS

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, with each experiment repeated three times. All data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range test with IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software. Different lowercase letters on the tops of the bars indicate significant differences among groups (P < 0.05). The figures were created and processed using Origin 2022, Adobe Illustrator 2021, TBtools-II 2.20, and SIMCA 14.0.

Results and discussion

Sensory and nutritional quality differences of yam after different thermal processes

In terms of sensory attributes, the appearance of yam undergoes obvious changes after being thermal treatment, with the overall color turning yellow. Specifically, BY (boiled) and SY (steamed) result in a faint transparency, while KY (baked) leads to a dry and crusty surface (Fig. 1A). The color difference analysis also revealed significant changes in the brightness (L* value) and yellowness (b* value) of yam after thermal processes (Fig. 1B). In terms of sensory quality (Fig. 1C), the mouthfeel of BY and SY was significantly better than that of KY (P < 0.05), while there was no significant difference between SY and BY (P > 0.05). This might be because boiling and steaming better retain the moisture of yam and enhance the mouthfeel, while baking causes yam severe moisture loss and results in a poor mouthfeel (Zhang et al., 2023). The tastes of the three processed yams were significantly different, and the scores were KY > BY > SY (P < 0.05). In terms of glutinous (adhesiveness), BY scored the highest and KY scored the lowest. Furthermore, the smell score of KY was significantly higher than that of BY and SY (P < 0.05), while there was no significant difference between BY and SY (P > 0.05). Soluble sugar is the primary source of sweetness in foods and directly influence flavor. The soluble sugar content of raw yam (RY) was 3.57%, which is significantly lower than that of KY and SY. (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D), similar to findings in studies on processing potatoes by Yao et al. (2023). In contrast, BY had significantly lower soluble sugar content than RY (P < 0.05), likely due to the cleavage of weak bonds between glycosidic linkages and polysaccharide chains during boiling, causing soluble sugars to dissolve into the processing water (Gong et al., 2022). After processing, the protein content of yam decreased significantly (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E). This might be related to the leaching of water-soluble proteins and the degradation of amino acids caused by high-temperature treatment. Baking significantly reduced the moisture content (Fig. 1F), while steaming and boiling significantly increased it (P < 0.05). The starch contents of KY, SY and BY were all significantly lower than that of RY (66.11%) (Fig. 1G), possibly due to the hydrolysis of starch into free sugars at high temperatures (He et al., 2024).

As shown in Fig. 1H-I, after processing, the contents of amylose and amylopectin in yam changed significantly (P < 0.05). The amylose content decreased in the order RY (17.52 %) > KY (11.38 %) > SY (9.49 %) > BY (8.79 %). KY amylopectin increased significantly, while that in SY and BY decreased significantly (P < 0.05). It is possible that during the thermal process, amylose and amylopectin were hydrolyzed by the yam's own enzymes. However, amylopectin, which contains α-1,6-glycosidic bonds, is less prone to hydrolysis compared with amylose. BY has the lowest amylose content (P < 0.05), which may be related to starch dissolution and the fact that high temperatures cause the molecular chains of amylose to break, reducing the structural units that form resistant starch and thereby lowering its content (Wang et al., 2021a). Except for KY, the fat content of yam decreased significantly after processing (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1J). The reason may be related to the oxidative decomposition of unsaturated fatty acids in yam during high-temperature processing (Li et al., 2025). Overall, processing markedly affects yam nutrients, with the greatest effects on starch and soluble sugars.

Texture difference of yam under different thermal processes

The texture characteristics of yam are shown in Table 1. The hardness of processed yam was significantly lower than that of raw yam, while KY was significantly higher than BY and SY (P < 0.05), with no significant difference observed between BY and SY (P > 0.05). This may be attributed to the gradual gelatinization and expansion of starch granules during processing, which exert pressure on the cell walls, leading to cell wall disruption and loosening of the intercellular layers, thereby softening the yam texture (Huang et al., 2024). For BY and SY, the increased moisture content promotes more extensive gelatinization, resulting in reduced hardness. The higher hardness of KY might be due to starch degradation forming an amylose gel on the yam surface and the rapid evaporation of surface moisture. Both SY and BY exhibited significantly lower chewiness than RY (P < 0.05), with BY being the lowest and KY was higher than RY. It might be because BY absorbs more water than SY, thereby softening the structure of yam more effectively, while KY loses water and has a firm texture. The adhesiveness of KY and BY was significantly lower than that of RY (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between SY and RY (P > 0.05). VOCs differences of yam under different thermal processes

Overall difference of VOCs

As shown in Table S2, a total of 24 VOCs were detected from the 4 yam samples by HS-SPME-GC-MS. Among them, 7, 21, 8, and 8 VOCs were identified in RY, KY, SY, and BY, respectively. These VOCs include alcohols (7 types), aldehydes (6 types), alkanes (4 types), ester (1 type), ketones (2 types), and other components (4 types). It also confirms that raw yam has only a faint flavor, while thermal processes induce the formation of desirable aroma compounds in yam (Lasekan & Teoh, 2019). Moreover, different processing methods induce the formation of distinct VOCs in yam, thereby significantly influencing the volatile flavor profile of the yam.

Differences in VOCs

PCA score plot (Fig. 2A) shows that the VOCs of the 4 samples are different based on the degree of sample clustering and dispersion. The VIP (variable importance in projection) values based on the OPLS-DA model can quantify the contribution of each variable to the category differences between sample groups in OPLS-DA (Fig. 2B), and VIP ≥ 1 is generally considered to play a crucial role in the identification process. In this study, VOCs with VIP > 1 were identified. A total of 18 differential VOCs were obtained by limiting the VIP values of flavor components, including 2-Ethylcyclobutanol, (R,R)-(+)-Hydrobenzoin, dimethylamine, hexanal, 1-Octen-3-ol, 2-Ethylhexan-1-ol, acetophenone, pentanal, phenylsuccinic acid, 2-Methylbutyraldehyde, acetone, 1-Butanol, 3-methyl-2-phenyl-pent-4-en-2-ol, benzeneacetaldehyde, dodecane, 3-Methylbutanal, oxalic acid, butyl propyl ester, and 2,2,3,4-Tetramethylpentane. Analysis of ROAV for VOCs

To further investigate the impact of processing methods on the flavor profile of yam samples, the ROAVs were employed to evaluate the contribution of different processing methods to the overall flavor of yam. Fig. 2D shows that 10 out of the 24 identified VOCs have an impact on the overall flavor of yam (0.1 ≤ ROAV ≤ 100), including alcohols (2-Ethylhexan-1-ol, 1-Butanol, 1-Octen-3-ol), aldehydes (2-Methylbutyraldehyde, 3-Methylbutanal, hexanal, nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde), ester (ethyl acetate), and ketone (acetophenone). Among them, KY contains 8 of these compounds, SY contains 3, BY contains 3. As shown in Table 2, 5 VOCs with 1 ≤ ROAV ≤ 100 were identified as the major contributors to the flavor profile of yam, including aldehydes (3-Methylbutanal, hexanal, nonanal), alcohols (1-Octen-3-ol, 1-Butanol). Specifically, KY contains 4 of these compounds, SY contains 2, BY contains 2, and RY contains 1. Although there are differences in the types of VOCs among 4 samples, hexanal is a common VOC that distinguishes KY, SY, and BY from RY after processing. This indicates that yam processed by different methods all exhibit flavors described as “fresh, green, fatty, aldehydic, grass, leafy, fruity, and sweaty.” The common characteristic VOCs shared by RY and KY is 1-Octen-3-ol, but the content of KY is relatively low. 1-Octen-3-ol, also known as mushroom alcohol, is a higher alcohol with a fatty composition and has a flavor similar to mushrooms and soil. Among the 10 flavor compounds, KY contains the highest number of aldehydes, totaling 5. Yam inherently contains diverse volatiles such as aldehydes, alcohols, ketones and esters, generated mainly through Maillard reactions, lipid oxidation, and thermal degradation of carbohydrates and proteins. During processing, these compounds undergo further thermal cleavage, oxidation and Maillard reaction that create new aroma compounds or shift existing ratios, producing a volatile profile markedly different from that of the raw tuber (Chen et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). However, the differences in heat transfer media and temperature conditions among different processing methods (such as steaming, boiling, stir-frying, and baking) determine the direction and degree of reactions between soluble sugars, proteins, and starch in the yam, as well as the moisture content, affecting the generation, transformation, and retention of volatile substances, leading to significant flavor differences in yams processed by different heat treatment methods (Zhu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). These can also be confirmed by the changes in the content of proteins, fats, and soluble sugars in the yam. Therefore, processing increases the number of flavor compounds in yam, with KY showing the most pronounced changes. So combining sensory evaluation and key flavor compounds reveals that different processing methods produce distinct VOCs in yam, with KY having an advantageous flavor profile and acceptability.

Non-VOCs differences of yam under different thermal processes

Overall differences in non-VOCs

In order to comprehensively analyze the overall metabolic changes of yam caused by different processing methods, a total of 287 non-VOCs were identified from 4 groups of yam samples using LC-MS (Table S3). These non-VOCs included 51 carboxylic acids and derivatives, 38 fatty acyls, 28 organooxygen compounds, 24 benzene and substituted derivatives, 11 phenols, 11 prenol lipids, 11 steroids and steroid derivatives, 9 organonitrogen compounds, 8 flavonoids, 7 keto acids and derivatives, 6 imidazopyrimidines, 5 purine nucleotides, and 78 other non-VOCs (including amino acids and derivatives, azacyclic compounds, azoles, etc.).

Differential non-VOCs analysis

PCA analysis showed that the 4 yam samples were significantly separated (PC1, 29.9%; PC2, 23%), indicating that different thermal processes have a significant impact on the non-VOCs of yam (Fig 3A). Further, the differential non-VOCs were screened through the OPLS-DA model, with VIP > 1.2, P < 0.05, and 1 < log2FC ≤ -1 (Fig 3B-D). Compared with RY, there were 71 differential non-VOCs in KY (20 up-regulated and 51 down-regulated), 68 in SY (33 up-regulated and 35 down-regulated), and 60 in BY (40 up-regulated and 20 down-regulated) (Fig 3E-G). The results indicate that different thermal processes lead to different changes in the non-VOCs of yam. In the pairwise comparisons of RY vs KY, RY vs SY, and RY vs BY, baking resulted in the most significant downregulation of non-VOCs in yam (Table S4). This finding is consistent with the analysis of VOCs, where baking had the most pronounced effect on yam flavor. Fatty acyls and carboxylic acids and their derivatives were common and abundant non-VOCs in the comparisons of RY vs SY and RY vs BY. In the RY vs KY, fatty acyls were solely detected among the up-regulated non-VOCs. Compared with RY, fatty acyls in 3 thermal processes groups exhibited greater up-regulation than down-regulation. This may be due to the destruction of cell structures caused by heating, which releases more lipid substances. The newly formed fatty acyls increase the complexity of the lipid profile, thereby leading to an increase in the number of up-regulated fatty acyls compounds. In contrast, carboxylic acids and derivatives were predominantly found among the down-regulated non-VOCs. Additionally, in the comparisons of RY vs SY and RY vs BY, more phenols were up-regulated among the non-VOCs. This may be because the elevated temperature inactivated polyphenol oxidase, thereby inhibiting the degradation of total phenols and increasing the content of phenolic compounds through the Maillard reaction (Kourouma et al., 2019). Benzene and substituted derivatives are among the high-difference non-VOCs with a relatively large increase in quantity after thermal processes.

Unique non-VOCs analysis

Compared with RY, 26 unique non-VOCs were identified in the samples treated with three thermal methods (Fig. 4D). These included 13(S)-HpOTrE, 5'-Methylthioadenosine, lunarine, procollagen 5-hydroxy-L-lysine, urocanic acid, 4-Methoxybenzaldehyde, o-Toluate, (5-L-Glutamyl)-L-glutamate, L-Glutamic gamma-semialdehyde, L-Methionine S-oxide, pyroglutamic acid, 12,13-DHOME, 9-OxoODE, methyl jasmonate, stearidonic acid, catechin, 5-Amino-2-oxopentanoic acid, ketoleucine, (S)-2-Methylmalate, mannitol, dopamine, sinapyl alcohol, curcumenol, senecionine, beta-Sitosterol, and pentaporphyrin I. Compared with RY, KY contained 26 unique non-VOCs, including alpha-Linolenic acid, citric acid, and (S)-Abscisic acid, etc. (Table S5). Alpha-Linolenic acid, a polyunsaturated fatty acid, can be directly utilized by human cells for the construction of cell membranes. It also exerts neuroprotective effects, enhancing memory and cognitive function, and reduces the risk of cardiovascular diseases through its metabolites eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid (Zhu et al., 2024). Moreover, Alpha-Linolenic acid can be enzymatically cleaved via the lipoxygenase pathway into 1-penten-3-ol, 3-hexen-2-one and other volatiles that impart green, nutty or roast-like notes, thereby accounting for the stronger aroma observed in KY (Yuan et al., 2023). Citric acid is one of the major metabolites in yam, and its content varies with different origins and cooking treatments (Wang et al., 2021b). SY contained 14 unique non-VOCs, including caproic acid, azelaic acid, and 3-Hydroxymethylglutaric acid, etc (Table S5). Caproic acid primarily functions as a flavor compound in food, imparting a distinctive taste. For instance, in strong-aroma Chinese liquor, caproic acid is considered a key component in balancing the pineapple aroma and pungent flavors (Zhang et al., 2025). BY contained 11 unique non-VOCs, including pantothenic acid, 1,2,3-Trihydroxybenzene, and 3'-AMP, etc (Table S5). Pantothenic acid, a water-soluble vitamin, serves as a precursor of coenzyme A and is involved in the metabolism of fats, carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids.

The above results show that after thermal processing, KY exhibited the highest number of increased unique non-VOCs (26), followed by SY (14) and BY (11). KY exhibited the highest content of VOCs and the highest sensory acceptance scores, while retaining the most basic nutritional components. Baking promotes the generation of VOCs and non-VOCs in yams to a certain extent, thereby playing the biggest role in enhancing the edible quality, overall flavor, and texture of yam.

Impact of thermal treatments on the metabolic pathways non-VOCs in yam

To further investigate the effects of different thermal processes on yam quality, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the differential non-VOCs in yams before and after processing. As shown in Fig. 4E, there were 11 significantly different non-VOCs pathways for KY vs RY (P < 0.05), including degradation of flavonoids, α-Linolenic acid metabolism, taste transduction, etc.; The differential non-VOCs of SY vs RY were enriched in 12 significantly different pathways (Fig. 4F), including linoleic acid metabolism, ABC transporters, degradation of flavonoids, etc.; The differential non-VOCs of BY vs RY were enriched in 18 significantly different pathways (Fig. 4G), including linoleic acid metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, ABC transporters, etc. These metabolic pathways mainly involve the synthesis and metabolism of amino acids, fatty acids, bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, and starch. The metabolism of these substances has been reported to be closely related to yam quality. For example, yam is rich in unsaturated fatty acids, mainly composed of linoleic acid, linolenic acid, and oleic acid. Li et al. (2025) reported that changes in the accumulation of non-VOCs in the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway significantly affect the cold tolerance of different yam cultivars.

Moreover, the metabolic pathways enriched by the differential non-VOCs produced by the three thermal processing methods are similar, but there are also differences. This indicates that the changes caused by the three thermal processing methods in the yam are both similar and different.

Effect of thermal processing on the physicochemical properties of yam starch

As shown in Fig. 5A, compared with RY, after thermal processing, the water binding capacity of yam starch was significantly increased (P < 0.05), with values ranging from 156.11% to 308.99%, and BY > SY > KY > RY. Thermal stability affects the ability of starch to thicken, gel, and stabilize food. Compared with RY (Fig. 5B), the T0 of SY and KY was increased, while that of BY was decreased. There were no significant differences in TP (P > 0.05), Tc was significantly increased, while ΔH was significantly decreased (P < 0.05). ΔH represents the energy required to disrupt the double helical structure of starch during gelatinization, reflecting the loss of molecular order and crystalline structure in starch, and is proportional to the degree of crystallinity. So, a higher value indicates the lower the proportion of gelatinized starch and the higher the starch crystallinity. TP is positively correlated with the degree of crystallinity, possibly related to factors such as particle size, microstructure, and amylose content, among others (He et al., 2024). In this experiment, the ΔH and TP values of starch extracted from processed yam were significantly reduced, in the order of RY > SY > BY > KY, and RY > BY > SY > KY, respectively, indicating that three thermal treatments resulted in a reduction of the crystallinity of yam starch, thereby causing obvious changes in its thermal properties. Furthermore, studies have shown that the content of amylose has an important influence on the phase transition behavior of starch. A higher amylose content requires more energy for gelatinization, resulting in a higher enthalpy value (Wang et al., 2024c). The differences in post-processing amylose content also confirm changes in starch crystallinity and thermal stability.

As shown in Fig. 5C, the 4 yam starch samples all have typical starch characteristic peaks at 995 cm−1, 1022 cm−1, and 1047 cm−1. The characteristic peak at 995 cm−1 is related to the intramolecular hydrogen bonds of the C-6 hydroxyl groups of starch, the characteristic peak at 1022 cm−1 is associated with the amorphous structure of starch, and the characteristic peak at 1047 cm−1 is related to the crystalline structure of starch (Liu, 2020). This indicates that the 4 yam starch samples have the structural composition of starch. In addition, the higher the peak intensity ratio of R1047/1022, the higher the short-range order of starch molecules on the surface, and the higher the peak intensity ratio of R1022/995, the stronger the intramolecular hydrogen bond force (He et al., 2024). Through calculation, the R1047/1022 and R1022/995 of RY, KY, SY, and BY were 1.041, 1.005, 0.997, 1.001 and 1.036, 1.021, 1.010, 1.012, respectively. Compared with RY, the R1047/1022 and R1022/995 of yam starch after thermal treatment were both reduced, indicating that the short-range order of starch structure and the strength of intramolecular hydrogen bonds were weakened in the starch extracted from yam after thermal treatment, and the internal double helical structure of starch was destroyed. Studies have demonstrated that the weakening of short-range order in starch molecules can inhibit the formation of resistant starch. The disruption of short-range order affects the retrogradation and crystallization processes of starch, thereby reducing the content of resistant starch (Huang et al., 2021). Moreover, no new characteristic absorption peaks appeared in the infrared spectra of starch samples after different thermal treatments, indicating that thermal treatments do not lead to derivatization of molecules in the samples. In addition, the absorption peaks of starch after thermal changed significantly around 3400 cm−1. The absorption in this region is mainly attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups (O-H) in starch molecules. It has been reported that when the amylose content is high, the linear structure of amylose may lead to stronger intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which can cause the absorption peak to broaden slightly or increase in intensity (Zhu et al., 2022). These results are consistent with the amylose and amylopectin content and thermal stability.

The crystalline signatures of the 4 starches are shown in Fig. 5D. All samples shared a principal reflection at 17.2°. RY displayed the characteristic C-type pattern with strong peaks at 5.7°, 15.1°, 17.2° and 22.9°, representing a composite of A- and B-type polymorphs. Processed starches adopted a CB structure approaching the B-type; their diffractograms were substantially weaker, with SY and BY exhibiting only a faint 15.1° signal, whereas KY retained reflections most similar to those of RY (He et al., 2024). Relative crystallinity decreased from RY (26.8 %) to KY (18.6 %), SY (10.6 %) and BY (10.2 %). The higher value of RY indicates intact long-range order, whereas processing disrupted molecular alignment, most severely in BY. This loss is attributed to heat-induced cleavage of hydrogen bonds and dissolution of helical segments, amplified by enhanced chain mobility at elevated temperature. Crystallinity is also modulated by the amylose/amylopectin ratio (Hu et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2023). The pronounced reduction in BY possibly because the additional mechanical agitation during boiling, which promotes extensive hydrogen-bond disruption; in contrast, the static conditions imposed during baking and steaming exert a milder effect on the crystalline lamellae.

In terms of appearance, the starch granules of RY exhibited a smooth surface, elliptical or spherical shape, and compact structure (Fig. 5E). Some granules had raised blocky substances on their surface, likely formed by fibers and proteins. After thermal treatments, the yam starch granules were damaged to varying degrees. The majority of starch granules increased in volume compared to RY, while a small part were fragmented into smaller pieces. The reason is that thermal treatment causes starch granules to absorb water and swell, leading to gradual disintegration of crystalline regions, the disassembly of double helical structures, disappearance of crystalline structures, weakening of intermolecular forces, and changes in the microstructure (Wang et al., 2024b). The starch granules after baking showed more pronounced wrinkles and cracks on their surfaces compared to steaming and boiling. This may be attributed to the evaporation of moisture during baking, creating a low-moisture environment. The Maillard reaction and caramelization of carbohydrates and proteins during baking may also contribute to these surface features (Wang et al., 2021a). In contrast, yam starch after steaming and boiling retained higher moisture content, which likely helped maintain the shape of the starch granules with minimal surface damage, resulting in only slight irregularities and unevenness.

Differences in vitro digestibility of yam starch

As shown in Fig. 6, the contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) in raw yam starch (RY) were 6.43%, 29.15%, and 53.32%, respectively, with the highest content of resistant starch. After thermal processing, the RDS and SDS contents in the extracted yam starch increased significantly relative to RY, reaching maxima of 34.81% and 31.53%, respectively, while the RS content decreased markedly by up to 46.23% (P < 0.05). Moreover, the digestibility of yam starch after thermal processing also increased (reached 51.63%). Natural starch granules are rich in SDS and RS, which are often converted to RDS during most food cooking procedures. RS cannot be digested and absorbed in the small intestine, but it can be fermented by microbes in the large intestine. It is mainly found in the crystalline regions of native starch (Lu et al., 2024). During high-temperature gelatinization, the crystalline structure within starch granules is disrupted, molecular chains are broken, and double helical structures are disassembled, reducing the possibility of recrystallization and leading to a significant decrease in the content of RS (Hu et al., 2011). Compared with RY, the contents of RDS and SDS increased significantly after thermal processing (P < 0.05). The reason is that high-temperature gelatinization disrupted the crystalline structure of starch, making starch molecular chains more exposed and increasing the contact area with digestive enzymes. As a result, a part of the starch was converted to RDS and another part to SDS, thereby increasing the contents of RDS and SDS. Moreover, the digestibility of starch is closely related to the ratio of amylose to amylopectin. Amylose is a linear polymer of glucose with strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds, forming a compact structure that makes it difficult for enzymes to access, resulting in slower digestion rates. Studies have shown that starch with a higher amylose content also has a higher content of RS (Du et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2019). This also confirmed the consistency between the amylose content and resistant starch content in unprocessed yam in this study. However, amylose content is not the sole determinant, as thermal processing technology also affect digestibility. For instance, after extrusion, high-amylose corn starch also shows increased contents of rapidly digestible starch and slowly digestible starch, with a decreased content of resistant starch (Ge et al., 2023). It has been found that starch with higher contents of B2 and B3 chains in amylopectin is more resistant to digestion (de la Rosa-Millan, 2025).Fig. 6Digestive properties differences of yams' starch with different thermal processing methods.Fig. 6

Moreover, the thermal processing also has a significant impact on the digestibility of yam starch. Specifically, the starch digestibility was in the order of SY > BY > KY, the content of RDS was: BY > KY > SY, and the content of SDS was: SY > BY > KY. Therefore, different thermal processing have different effects on the digestion of yam starch, which may be related to the temperature and moisture content of the three thermal processing, as these factors directly affect the gelatinization, digestion rate, and degree of digestion of starch. Overall, in terms of starch digestibility, SY had the highest digestibility at 87.21%, while KY had the lowest (74.20%). Regarding the glycemic response, BY caused the fastest increase in blood glucose levels, whereas SY had the slowest effect. From the perspective of nutrition, health, and weight management, KY was considered the best option.

Conclusion

Yam is one of the four major tuber crops, but research on the effects of different thermal treatments on its quality is limited. This study proves that thermal processing alters the sensory, color, nutritional composition, and texture of yam. Soluble sugars and starch exhibited the most significant changes. HS-SPME-GC-MS and LC-MS analyses identified 5 key volatile flavor components and 26 unique non-volatile components, which primarily originated from chemical reactions involving starch, protein, and fat in yam during thermal processing. XRD and FTIR analyses revealed that thermal treatment caused the disintegration of starch crystalline regions, decomposition of the double helix structure, and weakening of intermolecular forces, thereby enhancing starch water absorption (approximately 150%) and digestibility (up to 51.63%). Overall, KY showed better flavor, while BY and SY were more easily digestible. These studies provide scientific basis for the processing and consumption of yam.

However, this study still has some limitations, such as the lack of comparisons of new processing technologies like pulsed electric field, high-pressure treatment, and radio frequency heating, as well as explorations in terms of in vivo digestion. In the future, the application and optimization of nove thermal processing technologies in yam processing, the evaluation of the bioavailability and stability of nutritional components in yam after different processing, and the joint research on yam processing and storage stability will be more conducive to supporting this study.
