Mapping health evidence ecosystem in Brazil: a mixed-methods study protocol for developing a framework
Authors: Rebeca Cardoso Pedra, Tamille Sales Dias, Maritsa Carla de Bortoli, Luis Phillipe Nagem Lopes, Giulia Vaz da Silva, Fernanda Campos de Almeida Carrer, Silvio Barberato-Filho, Cristiane de Cássia Bergamaschi, Danielle Maria de Souza Serio dos Santos, Luciane Cruz Lopes, Laura dos Santos Boeira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence ecosystems play a vital role in informing and shaping policy; however, little is known about how these systems function, particularly in diverse national contexts. This article presents a mixed-methods study designed to be the first to systematically map and characterize Brazil’s health evidence ecosystem, focusing on key institutions, actors, and practices across the country. We aim to map, describe and analyse the ecosystem of production, use and dissemination of policy evidence in Brazil’s five regions.MethodsWe will adopt a five-step mixed-method approach to understand Brazil’s health evidence ecosystem comprehensively. Five steps will be undertaken: (a) cultural adaptation of two published situation analysis tools, (b) digital ethnography to identify and categorize organizations that are evidence producers, users, or intermediaries as well the types of evidence demanded, (c) autoethnography workshops with selected organizations, (d) interviews to apply an evidence institutionalization and (e) focus groups to produce a situation analysis of institutional practices, governance and the overall environment for evidence use in policy in each of the five Brazilian regions.DiscussionThis study combines diverse tools to understand how evidence is produced, disseminated and utilized in Brazil. The study of regional differences and institutional practices can help to identify barriers and facilitators to the effective use of evidence in policy. Furthermore, it may inform the development of strategies to strengthen the evidence ecosystem in Brazil and serve as a roadmap for other countries that aiming to conduct a comprehensive evidence ecosystem analysis.

Background

Evidence-informed knowledge-to-policy products that are concise, update and unbiased knowledge-to-policy products are among the most valuable contributions a research community can offer to citizens, policymakers and other decision-makers. [1]. Changes in social research are converging on innovations in ways to produce syntheses and other higher-quality evidence products (that is, on the basis of more rigorous methods and a timely comprehensive search) for better public policy decision-making. However, these developments imply rethinking the infrastructure and management of research production, use and dissemination, moving towards an evidence ecosystem [2].

An evidence ecosystem comprises the heterogeneous set of institutions, actors, practices and infrastructures that produce, circulate, interpret and apply evidence to inform policies [3]. An ecosystem has many stakeholders: citizens, professionals, researchers and funders; journal editors and writers; politicians and policymakers; research organizations and academic institutions; and the private sector, among others [2]. The mosaic of knowledge these social actors represent call for more effective and inclusive practices to be adopted nationally and internationally to respond to different social development challenges.

The Sustainable Development Goal 16 (SDG 16), part of the United Nations 2030 Agenda, aims to promote peaceful and inclusive societies for sustainable development, provide access to justice for all and build effective, accountable and inclusive institutions at all levels [4]. SDG 16 emphasizes the importance of strong, cohesive and well-coordinated institutions, highlighting the need for mapping and understanding countries’ evidence ecosystems to support transparent and informed decision-making processes [4].

Powered by the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, numerous initiatives emerged across Brazil, including rapid evidence synthesis efforts by the Brazilian Cochrane Center, policy briefs developed by the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (Fiocruz) and data-sharing platforms coordinated by academic consortia such as the COVID-19 Brazil Observatory. While these actions demonstrate the growing interest in evidence-informed policymaking, they often occurred independently and without centralized coordination, leading to potential inefficiencies and duplication of efforts [5]. Better coordination is needed to identify persistent issues and address them globally. For each topic, a large multidisciplinary research community must undertake the effort and commit to maintaining an up-to-date synthesis over time [6].

The integration of these knowledge actors has become increasingly essential.. The Global Commission on Evidence to Address Societal Challenges, in its report published in 2022, highlights that the focus on evidence is now shared between different key actors [7]. It also highlights that the current moment demands systematizing the aspects of the use of evidence that are achieving success and analysing the various weaknesses [7]. It also highlights what it means to develop capabilities for producing and using evidence in different contexts and the opportunities and motivations to use evidence to respond to social challenges, from an equity perspective [7].

In 2021, Cochrane Convenes highlighted the importance of advancing partnerships between universities, civil society and decision-makers in low- and middle-income countries to enable them to be better prepared to respond to future social emergencies [8]. This effort involves structuring communities of practices and situational diagnoses to understand and adjust practices to local needs. It also highlighted the importance of financing a new generation of researchers attentive to producing evidence connected to social demands.

In Brazil, the health sector has fostered an evidence ecosystem that now spreads to other policies. Since 2005, the Evidence-Informed Policy Network (EVIPNet), globally led by the World Health Organization (WHO) and coordinated in the Americas by the Pan-American Health Organization (PAHO/WHO), has promoted the use of evidence specifically in health policies [9]. Brazil’s Evidence Centres are accredited by the Department of Science and Technology of the Ministry of Health [9]. A recent initiative to promote an integrated evidence ecosystem for other social policies, the Brazilian Coalition for Evidence, was created in 2021 [10]. The Coalition brings together 45 organizations among civil society, university groups and government institutions to promote the use of evidence in decision-making in public policies and social interventions [10].

From an epistemological perspective, we draw on the concept of co-production between science and society to critically examine how evidence is generated [11]. These processes are not linear; instead, they are shaped by social norms, knowledge systems and institutional structures, which interact and mutually influence each other [11]. Thus, the evidence ecosystem in Brazil refers to a relational and dynamic field where science and politics are co-produced in specific contexts. This also includes considering the role of evidence in public decisions as a formative process that translates existing knowledge into new ideas, concepts, perspectives and ways to intervene on priority health problems [12].

In the Brazilian context, the government, especially through its ministries, state and municipal health secretariats, plays a multifaceted role that goes beyond mere regulation or procuring research. Indeed, various governmental bodies directly produce evidence through their health information systems, epidemiological surveillance, linked institutes and specialized technical units. Moreover, Brazilian public agencies frequently conduct applied research, administrative data analyses and program evaluations, thereby producing a significant amount of technical and scientific evidence. These internally generated data and analyses are used to support the formulation and adjustment of public policy, budget configurations and strategic decisions.

Despite the growing number of structured evidence centres, the current evidence ecosystem in Brazil is not properly mapped, nor is there any clarity regarding its installed capabilities. This fragmented approach results in an incomplete body of evidence and weak links to policymaking. The current moment demands identifying the actors and organizations operating in Brazil, systematizing the aspects of evidence use that are achieving success and analysing their various weaknesses, barriers and opportunities.

Therefore, the study to present the protocol for developing a framework to guide researchers and policymakers in mapping, describing and analysing the ecosystem of evidence production, use and dissemination across Brazil’s five geographic regions (North, South, Southeast, Central-West and Northeast).

Methods

Study design

This protocol will adopt a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative strategies (for example, quantitative mapping of institutions) with qualitative methods (for example, semi-structured interviews and deliberative workshops with key stakeholders). The integration of these complementary methods is designed to support both the development and the empirical application of a conceptual framework for understanding Brazil’s health evidence ecosystem; by identifying where and how different forms of knowledge are mobilized in the design and implementation of public policies. Mixed methods are necessary because the evidence ecosystem encompasses both measurable dimensions (such as the volume, type and distribution of evidence sources) and interpretative dimensions (such as stakeholders’ perspectives, barriers and facilitators for evidence use).

We will employ comprising five steps: Step 1 – cultural adaptation of two situation analysis tools; Step 2 – digital ethnography to identify and categorize organizations that are evidence producers, users, or intermediaries, as well as the types of evidence demanded; Step 3 – autoethnography workshops with selected organizations; Step 4 – interviews to apply an evidence institutionalization checklist; and Step 5 – focus groups to produce a situation analysis of institutional practices, governance and the overall environment for evidence use in policy in each of the five Brazilian geographical regions (North, South, Southeast, Central-West and Northeast).

We developed a conceptual framework (Fig. 1) to systematically represent the multiple stakeholders involved in an evidence ecosystem, as well as the key dimensions that influence their interactions and the institutionalization of evidence use in policymaking processes. This framework integrates theoretical and empirical knowledge to map, describe and analyse the complex relationships between actors, institutions and practices that shape evidence-informed policymaking. Data collection procedures

Step 1 – cultural adaptation

Of the tools “Supporting routine use of evidence during the policymaking process: a WHO Checklist for policy-makers” [13] and “EVIPNet Europe Situation Analysis Manual” [14]. Notably, the Pan-American Health Organization and the Brazilian Ministry of Health translated the tools into Portuguese.

We will use the hybrid DELPHI method, which uses an interdisciplinary panel of experts and a consensus methodology to leverage different opinions, avoiding common pitfalls in group dynamics when generating pre-agreed results. This hybrid Delphi leverages the strengths of both methods of information exchange: an anonymous survey with a group of geographically dispersed experts and stakeholders and a synchronous online discussion at the end, with an opportunity for clarifications in real-time to exchange views and resolve uncertainties. Initially, a diverse committee of up to 30 experts on the topic will be mobilized to appraise the translation of the tools. The experts will be purposely selected to represent public policy decision-makers, knowledge intermediaries and knowledge producers with a minimum of 2 years of experience in policy debate informed by evidence, with regional and gender diversity. The committee will be invited to evaluate semantic (checking whether words have the same meaning in the original questionnaire and the pre-test version), idiomatic (whether idiomatic expressions and colloquialisms were translated correctly), cultural/experimental (whether the tool items represent everyday experiences) and conceptual equivalences (compare the conceptual meanings of words in English with the Brazilian cultural context) of the translated tools.

The content validity index (CVI) [15] will be used to verify the proportion of agreement between the expert committee members regarding the equivalences of the translated tools. This index comprises a Likert Scale with a score from one to five, with 1 = non-relevant or non-representative item; 2 = item that requires significant revision to become representative; 3 = item that requires minor revision to become representative; and 4 = relevant or representative item and five high relevant or high representative. The CVI will be calculated by adding the items that received a score of 3, 4 and 5, divided by the total number of responses. Participants’ responses will be categorized as irrelevant (1 to 2), adequate with revisions (3) and relevant (4 and 5). Consensus is reached if at least 80% of the participants’ votes belong to the same category (1–2, 3, or 4–5). Panellists in the online face-to-face meeting will receive feedback and scores obtained in the previous round. Clarifications will also be provided, and evidence and the rationale for each flowchart will be available. After alignment of understanding, the latest version of the tools will be applied to the team of an evidence centre registered with the EVIPNet Brazil Network. The evidence centre will be selected based on convenience and availability. The tools will be applied online through one group interview and four focus groups. An assistant will be responsible for taking notes, recording the team’s doubts and difficulties in responding to the content of the tools, and transcribing the activities. The data will be systematized narrative summaries. As the tools do not have psychometric properties, the objective of validation will be to describe whether the team exposed to the application of the tool had difficulties in answering any questions. Feedback from the pilot application will be used to adapt the tools further.

Lastly, a dialogue among different stakeholders will be held to appraise the final version of the tools. It will follow the step-by-step procedure described in the Brazilian Ministry of Health’s Methodological Guidelines on stakeholder dialogues [16]. This dialogue will be held online and will include representatives of the expert committee, the team from the evidence centre selected for the pilot application and other key stakeholders identified through stakeholder mapping, such as policymakers, researchers and health system managers. During the dialogue, narrative summaries will be assessed to identify: a) understanding of the content, b) perceived difficulties and c) final suggestions for improving the tools. A dialogue report will be produced, and the research coordinating team will incorporate all pertinent suggestions into the final version of the tools.

Step 2 – Mapping and characterizing institutions and key actors in the evidence ecosystem in Brazil

This activity involves the mapping and characterizing institutions and key actors that produce, use, or disseminate evidence for policy in Brazil (classified below as Evidence Producers, Users and Intermediaries). The methodology for mapping will be an digital ethnography [17–19] of websites, as well as the analysis of official documents such as normative and constitutional acts. Ethnography is a process of exploring in the field the forms of action and agency of different groups, composing a “dense description”, the product of an intellectual effort by the researcher to study significant structures, determining their social basis and their importance [17]. Digital ethnography environments is a subfield type of exploring online environments available on the internet to understand their representation [18]. Digital ethnography enables the study of social phenomena as they occur in and through online platforms, institutional websites, networks and forums [19]. Researcher is both the subject and the context of the study [17]. This approach is particularly relevant in the context of an increasingly digitized policy landscape, where knowledge mobilization often takes place in hybrid physical-digital spaces. In the data selection, collection and analysis stages, the research team will use various types of tools, such as search engines such as Google, and exploratory navigation through government, university and civil society organization websites.

To register the ethnography of websites, researchers will use the mapping spreadsheet Domestic-evidence Infrastructure Assessments developed by the Global Commission for Evidence to Address Societal Challenges [20]. The spreadsheet explores the following topics: the existence of internal units within organizations dedicated to the production, use, or dissemination of evidence; types of evidence included; support from external partners for the production, use and dissemination of evidence; support from United Nations system entities (including regulatory guidance, technical assistance and global public goods); policies, processes and instruments that facilitate the production, use and dissemination of evidence; and general insights. The spreadsheet will be translated from English to Portuguese and validated in an online Delphi [21] questionnaire and panel with invited experts to ensure the terminology is understandable for the Brazilian context. For each dimension, a Likert scale will be constructed, where score 1 represents “Not at all understandable/relevant/coherent” and score 5 represents “Totally understandable/relevant/coherent”. The translation will be adapted from the feedback received. The final questionnaire will guide the research team’s records in their exploration of government, university and civil society organization websites, searching for key organizations and actors that are part of the evidence ecosystem in Brazil.

Aside from the spreadsheet, institutions will be characterized using the Health System Evidence [22] and Social System Evidence [23] taxonomies to classify them and their work. To adapt the taxonomies to the Brazilian context, a literal translation was initially done, which led to the development of a structured questionnaire with summarized questions related to the categories of each taxonomy. We conducted pilot interviews with two organizations of the ecosystem, sending the taxonomies by email beforehand. These pilot interviews revealed that certain taxonomy topics, particularly the financial aspects of the health taxonomy, were not fully applicable to the Brazilian context.

As a result, we adapted a new translation of both taxonomies by translating each topic to Portuguese, analysing available studies on the Health System Evidence websites, conducting group discussions to reach a consensus on each topic, making necessary rephrasing, additions, or exclusions and validating the adapted taxonomy with stakeholders and making adjustments. The taxonomies will be used as a framework to categorize the organizations both in the initial mapping and in Step 4 when each organization is interviewed on the domains for the institutionalization of the use of evidence.

Step 3 – Autoethnography workshops with invited organizations

Autoethnography is a genre of ethnography, a qualitative research method that involves the analysis of experience recorded from autobiographical texts, where the researcher is both the subject and the context of the study. This method is grounded in self-reflection, that is, the recognition and inclusion of the experience of the research subject both in the definition of what will be researched and in the development of the research. It also considers the relational factors during the investigation. What stands out in this method is the importance of the personal narrative and experiences of the subjects and authors of the research. Thinking about the political role of the author concerning the topic, the influence of this author on the choices and investigative directions, and their possible advances [24].

On the basis of the initial mapping in digital environments, representatives from up to 15 organizations (5 linked to government bodies, 5 linked to civil society organizations and 5 linked to universities, seeking, whenever possible, representation of all regions in each category) will be invited to form two autoethnography workshops. This follows the method developed by Professor Sandy Oliver from the Evidence for Policy and Practice Information Centre (EPPI-Centre) in the United Kingdom [25]. The workshops aim to promote the writing of each organization’s stories, identifying events, partners and key themes in the development of evidence ecosystems for policy in Brazil.

In the first workshop, participants will join small groups to reflect on a biographical approach and cross-registration, answering the following questions:When and why did you choose to work with evidence for policy? Step 5 – application of the validated tool “EVIPNet Europe Situation Analysis Manual”

This manual aims to help countries plan and conduct a situation analysis to understand the national context for evidence-informed policies, identifying facilitators and barriers. Owing to the country size and regional differences, we will apply this tool by region. At this stage, organizations mapped in Step 2 will be contacted to participate in five focus groups, with a maximum number of 20 participants per region. We will seek balance in the representation of evidence users, intermediaries and producers, as well as local funders.

The five online focus groups will follow the script described in the tool:

Focus group 1 – Description of the region’s political system, reflecting on policies at the state level. The focus on the political context helps users develop a general understanding of the region’s situation and its main political, socioeconomic and cultural characteristics. The focus on the context of health policy formulation describes in detail the characteristics of its stakeholders, structures, decision-making processes and key issues. The focus is on elements that can facilitate or hinder the use of evidence in policy development.

Focus group 2 – Description of the region’s data system, describing information systems, data use policies and their challenges and key actors – The focus on the information/data systems describes key aspects of how information is collected, evaluated, used and disseminated and in the way the data system is governed.

Focus group 3 – Description of the region’s research system, describing the main themes of focus in research for development, the main funders and the most important gaps. The focus on the context of the research system in the region describes in detail the main research carried out, the gaps, the stakeholders, the available structures, the financing processes and mechanisms, as well as the main areas of research.

Focus group 4 – Description of the region’s evidence system, describing capabilities for knowledge translation and different types of evidence developed in the region. Focus on the existing evidence system landscape provides an overview of current evidence-informed policy efforts, describing potential bottlenecks and strengths in the regional context. Also, the demand for evidence and the processes/structures that are used to translate evidence into policymaking.

Focus group 5 – Review and validation of findings – a summary of the findings will be consolidated for review and validation with the team.

Each session will address a key topic, and trigger questions will be circulated by email in advance so that participants can prepare response topics for each topic. During the online focus groups, registration aid tools, such as Jamboard, will be used to ensure individual registration before the group discussion. Focus groups will be facilitated by a researcher serving as a moderator and documented by two colleagues. After each focus group, the research team will seek additional information to address gaps identified by the participants.

Data analysis

Data will be structured to enable integration and triangulation across the protocol’s multiple methodological components, ensuring coherence between qualitative and quantitative data and supporting the development of a framework for a comprehensive understanding of Brazil’s evidence ecosystem. Given the sequential and complementary design, analysis will occur both within and across stages to identify convergences, divergences and emerging themes.

Data analysis will combine thematic analysis [28] for qualitative data and appropriate statistical tests for quantitative data, such as descriptive statistics, measures of central tendency and dispersion and agreement indexes (for example, content validity index). Thematic analysis will follow established coding procedures to identify patterns, meanings and emerging themes across data sources. The analytical strategy will be regionally sensitive, allowing for the identification of contextual specificities, divergences and convergences across Brazil’s geographic regions. Triangulation will be employed to integrate findings from multiple sources and methods, enhancing the depth and validity of interpretations related to the evidence ecosystem.

Ethical aspects

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of São Paulo (approval number 77587824.3.0000.0075), in compliance with the National Research Ethics Commission (CONEP) regulations, which oversee research involving human participants in Brazil. The Committee is responsible for reviewing and ensuring that all research activities adhere to ethical standards and protect the rights and well-being of participants. Participants, in all stages of the study, will sign an informed consent form and will be guaranteed confidentiality regarding the team’s responses, as well as respect for their informed consent.

Discussion

The objective of this article was to develop and present a protocol, in the form of a conceptual framework, to be used by researchers, practitioners and policymakers for mapping, describing and analysing the ecosystem of evidence production, use and dissemination. The methods to be employed will include digital ethnography, autoethnography workshops, the application of checklists and regional situational analysis, which are intended to provide insights into evidence utilization in policies. Mapping the interactions between institutional, political and cultural elements that influence evidence ecosystems, while considering the specific characteristics of each of the five regions, will support the development and future application of the framework.

All the tools used in this project support the analysis of evidence ecosystems comprehensively, reflecting on political aspects, data systems and research, financing and evidence-informed policies. They can also be used in future studies to monitor the degree of institutionalization of the use of different types of evidence in policies. Through the products, it will be possible to identify the performance of government, academic and civil society organizations through different types of evidence produced, such as assessments, data analysis and modelling, evidence syntheses and qualitative research, among others. This will verify the concentration of organizations per region on certain topics, which can support the future development of the evidence-informed policymaking agenda.

The findings can open up a strong platform for the implementation of evidence in public policy. The data collected from the interviews will be the basis for the identification of the main barriers facing evidence use at the institutional level. This information might serve as the basis for overcoming these problems with appropriate strategies. Further, comparing different regions may determine the existence of best practices and policies that can be adapted and implemented nationally to improve the evidence ecosystem.

The project follows the precepts of open science, ensuring transparency in the methodologies used, and active efforts to disseminate the findings applicable to its potential beneficiaries [29]. The adaptation of the tools to the Portuguese language and Brazilian context aims to encourage their use by local researchers. Different strategies will be developed to address key audiences of managers, researchers, civil society and knowledge intermediaries, depending on their specificities. The dissemination of knowledge produced by research has the potential to multiply and strengthen the actions of evidence centres.

However, this study also might face some potential limitations. Data collection may encounter difficulties, for example, information availability and accessibility barriers in certain locations such as remote areas. In addition, the participants in the workshops and interviews may be biased or ideologically rooted in political and institutional factors, and, hence, fact-checking procedures might be needed. To minimize risks of this kind, grounded in the principles of the methodology, the triangulation of data and confidentiality guarantees have to be ensured for credibility and the integrity of the research.

The analysis proposed in this article recognizes that the use of evidence in public policy does not occur in a linear, direct, or fully traceable way [30]. On the contrary, it is often a fragmented, contingent and situated process in which evidence can be mobilized both to inform decisions and to rhetorically legitimize them [31]. This perspective helps delimit the analytical boundaries of the present proposal: although mapping institutional evidence flows, their actors and devices allows tracing relevant structural configurations of the ecosystem, it does not capture the totality of uses, appropriations and interpretations of evidence. Furthermore, the very concept of evidence is unstable and subject to disputes. In diverse contexts, what is considered “valid evidence” may range from randomized clinical trials to lived experience narratives, economic analyses, or legal arguments [32]. This epistemological heterogeneity increases the complexity of mapping and demands, as a methodological response, an approach sensitive to multiple forms of knowledge production and circulation.

Regarding benefits, we will ensure that participants receive a clear feedback, such as access to research results and opportunities for horizontal dialogue, in participatory knowledge co-production formats. In some cases, the generated products (for example, infographics, technical summaries, regional reports) will serve as useful tools for institutions for planning or advocacy. The research results will also represent future opportunities to improve and strengthen the evidence ecosystem by proposing training and research incentives to stakeholders, as the Brazilian Ministry of Health is also involved in the research.

Guided by the proposed framework, the study of regional differences and institutional practices will support the identification of potential barriers and facilitators to evidence use in policy. The framework also provides a structured foundation for developing strategies to strengthen Brazil’s evidence ecosystem and may serve as a reference for other countries seeking to map and improve their own evidence ecosystems.

In a subsequent article, we plan to apply the framework developed in this protocol to empirically map and analyse the evidence ecosystem in Brazil, testing its utility in identifying barriers and facilitators to evidence-informed policymaking and refining the framework on the basis of these findings.
