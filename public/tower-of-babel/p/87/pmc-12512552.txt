Conjunctival and retinal microvascular loss in systemic lupus erythematosus: a swept-source OCTA study
Authors: Lulu Chen, Lu Sun, Lihui Meng, Chuting Wang, Youxin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to characterize conjunctival and retinal microvascular alterations in Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) using swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA), and to evaluate their correlation with systemic clinical manifestations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 32 SLE patients and 32 age, sex, and spherical equivalent matched controls. Participants underwent swept-source OCTA imaging of nasal and temporal conjunctiva (16 × 12 mm scans) and macula (6 × 6 mm scans). Vessel density of conjunctival vessel, superficial vascular plexus (SVP), and deep vascular plexus (DVP) were quantified using automated segmentation. Correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between conjunctival and retinal vascular densities, as well as their relationships with clinical parameters of systemic involvement and disease activity.ResultsSLE patients showed significant reductions in nasal (45.0% vs. 60.1%, p < 0.001) and temporal (51.8% vs. 60.5%, p = 0.009) conjunctival vascular density, alongside pan-retinal DVP attenuation (p < 0.05). Combined nasal conjunctival and retinal nasal DVP analysis yielded high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.897). Microvascular loss was independent of organ involvement or disease activity (p > 0.05).ConclusionsSLE is associated with significant reductions in conjunctival and retinal DVP vascular density. Combined multimodal SS-OCTA analysis achieved superior diagnostic accuracy, with microvascular loss independent of disease activity or organ-specific involvement, suggesting systemic microvascular injury as a unifying SLE feature. Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a chronic autoimmune disease affecting multiple organ systems, manifests through diffuse endothelial dysfunction and microvascular compromise—pathological hallmarks that drive irreversible tissue injury in target organs such as the kidneys, retina, and central nervous system [1]. Ocular involvement, ranging from keratoconjunctivitis to sight-threatening retinal vasculopathy, affects up to 30% of SLE patients [2–4], yet early detection remains challenging due to the subclinical nature of microvascular changes. While retinal vascular alterations in SLE have been increasingly characterized via optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), the conjunctiva—an accessible vascular bed reflecting systemic microcirculatory health—has been largely overlooked in lupus research.

Recent advancements in swept-source OCTA (SS-OCTA) enable unprecedented resolution of both anterior and posterior segment vasculature [5], offering a unique opportunity to map SLE-related microangiopathy non-invasively. Recent studies have consistently demonstrated reduced vessel density in the SVP and DVP of SLE patients [6, 7], even in the absence of overt retinopathy [8, 9]. Emerging studies highlight the conjunctiva as a sensitive indicator of systemic vascular pathology. In dry eye disease, conjunctival vascular density serves as a biomarker for assessing ocular surface inflammation and therapeutic response [10, 11]. Among scleral contact lens wearers, alterations in conjunctival vascular density reflect the degree of ocular surface hypoxia [12]. Recent research in thyroid-associated orbitopathy (TAO) further underscores this vascular responsiveness, demonstrating marked increases in vascular complexity and perfusion within inflamed bulbar conjunctival regions during active disease phases, suggesting its utility in monitoring inflammatory activity [13, 14]. In diabetic patients, reduced conjunctival vascular density may correlate with the progression of diabetic retinopathy [15]. Furthermore, conjunctival microvascular changes have been associated with both the presence and severity of coronary artery disease (CAD), suggesting potential utility in cardiovascular risk screening and severity evaluation [16]. However, research on conjunctival vascular density in SLE remains limited. While Shi et al. identified reduced conjunctival vascular density in SLE patients, their study was constrained by a small sample size and lacked analysis of organ-specific involvement [17].

Building upon existing methodologies, our study employs swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) to establish a comprehensive vascular profiling system. By evaluating both the conjunctival capillary plexus and retinal vascular complex, we systematically characterize circulatory alterations in SLE patients. Through stratified comparisons of ocular microvascular parameters among subgroups categorized by multisystem involvement and disease severity, we aim to provide novel insights for improving SLE diagnosis and monitoring.

Methods

Study population

This single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH), between December 1, 2022, and Jun 20, 2023. This study enrolled 32 consecutive patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), diagnosed according to the 2019 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria, from outpatient rheumatology clinics or inpatient ophthalmology consultations at a tertiary referral center. Exclusion criteria included age < 18 years, spherical equivalent>±4.00 diopters, ocular comorbidities (glaucoma, uveitis, choroidal neovascularization, central serous chorioretinopathy, or trauma), prior ocular surgery (e.g., cataract extraction, vitrectomy, or scleral buckling), poor-quality optical coherence tomography (OCT) images (signal strength index < 7), and coexisting autoimmune diseases (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome or rheumatoid arthritis) to minimize confounding effects. Patients presenting with significant hemorrhages, exudates, or other abnormalities on fundus examination were excluded from the study. The control group consisted of 32 age, sex, and spherical equivalent-matched healthy controls recruited from individuals undergoing routine ophthalmic screenings at PUMCH. For each SLE patient, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were systematically documented, including initial disease manifestations, disease duration, and current therapeutic regimens (e.g., corticosteroids, immunosuppressants). Comprehensive laboratory profiling encompassed hematologic parameters (complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate), inflammatory biomarkers (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein), renal function markers (serum creatinine), and immunologic indices: antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), complement components (C3/C4), and antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin IgG/IgM, and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies). Disease activity was stratified using the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K) with predefined categories: inactive/mild (0–6), moderate (7–12), and severe (≥ 13). All participants underwent standardized ophthalmic assessments, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), slit-lamp biomicroscopy of anterior and posterior segments, and anterior segment imaging using a swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography system (BM-400 K BMizar; TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China). One eye was randomly selected for subsequent data analysis using the RANDBETWEEN function in Excel for randomization. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Beijing, China) (Approval No. I-22PJ1024) and strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

SS-OCTA image acquisition

Conjunctival OCTA images

Swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA; BM-400 K BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology, China) was utilized to acquire high-resolution conjunctival microvascular images. The system employs a near-infrared light source with a central wavelength of 1060 nm, achieving an A-scan acquisition rate of 40 kHz. The device enables high-resolution imaging at tissue depths up to 6 mm, delivering an axial resolution of 3.8 μm and lateral resolution of 10 μm under optimized focusing conditions. The algorithm of higher-order moments amplitude decorrelation angiography was utilized for detecting motion signals. A + 35 diopter adaptor lens was mounted to optimize anterior segment imaging. Adjustable chin and forehead rests were utilized to stabilize participants’ head position. The participants were instructed to maintain steady fixation on an external target during scanning. For nasal and temporal conjunctival imaging, subjects were directed to shift their gaze nasally or temporally, respectively. A rectangular scan pattern (16 mm × 12 mm) centered on the target conjunctival quadrant was employed, capturing the limbus-adjacent vasculature. All images were acquired by the same experienced investigator. A standardized circular region of interest (ROI) centered 2.5 mm from the corneoscleral limbus with a 1.5 mm radius was selected for quantitative assessment of conjunctival microvasculature parameters (Fig. 1). The conjunctival microvascular plexus, spanning from the conjunctival epithelium to a depth of 200 μm beneath the epithelial surface, was isolated using automated segmentation algorithms. When segmentation is inaccurate, manual correction was performed. Conjunctival vessel density (CVD) is defined as the ratio of flow area to the total measured area.

Macular OCTA images

The OCTA images were acquired centered on the macular fovea, covering a 6 × 6 mm2 area. Central macular thickness (CMT) and subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) were measured automatically with the installed software. The retinal vascular layers were divided into superficial and deep layers. The superficial vascular plexus (SVP) was defined as extending from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to 9 μm beneath the inner plexiform layer (IPL), with the deep vascular complex (DVC) encompassing the region spanning 6 μm below the IPL to 9 μm inferior to the outer plexiform layer (OPL). Using the ETDRS segmentation method, the perifoveal retina was divided into four quadrants: nasal, inferior, temporal, and superior. The software automatically measured the VD of the superficial and deep retinal layers in each quadrant. The system simultaneously segmented the choroidal capillary layer. A ROI centered on the fovea with a diameter of 1.5 mm was delineated. Choroidal capillary (CC) blood flow density was defined as the ratio of flow area to the total measured area.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), while GraphPad Prism (version 5, CA, USA) was utilized for generating graphs. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess data normality. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical data are represented by counts (percentages). Continuous variables were analyzed through parametric methods (independent t-test, ANOVA) when normality criteria were satisfied, with non-parametric equivalents (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis) applied to skewed distributions. Categorical comparisons utilized chi-square testing, supplemented by Fisher’s exact test for contingency tables with limited observations. The associations between continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to determine the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and cutoff values. A P value of less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

The SLE patients had a mean age of 36.7 ± 11.1 years, with 27 females (84.4%) and a mean disease duration of 5.7 ± 4.7 years. Disease activity was categorized as mild, moderate, and severe in 9 (28.1%), 15 (46.9%), and 8 (25.0%) patients, respectively. Discussion

SLE, a multisystem autoimmune disorder, manifests with diverse ocular complications including keratoconjunctivitis, uveitis, retinal vasculopathy, and optic neuropathy. OCTA, a non-invasive imaging modality, has revolutionized the evaluation of microvascular integrity in SLE, particularly for assessing retinal vascular abnormalities. The conjunctiva, as the most superficial vascularized ocular tissue, provides a unique window for observing dynamic microcirculatory changes. Recent advancements in anterior segment OCTA (AS-OCTA) have enabled novel insights into conjunctival microvasculature, revealing its associations with systemic conditions such as myocardial infarction [16], hyperthyroidism [14], and dry eye disease [10], thereby positioning conjunctival vascular metrics as potential biomarkers for systemic pathologies. Additionally, conjunctival vessel density and vessel diameter indices can also serve as prognostic predictors following ocular chemical burns [18]. After pterygium combined with ​​conjunctival autografts​​ (CAG), conjunctival vessel density can be used to evaluate graft vascularization, thereby enhancing the monitoring of postoperative healing processes [19]. Conjunctival vessel density was significantly reduced in patients with coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke [20, 21]. Previous studies have suggested that these conjunctival vascular alterations may be linked to the pathophysiological mechanisms of systemic diseases. Specifically, the decrease in conjunctival vessel density might stem from systemic vascular endothelial dysfunction, which can induce impaired vasodilation, reduced blood flow perfusion, and diminished blood flow density [22, 23]. However, limited research has focused on conjunctival microvascular alterations in SLE. This study addresses this gap by systematically comparing conjunctival and retinal vascular density between SLE patients and healthy controls, while evaluating the diagnostic efficacy of multimodal vascular analysis. Our findings demonstrate significant reductions in both conjunctival and deep retinal vascular density in SLE, with enhanced diagnostic accuracy achieved through combined vascular parameters. Notably, these microvascular impairments were independent of conventional disease activity indices, suggesting their role as biomarkers of subclinical endothelial dysfunction. This integrative approach offers a novel strategy for early SLE detection and personalized therapeutic monitoring, bridging the gap between ocular imaging and systemic disease evaluation.

The superior reproducibility and accuracy of AS-OCTA in conjunctival vascular quantification, demonstrated by its high intraclass correlation coefficients in recent technical validation studies, position it as a robust tool for detecting subtle microvascular alterations [24, 25]. Previous studies have predominantly utilized Spectral-Domain Optical Coherence Tomography Angiography (SD-OCTA) for anterior segment imaging, typically with a scan speed of 70,000 A-scans per second, a center wavelength of 840 nm, and an axial resolution of 5 μm. In contrast, our study employed SS-OCTA for anterior segment scanning [16]. The SS-OCTA device used in our study featured an A-scan rate of 400,000 scans per second, providing an enhanced axial resolution of 3.8 μm. Additionally, the higher-order moments amplitude decorrelation angiography algorithm was utilized to detect motion signals. The superior scan speed and resolution of SS-OCTA allowed for clearer visualization of conjunctival vasculature. Wu et al. used the Angio OCT optvue RTVue XR Avanti system to measure conjunctival vessel density in 12 SLE patients and 12 healthy controls, reporting higher conjunctival vessel density in SLE patients compared to healthy controls [17]. However, our findings diverge from theirs, as we observed lower nasal and temporal vessel density in SLE patients compared to healthy individuals. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. The baseline characteristics of Wu’s patient cohort differed from ours. Although both cohorts comprised SLE patients, Wu’s group had a mean SLEDAI-2 K score of 4.25 ± 2.83, whereas our patients had a mean score of 12.0 ± 6.0. Despite the lack of statistical significance in conjunctival vessel density differences among patients with varying disease activity in our study, a trend of lower vessel density was still noted in patients with severe disease activity compared to those with mild activity. Both our study and Wu’s investigation were limited by small sample sizes, which may have influenced the results. Future research should consider larger cohorts to further elucidate the relationship between conjunctival vessel density and disease activity in SLE. Additionally, the differences in imaging systems used—SD-OCTA by Wu and SS-OCTA in our study—may have contributed to the observed differences in conjunctival vessel density measurements. The higher resolution and faster scan speed of SS-OCTA could potentially provide more detailed and accurate assessments of conjunctival vasculature, which may explain the differences in our findings.

The observed reduction in DVP density across all retinal quadrants in SLE patients, contrasting with preserved SVP integrity, likely reflects selective vulnerability of metabolically active deeper microvasculature to SLE-driven endothelial injury [26]. The DVP, which primarily nourishes the inner nuclear layer and outer plexiform layer, exhibits heightened sensitivity to hypoxia and oxidative stress due to its high metabolic demands—a phenomenon corroborated in diabetic retinopathy and hypertensive choroidopathy studies [27–29]. Complementing these findings, recent cohort studies identified reduced central macular thickness and concurrent DVP VD loss even in asymptomatic SLE patients, suggesting that OCTA-derived microvascular metrics may detect subclinical retinopathy prior to overt fundoscopic changes [30, 31]. While our study similarly highlights DVP-specific impairment, the lack of correlation between VD reductions and SLEDAI-2 K scores across both cohorts implies that microvascular rarefaction reflects cumulative endothelial injury rather than transient inflammatory activity.

Emerging evidence underscores the diagnostic potential of ocular microvascular assessment in SLE. Shi et al. previously established the diagnostic utility of conjunctival vascular metrics, reporting superior sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing SLE cohorts from healthy individuals [30]. Building on this, our findings corroborate the discriminative capacity of conjunctival vessel density, particularly in the nasal quadrant, which demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, Subasi et al. recently highlighted the prognostic value of DVP vessel density in identifying subclinical SLE progression, suggesting its role as a dynamic biomarker for subclinical vascular accrual [31]. Our multimodal approach, integrating conjunctival and DVP metrics, achieved enhanced diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.897), surpassing isolated vascular bed analyses. Collectively, these findings position conjunctival-DVP composite indices as promising non-invasive biomarkers for SLE. Standardization of imaging protocols and validation in multiethnic cohorts will be essential to translate these advances into clinical practice.

Notably, when analyzing conjunctival vascular density across SLE subgroups stratified by organ system involvement, we observed no significant intergroup differences. This suggests that conjunctival microvascular rarefaction reflects a systemic, rather than organ-specific, manifestation of endothelial pathology. Similar findings have been reported in other systemic diseases, including coronary artery disease (CAD) and diabetes mellitus (DM), where reduced conjunctival vessel density was not significantly correlated with disease severity [15, 21]. In these conditions, reduced conjunctival vessel density may instead reflect widespread vascular endothelial injury. This pan-vascular pattern aligns with recent insights into SLE-related microangiopathy, wherein diffuse endothelial dysfunction—driven by circulating anti-endothelial cell antibodies, complement activation, and chronic interferon signaling—transcends anatomical boundaries [32]. The absence of organ-specific vascular signatures implies that conjunctival microvascular loss reflects global endothelial injury. Nevertheless, due to the limited sample size, larger prospective studies are warranted to further explore the relationship between conjunctival vessel density, organ involvement, and disease severity in SLE.

Previous studies have linked hydroxychloroquine use to reduced retinal and choroidal vascular density, attributed to lysosomal dysfunction in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)-choriocapillaris complex and subsequent microcirculatory imbalance, with cumulative dose and duration as critical risk factors detectable via SS-OCTA prior to symptom onset [33–35]. However, conjunctival vascular changes have not been systematically evaluated. In this study, no significant differences in nasal or temporal conjunctival vessel density were observed between HCQ-treated patients and untreated patients (p = 0.923 and p = 0.715). The small control cohort (n = 7) may introduce selection bias, limiting generalizability. Future larger studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore potential mechanisms.

While this study provides novel insights into conjunctival and retinal microvascular alterations in SLE, several limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the modest sample size may have limited statistical power for subgroup analyses, particularly in detecting organ-specific microvascular variations or disease activity-stratified differences. Second, the single-center, cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding temporal relationships between vascular attrition and SLE progression, while potentially introducing selection bias inherent to tertiary referral centers. Third, although we controlled for major confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables—such as cumulative corticosteroid exposure, hydroxychloroquine retinal toxicity thresholds, and regional environmental factors—could influence microvascular metrics. Future research should involve larger prospective cohort studies to further validate our findings, more precisely delineate the correlation between conjunctival vessel density and organ involvement in SLE, and analyze whether these microvascular changes represent persistent SLE features or transient alterations. Additionally, future studies may consider analyzing a broader range of conjunctival vessels to achieve more comprehensive results.

In conclusion, our study reveals significant reductions in conjunctival and deep retinal vascular density in SLE, with combined multimodal OCTA analysis demonstrating robust diagnostic efficacy. The findings suggest that conjunctival-retinal vascular metrics may serve as potential new biomarkers for SLE, offering a possible approach to early detection and therapeutic monitoring in clinical practice.
