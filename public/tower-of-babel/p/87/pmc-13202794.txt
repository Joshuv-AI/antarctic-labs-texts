Low level of selenium predicts high incidence of colorectal cancer
Authors: Jiacheng Ying, Hongsheng Fang, Cheng Zhang, Tianlong Ling, Jiayin Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the relationship between selenium and its role in the incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted by searching PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases to identify studies evaluating the association between selenium exposure and CRC risk. Studies assessing selenium by serum concentration, dietary intake, or tissue level were eligible. The relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the random effects model.ResultsA total of 16 studies were included, and the overall pooled analysis showed that higher selenium exposure was associated with a reduced risk of CRC (RR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.72–0.91). When stratified by study design, significant inverse associations were observed in both cohort studies (RR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.78–0.90) and case-control studies (RR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.64–0.92). Subgroup analyses revealed significant inverse associations in European (RR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.67–0.98) and Oceanian (RR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.56–0.87) populations, and in colon cancer (RR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.68–0.97) but not rectal cancer. Dietary selenium intake showed a protective effect (RR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.63–0.86), whereas serum and toenail selenium were not significantly associated with CRC risk.ConclusionCollectively, our findings indicate that selenium sufficiency is linked to a lower risk of colorectal cancer. The results emphasize the potential role of selenium status as a modifiable nutritional factor in colorectal cancer prevention and warrant further mechanistic and interventional research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26379-4.Keywords: Selenium, Colorectal cancer, Diet, Prognosis, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Selenium is a trace micronutrient essential for optimal body functioning and for proper growth and development of organisms, such as selenocysteine and selenomethionine. Selenium is a naturally occurring trace element that is essential for human health but may become toxic at higher levels; however, its safe range of exposure has not been clearly defined. Nevertheless, selenium exposure and supplementation have been suggested to have a potential protective role against several types of cancer [1]. As a component of selenoproteins, selenium plays a crucial role in the regulation of various cell signaling pathways, which are essential for the proper functioning of the cell. These pathways control a wide range of cellular processes, including growth, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Selenium-containing selenoproteins, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and thioredoxin reductase (TrxR), act as potent antioxidants, scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protecting cells from oxidative stress. This may help to maintain cellular homeostasis and inhibit the development of colorectal neoplasms.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common forms of cancer worldwide and ranks third for cancer incidence globally with about 1.85 million cases annually. Long-term selenium deficiency may lead to cancer development [2, 3], and selenium has garnered significant attention for its potential role in CRC prevention and prognosis. Several potential biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain this link, highlighting the intricate relationship between selenium and CRC development [4, 5]. However, several studies have demonstrated that certain selenoproteins such as TXNRD1, SELENOF, and GPx2 appear to exert dual roles in tumor biology, suggesting that the relationship between selenium and CRC is complex [6, 7]. Although the epidemiological evidence remains controversial, many studies have suggested an inverse association between selenium status and CRC risk [8]. The present meta-analysis is conducted to synthesize existing evidence and provide a more consistent estimate of the relationship between selenium and CRC.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed through PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase, covering publications up to 6 December 2025. The detailed search terms used were presented in Table S1. In addition, references from relevant articles were manually checked to identify any further eligible studies. Two authors independently extracted data from each eligible study, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Participants were diagnosed with CRC. (2) Selenium exposure was assessed through dietary intake (e.g., food frequency questionnaires), serum concentrations, or tissue (toenail) measurements. (3) The study reported an effect estimate [odds ratio (OR), relative risk (RR), hazard ratio (HR), incidence rate ratio (IRR), or incidence rate difference (RD)] with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Exclusion criteria were: (1) Reviews, meta-analyses, basic science articles, and animal studies. (2) studies focusing exclusively on precancerous lesions such as adenomas or polyps. (3) Studies without extractable quantitative estimates of selenium exposure and CRC risk. (4) Duplicate reports or overlapping populations (the study with the most complete or recent data was retained).

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors independently extracted data using a standardized form. The following information was collected from each study: author, year of publication, study region or country, tumor anatomical location, gender, selenium assessment method (serum, toenail, or dietary intake), number of sample size, selenium concentration or intake levels, risk estimates (RR, OR, HR, IRR) with 95% CI, and covariates used for adjustment. When multiple models were presented, we preferentially extracted the most fully adjusted estimates. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Quality assessment of the included studies was performed using Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies and the Jadad scale for randomized controlled trials (RCT). Each study was evaluated independently by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. For observational studies, a NOS score of 7–9 indicated high quality, 5–6 indicated moderate quality, and ≤ 4 indicated low quality. For RCTs, studies with a Jadad score ≥ 3 were considered high quality, while those with a score < 3 were considered low quality.

Statistical method

Given the very low absolute risk of colorectal cancer in the general population within typical follow-up periods, and consistent with epidemiologic guidance, we treated ORs and HRs as approximately comparable to RRs for pooling purposes; this approach is widely used in nutritional epidemiology meta-analyses. For studies that reported three or more exposure categories relative to a common reference, we used the lowest category as the reference and combined the risk estimates into a single estimate. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 15.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station TX USA). Pooled RR with 95% CI were used to evaluate the association between selenium exposure and CRC, where RR < 1 indicates a reduced risk of CRC in the higher selenium group. The “inverse variance” method was applied to calculate summary estimates, and I2 statistics were evaluated to assess the heterogeneity across studies [9]. A series of subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis were carried out to identify the source of heterogeneity and the robustness of the results. Given the expected clinical and methodological diversity among observational studies, all analyses were performed using a random-effects meta-analysis model, regardless of the level of heterogeneity. Given that our meta-analysis included fewer than 20 studies (n = 16), Begg’s rank correlation test was adopted to evaluate potential publication bias, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant [10].

Results

Study selection

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 3,393 articles were initially retrieved from PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases. After removing duplicates, 1,862 unique records were subjected to detailed screening. Following the exclusion of articles with unrelated topics, the full texts of 19 potentially eligible studies were assessed. Among them, one study by M. Augustyniak et al. 2023 [11] was excluded because it evaluated patient survival rather than colorectal cancer (CRC) incidence. Additionally, two studies (F. Vahid et al. 2022 [12], F. Nozadi et al. 2021 [13]) were excluded since their results were only reported as mean ± sd, which could not be used for further quantitative analysis in this study. Ultimately, a total of 16 articles were included in the final meta-analysis.

Flowchart of the process of study inclusion and exclusion for meta-analysis. Abbreviation: RCT, randomized controlled trial

The main characteristics of the 16 included studies are presented in Table 1. Altogether, these studies comprised 192,008 participants with 9,807 CRC patients. Of the 16 included studies, two were RCT studies, three were prospective cohort studies, and eleven were case-control studies. With respect to selenium measurement, eight studies assessed selenium exposure based on dietary intake, five studies measured serum selenium concentrations directly, and two studies used toenail samples as biomarkers of selenium status. From a study region perspective, two studies were from Asia, six studies were from Europe, seven studies were from the North America, and one was from Oceania (Australia). Since CRC can be further classified into colon cancer (CC) and rectal cancer (RC), we also examined the anatomical site grouping among the 16 included studies. Of these, nine studies reported CRC as a whole without distinguishing between tumor sites, five studies provided independent analyses for both CC and RC, and two studies focused exclusively on CC.

Higher selenium level was associated with reduced CRC risk

Our primary random-effects meta-analysis of 16 studies showed a statistically significant inverse association between selenium and CRC risk (RR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.72–0.91, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The heterogeneity among these studies was high (I2 = 84.5%, P-heterogeneity < 0.001). Cohort studies showed a consistent and statistically significant association (RR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.78–0.90, P < 0.001), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I² < 0.01%, P-heterogeneity = 0.809). Similarly, case-control studies also indicated an inverse association (RR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.64–0.92, P = 0.004), though with substantial heterogeneity among studies (I² = 86.3%, P-heterogeneity < 0.001). For RCTs, the pooled risk estimate (RR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.34–1.66, P = 0.482) was not statistically significant, and moderate heterogeneity was observed (I² = 71.5%, P-heterogeneity = 0.061). There was no significant difference in effect size across study designs (P = 0.595), and the overall direction of association suggested a potential protective role of selenium against CRC.

Subgroup analysis

We performed a series of subgroup analyses according to population region, tumor site, gender and exposure source. We found that the inverse association between selenium and CRC risk persisted even when studies were stratified according to the factors described above.

As shown in Fig. 3, when stratified by population region, a significant protective association between selenium and CRC was observed in European (RR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.67–0.98, P = 0.028) and Oceania (RR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.56–0.87, P = 0.003) populations, whereas the associations in North America (RR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.69–1.06, P = 0.160) and Asia (RR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.37–1.39, P = 0.325) subgroups were weaker and not statistically significant. The direction of effect was generally consistent across regions, suggesting a potential universal protective role of selenium against CRC, although regional heterogeneity might be attributable to differences in baseline selenium intake and environmental exposure.

Figure 4 presents the subgroup analysis stratified by tumor site. Seven studies provided separate estimates for colon cancer, and five studies for rectal cancer. A significant inverse association between selenium level and colon cancer risk was observed (RR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.68–0.97, P = 0.023), indicating that higher selenium level was associated with a reduced risk of colon cancer. In contrast, the pooled estimate for rectal cancer (RR = 1.05, 95% CI = 0.83–1.33, P = 0.689) showed no statistically significant association. These results suggest that the protective effect of selenium may be more pronounced for colon cancer than for rectal cancer.

According to gender grouping (Fig. 5), no statistically significant associations were observed between selenium levels and CRC risk in either men or women. The pooled risk estimates were RR = 1.03 (95% CI = 0.89–1.19, P = 0.694) for men and RR = 0.92 (95% CI = 0.71–1.19, P = 0.508) for women. Although the association did not reach statistical significance, a potential trend toward an inverse relationship was observed among women.

Moreover, we further stratified the analysis according to the selenium assessment method (dietary intake, serum, and toenail; Fig. 6). Eight studies estimated dietary selenium intake, six directly measured serum selenium concentrations, and two assessed selenium level in toenails. A statistically significant inverse association was observed for dietary selenium intake (RR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.63–0.86, P < 0.001), suggesting that higher dietary selenium levels were associated with a reduced risk of CRC. In contrast, the pooled risk estimates for serum selenium (RR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.78–1.13, P = 0.514) and toenail selenium (RR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.73–1.03, P = 0.114) were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the pooled results. As shown in Figure S1, sequential omission of individual studies did not materially alter the overall pooled estimate, indicating the robustness of our results. Besides, sensitivity analysis including only high-quality studies (NOS ≥ 7 or Jadad scale ≥ 3) produced consistent results with the overall analysis, indicating that study quality did not materially affect the association (Figure S2).

Publication bias analysis

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot and Begg’s regression test. As shown in Figure S3, the funnel plot exhibited mild asymmetry, with a relative paucity of small studies reporting null or positive associations. Begg’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.669).

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of 16 studies comprising 192,008 participants and 9,807 CRC cases, we found that higher selenium exposure was significantly associated with a reduced risk of CRC. This inverse association remained consistent across most subgroup analyses, suggesting a potential protective role of selenium in CRC development. The association was particularly evident in dietary selenium intake, while serum and toenail selenium concentrations showed weaker and nonsignificant trends, possibly reflecting variations in assessment methods. We assessed study quality using established scoring systems, and the majority of studies were of high quality (NOS ≥ 7 or Jadad scale ≥ 3). Only two studies were rated as moderate quality, and importantly, when we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding these lower-quality studies, the overall result was essentially unchanged. This suggests that our findings are robust and not driven by any specific low-quality studies. These findings support the hypothesis that optimal selenium exposure contributes to colorectal cancer prevention through antioxidative and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

Our results are consistent with previous meta-analyses, including those by Y. Ou et al. [14] and M. Vinceti et al. [1], which also reported that higher selenium levels or intake were associated with a decreased risk of colorectal adenoma and CRC. Besides, multiple recent observational studies have consistently found that CRC patients exhibit lower selenium levels in blood compared to non-cancer controls. For example, a 2024 case-control study in a low-selenium European population reported that serum selenium in CRC patients was about 7.9% lower than in healthy controls (and 9.1% lower than in colorectal adenoma patients) [15]. Similarly, Chalcarz et al. (2024) found early-stage CRC patients had a mean serum Se concentration of ~ 67.2 µg/L versus ~ 78.8 µg/L in non-cancer surgery patients, a highly significant difference (P < 0.001) [16]. An exploratory investigation measuring 29 micronutrients in serum also identified selenium as one of the nutrients significantly decreased in CRC patients compared to controls [17]. Collectively, these findings support the notion that low serum selenium is associated with the presence and risk of colorectal cancer, reinforcing earlier observations that selenium deficiency may be a risk factor for CRC.

Although the significant inverse association between selenium exposure and CRC risk was observed in the subgroup analyses of cohort and case–control studies, the RCT subgroup yielded null results. These discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in baseline selenium status, intervention formulation, and study design. In the SELECT trial, participants had relatively high baseline selenium levels (median ≈ 137 µg/L; IQR 124.7–151.8 µg/L), a range at which selenoprotein activity is near saturation. Consequently, additional selenium supplementation (200 µg/day of L-selenomethionine) provided little incremental benefit, and the trial was terminated early after a median follow-up of 5.46 years for futility. In contrast, the Nutritional Prevention of Cancer (NPC) trial, which used selenized yeast (also 200 µg/day), showed a protective effect primarily among individuals with low baseline plasma selenium (≤ 113.4 ng/mL), whereas participants in the higher-level group exhibited a non-significant increase in overall cancer incidence. Moreover, differences in selenium formulation (selenized yeast vs. L-selenomethionine), primary endpoints (prostate vs. colorectal cancer), limited statistical power for CRC (a secondary outcome in SELECT), shorter follow-up, and restriction to male participants further explain the divergent findings. Taken together, these results indicate that the lack of effect in RCTs does not necessarily contradict observational evidence; instead, it suggests that selenium’s preventive benefits may be confined to populations with suboptimal baseline selenium status and may depend on the chemical form of selenium supplementation.

The inverse association between selenium exposure and CRC risk observed in our meta-analysis is biologically plausible and supported by multiple mechanistic pathways. Selenium exerts its anti-carcinogenic actions mainly through selenoproteins, which are integral to redox regulation and cellular antioxidant defense. Several selenoproteins play pivotal roles in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory damage—two hallmarks of colorectal carcinogenesis—by maintaining redox balance, regulating reactive oxygen species, and modulating inflammatory signaling cascades [4, 5]. Beyond these antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, selenium and its selenoproteins are also involved in regulating programmed cell death, controlling cell cycle progression, and suppressing abnormal cellular proliferation, thereby impeding tumor growth and progression [4, 5]. Moreover, selenium has been shown to inhibit tumor angiogenesis by downregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, as reported by Jiang et al., suggesting that selenium may also interfere with the formation of new blood vessels essential for tumor expansion and metastasis [18]. Selenium may further exert anti-metastatic effects by downregulating genes involved in osteopontin and collagen metabolism, thereby reducing extracellular matrix remodeling and cancer cell invasiveness [19, 20]. Interestingly, selenium has also been found to interfere with tumor cell metabolism, particularly by suppressing glutaminolysis—a key energy-producing pathway in cancer cells—thus limiting tumor bioenergetic capacity [21]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that not all selenoproteins exert uniformly protective roles. Certain members, such as thioredoxin reductase 1 (TXNRD1), selenoprotein F (SELENOF), and glutathione peroxidase 2 (GPx2), have been reported to exhibit dual, context-dependent effects—potentially promoting or inhibiting tumorigenesis depending on selenium concentration, redox state, and tumor microenvironment [6, 7]. This duality may partly explain the inconsistent findings observed in some clinical and experimental studies, reinforcing the concept of an optimal selenium range for cancer prevention.

However, the current research still has some limitations. First, the included studies were predominantly observational (prospective cohorts or case–control studies), which are susceptible to residual confounding and cannot establish definitive causality. Secondly, there was heterogeneity across included studies in our meta-analysis. Although we conducted several subgroup analyses, the major source of heterogeneity was not detected. It may results from study design and undefined baseline characteristics of participants. Additionally, ORs, HRs, and IRRs were approximated as RR for pooling, which assumes a rare outcome; although the absolute incidence of colorectal cancer is relatively low, this approximation may introduce bias. Moreover, this meta-analysis was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or any other registry.

Conclusion

In summary, this meta-analysis provides comprehensive evidence that higher selenium exposure is associated with a reduced risk of colorectal cancer. The protective effect of selenium was generally consistent across most subgroups and appears biologically plausible, supported by its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-proliferative functions. These findings highlight the potential importance of maintaining adequate selenium status in colorectal cancer prevention. However, given the observed heterogeneity and the dual roles of certain selenoproteins, further large-scale prospective studies and well-designed randomized trials are warranted to clarify the optimal range of selenium intake and its mechanistic pathways in colorectal carcinogenesis.
