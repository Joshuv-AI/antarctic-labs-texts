Incidence and risk factors for postoperative vaginal events following radical cystectomy for bladder cancer: a nationwide population‐based study
Authors: Elin Kalén, Charlotte Ginstman, Fredrik Liedberg, Oskar Hagberg, Martin Holmbom, Tomas Jerlström, Staffan Jahnson, Truls Gårdmark, Viveka Ströck, Lars Holmberg, Christel Häggström, Firas Aljabery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the probability of vaginal events (diagnosis and/or surgery) following radical cystectomy (RC) and explore possible risk factors in a nationwide population‐based observational registry based study.Patients and MethodsWomen undergoing RC for urinary bladder cancer in Sweden, from 1 January 1997 to 31 December 2019, were identified within national registries. Women with any postoperative vaginal event (PVE), either a diagnosis or surgical repair related to a vaginal complication, were identified using diagnostic and treatment codes. The probability of developing a PVE was estimated based on the cumulative incidence proportion using a competing risk model. Additionally, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was used to explore the risk factors for PVEs. Subgroup analysis was performed in patients operated from 2011 to 2019, where additional perioperative variables were registered.ResultsThe study encompassed 1914 women with a median age of 69 years at the time of bladder cancer diagnosis. The 5‐year cumulative risk of PVEs in the entire cohort was 11% (95% confidence interval [CI] 9.5–12.5%). Subgroup analysis showed that robot‐assisted RC and a body mass index (BMI) >30 kg/m2 were more often associated with PVEs after RC (hazard ratio [HR] 2.82, 95% CI 1.81–4.40; and HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.05–2.79, respectively).ConclusionsA clinically relevant cumulative incidence of PVEs following RC was identified. An association between robot‐assisted RC or high BMI with increased risk of a PVE indicate the need for further studies on risk assessment of vaginal complications.Keywords: bladder cancer, cumulative incidence, radical cystectomy, risk factor, vaginal events

Introduction

The standard treatment for muscle‐invasive urinary bladder cancer is radical cystectomy (RC), often preceded by neoadjuvant chemotherapy [1]. It is an extensive surgical procedure associated with significant morbidity.

To ensure radical resection of the tumour in female patients, the anterior vaginal wall is resected en bloc with the urinary bladder in most females who undergo the operation [2]. The posterior vaginal wall is then either folded anteriorly, attached to the remaining anterior vaginal wall (clam reconstruction), or reconstructed through combined lateral and posterior closure [3]. In some instances, additional mobilisation of the vaginal wall, both posteriorly and/or laterally, may be necessary prior to reconstruction. Regardless of the technique used, the procedure can result in the shortening and narrowing of the vaginal compartment. Moreover, incisions made in endopelvic fascia during the excision of the anterior vaginal wall can potentially increase the risk of postoperative dehiscence and prolapse [4, 5, 6, 7]. Postoperative vaginal complications may result in significant physical impairment and often requires surgical repair [4].

While several case series have reported postoperative vaginal events (PVEs), including postoperative vaginal complications diagnosis and/or surgical repair, the cumulative incidence of PVEs in a larger population base remains unknown.

Our primary aim was to estimate the cumulative incidence of PVEs following RC for bladder cancer. Additionally, to explore possible risk factors associated with the development of PVEs.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a nationwide, descriptive observational registry based study including all women diagnosed with bladder cancer in Sweden, from 1 January 1997 to 31 December 2019, who underwent RC, as recorded in the Bladder Cancer Database Sweden (BladderBaSe) 2.0. The study design followed the Strengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies [8].

The full cohort (1997–2019) was used for cumulative incidence estimation and general risk factor analysis. Subgroup (2011–2019) analysis was used for enhanced risk modelling, including additional perioperative variables available in later registry versions.

Healthcare System, Setting, and Population

Sweden has a population of 10 million individuals with a healthcare system of hospitals in larger towns and cities. Furthermore, there is a network of healthcare institutions giving primary care all over the country. A majority of all healthcare institutions are financed by regional administrations through taxes. All individuals in Sweden have access to the healthcare facilities by paying a minor fee. The costs of these fees and costs for pharmaceutical prescriptions for a given patient are limited in order to avoid damaging patients with low income or high need for medical care.

The SNRUBC includes, since 1997, information on patient, tumour, and primary treatment characteristics for Swedish patients with bladder cancer, with a coverage of >95% against the National Cancer Registry each year. Since 2011, additional detailed data are routinely collected in conjunction with RC, including surgical parameters, complications, and re‐operations within 90 days [14].

Extracted Variables

We collected comprehensive demographic, clinical, and perioperative data. This included age, tumour stage, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which was based on a pre‐defined list of diseases with specific weights assigned to each disease category according to data from the National Patient Register within a 10 year period prior to bladder cancer diagnosis [15]. The surgical approach: open RC (ORC) vs robot‐assisted RC (RARC). Data on the type of urinary diversion divided into orthotopic diversion vs other, which encompassed ileal conduit and continent cutaneous diversion. Marital status. History of vaginal or pelvic surgery or radiation. Body mass index (BMI) dichotomised as ≤30 and >30 kg/m2. Type and extent of lymph node dissection (limited, standard, extended). History of vaginal events before RC, date of RC, vital status (date of death or emigration), date of PVE (if any), International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD‐10) and the Swedish Classification of Health Care Interventions (Klassifikation av vårdåtgärder [KVÅ]) codes identifying PVEs (Table 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the first occurrence of a PVE, defined as either: a diagnosis of a vaginal complication (ICD‐10‐SE codes N80–N99), or a related surgical procedure identified using the KVÅ (LEA–LEW and TLE00, excluding vaginal biopsies).

The secondary outcomes included: time to first PVE diagnosis or PVE‐related surgery; and the association of the risk of PVEs with other variables related to patient, tumour, or surgery.

Statistical Analyses

In all time‐to‐event analyses, we used the date of RC as the starting point, and the end of follow‐up was determined by the date of death, the date of emigration, or 31 December 2019, whichever occurred first. Year was used as the underlying timescale in the analysis.

Descriptive statistics are presented for all women who underwent RC for urinary bladder cancer in Sweden between 1997 and 2019. Data are reported for the full cohort, a subgroup treated between 2011 and 2019, and patients who underwent RARC exclusively. Each characteristic is listed in rows, with the absolute number (N) and corresponding percentage (%) displayed for each group across the columns.

The cumulative incidence of PVEs was calculated using the Fine‐Gray competing risk model, accounting for all‐cause mortality.

Covariates included in the multivariable models were pre‐defined based on clinical relevance and prior literature, with input from domain experts in urology, oncology, and epidemiology. No data‐driven variable selection procedures (e.g., stepwise methods) were applied. The variables were chosen a priori to adjust for known or plausible confounders associated with the risk of PVEs and surgical outcomes.

Risk factors were explored using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. We explored the full cohort for variables of importance for PVEs, including type of surgery, age at diagnosis, tumour stage, CCI, marital status, urinary diversion type, and vaginal event before RC.

In a separate Cox analysis of the subgroup having RC during the years 2011–2019, we also included the extent of lymphadenectomy, BMI, previous pelvic surgery, or radiotherapy. The level of α error was set to 0.05 and all tests were two sided. The follow‐up was measured as observed deaths and cause of deaths or vaginal events and time to vaginal events.

The proportional hazards assumption was tested using the method of plotting the log of the negative log of the survival function against the log of time and no substantial aberration from the assumption was found.

All analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Uppsala University (file number 2015/277), which granted an exemption from individual informed consent in accordance with Swedish law (SFS 2003:460) and ethical guidelines for research using de‐identified registry data.

Results

Study Population

A total of 2012 women undergoing RC for urinary bladder cancer in Sweden between 1997 and 2019 were identified. After exclusions for date inconsistencies and missing data, 1914 patients were included in the final full cohort. A subgroup of 866 patients treated between 2011 and 2019 with additional registry data was also identified for further analysis (Fig. 1).

Selection of patients to study PVEs based on data from all women undergoing RC for urinary bladder cancer in Sweden 1997–2019. Subgroup of 866 women undergoing RC 2011–2019 with additional registry data available.

The median (interquartile range) age at diagnosis was 69 (62–75) years. A total of 321 patients (17%) had non‐organ‐confined disease (>T2), and 85% had no history of prior vaginal events. Most surgeries were ORC (85%). Orthotopic bladder substitution was used in 6% of patients (Table 1).

Among the subgroup of 866 women who underwent RC from 2011 to 2019, additional perioperative details were available. In this group, 18% had a BMI >30 kg/m2, and 79% underwent a standard or extended lymphadenectomy.

Cumulative Incidence of PVEs

Over a median follow‐up of 2.1 years (mean 4.1 years), 225 women (12%) experienced a PVE, and 46 of these had surgical repair for a PVE.

The cumulative incidence of PVEs was: 9.0% at 2 years (95% CI 7.7–10.3%), 11.0% at 5 years (95% CI 9.5–12.5%), and 13.2% at 10 years (95% CI 11.5–14.9%) (Fig. 2).

Probabilities of PVEs at certain time points after RC estimated by cumulative incidence proportion using all‐cause death as a competing event in a competing risk analysis in the full cohort (n = 1914).

Among the 292 women undergoing RARC, the risk of PVEs was notably higher: 17.6% at 2 years (95% CI 12.9–22.4%), 20.7% both at 5 and 10 years (95% CI 15.1–26.4%) (Fig. 3).

Probabilities of PVEs at certain time points after RC estimated by cumulative incidence proportion using all‐cause death as competing event in a competing risk analysis in patients who underwent RARC (n = 292).

In the full cohort, multivariable Cox regression identified RARC as significantly associated with PVEs (HR 2.17, 95% CI 1.52–3.09). No statistically significant associations were found for age, tumour stage, CCI, or marital status in this group (Table 2).

Discussion

In this large, nationwide population‐based observational register study, we found that >11% of women experienced a PVE within 5 years of RC for bladder cancer. Most of these PVEs were registered during the first postoperative years, indicating a possible causal relation to the surgical procedure. Notably, RARC and obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2) emerged as potential risk factors, highlighting the importance of tailored perioperative strategies for at‐risk patients.

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date focused on vaginal complications following RC for bladder cancer, using population‐based nationwide data with the possibility to capture most serious complications through a national hospital registration of diagnosis and surgical procedures. While prior studies, such as that by Richter et al. [5], reported higher rates of vaginal complications with >20% of women affected and ~5% requiring surgical intervention within 2 years, those findings were based on a smaller cohort of 481 patients, including both benign and malignant indications for RC, and limited to women aged ≥65 years. In contrast, our study offers a more generalisable estimate based on a nationwide, cancer‐specific population and capture of serious complications by hospital registration data. The discrepancy in complication rates may reflect differences in patient selection, coding practices, or healthcare settings, as well as the broader inclusion criteria used in earlier studies. Potential misclassification or under reporting of PVEs in registry data should also be considered when interpreting our findings.

Surgical Technique and Risk of Complications

The association between RARC and an elevated risk of PVEs is particularly noteworthy. In our analysis, women who underwent RARC demonstrated nearly a threefold increased risk of developing a PVE compared to those who underwent ORC. Although vaginal evisceration is a rare but recognised complication after conventional hysterectomy [16], our findings are consistent with gynaecological literature linking minimally invasive approaches to higher rates of vaginal cuff dehiscence [17, 18, 19]. In a retrospective multicentre cohort study of 100 women undergoing laparoscopic RC, Kanno et al. [20] reported that 7% required emergency surgical re‐operation for transvaginal bowel evisceration due to vaginal dehiscence, with a 5‐year risk of vaginal dehiscence of 12.7%. Similarly, other studies have described a higher occurrence of vaginal dehiscence following robot‐assisted and laparoscopic hysterectomies, suggesting a potential association between minimally invasive approaches and impaired vaginal healing [19, 21]. Potential mechanisms include extensive use of electrocautery during colpotomy, vaginal atrophy in older women, and inadequate suturing of the vaginal cuff following anterior pelvic exenteration, which often accompanies bladder resection during RC [21, 22]. In our cohort, 15% of women undergoing RARC developed a PVE, with a cumulative incidence of 17.6% at 2 years postoperatively.

The aetiology of vaginal prolapse and related complications likely varies between early and late presentations. Early PVEs may result from impaired vascularisation and poor wound healing at the vaginal closure site [23], while later prolapse may be driven by progressive failure of pelvic support structures. Multiple preoperative factors such as previous hysterectomy, pelvic organ prolapse (POP), pelvic surgery, pelvic radiation, and tobacco use as well as intraoperative factors like surgical technique, suture material, redundant small bowel mesentery, and failure to adequately re‐approximate the levator ani muscles, may contribute to prolapse development [24, 25]. Postoperative complications, including surgical site infection, may further compromise healing. In our study, prior POP, pelvic surgery, or radiation, although biologically plausible contributors, did not reach statistical significance.

To date, no previous study has specifically investigated the impact of operative technique (open, laparoscopic, or robot assisted) on PVE risk following RC. In contrast, gynaecological studies comparing laparoscopic and open hysterectomy for benign conditions have found no significant difference in the development of post‐hysterectomy POP [26, 27]. However, these findings may not be directly applicable to RC, given the greater extent of tissue resection and more profound disruption of pelvic anatomy associated with bladder cancer surgery. One small previous study investigating POP after ORC found a high rate of posterior (82%) and anterior (35%) prolapse but no apical prolapse, although only 17 patients were evaluated [28]. Most of these patients reported minimal symptoms, suggesting that prolapse after RC may be under recognised unless specifically assessed.

The exact pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PVEs remain unclear but are likely multifactorial, involving the surgical learning curve, variations in technique, and mechanical factors such as increased intra‐abdominal pressure during laparoscopic or robot‐assisted surgery or specimen extraction via the vaginal route [20]. These factors may promote oedema, inflammation, and tension on suture lines, ultimately leading to tissue breakdown. Although our findings were adjusted for known clinical confounders, we acknowledge that the non‐randomised, observational nature of our study introduces the possibility of residual bias. Nonetheless, the consistent association between RARC and PVEs strongly supports the need for prospective studies or randomised trials to more precisely define technical risk factors and optimise surgical strategies to mitigate vaginal complications after RC.

Obesity as a Modifiable Risk Factor

Patients with a BMI >30 kg/m2 were also at significantly higher risk of a PVE. Obesity may contribute to impaired wound healing, increased intra‐abdominal pressure, and altered tissue support dynamics – all of which could predispose to prolapse or dehiscence. Given the rising prevalence of obesity, this association calls for increased preoperative counselling, weight management strategies, and perhaps more vigilant postoperative surveillance in this population.

Clinical Implications and Research Gaps

Our results support the need for structured preoperative risk assessment in female patients with bladder cancer undergoing RC, particularly those with a high BMI or those considered for RARC. Given the functional and psychological implications of vaginal complications, future care models should integrate pelvic floor evaluation, sexual function assessment, and patient‐reported outcomes into standard follow‐up protocols.

Currently, no consensus exists regarding classification, prevention, or management of PVEs after RC. Treatment remains highly individualised, and coding limitations within national registers likely underestimate the true burden of less severe or non‐surgical complications. Future efforts should aim to establish validated grading systems for PVEs severity and explore the role of prophylactic interventions, such as pelvic support reconstruction using synthetic or biological mesh, and different flap techniques that are somewhat controversial and not yet universally standardised [29, 30].

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study are the large sample size, population‐based and nationwide design, high‐quality data and extensive coverage in the SNRUBC, as well as the utilisation of other national register data in the BladderBaSe 2.0, with high capture ratios and follow‐up. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large population‐based study of PVEs after RC. Furthermore, the exploration of the risk factors influencing vaginal complications, and the use of a competing risk model adds new knowledge to the literature. Our study has potential limitations. PVE reporting to the SNRUBC was not mandatory, and our results relied solely on register data from inpatient and outpatient registers. Register data are likely to report foremost overt complications that require larger healthcare resources. Many patients may have problems that do not require inpatient healthcare or surgical interventions but still have frequent or even chronic problems that impair their quality of life. Given that both urologists and gynaecologists may be involved in postoperative care and documentation, diagnostic coding may vary based on specialty focus, experience, and interpretation. This could lead to inconsistent classification of pelvic complications, potentially affecting the accuracy of our outcome estimates. We were not able to retrieve specific ICD‐10 codes for individual PVE diagnoses. This poses a risk of misclassifying vaginal problems unrelated to RC as postoperative complications. The observed higher incidence of PVEs among patients undergoing RARC might be influenced by longer survival times, as these patients generally exhibited more favourable baseline characteristics; however, most PVEs were registered within the first 2 years after surgery. Competing risks, such as early mortality in the ORC group, could have led to an underestimation of late complications in that cohort. A direct, adjusted comparison of survival by surgical approach was not available and represents an important area for future research.

In conclusion, vaginal complications are frequent and clinically meaningful yet under recognised consequence of RC in women. Register data are likely to report foremost the problems requiring larger interventions and likely underestimate the risk of patient bother associated with PVEs. We also show that it is possible to define surgical and patient‐associated risk factors. Further research, particularly incorporating prospective, technical, and patient‐centred data is essential to improve outcomes and guide evidence‐based standards for care.
