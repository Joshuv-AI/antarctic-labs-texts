The persistent effects of exposure to a predator odor stressor on sensitivity to alcohol
Authors: Ryan E. Tyler, Maya N. Bluitt, Kalynn J. Van Voorhies, Wen Liu, Sarah N. Magee, Elisabeth R. Pitrolo, Victoria L. Cordero, Laura C. Ornelas, Caroline G. Krieman, Brooke N. Bender, Alejandro M. Mosera, Joyce Besheer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traumatic stress is linked to problematic alcohol use, yet its persistent effects on alcohol sensitivity remain unclear. This study examined the long-term effects of traumatic stress on alcohol sensitivity in male and female Long-Evans rats. Rats were trained to discriminate alcohol (2 g/kg, i.g.) from water, and two weeks after a single, inescapable exposure to the predator odor stressor TMT, substitution for an alcohol dose curve and GABAA agonism were assessed both systemically (pentobarbital, i.p.) and site-specifically [muscimol in the prelimbic cortex (PrL) or anterior insular cortex (aIC)]. Additional experiments in alcohol-naive rats examined the effects of TMT on alcohol-induced behaviors, c-Fos expression, and GABAA receptor gene expression in the PrL and aIC. TMT exposure produced a leftward shift in the alcohol dose-response curve, indicating increased interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol in males, but not females. The alcohol-like effects of systemic pentobarbital were unaltered by TMT exposure. TMT reduced Gabra1 expression and increased c-Fos in the PrL of males, whereas TMT increased Gabra1 expression without altering c-Fos in the PrL of females. Alcohol-induced effects on locomotion and startle response were not observed in the TMT-exposed group when analyzed in both sexes. These findings highlight sex-specific effects of traumatic stress on alcohol sensitivity, with evidence that stress-induced PrL GABAA adaptations may contribute to increased interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol in males. These results may help to better understand the association between traumatic stress and alcohol use disorder (AUD).

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating neuropsychiatric condition that develops in some individuals following exposure to a traumatic event [1]. PTSD is highly co-morbid with alcohol use disorder (AUD) [2–4], and stress is commonly linked to increased alcohol consumption or craving across multiple species [5–13]. Sensitivity to the subjective effects of alcohol is a strong predictor of AUD development and associated with differences in alcohol use [14–16]. However, how the persistent effects of trauma impact sensitivity to alcohol, and associated neurobiological mechanisms, are unknown.

Exposure to a predator odor has been used as an animal model of a traumatic stressor [7,17–20], and the resulting behavioral and neural adaptations have some relevance to understanding symptoms associated with PTSD. In the present work, rats were exposed to the predator odor 2,5-dihydro-2,4,5-trimethylthiazoline (TMT), a synthetic component fox feces, in an inescapable enclosure to model a traumatic stressor [5,6,21–27]. Alcohol sensitivity outcomes were always collected two weeks after the TMT exposure to assess persistent effects, which more closely models the lasting effects of trauma that are observed in individuals with PTSD.

In animal models, sensitivity to the effects of alcohol can be assessed using drug discrimination procedures [28–36]. The learned behavior is guided by the internal state of the animal following alcohol administration, and as such we will refer to these effects of alcohol as interoceptive effects. There is extensive literature supporting the hypothesis that the interoceptive effects of alcohol are predominantly driven by the effects of alcohol on Gamma-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors [29,32,33,37–39]. Generally, GABAA receptor-mediated disinhibition by alcohol is associated with the stimulatory effects of alcohol [40–43], and NMDA receptor internalization by alcohol is associated with its sedative effects [42,44,45]. The involvement of the GABAA and NMDA receptors in the interoceptive effects of alcohol are confirmed by the ability of GABAA agonists (e.g., pentobarbital, muscimol) and some NMDA receptor antagonists (e.g., MK-801) to substitute for the discriminative stimulus / interoceptive effects of alcohol [32–34,37,46,47]. Substitution testing for the interoceptive effects of drugs in drug discrimination procedures has historically demonstrated a remarkable ability to pinpoint the receptor biology underlying drug effects [29,34,37]. As such, we employed substitution experiments to investigate the receptor biology underlying potentiated sensitivity to the interoceptive effects of alcohol following exposure to the predator odor stressor (TMT). In addition, stress-induced neuroadaptations are driven in part by synaptic plasticity induced by adaptations to the GABAA receptor [48–51], suggesting potential interactions and overlapping mechanisms between stress and the expression of the interoceptive effects of alcohol. Furthermore, the prelimbic cortex (PrL) and the anterior insular cortex (aIC) are highly sensitive to acute and persistent effects of stress [52–54], as well as having involvement in the subjective/interoceptive effects of alcohol [29,39,55–57]. Additionally, both brain regions are strongly implicated in PTSD and AUD neuropathology [58–68]. As such, we hypothesized that traumatic stress may impact interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol through changes in the GABAA receptor in the PrL and/or the aIC.

We recently showed that TMT exposure potentiated the interoceptive effects of alcohol in a small sample of male rats using an operant drug discrimination procedure [30]. The present work expands on this finding using Pavlovian drug discrimination methods in both sexes to further investigate the neurobiological and behavioral mechanisms that may underlie this effect. We first assessed the behavioral response to TMT exposure during the 15-min exposure to quantify “stress-reactivity” as assessed by digging and immobility [6,18,21,24,30]. Next, we assessed the effects of TMT exposure on substitution for the alcohol training dose (2 g/kg) by an alcohol dose curve (0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 g/kg) and GABAA receptor agonism both systemically and microinjected into the PrL or aIC. We then evaluated the effects of TMT exposure on GABAA receptor subunit gene expression in the PrL and aIC in alcohol-naïve rats. Next, we tested the effects of TMT exposure following acute alcohol administration on c-Fos expression in the PrL and aIC, locomotion in the open-field test (OFT), and acoustic startle response (ASR) in alcohol--naïve rats. Together, these experiments may help us better understand the high co-morbidity between PTSD and AUD by elucidating how traumatic stress affects sensitivity to alcohol and the underlying neurobiological changes.

Methods and materials

Animals:

Male and female Long-Evans rats (Envigo) arrived at 7 weeks of age and were housed individually under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with experiments conducted during the light phase. For Pavlovian drug discrimination (Experiment 1) and alcohol metabolism (Experiment 2), rats were fed daily to maintain 85 % of free-feeding weight. Alcohol-naïve rats (Experiment 3–5) had ad libitum access to food. All rats had ad libitum access to water. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the University of North Carolina – Chapel Hill Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to National Institutes of Health guidelines for animal care and use.

TMT Exposure:

Rats were exposed to 2,5-dihydro-2,4,5-trimethylthiazoline (TMT) or water (control) in separate test chambers (45.72 × 17.78 × 21.59 cm) under conditions consistent with previous studies [6,21,30]; see [18] for detailed protocol description). Rats were transported from the vivarium to a ventilated room containing the test chambers. Each chamber had opaque white and black walls, a clear plexiglass front for video recording, a sliding lid, and a layer of white bedding. A metal basket on the chamber wall held filter paper with 10 μL of TMT or water pipetted immediately before placing the rat in the chamber. Control (water) exposures were conducted before TMT sessions to prevent odor contamination. Rats were exposed to the odor for 15 min (10 min in Experiment 1), with behavior video-recorded and analyzed using the ANY-maze Video Tracking System. Similar to our prior work, the primary stress-reactivity behaviors that were analyzed were digging and immobility [6,18,21,28,30]. Digging refers to pushing bedding with forepaws, often towards the TMT source and usually resulting in a mound of bedding directly below the TMT source. Immobility was defined as no movement for ≥ 2 s, which captures freezing behavior and general immobility. Following exposure, rats were returned to their home cages for a 2-week “incubation period” before behavioral testing or molecular assessments [30].

Drugs:

Alcohol (95 % w/v) was diluted in tap water to a concentration of 20 % (v/v). Alcohol was administered via oral gavage (i.g.) at varying volumes to achieve the desired dose. Sodium pentobarbital (Patterson Veterinary, MA) was dissolved in saline (0.9 %) to a concentration of 10 mg/mL and administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at 0.5 ml/kg body weight for a 5 mg/kg dose. Muscimol (R&D systems, Minneapolis, MN) was dissolved in saline (0.9 %) to produce a 10 mM (for aIC microinjections) and a 2.5 mM (for PrL microinjections) solution.

Experiment 1: effects of TMT exposure on interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol

Male (N = 67) and female (N = 56) rats underwent Pavlovian drug discrimination training to discriminate the interoceptive effects of alcohol (2.0 g/kg, i.g.) from water, following established protocols [28,30,31,37,57,69,70]. Two weeks after TMT exposure, all rats that met criteria for discrimination (see criteria described below in Acquisition Training) were tested on an alcohol dose-response curve (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 g/kg, i.g.) (Fig. 2A). Before the alcohol dose-response testing, a subset of rats underwent systemic pentobarbital (GABAA receptor agonist) injections and site-specific injections of muscimol into the PrL or aIC (see detailed methods below). We used a sub-threshold dose of pentobarbital and muscimol (i.e., doses that would not fully substitute) for substitution experiments as we observed potentiated sensitivity to low doses of alcohol and thus predicted substitution at these sub-threshold doses [39,71]. Results for Experiment 1 are shown in Fig. 1 (A and B), Fig. 2 and Supplemental Fig. S1.

Apparatus:

On one side of the chamber, cue lights were located on either side of a liquid receptacle. A photobeam detector was used to measure head entries into the liquid receptacle.

Sucrose Access Training:

Training started with three 50-min sessions which provided sucrose (26 %, w/v) randomly throughout the session. The probability of sucrose presentation decreased from the first to the last session and by the last 10 min of the final session rats received approximately 0.75 sucrose presentations/min.

Acquisition Training:

After sucrose access training, acquisition sessions were conducted five days a week (Monday-Friday). During training, rats received alcohol (2 g/kg, i.g.) or water (i.g.) and were placed in the chambers. After a 20-min delay to allow for alcohol absorption, a 15-min session began. During alcohol sessions, the offset of each 15-s cue light (presented 10 times randomly) was followed by sucrose delivery (26 % w/v, 0.1 mL for 4 s). During water sessions, no sucrose was delivered after the cue light. Sessions occurred in a double alternation pattern (e.g., Alcohol, Alcohol, Water, Water). Testing began after a minimum of 12 alcohol and water sessions. To meet criteria for discrimination and qualify for testing, rats had to achieve a discrimination score difference (alcohol session score - water session score) of ≥ 2 based on the mean of the last two alcohol and two water sessions. Only rats meeting this criterion were included in the experiment. Test sessions included a 20-min timeout period followed by a single 15-s cue light presentation that was presented randomly during the 2-min test and no sucrose was delivered after the light presentation.

Alcohol dose response testing:

All rats that met criteria for discrimination received alcohol dose response testing. Alcohol was administered at one of four doses in ascending order (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.0 g/kg, i.g.) on separate testing days. Note that all discrimination experiments included an alcohol dose response test, but the alcohol dose testing followed the systemic pentobarbital or site-specific muscimol injection substitution testing for the subset of rats used in these experiments (see below). Males: CTRL: n = 21, TMT: n = 40; Females: CTRL: n = 18, TMT: n = 15.

Systemic pentobarbital (5 mg/kg) testing:

Prior to alcohol dose response testing days, a subset of rats received a pentobarbital test session. Rats were injected i.p. with 5 mg/kg pentobarbital and then received water (i.g.), identical to water training sessions, and were placed in the chamber for a test session. Males: CTRL: n = 15, TMT: n = 19; Females: CTRL: n = 9, TMT: n = 9.

Site-specific muscimol testing: Surgeries:

After training on the alcohol drug discrimination task, a subset of male rats underwent bilateral cannulae implantation surgeries. Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (5 % induction, 2 % maintenance) and implanted with 26-gauge guide cannulae (Plastics One, Roanoke, VA) positioned 2 mm above the aIC (AP +2.5 mm, ML ±4.0 mm, DV −4.6 mm) or PrL (AP +3.2 mm, ML ±0.6 mm, DV −2.0 mm) based on coordinates from [72]. Banamine (2.5 mg/kg, s.c.) was given for analgesia the day before, the day of, and the day after surgery. After a 1-week recovery from surgery, rats completed at least four additional acquisition training sessions (2 Alcohol, 2 Water) before undergoing TMT exposure.

Testing:

Prior to alcohol dose response testing, this subset of rats underwent muscimol testing in the aIC or PrL. On the test session, muscimol was delivered using 33-gauge injectors extending 2 mm beyond the guide cannulae. Microinjections were administered via a microinfusion pump (Harvard Apparatus, MA) connected to 5.0 μL Hamilton syringes at a rate of 0.5 μL/min. Muscimol was infused at 0.5 μL per side over 1 min (aIC: 10 mM; PrL: 2.5 mM). Injectors were left in place for 2 min post-injection to allow for diffusion. Immediately after the diffusion period, rats received water (i.g.), identical to water training sessions, and were placed in the chamber for a test session.

Perfusions and cannulae verification:

At the end of the experiment, rats were transcardially perfused and brains collected to verify cannulae placement. Brain tissue was stained with cresyl violet, and only data from rats with confirmed cannula/injector tracts in the target regions were included. Cannulae placements for successful hits included in the analyses are shown in Supplemental Fig. S1B. Initial sample size: PrL: CTRL n = 6, TMT n = 16; aIC: CTRL n = 6, TMT n = 7. 5 rats from the PrL and 1 from the aIC were removed from analyses due to incorrect bilateral cannulae placement (see Figure Captions for final sample sizes included in analyses).

Experiment 2: effects of TMT exposure on alcohol metabolism

To determine whether TMT exposure altered alcohol levels, two weeks after TMT exposure, male and female rats received alcohol (1.0 g/kg, i.g.) and tail blood was collected across time points (20, 60, 90, and 120 min later). The 1.0 g/kg alcohol dose was selected as this was the dose that showed potentiated sensitivity to alcohol in Experiment 1. These rats were alcohol-naïve. Results are displayed in Fig. 3. Males: CTRL: n = 4, TMT: n = 8; Females: CTRL: n = 4, TMT: n = 8.

Experiment 3: effects of TMT exposure on PrL and aIC gene expression

Male (N = 10/exposure group) and female (N = 10/exposure group) alcohol-naïve rats underwent TMT exposure and were sacrificed 2 weeks later for gene expression analysis in the PrL and aIC using qRT-PCR. GABAA receptor subunit genes were examined (primer sequences in Table 1). Tissue collection and analysis methods followed established protocols [24,30], and are briefly summarized below. Results are shown in Fig. 4.

Tissue Collection:

Brains were collected under isoflurane anesthesia, flash-frozen in isopentane, and stored at −80 °C. Target regions were sectioned on a cryostat and excised with a micro-punch tool.

RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis:

RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and quantified with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the SuperScript™ III system and diluted 1:5 before RT-PCR.

RT-PCR:

Gene expression was quantified using the QuantStudio3 PCR system with PowerUp Syber Green dye, primers, and cDNA templates. Reactions included 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 15 s) and annealing/extension (60 ரC, 60 s). Melt curves confirmed single amplicons, and Gapdh was used as a housekeeping gene.

Experiment 4: effects of TMT exposure and acute alcohol administration on c-Fos expression in the PrL and aIC

Alcohol-naïve male (N = 8/exposure group/alcohol pretreatment) and female (N = 8/exposure group/alcohol pretreatment) rats underwent TMT exposure. Two weeks later, rats received a single oral gavage of alcohol (2.0 g/kg) or water (vehicle control) and were sacrificed 90 min post-gavage for brain collection and c-Fos immunohistochemistry analysis in the PrL and aIC. Results shown in Fig. 5.

Brain Tissue Collection and Sectioning:

Rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 0.1 M PBS (4 °C, pH=7.4) followed by 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, 4 °C, pH=7.4). Brains were stored in PFA for 24 h, then transferred to 30 % sucrose in PBS. Coronal sections (40 μm) were collected using a freezing microtome and stored in cryoprotectant at −20 °C until immunohistochemistry (IHC).

c-Fos Immunohistochemistry:

Coronal sections (free floating) were rinsed in PBS (0.1 M) before a 5-min wash in 1 % hydrogen peroxide. Next, sections were blocked in 3 % normal goat serum (NGS; Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA) in 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 2 h. Sections were then incubated in rabbit anti-c-Fos antibody (1:4000 in 3 % NGS + 0.1 % Triton; Synaptic Systems, Gottingen, Germany; Lot# 226003/3–45) for 16 h at 4 °C. This was followed by an incubation with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200 in 3 % NGS + 0.1 % Triton X-100; Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA; Lot # 140893). Next, sections were incubated in Vectastain Elite ABC HRP (Vector labs, Burlingame, CA). Lastly, brain sections were treated with diaminobenzidine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and then mounted on glass slides.

Images were captured with an Olympus CX41 microscope (Olympus America, Center Valley, PA), and bilateral c-Fos-positive cells/mm2 were quantified using Image-Pro Premier software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD). Data were averaged from at least two sections per brain region per animal (aIC and PrL, bregma +3.00 to +3.72, Fig. 5D). Regions of interest were defined based on prior studies [5,30,56,69].

Experiment 5: effects of TMT exposure and alcohol administration on locomotion and acoustic startle response behavior

Alcohol-naïve male (N = 12/exposure group/alcohol pretreatment) and female (N = 12/exposure group/alcohol pretreatment) rats underwent TMT exposure. Two weeks later, rats received alcohol (2.0 g/kg, i.g.) or water (vehicle control, eq. v/v, i.g.) and general locomotor behavior was assessed in the open field test (OFT) and acoustic startle response (ASR) in counterbalanced sessions. Results shown in Fig. 6.

Open-Field Test:

Rats acclimated to the test room for 30 min. Twenty minutes after alcohol or water administration under red light, rats were placed in a dark open-field arena (43 × 43 cm; Med Associates, St. Albans, VT) for 20 min to assess locomotion, recorded via 32 orthogonal infrared beams using Activity Monitor software (Med Associates).

Acoustic Startle Response Test:

The next day following a 30-min acclimation period, rats were tested for startle response 20 min after alcohol or water administration. They were placed in a sound-attenuating chamber (S-R Lab; San Diego Instruments, San Diego, CA) with 60 dB background noise. Following a 5-minute habituation, rats experienced 30 trials of a 110 dB startle noise (100 ms) with randomized intertrial intervals (30–45 s)[6]. Startle amplitude was recorded using an accelerometer and analyzed with SR-Lab software.

Data analysis

For all experiments, digging and immobility during TMT exposure (Fig. 1), were analyzed in 3-min bins (3, 6, 9, 12, 15 min). Three-way ANOVAs were used with TMT and sex as between-subject factors and timepoint as a within-subject factor. Sidak’s multiple comparisons were conducted for post-hoc analyses.

For Experiment 1 (drug discrimination), the primary dependent measure was the discrimination score, calculated as [head entries during the 15-s light presentation] minus [head entries during the 15-s period before the light] consistent with previous work [28,30,31,69,73]. Locomotor rate (beam breaks/min) in the chambers was also recorded during sessions. The discrimination score and locomotor rate were analyzed using a two or three-way ANOVA depending on the experiment with group (CTRL vs. TMT) and sex (male vs. female) as a between-subject factor and alcohol dose as a within-subject factor. Main effects of sex or interactions that involved sex prompted separate analyses for males and females as we were most interested in the effects of TMT within sex. This strategy was implemented for all experiments. For the muscimol site-specific substitution experiments, only males were used. A one-tailed, unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction was used due to established potentiated sensitivity (effect direction) and differences in sample sizes between the control and TMT group. Due to the low final sample size of these experiments, site-specific muscimol injection results are included as Supplemental Fig. S1. To determine substitution for the 2.0 g/kg alcohol training dose, the post-TMT discrimination scores were compared to the group average discrimination score at the training dose pre-TMT in males and females separately using a one-sample t-test, similar to analyses used in previous work [69,74]. The lack of a significant difference indicated “substitution” for the training dose, and a significant difference indicated “no substitution”.

For Experiment 2 (alcohol metabolism), blood alcohol concentration was analyzed using a three-way ANOVA with TMT exposure and sex as between-subject factors and timepoint as a within-subject factor.

For Experiment 3 (gene expression), the ΔΔCt method was used to calculate fold change relative to controls, normalized so the average male control fold change equaled 1. A two-way ANOVA was performed with group (CTRL vs. TMT) and sex (male vs. female) as between-subject factors, with post-hoc Sidak’s tests.

For Experiment 4 (c-Fos immunoreactivity), the average number of c-Fos-positive cells per mm2 was the dependent measure. A three-way ANOVA was conducted with group (CTRL vs. TMT), pre-treatment condition (alcohol vs. water), and sex (male or female) as between-subject factors.

In Experiment 5 (OFT and ASR), the dependent measure for the OFT was total distance traveled during the 20-minute session. For ASR, the average peak startle response across 30 trials was used. A three-way ANOVA was performed with group (TMT vs. CTRL), pretreatment condition (water vs. alcohol), and sex (male vs. female) as between-subject factors. Based on a priori hypotheses that alcohol would decrease locomotor behavior and startle response, planned comparisons were conducted to follow up on significant main effects of alcohol (2 g/kg) to evaluate its impact within the CTRL and TMT groups separately.

Results

TMT exposure induced digging and immobility behavior in alcohol discrimination-trained and alcohol-naïve rats (Fig. 1)

Rats trained on the alcohol discrimination task were exposed to a single 15-min TMT exposure. For digging (Fig. 1A), there was a main effect of TMT [F(1, 119)=121.6, p < 0.0001] and timepoint [F(3.35, 398.4)=42.19, p < 0.0001], but no main effect of sex. There was a significant TMT x timepoint interaction effect [F(4, 476)=40.47, p < 0.0001]. Post-hoc tests showed greater time spent digging in the TMT group compared to the control group at every timepoint (3, 6, 9, 12, 15 min; p < 0.0005).

For immobility in alcohol discrimination-trained rats (Fig. 1B), there was a main effect of TMT [F(1, 119)=8.29, p = 0.005], with increased immobility in the TMT group and a main effect of timepoint [F(2.56, 304.1)=120.2, p < 0.0001], with increased immobility across the session, but no main effect of sex. There was a significant TMT x timepoint interaction [F (4, 476) = 25.94, p < 0.0001)] and TMT x sex interaction [F (1, 119) = 7.02, p = 0.009]. Post-hoc tests showed differences between the TMT and control groups at timepoint 3, 6, 12, and 15 min (p < 0.0005). Together, these data demonstrate that during the TMT exposure male and female rats engaged in digging behavior during the early part of the session and transitioned to immobility behavior later in the session. Additionally, there were no sex differences in the degree of digging and immobility behavior.

Several other follow-up experiments were then conducted to further investigate the effects of TMT exposure on molecular and behavioral adaptations. These rats experienced the same TMT exposure protocol and underwent the same 2-week incubation period as the discrimination-trained rats, but were alcohol naïve. The alcohol-naïve cohorts were combined for an overall analysis of digging and immobility behavior during the TMT exposure (Males: CTRL: n = 50, TMT: n = 48; Females: CTRL: n = 49, TMT: n = 49) (Fig. 1C and D). Rats showed a significant increase in digging behavior (Fig. 1C), as confirmed by a significant main effect of TMT [F(1, 192)=149.4, p < 0.0001] and timepoint [F(2.97, 569.9)=50.42, p < 0.0001]. Significant interactions between TMT x sex [F(1, 192)=4.14, p = 0.04], TMT x timepoint [F(4, 768)=51.49, p < 0.0001], and TMT x sex x timepoint [F(4, 768)=7.80, p < 0.0001] were observed. Males showed a main effect of TMT [F(1, 96)=65.01, p < 0.0001], timepoint [F(2.88, 276.9)=41.95, p < 0.0001], and a TMT x timepoint interaction [F(4, 384)=45.89, p < 0.0001], with increased digging at 3, 6, and 9 min relative to the water group (p < 0.05). Females showed a main effect of TMT [F(1, 96)=84.56, p < 0.0001], timepoint [F(2.78, 266.5)=17.97, p < 0.0001], and a TMT x timepoint interaction [F(4, 384)=17.83, p < 0.0001], with greater digging in the TMT group at all time points relative to the control group (p < 0.05).

For immobility (Fig. 1D), there was a main effect of TMT [F(1, 191)=207.3, p < 0.0001] and timepoint [F(2.90, 554.0)=328.6, p < 0.0001], no main effect of sex, a TMT x sex interaction [F(1, 191)=4.18, p = 0.04] and TMT x sex x timepoint interaction [F(4, 764)=2.88, p = 0.02]. Males showed a main effect of TMT [F(1, 95)=166.8, p < 0.0001], timepoint [F(3.17, 301.4)=221.0, p < 0.0001], and a TMT x timepoint interaction [F(4, 380)=99.98, p < 0.0001], with greater immobility in the TMT group at timepoints 3, 9, 12, and 15 relative to the control group (p < 0.05). Females showed a main effect of TMT [F(1, 54)=20.60, p < 0.0001], timepoint [F(2.35, 127.1)=54.79, p < 0.0001], and a TMT x timepoint interaction [F(4, 216)=19.98, p < 0.0001], with greater immobility in the TMT group at 3, 12, and 15 min time points relative to the control group (p < 0.05). Similar to the patterns observed in the discrimination-trained rats (Fig. 1A, B), these results show a transition from digging behavior to immobility behavior during the TMT exposure.

Experiment 1: TMT exposure potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol in male but not female rats and did not potentiate the alcohol-like effects of pentobarbital (Fig. 2)

Prior to TMT exposure, the discrimination-trained rats showed accurate discrimination performance. That is, discrimination scores (Fig. 2B) showed a main effect of alcohol [F(1, 180)=371.4, p < 0.0001], with higher discrimination scores following alcohol (2 g/kg) administration relative to water confirming stimulus control over behavior. There was no effect of group (TMT or CTRL) or sex. Locomotor rate (Fig. 2C) showed a main effect of alcohol [F(1, 180)=4.90, p = 0.028], sex [F(1, 180)=67.85, p < 0.0001], group [F(1, 180)=4.98, p = 0.027], and a significant alcohol x sex interaction effect [F(1, 180)=5.82, p = 0.002]. In males, there was no effect of group, alcohol, and no interaction. In females, there was no effect of group, but there was a main effect of alcohol dose [F(1, 31)=6.48, p = 0.02], with an alcohol-induced reduction in locomotor rate, and no interaction effect. These results confirm that both males and females appropriately discriminated the interoceptive effects of the alcohol training dose (2 g/kg) from water, and that no group differences were observed prior to the TMT exposure. Furthermore, females showed higher general locomotor rates than males and were more sensitive to the locomotor-suppressing effects of alcohol prior to TMT exposure.

Two weeks after TMT exposure, an alcohol substitution curve was conducted. For discrimination scores (Fig. 2D), there was a main effect of TMT [F(1, 90)=5.20, p = 0.02], alcohol dose [F(2.65, 238.0)=75.76, p < 0.0001], but no effect of sex. There was an alcohol dose x sex interaction [F(3, 270)=5.04, p = 0.002], and an interaction between TMT and sex [F(1, 90)=4.56, p = 0.03]. Male rats showed a main effect of alcohol dose [F(2.38, 140.3)=63.83, p < 0.0001], a main effect of TMT [F(1, 59)=11.32, p = 0.001] and an alcohol dose x TMT exposure interaction [F(3, 177)=3.09, p = 0.03]. Post-hoc tests showed higher discrimination scores at the 0.5 g/kg dose (p = 0.02) and the 1.0 g/kg dose (p = 0.009) in the TMT group vs. the control group, but not at 0.0 or 2.0 g/kg doses.

Locomotor rate (Fig. 2E) decreased with dose as confirmed by a main effect of alcohol dose [F(2.84, 254.4)=12.93, p < 0.0001], but there was no main effect of TMT. There was a significant main effect of sex [F(1, 90)=4.89, p = 0.03], an alcohol dose x sex interaction [F(3, 269)=3.40, p = 0.01], and a TMT x alcohol dose x sex interaction [F(3, 269)=3.31, p = 0.02]. In males, there was no effect of TMT or alcohol dose, and no interaction effect. In females, there was a main effect of alcohol dose [F(3, 93)=11.77, p < 0.0001] and no main effect of TMT. There was a significant alcohol dose x TMT interaction [F(3, 93)=3.70, p = 0.01], with locomotor rate decreased at every alcohol dose compared to vehicle in the control group (p < 0.05), but no differences in the TMT group, suggesting blunted sensitivity to the motor-suppressing effects of alcohol following TMT in females. Together, these findings demonstrate that TMT exposure potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol in males, but not females. However, females were more sensitive to the motor-suppressing effects of alcohol both before and after TMT exposure, an effect that was blunted in the TMT group following TMT exposure.

A subset of the rats were first tested for substitution with the GABAA agonist pentobarbital (5.0 mg/kg, i.p.) to determine whether TMT exposure affected sensitivity to GABAA activation. For discrimination scores (Fig. 2F), there was no effect of TMT, but there was a main effect of sex [F(1, 48)=6.90, p = 0.01], with females showing overall higher discrimination scores compared to males. There was no interaction between TMT and sex. Interestingly, pentobarbital produced substitution for the alcohol training dose in females for the CTRL and TMT group, but not in males in either group.

Locomotor rate (Fig. 2G) following pentobarbital administration showed no main effect of TMT, but a main effect of sex [F(1, 48)=29.67, p < 0.0001], with females showing higher locomotor rate, and a TMT x sex interaction effect [F(1, 48)=4.50, p = 0.04]. In males, locomotion was decreased in the TMT group compared to controls (p < 0.05), but there was no effect of TMT in females. Together, these data demonstrate that females were more sensitive to the alcohol-like stimulus effects of systemic GABAA agonism compared to males and TMT exposure did not impact this sensitivity in either males or females.

Experiment 2: no effect of TMT exposure or sex on alcohol metabolism (Fig. 3)

This follow up experiment was conducted in a separate group of rats to determine whether the potentiated sensitivity to alcohol following TMT in males (Fig. 2D) may be related to TMT-induced changes in alcohol levels at the time of testing (20 min post alcohol administration) which could directly influence discrimination behavior. As potentiation to the lower doses (0.5 and 1 g/kg) was observed, this experiment was conducted to determine whether there was a change after administration of 1 g/kg alcohol. There was no main effect of TMT or sex on alcohol metabolism. As expected, there was a significant main effect of timepoint on blood ethanol concentrations [Fig. 3; F(3, 60)=17.53, p < 0.001], with no interaction between TMT, timepoint, and sex. These findings suggest that the effects of TMT exposure on interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol was likely not due to variations in alcohol metabolism.

Experiment 3: TMT exposure sex-dependently altered gabra1 expression in the PrL (Fig. 4)

Gene expression of GABAA receptor subunits in the PrL and aIC was assessed two weeks after the TMT exposure (Fig. 4A). Tissue extraction location and depth for PrL and aIC are shown in Fig. 4B For Gabra1 in the PrL (Fig. 4C), there was a significant effect of sex [F(1, 28)=9.52, p = 0.005], no main effect of TMT, and a significant TMT × sex interaction [F(1, 28)=18.12, p = 0.0002]. TMT exposure decreased Gabra1 expression in males (p = 0.01) but increased Gabra1 in females (p = 0.01). No significant effects of TMT, sex, or interactions were observed for other GABAA subunits tested in the PrL (Fig. 4D–G). In the aIC, TMT and sex did not affect Gabra1 or Gabra4 (Fig. 4H, I). Some GABAA subunits showed significant TMT × sex interactions: Gabarb2 [F(1, 33)=3.97, p = 0.05], Gabrd [F(1, 33)=5.21, p = 0.03], and Gabrg2 [F(1, 31)=5.04, p = 0.03]. Post-hoc tests showed no statistically significant effects of TMT or sex for these subunits (Fig. 4J–L). These results demonstrate that TMT exposure sex-dependently alters Gabra1 expression in the PrL. This GABAergic adaptation could account for the potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to low-dose alcohol and the alcohol-like effects of GABAA receptor activation in the PrL. Additionally, these sex-dependent effects of TMT exposure may also explain the sex differences observed on interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol following TMT exposure.

Experiment 4: TMT exposure increased PrL c-Fos expression in males, which was not observed in rats pre-treated with alcohol (Fig. 5)

We next tested the effects of TMT exposure and a single alcohol administration (2.0 g/kg, i.g.) on c-Fos expression in the PrL and aIC (Fig. 5A). In the PrL (Fig. 5B), there was no main effect of TMT, alcohol, or sex. There was a significant TMT x sex interaction effect [F(1, 42)=11.39, p = 0.002] and a TMT x alcohol x sex interaction effect [F(1, 42)=6.24, p = 0.01]. In males, there was a main effect of TMT [F(1, 22)=12.70, p = 0.002], no main effect of alcohol, but a significant TMT x alcohol interaction [F(1, 22)=6.83, p = 0.02]. Post-hoc tests in males showed that there was a greater number of c-Fos positive cells in the TMT group compared to controls in the water-treated group (p = 0.0003); however, this effect of TMT was not observed in the alcohol-treated group. PrL c-Fos expression in females did not show an effect of TMT or alcohol pretreatment, nor an interaction effect.

For c-Fos expression in the aIC (Fig. 5C), there was no effect of TMT, alcohol, sex, and no interaction effect. Fig. 5D illustrates the areas of quantification for the PrL and aIC. Fig. 5E shows representative images (20X) of c-Fos in the PrL for male rats. Together, these results suggest that TMT exposure results in greater excitatory neuronal activity in the PrL. Interestingly, this change in activity may be driven by adaptations in the GABAA receptor (Fig. 5).

Experiment 5: alcohol produced locomotor and startle response effects in the control group, but not in the TMT group (Fig. 6)

Alcohol-naïve rats were exposed to TMT, underwent the 2-week incubation period, and were then tested on OFT and ASR following acute administration of alcohol (2 g/kg, i.g.) or water (Fig. 6A). For locomotion during the 20-min OFT (Fig. 6B), there was no main effect of TMT or alcohol, but there was a main effect of sex [F(1, 80)=22.54, p < 0.0001] and a significant sex x alcohol interaction [F(1, 80)=14.47, p = 0.0003]. In males, a main effect of alcohol was observed on total distance traveled [F(1, 39)=10.63, p = 0.002], with an overall decrease in locomotion. There was no main effect of TMT or interaction. Planned comparisons showed that alcohol decreased locomotion in the control group [t(18)=4.31, p = 0.0004] but not in the TMT group (p = 0.14). In females, there was a main effect of alcohol on total distance traveled [F(1, 41)=4.64, p = 0.04], with an overall increase in locomotion, but no effect of TMT exposure or interaction. Planned comparisons showed that alcohol increased locomotion in the control group [t(20)=2.08, p = 0.05], but not in the TMT group (p = 0.34).

For ASR (Fig. 6C), there was a main effect of alcohol [F(1, 77)=15.62, p = 0.0002], with suppression of startle response following alcohol, but no main effect of TMT, sex, and no significant interactions. Despite not seeing an interaction effect of TMT, we conducted two-way ANOVAs in males and females separately for consistency with other results and to investigate post-hoc effects of TMT. For startle response in males, alcohol induced an overall decrease in average peak startle response as supported by a significant main effect of alcohol [F(1, 40)=1.65, p = 0.002], and no effect of TMT exposure or interaction. Planned comparisons showed that alcohol decreased average peak startle response in the control group [t(19)=2.79, p = 0.01], but not in the TMT group, although there was a trend for a decrease [t(21)=1.93, p = 0.07]. For startle response in females, alcohol induced an overall decrease in average peak startle response as supported by a significant main effect of alcohol [F(1, 37)=5.35, p = 0.03], and no effect of TMT or interaction. Planned comparisons showed that alcohol decreased average peak startle response in the control group [t(18)=2.18, p = 0.04], but not in the TMT group (p = 0.34).

Discussion

Exposure to the predator odor stressor TMT potentiated the interoceptive effects of alcohol in male rats. Results from follow-up molecular and behavioral experiments suggest that the effect of TMT in males may potentially be driven through GABAA receptor adaptations in the PrL that may result in enhanced perception of the stimulatory effects of alcohol. In contrast, females did not demonstrate an effect of TMT exposure on interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol, which may be driven by differences in molecular and behavioral effects of TMT exposure in females relative to males. These findings contribute to an enhanced understanding of the relationship between traumatic stress and AUD.

Alcohol produces biphasic effects, starting with stimulation during the ascending limb of the blood alcohol concentration (BAC) curve, followed by sedation during the descending limb of the BAC curve [75,76]. Both stimulation and sedation are components of the interoceptive stimulus of alcohol [33,34]. GABAA receptor activation or NMDA receptor antagonism substitute for the interoceptive effects of alcohol, meaning that the interoceptive effects produced by these drugs are similar to that produced by alcohol, with the degree of substitution depending on the alcohol training dose. At lower alcohol doses (< 1.5 g/kg), GABAA receptor activation achieves full substitution, while NMDA receptor antagonism often results in partial substitution; at higher alcohol doses, this trend reverses with greater recruitment of NMDA receptors [32,33]. Lower alcohol doses tend to be more stimulating and higher alcohol doses tend to be more sedative [40,41,77], recruiting GABAA and NMDA receptor contributions, respectively. Here we found that male, but not female, rats showed potentiated sensitivity to the interoceptive effects of 0.5 and 1.0 g/kg alcohol (trained at 2.0 g/kg alcohol) two weeks after TMT exposure, indicating a potential GABAA receptor mechanism. Despite this, TMT exposure did not potentiate the alcohol-like effects of systemically-administered pentobarbital, but did result in reduced locomotion in males.

In follow-up experiments in alcohol-naïve rats, two weeks following TMT exposure, Gabra1 expression in the PrL was decreased in males, but increased in females in the TMT group compared to controls. TMT exposure also increased PrL c-Fos expression in males (TMT vs. Control following water i.g.), but not females, suggesting a general increase in neuronal activity in the males two weeks after TMT. However, this increase in c-Fos could also reflect differences in stress reactivity to the i.g. injection, which nevertheless reflects an important molecular adaptation (whether at baseline or inducible). Because the PrL is predominantly composed of excitatory, glutamatergic neurons [78], and stress is associated with increased glutamatergic neuronal activity in the PrL [52], we suspect the increase in c-Fos IR reflects increased activity of these excitatory neurons, which could be driven by the observed Gabra1 expression decrease in the PrL in males. Further, we found some preliminary evidence to suggest that PrL GABAA receptors may be involved in TMT-induced potentiation of the interoceptive effects of alcohol. That is, PrL-specific microinjection of muscimol (GABAA receptor agonist) resulted in higher discrimination scores in the TMT group compared to controls (Supplemental Figure S1D). However, note that this PrL-muscimol administration did not produce substitution for the alcohol training dose and along with the low sample size makes it difficult to draw any definitive conclusions. PrL-muscimol did produce enhanced locomotion in the TMT group compared to controls, which appears to be a more robust effect (Supplemental Fig. S1E). Thus, although we did not find direct evidence for PrL-GABAA receptors involvement in the potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol, the overall pattern of findings suggests potential involvement. Of clinical relevance, PTSD is associated with glutamatergic and GABAergic adaptations in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) [67,79,80], indicating that this model may capture a clinically important result found in PTSD and traumatic stress.

In alcohol-naïve rats, the locomotor and startle response effects of acute alcohol administration were not observed in the TMT-exposed groups. In males, this was reflective of a significant motor-suppressing and startle response-suppressing effect of 2 g/kg alcohol observed in the CTRL, but not the TMT group. These results, along with the known stimulatory effects of low-dose alcohol, enhanced PrL neuronal activity after TMT exposure, and potentiated locomotion following PrL-muscimol microinjection, suggest that male rats may experience enhanced stimulatory effects of alcohol following TMT exposure. This enhancement may contribute to the observed potentiation of the interoceptive effects at 0.5 and 1.0 g/kg alcohol (Fig. 2D). However, this hypothesis is contradicted by the observation that locomotor rate was not changed (Fig. 2E - Males). Therefore, it is possible that there is a perception in the enhancement of the stimulatory effects of alcohol, without a detectable change in locomotion. However, note that while PrL-muscimol did result in increased locomotion in the TMT group, a decrease in locomotion was observed following systemic pentobarbital in the males with no change in discrimination performance. This supports the hypothesis of increased stimulatory effects of alcohol being driven by GABAA receptor changes specifically in the PrL. The discrepancy between systemic pentobarbital vs. site-specific muscimol (both for discrimination score and locomotion effects) may also reflect differences in drug pharmacology (pentobarbital is an allosteric modulator and muscimol is an orthosteric agonist).

Because the stimulatory effects of alcohol are associated with its rewarding effects, a potentially perceived increase in the stimulatory effects of alcohol in males may contribute to increased alcohol consumption. However, our previous work using the same TMT exposure protocol did not find increases in operant alcohol self-administration in males following TMT exposure; increases were found in female rats [6]. This may be due to several factors including the alcohol consumption measure (binge-like intake or home cage drinking was not assessed) or the differences in alcohol history (the present drug discrimination-trained rats were exposed to considerably more alcohol than the previously reported operant self-administration study), which we also discuss in previous work [30]. Conversely, it is also possible that potentiated sensitivity to the effects of alcohol “protects” against increases in subsequent drinking in male rats because the effects of lower doses of alcohol are enhanced, enabling less consumption to achieve similar interoceptive effects.

While female rats did not show changes in interoceptive effects of alcohol, there were notable effects of TMT exposure in females. Females showed an increase in Gabra1 in the PrL following TMT exposure and potentially diminished sensitivity to the locomotor-enhancing and startle response-suppressing effects of alcohol. Female rats trained on the Pavlovian drug discrimination showed an alcohol dose-dependent decrease in locomotion in the control, but not the TMT group. However, control, alcohol-naïve rats showed increased locomotion following 2 g/kg alcohol that was not observed in the TMT group. This discrepancy in the effects of TMT exposure on locomotor activity may be due to differences in alcohol exposure history and/or the assessments (novel open field vs. a familiar operant chamber). As such, while female rats did not show potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol, they did show changes in response to alcohol and related neurobiological adaptations. The reason that females did not show potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol like males could be due to sex differences in the GABAA receptor [81,82], which may drive the effect of TMT in males. Females showed an opposing effect of TMT on Gabra1 expression in the PrL relative to males, indicating that females adapt to TMT exposure differently than males. Most importantly, females showed greater alcohol-like effects of systemic pentobarbital than males. Therefore, females were more sensitive to the GABAA-mediated interoceptive effects of alcohol, and as such may have been “protected” from the consequences of TMT exposure on increased interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol. This may also account for why alcohol-naïve female rats showed potentiated locomotion when male rats showed attenuated locomotion to the same alcohol dose on the OFT. However, it is important to note that females suffer from PTSD at greater rates than males [1], and female rats are particularly sensitive to the effects of TMT exposure on driving escalations in alcohol self-administration [6]. Therefore, this model or methodology may not capture the complex relationship between traumatic stress and interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol in female rats.

Females compared to males with AUD are more likely to drink in response to negative emotions and have co-occurring psychiatric comorbidities [83–85]. In individuals with PTSD and alcohol dependence, both men and women drank alcohol for coping motives, but only men showed greater drinking for reward enhancement motives [86]. As such, women may be more motivated to drink alcohol to relieve negative emotionality (“relief drinking”), whereas men may be more likely to drink for reward. This may also suggest that our previous work showing escalations in alcohol self-administration in female, but not male rats after the same TMT exposure protocol [6] may reflect drinking to relieve a negative state, rather than reward-related drinking. Therefore, this work may have identified a sex-specific, novel vulnerability factor by which traumatic stress affects vulnerability for AUD.

Together, these data show that TMT exposure enhanced interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol potentially through GABAA receptor adaptations in the PrL in male, but not female rats. Additional behavioral and molecular evidence suggested a potential for greater perceived stimulatory effects of alcohol in males. Because the stimulatory effects are associated with the rewarding properties of alcohol, and the sedative effects are associated with aversive properties [75], these adaptations may be one mechanism by which traumatic stress increases alcohol consumption and/or vulnerability to develop AUD, particularly in males. Because men may drink more for reward vs. relief relative to women [83–86], these findings may have identified a novel vulnerability factor by which males escalate drinking following traumatic stress. In contrast, females were resistant to the potentiated interoceptive sensitivity to alcohol that was observed in males and showed greater sensitivity to the GABAA receptor component (alcohol-like effects of pentobarbital) of alcohol relative to males. Additionally, there was a stark sex difference in the effect of TMT exposure on PrL expression of the GABAA α–1 subunit mRNA. These findings may provide insights into the high comorbidity between PTSD and AUD by elucidating how traumatic stress affects alcohol sensitivity.
