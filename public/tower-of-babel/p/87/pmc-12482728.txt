Deep brain stimulation for Meige syndrome
Authors: Stephen Jaffee, Syed Hussain, Mikayla Spott, Trent Shane Kite, Nestor Tomycz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Meige syndrome is a cranial dystonia which presents with blepharospasm and oromandibular dystonia, identified in patients with Parkinson’s disease, atypical Parkinsonism, and essential tremor. Treatment of Meige syndrome involves both pharmacological and surgical interventions including botulinum toxin injections, muscle relaxants, dopamine receptor antagonists, and anticholinergic drugs. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has also been used to treat these patients with medically refractory Meige syndrome with a high degree of efficacy. Herein, we present a case of medically refractory Meige syndrome treated with bilateral globus pallidus internus (GPi) DBS with improvement of dystonia.Case Description: This is a 74-year-old female diagnosed with blepharospasm 15 years ago, after she noted increased blinking frequency and difficulty keeping her eyelids open. She was initially managed with extraocular Botox injections, but her symptoms progressed and began to include shoulder shrugging, jaw clenching, and uncontrolled head turning. A diagnosis of Meige syndrome was made and the patient responded appropriately to pharmacological management with benzodiazepines, baclofen, and carbidopa-levodopa. The patient eventually developed severe dystonia secondary to her Meige syndrome diagnosis and her symptoms were now refractory to botulinum toxin injections and medications. The patient underwent DBS of the bilateral GPi. After 52 weeks of follow-up, significant improvements in involuntary blinking, squinting, photophobia, teeth clenching, and neck tightness were noted.Conclusion: DBS is a safe and effective means of treating dystonia related to Meige syndrome. Further studies are needed to elucidate optimal targeting for these patients and programming specifics for long-term outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Meige syndrome is a rare neurological movement disorder that presents with blepharospasm and oromandibular dystonia.[1] This condition has previously been identified in patients with Parkinson’s disease, atypical Parkinsonism, and essential tremor.[4] Meige syndrome may develop due to dysfunction of cholinergic and dopaminergic pathways, and animal models have displayed atypical interactions within the basal ganglia and the cerebellum, which may contribute to the condition.[2,5] Treatment of Meige syndrome involves both pharmacological and surgical interventions, including botulinum toxin injections, muscle relaxants, dopamine receptor antagonists, and anticholinergic drugs. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has also been used to treat patients with refractory Meige syndrome with a high degree of efficacy.[12]

The two targets that have been used for Meige syndrome are globus pallidus internus (GPi) stimulation and subthalamic nucleus (STN). Herein, we describe a patient with medically refractory Meige patient with medically refractory Meige syndrome who was treated with bilateral GPi DBS and found improvement of her dystonia.

CASE REPORT

The patient was a 74-year-old female with a medical history of anxiety who was diagnosed with blepharospasm 15 years ago and treated with extraocular Botulinum toxin injections. While this intervention reduced the frequency of her blepharospasm, the patient began to develop other debilitating symptoms, including uncontrollable shoulder shrugging, jaw clenching, and head turning. Based on the constellation of her symptoms, the patient was diagnosed with Meige syndrome. She was treated with extraocular Botox injections and pharmacological management with benzodiazepines, baclofen, and carbidopa-levodopa. Unfortunately, the patient’s condition continued to progress, and she developed severe cranial cervical dystonia refractory to medications and injections. The patient was then referred to the neurosurgery clinic, and she underwent DBS of the bilateral GPi [Figure 1]. The patient had an uncomplicated post-operative course and had significant improvement in her symptoms, including involuntary blinking, squinting, photophobia, teeth clenching, and neck tightness, at her 1-month follow-up appointment.

DISCUSSION

In this case, we demonstrate that DBS of the GPi is an effective treatment option for a patient with refractory Meige syndrome. This finding is in agreement with the current literature. DBS has been shown to be an effective treatment for Meige syndrome and multiple DBS targets have been explored. However, there is debate on which target for DBS is optimal.[2,4,5,9,11,12] The two debated targets are the STN and GPi, our paper forwards this discussion by providing additional evidence for GPi as a viable target for DBS. Additional we have emphasized our position on the need for a grading system for Meige syndrome to better define criteria for selecting either of these targets. This has received little discussion in previous literature. Wu et al. conducted a study comparing GPi and STN as targets for Meige syndrome demonstrating no clinically significant differences in BFMDRS-M scores.[10] Moreover, Liu et al. documented the same findings in their study of 42 patients with 21 patients in each group (GPi and STN DBS). They found no significant differences in outcomes or complications.[3] Wang et al.[8] conducted a meta-analysis regarding cases of refractory Meige syndrome patients undergoing GPi stimulation or STN stimulation. Patients showed significant improvement in Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) movement and disability scores at follow-up visit.[8]. Moreover, Xie et al. demonstrated the efficacy of STN for Meige syndrome as well, highlighting both GPi and STN as effective targets of DBS for dystonia associated with Meige syndrome.[11] Williams et al.[9] provided an overview regarding the differences between GPi and STN in terms of benefit profile. They remarked that the advantages of the STN target included a greater medication reduction, fewer battery changes, and a more favorable economic profile, whereas the advantages of GPi included more-robust dyskinesia suppression, easier programming, and greater flexibility in adjusting medications.[12] A review of STN versus GPi outcomes in Meige syndrome is appended in Table 2. Furthermore, while there have been considerable efforts taken to compare the efficacy of GPi versus STN stimulation for Parkinson’s disease in trials such as COMPARE, Veterans affair (VA) Cooperative Study, and the NSTAPS trial, there have been no significant trials for comparison of targets in Meige syndrome, likely related to lower prevalence of patient population.[12]

The patient’s initial settings were a monopolar configuration with 3.1 V, 130 Hz, and 120 μs on the left electrode and 3.0 V, 130 Hz, and 130 μs on the right. The maximal settings throughout her follow-up period were 8.1 V, 130 Hz, and 260 μs on the left and 8.0 V, 130 Hz, and 260 μs on the right. Our patients’ final program settings as visualized in Table 1 reflect similar frequencies but differ significantly in output. This highlights that programming for this condition is variable in nature; furthermore, this showcases the benefits of titratable programming.

A notable consideration is the lack of formalized scoring criteria for Meige syndrome specifically like that of the UPDRS-III to monitor for patient’s symptoms resolution or reduction after DBS. The BFMDRS has been used as biomarker for the severity of dystonia in the STN versus GPi literature but is scored for dystonia throughout the body, whereas Meige syndrome may benefit from a scale solely focused on facial dystonia for sensitivity of therapeutic effect. As DBS becomes a more widely utilized therapy for the treatment of Meige syndrome, such a criteria may be beneficial to develop generalizable programming settings and superiority of target.

CONCLUSION

DBS of the GPi is a safe and effective means of treating dystonia related to Meige syndrome. There have been many studies comparing the efficacy of STN versus GPi as targets for stimulation, demonstrating apparent equivocal outcomes in symptom treatment. Further studies are needed to elucidate optimal targeting for these patients and programming specifics for long term outcomes. Future research should focus on the pathophysiology of Meige syndrome, specifically as it relates to dopaminergic pathways, due to the syndrome’s similarity to Parkinson’s disease.
