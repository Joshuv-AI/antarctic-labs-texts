Preoperative physical exercise training for people scheduled for major abdominal surgery
Authors: Luuk JF Siemes, Hans Timmerman, Linda AG Heusden-Scholtalbers, Sandra Ridder, Rogier Donders, Han J Bonenkamp, Harry Goor, Maria Nijhuis-van der Sanden, J. Bart Staal, supported by The Cochrane Hepato-Biliary Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To assess the benefits and harms of physical prehabilitation (i.e. exercise‐based interventions delivered before surgery) alone or as part of a multimodal programme compared with no exercise‐based intervention, usual care, or a different intervention in people undergoing major abdominal surgery.

Background

This protocol updates a previous Cochrane protocol published in 2012, for which the full review was not completed [1]. The protocol has therefore been updated to reflect current review processes.

Abdominal surgery is a stressful event, with various physical and psychological consequences. Major surgery affects the cardiovascular and respiratory systems and is associated with a high risk of postoperative complications and postoperative decline in functional capacity [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Moreover, people experience decreased health, including fatigue and functional problems with quality of life and mental concentration [7].

Anaesthesia administration and surgical procedures significantly disrupt homeostasis (state of balance), leading to compromised respiratory function due to incisions and triggering an inflammatory response [6, 8]. Postoperative functional decline is further exacerbated by deconditioning associated with prolonged bed rest and reduced mobility [9]. Notably, not only does a decrease in postoperative physical activity negatively impact outcomes, but lower levels of preoperative exercise capacity may also adversely influence surgical outcomes [7, 10].

Preoperative physical exercise has been shown to reduce postoperative complications, improve functional recovery, and shorten hospital stay in patients undergoing major abdominal surgery. Multiple systematic reviews and meta‑analyses have demonstrated that structured exercise programmes significantly lower overall morbidity and pulmonary complications, indicating that physical conditioning is a modifiable risk factor for surgical outcomes [11, 12, 13]. Poor baseline physical activity levels have been shown to increase the risk of complications after major abdominal surgery and prolong recovery [14]. Gillis and colleagues reported that low preoperative exercise capacity results in limited cardiopulmonary reserve to cope with postoperative physiological stress [10]. Consequently, enhancing functional capacity by increasing physical activity before surgery may improve patients’ ability to withstand the stressors of surgery and postoperative inactivity [2, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

'Prehabilitation' is a proactive approach that introduces components of rehabilitation to patients before they undergo intensive medical interventions. It aims to enhance patients’ health, physical functioning, and tolerance of a planned intervention, such as major abdominal surgery. This approach is primarily used in the context of elective surgery, with common patient populations including individuals with cancer and those awaiting bariatric surgery (metabolic and weight loss surgery) [20, 21]. In addition, prehabilitation is intended to enhance recovery after a surgical intervention [22, 23]. This may result in better postoperative outcomes like decreased postoperative pulmonary complications, faster recovery, shortened hospital stay, and a shorter period of dependent functioning before returning to initial functioning levels [2, 16, 18, 19, 24]. Furthermore, prehabilitation may positively influence the psychological consequences of the surgery, as it is likely that active engagement of the individual in the preparation process alleviates some of the emotional distress surrounding the anticipation of surgery and the recovery process [25].

Description of the condition

Every year, worldwide, many people with various diagnoses undergo elective abdominal surgery. Of all individuals undergoing abdominal surgery, between 30% and 40% are expected to experience postoperative complications [8, 26, 27]. Types of major abdominal surgeries that are prone to the risk of developing postoperative complications are hepato‐pancreato‐biliary surgery, upper gastrointestinal surgery, colorectal surgery, abdominal gynaecological surgery, open abdominal aortic surgery, and abdominal transplant surgery. Even without complications, major surgery is associated with a 20% to 40% reduction in physiologic and functional capacity when measured by energy expenditure, endurance time, workload, and heart rate during maximum exercise [8, 28]. Participants experience decreased health, reduced strength, fatigue, functional problems with quality of life, raised anxiety, limitations in social life and reduced ability to concentrate [10, 29, 30]. Furthermore, many participants undergoing major abdominal surgery have cancer or chronic disease, which is a prominent additional factor for a postoperative decline in physical activity and for emotional distress [31].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

The benefits of regular exercise and physical conditioning are widely known and result in positive effects after surgery [15, 32]. Since the 1990s, the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) programme has led to several publications on multimodal approaches to resolve issues that delay recovery and cause complications after surgery [33]. Recommendations for the preoperative and postoperative periods are widely published; yet, more attention has been given to the preoperative phase, of which exercise training is a main component [27]. Exercise mainly focuses on improving the cardiovascular and musculoskeletal systems as these are central to helping an individual to endure the demands of surgery [10]. Several studies have shown positive effects of aerobic exercise, resistance training, or both as a preoperative strategy for postoperative outcomes [11, 34, 35, 36, 37].

There may also be some negative aspects related to the outcome of prehabilitation in patients undergoing major abdominal surgery, particularly cancer surgery. First, participation in prehabilitation may lead to a delay in surgery if the intervention period is prolonged due to the prehabilitation programme. However, these programmes are generally designed to fit within established treatment timelines. Secondly, many studies are currently non‐randomised and may be subject to selection bias, whereby fitter and more motivated individuals are preferentially included. However, this does not necessarily lead to an overestimation of effectiveness, as training benefits are typically greatest in individuals with a low baseline fitness level, while gains in already fit individuals tend to be smaller given their proximity to an optimal level. Thirdly, the duration and intensity of the offered prehabilitation interventions may be insufficient to achieve meaningful physiological adaptations before surgery, particularly when the preoperative time window is short, while the considerable variation in intervention design across studies and the absence of a clearly defined endpoint mean that the optimal duration has not yet been definitively established [38, 39, 40].

The time available for exercise before elective abdominal surgery is often limited; therefore, the length of the interventions must be considered. Prehabilitation programmes ranging from seven days to six weeks before surgery were conducted [41]. In the review by Carli and colleagues, the suggested duration of a prehabilitation programme was a minimum of four weeks, and preferably longer, up to three months [15]. While a longer duration of a preoperative intervention might be preferable, even studies with a short duration of prehabilitation (< 2 weeks) have shown positive results on postoperative outcomes [8, 24, 42].

The initial level of training and the progression of intensity, duration, frequency, and training mode can vary for each individual, based on their initial physical capacity and the degree to which they, individually, respond to increases in physical activity [15]. In the absence of a patient‐centred approach, adherence to the prehabilitation programme might be compromised, potentially resulting in a suboptimal response to the prescribed exercise [7].

Surgery has demands, and a person scheduled for major abdominal surgery is more likely to suffer complications when the cardiovascular system is unable to adapt to the stress of surgery [17]. Exercise training can improve cardiovascular function in several ways; a more physically active and fitter individual will have an improved cardiac response to the surgery [17]. Improving physical fitness before surgery, as quantified by the VO2 max (maximal oxygen uptake), is likely to be beneficial for the participant before surgery, as it increases the cardiopulmonary reserve and thereby improves the margin of safety above the minimum threshold required for performing daily activities [10, 15]. In addition to the cardiovascular system, the musculoskeletal system influences the achievement and maintenance of functional ability. Surgery results in a loss of muscle strength and mass, and reduces daily functional ability and independence [43]. Exercise can counteract atrophy and loss of strength, and it is recommended to perform resistance exercises in addition to aerobic exercise [43]. After major abdominal and thoracic surgery, inspiratory muscle weakness can lead to respiratory failure and pneumonia [8]. Training inspiratory muscles preoperatively may help to decrease postoperative pulmonary complications [24, 42, 44, 45].

Participants with a chronic, benign gastrointestinal disease or cancer generally have poor preoperative physical performance [31]. Successful prehabilitation before surgery may even be more effective regarding early and long‐term outcomes after major abdominal surgery than postoperative interventions. Success is defined as increased physical functioning, improved quality of life, reduced postoperative pain and postsurgical complications, increased functional capacity, physiological and psychological benefits, and decreased mortality [15, 46].

Why it is important to do this review

Since the mid 2000s, a substantial body of research has demonstrated promising outcomes regarding the efficacy of (physical) prehabilitation, based on exercise interventions, in improving postoperative results for people undergoing major abdominal surgery [5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 27, 41, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51].

The purpose of the previously published systematic reviews was to describe the effectiveness of prehabilitation on postoperative outcomes [5, 8, 12, 27, 41, 47, 48]. However, there were small differences in focus. For example, Zhang and colleagues focused on the effectiveness of prehabilitation and preoperative lifestyle interventions [48], and Jain and colleagues also incorporated the combined effect of nutrition and exercise [5].

This Cochrane review aims to provide an overview of the effectiveness of prehabilitation for people scheduled for major abdominal surgery. Previous reviews concluded that prehabilitation increased perioperative functional capacity and likely decreased the incidence of postoperative adverse events, reduced morbidity, pneumonia, and length of hospital stay [5, 8, 12, 27, 41, 47, 48]. In addition, it stated that prehabilitation programmes in people undergoing major abdominal surgery were heterogeneous in origin and differed by the nature of the intervention. There is also a difference in the length of application (days to weeks), and most trials aimed to analyse postoperative complications and the short‐term effects of prehabilitation. Therefore, this systematic review will also assess the medium‐term and long‐term effectiveness of prehabilitation.

Based on a previous Cochrane systematic review, prehabilitation may improve functional capacity, but no clear effect has been shown on postoperative complications [52]. Three recently published systematic reviews by Ambulkar and colleagues [51], Bai and colleagues [49], and Maroto‐Izquierdo and colleagues [50], have all included trials identified through searches conducted in June 2024, December 2023, and March 2025. The aims of these reviews are only partially aligned with our objectives. Over 50 potentially relevant new randomised clinical trials on preoperative exercise have been published in the last 12 months. Therefore, it is relevant to assess all available evidence, using the rigorous Cochrane systematic review methodology.

Objectives

To assess the benefits and harms of physical prehabilitation (i.e. exercise‐based interventions delivered before surgery) alone or as part of a multimodal programme compared with no exercise‐based intervention, usual care, or a different intervention in people undergoing major abdominal surgery.

Methods

We will follow the Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting the review [53] and PRISMA 2020 for the reporting [54, 55].

This protocol is a major revision from the version first published in 2012 [1]. For differences between protocol versions, see Supplementary material 2.

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include randomised clinical trials of any design, including parallel‐group, cross‐over, and cluster‐randomised trials, that assess the benefits and harms of exercise‐based interventions in the preoperative phase for adults scheduled for major abdominal surgery compared with no intervention, usual care, or an intervention other than the experimental intervention. If we identify cross‐over randomised clinical trials, we will use data from the first period only (i.e. before the cross‐over) to avoid residual effects from the cross‐over [56]. We will include trials irrespective of trial setting, year, form, or publication language, or planned or reported outcomes.

We will exclude quasi‐randomised clinical trials (i.e. trials without truly random methods of allocation) and other observational studies and review articles.

Types of participants

We will include all adult participants (≥ 18 years) of both sexes, undergoing major abdominal surgery with conditions (i.e. cancer) resulting in elective surgery. Eligible procedures defined as major abdominal surgery include hepato‐pancreato‐biliary surgery, upper gastrointestinal surgery, colorectal surgery, abdominal gynaecological surgery, open abdominal aortic surgery, and abdominal transplant surgery. Participants may be recruited in inpatient, outpatient, or home‐based settings.

We will exclude participants undergoing urological or vaginal gynaecological procedures.

If trials include a mixed population (i.e. eligible and ineligible participants), and separate data cannot be extracted, we will contact the trial authors to request data specific to the relevant subgroup of interest. If this is not possible, we will exclude the trial unless the relevant subset represents at least 90% of participants.

Types of interventions

We will include trials that evaluate the effects of exercise interventions performed during the preoperative period. Exercise is a subcategory of physical activity that is planned, structured, repetitive, and purposeful, aimed at improving or maintaining one or more components of physical fitness [57]. Physical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure. Physical activity encompasses exercise as well as other activities that involve bodily movement and are done as part of playing, working, active transportation, household chores, and recreational activities [58].

Experimental interventions

The exercise‐based interventions may comprise cardiovascular (aerobic) training, strength (resistance) training, breathing exercises (e.g. inspiratory muscle training), or a combination of these. The exercise intervention may be individualised (tailored to the participant's baseline fitness, needs, or surgical risk) or generic; individual‐ or group‐based; home‐based, hybrid, digital, or facility‐based; and supervised or unsupervised. The exercise intervention may be delivered alone or as part of a multimodal programme. We will place no restrictions on duration, frequency, or intensity. However, we will analyse differences in content, intensity, and duration descriptively and, where possible, in subgroup analysis.

Control interventions

The control group may receive no exercise‐based intervention, usual care, or an intervention other than the experimental one. Usual care refers to the standard preoperative management as routinely provided within each clinical setting and may vary across institutions.

Co‐interventions

Co‐interventions will be allowed if they are applied equally across relevant intervention groups for our review.

Outcome measures

The Core Outcome Measures from Perioperative and Anaesthetic Care (COMPAC) represent a guideline for reporting outcome measures within the field of anaesthesia and perioperative medicine research. The outcome measures that we will assess in our review are derived, and adapted to our review, from the COMPAC core outcome set: (1) perioperative complications, (2) short‐term recovery after surgery, (3) longer‐term recovery after surgery, and (4) mortality/survival. The outcome domains comprise a combination of clinical outcomes and patient‐centred outcome measures [59]. For further details on the domains, see Critical outcomes and Important outcomes.

When multiple measures for the same outcome domain are reported within a study, we will select one outcome measure per domain and time point for meta‐analysis according to a predefined hierarchy, independent of the results. We will base selection on the following hierarchy.

Search methods for identification of studies

To minimise bias in our search results, we will follow the guidance in Chapter 4 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [65], and in PRISMA‐S Checklist [66], to plan and describe the search process for the review. An information specialist (Sandra de Ridder (SdR)) from the HAN University of Applied Sciences has developed and peer‐reviewed the search strategies using the PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies) checklist, in close collaboration with two review authors (Luuk Siemes (LS) and Hans Timmerman (HT)). SdR will run the searches, with no restrictions on language, publication year, or publication status. When data or study details are unclear, we will contact the trial authors for clarification. Where necessary, we will arrange translations of papers.

Each search will use a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g. MeSH, Emtree terms) and free‐text keywords. To identify eligible randomised clinical trials, we will incorporate database‐specific study design search blocks based on terms related to randomisation, controlled trials, and trial methodology. We will not apply a single published randomised controlled trial filter across all databases because validated filters are not available or appropriate for all included databases. Instead, the review team and information specialist will tailor study design blocks to each database. The full electronic search strategies are presented in Supplementary material 1.

We will rerun and update the searches within six months before publication to ensure that we include all eligible trials. We will fully incorporate any trials that we identify during the updated search into the systematic review. We will also search trials registry databases through the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP, trialsearch.who.int) and ClinicalTrials.gov (clinicaltrials.gov) for ongoing or unpublished trials to identify potential publication bias or selective reporting. We will provide the date of all electronic searches at the review stage.

Searching other resources

To reduce the risk of missing relevant trials, we will scan the reference lists of identified publications for additional trials. In addition, we will search PubMed and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR) to retrieve existing systematic reviews relevant to this review, and we will use their reference list for identifying additional trials. We will contact trial authors and leaders in the field to ask if they are aware of any relevant, but unpublished, studies. We will search OpenGrey (archived, opengrey.eu) for grey literature (research reports, doctoral dissertations and theses, conference papers, meeting presentations, official publications, and other types of grey literature.

We will use PubMed/MEDLINE's related articles search tool for all included trials. We will search for and examine any relevant retraction statements (through the Retraction Watch Database (retractionwatch.com/retraction‐watch‐database‐user‐guide/)) and errata of all potentially eligible studies, as errata can reveal important limitations or even fatal flaws in included studies [67].

We will use items from the PRISMA‐S checklist relevant to our review to ensure that we report and document our searches as advised (PRISMA‐S Checklist) [66]. We will provide the search terms used to search for 'Other resources' as well as the exact date of these searches at the review stage.

Data collection and analysis

We will perform this review in accordance with the instructions in the Cochrane Handbook for data collection and analysis [68]. Following the electronic search, we will download titles and abstracts. We will remove duplicates, and we will upload results to Rayyan for screening and data management [69].

Selection of studies

Two review authors (LS, HT) will independently screen all titles and abstracts resulting from the search following the standard approach from the Cochrane Handbook [65]. They will exclude all abstracts that do not fulfil the inclusion criteria. We will retrieve full‐text articles from all relevant abstracts for more detailed evaluation. The two review authors will review the retrieved full‐text articles independently and determine the eligibility of the trials; they will record reasons for exclusion. We will include trials regardless of the reported outcomes. We will identify and collate multiple reports of the same trial so that each trial, rather than each record, is the unit of interest in the review. In the case of non‐English language publications, we will use Google Translate (translate.google.com). We will ensure the accuracy of the translation by contacting someone with a command of that language. We will report and document our searches using items from the PRISMA‐S checklist, as advised in the checklist [66]. Any discrepancies that may arise between the two review authors will be resolved through discussion. Should this not be possible, a third review author (Bart Staal (BS)) will act as an arbitrator. We will record and present the selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram [54, 55].

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Two review authors will independently assess the risk of bias of each included trial result using the Cochrane Risk of bias tool 2 (RoB 2) for randomised trials [71]. We will follow the recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook [72]. We will assess the effect of the assignment on the intervention, which requires the use of the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) data. ITT includes all randomised participants, regardless of the interventions they received.

We will use the RoB 2 Excel tool, which has a built‐in algorithm for risk of bias judgements. It is available at www.riskofbias.info. Measures of treatment effect

We will perform statistical analyses according to the recommendations in Chapters 6 [74], and 10 [75], of the Cochrane Handbook. We will use Cochrane's review management software, RevMan [70].

Continuous variables (such as the preoperative functional status, postoperative functional status, short‐, medium‐, and long‐term recovery, health‐related quality of life, psychological or mental health) are expressed either as mean difference (MD) or as standardised mean difference (SMD), with 95% confidence intervals (CI). We will analyse the data using the SMD when trials assess the same outcome but use a variety of measurement instruments. Ordinal data (health‐related questionnaires such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, or for psychological or mental health) will be summarised as dichotomous variables when the number of categories is small, and a clear distinction can be made between categories that can be interpreted as a success versus a failure. If the number of categories is large, we will express the data as an MD or SMD [74].

We will meta‐analyse dichotomous data, such as all‐cause mortality or proportion of people experiencing one or more postoperative complications using risk ratios (RRs) with the 95% CI. We do not expect time‐to‐event data unless mortality is reported as survival [76]. If survival data are reported at a fixed time, we will convert survival into mortality. Otherwise, we will either convert the odds ratio (OR) into relative risk where possible, or we will analyse separately the data reported with hazard ratio (HR) using log(HR) and standard errors, and using the generic inverse variance in RevMan [75].

Unit of analysis issues

The unit of analysis, for trials with a parallel group design, will be the participant as randomised to the intervention groups of a trial.

We will use methods recommended in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook [74].

If we identify multi‐arm randomised clinical trials with more than two intervention groups, we will treat them with care to avoid double‐counting, as advised by the Cochrane Handbook [74]. If some groups in a multi‐arm trial are not relevant for our review or cannot be combined, then we will select for analysis only the relevant groups. We will divide the control group into the number of experimental groups when used in the same comparison.

For trials that report the number of complications in a trial group, we will use the number of participants with one or more complications and not the overall number of complications to avoid unit‐of‐analysis errors per group. We will analyse minor and major complications separately as defined previously. If trials do not report how many participants had complications, we will describe this information narratively. We will present a table with each type of complication separately. We will note all treatment groups in the 'Characteristics of included studies' tables, even if they are not used in the review.

For cross‐over randomised clinical trials, we will use data from the first period.

If we identify any cluster‐randomised trials, we will treat the cluster (i.e. families, schools, hospitals, etc.) as the unit of analysis [77]. If the baseline characteristics are imbalanced, then we will discuss this, and we will refer to it when assessing the certainty of evidence with GRADE. We will consider combining data from cluster‐randomised trials with data from the individually randomised trials in a meta‐analysis, provided that the cluster‐effect estimate takes into account the potential clustering. If clustering has not been appropriately accounted for in the original analyses, we will adjust the data using an estimate of the intra‐cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) and calculate the effective sample size using the design effect, in accordance with guidance in the Cochrane Handbook [77]. Otherwise, we will analyse the cluster‐randomised trials separately to avoid 'unit of analysis error'. We will consider the generic inverse‐variance approach to analyse the effect estimates and their standard errors (SEs) from correct analyses of cluster‐randomised trials as stated in the Cochrane Handbook [77].

Dealing with missing data

We will follow the recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook to deal with missing data [78]. We will conduct the analyses, as far as possible, based on the ITT principle, including all participants as randomised, regardless of whether they completed the intervention according to the study protocol. We will analyse together trials with ITT data and trials with available data if ITT data are not available. The available‐case analysis includes only participants with outcome data reported at follow‐up and assumes that data are missing at random.

We will contact the primary author of the trial to request missing data. We will record the number of dropouts, losses to follow‐up and withdrawals to appraise the missing data. If standard deviations (SD) of continuous data are not reported, we will calculate them from the study statistic (95% confidence intervals (CI), standard errors (SE) or P values [74], using the RevMan calculator [70]. To explore the potential impact of missing data, we will conduct sensitivity analyses using 'best‐case' and 'worst‐case' scenarios for the critical outcomes only (See Sensitivity analysis).

Reporting bias assessment

We will follow the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook to study investigation bias [72]. If there are at least 10 eligible trials in an outcome analysis, we will use a funnel plot to investigate publication bias visually.

Synthesis methods

We will conduct a meta‐analysis when the trials are sufficiently similar, that is, in terms of trial participants (P), interventions (I), comparators (C), outcomes (O) (i.e. PICOs, determined by consensus) [75]. We will not limit our primary analysis to trials at an overall low risk of bias. If a meta‐analysis is not possible, we will follow the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) guidelines to summarise and compare characteristics and main findings of the included trials [79].

For dichotomous outcomes, we will use the risk ratio with 95% confidence intervals. For continuous outcomes, we will use mean difference or standardised mean difference (Hedges g) when trials use different instruments. For ordinal outcomes, we will prespecify whether to dichotomise at clinically meaningful thresholds or treat scales as continuous (with consistent direction of benefit). If both change‐from‐baseline and final‐value data are reported, we will combine them using the Cochrane‐recommended formulae and impute correlation coefficients when necessary [75].

Where reported, we will extract the reported intensity of the interventions in terms of, amongst others, heart rate, the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (Borg RPE), or metabolic equivalents (METs). The Borg RPE is a subjective scale reflecting how hard exercise feels [80]. The MET measures the body energy used during physical activity: the higher the MET values, the more intense the activity. When possible, we will classify the exercise interventions as light (0 to 3 MET), moderate (3 to 6 MET), vigorous (6 to 9 MET), or high (> 9 MET) [81].

We anticipate between‐study heterogeneity; therefore, we will use a random‐effects meta‐analysis and a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to estimate between‐trial variance (Tau²). We will use the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) method to calculate a CI for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three trials, and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero. In other situations (i.e. when only two trials are analysed, or where the estimate of heterogeneity is equal to zero), we will use the Wald‐type method. We will also present prediction intervals and 95% CI for random‐effects meta‐analyses [75].

We will assess continuous outcomes for skewness of data (not for change‐from‐baseline measures) by visual inspection and descriptive statistics. If strong evidence for skewness is observed, we will apply appropriate data transformations (e.g. long‐transformation) or report medians and interquartile ranges narratively. We will use RevMan to perform our meta‐analyses [70].

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

We will explore and describe clinical, methodological, and statistical heterogeneity amongst the included trials [75]. We will assess clinical and methodological heterogeneity by comparing the characteristics of the trials, including design features, participants, interventions, comparators, outcomes, treatment duration, and follow‐up periods. We will create a descriptive summary table for clinical and methodological characteristics [75].

We will assess statistical heterogeneity by visual inspection of forest plots, considering the direction and magnitude of effects, and the degree of overlap between the confidence intervals. We will evaluate statistical heterogeneity using the Chi2 test, using P < 0.10 as a cutoff point for statistical heterogeneity and the I² statistic, which describes the proportion of total variation due to heterogeneity rather than chance [82].

I2 statistic values are interpreted as follows:

0% to 40%: might not be important;30% to 60%: may represent moderate heterogeneity;50% to 90%: may represent substantial heterogeneity;75% to 100%: considerable heterogeneity [75].

Sensitivity analysis

We will conduct sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of the pooled estimates and the influence of methodological decisions on the overall results. To assess the potential impact of missing data on the robustness of our findings, we will perform best–worst and worst–best case sensitivity analyses [75]. In the best–worst case scenario, we will assume that all participants with missing data in the intervention group have experienced a favourable outcome, and all with missing data in the control group an unfavourable outcome. We will apply the opposite assumptions for the worst–best case scenario. We will compare these results with the main analysis to judge the potential influence of missing outcome data.

In randomised clinical trials, continuous outcomes can be analysed as changes from baseline or as follow‑up (outcome) scores. Change scores may increase precision when baseline values strongly predict outcomes, but they require complete baseline data and can amplify measurement errors. Endpoint analyses, especially using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusting for baseline, are usually more efficient and less biased when randomisation is successful, and they handle missing baseline data more robustly. Simulation and empirical work consistently show ANCOVA on follow‑up as superior or at least equivalent to change‑score analysis; unadjusted change scores are rarely preferred. Choice should consider baseline imbalance, missing data patterns, and clinical interpretability [83, 84, 85]. We will compare the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of the pooled effect estimates across sensitivity scenarios. We will highlight any substantial differences that we observe and discuss them as an indicator of the limited robustness of the evidence [75].

In addition, we will perform Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) to assess the reliability and conclusiveness of cumulative evidence [86, 87]; see Supplementary material 3.

For the assessment of imprecision, we will use the latest guidance based on a minimally contextualised, CI‐based approach [91, 92]. We will downgrade when CIs cross thresholds of clinical importance. We will downgrade one level when the CI includes either important benefit and unimportant harm, or vice versa; by two levels when the CI interval includes both important benefit and important harm, and may downgrade three levels when the CI is extremely wide and would lead to distinctly different clinical decisions. When the CI does not cross the threshold of clinical importance, we will calculate the relative risk CI ratio or the odds ratio CI ratio. If the relative risk CI ratio is 3 or higher, or the odds ratio CI ratio is 2.5 or higher, we will downgrade two levels for imprecision. If the CI ratios are less than the given values, we will calculate the optimal information size (OIS), and we will downgrade by one level if the OIS is not reached. Thresholds for clinical importance will be informed by clinical consensus of the review team and supported by relevant literature. For relative measures, we will consider 0.25 as a trivial effect threshold [91, 92].

Equity considerations

We will attempt to include a health equity analysis where possible. Health equity is defined as follows: "Health inequalities are differences in health outcomes between individuals or groups. While some differences may be expected due to biological variations, systematic, socially produced, and avoidable differences are considered unjust. These unjust differences are often referred to as health inequities" 93]. The health equities can be defined using the PROGRESS‐Plus framework: PROGRESS stands for Place of residence, Race/ethnicity/culture/language, Occupation, Gender/sex, Religion, Education, Socioeconomic status, and Social capital. Plus refers to additional characteristics that are related to social disadvantage [94].

We will assess differences between men and women if data are available. We will take health equities into account within other topics such as place of residence, race or ethnicity, occupation, religion, education, socioeconomic status, and social capital. [94].
