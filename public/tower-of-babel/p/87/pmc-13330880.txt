Understanding the wider determinants of child road fatalities in England and opportunities for prevention: protocol for a mixed-methods study of the national child mortality database
Authors: Sarah E O’Toole, Emma L Bird, Erdem Dikici, Pallavi Govind, Sylvia Stoianova, David Odd, Karen Luyt, Elizabeth Orton, Julie Mytton, Nicola Christie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroduction In Great Britain, an estimated 4% of road deaths occur among children under 16 years-old. Previous research has often focused on individual-level risk factors, with less attention to the broader social, environmental and system-level determinants shaping child road safety. Emerging systematic reviews highlight the need for integrated approaches that consider inequalities, built environment characteristics and transport systems in understanding child injury risk. The establishment of the National Child Mortality Database (NCMD) in 2019 provides a unique opportunity to comprehensively analyse child road fatalities in England. This study adopts a systems-based approach, using Dahlgren and Whitehead’s (1991) Social Determinants of Health model to explore the interplay of modifiable risk factors and inform evidence-based interventions.Methods and analysisA sequential mixed-methods design will analyse all child road traffic fatalities (0–17 years) recorded in the NCMD between 2019 and 2024 (estimated n ≈ 262). A qualitative template analysis of child death review narratives will provide deeper insight into the systemic conditions contributing to child road deaths. Context-Mechanism-Outcome models will be developed to explore how different risk factors interact to increase vulnerability. Background

In Great Britain, 64 children (aged 0–16 years) were killed on the roads in 2024, with a further 3,033 sustaining serious injuries.1 The sex disparity in child road injury remains, with 65% (n=2022/3097) of children who experience fatal and serious road injuries being male. Among under 18 year-olds, pedestrians made up 46% (n=1435/3097) of fatal and serious road injuries, while car occupants accounted for just under a quarter (24%, n=732/3097).

Child road deaths are preventable through evidence-based policy and systemic action.2 Child road deaths are shaped not only by individual and behavioural factors but also by upstream determinants, including environmental and urban design, vehicle safety standards, speed management and national road safety policy and legislation. These structural influences are central to the ‘safe system’ approach, which emphasises that road systems should be designed to anticipate human error and minimise the risk of death or serious injury. While existing research has examined individual, family and environmental risk factors, many studies have overlooked key social determinants, such as ethnicity, and the ways multiple factors intersect to heighten risk.3 Moreover, the dominant focus on child-specific behaviours rather than modifiable environmental and social conditions has led to critical gaps in understanding which children are most vulnerable and under what circumstances. Without addressing these structural inequities, current interventions may continue to fall short in protecting those most at risk.

Disparities in child road traffic injury risk

Children from disadvantaged backgrounds face the highest risk of road traffic injury (RTI). In Great Britain in 2023, 27 children (38%) killed on the roads lived in the most deprived areas, compared with six (8%) in the least deprived areas.1 This stark disparity is unjust. Children in deprived areas are more likely to encounter hazardous road environments, including higher traffic volumes, fewer safe crossing points and limited access to traffic-calming measures.3

While global evidence consistently shows sex differences in road injury risk, these disparities remain underexplored in the UK. Existing UK research suggests males face a greater risk than females,4 5 though risk factors may vary by sex. For instance, stressful life events have been linked to increased road injury involvement among 16-year-old girls but not boys.6

Developmental factors, including special educational needs (SEN), further compound vulnerability. Around 18% of children in the UK have SEN,7 and the cognitive, physical and behavioural challenges associated with these conditions increase their likelihood of being killed or seriously injured in road traffic incidents.8 The Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children found that children aged 13–14 with learning disabilities were at the highest risk as pedestrians and cyclists.9 Additionally, the higher prevalence of SEN among boys7 may partially explain sex disparities in RTI risk.

Parenting behaviours also play a crucial role in shaping children’s road safety practices. Growing parental concerns over road dangers have contributed to increased car dependency for school transport, exacerbating traffic congestion, reducing pedestrian safety and discouraging active travel.10 These trends have wider consequences for children’s physical and mental health, limiting opportunities for independence, physical activity and social interaction.11,13

Overemphasis on child behaviour in road safety research

Research on child road safety has traditionally focused on children’s behaviour rather than the environments that put them at risk. Much of the existing research frames child road safety as a matter of individual responsibility, reinforcing the idea that children must be trained to adapt to road environments rather than questioning whether those environments are designed with their safety in mind. Young people themselves hold mixed views on road safety responsibility—some see it as their own duty, while others place responsibility on parents or drivers.14

Despite widespread recognition of a systems approach in road safety (emphasising safer roads, safer vehicles and safer speeds (https://www.pacts.org.uk/safe-system/#:~:text=InaSafeSystemapproach,eitherdeathorseriousinjury), there remains a disproportionate focus on educating children rather than addressing the hazardous conditions they face. The Global Plan for Road Safety has emphasised the need to move away from a ‘business as usual’ approach to road safety for children and take a more systems-based approach.15

Global road safety reports consistently highlight the need for safer infrastructure, particularly on routes to school, to protect children.16 Indeed, the National Child Mortality Database (NCMD) trauma report found that in 71% (n=80/113) of child road deaths, at least one contributory factor in the physical environment was reported.17 For example, speeding or other forms of risk-taking behaviour (eg, driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs, using a mobile phone while driving) was recorded as a contributory factor in 27% (n=31/113) child road deaths. Yet, interventions remain skewed towards modifying children’s behaviour instead of addressing systemic factors by making road environments and driver behaviour inherently safer for children.

A shift in focus is needed, from expecting children to navigate unsafe spaces to redesigning those spaces to prioritise their safety.

Emerging risks: changing travel patterns

Recent changes in urban travel have introduced new risks for children, including the increasing use of e-scooters and e-bikes. While the overall contribution of e-scooters to child road injury remains relatively small compared with other transport modes, emerging evidence suggests that injuries among children are increasing alongside greater availability and use.

Paediatric-specific data, although limited in the UK, indicate rising injury incidence. Analysis of US emergency department data identified over 2000 paediatric e-scooter injuries between 2020 and 2024, with incidence increasing annually and peaking in 2024.18 Similarly, a longer-term analysis demonstrated a significant increase in paediatric injuries between 2014 and 2023, including a 2.4-fold rise in the post-COVID period.19 These studies indicate that injuries are most common among adolescent males and frequently involve fractures and head injuries.18 19

UK evidence on paediatric e-scooter injuries remains sparse. A case series from two hospitals in London identified a small number of paediatric cases, but highlighted that injuries can be severe, with half of patients requiring orthopaedic surgery and several requiring hospital admission.20 However, the limited scale of such studies and the absence of national paediatric data restrict understanding of the true burden of injury among children.

Among all age groups, national data estimate 13 casualties per million miles travelled in Great Britain21 and hospital-based studies indicate that e-scooter users may sustain serious injuries, including head and limb trauma, and require admission to major trauma centres.22 23 However, much of this evidence relates to adult populations, limiting its direct applicability to children.

Emerging research also suggests that e-scooter-related risks are not evenly distributed. Spatial analyses indicate higher crash prevalence in deprived and densely populated urban areas, with distinct temporal patterns of injury occurrence.24 Despite this, current evaluations often do not distinguish between adult and child users, limiting insight into age-specific risk and exposure.

Overall, while e-scooters currently represent a relatively small proportion of child road injuries, the increasing incidence of paediatric injuries and the evolving nature of transport systems highlight the importance of capturing these risks within national datasets such as the NCMD.

The present research

Until recently, child road fatality data in England was held locally and was not easily accessible for research. The establishment of the NCMD (https://www.ncmd.info/) now provides an unprecedented opportunity to examine these fatalities using a multi-level framework, considering individual, social and environmental risk factors. Analysis of this dataset enables a more comprehensive exploration of key influences of road traffic fatalities, including underexplored areas such as SEN, evolving transport modes (eg, scooters and e-scooters) and environmental risk factors. The study embraces an intersectionality25 perspective to develop a better understanding of how various factors such as socio-economic status, ethnicity and gender can intersect and influence the risk of RTIs and outcomes for children. Understanding these intersecting factors is crucial to develop tailored and targeted interventions addressing specific needs of various child populations.

The study focuses on road fatalities among children and adolescents aged 0–17 years in England. Using the NCMD, the project will examine detailed, multi-agency data for up to 262 cases between 2019 and 2024. A mixed-methods approach will be employed, integrating quantitative analysis to identify patterns and qualitative analysis to interpret the narratives surrounding these fatalities. By situating child road deaths within a broader socio-environmental context and employing a realist approach to understanding underlying mechanisms and contextual influences, this research will move beyond individual-level explanations to explore how structural and systemic factors contribute to risk and how therefore they should be addressed.

Methods

This project started in August 2024 and will be completed in July 2026.

Data

Completed child death review cases will be selected from the NCMD. NCMD collates data from the 58 child death overview panels that cover England. NCMD frequency counts indicate that up to 262 cases will be available for analysis from April 2019 to March 2024. Detailed information on each case is provided and collated within NCMD including multiagency reports (eg, health, social care, education, postmortems, coroner reports, police forensic investigations) and crash-specific data are collected through a Road Injury Supplementary Reporting Form,1 and collected with multiagency reports (eg, health, social care, education, postmortems, coroner reports, police forensic investigations). These data will provide multiple perspectives on the context of the fatalities.

Framework development

A structured framework was developed to review the contributory factors of road fatalities, serving as the initial programme theory for this realist-informed analysis. The framework was informed by previous research on child road injury risk, including coronial record analyses, injury epidemiology and socio-economic influences on child pedestrian safety.526,28 Key risk domains included individual child characteristics, family and social environment, road and vehicle conditions and involved road user behaviour. Post-collision factors, including emergency service attendance, pre-hospital and hospital care, will be explored. Cause of death will be analysed as an outcome measure. The risks were framed within a pre-event, event and post-event framework based on the Haddon matrix.29

Our framework operationalises realist principles by identifying not just what factors contribute to road deaths but how these factors interact within specific contexts to trigger mechanisms that produce fatal outcomes. It is designed to facilitate both qualitative and quantitative analyses by ensuring comprehensive and consistent data collection across all cases. The framework is grounded in the Social Determinants of Health Model,30 which provides a multi-level perspective on risk factors influencing child road deaths, allowing us to explore the complex interplay between individual agency and structural conditions that shape road safety outcomes.

The draft framework underwent review by the wider research team and advisory board to ensure robustness, relevance and feasibility. Following review, the framework was refined to incorporate incident-specific contextual factors, enabling a holistic understanding of each fatality and providing the foundation for developing testable CMO configurations in the analytical phase.

Stakeholder focus groups

Four focus groups will be conducted with expert stakeholders who either work directly in child road injury reduction (eg, road safety officers, traffic engineers) or have significant influence in this domain (eg, health/clinical, social care, education professionals, urban planners). Participants will be selected through purposive sampling to ensure representation across relevant sectors, supplemented by snowball sampling to optimise recruitment. Each focus group will comprise 6–8 participants from geographically diverse locations across England to capture regional variations in context and practice. Written informed consent will be obtained for all participants.

The in-person, semi-structured focus groups will specifically aim to validate and refine the preliminary CMO configurations developed from the quantitative and qualitative analyses. To facilitate this process, participants will be presented with narrative case studies—representative stories based on analysed case data of child road fatalities. For each narrative case, stakeholders will:

Assess whether the identified contexts, mechanisms and outcomes align with their professional experiences, explicitly identifying what mechanisms are at play in each case and what contextual factors may have been missed.

Identify feasible intervention opportunities that address specific contexts and mechanisms.

Identify feasible intervention opportunities that address specific contexts and mechanisms.

Determine which agencies or individuals should be responsible for implementing interventions and consider the resources and systemic changes required.

Determine which agencies or individuals should be responsible for implementing interventions and consider the resources and systemic changes required.

This approach will ground theoretical understandings in practical implementation considerations relevant to various stakeholders’ operational contexts.

All focus groups will be audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts will undergo thematic analysis conducted independently by two public health researchers to ensure analytical rigour. Regular consensus meetings will be held to refine the coding and resolve any coding discrepancies. The findings from the thematic analysis will be used to refine CMO configurations, bridging theoretical understanding with practical application. If a consensus on CMO configurations cannot be reached, then the CMO will be reviewed by the wider researcher team and advisory board members. The resulting refined CMO configurations and stakeholder views on interventions will provide actionable insights for developing targeted, context-sensitive interventions to prevent child road deaths.

Study governance

A multi-disciplinary advisory board provides expert oversight. The advisory board includes professional members including representatives from the police, public health, non-government organisations including the Child Accident Prevention Trust and Playing Out, as well as a parent whose child died as a result of a road collision. The advisory board will meet at key points of the project to provide expert input into planning the analysis and interpretation and framing of outputs.

Patient and public involvement

Practitioners and professionals within the transport and health fields were involved in developing the study methodology and analysis framework. Input in the study objectives and methods has been gained from caregivers of children who died as a result of a road traffic collision.

Ethics and dissemination

This project received ethical approval from University College London, Civil, Environmental and Geomatic Engineering Local Ethics Board.

Consent from individuals who have contributed data to the NCMD does not need to be obtained. The NCMD legal basis to collect confidential and personal-level data under the Common Law Duty of Confidentiality was established through the Children Act 2004, Sections M and N; Working Together to Safeguard Children 2018 (https://consult.education.gov.uk/child-protection-safeguarding-and-family-law/working-together-to-safeguard-children-revisions-t/supporting_documents/WorkingTogethertoSafeguardChildren.pdf); and associated Child Death Review Statutory & Operational Guidance (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/859302/child-death-review-statutory-and-operational-guidance-england.pdf). The NCMD legal basis to collect personal data under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) without consent is defined by GDPR Article 6 (e) Public task and by GDPR Article 9 (h) Health or social care (with a basis in law).

Findings will be disseminated through multiple academic, policy and practice routes to maximise impact. Academic dissemination will include peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and methodological outputs relating to mixed-methods integration and systems-based injury prevention.

Policy and practice dissemination will involve collaboration with national stakeholders including road safety organisations, public health agencies, local authorities, transport practitioners and child death review partners. Outputs will include policy briefings, webinars, practitioner guidance and stakeholder workshops to support translation of findings into intervention development and policy change.

Data sharing will comply with NCMD governance requirements. Individual-level data cannot be made publicly available due to confidentiality and statutory restrictions; however, analytic code, derived frameworks (eg, Context–Mechanism–Outcome configurations) and aggregated findings will be shared where permissible to support transparency and reproducibility. Study outputs will be curated in institutional repositories where appropriate.

Quantitative analysis

The quantitative component of this study will generate a comprehensive descriptive profile of children killed on the roads in England between 2019 and 2024. It aims to identify key patterns and risks across individual, family, social and environmental risk factors, and examine how these patterns vary by mode of travel, age and other demographic features.

Where statistically feasible, separate analyses will be conducted for different modes of travel (e.g. pedestrian, car occupant, motorbike, bicycle, e-scooter, etc) and by key demographic groups (eg, preschool, primary-aged children, adolescents).

Descriptive analysis will consider the following factors:

Child characteristics

Age, sex, ethnicity, SEN status, mental health history, history of injuries, substance use, school exclusions and criminal involvement.

Family and social context

Number of siblings, family composition, parental mental health, social service involvement, family history of criminal behaviour or substance use and level of supervision.

Neighbourhood/environmental context

Index of Multiple Deprivation for home and collision location, region of England, road type (eg, urban vs rural, single vs dual carriageway) and speed limits.

Injury event

Time of day, weather, road conditions and specific features of each mode of travel (eg, helmet or seatbelt use, accompaniment, type of collision, licensing and insurance status of the other road user, presence of vehicle endorsements).

Post collision care

Emergency response, pre-hospital care, hospital care and indicators of timeliness and appropriateness of care will also be examined where available.

Temporal analyses will explore trends across the 2019–2024 period, particularly in relation to the COVID-19 pandemic and potential changes in travel behaviours.

Where appropriate, evidence of statistical differences will be tested using χ2 for frequency differences, t-test or equivalent non-parametric tests; while a Poisson distribution will be assumed for comparisons of individual or groups risk and rates. Advanced statistical modelling is not anticipated to be used in this study, due to the limited data from a comparison group and the relatively small sample size. However, interactions and co-occurrences of risk factors will be explored descriptively to support the development of CMO configurations and informed by qualitative work.

The quantitative analysis will culminate in the identification of representative case scenarios that capture typical patterns of child road fatalities across different modes of transport and age groups. These representative scenarios will serve as the foundation for the qualitative phase of analysis.

Qualitative analysis

Template analysis31 will be conducted to supplement the quantitative findings, guided by the Social Determinants of Health model.30 This approach will facilitate a structured exploration of how individual behaviours, family dynamics, social conditions and environmental factors contribute to child road deaths.

Child death narratives will be systematically coded to identify recurring themes related to risk factors, systemic failures and intervention opportunities. This will provide deeper insight into contextual influences and barriers to prevention. By combining quantitative and qualitative analyses, this study will generate a holistic understanding of the determinants of child road fatalities and inform actionable recommendations for prevention.

The qualitative analysis will move beyond description to explore the underlying mechanisms that connect contexts with outcomes, asking not just what happened but how and why it happened. This mechanistic understanding is essential for developing effective interventions that address key mechanisms.

CMO analysis

To better understand the intersectionality of risk factors, CMO diagrams will be developed from the findings of the quantitative and qualitative analyses. These will explore how individual, family, social and environmental factors interact to increase the risk of child road deaths. Separate CMO diagrams will be generated for different modes of transport (eg, pedestrian, car occupant, e-scooter user) and different age ranges (preschool (0–4 years), children (5–10 years), adolescents (11–16 years) and young adult (17 years)) to capture distinct contextual risks.

These preliminary CMO configurations represent initial programme theories that explain: (1) the contexts in which children are vulnerable to road fatalities, (2) the mechanisms through which these contexts create risk and (3) the outcomes in terms of fatal injury patterns. This realist-informed analysis moves beyond simple description to provide explanatory accounts that can inform targeted intervention development.

The preliminary CMO configurations will then be subjected to critical review and refinement based on the findings from the stakeholder focus groups described above. This iterative process ensures that the theoretical CMO configurations are validated against stakeholder expertise and enriched with practical implementation considerations.

Data from stakeholder focus groups will be analysed using thematic analysis. This analytical process will then inform refined, validated CMO diagrams that incorporate practical intervention pathways and implementation considerations from the perspective of key stakeholders. The final CMO configurations will thus bridge theoretical understanding with practical application, providing actionable frameworks for intervention development across different transport modes and age groups.

Discussion

The establishment of the NCMD provides a unique opportunity to examine child road fatalities in England using a comprehensive, multi-agency dataset. As a national dataset collating information from 58 child death overview panels, NCMD offers a level of depth not previously available for research in this area within England, enabling the exploration of child road deaths across multiple domains.

A key strength of this study is its systems-based theoretical framework, drawing on the Social Determinants of Health model.30 This approach is expected to support a more comprehensive understanding of how individual, social and environmental factors interact to influence risk and how these interactions may disproportionately affect certain groups of children.

A further strength of this study is its sequential mixed-methods design, which integrates quantitative analysis of national-level patterns with qualitative analysis of child death review narratives. This approach is expected to enable a more holistic understanding of the contextual and systemic factors contributing to child road fatalities, moving beyond purely descriptive epidemiology. The application of realist principles, through the development of CMO configurations, represents an additional strength, as it facilitates exploration of how and why risks arise in specific contexts rather than simply identifying associations.

Another important strength is the use of stakeholder engagement to refine emerging programme theory. The inclusion of structured focus groups with practitioners and professionals working in road safety and related sectors is expected to enhance the validity, relevance and applicability of the findings. By incorporating stakeholder perspectives, the study aims to ensure that emerging interpretations are grounded in real-world practice and that potential intervention points are feasible and context-sensitive.

The study is further strengthened by its systems-based theoretical framework, drawing on the Social Determinants of Health model. This approach is expected to support a more comprehensive understanding of how individual, social and environmental factors interact to influence risk, and how these interactions may disproportionately affect certain groups of children.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relies on routinely collected child death review data, which may vary in completeness, quality and level of detail across cases and geographical areas. In particular, narrative data may be subject to differences in reporting practices between panels, which could influence the depth of qualitative analysis. Second, the study is limited to fatal cases only, meaning that findings may not be generalisable to the broader population of children experiencing non-fatal road traffic injuries. Third, as an observational study without a comparison group, the analysis will not support causal inference, and identified relationships between factors should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, the relatively small number of cases may limit the ability to conduct more advanced statistical analyses or detect less common patterns.

The study is expected to generate new insights into the wider determinants of child road fatalities, including how multiple risk factors interact within specific contexts. The development of stakeholder-informed CMO configurations is anticipated to provide a structured framework for identifying potential intervention points and implementation pathways. In addition, the study is expected to contribute to the refinement of road injury reporting frameworks by identifying gaps in current data collection, particularly in relation to emerging transport modes and contextual risk factors.

Overall, this study is designed to advance understanding of child road fatalities from a systems perspective and to support the development of more targeted and context-sensitive approaches to prevention.
