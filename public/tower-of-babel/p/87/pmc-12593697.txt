Food and Nutrition Security in the context of structural inequities: insights from Brazilian favelas
Authors: Luana Lara Rocha, Elisabetta Recine, Letícia Lopes Vieira, Emanuelly Porto Oliveira, Larissa Loures Mendes
Published: 2025

The resumption of Food and Nutrition Security (FNS) policies has brought the social agenda back to the center of debate, following setbacks that affected 33 million people who experienced severe food and nutrition insecurity (FNI), that is, hunger.1 In 2025, the SOFI report confirmed that the country was once again removed from the Hunger Map.2 While this is an important achievement, it is insufficient to declare the issue resolved, particularly among vulnerable populations.

Although leaving the Hunger Map is a milestone to be celebrated, it does not represent the eradication of the problem in the country. Hunger still persists, especially among vulnerable populations. Data from the second national VIGISAN survey, conducted by the Penssan Network, reveal a troubling pattern: Black women living in poverty and in urban peripheries remain the most affected.1 These intersections of gender, race/skin color, and income highlight structural inequalities that public policies have not yet been able to fully address. The SOFI 2025 report also points to worrisome trends, including gender disparities, albeit with a distinct methodological scope and a more aggregated focus.2 Both surveys, despite their different approaches, point in the same direction regarding the persistence of food inequalities.

But are FNS public policies truly reaching those who need them most? And what challenges remain for ensuring that FNS policies address population groups where the structural causes of hunger persist?

A study conducted in two favelas of Belo Horizonte, a Brazilian metropolis, with 1194 participants, analyzed FNI through the lens of intersectionality.3 The results were striking: 35.9% were in moderate or severe FNI. Among Black participants, the prevalence was 40.1%, compared to 23.9% among white participants.3 The situation was even more critical among residents living in unfavorable socioeconomic conditions, which reflect poorer employment, income, education, and housing conditions (60.9% vs. 31.6% among those in more favorable conditions).3 Logistic regression modeling further showed that Black women in unfavorable socioeconomic conditions were seven times more likely to be in FNI compared to white men in favorable situations (OR = 7.50; 95% CI: 3.20–17.58).3

These findings reveal that FNI in favelas is shaped by intersectional processes of vulnerability, in which poverty, sexism, patriarchy, and structural racism overlap. This constitutes robust scientific evidence that FNS policies are not fully reaching those who need them most. Despite Belo Horizonte, the metropolis where the study was conducted, being considered a national reference in FNS policies, with initiatives such as community restaurants, food banks, and farmers’ markets offering fruits and vegetables at lower prices, the distribution of these services continues to prioritize central, high-traffic areas. These initiatives remain distant from favelas and insufficiently responsive to the needs of the most vulnerable territories.4

Thus, in vulnerable neighborhoods, particularly those characterized as food deserts and food swamps, food inequalities persist and are perpetuated, marked by little or no access to fresh and minimally processed foods.5, 6, 7 These territories continue to be rendered invisible and remain deeply affected by structural inequities. It is important to note that FNI in these spaces overlaps with the historical absence of other essential public policies, such as health, education, sanitation, and transportation, which further deepen the social and territorial exclusion of these populations.5,7 Healthy and affordable food must also reach the favelas, territories long marked by historical inequalities and multiple layers of state neglect.

This gap cannot be left to be filled by the ultra-processed food industry or by large supermarket and retail chains in the private sector: recent meta-analyses have associated the consumption of such foods with more than 30 health problems, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and mental disorders.8 Food is a human right, and it must promote health rather than disease.

Some recent initiatives offer insights into how these barriers might be overcome. At the national level, the “Alimenta Cidades” Strategy seeks to support municipalities with a focus on urban peripheries.9 In some Brazilian metropolises, experiences such as community gardens, public outlets for healthy food retail, and the establishment of farmers’ markets within bus stations have brought healthy foods closer to peripheral populations.10

These initiatives demonstrate that it is possible to bring adequate and healthy food closer to those who need it most. However, these actions remain isolated, limited in scope, and often dependent on local political will for their continuity. Furthermore, they should be understood as starting points for more territorialized, intersectional, structural public policies that are sensitive to the specificities of favelas. We must overcome political inertia and unequivocally recognize that food is a human right guaranteed by the Constitution, and that it must be healthy. It is essential that such actions incorporate active community engagement, be intersectoral in nature, and aim to reduce structural inequities. Only then will Brazil be able to translate national achievements into concrete improvements in the daily lives of historically marginalized populations.

Finally, it must be emphasized that Brazil has exited the Hunger Map, but hunger has not left the favelas. Food equity will only be achieved if public policies intentionally and systematically address the structural racism and feminization of poverty that shape FNI in these territories. Without such transformation, food exclusion in favelas will continue to be treated as an exception, when in fact, it is part of the prevailing pattern.
