Dynamic evolution of water conducting fracture zones and roof water hazard early warning based on 3d spatial clustering
Authors: Mingyi Cui, Enke Hou, Jichao Fan, Pengfei Hou, Dong Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Roof water inrush, induced by upward-developing water-conducting fracture zones (WCFZ) into aquifers, poses significant risks in coal mining. Conventional monitoring methods struggle with accurately capturing the complex 3D spatial structure and dynamic evolution of WCFZs. To address this, we propose an integrated early-warning framework combining microseismic spatial clustering and dynamic multi-indicator risk assessment. Our improved volume-corrected DBSCAN algorithm incorporates the spatial energy influence radius (apparent volume) of microseismic events, substantially enhancing dominant cluster identification. This improvement enables dynamic segmentation of WCFZ development into slow-growth, rapid-growth, and stable stages. We developed a Water-Risk Prediction Index (WRPI) model using twelve key indicators spanning spatial structure, energy characteristics, fault anomalies, and mining progress. A game-theoretic fusion optimizes indicator weighting, generating quantitative scores and four-tiered risk alerts. The model effectively identifies potential surge zones and fault-induced anomalies in real time. Field validation at Wenjiapo Coal Mine’s 3# and 6# workfaces confirmed the enhanced accuracy and adaptability of our method, demonstrating its significant potential for intelligent WCFZ recognition and proactive water hazard management under complex geological conditions.

Introduction

Coal remains a vital component of the global energy mix, and is expected to remain irreplaceable in the near term1,2. As shallow coal reserves are depleted, mining has shifted to deeper strata, especially in China3,4. Deep and ultra-deep mining markedly increases the risk of roof water inrush, threatening personnel safety and mine operations5,6. These disasters exhibit characteristics such as sudden onset, complex mechanisms, and high unpredictability7,8; they often precipitate catastrophic flooding, casualties, and economic losses9–11. Therefore, achieving accurate prediction and dynamic early warning of roof water hazards represents an urgent and unresolved challenge in coal mine safety engineering.

In response, various methods have been developed by researchers, including borehole water level monitoring, numerical simulations, similarity modeling, geoelectrical methods, microseismic detection, and multi-physics coupling12–18. However, in deep, structurally complex mines these methods often suffer from limited spatial resolution, latency, and weak adaptability. A particular difficulty is capturing the three-dimensional (3D) evolution of water-conducting fracture zones (WCFZ), the primary conduits for aquifer inflow.

Microseismic monitoring has gained prominence due to its high sensitivity, non-intrusive deployment, and continuous wide-area coverage19,20. This method effectively captures stress release and crack propagation during mining, providing a real-time perspective on the damage evolution of roof strata. In previous studies, microseismic analyses have often relied on statistical descriptors such as event density, energy, and frequency, which have proven valuable for characterizing mining-induced fracture processes. However, the integration of spatial structure with energy characteristics for dynamic fracture zone identification has been less common. In addition, many existing applications have been retrospective in nature, focusing on post-event interpretation, which, while insightful, offers limited capability for proactive, real-time warnings21,22.

Recent advances in machine learning have expanded the analytical toolkit for microseismic data. Spatial clustering methods (e.g., DBSCAN23–25, fuzzy c-means25,26, and K-means), and deep learning models including CNN27–29, DNN30, U-Net31, and LSTM32 have demonstrated strong potential in pattern recognition and predictive modeling. Although these methods have been explored in water hazard forecasting, real-time, quantitative applications remain relatively underdeveloped. This gap is partly due to the inherently nonlinear33, multidimensional34, and nonstationary characteristics of microseismic data, characteristics that challenge the robustness of traditional predictive frameworks.

This study proposes a novel framework to bridge this gap, a framework that integrates physical knowledge with data-driven learning to achieve dynamic 3D identification and early warning of WCFZ. Our first step is the development of a volume-aware DBSCAN clustering algorithm; this algorithm incorporates the spatial influence radius (apparent volume) of microseismic events into the similarity metric. The method achieves more accurate and robust recognition of dominant clusters for the dynamic delineation of WCFZ evolution across its three stages: slow-growth, rapid-expansion, and stabilization. Secondly, we have constructed a Water-Risk Prediction Index (WRPI) model. This model fuses 12 multi-source features—including spatial clustering metrics, energy anomalies, fault structures, and mining activity—using stage-specific game-theoretic weight optimization. Quantitative scoring and four-level graded risk warnings are enabled by the WRPI, and they also support the detection of fracture surges and fault-triggered anomalies.

Real-world microseismic data from Working Faces #3 and #6 of the Wenjiapo Coal Mine were utilized for the validation of the proposed framework. The capability of this framework to accurately track WCFZ evolution, forecast risk intensification, and issue timely alerts is confirmed by the results. A generalized, scalable, and practically effective method for intelligent roof water hazard prediction in deep mining environments is contributed by this study, which also offers valuable insights into data-physical fusion approaches for mine disaster prevention.

Principles and algorithms

Microseismic monitoring and source characteristics

The principles of acoustic emission and seismology form the basis for the microseismic monitoring method. This method involves capturing seismic waves and energy signals that originate from rock mass fracturing and instability, enabling real-time observation of dynamic failures in roof strata. A YTZ-3 microseismic monitoring system was utilized for this research. The system consists of data collectors, chargers, signal cables, three-component geophones, and a GPS timing unit. As shown in Fig. 1, a distributed monitoring network was established with components installed both on the surface and underground to form a 3D spatial layout. This configuration offers several practical advantages: GPS-based time synchronization ensures high-precision event localization (typically within ± 10 m), and the combination of broadband sensors and real-time transmission enables full-time, high-resolution monitoring in complex mining environments.Fig. 1YTZ-3 microseismic monitoring system and its typical configuration in field deployment. The system provides high-precision real-time monitoring (± 10 m localization accuracy) and broad spatial coverage through a surface–underground 3D sensor layout.

Improved DBSCAN spatial clustering method

An unsupervised, density-based clustering algorithm known as Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN)35 is utilized. This algorithm has the capability to automatically identify core points, border points, and noise from spatial datasets as an effective means to determine clusters with arbitrary shapes. Neighborhood radius (Eps) and the minimum number of points (MinPts) constitute the two critical parameters for this algorithm.

Traditional DBSCAN, as depicted in Fig. 2, employs Euclidean distance for computing the separation between data points. A challenge arises with microseismic data from coal seams because events of different energy levels have dissimilar effects on fracture development. The exclusive use of Euclidean distance can result in the incorrect classification of high-energy events as noise, which reduces the accuracy of identifying dominant clusters. This study addresses this limitation by incorporating the apparent volume radius—which signifies the spatial energy influence zone for each microseismic event—into the distance computation. It represents a spatial-energy dual-constraint distance metric (Eq. 5); this metric significantly enhances the capacity to identify dominant clusters and elevates the general accuracy of clustering results.Fig. 2DBSCAN clustering schematic diagram.

Weighting methods in game theory

This study introduces a game-theoretic weighting approach to effectively balance the merits and drawbacks of various weighting methods for an optimal fusion of risk indicator weights relevant to water hazard early warning. In this method, every weighting strategy functions as a decision-maker involved in the game. The game-theoretic model addresses potential inconsistencies or conflicts among weights from different methods through assessing inter-method coordination and conducting inverse analysis to find an optimal combination. Obtaining a final weight vector that closely aligns with the outputs of all contributing methods is the objective, which maximizes information integration and reduces discrepancies.

Participants in the game can be selected from typical weighting approaches. For this research, the entropy weight method and expert knowledge-based weighting were chosen as two representative methods, embodying objective and subjective viewpoints, respectively. Study area and microseismic data analysis

Study area

Figure 3 illustrates the study area’s location, which is in the 3# Workface of the Wenjiapo Coal Mine in the Binchang Mining Area, Xianyang City, Shaanxi Province. The working face, positioned at a depth of 680–690 m, is classified as a moderately to deeply buried coal seam and holds an average coal thickness of 4.0 m. Beginning 159 m from the crosscut, the microseismic monitoring zone spans a total distance of 396 m; its termination point is 555 m from the crosscut. During mining processes, a normal fault (F1), reflected by a vertical displacement of 4.6–6.0 m, was identified at an approximate distance of 340 m from the crosscut.Fig. 3Survey of study area and scope of microseismic monitoring. The maps were generated using ArcGIS Pro 3.0.2 (Esri, Redlands, CA, USA; https://pro.arcgis.com).

Survey of study area and scope of microseismic monitoring. The maps were generated using ArcGIS Pro 3.0.2 (Esri, Redlands, CA, USA; https://pro.arcgis.com).

Deployment of the microseismic monitoring network utilized a combined surface and underground layout. The surface installation included eight geophones, while three geophones were positioned along each side of the 3# Workface’s entries and an additional set on one side of the 4# Workface, creating an initial configuration of 17 monitoring points. As mining activities progressed, the relocation of underground sensors became necessary along the 3# entry at 50-m intervals and along the 4# entry at 100-m intervals. This progressive relocation finally expanded the network to consist of 29 monitoring points.

The stratigraphy of the study area, presented in Fig. 4, comprises eight geological units arranged from oldest to youngest: the Fuxian Formation (J1f.), Yan’an Formation (J2y), Zhiluo Formation (J2z), Anding Formation (J2a), Yijun Formation (K1y), Luohe Formation (K1l), Baode Formation (N2b), and Quaternary deposits (Q4). Mining activities mainly target the No. 4 coal seam, which is situated in the Yan’an Formation. The Luohe Formation aquifer is recognized as the main aquifer in the roof of the 4 coal seam; this aquifer exhibits considerable thickness, features a lithology primarily of sandstone and conglomerate, and exhibits moderate water richness.Fig. 4Integrated Stratigraphic Column of the Study Area.

Integrated Stratigraphic Column of the Study Area.

Approximately 210 m represents the vertical distance from the roof of the 4 coal seam to the base of the Luohe Formation. The risk of roof water inrush is directly determined by whether the WCFZ extends upward to breach the Luohe aquifer. This particular issue therefore constitutes the central concern for implementing water hazard prevention and control measures in this mining area.

Results of microseismic monitoring

The microseismic monitoring campaign spanned a total duration of 81 d, from which 66 d produced data considered valid for analysis. Following the removal of recordings represented by a low signal-to-noise ratio, a total of 13,295 microseismic events were successfully detected. Statistical analysis (Fig. 5) was performed utilizing 10-m elevation intervals relative to the roof of the No. 4 coal seam. A majority of these microseismic events were distributed in an elevation range of 400–610 m. Above the 610 m elevation, the quantity of events decreased sharply, to a level considered negligible. Based on the elevation distribution, the vertical development of events can be categorized into three zones: a growth zone (400–520 m), a decline zone (520–610 m), and a termination zone above 610 m, which is proximate to the bottom boundary of the Luohe Formation aquifer.Fig. 5Elevation distribution map of microseismic events.

Elevation distribution map of microseismic events.

According to the energy distribution diagram (Fig. 6), energy levels between 0–100 J represented 63.72% of the recorded events (equating to 8,472 events); these are indicative of low-energy events typically associated with the development of numerous micro-fractures. The primary concentration of these events was determined in the 400–620 m range. In comparison, high-energy events (> 1000 J) constituted only 26 events (0.19% of the total), with their locations mostly confined to the 400–600 m elevation range. It is plausible that these high-energy events signify instances of fracture connectivity and periodic pressure release from the overlying roof strata.Fig. 6Energy distribution of microseismic events.

Energy distribution of microseismic events.

The XY plane distribution of microseismic events, as depicted in Fig. 7, clearly indicates two zones of high density. These zones are mainly concentrated in the goaf area of the 2# Workface and in the coal pillar area situated between the 2# and 3# Workfaces. In the XZ cross-section, event density is significantly higher on the side corresponding to the 2# Workface goaf, an observation that indicates localized surrounding rock damage and enhanced fracture connectivity in that region. Several microseismic events are observed to extend beyond the base of the Luohe Formation; this suggests that the fracture zone has already penetrated the aquifer, thereby posing a potential hazard of water inrush. The YZ cross-section demonstrates microseismic events displaying a “cloud-like” distribution pattern. A difference in event density is observable across the F1 fault: events are identified to be densely clustered in front of and above the fault, while their distribution is sparse behind it, which forms a characteristic “trailing tail” pattern. This pattern strongly suggests that the fault significantly affects the spatial propagation characteristics of fractures.Fig. 7.3D spatial distribution characteristics of microseismic events.

3D spatial distribution characteristics of microseismic events.

In general, a dense distribution pattern of microseismic events is apparent above the roof of the 3# Workface, and their spatial distribution exhibits significant heterogeneity. This means that the number and density of microseismic events vary noticeably across different regions, which reflects the spatial complexity associated with the development of the WCFZ.

Clustering-based model for warning of roof water hazards

Stage identification and evolution characteristics of the WCFZ

This study puts forward a three-stage mechanism for identifying and classifying risks associated with WCFZ development; this mechanism facilitates accurate prediction and dynamic early warning of roof water inrush hazards. The foundation for this mechanism rests upon results from microseismic spatial clustering. Specific diagnostic criteria define each evolutionary stage of the WCFZ, and these criteria integrate spatial clustering patterns, characteristics of energy release, and the effect of geological structures.

Identification of the slow-growth zone and fracture development characteristics

The slow-growth zone (07–22 to 08–02) is reflected by low microseismic event frequency and minimal energy release. Throughout this period, the dominant cluster’s height demonstrates a gradual increase, while its spatial distribution remains dispersed. At this juncture, early warning efforts concentrate on identifying potential connections between the dominant cluster and any secondary clusters. A key concern is whether these connections might promote the upward extension (“guiding rise”) of the fracture zone.

Several secondary clusters appeared in 30 m of the dominant cluster during the late period of this stage (07–29 to 08–02). By 08–02, it was observed that some of these secondary clusters displayed maximum elevations significantly exceeding that of the dominant cluster (Table 3). Such a development indicates a potential transition from the slow-growth stage to the rapid-expansion stage. Identification of the rapid-growth zone and surge mechanism

Significant increases in both the elevation and density of the dominant cluster characterize the rapid-growth stage (August 3–August 10); these increases are coupled with concentrated energy release. The vertical expansion of the WCFZ primarily occurs during this core period. All secondary clusters are situated below the dominant cluster, which functions as the core cluster at this stage. Therefore, detection of noise frequency peaks occurring above the dominant cluster forms the primary basis for identifying WCFZ surges.

The main early warning criteria for this stage include:

① Sustained Elevation of the Dominant Cluster: This criterion involves observing a continuous upward trend in the dominant cluster’s elevation, maintaining it at a level significantly above all secondary clusters.

② Surge Prediction: The system considers a specific elevation a potential surge point for the following day when a noise frequency peak zone is detected above the dominant cluster’s current elevation, offering that the cumulative energy in this peak zone surpasses 2,000 J.

An evolutionary pattern of “weak noise precursor → structural connectivity → dominant cluster surge” is typically observed for this stage.

Identification of the stable zone

The growth rate of the dominant cluster’s height experiences a significant slowdown when the working face progresses to the “first square completion” stage (i.e., when the length equals the width). Meanwhile, the spatial structure of the fracture zone gradually matures and becomes more stable. During this period, lower height fluctuation in the dominant cluster is apparent. Nevertheless, special attention should be paid to areas near local faults. As shown in Table 2, in the stable zone, the average daily cumulative energy increment is 27,495.20 J/d, and the average daily WCFZ height increment is 0.28 m/d. However, during the fault-crossing period from August 25 to August 28, these values increased sharply to 42,857.46 J/d and 4.01 m/d, respectively—far exceeding the average statistical results. Such a short-term surge in cumulative energy, coupled with a rapid rise in WCFZ height, indicates that fault-crossing can significantly compromise rock mass stability and accelerate fracture zone propagation.

Discussion

The effectiveness of volume-improved clustering

Traditional DBSCAN algorithms commonly employ Euclidean distance as the similarity metric for spatial clustering of microseismic events, and have been successfully applied in many microseismic monitoring studies. However, under complex geological conditions in coal mines, this metric may not fully capture the actual influence range of microseismic events, which can affect the classification of dominant clusters and outliers. In addition, because DBSCAN is sensitive to density variations, selecting unified parameters in cases of uneven event distribution remains a recognized challenge that can influence clustering stability. To address these issues, spatio-temporal DBSCAN (ST-DBSCAN) extends the approach by integrating both spatial and temporal attributes into the distance metric, thereby enhancing the accuracy of cluster identification in complex microseismic sequences38. Other approaches have adopted two-step clustering strategies, such as first performing spatial clustering and then refining event classification based on cosine similarity of moment tensors, which has been shown to improve the handling of high-dimensional, multi-feature microseismic datasets39.

Building on these advances, this study develops a WRPI method that incorporates an apparent volume–adjusted DBSCAN to further refine the spatial similarity measure, enabling a more precise delineation of the WCFZ’s spatiotemporal evolution. By integrating multiple microseismic indicators, the WRPI model not only improves stage identification of WCFZ development but also provides a practical and transferable framework for dynamic water hazard risk assessment in coal mines.

Comparing the two clustering results for the 3# Workface (Fig. 22) demonstrates similar curves from both methods for the dominant cluster’s maximum height during the WCFZ’s early development stage, as the effect of radius correction is limited at this point. As the mining face advances and event frequency increases, the traditional Euclidean approach frequently absorbs spatially isolated high points into the dominant cluster; this action results in anomalous spikes in maximum height. The radius-corrected method, in comparison, is more conservative as it effectively excludes anomalously high points with small energy and small influence radii, leading to a smoother curve for the dominant cluster height and offering stronger engineering interpretability.Fig. 22Comparison of WCFZ in Clustering Results Optimized for Volume.

In general, the influence-radius corrected DBSCAN demonstrates performance similar to the Euclidean method during the early stage. It thus offers improved outlier resistance and spatial clustering robustness in the later stage. Such improvements enhance the method’s practical applicability and engineering value for real-world implementations.

The spatial development morphology of 3D WCFZ

Research into the three-dimensional spatial development of WCFZs has significantly advanced in recent years40. Hou et al.41, for instance, employed 3D geological modeling and seismic forward modeling, which indicated that the WCFZ typically demonstrates a semi-saddle-shaped distribution along the strike direction and exhibits a saddle-shaped structure with higher elevations on both sides and a slightly lower center along the dip direction. Other studies also indicate that under certain geological conditions, the WCFZ may present an approximately trapezoidal spatial morphology42,43.

A significant portion of existing work, however, remains limited to qualitative two-dimensional profile analysis; thus, high-precision, dynamic 3D modeling and the spatiotemporal evolution of the WCFZ have not yet been sufficiently addressed.

This study integrates microseismic monitoring with a radius-corrected DBSCAN clustering algorithm, thereby achieving automatic recognition and visualization of the WCFZ’s three-dimensional dynamic development. The results from this approach indicate unique stage-dependent spatial evolution characteristics of the WCFZ.

This approach offers a new framework for 3D modeling and dynamic analysis of WCFZs under complex geological conditions. It also enhances the capability for real-time risk identification and the accurate prediction of roof water hazards.

Discussion on engineering auxiliary verification and model application

To verify the engineering applicability of the proposed radius-corrected DBSCAN-based WCFZ identification method alongside the WRPI-based roof water inrush early warning model, a cross-site evaluation was conducted. This evaluation utilized actual WCFZ observations from the 1# workface and microseismic monitoring data from the #6 workface of the Wenjiapo Coal Mine.

After mining of the #1 workface concluded, borehole imaging and logging indicated the fracture zone terminated at approximately 174.23 m above the roof; this corresponds to a fracture-to-mining height ratio (FMR) of 50.20. For the 3# Workface, application of the improved DBSCAN clustering approach presented in this study produced a maximum WCFZ height of 223.57 m, which resulted in an FMR of 58.83. Under a mining height of 3.8 m, the WCFZ in the Wenjiapo Mine could reach up to 199 m, with a minimum FMR of 52.3744. Confirmation from microseismic monitoring data at the #3 workface also demonstrated that the WCFZ had developed into the bottom boundary of the Luhe Formation aquifer (approximately 210 m above the workface), indicating a minimum FMR of 55.26. Besides, actual water inrush events during the production period offered further support for the conclusion that the WCFZ had penetrated the aquifer. The final WCFZ height and related FMR values determined in this study are considered reasonable, considering the high similarity in geological conditions between the 1# and 3# Workfaces.

The model’s generalization capability was further assessed by applying the method to the 6# Workface, a site sharing similar geological conditions. The 6# Workface exhibited a mining height of 7.5 m, and its microseismic monitoring period spanned 35 d. Limitations and prospects

This study has achieved notable progress in theory and methodology. Compared with purely surface-based or underground microseismic monitoring approaches, the proposed method offers improved accuracy in microseismic event localization. Furthermore, the improved DBSCAN algorithm, through the integration of apparent volume, provides a more effective description of the development extent and characteristics of the water-conducting fracture zone (WCFZ).

However, its application in different mining areas and geological conditions still presents certain limitations. The proposed WRPI model demonstrates good applicability in coal mines equipped with microseismic monitoring systems and relatively simple structural frameworks; nevertheless, its predictive accuracy may be affected in mines with sparse sensor layouts or complex fault structures. Field monitoring has revealed that in the vicinity of faults, the WCFZ height can surge significantly within a short period, accompanied by intensive high-energy events. This phenomenon can be attributed to local rock mass instability caused by fault reactivation. Li45 noted in strain energy evolution studies that coseismic fault slip leads to localized strain energy concentration in the area adjacent to the fault–working face, which not only increases the likelihood of rock mass instability but also provides triggering conditions for phenomena such as rock bursts or coal–rock outbursts. In the F16 fault case study, slip near the working-face side resulted in significant strain energy accumulation, coinciding with observed strong microseismic events, demonstrating that the superposition of fault reactivation and mining-induced disturbance is an important mechanism for localized anomalies. Furthermore, Li46 used three-dimensional finite element simulations to investigate the mechanism of reverse fault slip induced by mining. The results showed that the intermediate principal stress (σh) in the far field and the working-face length have a significant influence on the range of fault slip; when the strike of the working face is nearly parallel to the strike of the fault, mining-induced disturbances more readily promote fault slip and broaden the slip zone, thereby intensifying local structural stress concentration.

Therefore, the WCFZ height surge observed during the fault-crossing period from August 25 to 28 (average increment of 4.01 m/d, far exceeding the stable zone value of 0.28 m/d) in this study can be regarded as an expression of fault reactivation. When mining-induced stress disturbance is coupled with fault geometric and stress conditions (e.g., dip angle, strike, and principal stress orientation), the probability and magnitude of fault slip are significantly increased, resulting in rapid expansion of the fracture zone and weakened roof stability. This mechanism suggests that, during mining operations near faults, special attention should be paid to local energy concentration and anomalous WCFZ height changes induced by fault slip. In future applications, structural activation risk criteria for fault-adjacent zones should be incorporated into WRPI early-warning indicators.

Meanwhile, noted that in mines with complex or extremely complex hydrogeological conditions, the setting of stage-specific indicator weights and thresholds may require targeted adjustments based on site-specific characteristics. In deep or ultra-deep mines, high in-situ stress environments may alter the characteristics of microseismic signals, necessitating the inclusion of stress-related features in the model; whereas in shallow, unconsolidated strata, frequent low-energy events may require alternative event-clustering and filtering strategies. These differences indicate that although the model framework is transferable, its parameterization process and early-warning thresholds must be locally optimized according to the geological and operational conditions.

In terms of multi-source data fusion, future research should introduce advanced deep learning architectures to fully integrate heterogeneous monitoring data such as microseismic parameters47, borehole water levels48, mine water inflow49, and hydrochemical analyses50. It is recommended that each data type be modeled separately to independently output stage-specific risk levels, which are then fused within an improved Dempster–Shafer (DS) evidence theory framework. The improved DS framework should be capable of handling conflicting information by incorporating AI-driven feature extraction, attention mechanisms, and adaptive weighting strategies, thereby enhancing decision robustness under incomplete or uncertain information and improving the comprehensiveness and reliability of early-warning criteria.

From an engineering deployment perspective, although in theory only four sensors are sufficient to invert all required microseismic parameters, in practice it is advisable to include more than ten microseismic monitoring points. The deployment should adopt joint layouts combining underground–surface or underground–surface–in-borehole configurations to improve inversion accuracy and event localization precision. In underground deployment, the spacing between sensors within the same roadway should be controlled between 20–50 m, while surface sensor spacing should be maintained at 300–700 m. Given that working faces are generally small in extent, excessive spacing can degrade localization accuracy. In addition, loose surface layers (e.g., sandy soils) can attenuate seismic wave propagation, so removing such material during surface sensor installation is recommended to ensure coupling quality and signal integrity.

For future work, efforts should focus on building a multi-source, dynamically adaptive early-warning platform capable of automatically adjusting model parameters in response to changes in geological and operational conditions, and on conducting regional optimization and long-term validation in different mining areas to enhance the applicability and resilience of the WRPI model in real-world engineering. At the engineering implementation level, two directions are recommended: (1) improving sensor sensitivity and stability, which largely depends on the technological advances of manufacturers, to ensure reliable detection of weak signals and subtle anomalies; and (2) achieving higher-precision microseismic event localization in the data processing stage to minimize the impact of errors on early-warning criteria. In addition, artificial intelligence and deep learning algorithms should be incorporated to enable automatic microseismic waveform recognition and event picking, and to explore more precise microseismic response mechanisms and physical causes of water-hazard precursors, thereby enhancing early anomaly detection capability.

Conclusion

Traditional DBSCAN’s reliance on Euclidean distance frequently leads to inaccurate dominant cluster identification. To overcome this limitation, a DBSCAN-based spatial clustering method was developed, which incorporates microseismic event apparent volume correction to filter anomalous high-elevation noise points. Then, the spatial accuracy for clarifying the development extent of the WCFZ is enhanced.The improved clustering results facilitated the revelation of the WCFZ’s three-stage spatiotemporal evolution pattern, which consists of slow-growth, rapid-growth, and stable stages. During the slow-growth stage, fractures are sparsely distributed and small in scale. Then, the rapid-growth stage is represented by the rapid vertical propagation of fractures accompanied by concentrated energy release. The WCFZ structure gains coherence in the stable stage, developing a “saddle-shaped” spatial morphology.For dynamic water inrush risk assessment, a WRPI model was created through the integration of 12 indicators, such as spatial structure, energy release, and geological anomalies. The model also incorporates stage-specific weight optimization, which is based on game theory. This approach facilitates the dynamic assessment of water inrush risk levels alongside the early identification of high-risk zones. The model demonstrates particular effectiveness during the rapid-growth stage, where it can accurately predict WCFZ development height and offer timely early warnings, thus enhancing the responsiveness and accuracy of roof water hazard forecasting. The strong generalizability and robustness of the proposed early-warning method, based on microseismic spatial clustering, were confirmed in a validation study conducted at the 6# Workface of the Wenjiapo Coal Mine. This method exhibits high engineering applicability and potential for field deployment. Expanding data sources and integrating multi-scale, multi-source hydrological monitoring data will be the focus of the future research. Further efforts will also be directed towards enhancing the WRPI model’s predictive accuracy and adaptability, which offers a more in-depth and reliable technical method for early warning regarding deep coal mine roof water hazards. Although the proposed method demonstrates high applicability and prediction accuracy in the study area, certain limitations remain with respect to data collection density, monitoring cost, and adaptability under complex geological conditions. For instance, in areas with sparse sensor deployment, complex structural settings, or strong surface environmental interference, the accuracy of microseismic event localization and the predictive performance of the WRPI model may be compromised. To further enhance the engineering applicability and practical value of the method, future research should focus on the following: (1). Optimizing sensor deployment strategies according to the geological characteristics of different mining areas, and establishing minimum monitoring density and placement standards; (2). Integrating multi-source hydrological and geological monitoring data to construct a more comprehensive multimodal early-warning model; (3) Evaluating and reducing the deployment and maintenance costs of the monitoring system to facilitate broader application of the method under diverse coal mining conditions.
