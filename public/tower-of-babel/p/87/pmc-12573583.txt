Advancements and current trends in bearing surface technology for total shoulder arthroplasty: a comprehensive review
Authors: Daniel J Maunder, Sara M Marshall, Anand M Murthi, Ryan A Hoffman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplications related to wear-induced osteolysis, such as glenoid component loosening in anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty and scapular notching in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty continue to impact clinical outcomes following shoulder replacement. This review explores the evolution of prosthesis design, and how advancements in bearing surface technology may influence implant longevity and reduce complications related to wear, osteolysis, and loosening.Keywords: Total shoulder arthroplasty, Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Bearing surfaces, Metal-on-polyethylene, Cross-linked polyethylene, Pyrocarbon, Ceramic, Prosthesis wear

Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is a widely performed surgical procedure that offers significant pain relief and restores joint function in patients suffering from a variety of debilitating shoulder pathologies. TSA involves replacing the diseased native bone with implants on both the glenoid and humeral sides. The 2 most common methods of TSA are anatomic (aTSA) and reverse TSA (rTSA). aTSA aims to replicate the native shoulder anatomy, with a sphere on the humeral side and a resurfaced material on the glenoid side. aTSA requires an intact rotator cuff to maintain joint stability, function, and is most commonly performed in the setting of glenohumeral osteoarthritis.

rTSA inverts the normal shoulder anatomy by implanting a convex glenoid ball on the scapula and a concave socket on the humerus. Initially introduced by Grammont in 1987 for patients suffering from rotator cuff tear arthropathy, this design compensates for a deficient rotator cuff by shifting the center of rotation inferomedial, increasing the mechanical advantage of the deltoid and reducing reliance on the rotator cuff for shoulder abduction and rotation.24,50

Indications for TSA, particularly rTSA, have broadened considerably in recent years,71 leading to a steady rise in annual procedure volume, with increasing utilization in younger patient populations.9,22,57,71 This trend has been accompanied by a growing burden of revision shoulder arthroplasty, highlighting the need for enhanced implant longevity.22 One of the primary causes of TSA failure is implant loosening, often caused or exacerbated by osteolysis secondary to the wear debris from prosthetic bearing surfaces.60 To address this challenge, advancements in bearing surface technology have focused on developing materials with superior biomechanical properties to reduce wear rates and improve longevity. This review explores the evolution of prosthesis design and provides an up-to-date overview of current bearing surface technology in both aTSA and rTSA.

History of total shoulder arthroplasty and bearing surfaces

Jules-Émile Péan performed the first documented shoulder arthroplasty on March 11, 1893, at the Hôpital International in Paris.7 The prosthesis, designed and constructed by Parisian dentist J. Porter Michaels, consisted of a platinum shaft and a rubber humeral head with 2 intersecting equatorial grooves. Two metal loops, placed in each groove, secured the rubber head to the platinum shaft and native glenoid.7 Péan acknowledged Themistocles Gluck's earlier contributions to joint replacement surgery as influential in the development of his prosthesis. Notably, Gluck had designed shoulder replacement implants using ivory and cadaveric bone at least 3 years prior.7

Contemporary bearing surfaces in TSA did not emerge until the 1970s. With limited treatment options for proximal humerus fracture dislocations, Dr Charles Neer developed a vitallium hemiarthroplasty system in 1951, now known as the “Neer 1 prosthesis”.42,53,54 Recognizing that patients with an arthritic glenoid may achieve superior outcomes with TSA rather than hemiarthroplasty, Neer pioneered the first widely accepted aTSA. By redesigning the head of the original hemi-replacement, the Neer 2 system was compatible with a polyethylene glenoid component.55 A decade later, Dr Paul Grammont introduced key modifications in rTSA design that provided the foundation for modern rTSA systems.11 His first generation “Trompette prosthesis” featured a metal glenosphere and a trumpet-shaped, all-polyethylene humeral component.11,42 While Neer and Grammont's original designs have undergone multiple refinements to improve biomechanical performance and implant modularity, metal-on-polyethylene (MoP) bearing surfaces have remained the gold standard in TSA.78

Challenges with early prostheses and polyethylene wear

Early TSA implants exhibited high rates of periprosthetic radiolucent lines and aseptic loosening, particularly of the glenoid component.74,76,79 The properties of bearing surface materials were identified as a major contributing factor to these complications. One primary mechanism driving aseptic loosening and implant failure was osteolysis, an inflammatory macrophage-mediated response to polyethylene wear debris.4,35,68 The phagocytic uptake of wear particles triggers cytokine release, which promotes osteoclastic activity at the bone–implant interfaces, leading to progressive bone resorption and implant instability.1,32 Along with an imbalance of forces on the polyethylene glenoid and lack of backside support early loosening can be seen.

Polyethylene wear occurs through 3 fundamental mechanisms: adhesion, abrasion, and fatigue. Adhesive wear results from localized bonding between surfaces, leading to the transfer of material, which later detaches during motion.48 Abrasive wear occurs when hard asperities or embedded particles shear the surface, removing material.48 Fatigue wear develops due to repeated cyclic loading, which induces crack formation and propagation with the material.48 Recognizing the relationship between wear debris and implant failure, innovations in bearing surface technology have focused on improving the wear resistance and extending implant longevity. These efforts have included enhancements to existing materials, such as cross-linked polyethylene, the introduction of novel materials like ceramics, and the exploration of different bearing surface combinations.

Not all innovations have been successful. Metal-on-metal (MoM) bearings, initially developed for hip arthroplasty, were trialed in TSA with the goal of improving durability in younger, more active patients. Theoretically, the MoM design was more resistant to wear compared to polyethylene components; however, clinical results were poor.18 In 2016, Riley et al.67 evaluated early outcomes in patients younger than 50 years who underwent rTSA with a MoM prosthesis and reported a high complication rate (55%), including dislocation, glenoid fractures, and implant dissociation. In additional to mechanical failure, MoM implants generated metallic debris, leading to adverse reactions such as metallosis, aseptic tissue necrosis, and pseudotumor formation.28 Due to these complications, MoM prostheses have been largely discontinued in TSA and joint arthroplasty as a whole.

Innovations in bearing surface technology

Metal-on-Polyethylene

MoP remains the gold standard for prosthetic interfaces in TSA (Figs. 1 and 2). In both anatomic and reverse shoulder arthroplasty, the conventional configuration consists of a convex metal head articulating with a concave polyethylene socket. The most commonly used metal alloys in TSA implants are titanium–aluminum–vanadium (Ti-6Al-4V) and cobalt–chromium–molybdenum (CoCrMo).49 CoCrMo, known for its superior strength and hardness, provides excellent wear resistance, making it an ideal metal bearing surface.49 In contrast, the relatively softer Ti alloy is less wear-resistant but offers unique advantages in nonarticulating components that interface with bone.49 Its use as a bearing surface is generally reserved for patients with nickel allergies.75

Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) is the predominant material used in MoP interfaces, but its susceptibility to wear can lead to debris generation, osteolysis, and aseptic loosening. To address these issues, cross-linked and highly cross-linked polyethylene (HXLPE) was developed and has become the industry standard in total hip arthroplasty.23 In a study comparing cross-linked and non–cross-linked polyethylene glenoid components in primary aTSA, Page et al.58 found that cross-linked polyethylene was associated with a lower revision rate at 1.5 years. HXLPE is produced by exposing UHMWPE to gamma irradiation, which induces cross-linking of carbon chains in a dose-dependent manner.5,23 Increased cross-linking improves wear resistance, but at the expense of mechanical properties such as fracture resistance.5 Therefore, optimizing the degree of cross-linking in HXLPE requires some balance. Although much of the research on HXLPE has focused on hip arthroplasty, Peers et al.62 demonstrated that HXLPE liners in rTSA were more resistant to wear compared to conventional UHMWPE.

A potential drawback of polyethylene irradiation is the generation of free radicals, which can lead to oxidation and material degradation over time. To mitigate this, antioxidants such as vitamin E have been incorporated into HXLPE. Early findings in total hip arthroplasty have shown that vitamin E-enhanced polyethylene (VEXPE) prostheses reduce wear and lower all-cause revision rates.15,26,69 Although long-term clinical data on VEXPE in TSA remains limited, Alexander et al.2 reported that VEXPE glenoid components demonstrated significantly lower wear rates compared to conventional cross-linked polyethylene when paired with a ceramic humeral head. Similarly, Khan et al.38 compared VEXPE glenoid components to both HXLPE and conventional UHMWPE using CoCrMo humeral heads, finding that VEXPE exhibited superior initial wear characteristics and favorable long-term wear rates under abrasive conditions. These findings indicate promising potential benefits of VEXPE components in TSA; however, further long-term and clinical studies are needed to confirm these advantages.

Metal-backed glenoid

Cemented all-polyethylene glenoids (PEG) have traditionally been used in aTSA, but high rates of glenoid component loosening led to the development of metal-backed glenoids (MBGs) in an effort to improve fixation.40 Early MBG designs featured a thin polyethylene liner, which was associated with increased wear rates and implant failure.10,59 When the polyethylene inserts wore through or dissociated from the metal backing, metal-on-metal articulation occurred between the exposed glenoid baseplate and the humeral head. In a 2014 systematic review, Papadonikolakis et al.59 recommended against MBGs after finding significantly higher revision rates compared to conventional cemented PEGs. These findings were likely related, in part, to metallosis observed in MBG failures.13

Despite these early challenges, manufacturers have continued refining MBG designs to address glenoid component loosening, the most common cause of reoperation in cemented PEG prostheses.40 These advancements led to what Castagna and Garofalo13 define as “modern designs”. The second-generation Systema Multiplana Randelli MBG by LimaCorporate incorporated a curved-back, a 5 mm thick rigid metal backing, a hydroxyapatite-coated peg, and secure fixation with 2 screws and a hollow central peg.14,40 In a study by Castagna et al.,14 patients implanted with this second-generation MBG demonstrated excellent clinical outcomes, with zero revisions at a mean follow-up of 75.7 months. The rigid metal backing was thought to reduced polyethylene wear by decreasing stress on the liner and underlying bone, while the hollow central peg promoted bone ingrowth for improved glenoid fixation.14

Other modern MBG designs include Zimmer's first- and second-generation trabecular MBGs, (Fig. 3).13,40 The porous-coated baseplate is designed to promote osseous integration for more durable glenoid anchoring. Investigation of this implant by Murphy et al.52 reported excellent clinical outcomes and 100% implant survivorship at a mean follow-up of 7 years, despite finding evidence of metallic debris in 27% of shoulders. A 2020 systematic review comparing modern MBGs with cemented PEGs found that modern MBGs had significantly lower rates of loosening and revision at follow-ups beyond 72 months.40 Another systematic review of second-generation trabecular MBGs reported low rates of loosening and revision, with an overall survivorship of 96% at a mean follow-up of 43 months.45 However, the authors expressed concerns regarding long-term survival due to the high rate of radiolucency and metal debris formation observed over a relatively short time frame.45 In a case series investigating the long-term outcomes of modern MBGs, Ranieri et al.64 reported that revision-free survival was 73% at 15 years, but dropped to 38% at 20 years.

Renewed interest in MBGs for aTSA has also been driven by the success of rTSA and the emergence of universal convertible baseplates. These convertible MBGs allow for aTSA revisions to be converted to rTSA without removing the glenoid baseplate, a key advantage over cemented PEGs. In the case of revision with a convertible MBG, it has been reported that the MBG was retained in 92.8% of cases.6 A retrospective review of 26 patients who underwent aTSA with a universal MBG component (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA, or PH Orthopedics) between 2017 and 2022 demonstrated acceptable outcomes at short-term follow-up.19 Similarly, Kim et al.39 reported favorable short-term outcomes with cementless convertible MBG that utilized a VEXPE liner (Zimmer Biomet). However, another small study examining 25 patients who underwent aTSA between 2009 and 2020 using modular cementless flat-backed MBGs (Universal Glenoid convertible baseplate from Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) reported poor clinical and radiologic outcomes at a longer mean follow-up of 5.7 years.56 The revision rate was 68%, with the majority (71%) of revisions attributed to polyethylene wear.56

The heterogeneous outcomes reported in the literature for modern MBGs likely reflect significant variations in prosthetic design, and bearing surfaces, underscoring the need for further research to determine the most effective configuration for optimal patient outcomes. Additionally, limited long-term data make it difficult to fully assess the durability and potential advantages of MBGs compared to other glenoid components. Incorporating emerging technologies such as VEXPE into MBG designs has the potential to improve outcomes, and future research should continue exploring the use of alternative bearing surfaces in these components. Many of the modern MBG are platform, convertible-type glenoids that allow easier conversion to rTSA which may be of benefit in the younger arthritic patient needing an aTSA early in life.

Ceramic components

The use of ceramic materials in both aTSA and rTSA systems has gained increasing attention due to findings that ceramic heads significantly reduce wear on polyethylene glenoid surfaces (Fig. 4). This suggests that ceramic-on-polyethylene bearing surfaces could enhance implant longevity.26,51 In a biomechanical study using a joint simulator, Mueller et al.51 reported a 26.7% reduction in wear rates when comparing ceramic to metal heads in aTSA prostheses, highlighting the potential of ceramics to mitigate wear-related complications commonly seen with MoP interfaces.

Several in vivo studies with a mean follow-up of 5 years have demonstrated good clinical outcomes with ceramic heads, with one study reporting a lower incidence of humeral osteolysis and glenoid radiolucent lines compared to metal heads.8,37,72 In a multicenter study of 207 patients, stemless ceramic aTSA demonstrated a survivorship of 94.7% at an 8-year follow-up.37 Additionally, a recent 2024 study by Davies et al.17 reported lower all-cause revision rates following aTSA using ceramic humeral heads compared to metal at an 8-year follow-up. Most revisions were attributed to cuff insufficiency, and while ceramic-on-polyethylene bearings had a lower overall revision rate, there was no significant difference in glenoid component wear or implant survival between the groups.17

Currently, no published studies have evaluated ceramic components in rTSA. Although ceramic humeral heads are approved internationally, they have yet to receive the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in the United States. Despite the lower risk of osteolysis and implant loosening, the hardness of ceramic makes it more prone to fracture when compared to metal.51

Pyrolytic carbon

Pyrolytic carbon, or pyrocarbon, is an advanced biomaterial that has emerged as a promising option in shoulder hemiarthroplasty, particularly for young and active patients. It exhibits unique mechanical properties, including a smooth bearing surface, high compressive strength, enhanced wear resistance, and an elastic modulus comparable to natural bone.21,30 This elasticity allows for even stress distribution, reducing the risk of bone resorption and implant loosening.31 Additionally, pyrocarbon may promote type II collagen expression in chondrocytes, facilitating the formation of a cartilaginous matrix at the articulating surface.21,30

Recent studies suggest that pyrocarbon outperforms traditional metal implants in terms of biocompatibility, wear resistance, and mechanical properties.70 In a comparative study of humeral hemiresurfacing, revision rates were significantly lower in the pyrocarbon group than in the metal hemi-resurfacing group for patients under 55 years old with glenohumeral osteoarthritis.47 However, when compared to metal stemmed hemiarthroplasty, no significant difference in revision rates was observed.47 Notably, none of the revisions in the pyrocarbon group were attributed to glenoid erosion, which was the leading cause of revision in the metal hemi-resurfacing (32.5%) and metal stemmed groups (46.7%).47 Retrospective analysis by Douven et al.20 looked at hemiarthroplasty with pyrocarbon, and when compared to metal, found significantly lower revision rates with pyrocarbon (2.3% vs. 12.8%).

In another study of pyrocarbon hemiarthroplasty, Garret et al.29 found that 86% of glenoids remained unchanged at 2-year follow-up, with 83% unchanged at a second follow-up between 5 to 9 years. Importantly, glenoid erosion was not reported as a reason for revision in any patients, reinforcing pyrocarbon's superior biocompatibility compared to metal when articulating with the native glenoid.29 Clinical outcomes have also been favorable, with patients reporting significant pain relief, improved function, and increased range of motion following hemiarthroplasty with pyrocarbon heads.29,77 Cointat et al.16 further demonstrated that in young patients under 60 years old, pyrocarbon hemiarthroplasty was associated with high rates of pain relief and return to sport and work.16

However, results from the New Zealand National Joint Registry suggested that while revision rates were comparable between patients treated with pyrocarbon hemiarthroplasty vs. aTSA, short-term postoperative function was superior in aTSA patients.27 Additionally, recent data from the 2024 Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry reported a 7-year cumulative revision rate of 7.0% for hemiarthroplasty with a pyrocarbon head—higher than that reported for total stemmed anatomic (5.7%) and reverse TSA (4.1%) prostheses.66 While confidence intervals overlap, the data from these large-scale registries raise concerns over whether pyrocarbon hemiarthroplasty should be favored over TSA in select patients and highlight the need for longer-term follow-up before widespread adoption.

Research on pyrocarbon in TSA systems remains limited. Shoulder simulator testing of pyrocarbon humeral heads articulating on UHMWPE glenoids has shown wear rates comparable to those observed in CoCr metal-on-UHMWPE interfaces.63,73 However, no studies have evaluated wear rates when pyrocarbon articulates on more contemporary polyethylene formulations.

The FDA's 2023 approval of pyrolytic carbon for hemiarthroplasty, marked by Stryker's Tornier Pyrocarbon Humeral Head, represents a significant milestone in the adoption of this advanced material (Fig. 5). While its unique properties theoretically appear well-suited for prosthesis-on-bone articulation,20,29 the role of pyrocarbon hemiarthroplasty as an alternative to current TSA prosthesis remains uncertain. Its incorporation into full TSA systems also remains largely unexplored.

Polyethylene glenosphere

Conventional bearing surfaces in rTSA mirror those of aTSA, with a metal “ball” component and a polyethylene “socket.” More recently, inverted-bearing rTSA implants, featuring a larger-diameter polyethylene glenosphere and a metal (or ceramic) humeral liner, have been introduced to reduce scapular notching and prevent polyethylene-induced osteolysis (Fig. 6).33,41 Scapular notching, a complication unique to rTSA, results from mechanical abutment between the humeral component's polyethylene liner and the inferior scapular neck, leading to both mechanical erosion and polyethylene debris-induced osteolysis. Increasing the polyethylene glenosphere diameter reduces liner-to-bone contact, while the inversion of materials prevents the generation of polyethylene debris.12 Research suggests that while scapular notching may still occur with this design, it is distinct from notching associated with traditional bearing surfaces and does not significantly impact clinical outcomes at mid-term follow-up.41

A retrospective review of nearly 300 patients found that scapular notching of any grade occurred less frequently with polyethylene glenospheres (11.1%) compared with conventional metal glenospheres (25%) at a 2-year follow-up.36 The study also reported smaller notch sizes and no polyethylene glenosphere failures.36 However, a substantial number of patients were lost to follow-up beyond 2 years, limiting conclusions regarding long-term notching differences. Overall, the study concluded that larger polyethylene glenospheres had a safety profile comparable to that of conventional metal glenospheres.36

In a multicenter mid-term study, patients with scapular notching following inverted-bearing rTSA showed similarly favorable clinical outcomes and implant survival when compared to those without notching.41 The hard metal humeral liner demonstrated a unique pattern of notching due to mechanical abrasion, with minimal evidence of polyethylene wear, suggesting a potential advantage in reducing the risk of polyethylene wear-induced osteolysis.41 A 2024 study by Ranieri et al.65 further supported these findings, assessing 78 patients at a mean follow-up of 10 years. The study observed a revision rate of only 1.3%, with no cases of late glenoid or humeral loosening.65 Although scapular notching was present in over half of the patients, only 1 case of grade 3 notching was reported.65

Polyethylene glenospheres have been available internationally for some time. In the United States, the first rTSA with an inverted-bearing surface was implanted in 2021, following FDA approval of the Systema Multiplana Randelli Stemless Reverse Shoulder System by Lima Corporate in 2020. Current literature suggests inverted-bearing designs are a safe and effective option for rTSA. However, without more long-term comparative studies, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding the advantages inverted-bearing rTSA may offer over conventional designs.

Future innovations

Future innovations in TSA bearing surfaces aim to further enhance the durability and reduce wear at the articulating surface. Advancements in hip and knee arthroplasty offer valuable insights into potential developments for shoulder replacements. In total hip arthroplasty, polyether-ether-ketone has demonstrated a capacity to reduce wear particle production and minimize stress shielding effects in stemmed implants.46 Poly 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (PMPC) is being investigated for its ability to mimic articular cartilage.34 However, PMPC's hydrophilic nature limits its durability, as it is prone to shearing off during frictional wear.34,43 It has been shown that the load-bearing performance of PMPC can be enhanced by creating a lubrication environment. By using free PMPC and Cu2+ ions in neonatal bovine serum, wear was reduced by 45% and PMPC shedding minimized—a promising finding for future joint arthroplasty applications.43

Polycrystalline diamond is also gaining attention in joint arthroplasty for its ability to mitigate osteolysis from polymers, metallosis from metals, and fracture failure in ceramics.3 Polycrystalline diamond has demonstrated superior hardness, a low friction coefficient, and resistance to mechanical damage in computational simulations of total hip prostheses.3 Meanwhile, oxidized zirconium alloy has been explored in total knee and hip arthroplasty due to having a smooth articulating surface similar to ceramic, but improved resistance to fatigue.61 However, clinical and simulator trials have reported variable outcomes regarding any advantages in wear rates compared to conventional CoCr.25,44

Conclusion

The evolution of bearing surface technology in TSA has been shaped by advancements in material science aimed at improving wear resistance, reducing complications, and enhancing implant longevity. Cross-linked and antioxidant-stabilized polyethylene have significantly improved polyethylene durability, while CoCrMo-on-polyethylene remains the gold standard TSA interface. In rTSA, inverted-bearing designs with polyethylene glenospheres have been introduced to address scapular notching and polyethylene debris-induced osteolysis, though long-term data are still emerging. Additionally, alternative bearing surfaces such as pyrocarbon, now FDA-approved for hemiarthroplasty, and ceramic humeral heads have demonstrated promising wear properties in early studies, and their longer-term clinical benefits are beginning to be published in the literature. As discussed in this review, future evolution of biomaterials can stem from enhancements to existing materials, the introduction of novel materials, and the exploration of different bearing surface combinations. Moving forward, future research and development in TSA should continue to explore how bearing surface properties can be optimized for the shoulder's unique biomechanical demand.
