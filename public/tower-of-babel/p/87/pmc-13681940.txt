Effects of EEG neurofeedback in adults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a systematic review of randomised and non-randomised studies
Authors: Małgorzata Rotkiewicz, Yassine Yachou, Sofia Charef, Noomane Bouaziz, Dominique Januel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacotherapy is recommended as the first-line treatment for adult attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), yet some individuals show partial or inadequate response associated with adverse effects. These limitations have prompted interest in adjunctive or alternative interventions. Electroencephalography (EEG) neurofeedback is a non-pharmacological intervention for ADHD, which enables self-regulation of neural activity. This systematic review synthesised clinical, cognitive, and electrophysiological outcomes of EEG neurofeedback in adult ADHD, and examined standard versus personalised training protocols.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, four databases (PubMed, ScienceDirect, Ovid, Web of Science) were searched from 1960 to October 2025 for this PROSPERO-registered review (ID: CRD420251020898). Eligible studies included adults (≥ 18 years) with a formal ADHD diagnosis undergoing EEG neurofeedback. Outcomes comprised symptom severity, cognitive performance, electrophysiology, and adverse events. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 and ROBINS-I V2.ResultsSeven studies (three RCTs, four non-randomised; n = 390) met inclusion criteria. Protocols included theta/beta (n = 1), slow cortical potential (n = 1), alpha downregulation training (n = 1) and personalised approaches guided by individual alpha peak frequency (n = 3) or QEEG profiles (n = 1). Naturalistic and personalised studies reported large symptom reductions, whereas RCTs revealed small, generally non-significant effects relative to controls. Cognitive improvements were inconsistently reported and largely attributable to practice effects. Electrophysiological changes were protocol-specific without convergence across studies. No treatment-related adverse events were observed.ConclusionsEvidence remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, small samples, variable controls, inconsistent learning metrics, and risk of bias. Current RCTs do not demonstrate evidence of efficacy over placebo. Blinded, adequately powered trials using standardised learning measures are needed to clarify mechanisms of neurofeedback.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11689-026-09720-1.Keywords: Neurofeedback, ADHD, EEG biomarkers, Neuromodulation, Neurotherapy

Background

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder defined by persistent inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity associated with functional impairment [1]. Moderate to severe symptoms cause substantial difficulties in social, academic, and occupational settings reducing overall quality of life [2, 3]. While childhood ADHD often manifests as forgotten homework, misplaced toys, or disruptive play, these behaviours frequently persist into adulthood in altered forms, such as missed deadlines, lost documents, and chronic restlessness.

Longitudinal research indicates ADHD persists into adulthood in approximately 50–70% of cases, with a global prevalence of 3.10% [4–7]. However, the developmental continuity of ADHD remains debated, as some adults meeting diagnostic criteria lack clear evidence of childhood symptoms or genetic risk markers, raising the possibility of distinct aetiological pathways [8, 9]. Nevertheless, retrospective adult assessments rely heavily on self-report, increasing the likelihood that childhood symptoms went unrecognised.

Neurobiological models highlight dysregulated dopaminergic neurotransmission within the fronto-striatal and fronto-cerebellar circuits. Alterations in dopamine receptors, transporters, and signalling efficiency are thought to impair attention, reward processing, and inhibitory control [10–12]. Genetic and neuroimaging evidence links dopamine-related polymorphisms to differences in neural activation patterns, which helps explain the clinical efficacy of stimulant medications that increase extracellular dopamine levels [13–16]. Consequently, clinical guidelines recommend pharmacotherapy as the first-line treatment for adult ADHD [17]. For example, stimulants enhance dopaminergic transmission and reduce core symptoms across age groups [16–22].

Despite the clinical efficacy of pharmacotherapy, approximately 20–40% of adults respond partially or inadequately [3]. This is associated with elevated risks of suicidal behaviour and premature mortality, mostly attributed to accidents [23–25]. Pharmacological treatments are also limited by adverse effects, including headaches, insomnia, cardiovascular changes, and exacerbated anxiety, contributing to high discontinuation rates [20, 22, 26–32]. Stimulants also carry risks of misuse, and long-term consequences of sustained adult pharmacotherapy remain uncertain [16, 27, 33–35]. Furthermore, adult treatment response is shaped by prolonged functional impairments, maladaptive coping strategies, and comorbid psychopathology [36, 37]. While non-stimulants may benefit emotional dysregulation or comorbid internalising symptoms, sex-related differences in symptom presentation and comorbidity highlight the need for complementary interventions addressing cognitive and self-regulatory processes beyond symptom suppression [38, 39].

Neurofeedback in ADHD

Neurofeedback (NFB) is a non-invasive brain-computer interface enabling self-regulation of neural activity through real-time feedback, promoting voluntary control over typically involuntary physiological processes [40–42]. Grounded in operant conditioning, NFB trains modulation of neural oscillations to improve cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and clinical symptoms [43, 44]. Nevertheless, interpreting NFB outcomes remains complicated by substantial variability in protocol design, control conditions, and outcome measures, leaving the mechanistic specificity of the intervention debated [45–47].

Electroencephalography (EEG) studies in ADHD frequently report increased frontal theta power and reduced beta activity, interpreted as markers of cortical under-arousal and early information processing deficits [40, 48–53]. These findings led to the development of the theta/beta ratio (TBR) as a potential biomarker of attentional dysregulation and a commonly used protocol, with early studies reporting high diagnostic sensitivity and sustained symptom reductions in paediatric ADHD samples [7, 54–58].

However, subsequent research revealed considerable heterogeneity, showing elevated TBR only in a subset of individuals, and noting distinct oscillatory profiles in adults [8, 59, 60]. Apparent theta activity increase may also reflect a slowed individual alpha peak frequency (iAPF) rather than genuine theta excess [61]. Misclassifying this stable marker of thalamo-cortical dynamics may obscure neurophysiological subtypes and limit treatment specificity [62–66]. Consequently, TBR is increasingly viewed as a subgroup-specific feature rather than a universal biomarker, underscoring the importance of personalised NFB approaches.

In addition to TBR, two NFB approaches used in ADHD are sensorimotor rhythm (SMR) and slow cortical potential (SCP) regulation. SMR targets activity in the 12–15 Hz range over the sensorimotor cortex and is linked to improved attentional stability and behavioural control, possibly via thalamo-cortical regulation and increased sleep spindle activity [67, 68]. SCP trains self-regulation of slow voltage shifts reflecting cortical excitability states, thereby supporting top-down control of arousal and attention. Meta-analyses indicate that both approaches can reduce ADHD symptoms, although effects vary with methodological rigour [55, 58].

Despite growing interest, most NFB research focuses on children and adolescents, leaving a critical gap in the adult evidence base. To our knowledge, no systematic review has synthesised clinical, cognitive, and electrophysiological outcomes of EEG-based NFB specifically in adults with ADHD. This review aims to integrate and evaluate current evidence on EEG NFB in adults with ADHD following both personalised and standard training protocols.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [69], and prospectively registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251020898). The PRISMA checklist is provided in the Appendix.

Search strategy

A literature search covering January 1960 to October 2025 was conducted by the first author across PubMed, ScienceDirect, Ovid (MEDLINE), and Web of Science. The following Boolean operators and syntax were applied: (“attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder” OR “ADHD”) AND (“neurofeedback” OR “EEG biofeedback”). In PubMed, the strategy was supplemented with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH): “Attention Deficit Disorder with Hyperactivity”[Mesh] AND “Neurofeedback”[Mesh]. These terms were selected to maximise sensitivity while retaining relevance to EEG-based NFB interventions. Given the limited literature on adults, the search was not restricted to randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Reference lists of relevant reviews and meta-analyses were manually screened to identify additional studies that may have been missed due to variation in terminology.

Eligibility criteria

Included studies met the following criteria: (1) publication in a peer-reviewed journal in English, (2) inclusion of participants with a primary ADHD diagnosis according to established diagnostic systems (e.g., DSM-IV or DSM-5), (3) inclusion of adults (≥ 18 years), either as an exclusive cohort or as a distinct, separable subgroup within mixed-age studies, (4) use of EEG-based NFB as the primary intervention, and (5) reporting outcomes related to ADHD symptoms, cognitive performance, or electrophysiological measures.

Studies were excluded if they lacked formal ADHD diagnosis, were restricted to paediatric populations, used non-EEG modalities (e.g., fMRI-based NFB), focused on unrelated outcomes, or did not report original empirical data. Our methodology was designed to evaluate the currently accessible evidence base while maximising reproducibility. Therefore, all extracted data were sourced exclusively from published materials or open repositories.

Outcome measures and data extraction

All records were imported into Zotero reference management software (version 7.0.15), and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full-text eligibility assessment was performed independently by the first and second authors. Publications reporting on overlapping samples were consolidated into single study records.

Data extraction was conducted by the first author and independently checked for accuracy by the second author, with discrepancies resolved by the senior author. Extracted variables included demographics, diagnostic criteria, protocol characteristics, control conditions, primary and secondary outcome measures, medication status, and reported adverse events. Adverse events were defined as any unfavourable medical occurrence during the study period, including but not limited to headache, nausea, or dizziness.

The primary outcome was defined as change in ADHD symptom severity from baseline to post-intervention, encompassing both inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity domains measured using validated rating scales. Secondary outcomes included cognitive performance, defined as changes in processing speed and working memory measured by standardised neuropsychological tests, and electrophysiological measures, reflecting shifts towards normative oscillatory patterns, providing complementary evidence of intervention effects beyond self-report. Quantitative data were extracted from abstracts, full-texts, tables, figures, and supplementary files. When multiple measures or time points were reported, all relevant outcomes were extracted, prioritising baseline to post-treatment comparisons. When data were missing or unclear, no imputation was performed and results were extracted as reported.

Risk of bias assessment

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) were assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2), evaluating bias across five domains: the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Non-randomised studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I V2 tool, evaluating seven domains: confounding, classification of interventions, participant selection, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Overall risk of bias was classified as low, moderate, serious, or critical.

Independent full-text verification was implemented to minimise selection bias at the screening stage. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, where eligibility remained uncertain, the article was independently assessed by the senior author. Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed due to the small number of studies.

Statistical analysis

A pooled meta-analysis was not performed due to study heterogeneity and the limited number of eligible trials. All procedures followed recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [70]. Missing standard deviations were derived from 95% confidence intervals using standard formulas, which are fully detailed in the Appendix. In case of multiple control groups, non-EEG NFB comparator arms were pooled to yield a single control estimate. Standardised mean differences (SMD) were calculated using Cohen’s d for both within-subject (pre- to post-intervention) and between-group comparisons based on post-treatment means and standard deviations. For studies with small samples (n < 30), Hedges’ g correction was applied. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional thresholds [71], and 95% CI were computed. No sensitivity analyses were conducted due to the limited number of studies. All analyses utilised Python 3.12 and results were summarised using structured tables.

Results

The search yielded 1,308 records. Following duplicate removal and screening, 44 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, seven studies met the inclusion criteria for narrative synthesis. A PRISMA flow diagram detailing the study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Across studies, a total of 390 adults were enrolled, with 230 participants (mean age = 32.75, range 18–68) allocated to EEG NFB groups. The combined RCT sample size was 224, with 86 individuals assigned to EEG NFB intervention arms (mean age = 36.44, range 22–57). Control groups were included in four studies, employing various comparators, such as sham NFB [74], waiting list [73], and active comparators [72].

Diagnostic criteria and medication

ADHD was diagnosed using validated DSM- or ICD-aligned instruments. Deiber et al. [76] specified 18 combined, six inattentive, and one hyperactive case, while Groeneveld et al. [77] reported ten inattentive, four combined, and one hyperactive subtype. The remaining studies did not differentiate the ADHD subtype. Comorbid disorders were reported in five studies [72, 74, 75, 77, 78], including depression or depressive symptoms, anxiety disorder (N min = 31), avoidant personality disorder (N = 20), antisocial personality disorder (N = 13), dysthymia (N = 2), and eating disorder (N = 2).

All studies included participants on medication (N minimum = 150), primarily treated with methylphenidate. Cowley et al. [73] required stable dosing during intervention, Deiber et al. [76] required discontinuation 24 h prior to assessments, while Schönenberg et al. [74] included only participants with stable or no medication for at least 2 months. In other studies, medication was not controlled for [75, 78] or left unrestricted [77].

Three studies, including two RCTs, employed standard protocols, specifically slow cortical potentials at Cz [72], alpha downtraining at Pz [76], and theta/beta ratio at the Vertex [74]. The duration of one NFB session across studies averaged 32 min (range 20–40 min). Training intensity varied from two sessions per week [74], 2–3 per week [75, 78], 2–5 per week [73], and 1–5 per week [72]. One study applied a single-day 30-minute NFB protocol [76]. Across protocols with multiple sessions, the average number of sessions was 32 (range 30–40). Transfer trials were included in all three RCTs, but absent in non-randomised studies, representing 25% of NFB blocks in each case. Follow-up assessments were completed in two RCTs, both at six months [72, 74].

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes varied across RCTs and non-randomised studies. Barth et al. [72] and Schönenberg et al. [74] observed significant within-group symptom improvements, but NFB was not superior to active or sham controls. Cowley et al. [73] reported significant symptom reductions in the NFB group compared to a waiting list control. Conversely, non-randomised studies consistently reported statistically significant and clinically meaningful symptom reductions across total, inattention, and hyperactivity scores [75, 77, 78]. Additional analyses showed that remitters presented with lower baseline hyperactivity scores [78].

All SMD for each domain of assessment are presented in Table 2. Between-subject treatment effects comparing active interventions with comparators were generally small and centered near zero across all assessed symptom domains (Hedges’ g from − 0.15 to 0.30), with only isolated significant advantages of NFB over sham, and NFB over meta-cognitive therapy in the inattention domain [74]. Within-subject effects, however, were generally moderate to very large over time across domains. Differences were observed for total score as well as for inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, with all confidence intervals excluding zero. Similarly, in Cowley et al. [73] moderate time effects for inattention (g = 0.69) were found, while the effects for hyperactivity did not reach significance.

Cognitive outcomes

Regarding cognitive assessments, Barth et al. [72] reported significant improvements in attention (d2-R) and intelligence tasks (CFT-20-R) across all groups, whereas Schönenberg et al. [74] demonstrated significant gains in complex attention (INKA) and flexibility (TAP) over time, although no group-by-time interactions were found. Cowley et al. [73] found no NFB effects on continuous performance test (CPT) or digit span performance.

Among non-randomised studies, Deiber et al. [76] observed improved CPT performance following a single NFB session, while Groeneveld et al. [77] reported significant improvements in Full Scale Attention Quotients, a broad composite scale that includes measures of speed, focus, and vigilance of auditory as well as visual input, but not in Response Control Quotients on the IVA (The Integrated Visual and Auditory Continuous Performance Test) assessment.

Electrophysiological outcomes

Electrophysiological data also showed heterogeneous effects. Barth et al. [72] reported reduced reaction times and P300 amplitudes across all groups without treatment-specific effects. Schönenberg et al. [74] found region- and condition-specific TBR changes, but no treatment-by-time interactions. Among non-randomised studies, Deiber et al. [76] found reduced baseline alpha power in ADHD compared to controls, followed by a significant post-training rebound in the ADHD group. Groeneveld et al. [77] demonstrated statistically significant normalisation in 18 out of 21 QEEG parameters. Arns et al. [75] reported significant increases in N200 and P300 amplitudes post-treatment, alongside reduced SMR power, while alpha and beta powers remained unchanged. Krepel et al. [78] observed shorter P300 latencies among female remitters and lower iAPF among male remitters.

Adverse events (AEs)

Three RCTs addressed safety monitoring and no treatment-related AEs were reported. Barth et al. [72] minimised risks of AEs by obtaining pre-treatment medical histories and ensuring the presence of a research assistant during measurements. Shonenberg et al. [74] reported no AEs but did not specify their monitoring methods. Finally, Cowley et al. [73] deferred AEs analysis to a follow-up paper, which ultimately omitted this data. The remaining four studies did not report safety protocols.

Discussion

This review evaluated the efficacy of EEG-based NFB in adult ADHD. Overall, current evidence does not robustly demonstrate efficacy beyond control conditions. Although non-randomised trials generally show large symptom reductions, RCTs report smaller, predominantly insignificant effects. This discrepancy likely reflects expectancy effects, limited blinding, and uncontrolled confounders, most notably concurrent pharmacotherapy, inherent to naturalistic designs. Additionally, only one RCT was judged at low risk of bias, underscoring the fragility of the evidence base [74].

Emerging findings suggest baseline neurophysiological characteristics may moderate treatment response. For example, individuals with elevated TBR appear to benefit more from TBR training [82]. Yet, conventional theta definitions (4–8 Hz) risk overlap with lower alpha frequencies, potentially disrupting alpha-mediated top-down inhibitory control [63, 83]. Tailoring NFB parameters, such as frequency bands and electrode placement, to individual features (e.g., using iAPF) seems to enhance cognitive and clinical efficacy [45, 84–86]. Despite these promising indications, most controlled adult trials fail to demonstrate clear superiority over active or sham controls, suggesting that non-specific factors, such as expectancy, motivation and therapeutic context, account for substantial benefits. Self-reported inattention is particularly susceptible to these effects, highlighting the need for blinded, clinician-rated assessments and objective behavioural measures [73].

Clinical improvements also extended beyond core symptoms to comorbid conditions [77, 78], For example, Arns et al. (2012) observed a significant effect of time on the Beck Depression Inventory, suggesting reductions in comorbid depressive symptoms following NFB. These findings raise the possibility that NFB exerts transdiagnostic effects rather than disorder-specific benefits [87], but this hypothesis would require direct empirical testing in future studies. One study noted a reduction in stimulant use from 23.1% pre-treatment to 12.8% post-treatment, although the absence of long-term follow-up limits interpretation [77].

Cognitive and electrophysiological improvements were observed across both NFB and control conditions, likely indicating practice effects rather than protocol-specific learning [74]. Deiber et al. [76] showed that alpha down-regulation improved perceptual and motor processes, supporting its role in inhibitory control in adult ADHD. Although both ADHD and control participants successfully down-regulated alpha, only the ADHD group showed a post-training resting-state rebound, potentially reflecting partial neurophysiological normalisation. Early ERP changes, including P3 modulation following a single session, suggest that NFB can influence attentional resource allocation [76], although habituation effects were also observed [72]. Cowley et al. [73] found no transfer of NFB training to CPT performance, whereas Schönenberg et al. [74] reported no change in TBR despite clinical gains, indicating a dissociation between EEG markers and symptomatic improvement. Combined NFB and HRV training improved sustained attention, but the contribution of HRV remains unclear [77].

A notable confounding variable in evaluating intervention efficacy across the reviewed literature is medication variability. Most studies lacked systematic monitoring or standardised reporting of medication use, precluding assumptions of stable dosing regimens throughout the intervention period. Furthermore, when medication was permitted, pharmacological treatments varied substantially across participants. As noted by Clarke et al. [88] in a study of children, psychostimulants may facilitate the learning process by normalising physiological arousal and enhancing task engagement, while simultaneously masking the independent effects of NFB by reducing baseline symptom severity and creating ceiling effects [88]. Given that different pharmacological agents exert distinct effects on EEG activity, disentangling medication-related influences from NFB-induced neuroplastic changes would require a dedicated evaluation of pharmacotherapy effects on specific EEG measures, which falls beyond the scope of this review. Future long-term studies should stratify participants by medication and comorbidity status, or include unmedicated cohorts where appropriate, to better isolate the therapeutic mechanisms underlying NFB.

Neurofeedback learning

Learning success varied considerably and was inconsistently linked to clinical outcomes. Barth et al. [72] considered learning as the capacity to differentiate between up- and down-regulation during the transfer condition, yielding a 30.8% learning rate and noting hyperactivity improvements exclusively within this subgroup. Cowley et al. [73] modelled learning using, , with a positive individual growth curve slope x indicating successful learning, yielding a 47.8% success rate. However, successful learning did not uniformly translate into behavioural gains, as learners exhibited increased omission errors and greater response time variability, and may therefore not constitute a prerequisite for symptomatic improvement [80]. Poor reporting on learning strategies and uncontrolled confounders in the remaining studies substantially constrain mechanistic interpretation.

Limitations

Several limitations of this review should be acknowledged. The small number of eligible studies and RCTs precluded robust moderator analyses and formal publication bias assessments. The inclusion of non-randomised studies, while increasing ecological validity, introduced bias susceptibility. Furthermore, two studies [75, 78] mixed children and adult cohorts, potentially affecting generalisability. Given the scarcity of adult-specific data and that no significant interaction effects were observed between age group and treatment outcomes, these studies were retained in the overall analysis. Finally, definitions of learning success and outcome measures were highly heterogeneous across domains, limiting cross-study comparability.

Conclusions

High-quality RCT data currently suggests that within-group improvements in adult ADHD following NFB are heavily driven by non-specific factors rather than targeted neurophysiological modulation. Methodological limitations, a reliance on self-report, and high variability in learning success preclude definitive conclusions regarding its specific efficacy. Future research must prioritise adequately powered, rigorously blinded RCTs utilising standardised outcomes and individualised EEG protocols. Establishing reliable predictors of successful learning and evaluating cost- and time- effectiveness against standard treatments remain essential steps for integrating NFB into evidence-based frameworks for adult ADHD.
