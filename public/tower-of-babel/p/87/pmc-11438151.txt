Inequities in sleep duration and quality among adolescents in Canada
Authors: Jessica Mitchell, Megan J. Magier, Markus J. Duncan, Jean-Philippe Chaput, Valerie Carson, Guy Faulkner, Emily Belita, Leigh M. Vanderloo, Negin A. Riazi, Rachel E. Laxer, Sarah Carsley, Scott T. Leatherdale, Karen A. Patte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral recent global events may have impacted adolescent sleep and exacerbated pre-existing disparities by social positions (i.e., social roles, identity or sociodemographic factors, and/or group memberships that are associated with power and oppression due to the structures and processes in a given society at given time). Current understanding of sleep among adolescents is critical to inform interventions for a more equitable future, given the short and long-term consequences of inadequate sleep on health and well-being. This study aimed to provide contemporary evidence on sleep disparities by key social positions among adolescents in Canada.MethodsCross-sectional analyses were conducted using self-reported data collected during 2020–2021 (the first full school year after the COVID-19 pandemic onset) from 52,138 students (mean [SD] age = 14.9 [1.5]) attending 133 Canadian secondary schools. Multiple regression models were used to test whether sleep quality (how well students slept during past week), duration (weekday, weekend, weighted daily average), and guideline adherence (8–10 h/day) differed by sex and gender, race and ethnicity, and socioeconomic status (SES).ResultsFemales reported a mean [95% CI] difference of -1.7 [-3.7, 0.4] min/day less sleep on weekdays than males, but 7.1 [4.5, 9.6] min/day more sleep on weekends, resulting in no difference in average daily sleep between males and females. Females were less likely to report good quality sleep compared to males (AOR = 0.57 [0.54, 0.60]). SES followed a generally monotonic trend where higher scores were associated with more sleep on weekdays (Δhighest: lowest = -28.6 [-39.5, -17.6]) and weekends (Δhighest: lowest = -17.5 [-3.8, -31.2]) and greater likelihood of higher sleep quality (AORhighest: lowest = 3.04 [2.35, 3.92]). Relative to White adolescents, weekday and average daily sleep duration were lower among all other racial identities; mean differences ranged from ∼ 5–15 min/day, with Black students reporting the least sleep.ConclusionsDifferences in sleep duration and quality were most profound among adolescents from the lowest and highest SES. Racial disparities were more evident on weekdays. Compensatory weekend sleep appears more pronounced in females than males. Addressing sleep inequities is critical, as a robust predictor of multiple health outcomes.

Introduction

Contemporary evidence on adolescent sleep patterns and inequities is needed. Sleep is essential for adolescents’ health, development, and daily functioning, yet many young people do not obtain high quality or sufficient sleep [1, 2, 4]. While the ideal amount and timing of sleep may vary from one person to another, the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines recommend that adolescents aged 14–17 years accumulate 8–10 h of uninterrupted sleep per day and keep consistent bed- and wake-up times [3]. About one-third of adolescents do not meet these recommendations, with the majority falling short and few exceeding the guidelines [4]. Consequences of short sleep during adolescence are wide-ranging, involving social, physical, and mental health outcomes, such as increased likelihood of depressive symptoms [5], risk-taking behaviours such as alcohol use [6], impairment of academic functioning [7], and early markers of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [8]. Adolescents that report long sleep may also exhibit declines in functioning (e.g., mood disturbances, fatigue, suicide ideation) [9].

Weekday sleep duration tends to decline over adolescence, largely as a result of later bedtimes [4]. A natural circadian shift towards delayed sleep onset occurs at puberty, alongside increasing social demands (e.g., evening extracurricular activities, part-time employment, homework) and reduced parental monitoring, yet school start times typically prohibit sleeping in [10]. Many adolescents compensate for any shortfall during the week by catching up on weekends with later bed- and wake-up times and longer total sleep times [4]. The discrepancy between school and free days, or social and biological time, is often referred to as social jetleg and has been adversely associated with mental health, cognitive functioning, and academic performance, particularly among adolescent girls [11, 12]. However, the sleep literature has typically failed to distinguish between weekday and weekend sleep, even though weekly sleep patterns may be just as important as total volume of sleep [6]. For example, in a cross-sectional survey of 10,123 US adolescents 13–18 years old, mood disturbance was associated with shorter sleep time on weekdays and oversleeping on weekends [13]. Furthermore, the majority of sleep research focuses on duration and clinical disorders, with relatively few population-level studies examining sleep quality.

Sleep patterns have been found to vary by social positions (i.e., social roles, identity or sociodemographic factors, and/or group memberships that are associated with power and oppression due to the structures and processes in a given society at given time) and sleep has been suggested as one mechanism through which sociopolitical and economic contexts impact health outcomes [14–16]. For instance, in North American samples, adolescents living in lower socioeconomic status (SES) households experienced shorter and poorer quality sleep, relative to those in higher SES households [15, 16, 17]. Black adolescents are also at higher risk of short sleep relative to their White counterparts [18–20]. Evidence is less consistent on sleep disparities by sex and/or gender; in Canadian adolescents, males were more likely to report adequate sleep compared to females [15]. While negligible research has explored sleep among transgender and gender diverse adolescents, existing evidence suggests they are more likely to report shorter sleep durations, poor sleep quality, and insomnia symptoms, relative to their cisgender peers [21, 22].

Several recent global events may have impacted adolescent sleep and exacerbated pre-existing disparities by social positions. In particular, the emergence of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and enacted public health measures (e.g., school closures and shifts to virtual learning and cohort models, closure of nonessential businesses and recreational facilities, cancelled extracurricular activities) disrupted the daily lives of adolescents worldwide. Across Canada, emergency remote learning was enacted in spring 2020, and in the 2020–2021 school year, partial school closures continued at times and in areas with high case counts, disproportionately impacting racialized and lower SES populations [23]. The early pandemic response has been linked to both positive and negative changes in adolescents’ sleep, including delayed sleep and wake times, increased nightmares, and longer sleep duration [24]. In Canadian youth aged 12–17 years, average sleep duration was longer and daytime sleepiness was reduced compared to before the pandemic, with 72% meeting sleep duration guidelines during the early COVID-19 outbreak [25]. It remains unclear whether these patterns continued into the prolonged and post pandemic period, as limited research has examined adolescent sleep after the initial lockdown and full school closures.

Current understanding of sleep among adolescents is critical to inform interventions for a more equitable future, given the short and long-term consequences of inadequate sleep on health and well-being [6–9]. Thus, in a large sample of secondary school students in Canada, this study aimed to assess differences in sleep quality, and weekday, weekend, and average sleep time, during the 2020–2021 school year (as the first full school year after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic) by student race and ethnicity, sex and gender, and SES.

Methods

Design and data collection

This study is a secondary analysis using cross-sectional data from the Cannabis, Obesity, Mental health, Physical activity, Alcohol, Smoking, and Sedentary behaviour (COMPASS) study [26] collected during the 2020–2021 school year. The ongoing COMPASS study collects data annually from a rolling cohort of students attending a convenience sample of secondary schools across Canada. The COMPASS study has received ethical approval from the University of Waterloo Human Research Ethics Committee (ORE#30118), Brock University Research Ethics Committee (REB#18–099), CIUSSS de la Capitale-Nationale–Université Laval (#MP-13-2017-1264), University of Alberta Research Ethics Office (Project #00040729), University of British Columbia (reference number H17-00167), and participating school boards. Additional details regarding study methods can be found online (www.compass.uwaterloo.ca).

As school delivery modes were heterogenous across participating schools for the 2020-21 school year due to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic, COMPASS student questionnaires were administered online; schools emailed students an initial survey link and one reminder. All students attending participating schools were invited to participate using active-information passive-consent parental permission protocols, which are critical for collecting robust data among youth [27]. Students could decline to participate at any time.

Measures

Sleep duration and guideline adherence

For both weekdays and weekend days, participants were asked to indicate “During the past week, what time have you usually turned out the light and gone to sleep?” and “During the past week, what time have you usually woken up in the morning?” using a combination of hours (1–12), minutes (00/15/30/45) and meridiem period (AM/PM). Self-reported sleep- or bedtimes and wake times are a common methodology for capturing sleep data in children and youth, with correlations with actigraphy often above 0.4 [28]. The difference between wake and sleep-time was used to calculate typical weekday and weekend sleep duration; possible scores ranged from 0 to 1425 min. A weighted average (5 weekdays, 2 weekend days) was used to calculate an average daily sleep duration. Outliers for weekday, weekend, and average sleep were identified as values outside the mean ± 3 SD rounded to the nearest quarter hour, which still captures the non-outlier range; outliers were winsorized to this accepted range [29] (accepted ranges of M-3SD to M + 3SD: weekday = 180–840, weekend = 165–915, and average = 225–810 min/day).

Weekday, weekend, and average daily sleep guideline adherence was determined based on Canada’s 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for youth aged 14–17 years, i.e., an average of 8–10 h of sleep per day. Individuals were classified as meeting, under sleeping or oversleeping.

Sleep quality

Perceived sleep quality was rated with a single item: “During the past week, how would you rate your sleep quality overall (how well you sleep)?”, with response options of “Very good”, “good”, “bad” and “very bad”. Due to violations in the proportional odds assumption, responses were dichotomized as “good” or “bad”. Comparable single-item self-report measures of sleep quality have been included in national surveillance studies (with response options also dichotomized) [30], adapted from items included in longer multidimensional scales that are not feasible in surveillance large sample studies (e.g., the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Scale [31, 32]). Single-item and subjective sleep quality measures have demonstrated validity and reliability, and been deemed suitable for studying relationships and comparisons among diverse populations in such large surveys [33, 34].

Variables of interest

Individual demographic variables available for comparison included sex and gender, race and ethnicity, and SES in the school region. Sex and gender responses included: male, female, I describe my gender in a different way, and I prefer not to say; the latter two responses were combined into a single category due to small cell sizes. Note that the variable was labelled “sex and gender” in this manuscript moving forward as the question does not specify which is being asked (“are you female or male?”); although one response option specifies gender (“I describe my gender differently”), it is not clear whether adolescents interpret “male” and “female” response options as sex or gender, but these are often regarded as sex terms. Race and ethnicity responses allowed individuals to select all that apply of: Asian, Black, Latin American/Hispanic, White or Other (relabelled Another); individuals who identified multiple racial and/or ethnic identities were recategorized as “Another”. The variable was referred to as race and ethnicity, given that response options include both and to avoid suggestion that the terms are interchangeable. Consistent with previous research [35], a SES composite score was derived from four items: (i) whether they sometimes go to bed hungry because there is not enough money to buy food (yes = 0, no = 1); (ii) perceived familial financial comfort relative to an average student in their class (more comfortable = 2, as comfortable = 1, less comfortable = 0); (iii) whether they have their own bedroom (yes = 1, no = 0); and, (iv) whether they were worried about their family paying bills due to COVID-19 (true/mostly true = 0, neutral or don’t know/false/mostly false = 1). Individuals scoring a 0 were reclassified as a 1 due to low frequency (n = 51), resulting in a 5 to 1 scale where 5 represents higher SES and 1 represents lower SES.

Control variables

Models controlled for: province, education year of the student, school mode (in person, online, or blended), urbanicity (rural, small urban, medium urban, or large urban), and regional median annual incomes (≤$40,000 [approximate national median income level [36], $40,001 to $60,000, $60,001 to $80,000, and >$80,000). Regional median income and population density data from Statistics Canada’s 2016 census data were matched based on school postal codes.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted in R v4.6.1. To assess the relationship between sleep outcomes and variables of interest a series of regression models with a random intercept to account for school clustering were generated where sleep outcomes were regressed on confounding variables and variables of interest. Sleep durations were assessed with a linear regression using lme4 [37]. Guideline adherence and sleep quality were assessed with logistic regression using glmmTMB [38]. Categorical predictor variables were dummy coded while ordinal variables were orthogonal polynomial coded to allow for the detection of trends across ordered predictors. Type II analyses of variance tests assessed overall group differences for each variable of interest while controlling for all other variables in the model; therefore any group differences which were detected also took into account other variables of interest before determining significance (e.g. any racial and/or ethnic differences also accounted for SES differences and vice versa). Pairwise post hoc comparison tests were conducted for statistically significant main effects based on estimated marginal means using emmeans [39]. The Benjamini-Hochberg [40] correction for the false discovery rate was used to adjust p-values for multiple post hoc comparisons. Confidence intervals (CI) are adjusted using the Bonferroni method to correct for multiple comparisons.

Sex and gender

While statistically significant, the sleep duration differences between males and females on weekdays and weekends were less than 10 min/day, with females accruing less sleep on weekdays and more sleep on weekends. There was no significant difference in average daily sleep duration, suggesting small differences in sleep time on weekdays may be compensated for on weekends. Female students were slightly more likely than males to meet sleep duration guidelines on average (AOR = 1.06 [1.01, 1.12]) and on weekends (AOR = 1.07 [1.02, 1.12]) but not weekdays. Despite meeting guidelines more frequently, females were less likely to report having good quality sleep than males (AOR = 0.57 [0.54, 0.60]).

Adolescents who reported describing their gender differently or preferred not to report sex/gender had worse sleep on all outcomes compared to their male and female counterparts. Other gender classified adolescents slept approximately 20–30 min/day less than males or females on nearly all days and were approximately half as likely to meet guidelines than on weekdays, weekends, and on average. Other gender classified adolescents were 0.60 [0.53, 0.68] times as likely to report good sleep as females and approximately one third [0.30, 0.39] as likely as males.

Race and ethnicity

Weekday and average daily sleep duration were higher among White adolescents compared to adolescents of all other racial and ethnic identities; additional pairwise comparisons are detailed in Table 4. Mean differences ranged from approximately 5–15 min/day. The largest differences were among Black adolescents, followed by their Latin American/Hispanic and then Asian peers, and finally, adolescents classified as Another racial or ethnic identity. However, differences in weekend sleep duration were not statistically significant.

Sleep guideline adherence followed the same general pattern on weekdays and on the weekly average with White adolescents reporting significantly higher guideline adherence relative to all other racial and ethnic groups. Significant differences between groups on guideline adherence on weekends were also detected, unlike sleep duration. Across all guideline adherence outcomes, Black adolescents were least likely to meet guidelines, followed by Asian, Another classified, Latin American/Hispanic, and White adolescents. Additional pairwise comparisons between racial and ethnic groups are reported in Table 4.

The only significant differences in sleep quality based on race and ethnicity were between Another classified and both Asian and White adolescents, where Another individuals were less likely to report achieving good sleep (AOR = 0.83 [0.71, 0.96] and 0.85 [0.77, 0.93], respectively).

Socioeconomic status

SES followed a generally monotonic trend where higher scores were associated with longer sleep, better guideline adherence, and higher sleep quality; there were indications of a plateau among the highest scores. On weekdays, adolescents with the highest SES score accrued approximately 30 min/day of sleep more than their peers with the lowest SES score and were 1.86 [1.43, 2.42] times as likely to meet sleep guidelines. On weekends the difference in sleep duration between adolescents with the highest and lowest scores was approximately 20 min/day and the highest scoring SES group was 1.54 [1.19, 1.99] times as likely to meet guidelines. The weekly weighted average difference between adolescents with the highest and lowest scores was approximately 24 min/day and the highest scoring SES group was 1.56 [1.20, 2.03] times as likely to meet guidelines. Adolescents with the highest SES score were 3.04 [2.35, 3.92] times as likely to report good sleep compared to those with the lowest SES score. Additional comparisons between SES score levels are described in Table 5.

Discussion

This study aimed to provide contemporary evidence of sleep patterns and inequities among adolescents. In a large sample of secondary school students in Canada, we assessed differences in sleep quality, and weekday, weekend, and average sleep time, during the 2020–2021 school year (as the first full school year after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic) by student race and ethnicity, sex and gender, and SES. The largest sleep disparities resulted when comparing adolescents with the highest and lowest SES scores. Higher SES scores were associated with longer sleep duration, better guideline adherence and a higher likelihood of reporting good quality sleep, with some evidence of a plateau at higher SES scores. Previous literature, before the COVID-19 pandemic, also found that youth from lower SES backgrounds experienced poorer sleep timing, duration and regularity across the week [15–17]. Previous research indicated increases in sleep duration during the COVID-19 pandemic [25], likely related to not having to commute to school in the mornings; these benefits may have been inequitably distributed given evidence of more school closures in areas with higher proportions of lower SES and racialized populations. We found evidence of SES disparities during the pandemic, despite controlling for school area median household income and school learning mode. As opposed to conventional SES measures such as parental education and household income, for which measurement can be challenging in younger populations, our SES score was based on student-level indicators. These items potentially more closely tap into adolescent’s perceptions, understanding, and experiences of their family’s SES. Stress and physiological mechanisms may contribute to links between sleep and their own worries about the family’s ability to pay pills and going to bed hungry due to a lack of money to buy food. Likewise, relative family affluence is an indicator of perceived relative socioeconomic position, rather than absolute SES; developmentally, adolescents can have heightened sensitivity to social inequalities, given their focus on peer acceptance and social comparisons. The fourth item, having one’s own bedroom, likely has benefits for sleep over sharing a bedroom (e.g., quieter, greater control over lights), and may have been particularly beneficial in the context of home confinement during the COVID-19 pandemic. Research has suggested that monetary resources allow families to create environments more conducive to sleep, in terms of comfortable and quiet sleeping spaces, reduced presleep worries, and more consistent bedtime routines, factors which contribute to the relationship between low SES and sleep problems in youth [41].

Racial and ethnic differences were evident on weekdays; in accordance with with previous findings [(18–20)], relative to White adolescents, shorter weekday and average daily sleep durations were reported by adolescents from all other racial and ethnic categories, with Black adolescents reporting the shortest sleep. Consistent with a systematic review including 23 studies that investigated racial and ethnic sleep disparaties in US school-aged children and adolescents, White youth reported more sleep compared to their peers belonging to other racial/ethnic groups, as in Hispanic youth relative to Black youth; Black adolescents tended to have shorter sleep durations and later bedtimes than their Hispanic peers [19]. Evidence among Asian populations was limited and inconsistent, but generally indicated longer sleep than Hispanic and Black youth and shorter sleep than White youth [19]. In contrast, Asian youth in our study reported shorter sleep than their White and Latin American/Hispanic peers, but did not differ in reports of quality from White youth. Experiences of discrimination have been found to contribute to racial disparities in adolescent sleep, with psychological distress mediating these relationships [20, 42]. For instance, a US study of 350 Black, Asian, and Latin American adolescents, that used daily diary and actigraphy measures, found daily discrimination to be associated with longer same-night sleep onset latency, more sleep disturbance, and greater next-day daytime dysfunction and sleepiness [20]. Cultural norms for attitudes towards sleep and sleep patterns may also constribute to differences and a mismatch between reported sleep duration and perceived adequacy of sleep [43].

Sex and gender differences were also found; the shortest and poorest quality sleep was reported by adolescents that described their gender differently or preferred not to report sex and gender. While the “other” gender category represents an amalgam of potential gender identities, the patterns observed are concerning, given that they are accruing approximately 24 min less sleep per day on average than their male and female identifying peers. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that gender diverse adolescents typically report poorer sleep quality [21]. Gender diverse adolescents are more likely to experience early life stress that includes maltreatment, adversity, and social rejection [44, 45]. Each of these stressors are connected with an increased risk of sleep problems which could persist into adulthood [46]. Examining the sleep patterns of transgender and gender diverse adolescents when more nuanced gender information is available is warranted to identify specific groups at risk of poor sleep habits.

Differences between females and males were minor. While females and males accrued the same weekly volume of sleep, females had poorer quality sleep and a greater discrepancy between their weekday and weekend sleep. That is, females slept less on weekdays, but compensated on weekends to achieve the same average daily sleep as males. In addition to nightly sleep duration recommendations, sleep guidelines advise consistent bed and wake times [3]. On average, the pattern of compensating on the weekend for shorter weekday sleep among females relative to males was small (less than 10 min of relative debt summed across weekdays and approximately 15 min of greater sleep across weekend days). However, these results suggest females may be experiencing greater circadian misalignment, or social jetleg [47]. Females are also more prone than males to internalizing symptoms, which can interfere with sleep, and are associated with short weekday sleep and social jetlag [11, 12, 48, 49]. Physiological sex differences may be one contributing factor, whereby adolescent females may experience an earlier onset of decreased slow wave sleep and circadian delay than males [48], contributing to later weekday bedtimes. Alternatively, gendered social roles may impact female sleep time on weekdays (e.g., greater time spent grooming, doing homework, and/or using social media) [50].

Several limitations warrant consideration. The use of self-report measures of sleep subject the results to recall error and social desirability bias. Self-report measures typically overestimate sleep duration compared to objective measures [51]. Large studies with objective sleep measures would be valuable. However, self-report measures are common practice to feasibly obtain data within a large sample and an important way to investigate individuals’ perceptions of their sleep quality. Further, the sleep measures used have demonstrated validity for studying group differences in youth samples [52, 53]. Relatedly, a single-item subjective measure was used to assess sleep quality; while consistent with previous population-level surveys [30] and evidence supports the value of such measures for examining group differences and relationships in large studies [32–34], they may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of sleep quality. Most existing scales are too long to be feasible in large population-level surveys [54]. Sleep duration, timing, and perceived quality are important components of sleep health, and few population-level youth studies have included sleep quality measures; however, other important sleep quality indicators warrant future research, such as sleep continuity, including latency, number of awakenings, and efficiency, and daytime sleepiness [54].

Further research is needed to examine sleep by additional social positions (e.g., also considering sexuality, immigration status, disability), and more nuanced considerations of their intersections. Broad subgroup categorization (e.g., by race [one Asian category]) may have masked important differences. The survey questionnaire measures in the 2020-21 school year limited understanding of transgender and gender diverse adolescents. Lastly, research is also needed to consider how these subgroups interact with different contextual factors, and to better understand the mechanisms contributing to sleep disparities.

Conclusion

Our results highlight inequities in sleep quality, and week day and weekend sleep duration, by social positions among adolescents in Canada during the first full school year after the COVID-19 pandemic onset. These disparities have important short- and long-term implications, and the potential to contribute to and exacerbate future health inequities, given the importance of sleep to countless health outcomes [5–9]. There is a need to address the structural factors contributing to socioeconomic, gendered, and racial disparaties in sleep. In the short term, delaying school start times has been shown to have a positive effect on sleep duration during week days [55], and may be particularly beneficial for adolescents belonging to equity denied populations, based on evidence of shorter weekday sleep times.
