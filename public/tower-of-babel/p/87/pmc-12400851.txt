Correlation between balanced fast field echo sequence and intraoperative findings in the surgical treatment of an intradural spinal arachnoid cyst: illustrative case
Authors: Audrey Huang, Jessica Dorilio, Hasit Mehta, Jared M. Pisapia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

While MRI is the standard for diagnosis, traditional sequences may not clearly define cyst borders and septations, which are important for guiding surgical intervention. Spinal arachnoid cysts are rare accumulations of CSF believed to arise from arachnoid adhesions. While the exact cause remains unclear, some theories suggest that these cysts are due to congenital defects. Other etiologies include trauma, inflammation, infection, and iatrogenic procedures as triggers for the arachnoid adhesions that lead to cyst formation.1,2 Most cysts are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging. However, symptoms may occur due to spinal cord compression and can include pain, sensory deficits, weakness, ataxia, and bladder or bowel incontinence.3 Previously, these cysts have been categorized into three types: type I, extradural meningeal cysts without spinal nerve root fibers; type II, extradural meningeal cysts with spinal nerve root fibers, and type III, intradural meningeal cysts.4 They are most often located on the dorsal spinal cord in the thoracic area (80%), and less commonly in the cervical (15%) and lumbar (5%) regions.5 Spinal arachnoid cysts are rare in the pediatric population, with limited cases reported in the current literature.6 Due to their nonspecific symptoms, spinal arachnoid cysts can be difficult to diagnose. Other spinal cord pathologies that can present similarly to spinal arachnoid cysts include spinal cord herniation, multiple sclerosis, arachnoid webs, and other cystic lesions.3,7

The diagnosis and decision to treat spinal arachnoid cysts are made based on symptoms and imaging findings. Imaging diagnosis is typically made using MRI, as it can reveal focal displacement of the spinal cord.8 However, this technique makes it difficult to visualize and distinguish the specific pathology causing the displacement, as spinal arachnoid cysts have the same signal characteristics as CSF.8 CT myelography may offer better visualization of the cyst and its borders, as it allows visualization of the free-flowing CSF between the cyst and the subarachnoid space.3,8 However, it might be difficult to identify pathology using CT myelography, and it comes with limitations due to the invasive nature of the procedure. Balanced fast field echo (B-FFE) is an advanced MRI technique that uses a balanced echo sequence that maintains transverse and longitudinal magnetization by applying a rapid series of radiofrequency pulses.7 It is a type of gradient-echo steady-state coherent MRI with high spatial resolution of pulsatile structures in the CSF space.9 Terminology for this method can vary among manufacturers and includes other terms such as balanced steady-state free precession, FIESTA (fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition), and true fast imaging with steady-state precession. B-FFE results in images that display a combination of T1-weighted and T2-weighted contrasts. The appearance of tissues depends on their ratio of T2 to T1. For example, tissues with higher T2 relative to T1 will appear brighter on imaging. This method provides excellent contrast between CSF and solid structures due to high signal-to-noise ratio without the need for contrast agents.7,10,11

Once a diagnosis is made, intradural cysts are typically excised or fenestrated.1 Accurately distinguishing spinal arachnoid cysts from other pathologies is important for effective management and planning of surgical interventions.7 We report the case of a pediatric patient with a symptomatic intradural spinal arachnoid cyst. The B-FFE sequence helped guide management and correlated with intraoperative findings.

Illustrative Case

A 13-year-old female with a past medical history of obesity, depression, anxiety, migraines, idiopathic peripheral neuropathy, and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis presented to our clinic with several months of bilateral lower extremity tingling and weakness. She also reported gait imbalance, leg and back pain, and, over the last 2 days, intermittent episodes of urinary incontinence. Her symptoms progressively worsened over several weeks leading up to presentation. On physical examination, the patient exhibited decreased sensation to light touch and pinprick on the medial aspect of both lower extremities. The patient also presented with patellar hyperreflexia, bilateral clonus, and a positive Romberg sign. There was no biceps hyperreflexia or Hoffman’s sign. The patient was admitted directly to the hospital from the office on the same day to expedite imaging workup.

The next day, MRI of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spinal regions was performed and it included a B-FFE sequence. An intradural arachnoid cyst was identified dorsal to the spinal cord, extending from C6–7 to the T3–4 levels. Septations delineating the superior and inferior walls of the cyst were visible (Fig. 1A). Imaging demonstrated dorsal compression and ventral displacement of the spinal cord from the cyst, with stable mass effect. The sagittal B-FFE sequence showed flattening of the spinal cord with cord signal hyperintensity, indicating compression (Fig. 1B). A laminectomy was recommended to fenestrate and partially excise the cyst with the aims of alleviating the mass effect and reducing the risk of recurrence. A midline incision was planned from C4 to T4. An anteroposterior radiograph was used for localization. Midline laminectomies were performed at the cranial and caudal ends of the cyst, from the C7 and T3 levels, respectively, with additional laminotomies at C6 and T3 for improved exposure. The cervical region of the cyst was the most compressive; therefore, the dura at this region was opened first. Intraoperative ultrasound was used as an adjunct to help identify the thick underlying arachnoid septations. Opening of the dural layer revealed a thick gray membrane underneath (Fig. 2A and B). The dural opening was extended caudally to address the internal septations noted on preoperative MRI. A change in the thickness and color of the arachnoid layer was noted at the cranial aspect of the incision, consistent with the upper pole of the arachnoid cyst. A large segment of the exposed cyst wall was excised and saved for pathological examination (Fig. 2C). The arachnoid appeared normal and was adherent to the cervical cord in regions of deep exposure. The dural opening was further extended caudally as a single dural opening. Thickened arachnoid was noted at the level of the T2–3 and T3–4 disc spaces, consistent with findings on the B-FFE sequence (Fig. 2D). Fenestration was performed at this level, and a portion of the arachnoid was excised. The caudal extent of the arachnoid cyst was also fenestrated such that the cyst was in open communication with the remainder of the thecal sac. The single dural incision was closed primarily, and soft tissues were closed in a standard layered fashion. The patient was extubated and transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit in stable condition. Her pain was well controlled, and her Foley catheter was removed on postoperative day 1. At the 2-week follow-up, the patient reported improvement in her back pain and balance. She had no further episodes of urinary incontinence. By the 8-week follow-up, she reported return of sensation in her legs, along with significant improvement in strength and coordination. She no longer had gait instability. She denied any neck or back pain. MRI performed at 8 weeks and then at 10 months after surgery showed reduced compression and deformity of the lower cervical to upper thoracic spinal cord, with no focal signal abnormalities (Fig. 3). At her most recent follow-up, 2.5 years postlaminectomy, she reported continued resolution of her preoperative symptoms, with no recurrence of urinary incontinence, lower extremity paresthesias, weakness, or gait instability. Her preoperative hyperreflexia and clonus findings had also resolved. Upright radiographs showed stable spinal alignment with no evidence of delayed kyphosis. Discussion

Observations

Spinal arachnoid cysts are typically diagnosed using MRI. However, because these cysts share signal characteristics with CSF, it can be difficult to differentiate them from other spinal pathologies on standard MRI. In this case report, we demonstrate precise correspondence of arachnoid cyst poles and internal septations with intraoperative findings. Laminectomy and subsequent fenestration of the cyst at the levels identified by B-FFE led to significant clinical improvements, including the resolution of preoperative symptoms, and postoperative imaging showed reduced spinal cord compression with no recurrence of symptoms over a 2.5-year follow-up period.

B-FFE is a valuable technique for visualizing challenging CSF spaces due to its high spatial resolution and ability to enhance contrast between fluid-containing tissues and adjacent regions. These features make it particularly effective for delineating the location of arachnoid cyst borders and internal septations, which might not have been captured as effectively on traditional MRI sequences. In turn, the identification of cyst borders and septations is critical for surgical planning and treatment. The superior and inferior poles, as well as internal septations, such as at the T2–3 and T3–4 levels, that were noted on preoperative imaging were confirmed intraoperatively. Preoperative knowledge of these locations permitted planning the surgical incision at the relevant spine levels and facilitated complete fenestration at both poles of the intradural cyst to accomplish effective fenestration. In addition, both static and cine-mode B-FFE may offer superior diagnostic capabilities for intradural arachnoid cysts. Cine-mode B-FFE, a cardiac-gated sequence, has the ability to monitor CSF pulsations, which is particularly beneficial as disruptions in these pulsations can indicate the presence of arachnoid adhesions in the intradural space.7

Other investigators have demonstrated the utility of B-FFE in the management of CSF disorders. Beyond intradural arachnoid cysts, use of the B-FFE sequence was reported to identify dynamic obstructive arachnoid adhesions not seen on conventional MR modalities that supported tailored surgical treatment.9 These sequences have identified etiologies such as cysts or adhesions in the spine or brain in cases of syringohydromyelia or communicating hydrocephalus otherwise diagnosed as idiopathic.9,12 In addition, B-FFE has demonstrated utility in visualizing the posterior fossa and cranial nerves with minimal susceptibility to pulsation artifact.10 B-FFE has been used to characterize intradural thoracic arachnoid cysts in the adult population.7 In a case series on adult patients, B-FFE was used to identify spinal arachnoid webs, which helped minimize the required surgical exposure for treatment.13 Despite its advantages, B-FFE has limitations. B-FFE is very susceptible to motion artifacts caused by bodily functions such as breathing, swallowing, and cardiac pulsations.7,12 Its sensitivity to magnetic field distortions can cause signal voids in areas with high susceptibility differences such as the skull base, sinuses, or near metal implants like dental work and surgical hardware.14 This limitation may affect the adult population more frequently than children. Use of the sequence requires careful coordination among healthcare providers to minimize image distortion. Additionally, while B-FFE excels at distinguishing fluid-filled spaces, it is less effective for soft tissue details compared with traditional T1- or T2-weighted MRI sequences.14

However, even with its limitations, B-FFE offers distinct benefits over other techniques like CT myelography, which can be invasive and difficult to visualize specific lesions.7 The noninvasive nature of B-FFE imaging and lack of radiation exposure compared with CT myelography is especially relevant to the pediatric population in which intubation and general anesthesia may be required for CT myelography, along with the desire to reduce radiation exposure. Unlike conventional T2-weighted sequences, B-FFE offers a higher signal-to-noise ratio, superior fluid-tissue contrast, and clearer delineation of fluid-filled structures due to its inherent T2/T1 contrast weighting. These benefits are particularly useful in differentiating CSF from structures like arachnoid cysts from adjacent brain parenchyma, subarachnoid spaces, and ventricular CSF, which might not be as clearly defined on traditional sequences. Additionally, B-FFE is less susceptible to flow artifacts, which can further improve visualization of cyst boundaries and potential communication with the ventricular system or subarachnoid space.15

Further research is needed to fully assess B-FFE’s clinical utility. Nonetheless, the application of B-FFE sequences in the treatment of intradural arachnoid cysts is highlighted in the current case.

Lessons

We demonstrate the advantages of using B-FFE imaging for the evaluation of intradural spinal arachnoid cysts. Cyst borders and internal septations noted on pre-operative B-FEE sequences, rather than traditional MRI, were shown to correspond precisely to intraoperative findings. As a less invasive alternative to traditional methods like CT myelography, B-FFE offers superior cyst wall visualization that can improve diagnostic accuracy and guide surgical treatment.
