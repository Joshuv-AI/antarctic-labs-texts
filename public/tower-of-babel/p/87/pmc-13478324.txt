A single-cell atlas of porcine hematopoietic development
Authors: Weihong Gu, Yuhan Wen, Yi Huang, Shah Jungy Ibna Karim, Alyssa May, Laurie Touchard, Wesley C. Warren, John P. Driver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Domestic pigs are both a globally important agricultural species and an increasingly valuable biomedical model that bridges the gap between rodents and non-human primates. Thus, a thorough understanding of the developing porcine immune system is important to improve pig health and advance preclinical research. Here, we coupled single-cell transcriptomic analyses with T and B cell receptor profiling to create a comprehensive cell atlas of pig hematopoietic development across fetal liver, bone marrow, and thymus tissues from fetal to adult life. We highlight tissue-specific and age-related features; identify gene modules underlying B cell and T cell development; describe the maturation and diversification of B and T cell receptor repertoires in bone marrow B cells and thymic αβ and γδ T cells; and distinguish transcriptionally distinct subsets of γδ T cells according to their γ chain usage. Our data provide key insights into pig immune system development and contribute a valuable resource for advancing pigs as a model of human blood and immune diseases.

Introduction

Swine are the most widely farmed large animal in the world, with an estimated 1.5 billion pigs reared annually1. Moreover, similarities between pigs and humans in terms of anatomy, physiology, size, immunology, and genome, as well as the ease with which swine can be genetically edited, have advanced the use of pigs as a biomedical model2. Indeed, pigs are increasingly used to study human congenital diseases, developmental processes, cancer, and host–pathogen interactions. They also have utility as xenotransplant organ donors and as a platform to develop vaccines and therapeutics. However, to make the best use of pig models and to protect commercial swine health requires a thorough understanding of the swine immune system. This should include early hematopoiesis, since immune development during gestation influences a wide range of immunological processes and illnesses that persist beyond birth3.

Swine have a gestation period of 114 days consisting of well-defined development stages, which allows for extrapolation to human fetal development. Pig hematopoiesis begins early in fetal growth, starting with the appearance of innate immune cells, such as macrophages and monocytes, at ~21 days of gestation (DG)4,5. The thymus develops between 22 and 32 DG while lymph nodes and spleen develop between 20 and 55 DG6. Pigs, like other artiodactyls, possess high concentrations of γδ T cells. These are the first T cells to develop in the embryonic thymus, becoming detectable by 45 DG, whereas αβ thymocytes become predominant at 55 DG7. In secondary lymphoid organs, γδ and αβ T cell zones appear at 45 and 55 DG, respectively, with B cell zones detectable as early as 40 DG6,8. B and T lymphocyte development occurs through a phased cell differentiation program alongside somatic recombination of pre-existing variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) gene segments8–12.

To date, most knowledge about porcine hematopoietic development has come from traditional techniques, such as bulk RNA sequencing and protein-based assays, which are limited in their ability to capture the cellular heterogeneity that characterizes hematopoiesis. On the other hand, recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) experiments in traditional laboratory models and humans have provided detailed insights into hematopoietic development ranging from embryogenesis to adulthood13–17. However, because the immune system matures at a rate and manner that is unique to each species, a comprehensive scRNAseq analysis of porcine hematopoietic development is required. Several scRNAseq studies have now characterized pig immune cells across lymphoid and non-lymphoid tissues18–29, with some showing that major cell types are largely conserved between pigs and humans at a transcriptional level18,25,26,30. Nevertheless, a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic characterization of porcine hematopoiesis and immune receptor repertoire formation is still needed.

Here, we applied single-cell transcriptomics to comprehensively profile the molecular and cellular states of pig fetal liver at 41 DG, when the liver is the principal site of porcine hematopoiesis, as well as bone marrow and thymus in fetal, neonatal, and adult stages of development. We coupled our results with a detailed analysis of the αβ and γδ T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor (BCR) repertoire to reconstruct V(D)J recombination kinetics in all three organs. Our results provide important insights into porcine immune system development, which offers an improved understanding of the scope of T- and B-cell diversity and provides a framework for identifying similarities and divergences between pig and human immune responses for a wide range of immune-related diseases.

Results

Overall composition of the developing porcine hematopoietic system

We performed scRNAseq on freshly isolated cells from 4 fetal livers from 41 DG, 18 bone marrow samples ranging from 47 DG to 7 months of age (MOA), the age when pigs reach sexual maturity31, and 15 thymi, ranging from 60 DG to 7 MOA (Fig. 1A, B, and Supplementary Data 1). Fetal liver samples were FACS sorted for CD45+ and CD45- cells, which were then remixed at a 1:1 ratio to increase representation of CD45+ leukocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1A). After quality control, the combined dataset contained a total of 185,658 cells (34,869 fetal liver, 65,247 bone marrow, and 85,542 thymi) with 118,635 mean reads and 2002 median genes per cell (Supplementary Data 1). Cell types were annotated based on the expression of lineage-specific marker genes defined in previously published pig, mouse, and human datasets14,25–27,32,33. Cells grouped first by broad cell type (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Figs. 1B, C, 2, and Supplementary Data 2), then by tissue-specific clusters (Fig. 1D), and finally by age (Fig. 1E), demonstrating that considerably more variation exists between cell types than among the same cell types across tissues or ages. While 41 DG fetal liver and 47 DG bone marrow contained many of the same cell types, the fetal liver was dominated by differentiating erythroid cells while the bone marrow was dominated by myeloid cell types (Fig. 1F). Cellular composition of pig fetal liver

A detailed assessment of the 41 DG fetal liver samples, which coincides with the age when porcine bone marrow is rapidly replacing the fetal liver’s hematopoietic activity, found 28 cell types or cell states according to established lineage markers14,34 (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 3A, and Supplementary Data 2). Hepatocytes and late-stage erythroid cells made up the majority of PTPRC (encoding CD45) negative cells (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 3A). An unsupervised trajectory analysis of HSC differentiation using partition-based approximate graph abstraction (PAGA) agreed with well-established hematopoietic development stages14 (Fig. 2B), whereby HSC/MPP progenitors give rise to three major lineages: (i) MEMPs, which differentiate into mast cell, erythroid, and megakaryocyte lineages, (ii) the myeloid lineage, which consists of two branches—a mononuclear branch that gives rise to monocytes, pDCs, and Kupffer cells, and a granulocytic branch, derived from GMPs, which differentiates into mature neutrophils, and (iii) CLPs, which give rise to B cells and NK cells. To examine the spatial organization of early hematopoietic progenitors and developing myeloid cell types, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on fetal liver samples using antibodies targeting cKit and Iba1. KIT, which encodes cKit, was enriched in HSC/MPP, MEMPs, CLPs, mast cells, and early erythroid progenitors, whereas AIF1, which encodes Iba1, was enriched in neutrophils, monocytes, and Kupffer cells (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Both cKit+ (Fig. 2D) and Iba1+ cells (Fig. 2E) were widely and uniformly distributed throughout the parenchyma.

To compare the pig fetal liver with its human counterpart, we integrated our dataset with existing scRNAseq data from 13 human fetal liver samples taken between 7 and 17 post-conception weeks (PCW), which were also enriched in CD45+ cells14 (Fig. 2F). Based on transcriptome-wide Pearson correlation-based distances, our 41 DG pig fetal liver had the greatest similarity in gene expression to the 14 PCW human fetal liver samples (Supplementary Fig. 3B). While many pig and human clusters overlapped, human samples were enriched in Kupffer cells, hepatocytes, B cells, and NK cells (Fig. 2G). Conversely, pig fetal livers harbored a much larger fraction of late-maturing erythroid cells. They also contained a substantial population of neutrophils, which were rare in human fetal liver, consistent with prior reports that human granulopoiesis begins during bone marrow hematopoiesis rather than in the fetal liver14,36.

In order to compare the expression patterns of genes involved in hematopoiesis in humans and pigs, we conducted a comparative pseudotime analysis of HSC/MPP differentiating to monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3C). We focused on this trajectory since both cell populations were present at comparable levels in both species. We found conserved profiles for genes involved in the cell division cycle (HMGA1, TUBA1B, and TUBB)37–39 and monocyte maturation and activation (CD63, FCER1G, JUNB, NFKBIA, and NLRP3)40–44 (Fig. 2H). However, there were also notable divergences. For example, expression of FTL, IFI30, TIMP1, S100A4, and CD93, which are involved in different aspects of monocyte activation, antigen processing, cell migration, and cell adhesion45–50, steadily increased during human monocyte differentiation but became downregulated in pigs. Conversely, ANXA4, B3GNT2, IL18, ST6GAL1, and GSDMD, which contribute to inflammasome activation, cytokine signaling, and glycosylation51–55 were upregulated during monocyte differentiation in pigs but downregulated in humans (Fig. 2H). These data indicate both conservation and species-specific differences in the monocyte differentiation program between pigs and humans.

The combined dataset, clustered into 30 clusters, comprised of 10 major cell types (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 4A, and Supplementary Data 2). Cell proliferation, driven by cycling erythroid and neutrophil precursors, peaked between 75 and 90 DG (Supplementary Fig. 4B–D). The concentration of HSC/MPP (cluster 1) peaked at 1 DOA, reflecting their highly elevated cell cycle scores at this timepoint (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). Similar findings have been reported for mice56,57. Monoblast-derived cell types, including monocytes (clusters 10–11), monocytes-macrophages (mono-mac, cluster 12), and macrophages (cluster 13), dominated 47 DG bone marrow and decreased as pregnancy progressed (Fig. 3C). Developing B cells made up a substantial portion of fetal and 1 DOA bone marrow, peaking at 60 DG. In contrast, most lymphocytes in 7 MOA samples were T cells, as is the case for adult human bone marrow58,59. Neutrophils in 47 DG bone marrow were predominantly mature, with immature stages appearing as gestation progressed. Seven MOA bone marrow was dominated by developing and immature neutrophils. Erythroid precursor MEMP cells were well represented in postnatal bone marrow but sparse in prenatal samples. Erythroid precursors, which were plentiful during late pregnancy and 1 DOA, were nearly absent from 7 MOA bone marrow, consistent with a known shift from predominantly proliferating to mature-stage erythroid cells in human adult bone marrow60,61.

To better understand early porcine hematopoietic development, we further analyzed HSC/MPPs in fetal liver and bone marrow. Recent studies have demonstrated that not all hematopoietic cells originate from HSCs62–70. Instead, a subset of HSC-independent progenitors, including erythroid–myeloid progenitors (EMPs), lymphoid–myeloid progenitors (LMPs), and embryonic multipotent progenitors (eMPPs), arises during embryonic development and persists into adulthood62–70. Genes commonly used to identify these cell types (ITGA2B, FCGR3A/FCGR2A, IL7R, and CDH5)62,64,70 were barely detected in our samples (Supplementary Fig. 4G), probably due to the rarity of these cells. However, a subpopulation of fetal liver HSC/MPPs were highly enriched in genes that distinguish multipotent long-term (LT)-HSCs (HLF, MECOM/EVI1, MLLT3, HOXA9, EMCN, and FGD5) (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 4H), a type of primitive HSCs, which possess unlimited lifelong self-renewal capacity30,65,69,71–75. On the other hand, bone marrow HSCs appeared more differentiated, especially after birth, with increased expression of FLT3, a canonical marker of MPPs76,77, CXCR4, which mediates HSC homing and retention in the bone marrow57,78, MHC-I genes (SLA-1, SLA-2), which are upregulated as HSCs mature79, and RUNX2, a negative regulator of HSC self-renewal and facilitator of T-cell commitment80 (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 4I). We also computationally traced HSC/MPPs as they differentiated into erythroid, myeloid, and lymphoid lineages (Fig. 3E, F). Fetal liver and bone marrow HSC/MPPs transitioning to myeloid cells were enriched for neutrophil–myeloid progenitor associated genes (MPO, AZU1, PRTN3, and ELANE)14 as well as calcium binding protein genes (S100A8, S100A9, S100A11, and S100A12) (Supplementary Fig. 4J), which promote monocyte differentiation through TLR4/RAGE signaling81. These genes were upregulated in postnatal compared to prenatal bone marrow, consistent with the increase in myeloid cell abundance that occurred after birth. In bone marrow, HSC/MPPs differentiating towards the lymphocyte lineage were enriched for B cell development genes (CD52, MEF2C, BCL2, CD93, and SYK)17,82–85 (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 4J), whereas their enrichment was markedly reduced in fetal liver samples, which contained very few lymphocytes (Supplementary Fig. 4J).

To characterize B cell differentiation in more detail, we further clustered bone marrow B cells from Fig. 3A (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Fig. 5A, and Supplementary Data 2). A trajectory analysis showed B cell progenitor cells progressing through pre-pro-, pro-, and pre-B cell stages, undergoing proliferation, and developing into terminally differentiated immature B cells (Fig. 3H). The 47 DG B cell compartment was skewed towards pre-/pro- B cells; however, the remaining fetal and 1 DOA bone marrow samples presented the full spectrum of B cell development stages (Fig. 3I). The small number of B cells in 7 MOA bone marrow were mostly late-stage immature B cells (clusters 11–12).

Next, we subjected B cells to a GeneTrajectory analysis. This package dissects out gene programs from the whole transcriptome to avoid the noise resulting from the presence of multiple concurrent gene processes that occur in cells, which can conceal a biological process86. Three inferred trajectories were identified, with genes in each trajectory ordered according to their pseudotemporal expression patterns (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Data 3). Trajectory 1, which was most prominent during early B cell differentiation (Fig. 3J, K, and Supplementary Fig. 5B), contained genes that initiate B cell development, including RAG1, RAG2, DNTT, and CD3487,88. Trajectory 2 was composed of genes enriched during the transition between early-stage precursor B cells to late-stage immature B cells, including FABP3 (Fig. 3J, K, and Supplementary Fig. 5B), which promotes the differentiation of IgM-producing plasma cells89, and DYNC2LI1, which supports the proliferation and survival of developing B cells90. Trajectory 3, which was prominent during late-stage B cell differentiation (Fig. 3J, K, and Supplementary Fig. 5B), was comprised of genes known to be involved in immature B cell maturation, including SOX5, KLF2, and LYZ91–93. Together, these findings delineate the sequential waves of gene activation and repression that coordinate B cell differentiation in pigs.

BCR repertoire of bone marrow and fetal liver B lymphocytes

Next, we analyzed the BCR repertoires of our bone marrow and fetal liver samples using pig-specific primers targeting IGK, IGL, IGHM+IGHD, IGHA, and IGHG chains94 (Supplementary Table 1). Since our IGHM+IGHD primer binds a CH1 region common to both IGHM and IGHD, we used isotype-specific single nucleotide polymorphisms to distinguish these chains as previously described95.

Across the 18 bone marrow samples, we obtained an average of 163,836,012 reads per library (Supplementary Data 4). Blasting V(D)J sequences from all bone marrow samples against the IMGT database found that 56.3% of B cells contained a single V(D)J contig with a fully annotated CDR3 region, which were used for subsequent downstream analyses. Of these, 42.6% expressed detectable IGH genes, while 20.9% expressed detectable IGK/IGL genes (Fig. 4A). During B cell development, IGH chains appeared at earlier stages than IGK and IGL chains and genes encoding both components of the surrogate light chain (VPREB1 and IGLL1) were prevalent through most of B cell differentiation (Fig. 4B). These observations are interesting as the prevailing model of porcine B cell development contends that light chain rearrangement begins before heavy chain rearrangements and that pigs are among those species that do not require the surrogate light chain for B cell generation96–98. We then analyzed V(D)J gene segment usage in the cells with paired light and heavy chains (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistent with the fact that pigs have a relatively simple IgH gene complex, with all porcine VH genes belonging to the ancestral VH3 family11,12,99–101, we observed that most BCR IGH chains were formed using a limited number of V, D, and J genes (Fig. 4H). We also observed age-dependent differences in V(D)J segment usage (Fig. 4I), including VJ and VDJ segment rearrangements, with samples clustering by fetal versus postnatal timepoints (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Fig. 7A). Similar altered immunoglobulin gene usage has been documented in children102,103. This pattern was not simply due to age-dependent changes in precursor and immature B cell subset proportions, as the same analyses using only late-stage immature B cell subsets (clusters 11–12) yielded similar results (Supplementary Fig. 7B).

We found that IGK/IGL light chain CDR3 amino acid sequences were highly conserved throughout pregnancy, with 40–60% of fetal and 1 DOA pig sequences detected in more than 2 cells. Several of these sequences were shared between samples, particularly among fetal and 1 DOA pigs (Fig. 4K and Supplementary Fig. 7C). Conversely, in 7 MOA pigs, most light chain CDR3 sequences were found in single cells, indicating a considerable increase in clonal diversity at this age. Heavy chain CDR3 sequences remained highly diverse throughout development, due in part to the inclusion of the D segment, with very few identical sequences detected in more than one B cell (Fig. 4L). Heavy chain CDR3 length increased during pregnancy and then decreased in adult pigs (Fig. 4M and Supplementary Fig. 7D), which corresponds to the disappearance of early stage pro- and pre-B cell subsets in adult pigs. Our findings are consistent with the increase in heavy chain CDR3 length seen during human fetal development104, but contrast with previous studies in pigs, which reported that CDR3 length remains unchanged during fetal development105,106.

Next, we estimated the frequency of somatic hypermutations (SHM) by aligning our BCR heavy chains to IMGT germline reference sequences. Consistent with the fact that SHM occurs primarily in antigen-experienced B cells107, we observed higher mutation rates in 7 MOA compared to fetal and 1 DOA samples (Fig. 4N), and in IGHA and IGHG chains compared to IGHM and IGHD chains (Fig. 4O).

In fetal liver, we observed that IGK, IGL, and IGH contigs aligned well with the small number of B cells present, and that IGH contigs were exclusively IgM (Fig. 4P and Supplementary Data 4). We also observed that the fetal liver VDJ heavy chain repertoire was composed of the same limited number of V, D, and J genes used by bone marrow IGH chains (Fig. 4Q). Heavy chain V segment usage was significantly more diverse, and CDR3 lengths were longer, in cells with unpaired heavy chains than in the few B cells with both heavy and light chains (Fig. 4R, S). This agrees with the notion that the heavy chain repertoire diversity decreases as B cells mature due to the selective pressures of receptor editing and negative selection108–110. While 47 DG and 60 DG bone marrow samples presented a similar difference in V segment diversity between paired and unpaired heavy chains (Supplementary Fig. 7E), differences in CDR3 lengths were less noticeable (Supplementary Fig. 7F).

Thymopoiesis across age

The combined thymus dataset (60 DG to 7 MOA) clustered into 26 clusters containing four non-thymocyte cell types and 22 thymocyte cell types (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 8A, and Supplementary Data 2)18. Thymocytes included a substantial number of differentiating γδ T cells. Pig γδ T cells progress through a CD1A hi γδ-TCR+CD2+ intermediate stage that branches into mature CD2+ and CD2- subsets that downregulate CD1A111,112. The CD2- subset, which also appears in other high-frequency γδ T cell species113,114, resemble murine Tγδ17 cells, including the ability to develop with little or no TCR stimulation115,116. In contrast, the CD2+ subset seems to require TCR stimulation for their induction, similar to IFN-γ-producing Tγδ1 cell in mice and humans117–120. Using RNAscope in situ hybridization, we observed that γδ thymocytes (TDRC+ cells) were concentrated in the thymic medulla, especially along the cortico-medullary junction (Fig. 5B). A trajectory analysis placed thymocyte clusters in an order consistent with well-established development stages (Fig. 5C). We found that, while 60 DG thymi contained the full complement of cell types present in older samples, this timepoint was notable for a high proportion of quiescent double positive (DP(Q)) and low proportions of early thymic progenitors (ETP), double negative (DN) and proliferating double positive (DP(P)) thymocytes, as well as terminally differentiated T and B cells (Fig. 5D). Sixty DG samples were also distinguished by a distinct population of interferon-stimulated gene (ISG)-expressing DP cells (cluster 13). Seventy-five DG and 90 DG thymi had the highest levels of proliferating thymocytes (Supplementary Fig. 8B–D). ETP and DN concentrations peaked at 90 DG, while DN/DP thymocytes, which follow DN in development, peaked at 75 DG. Proportions of SP CD4, SP CD8, Treg, and γδ T cells were similar from 75 DG to 1 DOA. In contrast, 7 MOA samples had lower SP CD4 concentrations and higher frequencies of Tregs, CD8αα and γδ thymocytes. B cell concentrations increased with age, with the largest increase between 60 DG and 75 DG.

Next, we employed CellOracle (v 0.20.0)121, a package that uses regularized linear regression models to construct cell-type or cell-state specific gene regulatory networks (GRNs), to predict transcription factor (TF) based regulatory programs driving different phases of thymocyte differentiation. Transcription factors are scored according to (i) the number of genes to which a TF is connected (degree centrality) (Fig. 5E), (ii) how often the TF acts as a bridge between other genes (betweenness centrality) (Fig. 5F), and (iii) a TF’s influence within the network, considering its connection to other highly influential TFs (eigenvector centrality) (Fig. 5G). Interesting findings included that the E2F family of TFs (E2F1, E2F7, and E2F8), which play a critical role regulating cell cycle progression and apoptosis122, were highly influential (high eigenvector centrality scores) during both the initial and terminal stages of thymocyte differentiation. Genes with high betweenness centrality included SOX5 and ETV6, both of which acted as bridges controlling the expression of many other genes in DP(Q) cells and T entry cells, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8E, F, and Supplementary Data 5). Highly influential TFs that differed in CD2- compared to CD2+ γδ thymocytes included KLF7, which regulates thymocyte survival123, and NR1D2 (REV-ERBα), which modulates T helper (Th) 17 cell differentiation by competing with RORγt124, in line with the Th17 phenotype that CD2- γδ T cells exhibit125.

Thymocytes send and receive essential maturation signals through interacting with thymic epithelial cells, dendritic cells, B cells, and macrophages126. To investigate these connections in pigs, we used LIANA+127 and Tensor-cell2cell128 in combination with the pig homolog ligand-receptor database to decipher cell-cell communication patterns (or factors) across development. Eleven significantly different age-dependent ligand-receptor communication patterns were identified (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Fig. 9). Among these, factors 5 and 10 differed between prenatal and postnatal samples. Factor 5, which was substantially higher in postnatal compared to prenatal thymi, connected B cells, CD8αα T cells, mature αβ thymocytes, and γδ thymocytes to ETP, DN, and DP cells, through transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) ligand-receptor signals (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). This could be related to the important role TGF-β plays in promoting thymic involution and a decline in thymic output with age129. On the other hand, factor 10, which declined throughout development, connected B cells, CD8αα T cells, and mature αβ thymocytes to multiple thymus populations through tumor necrosis factor-α (encoded by TNF) and lymphotoxin β (encoded by LTB) signaling (Fig. 5I and Supplementary Fig. 9A, C). This may be connected to the critical role TNF-α and LTβ play in thymocyte homeostasis, which includes regulating ETP entry and mature SP thymocyte egress from the thymus, and enhancing negative selection of DN prothymocytes130.

Cells with fully recombined TRG and TRB chains dominated early thymocyte development, whereas cells expressing TRA and TRD chains were rare until the DP and DN/DP stages, respectively (Fig. 6A, B). The DN/DP(P) cluster (cluster 4) emerged as a critical stage in αβ and γδ thymocyte bifurcation, being marked by a pronounced rise in TRB expressing cells as well as the first significant population of cells with paired TRG and TRD chains. Following this, cells either entered a γδ T entry stage during which cells with paired TRG and TRD chains became increasingly prevalent, or they progressed through different phases of DP development, during which the fraction of cells with paired TRA and TRB chains increased steadily as pre-TCR (encoded by PTCRA) expression declined (Fig. 6C). The CD8αα T cell cluster (cluster 20) was unusual for the fact that it consisted of similar proportions of γδ and αβ thymocytes. Moreover, this cluster was enriched in NK receptor and cytotoxic genes, consistent with a previously described CD2+ CD8α+ γδ T cell subpopulation in pigs25,27 (Supplementary Fig. 8A and Supplementary Data 2). Although generally alike, the αβTCR CD8αα cells had greater enrichment of cytotoxic and activation genes than the γδTCR CD8αα cells133–135 (Supplementary Fig. 10A).

Numerous cells had hybrid αβ-γδ TCR chains. These were most common among early developing thymocytes that co-expressed TRG and TRB chains, particularly at the DN/DP(P) stage (Supplementary Fig. 10B). Conversely, a considerable fraction of cells at the late stages of T cell maturation co-expressed TRA, TRB, and TRG chains, especially Tregs. Similar hybrid TRA/TRB-TRG/TRD cells were present in our recently published pig lung scRNAseq dataset136. We also detected numerous non-productive TCR chains, defined by stop codons either at the 3′ end of the V region or the 5′ end of the J region (Supplementary Fig. 10C–N). These non-productive sequences were enriched in DP thymocytes, rare in TRB chains, and present at similar frequencies across age groups.

Next, we performed a detailed analysis of TRA and TRB chain expression patterns using thymocytes with paired and productive TRA and TRB chains, regardless of whether they co-expressed TRG or TRD chains (Supplementary Data 6). An assessment of V(D)J recombination and gene segments found that Vα and Jα segment usage was diverse (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 11), and that usage of some segments changed with age (Supplementary Fig. 12A), as has been reported in humans137. As in bone marrow B cells, thymocyte VJ and VDJ segment usage (Fig. 6E) and rearrangements (Supplementary Fig. 12B), clustered according to age. This may have been influenced by swine leukocyte antigen (SLA) expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. 12C, D), which tended to cluster by age due to the fact that, except for the 7 MOA samples, pigs at each timepoint were full siblings.

An examination of CDR3α and CDR3β sequences revealed that, as in humans and mice137,138, pig TCR diversity increases progressively with age (Fig. 6F, G). While CDR3α and CDR3β sequences were both highly conserved in the 60 DG samples, with ~52% of CDR3α and ~26% of CDR3β sequence present in more than 1 cell, this proportion decreased as gestation progressed (Fig. 6H, I). The reduction was most striking between 75 DG and 90 DG and was evident across several thymocyte subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 13A, B). Numerous CDR3α and CDR3β sequences were shared across samples, especially among 60 DG and 75 DG thymi, with CDR3α sequences overlapping more frequently than CDR3β sequences (Fig. 6J, K). In contrast, cells with paired CDR3α and CDR3β rarely overlapped between samples (Supplementary Fig. 13C), indicating that inter-sample overlap arose primarily from a lack of non-templated nucleotide addition rather than clonal expansion. Consistent with increasing junctional diversity, the length of CDR3α and CDR3β sequences increased with age (Fig. 6L, M), especially in DP and T entry thymocytes (Supplementary Fig. 13D, E). The increase in TCR diversity coincided with an age-related increase in the expression of DNTT (encoding TdT), which adds random nucleotides to the junctions between V, D, and J gene segments during TCR gene rearrangement139, and decreased LIN28B, which suppresses TdT activity140 (Fig. 6N).

γδ thymocyte repertoire

We then delved deeper into the γδ TCR repertoire by analyzing cells with productive TRG or TRD chains, regardless of whether they were paired with TRA/TRB chains (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Data 6). As described in mice, naked mole rats, and humans143–146, a high proportion (~48%) of productive TRG sequences were canonical public CDR3γ sequences (lacking non-templated nucleotide additions) (Supplementary Fig. 14B), whereas only ~5% of CDRδ sequences were public (Supplementary Fig. 14C). Notably, the top five public CDR3γ sequences accounted for 30% of the overall repertoire across all the samples (Fig. 7B), and more than 50% of the 60 DG repertoire (Fig. 7C). Public CDR3γ sequences were especially enriched in the early stages of thymocyte maturation, particularly DN cells and αβ thymocytes with unpaired TRG chains (Supplementary Fig. 14D). Private CDR3γ sequences dominated mature CD2+ and CD2- γδ T cell clusters, especially in cells with paired TRG and TRD chains (Supplementary Fig. 14D, E). The diversity of CDR3γ sequences increased with age (Fig. 7D), but particularly after birth, resulting in an age-related decline in the ratio of public to private CDR3γ sequences (Supplementary Fig. 14E). This is likely from the increase in TdT expression with age (Fig. 6N)140. As regards CDR3 length, CDR3δ sequences were longer and more variable than CDR3γ sequences. Although most CDR3δ sequences were between 10 and 30 amino acids in length, a subset was much longer, exceeding 30 amino acids (Fig. 7E, F). Like TRA and TRB chains, CDR3δ lengths increased markedly with age (Supplementary Fig. 14F).

Unlike TRA and TRB chains, TRG and TRD V(D)J segments were dominated by a limited set of genes (Fig. 7G and Supplementary Fig. 14G–I). TRD had greater recombination diversity due to its six TRD D genes. In TRG chains, the two most prevalent TRGV segment families, TRGV3 and TRGV7, were expressed at similar frequencies across developing (clusters 2–4) and mature (clusters 20–22) γδ clusters (Fig. 7H). In contrast, the two most infrequently used TRGV families, TRGV6 and TRGV12, were highly enriched in CD2+ and CD8αα γδ T cells (Fig. 7H). Sequence alignments showed that TRGV6 and TRGV12 are more similar to each other (31% overall identity and 67% identity in the junction region) than to the other TRGV segments (Supplementary Fig. 14J). Additionally, TRGV6 and TRGV12 chains contained a high proportion of private CDR3γ sequences (Supplementary Fig. 14K) and exhibited increased usage after birth (Fig. 7I). TRGV10, another infrequently used TRGV family, was particularly enriched in CD8αα γδ T cells (Fig. 7H). Usage of this segment decreased with age (Fig. 7I). Regarding the TRD chains, TRADV16-3/TRDV1-3 was enriched in CD2+ and CD8αα γδ T cells, whereas the TRADV11/TRDV3 was preferentially enriched in CD2- γδ T cells (Fig. 7J). Among TRG and TRD paired cells, TRADV11/TRDV3 were more frequently paired with TRGV3 and TRGV7 segments, while TRADV16-3/TRDV1-3 preferentially paired with TRGV6 and TRGV12 segments, relative to TRADV11/TRDV3 (Fig. 7K).

Interestingly, TRGV6+/TRGV12+ γδ T cells were transcriptionally distinct from the remaining CD2+ and CD8αα γδ T cells (Fig. 7L and Supplementary Fig. 14L), being enriched in SLAM6, which is required for the development of pro-inflammatory Vγ4 and anti-inflammatory Vγ1 γδ T cells in mice147,148, as well as CD96, FCRL1, CD7, and XCL1 (also known as lymphotactin) (Fig. 7L). XCL1 is expressed by intestinal γδ T cells in mice, where it aids in maintaining oral tolerance149. Additionally, there were transcriptional differences in TRGV6+/TRGV12+ γδ T cells between the CD2+ and CD8αα clusters, with the CD2+ population being enriched for the co-stimulatory molecules CD48, CD28, and IL2RB and the CD8αα population being enriched for the tissue residency markers CXCR3 and ZNF683 (Fig. 7L). TRGV10+ CD8αα γδ T were also unique, in that they upregulated scavenger receptor SCART1, which identifies a population of IL-17-producing Vγ6⁺ γδ T cells in mouse skin150, as well as S100 family calcium-binding protein genes, including S100A4, S100A6, and S100A11, which regulate calcium signaling and promote activation151 (Fig. 7L). Of note, we identified similar transcriptional patterns and clustering preferences in our recently published pig lung scRNAseq dataset136 (Supplementary Fig. 14M). This demonstrates that the γ-chain associated phenotypes observed in our thymi samples are preserved in the periphery. However, tissue-specific differences were also found, including that pulmonary TRGV3+ and TRGV7+ γδ T cells expressed much higher levels of S100 family genes than in the thymus, and that lung TRGV6+/TRGV12+ γδ T cells express IFNG (Supplementary Fig. 14M), which was barely detected in their thymic counterparts. This resembles a population of peripheral SLAMF6+ γδ T in mice, which also express IFN-γ147. Cells grouped by TRDV segment usage did not exhibit strong transcriptional differences. Nevertheless, TRADV16-3/TRDV1-3+ γδ T cells had slightly higher expression of genes associated with thymic egress (TOX and IKZF2)152, TCR signaling (PRKCH)153, and T cell memory formation DAPL1154, whereas TRADV11/TRDV3+ γδ T cells expressed more CD84, a T cell activation suppressor155, and ETV5 and BLK, both regulators of γδ17 T cell differentiation156 (Supplementary Fig. 14N).

In summary, we demonstrate that the pig TRG repertoire remains mostly public until birth and then becomes increasingly diversified as pigs mature into adulthood. Additionally, we show that transcriptionally distinct subsets of γδ T cells can be distinguished according to the TRG chains they express and that these subsets are present in both the thymus and the periphery.

Discussion

Despite the increasing popularity of pigs as human biomedical models, current understanding of porcine hematopoiesis lags behind traditional laboratory models and humans. Using single-cell RNA and immune receptor profiling, we have reconstructed the cellular complexity and temporal dynamics of hematopoiesis in fetal liver, bone marrow, and thymus across a large portion of pig hematopoietic development. Notable findings include that: the pig fetal liver harbors substantially more differentiating neutrophils than humans at comparable stages of development14,36,157; there exists considerable conservation but also species-specific differences in pig and human monocyte differentiation programs; early HSC development in fetal liver and bone marrow show distinct, tissue-specific signatures; B cell development appears to follow conventional principles of BCR assembly rather than the current paradigm of porcine B cell development which maintains that light chain rearrangement begins before heavy chain rearrangements96–98; thymocyte development is accompanied by age-dependent changes in ligand-receptor communication patterns; and that age affects the usage and recombination of both BCR and TCR segments. Additionally, our analysis of the thymic γδ TCR repertoire revealed that: DN/DP(P) cells represent a critical stage in αβ and γδ thymocyte bifurcation; that a large fraction of the γ chain repertoire is composed of canonical public sequences, especially before birth; that a high percentage of the pig’s thymus output remains committed to γδ T cell production into adulthood, in contrast to humans where production declines sharply after birth13,117; and that transcriptionally distinct subsets of γδ T cells can be identified according to their γ chain usage.

It is important to note the limitations of our study: sampling across developmental stages and tissues was not fully uniform, which can influence cellular frequencies and functional interpretation. Furthermore, rare cell populations may have been underrepresented due to the limited sampling depth and inherent detection constraints of single-cell profiling. Accordingly, it will be important for future work to expand the dataset by incorporating more samples from additional time points to capture the full extent of porcine hematopoiesis, especially during the early stages of embryo development. Because of a lack of antibody reagents for pigs, some of our discoveries, for instance, γδ T cell subsets defined by γ chain usage, cannot be confirmed using protein-based assays at this time. Validation of our findings should be performed when these reagents become available. Finally, we cannot exclude that some of the observed species’ differences reflect biological and technical factors, such as differences in physiological age, tissue processing, and sequencing depth. Validation of our findings will require additional datasets as they become available.

Together, our comprehensive single-cell cross-tissue molecular map provides a foundational reference for understanding porcine immune system development and immune receptor repertoire formation. It also provides an important resource for utilizing pigs as biomedical models for a host of human immune-related disorders and for protecting pig health through improved vaccine and therapy design. Finally, our pig-customized single-cell transcriptomics pipeline provides a useful framework that can be adopted to study any immunological process in swine.

Methods

Pigs

Pigs on a commercial Large White crossbred background were provided by the University of Missouri swine farm. Euthanasia was performed via jugular injection of pentobarbital sodium and phenytoin sodium (Euthasol; Virbac, TX, USA). Fetuses were recovered from pregnant females euthanized at 41, 47, 60, 75, and 90 days of gestation (DG). Fetal liver samples were collected at 41 DG, thymus samples at 60, 75, and 90 DG, and bone marrow samples at 47, 60, 75, and 90 DG. In addition, post-natal thymus and bone marrow samples were collected from 1-day-old (DOA) piglets and 7-month-old (MOA) gilts. Samples were collected from at least three animals per time point. A detailed description of each pig is provided in Supplementary Data 1. The studies were in accordance with the University of Missouri Animal Care and Use Committee (project number 8813).

Tissue sampling and cell isolation

Fetal liver tissues were weighed and dispersed into single-cell suspensions using glass homogenizers. Cells were then filtered using 100 µm cell strainers and washed with PBS. After counting, 6 million cells from each liver were Fc-blocked with rat IgG (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and stained with anti-porcine CD45 antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Cells were then stained with the propidium iodine (PI) viability dye and sorted for live CD45+ and CD45- cells on a BD FACS Melody cell sorter (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Thymus tissues were incubated in 3 ml of Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) with 2 μL of collagenase Type 1 (Worthington, Lakewood, NJ) at 37 °C for 30 min after which they were dispersed into single cells using glass homogenizers, washed with PBS, filtered, counted, stained with PI, and finally sorted for live cells. Bone marrow was collected from the scapula, femur, tibia, humerus, ulna, and radius of 47 DG fetuses, from the femur and tibia of 60, 75, and 90 DG fetuses, and from the ribs and sternum of 1 DOA and 7 MOA pigs after they were cut open with scissors. Cells were filtered, washed with DMEM containing 10% FBS, counted, stained with PI, and FACS sorted for live cells.

Single-cell processing

Libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions with reagents supplied in the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Kit v2 (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). For the gene expression library, up to 50 ng of cDNA was fragmentated and end-repaired, after which A-tails were added, and sequencing adaptors were ligated. V(D)J libraries were constructed using pig-specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) for the enrichments of TCR and BCR transcripts, as previously described95. Primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Iowa, USA). All PCR reactions were performed in 100 µL volumes containing 50 µL Amp Mix, with the remaining volume adjusted using nuclease-free water unless otherwise specified. For TCR α/β-chain amplification, first-round PCR contained 2 µL cDNA, forward primers at 1.25 µM, and TCR α/β outer primer mix at 0.5 µM. Cycling was performed at 98 °C for 45 s; 12 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 59 °C for 30 s, and 72°C for 60 s; and 72 °C for 60 s. Products were purified using AxyPrep Mag PCR Clean-up Beads (Axygen, Union City, CA, USA). For second-round PCR, 46 µL purified product was used as template with forward primers at 1.25 µM and TCR α/β inner primer mix at 0.5 µM. The same cycling conditions were used for 11 cycles, followed by bead-based purification. For BCR amplification, first-round PCR contained 2 µL cDNA, forward primer at 1.0 µM, BCR outer primer mix at 1.4 µM, and EB buffer to volume. Cycling was performed at 98 °C for 45 s; 8 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 62 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s; and 72 °C for 60 s. After bead-based purification, 35 µL purified product was used for second-round PCR with forward primer at 1.0 µM and BCR inner primer mix at 1.4 µM. The same cycling conditions were used, followed by final purification. For TCR δ-chain amplification, first-round PCR contained 2 µL cDNA, forward primer at 0.2 µM, and TCR δ outer primer at 0.2 µM. Cycling was performed at 98 °C for 45 s; 15 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s; and 72 °C for 60 s. After bead-based purification, 46 µL purified product was used for second-round PCR with forward primer at 0.2 µM and TCR δ inner primer at 0.2 µM. The same cycling conditions were used, followed by final purification. For TCR γ-chain amplification, TRGC3/4/6 and TRGC5 were separately amplified using 2 µL cDNA, forward primer at 0.2 µM, and the corresponding TCR γ primer at 0.2 µM. The cycling conditions were the same as those used for first-round TCR δ-chain PCR, except that 30 cycles were performed. Products were purified by the same bead-based method. The concentrations for all libraries were measured with the Qubit HS DNA kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and fragment sizes were determined on a Fragment Analyzer 5200 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Libraries were pooled and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to obtain paired end reads. The minimum sequencing depths targeted were 40,000 reads per cell for 5′ GEX libraries and 10,000 reads per cell for V(D)J libraries.

Single-cell RNA analysis

The Sscrofa 11.1 genome assembly was used to align sequencing reads to generate gene matrix data by Cell Ranger (v8.0.0). Each dataset was pre-processed by removing genes expressed in <3 cells, excluding cells with aberrantly high or low gene counts and high mitochondrial gene expression. Afterwards, batch correction, cross-species integration using one-to-one orthologs to pigs, and clustering analyses were performed using Seurat (v.4.4.0)158 as previously described18,26. Briefly, transcript counts were log normalized, and highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction analysis. Canonical cell cycle markers were then used to regress out cell cycle effects. Next, the SelectIntegrationFeatures function was applied to genes that were consistently variable across datasets. Next, the FindIntegrationAnchors function identified a set of anchors between datasets using the top 30 dimensions from the canonical correlation analysis to specify the neighbor search space. Then, an integrated dataset was created by running the IntegrateData function. Then, the clustering analysis workflow was performed using RunPCA, FindNeighbours, FindClusters, and RunUMAP. Cell types were assigned based on the expression of known cell type markers.

Cell Ranger (v8.0.0) was used to map swine leukocyte antigen (SLA) transcripts in bone marrow samples to the pig SLA reference database, after which SLA reads were normalized and quantified according to our previously described approach95.

Cell and gene trajectory inference analysis

Developmental cell trajectories were constructed using the partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) method, implemented via the scanpy.tl.paga function in Scanpy (v1.9.1), which explores the connectivity between identified single-cell clusters and provides a global framework for lineage relationships159. HSCs from fetal liver and bone marrow were assigned to myeloid, erythroid, or lymphoid lineages using a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) classifier trained on all committed non-root cells with standardized top principal components as features. Each HSC cell was projected into the two-dimensional discriminant space and assigned to the branch with the highest posterior probability. Pseudotime analysis of the selected cell types was performed using the scanpy.tl.dpt function using a predefined root cluster. Afterwards, genes with varied expression across pseudotime were identified using the fitGAM function from tradeSeq (v1.5.10)160.

To infer gene dynamics along the B cell lineage in the bone marrow, we used the GeneTrajectory package (v1.0.4)86. We first identified highly variable genes and then built a cell–cell graph, where each cell was connected to its 10 most similar neighbors (k = 10). We then computed graph-based Wasserstein distances between cells using the get_graph_distance function, capturing the intrinsic geometry of the cell–cell graph. A Diffusion Map was applied to embed the genes into a low-dimensional space based on these distances, capturing smooth gene expression trends over the lineage. Finally, we visualized the gene trajectories in three dimensions using the plot_gene_trajectory_3d function.

Cell–cell interaction analysis

LIANA+ (v1.5.0) and Tensor-cell2cell (v0.7.4) were used in combination127,128, along with the pig homolog ligand-receptor database using HGNC Comparison of Orthology Predictions (HCOP) tool161, to infer cell–cell interactions across samples. Within each sample, the LIANA+ function li.mt.rank_aggregate.by_sample was used to systematically detect and aggregate ligand–receptor interactions between cell types, after integrating results from multiple computational methods, including CellPhoneDBv2162, CellChat163, SingleCellSignalR164, NATMI165, Connectome166, and log2FC for robustness. The resulting interaction scores, which reflect the coordinated relationships among ligand–receptor interactions, communicating cell-type pairs, and samples, were summarized into consensus communication scores (representative values capturing interaction patterns consistently observed across samples), and then analyzed using Tensor-cell2cell, which performs tensor decomposition to identify conserved and sample-specific patterns (factors) of cell–cell communication.

Gene regulatory network analysis

Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) in the thymus datasets were constructed using CellOracle (v0.20.0)121. We used the pig promoter database Sscrofa11.1_CisBPv2_fpr2, which links each transcription factor (TF) to genes whose promoters or enhancers contain the corresponding TF binding motifs. The top 3000 highly variable genes, and a random subset of 30,000 cells was used in the analysis. Cell-type-specific GRNs were inferred using the oracle.get_links function. Network statistics, including degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality, were calculated using get_network_score and merged_score functions.

Single-cell V(D)J data analysis

Single-cell TCR and BCR V(D)J sequencing reads were assembled into contigs using the cellranger vdj (10× Genomics) pipeline in de novo mode. To identify the V(D)J chains, we searched assembled contigs against inner-enrichment primers using the usearch_global command. The TCR β, TCR γ, and BCR V(D)J chains were respectively mapped to the pig TRB, TRG, and IG reference sequences in IMGT using the program IMGT/V-QUEST167. TCR α and δ chains are not annotated in the IMGT database. Therefore, a custom TCR α reference was constructed based on published data131, whereas a δ chain reference was built using published sequences132. MiXCR was then used to align V(D)J segments and to identify CDR3 regions168,169. Single cells with a single unique annotated contig in any of the TCR αβ, γδ, or BCR IGH, IGL, and IGK chains were retained for downstream analysis, while those with multiple contigs per chain were excluded. The filtered annotated contigs were then aligned to their corresponding gene expression profiles. Scirpy (v0.12.0) was used to analyze CDR3 clonal expansion, CDR3 amino acid sequence length, and V(D)J segment usage across samples170. Quantification of mutations in IGHV relative to germline sequences was carried out using shazam (v1.1.2)171. CDR3 clonal diversity was quantified using the alphaDiversity function from the Alakazam package (v1.3.0)171. Diversity was assessed across a range of diversity orders (q) based on the Hill diversity framework: species richness (q = 0), the exponential Shannon–Weiner index (as q = 1), the inverse of the Simpson index (q = 2), and the reciprocal abundance of the largest clone (as q → ∞)172.

RNA in situ hybridization

RNA in situ hybridization was performed on 0.5 μm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections using the RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex Reagent Kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Bio-Techne) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. A panel was designed to simultaneously visualize the RNA targets, RORC, TRDC, and CD2. Briefly, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were baked for 1 h at 60 °C, then deparaffinized and treated with hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature. Target retrieval was performed for 15 min at 98 °C, followed by Protease Plus RNAscope (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Bio-Techne) treatment for 30 min at 40 °C. After pretreatment, tissue coverslips were hybridized with RNAscope probe mix for 2 h at 40 °C. Hybridization signal amplifications 1, 2, and 3 were performed for 30, 30, and 15 min, respectively, at 40 °C, followed by probe signal marking performed according to the manufacturer’s directions. The slides were imaged using a Leica DM6 B Upright Microscope at ×10 magnification, capturing 4 field of views per slide.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry for Iba1 and cKit were performed on FFPE tissue by the Veterinary Medical Diagnostic Laboratory (VMDL) at the University of Missouri. Samples were deparaffinized and incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by heat induced antigen retrieval in Diva Decloaker (Biocare, Concord, CA). Immunostaining for Iba1 (019-19741, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and c-Kit (A4502, Dako) was performed. Samples were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Statistics

The samples used for sequencing were randomly selected. Three samples per age group were included for thymus and bone marrow, and four for fetal liver, based on tissue availability and to ensure adequate representation across developmental stages. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. However, three samples at each timepoint are adequate, since each sample provides thousands of single-cell measurements rather than just one averaged value. No data were excluded from the analyses. For gene set expression score, clonal expansion and CDR3 length, mean values were calculated across all positive cells within each sample. For TRGV and TRDV family segment usage, fold change was calculated as mean usage in each group relative to all other cells. The PCA analysis was performed in R (4.5.0) using percentage values for each group. Cell communication factor scores were analyzed using ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons in GraphPad Prism 10 (Boston, MA, USA). P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
