The use of the Edmonton Obesity Staging System compared to body mass index to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes
Authors: Sarah E White, David M Stamilio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn nonpregnant populations, obesity staging systems such as the Edmonton Obesity Staging System (EOSS) improve risk stratification of patients with obesity by also accounting for medical comorbidities. While the EOSS has been shown to better predict morbidity and mortality compared to body mass index (BMI)‐based obesity classification in the nonpregnant population, its utility in pregnancy and performance compared to the current standard remain unclear. Thus, the objective of this study was to compare a modified EOSS and BMI‐based obesity classification in predicting specific adverse pregnancy outcomes.MethodsWe created a modified EOSS using available diagnoses within the Consortium on Safe Labor (CSL) dataset and applied it to all women with a prepregnancy BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. The primary outcome was a composite of maternal adverse outcomes including chorioamnionitis, endometritis, preeclampsia with severe features, eclampsia, venous thromboembolism, wound infection/separation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and maternal death. Prespecified secondary outcomes included a severe maternal morbidity composite and a neonatal adverse outcome composite. In order to compare EOSS with BMI classification, the association between EOSS stages and adverse outcomes was first examined. Bivariate analyses assessed maternal and obstetric characteristics by EOSS stage. Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations between EOSS stage and outcomes. Predictive performance of EOSS versus the World Health Organization (WHO) BMI classification was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsAmong 228,668 subjects in the cohort, 139,126 pregnant individuals met inclusion criteria for analysis; 67,289 had BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. EOSS classification was associated with increased odds of the maternal composite outcome, secondary maternal severe composite outcome, and neonatal composite outcome (all p < 0.01). However, ROC analysis demonstrated that EOSS did not outperform the WHO BMI classification in predicting adverse outcomes, with both systems demonstrating poor‐to‐fair discrimination (AUC 0.57–0.67).ConclusionsA modified EOSS identified increased risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes when applied to a pregnant population but did not demonstrate improved predictive ability compared to traditional BMI classification. Both EOSS and WHO BMI systems exhibited only poor‐to‐fair predictive ability for select adverse outcomes.Keywords: BMI, obesity, obesity staging system, pregnancy

INTRODUCTION

Body mass index (BMI) has traditionally been used to classify obesity and to identify patients most at risk of obesity‐related outcomes, but its use is criticized for not accounting for age, gender, muscle mass, metabolic milieu, and distribution of adipose tissue. Because of this, other systems were developed to attempt to better classify risks, including the Edmonton Obesity Staging System (EOSS). The EOSS was developed in nonpregnant populations to classify and stratify management of patients with obesity based on the presence of risk factors as shown in Table 1 [1].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that the EOSS better predicts morbidity and mortality compared to BMI when applied to patients outside of pregnancy with higher stages being associated with increased risk of postoperative complications and mortality among other things [2, 3]. However, the EOSS has been sparsely evaluated in pregnancy. Prior investigations have been limited to infertility populations—where higher EOSS stages were associated with reduced conception rates—and to studies demonstrating increased cesarean risk with higher staging [4, 5]. To date, no study has directly compared the performance of EOSS and BMI with respect to prediction of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Therefore, it was our objective to apply a modified version of the EOSS to the Consortium on Safe Labor (CSL) cohort to determine its predictive ability for specific adverse pregnancy outcomes and compare this with the performance of BMI. We hypothesized that increasing EOSS stage would be associated with increasing rates of adverse pregnancy outcomes and, consistent with studies outside of pregnancy, would outperform BMI in predictive ability.

METHODS

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis of the CSL dataset, a retrospective cohort study of 228,668 deliveries ≥23 gestational weeks between 2002 and 2008 at 19 hospitals across all nine American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) districts. The dataset contains validated electronic medical record data, including demographic characteristics, prenatal complications, intrapartum details, and maternal and neonatal outcomes [6]. Analyses were performed using a deidentified dataset under a waiver of consent and Institutional Review Board approval. Women with missing prepregnancy BMI data as well as multiple gestations and anomalous fetuses were excluded given the independent association between these exposures and our chosen outcomes (Figure 1).

Flow chart of patient inclusion.

To apply the EOSS to the CSL population, we created a modified version based on available data within the existing CSL cohort. Patients were classified into EOSS Stages 0–3 (Table 2); Stage 4 was omitted because required clinical data (e.g., end‐organ failure measures) were not available and are rare during pregnancy. Obesity was defined per WHO BMI‐based classification: Class 1 obesity (BMI 30.0–34.9), Class 2 obesity (BMI 35.0–39.9), and Class 3 obesity (BMI ≥ 40) [7].

The primary outcome for this study was a composite of maternal adverse outcomes, including chorioamnionitis, endometritis, preeclampsia with severe features, eclampsia, venous thromboembolism, wound infection/separation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and maternal death. We defined a priori two secondary outcomes: (1) a severe maternal composite adverse outcome derived from the most severe component outcomes of the primary outcome, which included preeclampsia with severe features, eclampsia, venous thromboembolism, ICU admission, and maternal death and (2) a neonatal composite, which included pneumonia, necrotizing enterocolitis, intracranial hemorrhage or periventricular hemorrhage, asphyxia, mechanical ventilation, neonatal seizures, hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, stillbirth, and neonatal death. An individual was categorized as having the composite outcome if they had one or more of the binary outcomes. In order to compare the modified EOSS to BMI, EOSS performance first had to be assessed. Bivariate analysis of maternal and obstetric characteristics by EOSS stage was performed with χ2 tests for categorical data or ANOVA for continuous data. Multivariable logistic regression estimated the independent association between EOSS stage and each composite outcome, controlling for relevant confounding and interaction variables; included covariates were selected based on the bivariate analysis, clinical significance, or historical importance determined by prior studies. To compare predictive performance, we generated receiver operative characteristic (ROC) curves using the final multivariable model and then compared the areas under the curve (AUC) for EOSS and BMI using a nonparametric statistical method that employs an extension of the χ2 distribution [8]. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 16.1 (StataCorp) with two‐tailed tests and significance set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Of 228,438 deliveries in the CSL cohort, 139,126 had prepregnancy BMI available and met inclusion criteria for analysis. A total of 71,837 women had BMI ≤ 30. The remaining 67,289 women (48.3 %) met BMI criteria for obesity. Of these, 55,493 were classified as EOSS Stage 0; 3829 as EOSS Stage 1; 6301 as EOSS Stage 2; and 1666 as EOSS Stage 3 (Figure 1). Maternal demographics and obstetric characteristics are shown in Table 3. Compared with patients without obesity, meeting criteria for any EOSS classification was associated with increased rates of the primary maternal composite outcome, secondary maternal severe composite outcome, and neonatal composite outcome (Table 4). After adjusting for maternal age, insurance type, prior cesarean, and parity, EOSS classification remained associated with higher odds of the maternal composite (aOR, 2.0–3.4), severe maternal composite (aOR, 1.6–5.1), and neonatal composite (aOR, 0.98–1.6) compared with patients without obesity, though neonatal risk increases were limited to Stages 1–3, and odds did not increase progressively by stage (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

In this large retrospective cohort, patients with increasing obesity—whether categorized by BMI or by a modified EOSS—had increased odds of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes compared to the population without obesity. However, a central and unexpected finding was that the EOSS did not predict adverse outcomes better than BMI despite its theoretical advantages and better performance in nonpregnant individuals. Instead, both systems were only poor‐to‐fair predictors of adverse pregnancy outcomes, which emphasizes a critical limitation in risk stratification approaches for pregnant individuals.

BMI is traditionally used as a surrogate for obesity risk stratification despite its recognized shortcomings of not accounting for adiposity, body composition, metabolic state, and physiological changes that occur throughout pregnancy. Thus, patients with similar BMIs may have vastly different cardiometabolic health profiles yet, particularly in pregnancy, receive the same risk counseling and management. Obesity staging systems attempt to bridge this gap by incorporating clinical status and comorbidities into obesity risk stratification to better identify those most at risk. In nonpregnant cohorts, these staging systems have shown improved prediction of complications and mortality compared with BMI alone. Limited studies applying the EOSS to obstetric populations have found increased risks in those undergoing fertility treatments and cesarean delivery, but no study has directly compared EOSS with BMI in pregnancy to determine which performs better for risk prediction. Our study is unique in that it sought to answer this question. While it mirrored previous results in showing increased risk of adverse outcomes with both EOSS classification and meeting BMI criteria for obesity, EOSS did not outperform BMI, nor did either system provide clinically actionable predictive ability. These findings suggest that the pregnancy context may limit the use of a static measure, such as BMI or an obesity staging system, and that there is something intrinsic to obesity itself that is not yet fully understood.

However, the use of the CSL dataset may have degraded the predictive ability of the EOSS. Some diagnosis variables needed for a robust EOSS were not available in the CSL, which may have diminished its discriminatory ability. As an older dataset, the CSL cohort may not accurately reflect current health profiles, though the obesity prevalence (48%) is similar to that of the current United States population. A more contemporaneous dataset or the prospective application of a staging system that includes additional measures such as body composition, laboratory values, or machine learning may yield stronger predictive ability than BMI. Lastly, as 30% of the cohort lacked prepregnancy BMI data, these results may not be generalizable to patients with unknown prepregnancy BMI.

Our findings highlight a crucial gap in risk stratification of pregnant patients with obesity. Despite the theoretical advantages of the EOSS over BMI, our study demonstrated that neither classification system provides clinically meaningful prediction for adverse outcomes. As obesity continues to become more prevalent in the reproductive‐aged population, the ability to appropriately risk stratify patients with obesity and provide targeted counseling and testing is paramount yet something currently lacking in the field of obstetrics. A static measure of weight and clinical comorbidities is insufficient to predict outcomes for the dynamic process of pregnancy, and until obesity is better understood, clinicians should avoid the sole use of BMI or staging systems in risk assessment and instead consider the broader clinical context for counseling and management.
