The chromosome-level genome assembly of Malus robusta (Rosales: Rosaceae)
Authors: Mingxuan Sun, Haibo Wang, Lingtong Kong, Jingyuan Zhang, Jinrong Li, Yi Zheng, Sen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalus robusta Rehd. is widely used as an apple rootstock owing to its strong cold tolerance and desirable rootstock traits. Here, we report Haplotype-resolved, chromosome-level genome assemblies of the cultivar M. robusta, generated using an integrated strategy combining PacBio HiFi sequencing, Hi-C data, and Ultra-long ONT data. Two phased assemblies were produced, with HapA spanning 628.38 Mb and a contig N50 of 36.35 Mb, and HapB spanning 664.60 Mb with a contig N50 of 39.05 Mb. Repetitive elements accounted for 65.12% and 66.52% of the HapA and HapB genomes, respectively. A total of 49,268 and 51,070 protein-coding genes were predicted in HapA and HapB. Genome-wide synteny analysis revealed differential collinearity between the two haplotypes of M. robusta and the genomes of M. baccata and M. prunifolia, respectively. These Haplotype-resolved genome assemblies provide a comprehensive genomic resource for studies of cold tolerance–related traits, comparative genomics, and apple rootstock improvement.

Background & Summary

Malus robusta is a perennial woody species belonging to the genus Malus (Rosaceae) and is endemic to China. It is native to Hebei Province and is mainly distributed across northern China, including Beijing, Hebei, Shaanxi, and Shandong provinces. It has been proposed that M. robusta originated from hybridization between M. baccata and M. prunifolia,which may contribute to its robust cold adaptability. This species is characterized by a broad crown, flexible branches, and dense inflorescences, making it valuable for ornamental landscaping. In addition, M. robusta is well known for its exceptional cold tolerance and is widely used as an apple rootstock resource that can significantly enhance cold tolerance in grafted cultivars, highlighting its potential for fruit production in cold regions1. Consequently, elucidating the genetic basis underlying cold tolerance in M. robusta is of both biological and horticultural importance.

Previous studies have shown that cold stress responses in M. robusta are primarily mediated by low-temperature–responsive transcription factor families such as DREB/CBF2,3. For example, MrDREB2A and MrDREBA6 are strongly induced by cold, drought, high salinity, and abscisic acid (ABA) treatments4. Moreover, MrCBF1, a key regulator in the cold signaling pathway, can activate downstream cold-responsive genes, including COR15A and RD29A, and plays a central role in cold acclimation2. However, most existing studies have focused on individual gene characterization and expression profiling, while comprehensive analyses of regulatory networks at the genome scale remain limited. This lack of high-quality reference genome resources has constrained systematic investigations of cold tolerance mechanisms in M. robusta. Species in the genus Malus generally exhibit high levels of genome heterozygosity, posing substantial challenges for genome assembly and downstream functional analyses. The construction of chromosome-scale Haplotype-resolved genomes is therefore essential for resolving homologous chromosome structures, investigating potential Haplotype-specific gene distributions, and supporting analyses of complex traits such as cold tolerance. Such genomic resources are particularly valuable for rootstock improvement and comparative genomics within the genus Malus.

In this study, we generated a haplotype-resolved genome assembly for the apple species M. robusta by integrating PacBio HiFi, Oxford Nanopore ultra-long reads, and Hi-C sequencing. Through multi-technology integration, approximately 53.87 Gb of high-quality long-read data was obtained, with an average read length of 16,782 bp. K-mer analysis estimated a genome size of ~636 Mb, a sequencing depth of 41 × , a repeat content of 48.8%, and a heterozygosity rate of 2.99%. The final chromosome-level assemblies of the two haplotypes (HapA and HapB) reached sizes of 628.38 Mb and 664.60 Mb, respectively. BUSCO assessment revealed high completeness scores of 99.6% for HapA and 99.5% for HapB, indicating a high-quality and complete assembly. Repeat annotation showed that LTR retrotransposons accounted for 35.82% and 43.12% of the HapA and HapB genomes, respectively, while GC contents were 35.48% and 35.30%. For gene annotation, 49,268 and 51,070 protein-coding genes were predicted in HapA and HapB, respectively (Fig. 1).

This study presents a chromosome-scale, haplotype-resolved genome assembly of M. robusta with comprehensive annotations, providing a foundational resource for investigating cold tolerance, comparative genomics, and rootstock breeding in Malus. However, several functional analyses remain to be explored, including haplotype-specific cold-response gene clusters, CBF/DREB copy number expansion, and transposable element-mediated cold stress regulation. Transcriptomic validation of haplotype-specific expression patterns under cold stress also represents a priority for future studies to fully exploit this genomic resource.

Methods

Plant materials

On April 6, 2023, young leaves from five-year-old plants of the original cultivar M. robusta were collected at the Shandong Institute of Pomology, Tai’an, Shandong Province, China.

DNA extraction and genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue of the original M. robusta var. octandra using the CTAB method. Approximately 10 μg of high-molecular-weight DNA was assessed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis, showing that most fragments exceeded 40 kb, suitable for long-read library construction. The DNA was then sheared to ~15 kb using a Megaruptor instrument and subsequently purified and concentrated with AMPure® PB beads to prepare sequencing libraries.

A PacBio SMRTbell library was prepared using the SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 following the manufacturer’s protocol, including DNA repair, end polishing, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and exonuclease treatment. Fragments were size-selected with the SageELF system, and the library was sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform in circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode. This produced 53.87 Gb of high-quality HiFi data (3.21 million reads) with an average read length of 16.8 kb and mean quality value (QV) of 0.998, corresponding to ~83× genome coverage.

Hi-C libraries were prepared from fresh leaf tissue by cross-linking chromatin with formaldehyde. The cross-linked DNA was digested with HindIII, end-repaired with biotin-labeled nucleotides, and subjected to proximity ligation. Following reverse cross-linking and proteinase K treatment, DNA was purified, fragmented, and enriched for biotin-labeled fragments to construct sequencing libraries. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating ~61.4 GB of raw data (~106.3 Gb in total bases). After quality control, high-quality paired-end reads were obtained for downstream Hi-C analyses.

For ultralong-read sequencing, a genomic DNA library was prepared using the Oxford Nanopore ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK110) and sequenced on a PromethION R10.4.1 flow cell. Basecalling with a minimum quality threshold of Q10 produced ~22.5 Gb of raw data. Reads shorter than 20 kb were subsequently removed, resulting in a high-quality dataset of ~21.6 Gb, with an N50 of 100 kb, an average read length of 101.7 kb, and a mean Q-score of 12. These ultralong reads were primarily used to resolve complex repetitive regions during genome assembly.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Leaf tissue from M. robusta was collected for RNA extraction using the TRIzol reagent protocol. RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Following quality assessment, 3 μg of total RNA was used to construct transcriptome libraries. Library concentration was measured with a Qubit® RNA Assay Kit. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating ~9.93 Gb of high-quality paired-end reads (33.1 million read pairs, 2 × 150 bp).

Genome survey

The genome size and complexity of M. robusta was assessed using PacBio HiFi reads via k-mer analysis. K-mer counting was performed with Jellyfish5 (v2.2.10) using the parameters “count -t 32 -C -s 3 G -m 21”. GenomeScope6 (v2.0) was subsequently applied with parameters “-k 21 -p 2” to estimate genome size, the proportion of repetitive sequences, and heterozygosity. The results indicated that the genome of M. robusta is approximately 636.8 Mb in size, with repetitive sequences comprising ~48.8% and a heterozygosity rate of 2.99% (Fig. 2). Ploidy analysis supported a diploid model for M. robusta, with a model fit confidence of 94.76%, indicative of a highly heterozygous genome.

Genome assembly

In this study, the M. robusta genome was de novo assembled using HiFi reads, ONT reads, and Hi-C data. Initial assembly was performed using Hifiasm7 (v0.25.0-r726) in Hi-C mode, integrating the three data types to generate two haplotype-resolved contig sets, designated Hap1 and Hap2. The initial assemblies yielded total lengths of approximately 638.12 MB for Hap1 and 671.78 MB for Hap2, with contig N50 values of 36.35 MB and 39.05 MB, respectively(Table 1). Telomere screening of the initial contigs identified telomeric repeat sequences at the ends of 18 contigs in Hap1 and 23 contigs in Hap2.

We utilized the juicer, 3D-DNA8 and Juicebox pipeline to process the Hi-C interaction data, performing clustering, ordering, and orientation correction, which enabled the anchoring of contigs onto 17 chromosomes. To standardize chromosome nomenclature and facilitate comparative analyses, the assembled genomes were aligned against the genome of the closely related species M. baccata using MUMmer9 (v4.0.1). The analysis revealed that the majority of chromosomes of Hap1 exhibited higher sequence similarity to M. baccata than those of Hap2 (Fig. 3). Based on this relationship, the haplotype set with higher sequence similarity Hap1 was designated HapA, while Hap2 was designated HapB, and chromosome numbering was assigned accordingly.Subsequently, manual inspection and boundary correction of the chromosomes were performed using juicerbox 2matrix10 (v0.0.2). The Hi-C contact matrix displayed strong interaction signals along the chromosome diagonals with low off-diagonal noise, confirming the high quality and accuracy of the chromosome-scale assembly (Fig. 4a). Telomere identification was performed on the chromosome-level assemblies using Quartet11 (v1.2.5) with parameters “-c plant -m 100” (Fig. 4b). The telomeric repeat motif was identified as AAACCCT, corresponding to the reverse complement of the canonical plant telomere repeat (5′-TTTAGGG-3′) conserved in most angiosperms. The results showed that in HapA, telomeric sequences were detected at both ends of 14 chromosomes, while 3 chromosomes contained telomeric repeats at only one end. In HapB, 13 chromosomes harbored telomeric sequences at both ends, and 4 chromosomes contained telomeric repeats at only one end, indicating a high level of chromosome assembly completeness.

The final chromosome-level assemblies were subsequently evaluated using BUSCO12 (v6.0.0, embryophyta_odb10). The genome completeness (C score) of HapA was 99.63%, comprising 59.61% single-copy and 40.02% duplicated BUSCOs. HapB exhibited a genome completeness of 99.50%, with 58.36% single-copy and 41.14% duplicated BUSCOs, indicating that both haplotype assemblies possess highly complete gene space(Table 2).

Comparative analysis revealed that the proximal ~10 Mb region at the beginning of ChrB17 in HapB is syntenic with ChrB14 (Fig. 5a). To assess the accuracy of this structural configuration, PacBio HiFi reads were aligned to the assembled genome and visualized using IGV13 (v2.16.0). The alignments showed continuous and consistent coverage across this region, with no abrupt drops in coverage or misalignment patterns typically associated with assembly breakpoints. These observations indicate that the detected structural variation is unlikely to result from assembly errors. Genome annotation

Repeat sequences were annotated using an integrated strategy. First, an initial repeat library was constructed with RepeatModeler14 (v2.0.1) using the parameters “-database -pa 5 -LTRStruct”. Transposable element annotation of the genome was then performed using EDTA15 (v2.2.2) to generate a preliminary TE library. The classified repeats from RepeatModeler were subsequently combined with the EDTA library, and redundant entries were removed to obtain a non-redundant repeat sequence library. Finally, RepeatMasker16 (v2.0.1) with parameters “-e rmblast -pa 45 -lib” was used to identify transposable elements and low-complexity repeats in the genome.The results indicated a high level of genome repetitiveness. In HapA, long terminal repeats (LTRs) accounted for 35.82% and DNA transposons for 9.75%; in HapB, LTRs accounted for 43.12% and DNA transposons for 9.06% (Table 3).

Protein-coding genes in the M. robusta genome were identified using an integrated pipeline that combined deep-learning-based de novo prediction with transcriptome and homologous protein evidence. The workflow was executed in two stages: (1) de novo gene prediction: We employed Helixer (https://www.plabipd.de/helixer_main.html), a deep-learning-based framework, to generate initial structural gene annotations. This stage produced a primary set of gene models in GFF3 format based solely on genomic sequence patterns. (2) Evidence-driven integration and refinement: EviAnn17 (v2.0.4) was employed to generate high-quality gene models from the RNA-seq data, incorporating protein alignments from related species (M. domestica ‘Fuji’ and M. honanensis) and Helixer annotations. This integrative approach optimizes exon–intron structures and resolves conflicting predictions at the same locus by selecting models with the highest cumulative evidence support. The software was executed with parameters “-r rna.list -p ref.protein -c helixer.gff -t 40 -d 2 --partial --functional --verbose”. Functional annotation was performed for the predicted protein-coding genes. The predicted protein sequences were aligned against multiple public databases using DIAMOND18 (v2.0), including the GenBank Nr, SwissProt19, and TrEMBL. InterProScan20 (v5.59) was employed to identify functional protein domains and to assign Gene Ontology (GO)21 terms. Protein sequences were further mapped to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database using KAAS (https://www.genome.jp/tools/kaas/) to annotate gene functions and associated metabolic pathways. In addition, AHRD22 (v3.3) was used to integrate annotation results from SwissProt, and the TrEMBL, thereby providing more comprehensive and reliable functional annotations. Based on the combined annotations, 48,154 and 49,794 genes were annotated in NR, 35,912 and 36,733 in SwissProt, 48,070 and 49,703 in TrEMBL for HapA and HapB, respectively (Table 5).

Genome collinearity analysis

Homology analysis of the two Haplotypes of M. robusta and their parental genomes-M. baccata and M. prunifolia-was performed using JCVI23 (v0.1.dev1 + g902af29a1). Orthologous gene pairs were identified using the JCVI “catalog ortholog” module with a stringent c-score cutoff of “-cscore 0.99”, and syntenic blocks were defined using the “synteny screen” module with “-minspan 30”. Karyotype-based synteny analysis revealed structural conservation among the genomes. Between M. robusta HapA and M. baccata, 64 syntenic blocks and 28,393 gene pairs were identified, while between HapB and M. prunifolia, 317 syntenic blocks and 24,023 gene pairs were detected. Comparison of HapA and HapB showed strong collinearity, highlighting homologous gene regions and indicating a high degree of structural conservation across the genomes (Fig. 6).

Data Records

Raw PacBio HiFi, Hi-C, ONT and RNA-seq data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number SRP47016024. PacBio Sequel II HiFi sequencing data totaling ~53.87 Gb, corresponding to ~83× raw coverage (approximately ~41× effective haplotype coverage). Hi-C sequencing data generated on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (~106.28 Gb, ~164× coverage). ONT sequencing data totaling ~21.6 Gb and transcriptome sequencing data generated on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform totaling ~9.93 Gb of paired-end reads are available within this project. Chromosome-scale assembly and gene annotation datasets have been uploaded to figshare25. The assembly genomes files of M. robusta were stored under the accession GCA_056882635.126 and GCA_056882055.127.

Technical Validation

As shown in Fig. 4a, the accuracy of the genome assembly is reflected by the relatively independent Hi-C signals between 17 pairs of chromosomes. Genome completeness was further evaluated using BUSCO analysis based on the embryophyta_odb10 database, revealing completeness of 99.63% and 99.50% for HapA and HapB, respectively. Among these, single-copy BUSCOs accounted for 59.61% in HapA and 58.36% in HapB, further indicating the high quality of the assemblies.BUSCO assessment of protein-coding genes showed that, out of 1,614 highly conserved orthologs, 99.40% were complete in HapA, including 46.00% single-copy and 53.40% duplicated genes; HapB displayed 99.1% completeness, with 45.10% single-copy and 54.00% duplicated genes. Fragmented BUSCOs were 0.20% for HapA and 0.10% for HapB, while missing BUSCOs were 0.40% and 0.70%, respectively, indicating a high level of completeness in the protein-coding gene annotations.
