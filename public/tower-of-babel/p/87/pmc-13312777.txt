Reintroduction of lignin-modifying enzymes in the brown-rot fungus Gloeophyllum trabeum restores ligninolytic activities but diminishes lignocellulose degradation
Authors: Nandin Ganjoloo, Weiran Li, Jiwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungi have evolved two distinct strategies, white-rot and brown-rot, to degrade lignocellulose in plant biomass. White-rot fungi utilize lignin-modifying enzymes (LMEs) to deconstruct lignin and access carbohydrates, whereas brown-rot fungi, which arose from white-rot ancestral lineages, have largely lost LME activities. Instead, brown-rot fungi rely on a small redox metabolite-mediated Fenton system that generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) to rapidly deconstruct lignocellulose and selectively remove carbohydrates. The abandonment of LMEs in brown-rot fungi suggests an intriguing evolutionary strategy to streamline decay machinery, but it remains unclear why fungi evolved this way. Here, we reintroduced LME genes from the white-rot fungus Trametes versicolor into the model brown-rot fungus Gloeophyllum trabeum to create hybrid fungal systems and evaluate LME function within the Fenton-dominant brown-rot context. Analysis of lignocellulose degradation showed that lignin disruption can be partially restored in LME mutants, thereby shifting the typical carbohydrate-selective decay mode of G. trabeum. However, constitutive LME expression also caused pronounced growth defects and reduced the overall rate of lignocellulose degradation, specifically decreasing cellulose and hemicellulose removal. Correlation analysis between genotypes and phenotypes indicates that LMEs, although prevalently used for delignification in fungi, are fundamentally incompatible with the carbohydrate-selective brown-rot system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40694-026-00218-y.

Introduction

Wood-decay fungi are the primary decomposers of carbon sequestered in plant biomass and therefore play crucial roles as dominant bioagents in driving global carbon cycling [1–4]. Through photosynthesis, plants fix atmospheric carbon into carbohydrate polymers, such as cellulose and hemicellulose, and then embed these polymers in lignin to assemble the lignocellulose macromolecular [5]. The structural heterogeneity and diverse cross-linking bonds within lignocellulose confer its intrinsic recalcitrance to biodegradation [5]. In nature, fungi have evolved efficient strategies to degrade lignocellulose and have developed two distinct decay modes that give rise to white and brown rot in wood substrates [6–10]. White-rot fungi employ lignin-modifying enzymes (LMEs) to preferentially degrade lignin, with cellulolysis occurring later, or to degrade all lignocellulose components simultaneously; consequently, their decay can be either lignin-selective or simultaneous [6, 7]. In contrast, brown-rot fungi use a carbohydrate-selective degradation strategy, relying on oxidative agents to rapidly modify and partially degrade the lignin barrier, thereby enabling selective utilization of the embedded polysaccharides [8–10]. Owing to their remarkable capacity to deconstruct lignocellulose, both types of wood-decay fungi have attracted considerable research interest for uncovering novel lignocellulolytic mechanisms with potential biotechnological applications in biomanufacturing and environmental engineering [5–7].

Lignin removal is a key step in fungal lignocellulose degradation because it eliminates the protective barrier surrounding polysaccharides. To accomplish this, white-rot fungi employ LMEs, including manganese peroxidase (MnP; EC 1.11.1.13), lignin peroxidase (LiP; EC 1.11.1.14), versatile peroxidase (VP; EC 1.11.1.16), dye-decolorizing peroxidase (DyP), and laccase (LCC; EC 1.10.3.2), to degrade or modify the lignin polymer [6]. These LMEs were first isolated and characterized in the white-rot fungus Phanerochaete chrysosporium and later in Trametes versicolor, in which multiple manganese peroxidase isoforms were described [13–15]. Since then, the ligninolytic activity of LMEs has been recognized as a key characteristic of Basidiomycete white-rot fungi. Although collectively categorized as LMEs, these enzymes differ in their catalytic properties and, consequently, in their specific roles in lignin degradation. MnP, LiP, VP, and DyP are heme-containing class II peroxidases (PODs) that use H2O2 as the oxidant and catalyze two-electron transfer reactions to drive the oxidation of phenolic and non-phenolic lignin derivatives and the cleavage of C–C and C–O–C bonds in lignin polymers [16]. In contrast, LCC is a multicopper, low-redox-potential oxidase that uses O2 as the oxidant in one-electron transfer reactions. It primarily catalyzes the oxidation of phenolic lignin substrates, and its role in lignin depolymerization typically requires indirect oxidation mediated by small-molecule redox mediators [17, 18]. Since their discovery, the functions of white-rot fungal LMEs in lignin degradation have been extensively studied using enzymes purified from native fungal species or heterologous expression hosts, as summarized in several reviews [19–22]. However, the in vivo roles of LMEs within Basidiomycete wood-decay fungal systems remain poorly characterized.

In contrast to white-rot fungi, brown-rot fungi exhibit diminished LME activity and lignin-mineralization capacity [9, 13, 14]. Genomic studies indicate that, during fungal evolution, brown-rot fungi lost LME genes and more than 30% of the genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), relative to white-rot fungi [14–17]. Instead, brown-rot fungi rely on reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated via low-molecular-weight (LMW) redox metabolite-mediated Fenton chemistry to degrade lignin and other lignocellulosic polymers [8, 18–20]. These highly reactive ROS enable rapid lignin deconstruction, thereby facilitating the selective utilization of encrusted cellulose [18, 21]. One widely accepted model proposes that fungal-synthesized LMW metabolites or chelators, such as dimethoxyhydroquinone or variegatic acid, drive the extracellular Fenton reaction (Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + HO− + HO·), producing hydroxyl radicals (HO·) [8, 17, 28, 29, 31]. In this model, reductive low-molecular-weight (LMW) metabolites or chelators are small enough to diffuse through the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix and penetrate the pores of the wood cell wall. The EPS facilitates a gradient in which low pH and high oxalate concentrations occur near the fungal hyphae, while higher pH and lower oxalate concentrations occur within the lignocellulosic matrix. This gradient promotes the transport of iron and LMW chelators into the wood cell wall, where chelator-mediated Fenton chemistry and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production are localized to the lignocellulose rather than the hyphal surface. In doing so, LMW metabolites shuttle electrons from fungal metabolism to extracellular Fe3⁺ and O₂, driving oxidative depolymerization of the substrate [22–24]. Although LMW metabolite-mediated Fenton chemistry has been proposed as the primary mechanism underlying brown-rot efficacy, researchers have also reported that white-rot fungi possess a similar system [18, 24–29]. Thus, two distinct lignin-degrading machineries, the LMW metabolite-mediated Fenton system and LMEs, may coexist within a single fungus, raising the question of how they are regulated to cooperate. Because brown-rot fungi inherently lack LMEs, they provide an ideal platform for examining in vivo interactions between these two degradation machineries by genetically complementing the missing LME genes.

The goal of this research is to evaluate how the recombinant expression of LMEs in a ROS-dominant brown-rot fungus affects lignocellulose degradation. To this end, we recombinantly expressed the MnP1, MnP2, LiP2, and LCC3 genes from the white-rot fungus Trametes versicolor in the brown-rot fungus Gloeophyllum trabeum, for which we have recently established a robust transformation platform for genetic manipulation [30]. Given the ligninolytic functions of LMEs, we hypothesized that reintroducing these white-rot-characteristic genes into G. trabeum to create hybrid fungal systems would shift the carbohydrate-selective degradation pattern toward a decay mode with enhanced lignin removal. Meanwhile, because brown-rot fungal adaptation tends to thrive through strengthening the ROS mechanism and abandoning LME genes to achieve a faster lignocellulose deconstruction rate, LMEs may be incompatible with the LMW metabolite-mediated Fenton system [22–25]. This incompatibility is supported by our recent work showing that laccase overloading inhibits Fenton pathways and ROS production by competing for electron sources [31]. Therefore, we also hypothesized that LME expression in G. trabeum would interfere with the native brown-rot ROS systems, generating hybrid fungal strains with an overall slower lignocellulose degradation rate and, consequently, a reduced carbon utilization rate. In this paper, we tested these hypotheses by examining lignocellulose degradation patterns in G. trabeum mutants expressing different LMEs.

Results

Phylogeny and transcription of LME genes during lignocellulose degradation by host white-rot fungi.

We analyzed transcriptomic data from two representative white-rot fungi, Trametes versicolor and Pleurotus ostreatus, obtained from Data Set S1 of the Gene Expression Omnibus database GSE108189, to characterize the temporal transcriptional patterns and phylogenetic relationships of LME-encoding genes, with the aim of guiding gene selection for functional studies in this work (Fig. 1) [18, 32]. These transcriptomic data were generated in a previous study that used a spatially segmented experimental design to monitor temporal changes in fungal decay of aspen wood wafers from early to late stages [18]. The results showed that transcription of class II POD genes in both fungi was predominantly upregulated at later decay stages, consistent with increased lignin oxidation as decay progresses [18]. In contrast, only a small subset of genes, which includes three MnPs in P. ostreatus and one MnP, one VP, and one DyP in T. versicolor, was significantly upregulated during early decay, underscoring their potential roles in the onset of decay. To enable functional studies of white-rot fungal POD genes via recombinant expression in a brown-rot fungal system, we selected representative genes from the dominant phylogenetic clades of MnP and LiP, but not VP or DyP, given their extensively characterized, canonical ligninolytic functions in previous studies [33–36]. Genes were selected from T. versicolor, as this species contains a more diverse set of PODs. The significance of decay-stage regulation and transcriptional abundance was considered when selecting candidate homologous genes, because these have been reported to be crucial for the in vivo functions of LMEs in their white-rot host fungi [37]. Based on these criteria, representative LME genes encoding two MnPs, one LiP, and one LCC were selected. Specifically, these included TveMnp1 (gene ID: Tve|g2437, GSE108189) and TveMnp2 (Tve|g6978), which showed the highest transcriptional levels at the early and late decay stages, respectively, and represented the MnP clade (Fig. 1A, B). TveLiP2 (Tve|g6976) represented the LiP clade and exhibited the highest transcriptional abundance (RPKM > 10,000) among all LiP homologs that were universally upregulated at the later decay stages (Fig. 1A, B). Additionally, TveLCC3 (Tve|g248), which encodes a laccase and was used in our previous work [30], was selected based on its highest transcriptional abundance (RPKM > 400) among all laccase homologs (Fig. 1B).

LME recombinant expression in the brown-rot fungus Gloeophyllum trabeum generates the hybrid fungal system.

We transformed the selected LME genes, individually, into the typical brown-rot fungus, G. trabeum, to create hybrid fungal strains that possess lignocellulose-degrading machinery from both white- and brown-rot systems. Expression cassettes for MnP1, MnP2, and LiP2 were constructed in this study, driven by the Rhodonia placenta β-tubulin promoter (Pβ-tub) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase terminator (Tgpd) (Fig. 2A). The LCC3 expression cassette was adopted from our previous work, in which the Rhodonia placenta glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase promoter (Pgpd) and β-tubulin terminator (Tβ-tub) were used to control gene expression [30]. Co- transformation of each LME gene together with a hygromycin resistance marker into freshly prepared G. trabeum protoplasts was performed as described previously [30], followed by screening and validation of transformant colonies for hygromycin resistance (n = 50–60 per genotype) and for LME gene cassette insertion by PCR (Fig. 2B). Three rounds of subcultures on hygromycin-then-no-hygromycin conditions were conducted before PCR validation. Three representative transformants per genotype, LiP2: #2, #13, #21; MnP1: #2, #7, #26; and MnP2: #16, #17, #34, were selected for further functional analyses based on their wild-type (WT) colony morphology on C- and N-rich, Yeast extract-Malt extract-Glucose Agar (YMGA) plates (Figs. S1, S2). qRT-PCR analyses confirmed successful expression of the introduced LME genes in these transformants but not in the WT (see results for degradation phenotypic tests). Enzymatic activity assays ultimately verified the activities of the recombinant MnP1, MnP2, and LCC3 enzymes, although activity levels varied among transformants within the same genotype (Fig. 2C). However, no clear lignin peroxidase activity was detected in the LiP2 transformants. We observed no differences in colony radial growth between the mutants and the WT when grown on plates containing the C- and N-rich YMGA or the organic N-poor Highley’s minimal medium (HMM) supplemented with 1% glucose (Figs. S1 and S2A, B). This suggests that recombinant LME expression does not affect polarized mycelial extension when readily available nutrients are present. In contrast, the growth phenotype measured as total mycelial biomass showed a different pattern. Substantial reductions in total biomass accumulation were observed in the LME mutants (Fig. 3A, B), particularly in those expressing MnP2 and LCC3. This indicates that LME overexpression may impose a metabolic burden on protein synthesis and disrupt the metabolic flux required for mycelial biomass production, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that recombinant protein overexpression can interfere with cellular growth [38–41].

To assess the impact of LME recombinant expression on lignocellulose utilization, we measured the mycelial radial growth rates of the mutants on two natural biomass substrates, corn stover and spruce, and compared them with their growth on YMGA (Figs. S1 and S2G, H). Both lignocellulosic materials are well-studied, utilizable carbon sources for G. trabeum [42–45]. However, because it is technically challenging to separate mycelial cells from these substrates, we did not directly quantify total mycelial biomass on them. Our measurements revealed clear radial growth defects in mutants expressing MnP2 and LCC3 on lignocellulose relative to YMGA (Figs. S2A, B, E, F), suggesting that the complex lignocellulose substrates further diminished mutant growth. This impairment is likely due to additional metabolic and secretory burdens beyond those associated with the synthesis of abundant lignocellulolytic enzymes.

Overall, the impaired growth phenotype in the LME mutant is consistent with our hypothesis that complementing LME into the brown-rot fungal system interferes with its well-adapted machinery for lignocellulose utilization. Nevertheless, not all LMEs behaved similarly: MnP1 mutants showed no detectable growth defects on any of the tested carbon sources, including both simple carbon substrates and complex lignocellulose (Fig. 3, Figs. S1 and S2). It remains unclear whether this difference is due to variation in the catalytic properties of individual LMEs. Moreover, the absence of growth defects in MnP1 mutants and in mutants expressing only the hygromycin-resistant aminoglycoside phosphotransferase (HygR) makes it plausible to attribute all mutation defects to metabolic burden. Gene-specific contributions, such as disruption of the redox environment by LME's oxidative activity, cannot be definitively ruled out. Further experiments excluded potential LME-driven toxic effects by growing the mutants on two generally recognized aromatic toxins, 1,4-benzoquinone, and corn stover extractives rich in phenolics, both of which exhibited inhibitory effects in WT strains (Fig. 3C, D).

LME recombinant expression diminishes the overall lignocellulose-degrading capacity.

Using corn stover as the substrate for solid-state culture, we evaluated the lignocellulose-degrading capacities of the LME-expressing mutants (Fig. 4). Previous studies have demonstrated that G. trabeum readily degrades corn residues as a carbon source and represents a potential method for value-added conversion of agricultural waste [43–46]. Here, working on corn stover enabled us to assess the agriculturally relevant biotechnological potential of the LME mutants. Because soluble carbohydrates constitute a substantial fraction of corn stover (25–30%), conventional mass-loss measurements based on the whole substrate do not accurately reflect the fungal capacity to degrade lignocellulose polymers. Therefore, we quantified the net mass change of the “insoluble” lignocellulose fraction after subtracting the solubilized portion via hot-water extraction, as illustrated in Fig. 4A [9]. This approach allowed us to evaluate the overall lignocellulose-solubilizing intensity as a measure of fungal degradation capacity. We observed reduced lignocellulose-solubilization rates in LME mutants compared with the WT, which solubilized 36.8 ± 1.2% of the insoluble lignocellulose after 30 days of culture (Fig. 4B). This reduction was particularly evident in mutants expressing MnP1 and LCC3. Notably, all three LCC3-expressing mutants consistently exhibited slower degradation rates than the WT, in agreement with the impaired growth phenotype of LCC3 mutants on both simple and complex carbon sources (Fig. 3). Decreased lignocellulose-solubilization rates were also observed in MnP1 mutants and were statistically significant in all three strains (Fig. 4B). Given that growth phenotypes were not affected in MnP1 mutants, it is likely that recombinant MnP1 expression interfered with the intrinsic brown-rot degradation system, thereby reducing the overall lignocellulose-solubilizing capacity.

Variable effects on lignocellulose degradation were observed among the different MnP2 mutants. Consistent with the impaired growth phenotype, the #34 mutant showed a significant 51.1% decrease in lignocellulose-solubilizing capacity relative to WT (Welch’s t-test, p < 0.01; Fig. 4B). This is in line with the hypothesized causal mechanism of a “metabolic burden.” In contrast, the #17 mutant, which displayed growth impairment like that of #34, exhibited a 13.3% higher lignocellulose solubilization rate than WT (Welch’s t-test, p < 0.05), suggesting that a hidden factor may have compensated for lignocellulose solubilization. By comparison, the #16 MnP2 mutant showed no change in lignocellulose solubilization rate. Given that this mutant exhibited the same growth and degradation phenotypes as WT, including comparable removal rates of lignocellulose components and similar enzyme activities (as discussed in the following sections), it seems that MnP2 expression itself does not appear to confer ligninolytic function (Figs. 4C, and 5). The variable lignocellulose-solubilizing capacities among MnP2 mutants might instead reflect differences in MnP2 protein expression levels caused by random insertion sites or variable gene copy numbers in the transformants [30].

Recombinant expression of LME enhances lignin degradation and alters the fungal degradation pattern.

To evaluate the impact of recombinantly expressed LME on the degradation of primary lignocellulosic components, particularly lignin, we conducted compositional analysis of the fungal-degraded, remaining insoluble lignocellulose (Table 1), and calculated the removal rates of cellulose, hemicellulose, and Klason lignin relative to their original contents in the blank sample (Fig. 4C). The results showed that the WT strain solubilized and removed 37.5% of cellulose and 36.7% of hemicellulose, but only 20.7% of Klason lignin (Fig. 4C), leading to the increased L/C (lignin-to-cellulose; Fig. 4D) and L/H (lignin-to-hemicellulose; Fig. 4E) ratios in the remaining insoluble lignocellulose residue. This pattern is consistent with the typical carbohydrate-selective brown-rot degradation mode [9].

Our measurements showed that the removal rates of all three lignocellulose components were consistently reduced in the MnP1 and LCC3 mutants, as well as in MnP2 #34, relative to the WT (Fig. 4C), in agreement with the diminished overall lignocellulose-solubilizing capacities of these mutants. In contrast, MnP2 mutants #16 and #17 did not show significant changes in the removal rates of the three lignocellulose components, whereas the latter mutant exhibited slight increases in cellulose and hemicellulose removal.

The substantially decreased L/C and L/H ratios in the remaining lignocellulose residues indicate that lignin degradation was proportionally enhanced by the MnP1 and LCC3 mutants (Fig. 4D, E). This is consistent with previously reported ligninolytic functions of these two LMEs, suggesting that they contributed to the selective lignin degradation observed in the mutants [47, 48]. In contrast, the proportional removal of lignin was unchanged in MnP2 mutants #16 and #34 and was reduced in mutant #17, casting doubt on whether recombinant expression of MnP2 confers the anticipated ligninolytic function.

The enzyme assays verified the activities of the recombinant LMEs in mutants grown on corn stover and revealed their distinct effects on the production of cellulase and xylanase and on iron-reducing activity (Fig. 5 and Fig. S4). Consistent with the PCA results, LCC3 expression was strongly and negatively correlated with biomass growth on the simple carbon source glucose (Cr = − 0.90), as well as on corn stover (Cr = − 0.60) and spruce (Cr = − 0.91); with cellulase (Cr = − 0.68) and xylanase (Cr = -0.95) productivities; and with iron-reduction capacity (Cr = − 0.86). By contrast, LCC expression showed positive correlations with detoxification-associated growth on corn stover extractives (Cr = 0.57) and on 1,4-BQ (Cr = 0.65), suggesting a role in detoxification.

MnP1 likely impairs overall lignocellulose degradation by disrupting hydrolase production and the Fenton system, without causing broad defects in growth or detoxification. In contrast, LCC3 appears to impair degradation through the combined effects of reduced biomass growth, enhanced ligninolytic and detoxifying activities, and diminished (hemi)cellulolytic hydrolase and Fenton activities. Together, our results demonstrate that introducing LMEs into the brown-rot fungus can disrupt its native lignocellulolytic machinery, including cellulase, xylanase, and Fenton redox systems, alter carbon metabolism required for biomass growth, and shift the carbohydrate-selective decay mode toward a more non-selective pattern.

Discussion

Our research supports that the LMEs prevalently used by wood-decay fungi to degrade lignocellulose are incompatible with the degradative system that evolved more recently in brown-rot fungi. In brown-rot fungi, LME functions have been lost to strengthen a non-enzymatic system that relies on LMW metabolite-mediated Fenton chemistry and its ROS products (e.g., ·OH) [14, 19]. Here, we show that forced expression of white-rot LME genes encoding manganese peroxidases (MnPs) and laccase (LCC) in the model brown-rot fungus Gloeophyllum trabeum causes substantial phenotypic defects in fungal growth, enzyme production, and carbon utilization on lignocellulosic biomass, and reduces degradation of insoluble lignocellulose, particularly cellulose and hemicellulose removal. This is likely because reacquiring LME functions disrupts the well-adapted brown-rot system, which has been fine-tuned over long-term evolution to achieve rapid wood degradation [9, 14]. We also show that recombinant expression of LMEs proportionally increases lignin degradation, suggesting that restoring LME genes in the brown-rot fungus can recover ligninolytic functions and shift its typical carbohydrate-selective decay mode, albeit at the cost of reducing its overall lignocellulose-degrading capacity.

The proportionally enhanced lignin degradation observed in G. trabeum mutants expressing LMEs, particularly MnP1 and LCC3, aligns with the ligninolytic roles of LMEs reported in previous studies [37, 49–55]. For example, Saloheimo and Niku-Paavola used recombinant expression of a Phlebia radiata (a white-rot fungus) laccase gene in the cellulolytic fungus Trichoderma reesei to confirm the in vivo role of laccases in removing monomeric lignin-related compounds from pulping residues [49]. Sugiura et al. overexpressed MnP in the white-rot fungus Phanerochaete sordida YK-624 and elucidated the ligninolytic functions of a native manganese peroxidase in this species [50]. More recently, using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene disruption in the model white-rot fungus Pleurotus ostreatus, Nakazawa et al. finally proved in vivo that LMEs, especially MnPs and VPs, play essential roles in the degradation of natural lignin [37]. In addition to investigations of LMEs’ functions in their native white-rot producers, the brown-rot fungus G. trabeum has been used to express both heterologous and homologous laccase genes, although the in vivo ligninolytic activities of these laccases on natural substrates were not examined in earlier studies [30, 51]. Here, we further show that, in addition to laccase, the G. trabeum system can also express manganese peroxidases, and we confirm that recombinant expression of MnP and laccase proportionally enhances lignin degradation relative to holocellulose degradation. Thus, our results suggest that complementing the lost LME genes in the brown-rot fungus can restore its ligninolytic functions.

The two MnP isoforms examined in this study, MnP1 and MnP2, give rise to distinct mutant phenotypes. In contrast to MnP1, MnP2 mutants exhibit substantial strain-to-strain phenotypic variation, suggesting that the effects may be dosage-dependent rather than strictly genotype-determined. The unchanged proportional degradation of lignin suggests that MnP2 may influence these phenotypes through mechanisms other than its ligninolytic function. Previous biochemical studies have shown that MnP isozymes differ in expression levels, pH optima, isoelectric points, induction sensitivity, and stability [56–58]. Relevant to this work, the two MnPs analyzed here differ in their temporal transcriptional functions across decay stages, indicating that they may play distinct roles in catalyzing lignin degradation [18]. Further studies that delineate and compare the ligninolytic functions and catalytic properties of MnP1 and MnP2 will help clarify why they contribute differently to the phenotypes of G. trabeum mutants.

Although recombinant expression of LMEs enhances lignin degradation, we also observed a drastic decrease in the overall lignocellulose-degrading capacity of the G. trabeum mutants (Figs. 4 and 5), particularly in those expressing MnP1 and LCC3. This finding supports our hypothesis that restoring LME function interferes with the native brown-rot system, which primarily relies on LMW metabolite-redox-mediated Fenton chemistry, rather than LMEs, for efficient radical attack on lignocellulose structures, including lignin [8, 9]. Although LMEs and Fenton chemistry use mechanistically distinct catalytic processes, they share overlapping pools of redox-active metabolites and oxidants. For instance, H2O2 can be consumed by both PODs and the Fenton reaction as an electron acceptor, and the reduced LMW metabolites, such as hydroquinones (e.g., 2,5-dimethoxyhydroquinone) that drive the production of Fenton reagents, Fe2+ and H2O2, can be oxidized by LMEs [59–62]. Thus, it is likely that LMEs compete for electrons, thereby diverting them from Fenton chemistry and ROS production. Consistent with this, our previous cell-free study showed that supplementing the quinone redox system with laccase or heme-thiolate peroxidase reduced the production of Fenton reagents [31]. In this work, we further show that in vivo expression of LMEs diminishes iron-reduction capacity at the hyphal fronts of G. trabeum mutants (Fig. 5A), indicating a disruption of Fenton chemistry-related potential.

Plant- and lignin-derived quinones are known to be cytotoxic to fungal species such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae [63]. Similarly, our assays show that growth of the G. trabeum WT strain is inhibited by 1,4-benzoquinone and corn stover phenolics. We therefore initially hypothesized that the impaired mycelial growth observed in LME mutants, particularly those expressing LCC3 and MnP2, was attributed to LME-exacerbated phenolic oxidation and quinone formation. However, our tests showed that these mutants exhibited increased biomass accumulation in media containing corn stover phenolic extractives and 1,4-benzoquinone compared with glucose-only conditions (Fig. 3D, E), thereby indicating a role for LME in detoxification. We propose that LMEs can mitigate quinone-associated toxicity via semiquinone radical-mediated polymerization or quinone degradation [64–66]. As a trade-off, these detoxification pathways may simultaneously disrupt redox shuttles that rely on reductive phenolics (e.g., hydroquinones) to generate ROS. Thus, LME expression enhances tolerance to toxic quinones but weakens the redox system required for brown-rot Fenton chemistry.

It is more likely that the mycelial growth defects in the LCC3 and MnP2 mutants result from the metabolic burden imposed by enzyme overexpression. Previous studies have shown that protein synthesis is energetically costly and can divert cellular energy away from biomass accumulation [38–41]. This effect is amplified when fungi are grown on complex substrates such as lignocellulose, where cells must allocate additional energy to produce large quantities of lignocellulolytic enzymes. Consistent with this, we observed a further reduction in fungal growth when the LME mutant was cultured on corn stover and spruce, relative to simple nutrient media. Nevertheless, the unchanged growth phenotype in the MnP1 mutants suggests that the defects cannot be attributed solely to the metabolic burden of overexpression.

White-rot and brown-rot are two primary fungal strategies for degrading woody biomass, and both are essential in driving the global carbon cycle. incomUnderstanding their degradation mechanisms is crucial for developing new biotechnologies to advance biorefineries and biomanufacturing. Here, we show that forcing these two mechanisms to operate concurrently, by expressing white-rot LMEs in a brown-rot chassis, can negatively affect the overall fungal capacity to deconstruct plant biomass. This incompatibility between LMEs and the brown-rot system may help explain the evolutionary loss of LME genes in brown-rot fungal lineages. Notably, this does not mean that combining the strengths of both strategies is unattainable. Instead, our data suggest that successful integration will likely require temporally segregated decay chemistries to avoid competition for shared redox intermediates and mediators, together with controlled LME dosage via inducible or otherwise tunable promoters. If such timing- and expression-level control can be engineered, it may be possible to promote lignin modification while delaying extensive removal of cellulose and hemicellulose, enabling more controlled deconstruction strategies with industrial relevance.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and culturing conditions

The brown-rot fungal strain Gloeophyllum trabeum ATCC 11539 obtained from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA)—Forest Products Laboratory (Madison, WI, USA) was used for genetic manipulation and lignocellulose degradation in this research. Its genome information is publicly available on JGI’s MycoCosm database [67]. The brown-rot fungal strain Rhodonia placenta MAD-698R SB12 was used to retrieve the promoter and terminator sequences to drive expression of the cross-species lignin-modifying enzyme (LME) genes [68]. The white-rot fungus Trametes versicolor A1-ATF is stored at the University of Minnesota’s Forest Fungal Collections, along with its genome, which was used to study the expression functions of LMEs [69].

Fungal strains involved in this research were maintained on YMGA plates (4 g/L Yeast extract, 10 g/L Malt extract, 10 g/L Glucose, and 15 g/L Agar) at 28 °C. Actively growing mycelia were harvested for DNA/RNA extraction by culturing the fungus in 100 mL YMG liquid medium for 4 days at 115 rpm and 28 °C. The 10-mm mycelial plugs were used to inoculate 100 mL modified Highley’s minimal medium (HMM) with low nitrogen (2 mM NH4NO3), 0.1% w/v glucose, 1% w/v spruce wood powder, and 5 mM succinate at pH 4.5 for enzyme extraction and activity assays [70]. Solid cultures in Petri dishes were prepared by inoculating a 10-mm mycelial plug of the G. trabeum wild-type (WT) strain or a transformant to measure mycelial radial growth and biomass accumulation and analyze the functions of LMEs in mutants. More details about the culturing methods are available for each experiment below.

Expression patterns and phylogenetic analysis of LME genes

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted to associate the temporal expression patterns with LME genes in white-rot fungi and to select candidate genes for genetic manipulation. Genes encoding the functions of lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidases (MnP), versatile peroxidase (VP), dye-decoloring peroxidase (DyP), and laccase (LCC) were searched and analyzed in this work. The phylogenetic analysis was done as previously described [18]. Specifically, amino acid sequences were aligned with MafftWS (version 7.310) with preset E-INS-i (accuracy-oriented) and scoring matrix BLOSUM62. The evolutionary distances with less than 5% in alignment gaps, missing data, and ambiguous bases were calculated with the Poisson correction method. The phylogenetic tree was created using MEGA7.0.18 with 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Transcriptomic data that measure the gene expression levels as the white-rot fungi T. versicolor and Pleurotus ostreaus decay wood were obtained from Zhang et al. (2019), which can be found in dataset GSE108189 in the Gene Expression Omnibus database [17, 36]. Expression levels and temporal expression patterns of the LME genes were presented as RPKM (reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads), and FDR values were calculated to assess differential gene expression between pairwise conditions. The Log2-normalized RPKM levels for all LMEs of two white-rot fungi were presented in the heatmap (Fig. 1B). The temporal expression data were collected from three sections of decayed aspen wood wafers that represent early, mid, and late decay stages based on the distance from the hyphal front, as previously reported [18].

Gene expression cassettes and genetic transformation in G. trabeum

The expression cassette of the hygromycin-resistant gene HygR was synthesized using fusion PCR and cloned into the pUC19 plasmid (Invitrogen, MA, USA) to generate plasmid pGHT. Specifically, the CDS (coding DNA sequence) region of HygR was first PCR-amplified from pMD-1 using the primer pair GAPDH-HY-F2/R2 [38]. The promoter of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; PosplRSB12_1|1041472) and terminator of β-tubulin (PosplRSB12_1|1181067) were amplified from R. placenta MAD-698R SB12 genomic DNA by primer sets GAPDH-HY-F1/R1 and GAPDH-HY-F3/R3, respectively. The purified promoter, HygR, and terminator fragments were then fused with primers GAPDH-HY-F1/R3 by overlapping PCR to obtain the HygR expression cassette. Finally, the HygR expression cassette was digested with SbfI (Cat#R3642S, NEB, MA, USA) and ligated into the same sites of pUC19 with the DNA ligation kit (Cat#6022, TaKaRa, CA, USA) to obtain the pGHT plasmid.

To construct the TveMnP1 expression cassette, the TveMnP1 gene was amplified from T. versicolor A1-ATF cDNA by the primer set Mnp1-overlap-3/Mnp1-overlap-4. The 1515-bp β-tubulin (JGI ID: Pospl1|113871) promoter and 800-bp GAPDH (JGI ID: Pospl1|117665) terminator were amplified from R. placenta gDNA by primer sets RpTubulin-common-1/Mnp1-overlap-2 and Mnp1-overlap-5/RpGapdh-common-6, respectively. The above three PCR fragments were then fused with nesting primers RpTubulin-common-1/RpGapdh-common-6 by overlapping PCR to obtain the TveMnP1 expression cassette, which was then cloned into the KpnI/XbaI restriction sites of the pGHT plasmid to obtain pGHT-MnP1. Similarly, the three primer sets RpTubulin-common-1/Mnp2-overlap-2, Mnp2-overlap-3/Mnp2-overlap-4, and Mnp2-overlap-5/RpGapdh-common-6, along with the nesting primer pair RpTubulin-common-1/RpGapdh-common-6, were used for overlapping PCR to generate the TveMnp2 expression cassette driven by the same β-tubulin promoter and GAPDH terminator. The TveMnp2 expression cassette was cloned into the KpnI/XbaI sites of pGHT to generate pGHT-MnP2. Primer sets RpTubulin-common-1/Lip2-overlap-2, Lip2-overlap-3/Lip2-overlap-4, and Lip2-overlap-5/RpGapdh-common-6 were used for TveLip2 expression cassette fragments and fused with the same nesting primers RpTubulin-common-1/RpGapdh-common-6. Since we failed to amplify TveLip2 from cDNA, this gene was de novo synthesized by GenScript (NJ, USA) and then used for overlapping PCR. All PCRs involved in gene cassette construction were performed with Phusion Flash High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Cat# F548S, MA, USA) and purified using NucleoSpin Gel and a PCR cleanup kit (TaKaRa, CA, USA). Plasmids were transformed into competent E. coli cells (Cat#C2987H, NEB, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene sequences were confirmed with Sanger sequencing.

Genetic transformation of the constructed LME gene cassettes into G. trabeum protoplasts was performed using the modified PEG/CaCl2-mediated method, as previously described [33]. Transformants (n = 50–60 per genotype) were screened for antibiotic resistance on YMGA plates containing hygromycin B (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and confirmed by PCR amplification. MnP1 transformants were validated by primer sets Ptub-up-F1/MnP1-up-R1 and MnP1-down-F1/Tgdh-down-R1, MnP2 transformants by primer sets Ptub-up-F1/MnP2-up-R1 and MnP2-down-F1/Tgdh-down-R1, and LiP2 by Ptub-up-F1/LiP2-up-R1 and LiP2-down-F1/Tgdh-down-R1. Three representative transformants for each gene construct were selected as replicates for this study. Three LCC3 transformants #5, #36, and #37 containing a TveLcc3 were obtained from our previous work [33]. Like that of the MnPs and LiP, the TvLcc3 gene was controlled by a β-tubulin promoter and a glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase terminator from R. placenta. All LCC3 transformants were reconfirmed using primer sets LCC3-up-F1/LCC3-up-R1 and LCC3-down-F1/LCC3-down-R1 prior to downstream analyses. All primers used in this study are listed in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Lignin-modifying enzyme assays in G. trabeum transformants

To detect enzyme activities of interest in LME transformants, 10-mm mycelial plugs of each strain were inoculated into 100 mL of a modified Highley’s minimal medium (HMM, pH 4.5) containing low nitrogen (2 mM NH4NO3), 0.1% glucose (w/v), 1% spruce wood sawdust (w/v), and 5 mM succinate [70]. After 14 days of culturing the mycelia at 115 rpm and 28 °C, 1 mL of supernatant was collected by centrifuging the culture at 4000 rpm for 10 min and frozen at -20 °C prior to enzyme assays. The manganese peroxidase assay was conducted by mixing 50 µL of the sample with 60 mM sodium malonate at pH 4.5, then reacting with 0.2 µM MnSO4 in a 250 µL reaction. The reaction was initiated with 50 µM H2O2, and the formation of Mn3+-malonate was measured at 270 nm [48]. The lignin peroxidase assay also used 50 µL of sample and 60 mM sodium malonate at pH 4.5, with a substrate of 0.125 mM Azure B in a 250 µL reaction initiated with 50 µM H2O2. Oxidation of Azure B was measured as the decrease in absorbance at 651 nm [71]. Laccase activity was measured as the change at 420 nm from reacting 40 μL of the enzyme extract sample with 4 mM 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) in 40 mM pH 3.0 sodium citrate buffer [34]. All enzyme assays were measured on a Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX™ Multimode Microplate Reader. LME activity is reported in mkat/L, where mkat represents the amount of enzyme required to consume 1 mmol of substrate per second.

Fungal growth analysis on agar media and lignocellulose substrates

The mycelial phenotypes and growth rates of G. trabeum WT and transformants that successfully express LMEs of interest were assessed on both agar and lignocellulose plates. Agar plates conditions are YMGA, HMM + 1% glucose (w/v), HMM + 1% glucose + 250 μM 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ), and HMM + 1% glucose + 0.1% corn stover (CS) extractives (w/v). Lignocellulosic substrate plates contained CS or spruce sawdust.

To prepare lignocellulose plates, CS or spruce sawdust at a mesh size of 40–60 was autoclaved (121 °C, 30 min) and dried twice for sterilization, and then 5 g of substrates were added into a 9-mm Petri dish along with 12 mL of HMM solution, which is used to hydrate and mix the substrate overnight. A 10-mm actively growing mycelial plug of the test strain was inoculated at the center of the plate and grown for 7 days at 28 °C to measure radial growth. The mycelial front was traced on days 3, 5, and 7 to calculate radial growth rates, with the linear growth rate reported as mm of mycelial elongation per day.

Total fungal biomass on the agar plate was quantified using the agar melting method [72]. Specifically, after 14 days of fungal cultivation, the plate was cut into 12 pieces and transferred to a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask with 130 mL of hot water. Flask was microwaved just until boiling, then vacuum-filtered using pre-weighed Whatman™ Glass Microfiber Filters and washed twice with 10 mL of hot DI water. The fungal biomass retained on the filter was then oven-dried at 75 °C to a constant weight for gravimetric measurement. Mycelial radial growth rate was also traced on the agar plate as mentioned above.

The inhibition rates for 1,4-BQ and CS extractive conditions were calculated as the percentage of fungal biomass under each condition relative to the mean biomass of the same strain grown on their blank cultural condition, HMM + 1% glucose. Data is tabulated in Additional file 2: Table S3-C. CS extractives were prepared by hot water extraction. Specifically, about 250 g of oven-dried CS powder with a mesh size of 40–60 was extracted with 7.5 L of hot water at 75 °C and 80 rpm overnight. Extraction was conducted in three successive rounds, yielding an extractive stock at approximately 1.33% (w/v). The extractive stock was filtered using a 0.2 μm syringe filter and mixed with the HMM + 1% glucose (w/v) agar medium to a final concentration of 0.1%. The 250 μM concentration of 1,4-BQ that was tested was selected based on its toxic effects on the WT (data not shown). Triplicate plates were applied for each strain and condition mentioned above. Statistical significance of the fungal growth and inhibition rate was determined by Welch’s t-test comparing each transformant or genotype to the WT.

Lignocellulose mass loss and compositional analysis

CS sawdust was degraded by G. trabeum WT and transformant strains to study the effects of LME expression on lignocellulose degradation. To do this, the mycelia were first pre-grown on CS plates as mentioned above, and then 20 10-mm CS plugs containing actively growing mycelia were harvested and homogenized as the inoculum for the subsequent solid-state culture. Seventy-five mg of this inoculum was inoculated into the center of new CS plates, and cultures were incubated for 30 days at 28 °C before harvest for degradation analysis. Three replicates were prepared for each strain, and blank controls were prepared by autoclaving the inoculum.

To extract the crude enzymes in the solid cultures, the decayed CS plates were scraped into a 50 mL tube, and 35 mL of DI water was added. The samples were shaken at 80 rpm at 4 °C overnight and centrifuged for 15 min at 4,000 rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and frozen at -20 °C until assayed for cellulase, xylanase, and LME activities. Cellulase activity was measured by using azo-CM-cellulose (Megazyme, Bray, Ireland) and following the manufacturer’s endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase (endo-Cellulase) assay procedure [73]. The assay reaction consisted of 0.5 mL of enzyme solution and 0.5 mL of 2% w/v azo-CM-cellulose solution in 100 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5) and was stopped after incubation at 40 °C for 10 min with 2.5 mL of 95% v/v ethanol. Xylanase activity was similarly measured by following the manufacturer’s procedure for the endo-1,4-β-D-xylanase assay, using Azo-Xylan (Birchwood) (Megazyme, Bray, Ireland) [74]. The assay reaction uses 0.5 mL of the enzyme extract with 0.5 mL of 1% w/v azo-xylan solution in 100 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5) and is terminated after incubation at 40 °C for 10 min with 2.5 mL of ethanol. Cellulase and xylanase activities are reported in U/mL, where U represents the amount of enzyme required to catalyze 1 µmol of substrate per minute. LME activities were measured using the methods mentioned above. Specific enzyme activities were also presented as normalized to the total secreted proteins quantified by the Quick Start Bradford protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

After enzyme extraction, the extractives of the CS substrate were further removed by hot-water extraction three times to obtain the modified insoluble lignocellulose, following the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) standard method [75]. The insoluble lignocellulose was then dried at 75 °C for a week and weighed to calculate the mass loss relative to controls.

The compositional analysis of lignocellulose was performed on the collected insoluble fraction using diluted acid hydrolysis followed by HPLC, as described in the NREL Technical Report NREL/TP-510–42618 [75]. The acid-soluble portion was analyzed for carbohydrate amounts using HPLC with an Aminex® HPX-87P column, and the acid-insoluble portion was further separated by burning in a furnace to measure Klason lignin and ash content. The percentage of each lignocellulosic component is summarized in Table 1. Cellulose was based on the total glucose content, hemicellulose on the sum of measured xylose, arabinose, galactose, and mannose. The percent removal of each lignocellulose component by the fungal treatment was calculated based on the average amount of each component in the blank samples. L/C (lignin/cellulose) and L/H (lignin/hemicellulose) ratios were calculated for the remaining insoluble lignocellulose by dividing the Klason lignin amount by that of cellulose and hemicellulose, respectively. Illustration describing the compositional analysis and equations used for calculations is shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. Raw data used to generate graphs in Fig. 4B-E are tabulated in Additional file 2: Table S3.

Iron-reduction capacity and qPCR assays on the colony fronts

The young hyphae grown at the edge of the colony on the CS plate were sampled to assess iron-reduction capacity and LME gene expression by qPCR. About 0.5 g wet-weight samples were collected from the spot of 10 mm apart from the colony edge, homogenized by shaking, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until further measurements. The sample was equally split to quantify iron-reduction capacity, total RNA, and LME transcription.

The iron-reduction capacity of the sample was measured using a ferrozine-based assay [76]. The reaction consisted of 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.5), 5 mM Fe3+, and 2 mM ferrozine. The entire frozen biomass sample was thawed on ice, added to the reaction buffer at a 1:3 (w/v) ratio, incubated at room temperature and 80 rpm in the dark, and sampled at 5, 15, and 35 min. At each time point, 200 µL of the reaction mixture was removed, briefly centrifuged to clarify the solution, and aliquoted into two wells for technical replicates. The original Fe2⁺ concentrations in samples were quantified as negligible. Iron reduction rates of the biomass samples were reported as nmol Fe2⁺ produced per minute and then normalized to total RNA quantity as a proxy for biomass. Data are included in Additional file 2: Table S3-B.

One aliquot of frozen biomass samples was used to extract total RNA using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Total RNA was quantified with the Qubit 4 fluorometer RNA Broad Range assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity was assessed by running gel electrophoresis. Total RNA yield was used as a proxy for biomass normalization of enzymatic activities.

Total RNA was purified using the Qiagen Mini RNeasy kit with on-column DNase digestion to remove residual gDNA (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), followed by LME gene expression determination using a two-step RT-qPCR approach. For cDNA synthesis, 80 ng of purified total RNA was used per reaction, reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Bio, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then diluted tenfold. Quantitative PCR was performed in 10 µL reaction volumes using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Each reaction contained 5 µL of master mix, 1 µL of each primer, and 3 µL of the diluted cDNA. Primer amplification efficiencies were calculated using the web-based LinRegPCR tool, and average efficiencies for each primer pair are listed in Additional file 1: Table S3 [77].

Two internal reference genes of G. trabeum (Prefoldin subunit 6, JGI ID: 125075; eIF3-p35 subunit, JGI ID: 122605) selected in our previous work were applied here [78]. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were calculated using Diomni Design and Analysis (RUO) 3.1.0 software (ThermoFisher Connect platform) to estimate gene transcription levels by the Pfaffl method [79]. Gene expression levels were calculated as fold changes relative to WT, which is set to 1. All qPCR reactions were run in three technical replicates for three biological replicates per strain.

Integrated genotype–phenotype correlation analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to summarize multivariate datasets and identify variables contributing most strongly to genotype differentiation. PCA computation and visualization were performed in R (version 4.5.3) using the ggplot2 (version 4.0.3) and ggfortify (version 0.4.19) packages [80–82].

The genotype–phenotype associations, calculated as Pearson correlations between LME mutant and WT data, were integrated to elucidate the impact of LME expression on phenotypic traits in fungal growth and lignocellulose decomposition. Correlation analyses and data visualizations were performed with R (version 4.5.3) using the corrplot package (version 0.95) [80, 83]. Correlation matrices summarizing all pairwise comparisons of phenotypic variables are provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S4.

Statistical analysis

Welch’s one-sided t tests were used to determine the statistical significance of three biological replicates of each transformant strain compared to the WT strain.
