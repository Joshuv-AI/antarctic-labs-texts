Beta cell secreted GABA sets appropriate insulin secretion by modulating islet calcium oscillations
Authors: Sandra M Ferreira, D Walker Hagan, Austin E Stis, Adrienne E Widener, Alexandra E Cuaycal, Chad Rancourt, Arabella G Readey, Dylan S Smurlick, Dongtao A Fu, Martha Campbell–Thompson, Marjan Slak Rupnik, Edward A Phelps
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is produced in pancreatic beta cells and is implicated in modulating islet function, yet its precise physiological role remains uncertain. This study aimed to determine the function of endogenous beta cell-derived GABA on insulin secretion and islet calcium dynamics by developing a conditional beta cell-specific knockout of GABA-synthesizing enzymes (GAD65 and GAD67).MethodsConditional knockout mice (GadβKO) lacking both GAD65 and GAD67 specifically in pancreatic beta cells were generated. Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion was measured in isolated islets and in vivo and islet Ca2+ oscillations were recorded using calcium imaging. The effects of GABA and its receptor agonists/antagonists were tested under various glucose conditions. Additional analyses were performed in high-fat diet-fed mice and human islets from donors with and without type 2 diabetes (T2D).ResultsGadβKO islets were devoid of GABA and showed excessive insulin secretion in response to glucose without anatomical changes in islet composition. These islets had defective Ca2+ oscillations, with prolonged active phases and reduced amplitudes. GABA application suppressed Ca2+ oscillations, an effect mediated by GABAA and GABAB receptors. High-fat diet-fed and T2D human islets were unresponsive to GABA and exhibited impaired Ca2+ oscillations.ConclusionsThis is the first study using a beta cell-specific GAD65/GAD67 knockout model. Endogenous beta cell-derived GABA is critical for modulating insulin secretion by maintaining proper Ca2+ oscillation dynamics. GABA signaling likely operates as a delayed negative feedback mechanism that reinforces oscillatory homeostasis in islets. The loss of GABA responsiveness, as seen in metabolic stress or T2D, may contribute to islet dysfunction. This work establishes GABA as a key regulator of islet rhythm and glucose responsiveness.Keywords: GABA, Pancreatic islet, Calcium oscillations, Insulin secretion, GABA receptor, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that controls neuron excitability in adult vertebrates. Pancreatic beta cells are the only cells outside the central nervous system known to synthesize and secrete copious amounts of GABA [1]. This unique property of beta cells suggests that GABA is important for pancreatic islet function. The reduced GABA content observed in dysfunctional islets from donors with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) strengthens this theory [2,3].

GABA is generated from a decarboxylation reaction of a non-essential amino acid, glutamic acid, by the enzyme glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) [4]. Pancreatic beta cells express one of the two GAD isoforms, GAD65 and GAD67, depending on the species. GAD65 is found in human beta cells, GAD67 is found in mouse beta cells, while rat beta cells express both GAD65 and GAD67 [[5], [6], [7]].

GABA modulates the function of pancreatic islet endocrine cells in an autocrine and paracrine fashion since the beta, alpha, and delta cells express GABA receptors [3,[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, most studies have used exogenous GABA or applied GABA receptor agonists and antagonists to probe the islet GABA system [1], with contradictory findings [3,9,[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Many of these studies based their conclusions on the application of supra-physiological concentrations of GABA for long durations that occur on top of the endogenous islet GABA system.

A genetic knockout model is necessary to clearly understand the role of endogenous GABA on pancreatic islet function. Previous genetic models for GAD have used total mouse knockout of either GAD67 or GAD65 alone, which results in embryonic lethality or severe developmental disabilities, respectively [20,21]. A study using GAD65 ectopic overexpression in mouse beta cells showed an inhibited first phase of insulin secretion and impaired glucose tolerance [22], indicating that endogenous GABA might inhibit insulin secretion. However, no beta cell specific GAD knockout model has been generated until now.

Here, we developed a conditional knockout mouse lacking the GABA-synthesizing enzymes GAD65 and GAD67 in pancreatic beta cells (Gad βKO) to determine the role of endogenous GABA on pancreatic beta cell function. Completely lacking in endogenous GABA, islets from these mice hyper-secrete insulin, and male mice develop oral glucose intolerance. Loss of GABA also upsets the duration of islet Ca2+ oscillations. We used GABA receptor agonists and antagonists to identify GABAA and GABAB receptors as mediating GABA modulation of islet function. In addition, we found that a high-fat diet results in islet unresponsiveness to GABA and loss of Ca2+ oscillations, an effect that is paralleled in human islets from donors with T2D.

Results

GadβKO islets exhibit an increased response to glucose stimulation

To develop an appropriate model for determining the role of endogenous GABA in the islet, we crossed C57BL/6J mice possessing a triple mutation of Gad1loxp::Gad2loxp::Rosa26tdTomato [23] with Ins1cre mice, a line stably expressing Cre specifically within the beta cells on the C57BL/6J background [24]. Mice expressing beta cell Cre and homozygous for the triple mutation eliminate both Gad1 and Gad2 genes in beta cells and report tdTomato. We have termed the resultant mice Gad βKO. Mice only expressing Ins1cre, which we refer to as Ins1-cre, were used as controls.

Pancreata and isolated islets were obtained from Gad βKO mice to determine GABA concentrations in the GAD knockout. Immunohistochemical analysis of pancreata from Gad βKO mice demonstrated a total elimination of GABA immunoreactivity while preserving insulin content (Figure 1A). Deletion of GAD was confirmed by a western blot of islet lysates using an antibody reactive to both GAD65 and GAD67 [6] (Figure 1B). Only a single band is observed because mice predominantly express GAD67 with only trace expression of GAD65 [6]. In addition, analysis of islet lysate by HPLC with electrochemical detection showed GABA was undetectable in Gad βKO islets (Figure 1C), and there was no significant difference in the glutamate content (Figure 1D).

We performed dynamic glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in isolated islets to evaluate the effect of the GAD knockout on islet function. Gad βKO islets from both male and female mice oversecreted insulin in response to 16.7 mM glucose (Figure 1E–G), implying that endogenous GABA negatively modulates insulin secretion.

To determine whether intra-islet GABA influences the composition of islets and consequently insulin secretion, pancreas sections from both Ins1-cre and Gad βKO were co-stained for insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, and pancreatic polypeptide. No differences were observed in the composition of islet endocrine cells (Figure 1H,I), islet size (Figure 1J), or pancreas mass (Figure 1K), indicating that no anatomical changes resulted from the genetic removal of Gad1 and Gad2 in beta cells. Thus, over secretion of insulin by Gad βKO islets is due to functional regulation of islets, not a difference in beta cell mass or islet anatomy.

Insulin secretion is synchronized with intracellular calcium [Ca2+]i content, and any alteration in the Ca2+ dynamics is reflected in the insulin release [25,26]. We evaluated the [Ca2+]i in isolated islets loaded with the low-affinity [Ca2+]i indicator Calbryte 520 AM at different glucose concentrations (5.6, 7, 8, 9, and 11 mM) applied as a stepwise titration of escalating glucose. Each glucose concentration was incubated for 10 min [27] and [Ca2+]i responses from several islets were recorded by confocal microscopy. Individual beta cell and islet responses were plotted as a heatmap (Figures 1L,M). Gad βKO and Ins1-cre islets exhibited a similar glucose threshold for beta cell activation, and both islet types showed good synchronicity of oscillations, indicating that glucose-sensing and electrical coupling are similar in both islet types. However, we observed potential differences in the amplitude (F–F0) and duration of the [Ca2+]i oscillatory patterns in the Gad βKO islets, suggesting that GABA contributes to differences in islet function.

GadβKO male mice exhibit glucose intolerance and high insulin secretion in vivo

Alterations in insulin secretion may affect insulin signaling and glucose metabolism in vivo [26]. To evaluate if Gad βKO mice have these alterations, we assessed the mice's body weight and conducted in vivo experiments to evaluate glucose metabolism. Body weight was assessed starting from 30 days postnatal for 12 weeks. Gad βKO males exhibited higher rate of weight gain and significantly higher weight only in the eighth week (Figure 2A). On the other hand, Gad βKO females presented higher weight than Ins1-cre females in all the weeks analyzed but no difference in rate of weight gain (Figure S1A).

To evaluate the effects of GABA-synthesizing enzyme deletion in pancreatic beta cells on glucose metabolism in vivo, we assessed non-fasting glycemia for 12 weeks, and we did not observe any alteration in males (Figure 2B). Gad βKO females showed significantly lower non-fasting glycemia at two measurements but otherwise did not differ from controls (Figure S1B). An intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (ipGTT) showed mild differences in male but not female Gad βKO glucose tolerance (Figures S1C-S1F). Because ipGTT is known to cause a large glucose excursion but not a large insulin excursion, we also performed an oral glucose tolerance test (oGTT), which is more effective at stimulating insulin secretion in vivo [28]. With the oGTT, the Gad βKO males showed significant glucose intolerance (Figures 2C,D) and had higher plasma insulin (Figure 2E) and C-peptide secretion (Figure 2F) during the test. In Gad βKO females, the blood glucose during oGTT was not different (Figure S1G, H). The oGTT plasma insulin and c-peptide were also elevated for female Gad βKO mice, although non-significant due to a high variance and overall lower insulin and c-peptide secretion compared to males. (Figure S1I, J). An altered glucose tolerance, as observed in the Gad βKO males, can result in insulin resistance since oversecreting insulin can desensitize peripheral tissues' insulin receptors [29,30]. In an intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (ipITT) of Gad βKO mice of both sexes, there was no alteration in the rate of blood glucose disappearance (Figures 2G,H, and Figures S1K, L). One possible explanation for oral glucose intolerance in male Gad βKO mice, despite higher insulin secretion, is differences in glucagon secretion. It has been shown that GABA inhibits glucagon secretion [1,11,14,31]. Therefore, the lack of GABA in the Gad βKO islets may lead to increased glucagon secretion, which in turn increases glucose production in the liver [32]. We did not have sufficient plasma volume to also measure glucagon in these experiments.

GadβKO islets present a higher active phase in the Ca2+ wave

Because Gad βKO islets presented higher insulin secretion in vitro and in vivo, we evaluated the Ca2+ oscillations in Gad βKO islets to better understand how endogenous GABA modulates beta cell excitability. On the microscope stage, we dynamically perfused individual islets loaded with Calbryte 520 under basal conditions (5.6 mM glucose) and different stimulation conditions (8 mM, 11 mM, and 16.7 mM glucose) (Figure S2). To understand how Gad βKO islets can have a higher insulin secretion, we analyzed the behavior of Ca2+ oscillations.

First, we evaluated the Ca2+ activation behavior when transitioning from 3 mM to 11 mM glucose in male and female islets. Gad βKO male islets had a shorter delay until first-phase activation compared to Ins1-cre male islets (around 2 min earlier) (Figure 3A-C). However, Gad βKO female islets showed no difference in the delay until glucose activation (Figure 3C). In addition, both male and female Gad βKO islets exhibited a prolonged period (20+ minutes) of maximal first-phase activation before starting Ca2+ oscillations compared to Ins1-cre islets (Figure 3D), which may contribute to insulin over-secretion.

Next, we evaluated the Ca2+ oscillation waveform components. The Ca2+ wave is characterized by an active and silent phase that composes the period. The active phase of the Ca2+ wave is characterized by the cytosolic influx of Ca2+ through voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels (VDCC) due to the plasma membrane depolarization. After the fast depolarization, inward-rectifying potassium channels open to restore the plasma membrane voltage, and the VDCCs close, stopping the Ca2+ influx. The Ca2+ is removed from the cytoplasm by the plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase (PMCA) and Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCX), leading the Ca2+ wave to the silent phase. While glucose levels are elevated, this cycle is repeated successively, originating the Ca2+ oscillations that modulate insulin secretion in the same direction, which also exhibits an oscillatory release [[33], [34], [35], [36]].

Islet Ca2+ oscillations are composed of superimposed fast and slow waves [34,37]. Analyzing the shape of the slow Ca2+ oscillation waveform, it is possible to observe substantial differences between Gad βKO islets and Ins1-cre. Ins1-cre islets have a typical islet Ca2+ wave characterized by a sharp rise at the beginning of a burst, followed by a small plateau, and then by a sharp decline during the silent phase (Figures 3E, F) [33]. The shape of the Ca2+ waveform is different for Gad βKO islets, which have a smaller rise followed by a long plateau and a shallow silent phase (Figure 3E). Gad βKO islets of both sexes have a reduced Ca2+ oscillation peak-to-peak amplitude in all glucose conditions (Figures S2 and 3G). At 11 mM glucose, we observed alterations in the peak frequency per minute (Figure 3H), and a longer period in the Gad βKO islets (Figure 3I). We calculated the duration of the active and silent phases [35] for a 30-minute sample during the second phase response. In 11 mM glucose, Gad βKO islets had a longer active phase (Figure 3J), and no change in the silent phase duration (Figure 3K).

We also observed different behaviors in the Ca2+ waveform in 16.7 mM glucose (Figures 3L, M). As before, in 16.7 mM glucose, the amplitude was reduced (Figure 3N), but the number of peaks per minute was not different (Figure 3O). However, we observed that approximately 30% of male Gad βKO islets exhibited no oscillations or fewer than 2 oscillations for the full 50 min duration of glucose stimulation, remaining maximally activated throughout the entire period. In the Ins1-cre traces, this frequency was around 10% (Figure 3O). In 16.7 mM glucose, there was no significant difference in the period (Figure 3P), and active phase of the islets that did oscillate (Figure 3Q), but there was an increase in the silent phase (Figure 3R).

Our analysis of the Ca2+ slow wave oscillations showed that islets lacking GABA show a prolonged depolarization leading to prolonged Ca2+ entry, particularly during first-phase responses or at the highest glucose levels (16 mM), resulting in a long active phase of the slow wave oscillation. These results indicate that GABA is crucial to modulating the pulse duration of the Ca2+ wave components, maintaining the equilibrium between the active and silent phases, and setting the wave amplitude. While this effect may occur through direct autocrine signaling, it could also be due to changes in alpha and delta cell behavior. We suggest that these changes to the whole islet Ca2+ oscillation behavior in the absence of GABA correlate with the increased insulin secretion abnormality.

GABA inhibits Ca2+ oscillations in high glucose levels

Studies using exogenous GABA or GABA receptor agonists and antagonists have demonstrated contradictory results. Some studies showed an inhibitory role for GABA in insulin secretion, and others showed that GABA is stimulatory [3,9,[15], [16], [17], [18]]. A study using INS-1E cells demonstrated that GABA could have both stimulatory and inhibitory effects, depending on glucose concentrations [19]. These different effects were attributed to the GABAA receptor activity [15]. Rodent pancreatic beta cells express two types of functioning GABA receptors, GABAA Receptor (GABAAR) and GABAB Receptor (GABABR). For human islets, only functional GABAAR has been reported due to a lack of expression of one of the obligatory GABABR subunits [1,3]. GABAAR is a chloride channel, and the activity of this receptor promotes Cl− movement across the plasma membrane, thereby altering the membrane potential [1]. A study using the GABAAR antagonist SR95531 showed reduced insulin secretion in 6 mM glucose, leading the authors to conclude that GABA has a stimulatory effect [15]. A possible explanation is that 6 mM glucose is sufficient to reduce the inhibitory current of KATP channels and depolarize the membrane from -70 mV to -55 mV, but not fully activate Ca2+ mobilization. Under these conditions, the administration of GABA activates the GABAAR, leading to the efflux of Cl−, as this ion is more concentrated inside the beta cells, thereby increasing membrane depolarization [1]. In our view, the direction of the GABAAR on membrane potential, whether depolarizing or hyperpolarizing, depends on the instantaneous situation relative to the equilibrium Cl− potential.

Here, we tested whether applied GABA is stimulatory or inhibitory to islets under different glucose conditions. We dynamically stimulated islets with GABA (10 μM) in sub-activating 5.6 mM glucose and measured the Ca2+ response. We did not observe any alteration in Ca2+ mobilization in either Ins1-cre or Gad βKO islets (Figure S3A). As the plasma membrane should present a slight depolarization since GABA is hypothesized to have a stimulatory effect at this glucose level, we added tolbutamide (5 μM) in 3 mM glucose to reduce the hyperpolarizing current from the KATP channel [38], and then we incubated with GABA (10 μM), where we still did not observe any effect on [Ca2+]i during GABA application (Figure S3B). These results indicate that exogenously applied GABA does not directly stimulate Ca2+ mobilization in mouse islets at sub-activating glucose concentrations.

Next, we evaluated the effect of GABA during stimulation under activating levels of glucose. We used 8 mM glucose and incubated with different GABA concentrations of 10 nM, 100 nM, and 10 μM. We performed two transient GABA incubations of 5 min each during the second phase of the glucose response, with a 10-min recovery period in between (Figure 4A–F and Figure S3C-H). GABA showed an inhibition of Ca2+ oscillations at all GABA concentrations (Figures 4A–F and Figure S3C-H). GABA showed a slight effect at 10 nM and a successively more substantial effect at 100 nM and 10 μM (Figure 4G–H). After the stop of GABA incubations, a rebound effect was commonly observed (Figure 4A–F). These observations fit with our other data, indicating that GABA contributes to shaping the Ca2+ oscillation waveform. We performed two additional repeats of this experiment with fresh islet isolations (Figure S4C-H). Although the trend was similar, the threshold for exogenous GABA sensitivity varied from mouse to mouse, perhaps due to varying individual expression levels of GABA receptors.

Having identified significant differences in the shape of Gad βKO slow wave Ca2+ oscillations, we next analyzed Ca2+ events using a high-resolution analysis pipeline that examines Ca2+ transients across all relevant time scales from sub-second to minute time ranges, including superimposed short and fast oscillations (Figure S3I-L) [39]. We measured the number of events and the halfwidth of Ca2+ content in the traces incubated with 10 nM, 100 nM, and 10 μM GABA (Figure S4). We measured during the 5 min interval before GABA and 5 min during GABA administration. GABA significantly reduced the number of events in both Ins1-cre and Gad βKO islets, and the halfwidth in the Gad βKO islets (Figures S3K). In the second GABA incubation, we also observed a reduced number of events in both Ins1-cre and Gad βKO islets (Figure S3L). However, the halfwidth was not different between the two islet groups during the second incubation (Figure S3L). The reduction of the effect during the second GABA incubation at 10 μM may be due to a desensitization or inactivation of GABA receptors [3]. These results confirm that applied GABA has an inhibitory effect in stimulating glucose conditions, with a strong rebound effect.

GABA regulates pancreatic beta cell function through GABA receptors

Mouse pancreatic beta cells express both GABAA and GABAB receptors. GABAAR is a Cl− channel formed by combining 5 different subunits from isoforms α (1–6), β (1–4), γ (1–3), δ, ε, θ, π, and ρ (1–3) [1,40]. Mouse (Figure 5A) and human (Figure S5A) single-cell RNAseq showed that the β3 subunit is the most highly expressed GABAA subunit in beta cells of both species. We confirmed the expression of the β3 subunit by Western blot. (Figure 5B). Interestingly, islet expression of the β3 subunit was heterogeneous from mouse to mouse. GABABR is a metabotropic receptor associated with an inhibitory G protein (Gi/o). GABABR acts through Gi/o signaling which inhibits adenylyl cyclase activity, reducing cyclic AMP (CAMP) levels, limiting PKA activity and activating Na+/K+ ATPase (NKA) [41], as well as cytosolic Ca2+ release, and opening inward-rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels [1,42,43], which hyperpolarizes the plasma membrane and reduces insulin secretion [8,12,44]. Lowered cAMP also reduces the Ca2+ sensitivity of insulin secretory granules for exocytosis [43]. Of interest, the Jacobson lab recently demonstrated that Gi/o protein-coupled receptor inhibition of beta-cell electrical excitability and insulin secretion strongly stimulates islet Ca2+ oscillations [41]. The GABAB receptor is formed as a heterodimer of two obligatory subunits, GABBR1 and GABBR2 [45]. Mouse islets express both Gabbr1 and Gabbr2 subunits [8,44], presenting a functional receptor (Figure 5A). Human islets express only GABBR1 (Figure S5A), so GABABR is reported to be not functional in human islets [1,3].

First, we investigated the behavior of the GABAAR in our GAD knockout model by recording Ca2+ oscillations and incubating with GABAAR agonist (muscimol) and antagonist (SR95531). Muscimol (10 μM) was incubated transiently for 5 min in actively oscillating islets in 11 mM glucose. We observed an inhibition of [Ca2+]i oscillations with muscimol that was similar to that of applied GABA in both Ins1-cre and Gad βKO islets (Figure 5C–F and Figure S5B,C). We next used SR95531 (10 μM) to block the GABAAR. We first incubated the antagonist for 5 min and then added GABA (10 μM) for 5 min. The GABAAR antagonist increased [Ca2+]i and oscillation frequency, although the effect varied somewhat between islets from different mice, likely due to individual animal variability in baseline activation in 8 mM glucose and differences in GABA receptor expression levels (Figure 5G,H and Figure S5D,E). The subsequent addition of GABA counteracted the antagonist effect, reducing [Ca2+]i (Figure 5G,H and Figure S5D,E). SR95531 is a competitive inhibitor, so the addition of more GABA may counteract its effects. In the Gad βKO islets, which lack endogenous GABA, we did not observe an effect of SR95531 (Figures 5I,J and Figures S5E) in most recordings, confirming that the response to SR95531 observed in Ins1-cre was due to blocking the endogenous GABA signaling. These results confirm that GABAAR is inhibitory for [Ca2+]i when directly stimulated.

We next used GABABR agonist (baclofen) and antagonist (CGP55845). When baclofen (10 μM) was incubated transiently for 5 min in 11 mM glucose, effect on Ca2+ activity was inconsistent, where it increased in some Ins1-cre and Gad βKO islets (Figure 5K-N) and decreased in remained constant in others (Figure S5F,G). For the GABABR antagonist, we incubated the CGP55845 (10 μM) for 5 min and then added GABA (10 μM) for 5 min. The GABABR antagonist inhibited Ca2+ oscillations in Ins1-cre (Figure 5O,P and Figure S5H), and did not have an effect in Gad βKO islets (Figure 5Q,R and Figure S5I), showing the effect of the inhibitor is specific to GABA. While this experiment on the GABABR has some inconsistent results for the agonist, the antagonist was much more consistent. Counterintuitively, the results seems to suggest that the canonically inhibitory GABABR was stimulatory in mouse islets, it has been shown that promoting Gi/o signaling strongly induces islet Ca2+ oscillations and that blocking Gi/o downstream signaling prevents oscillations [41]. We suggest that genetic models would be important to further dissect the roles of the GABAAR and GABABR.

GadβKO male mice treated with a high-fat diet have impaired Ca2+ oscillations

There is reduced GABA content in islets from donors with T1D and T2D [2,3]. We hypothesize that this pathological loss of GABA is a crucial step to pancreatic beta cell dysfunction. The importance of GABA to the pancreatic beta cell function was confirmed here in our experiments using GAD knockout, where we showed alterations in the Ca2+ oscillations and higher insulin secretion in isolated islets and in vivo. Higher insulin secretion can lead to insulin resistance in peripheral tissues, mainly when it is associated with obesity. Insulin resistance leads to hyperglycemia, which induces a functional overload in pancreatic beta cells, which can lead to loss of function and cell death [26,30,46].

To mimic this effect in Gad βKO mice, we treated the mice with a high-fat diet for 8 weeks. Both Ins1-cre and Gad βKO mice (male and female) treated with the diet increased their body weight and energy efficiency equivalently (Figure S6A-C). The non-fasting glycemia was not different in both males and females (Figure S6D). The high-fat Ins1-cre and Gad βKO did not present alterations in the IP glucose tolerance test and insulin tolerance test when compared between both groups; a difference was observed only when comparing the low-fat and high-fat groups (Figure S6E,F), indicating no effect of islet GAD knockout on the high-fat diet-induced phenotype. In the insulin tolerance test, we observed more resistance in the high-fat Ins1-cre mice than in the low-fat Ins1-cre mice. We did not observe a difference in insulin tolerance between high-fat and low-fat Gad βKO (Figure S6G,H).

We evaluated β-cell function in isolated islets from low-fat and high-fat Ins1-cre and Gad βKO mice. We performed static glucose- and KCl-stimulated insulin secretion, and, as before, we observed that both low and high-fat Gad βKO islets from males secreted more insulin than the Ins1-cre islet groups, but we did not observe that high-fat diet changed insulin secretion for Gad βKO (Figure 6A). However, the high-fat male Gad βKO islets showed reduced insulin content when compared to high-fat Ins1-cre islets (Figure 6B). In isolated islets from females, although we did not observe alterations in static insulin secretion (Figure 6C), we observed higher insulin content in both low and high-fat Gad βKO than in Ins1-cre islets (Figure 6D).

We also evaluated the effect of GABA on the high-fat diet islets stimulated with 8 mM glucose. We observed 2 different responses to GABA (10 μM) in high-fat diet Ins1-cre islets: (1) GABA failed to inhibit Ca2+ oscillations and within around 3 min of addition of GABA, we observed a higher peak of Ca2+ (Figure 6E–G); and (2) GABA inhibited Ca2+ oscillations and we observed a rebound peak of Ca2+ after washing out GABA (Figure S6I). High-fat-diet Gad βKO male islets showed a poor Ca2+ response to glucose without oscillations (Figure 6H–J) and consistently did not respond to GABA (Figures 6H–J and Figure S6J). High-fat-diet Gad βKO female islets continued to present Ca2+ oscillations, and islets from some mice were unresponsive to GABA (Figure 6K-M), while in others GABA inhibited [Ca2+]i (Figure S6K). These results showed that a high-fat diet for 8 weeks may reduce islet responsiveness to GABA, which could contribute to loss of Ca2+ oscillations and islet dysfunction, particularly in male mice.

Islets from T2D donors lose the response to GABA

Since we observed a loss of GABA response in high-fat Gad βKO islets, we evaluated the GABA response in human pancreatic islets from donors with no diabetes (ND) and T2D. First, we conducted tests using ND islets. We evaluated the dynamic insulin secretion incubating with 10 μM GABA continuously during low and high glucose stimulation. GABA reduced insulin secretion in high glucose (Figure 7A), confirming the inhibitory effect of GABA on insulin secretion seen in Gad βKO applies to human islets.

We also incubated GABA transiently for 5 min in 3 mM glucose and 16.7 mM glucose. In low glucose, we observed an increase in insulin secretion in human islets after 3 min of GABA incubation. In high glucose, we observed a slight suppression of insulin secretion, followed by a post-inhibitory rebound response after stopping GABA incubation (Figure 7B). To evaluate if this effect is present in the Ca2+ oscillations, we also evaluated the acute GABA effect on [Ca2+]i in low and high glucose. Similar to the mouse islets, in human islets, GABA did not affect [Ca2+]i in low glucose (Figure 7C).

When GABA was applied in high glucose, it initially inhibited [Ca2+]i but then showed a recovery response that started before washing out the GABA (Figure 7D–E). This effect could be due to desensitization or inactivation of the GABAAR. Next, we tested exogenous GABA on Ca2+ oscillations in islets from T2D donors. Similar to what we observed in the high-fat Gad βKO male islets, the T2D human islets did not respond to glucose, and we did not observe an effect of GABA on [Ca2+]i (Figure 7F–H). These results confirm that T2D may coincide with a loss of functional GABA signaling in pancreatic beta cells.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to evaluate the effect of endogenous GABA on pancreatic beta cell function. Our study is the first to use a conditional knockout lacking GABA in pancreatic beta cells. As GABA was undetectable in Gad βKO islets using multiple methods, our study shows that beta cells are the only cell type responsible for GABA synthesis and secretion in the islet. Although alpha and delta cells can also express the GAD enzyme, they appear not to synthesize any GABA.

Islets from Gad βKO mice had normal islet size, number, and endocrine cell ratio, establishing that GABA is not required to maintain beta cell mass in mice. In vivo, male Gad βKO mice exhibited impaired glucose tolerance, increased insulin and c-peptide secretion, and faster weight gain than controls. Here, we investigated attributed altered islet function to the autocrine effect of GABA on beta cells. Gad βKO beta cells exhibited hypersecretion of insulin in response to glucose. However, GABA also exerts a paracrine effect on alpha and delta cells, which can further impact islet function [1]. Islet-secreted GABA may enter the portal circulation, where it could act on the liver, as GABA has been shown to alter the insulin sensitivity of hepatocytes [47].

Our results show a net inhibitory effect of endogenous GABA on insulin secretion in vivo and in vitro that is mediated by modulation of Ca2+ oscillations. GABA seems crucial to regulating the oscillatory behavior of Ca2+. Our results are consistent with our previous paper, Menegaz et al., in which GABA was shown to modulate pulsatile insulin secretion [2]. Menegaz et al. also demonstrated that islet GABA secretion is pulsatile with a period that matches the frequency of typical Ca2+ oscillations.

Our data now demonstrates that GABA is essential for normal islet oscillations. The islet Ca2+ oscillations occur due to a rhythmic cycle between the active and silent two phases [48]. During the active phase, a Ca2+ flux occurs into the cytosol, and during the silent phase, the intracellular Ca2+ is removed the cytosol [34]. Gad βKO islet oscillations exhibited a prolonged active phase and a reduced peak-to-peak amplitude, demonstrating that GABA signaling is important for generating a robust and balanced Ca2+ waveform. We conclude that both GABAAR and GABABR signaling influence the Ca2+ oscillations in mouse islets through ionotropic modulation of membrane potential and G-protein coupled signaling, respectively (Figure 8).

Autocrine endogenous GABA effect on pancreatic beta cells. Left: GABA is synthesized from glutamate by GAD65 and GAD67 enzymes and secreted in an oscillatory fashion by pancreatic beta cells through VRAC (volume-regulated anion channel). Secreted GABA activates the GABAAR, a Cl− channel, and the GABABR (a Gi/o-GPCR). In high glucose, the plasma membrane is in a depolarized state, and GABAAR activation promotes Cl− influx, leading to plasma membrane hyperpolarization, which inhibits VGCC (voltage-gated Ca2+ channel) and reduces cytosolic Ca2+. Since GABA secretion is oscillatory, when GABA secretion decreases, the GABAAR receptor closes. GABABR activation leads to the reduction of cAMP, reducing the cAMP-sensitivity to Ca2+. Plasma membrane depolarizes again due to glucose metabolism, leading to a higher cytosolic Ca2+ concentration. The switch between the GABA inhibitory effect and the absence of a GABA effect on pancreatic beta cells contributes to a strong Ca2+ oscillation. Right: In the absence of GABA in GadβKO, the GABAAR and GABABR do not provide any feedback to plasma membrane dynamics, leading to the deformed and prolonged Ca2+ oscillations, resulting in the oversecretion of insulin.

Negative feedback is a basic principle of any oscillating system and is required for sustainable oscillations when it is coupled with a delay or phase shift [49]. Others have shown that islet rhythmic activity is coordinated by delayed negative feedback [50]. Importantly, we previously showed that the release of GABA from the islet is pulsatile at a frequency that matches the timing of islet Ca2+ waves [2]. In addition to negative feedback, GABA may also provide positive feedback through post-inhibitory rebound responses, or through the alternating excitatory and inhibitory activity of the GABAA receptor, resulting in an oscillatory driver [51].

Gad βKO islets exhibited a delay in the initiation of Ca2+ oscillations. Indeed, in 16 mM glucose, one-third of male Gad βKO failed to initiate oscillations entirely, which further supports our theory that the role of GABA in the islet is to promote oscillation-stabilizing feedback. The fact that Gad βKO islets are still able to generate oscillations at all, although deformed, indicates that there are other sources of feedback, for instance, metabolic oscillations, the inward-rectifying potassium channels, and other Gi/o-GPCRs such as somatostatin receptors (SSTRs), α2A-adrenergic receptors (ADRs), and D2-like dopamine receptors (DRDs). However, the loss of GABA substantially perturbs the system.

Here, we specifically tested whether applied GABA stimulates Ca2+ mobilization in low glucose and found that it was never stimulatory to Ca2+ in resting conditions (low glucose) and always inhibitory in activating conditions (high glucose). We hypothesized that we might not have been able to observe a stimulatory effect of GABA in low glucose due to the overwhelming hyperpolarizing current of open KATP channels. We thus added a moderate amount of tolbutamide in glucose to reduce the control of KATP over membrane potential and then added GABA. We were still unable to observe a stimulatory effect of GABA on Ca2+, even with tolbutamide. Thus, at least in terms of Ca2+, applied GABA is never stimulatory to resting beta cells in our hands. When GABA was applied to the islet in stimulating glucose with active oscillations, it suppressed the oscillations. We believe the explanation for these effects is that the applied GABA acts as a voltage clamp through the GABAAR, holding the beta cell at the equilibrium Cl− potential and thus resisting any bursting or oscillatory behavior.

A study by Korol et al. evaluated the GABAAR single–channel activity in human islets. They observed channel activation with 10 nM exogenous GABA, indicating GABA could modulate pancreatic function through this receptor at the interstitial concentrations known to exist in the islet. They also observed reduced insulin granule exocytosis with GABA incubation [3]. Conversely, Braun et al. observed a reduction of insulin secretion when blocking GABAAR in 6 mM glucose (SR95531, 10 μM), leading them to conclude GABA has an excitatory effect on beta cells [15]. We were unable to observe any direct stimulatory effect of GABA on Ca2+ but did observe an increase in insulin secretion in human islets when GABA was applied in low glucose. The stimulatory effects of GABA on insulin in the absence of increases in Ca2+ may also be due to a nutrient effect of GABA being metabolized in the GABA-shunt pathway [1].

While the GABABR is typically understood to be inhibitory, our results showed that GABABR may promote Ca2+ oscillation in the beta cell. There is strong evidence supporting that GPCRs associated with an inhibitory Gi/o protein promote Ca2+ oscillations. The Jacobson group demonstrated that activation of Gi/o-coupled receptors, such as somatostatin and α2-adrenergic receptors, stimulates NKA activity and Ca2+ oscillations in beta cells. This activation leads to cyclic hyperpolarization of the beta cell membrane potential, which in turn modulates Ca2+ influx and oscillation [41]. Thus, GABABR signaling likely contributes to strengthening Ca2+ oscillations in mouse islets, while human islets are reported to lack a functional GABABR due to the lack of expression of one of the obligatory subunits.

Gad βKO islets from high-fat diet mice and human islets from T2D donors lost responsiveness to GABA, and the male Gad βKO islets did not produce oscillations in response to 8 mM glucose. The results from human islets from donors with T2D also produced poor oscillations and were unresponsive to GABA. This unresponsiveness in T2D human islets may be caused by changes in GABA receptor cell surface trafficking or expression. The results in high-fat diet mouse islets and T2D human islets reinforce our findings that GABA is a critical regulator of islet oscillations. The dysfunction caused by the metabolic stress placed on islets in the form of high-fat diet or T2D is likely compounded by a lack of GABA signaling, either through islet GABA content depletion or GABA non-responsiveness.

Conclusion

Here, we report on the first beta cell-specific model to knockout GABA biosynthesis to directly interrogate the role of endogenous GABA in islet physiology. We conclude that the primary role of GABA in the islet is to promote strong and balanced Ca2+ oscillations. Islets lacking in GABA had deformed oscillations or lacked oscillations entirely at higher glucose levels. We hypothesize that GABA contributes to Ca2+ oscillations through negative feedback. The effect of applied GABA appears to be modulated by GABAAR and is reproduced when specifically targeted by pharmacology. Thus, the inhibitory effect of applied GABA is dominated by the GABAAR pathway. On the other hand, interfering with the GABABR had a profound negative effect on Ca2+ oscillations, suggesting this Gi/o-GPCR may also be critical to oscillations, at least in mouse islets. The net effect of GABA is inhibitory to insulin secretion during a glucose challenge, as shown by the over-secretion of insulin in Gad βKO islets. Thus, GABA is crucial to maintain insulin secretion in the normal range. GABA is likely involved in diseases of the islet, including T1D and T2D.

Methods

Generation of the knockout model

Gad1loxp::Gad2loxp::Rosa26tdTomato mice (B6.Cg-Gad2tm1Rpa Gad1tm1Rpa Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/RpaJ) were generated in Richard Palmiter's lab at University of Washington [52] and provided by Dr. Qi Wu's lab at Baylor University College of Medicine were crossed with mice expressing Cre recombinase in beta cells (Ins1cre, B6(Cg)-Ins1tm1.1(cre)Thor/J) [20] to generate the Gad βKO mice. All offspring were genotyped post-wean using tail snips and custom primers generated by Transnetyx specific to each Gad1loxp, Gad2loxp, Rosa26tdTomato, and Ins1cre sequence. Ins1-cre mice were used as controls.

Parameters development evaluation, euthanasia, and tissue collection

Mice 30 days old were used to evaluate weekly the body weight, food ingestion, and non-fasting blood glucose. The data was collected for 12 weeks. For in vitro experiments, mice were euthanized using deep isoflurane anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation. Post-sacrifice, either islet isolation occurred or pancreata were collected, weighed, trimmed for excess fat, and processed for fixation in 3.2% paraformaldehyde overnight. Islets were isolated according to the protocol below. Isolated islets were used in western blot, HPLC, and immunohistochemistry experiments to confirm knockout. Additional islets were used in functional perifusion studies and calcium imaging.

Oral glucose tolerance test (oGTT)

Fasted (6–8 h) adult mice were used to determine glucose tolerance. Post-fasting, blood glucose was measured from the tail-tip using a commercially available AccuCheck blood glucose meter to determine timepoint 0.2 g/kg of 25% dextrose (Vedco) solution was administered by gavage, and the blood glucose was measured at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after glucose administration. Blood samples from the cheek were collected in a microtube with EDTA (Sarstedt n° 16.444.100) and spun down at 2000G for 20 min to collect the plasma. Plasma was then analyzed using insulin and C-Peptide crystal chem ELISA kits (n° 62 100 and n° 90 050) according to the manufacturer's instructions and read on a MicroDevices M5 or M5e spectrophotometer.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (ipGTT)

Fasted (6–8 h) adult mice were used to determine glucose tolerance. Post-fasting, blood glucose was measured from the tail-tip using a commercially available AccuCheck blood glucose meter to determine timepoint 0.1 g/kg of 25% dextrose (Vedco) solution was administered intraperitoneally, and the blood glucose was measured at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after glucose administration. Blood samples from tail-tip pricks were collected in a microtube with EDTA (Sarstedt n° 16.444.100) and spun down at 2000G for 20 min to collect the plasma. Plasma was then analyzed using insulin and C-Peptide crystal chem ELISA kits (n° 62 100 and n° 90 050) according to the manufacturer's instructions and read on a MicroDevices M5 or M5e spectrophotometer.

Intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (ipITT)

Fasted (2–4 h) adult mice were used to determine insulin sensitivity. Post-fasting, blood glucose was measured from the tail-tip using a commercially available AccuCheck blood glucose meter to determine timepoint 0.0.75U/kg of insulin (Humulin R, Lilly) was administered intraperitoneally, and the blood glucose was measured at 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 30, and 60 min after insulin administration. The rate of glucose decay constant (kITT) was calculated from the blood glucose concentrations during the linear decay phase.

High-fat diet

Mice 30 days old were treated with a low-fat (Research diets n° D12450J) or high-fat diet (Research diets n° D12492) for 60 days. Body weight, food ingestion, and non-fasting blood glucose were evaluated weekly. After 60 days on the diet, glucose and insulin tolerance tests were performed, and the mice were euthanized to isolate islets to analyze insulin secretion and for calcium imaging.

Immunostaining

Pancreata from mice were fixed in 3.2% paraformaldehyde and prepared for either cryosectioning or paraffin embedding. For cryosections, tissues were cryoprotected in 15% and 30% sucrose, embedded in O.C.T. (Fisher Scientific n° 4585), sectioned with a Leica CM1860, and stained using standard immunofluorescence protocols with anti-GABA (Sigma Aldrich n° A2052), Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen n° A11008), anti-insulin (DAKO n° A0564), and Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen n° A21450). Slides were mounted with ProLong Gold (Invitrogen n° P36934), imaged on a Leica SP8, and analyzed in ImageJ. Paraffin sections underwent graded dehydration, paraffin embedding, and 4 μm serial sectioning. Multiplex IHC involved citrate antigen retrieval (BioGenex n° HK086–9K), blocking, and sequential staining with anti-somatostatin (Agilent n° A0566), anti-PP (Sigma–Aldrich n° AB939), anti-glucagon (Abcam n° ab10988), and anti-insulin (Agilent n° A0564), using corresponding secondary reagents and chromogens including DAB (Vector Labs n° SK-4105), Ferangi Blue (BioCare n° FB813S), and Liquid Permanent Red (Agilent n° K064030-2) and counterstained with hematoxylin (BioCare n° CATHE-M). Slides were scanned on an Aperio ScanScope CS and analyzed in ImageScope with color deconvolution and manual annotation for endocrine cell quantification.

Mouse islet isolation

After the mouse was euthanized, an incision was made in the abdomen to expose the pancreatic duct, where 3 mL of collagenase (0.6 mg/mL in HBSS) (Sigma Aldrich n° C9263) solution was injected. The pancreas was dissected and placed in a 15 mL conical tube and incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 17 min. After the incubation, 10 mL of cold HBSS (Gibco n° 14025092) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cytiva n° SH30071), 1% pen/strep (Gibco n° 15 140–122), and 2% Hepes buffer (Fisher Scientific n° BP299100) was added to stop the collagenase activity, and the tube was shaken to complete the tissue dissociation and spun for 30 s at 1800 G. The supernatant was discarded, and 10 mL of cold HBSS with FBS was added. The islets were hand-picked up with a micropipette in the stereoscope. The islets were cultured overnight in RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific n° 1187–093) with 10% FBS, and 1% pen/strep at 37 °C.

Western blotting

Islet lysates were prepared by extracting whole islet proteins in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific n°89 900) plus a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche n° 04 693 159 001). A BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific n° 23 227) was used to measure the protein content of islet lysate for normalization. Electrophoresis of proteins from islet lysates was performed using the Bolt western blot system. Forty micrograms of protein from each lysate were loaded into Bolt 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen n° NW041120) and run at a constant 200V for 25 min. The transfer was performed using standard transfer settings on the iBlot 3.0 device (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked with Intercept blocking buffer (LiCOR n° 927–70001) and incubated in a custom rabbit anti-GAD65/GAD67 primary antibody [6,53] (1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were then rinsed 3x with 1x PBS at 5 min each, then incubated in IRDye 680RD goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10 000) (LiCOR n° 922–68071) for 1 h. After scanning the GAD65/GAD67 bands, the membrane was incubated with mouse anti-β actin primary antibody (1:10000) (Sigma Aldrich n° A1978) for 30 min, RT, washed 3x in 1x PBS, and incubated IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:10 000) (LiCOR n° 922–68071) for 1 h, RT. After secondary antibodies incubation, the membrane was rinsed again 3x in 1x PBS, followed by imaging on the Li-COR Odyssey CLx scanner.

HPLC analysis of GABA and glutamate content

To determine GABA and Glutamate content, electrochemical analysis of ortho-phthalaldehyde (OPA)-derivatized free amino acids was performed [53]. Islets were lysed directly in the mobile phase containing 7% acetonitrile and 13% methanol in a phosphate buffer, using vigorous agitation, at a concentration of 10 islet equivalents per 50 μL. Lysates were prepared using a 1:2 dilution in methanol. Samples were filtered through 0.22 μm PVDF centrifugal filters (Corning n°8160) at 5,000 G for 2 min. Samples were then analyzed using the EICOM HTEC-510 HPLC automated system with a FA-3ODS separation column. Serial dilutions of GABA and Glutamate were analyzed and fitted to a quadratic calibration curve, with sensitivity from 10 nM to 10 μM, which was used to determine the total GABA and Glutamate content of islet lysate.

Human islet preparation

Isolated and purified human islets were obtained from non-diabetic donors and type 2 diabetic donors from the NIDDK-funded Integrated Islet Distribution Program (IIDP) at City of Hope and from Prodo Laboratories, Inc. Use of cadaveric human pancreatic islets has been approved and classified as “Non-Human Subjects” by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board (IRB Nr. 201702860). Briefly, human islets, upon receipt, were removed from their shipping containers, centrifuged at 180G for 2 min, and changed into Prodo Islet Media (PIM (S)) (PRODO laboratories n° 123456789) supplemented with 5% Human AB Serum Supplement A cGMP (PIM (ABS, G & 3X) (PRODO laboratories #PIM-ABS001GMP. The islets were visually inspected under a Leica M80 stereomicroscope to confirm purity and islet count. Islets were separated into non-adherent cell culture dishes at a density of 500 islets per dish. Dishes were cultured at 24 °C upon initial receipt and up to one-week post receipt. One hour before experimenting, islets were moved to culture at 37 °C.

KRBH buffer preparation

Kreb's ringer bicarbonate HEPES buffer (KRBH) was prepared initially from Krebs ringer buffer containing 115 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4 and adding 25 mM sodium bicarbonate, 25 mM HEPES, and 0.2% BSA. Final solutions were equilibrated to a pH of 7.4 and an osmolarity of 294 mOsmol/L. Low glucose KRBH contained an additional 3 mM glucose, high glucose contained 16.7 mM glucose, and KCl KRBH contained 30 mM potassium chloride.

Perifusion of human and rodent islets

Isolated islets were used in conjunction with the Biorep Perifusion v2.0 system. Chambers loaded with a combination of 25 islet equivalents and Bio-Gel P-4 gel (Bio-Rad n° 150 124) were loaded into the machine and perfused with KRBH buffer at a rate of 100 microliters per minute. The protocol for mice islets started with a 60-min equilibration period using heated 3 mM glucose KRBH, followed by a collection period of 10 min of 3 mM glucose, 20 min of 16.7 mM glucose, 20 min of 3 mM glucose, 5 min of 30 mM KCl, and 10 min 3 mM glucose. Perfusate was collected using a fraction collector designed for 96-well plates and stored at −80 °C prior to analysis of insulin content. The protocol for human islets started with a 60-min equilibration period using heated 3 mM glucose KRBH, followed by a collection period of 5 min of 3 mM glucose, 5 min of 3 mM glucose +10 μM GABA, 10 min of 3 mM of glucose, 10 min of 16.7 mM glucose, 5 min 16.7 mM glucose +10 μM GABA, 10 min of 16.7 mM glucose, 20 min of 3 mM glucose, 5 min of 30 mM KCl, and 10 min 3 mM glucose. Perfusate was collected using a fraction collector designed for 96-well plates and stored at −80 °C prior to analysis of insulin content. The ALPCO Chemiluminescent human (#80-INSHU) or rodent insulin (#80-INSMR) ELISA Kit was used according to the manufacturer's instructions and read on a MicroDevices M5 or M5e spectrophotometer to analyze insulin content.

Static insulin secretion

Mouse islets were isolated one day before usage and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Ten islets were incubated in 1 mL of no-glucose KRBH solution for 1 h. For incubations, the islets were incubated for 1 h in 500 μL of different glucose concentrations KRBH solutions: 5.6, 8,11.1, and 16.7 mM), and then in 30 mM KCl. The supernatant was collected and maintained at −80 °C to measure the insulin. Islets were lysed in 75 μL of RIPA buffer, sonicated for 10 s at 20% amplitude, and maintained at −80 °C to measure the insulin total content and protein. The ALPCO Chemiluminescent rodent insulin (#80-INSMR) ELISA Kit was used according to the manufacturer's instructions and read on a MicroDevices M5 or M5e spectrophotometer to analyze insulin content.

Calcium imaging

Human islets were cultured at 37 °C 1 h prior to imaging, and mouse islets were cultured at 37 °C 1 day prior to imaging. A group of ∼10 Islets per recording was loaded with 20 μM Calbryte-520 (AAT Bioquest n° 20 650) in 5.6 mM Glucose KRBH solution and left to incubate for 35 min prior to imaging. Islets were then placed on coverslips (VWR n° 48393–172) previously treated with Poly-l-lysine (Sigma Aldrich n° P4707) inside a perfusion chamber connected to a series of syringe pumps connected by a single manifold. Islets were perfused at a rate of 100 μl/min. Islets were equilibrated with 10 min of perfusion in 5.6 mM glucose KRBH before image collection. The image collection consisted of 5–10 min of 3 or 5.6 mM glucose followed by 50 min of high glucose (8, 11, or 16.7 mM), plus GABA, GABA receptor agonists and antagonists, and tolbutamide, depending on the experiment's objective. The calcium images were acquired using a Leica SP8 with HyD detectors, live recordings were performed using the resonance scanner, and a time interval of 0.4 s per acquisition at a pixel resolution of 512 x 512. The pinhole was opened to a value of airy >1.6 to maximize the amount of signal gathered from cells further from the surface. Post-collection images were analyzed for mean fluorescence intensity using ImageJ. Second-phase insulin secretion was defined as the mean resting fluorescence level during the sustained portion under glucose stimulation, while the first phase was defined as the region prior to the sustained second-phase level following the initial calcium response. Minute perturbations in islet movement were corrected using StackReg [54]. Fluorescent intensities were normalized to their initial values. The calcium oscillations analysis was performed using Python and MATLAB software. The chemicals used in calcium recordings were GABA (Sigma–Aldrich n° A5835), CGP55845 hydrochloride (Tocris Bioscience n° 1248/10), SR95531 Hydrobromide (Tocris Bioscience n° 1262), Muscimol (Tocris Bioscience n° 0289), Baclofen (Tocris Bioscience n° 0417), and Tolbutamide (Sigma–Aldrich n° T0891).

Gene expression analysis of mouse and human islet scRNA-seq datasets

Mouse islet scRNA-seq datasets of three control mice (WT, m/m) (GSE200531) [55]. The Seurat object of human islet scRNA-seq from 65 Human Pancreas Analysis Program (HPAP) donors was downloaded from http://www.isletgenomics.org/ [56]. The non-diabetic donors were selected and used in the analysis. Mouse and human datasets were analyzed in R Studio v2024.04.0 with the R package Seurat v5.1.0 [57]. Gene expression plots were performed with the graphical tools of Seurat and ggpubr v0.6.0 (https://rpkgs.datanovia.com/ggpubr/.). Statistical testing was performed on global-scaled, log normalized single cell expression with a one-way ANOVA (aov function) followed by a Tukey post-hoc with the tukey_hsd function from rstatix package v0.7.2 (https://rpkgs.datanovia.com/rstatix/).

Mouse WT datasets were loaded onto R Studio with the Read10x function. Seurat objects for each dataset were created, merged, quality control was performed, and filtered for a minimum of 1000–7500 expressed genes per cell and <7.5% mitochondrial reads. Doublets were removed with DoubletFinder on a per-dataset basis. After doublet removal, WT datasets were re-merged, normalized, and scaled. The 2000 most variable features were obtained and used for PCA dimensionality reduction with 20 principal components. Harmony v1.2.3 [58] was used to perform batch correction between each dataset. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was performed with Harmony reduction. Clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm at a resolution of 0.5. Mouse islet cell marker genes were used to identify clusters and/or perform additional subclusters with 0.25 resolution. Any clusters/subclusters expressing multiple cell type markers were flagged as residual doublets and manually removed. Harmony integration was rerun, and final clusters were annotated according to cell type marker expression.

Final Seurat objects for mouse and human scRNA-seq were filtered for endocrine cells only, and gene expression plots of genes of interest were performed with DotPlot (Seurat).

Statistical analysis

Standard analysis of statistical significance utilized analysis of variance (ANOVA), if between multiple groups, or the standard student's t-test between two groups, or Mann–Whitney test, for not normally distributed values, as performed in GraphPad Prism 10. Post-hoc testing was performed with a default of Tukey for multiple comparisons or Bonferroni correction for t-testing. One-tailed tests were used when applicable. Statistical significance shown in the figures of ANOVA analysis may include significance in both the main effects model and the post-hoc multiple comparisons. The p-values are indicated in the figure legends. All error bars represent mean ± sem.
