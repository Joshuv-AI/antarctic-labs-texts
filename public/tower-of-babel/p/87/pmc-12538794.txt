The association between the aggregate index of systemic inflammation and abdominal aortic calcification: a National health and nutrition examination survey
Authors: Youfu Wang, Yiming Su, Changzhi Luo, Wenhong Jiang, Han Yang, Xiao Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe occurrence of abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) is associated with inflammation. This study aims to explore the relationship between the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) and AAC.MethodsWe utilized data from the 2013–2014 cycle of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). A loge transformation was applied to AISI (logeAISI) to preserve its normal distribution. Weighted multiple regression analysis, trend tests, smoothing curves, and threshold effect analysis were employed to evaluate the relationship between logeAISI and both AAC scores and severe AAC (SAAC). Subgroup analysis and interaction tests were conducted to evaluate the stability of their relationship across different populations. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) were used to compare the predictive ability of logeAISI with systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR).ResultsThis study included 2,730 participants. After adjusting for covariates, a positive correlation was observed between logeAISI and both AAC scores and SAAC. Compared to the first quartile of logeAISI, the highest quartile showed increased AAC scores (β = 0.37, 95% CI = 0.20–0.55) and a higher risk of SAAC (OR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.06–1.62) in the fully adjusted model. Smooth curve and threshold effect analysis demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between logeAISI and both AAC scores and SAAC, with a breakpoint at 6.64. Subgroup analysis revealed that the relationship between logeAISI and AAC scores is more pronounced in individuals aged 65 and older, those with coronary artery disease, and diabetic patients, while the association with SAAC is more significant in diabetic patients. Compared to other inflammatory markers (SII, NLR, PLR), AISI has a higher predictive ability for AAC (AUC = 0.57, 95%CI: 0.54–0.59) and SAAC (AUC = 0.61, 95%CI: 0.58–0.65).ConclusionOur study highlights that AISI is correlated with both AAC and SAAC. However, extensive prospective studies are required to validate our results.Keywords: Aggregate index of systemic inflammation, Abdominal aortic calcification, Inflammation, NHANES, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) is a pathological process characterized by the deposition of minerals in vascular smooth muscle cells [1]. Studies have reported that the prevalence of AAC increases with age, reaching up to 100% in individuals over 75 years old [2]. Research indicates that AAC is closely associated with cardiovascular events and increased mortality risk in dialysis patients, making it a predictor for both cardiovascular events and mortality in this population [3–5]. Kevin et al. reported that patients with AAC have a 1.85 (95% CI, 1.44–2.39) times higher risk of fatal cardiovascular events compared to the general population [6]. Inflammation plays a critical role in the development of AAC, with mechanisms primarily involving inflammatory factors produced by immune cells, which induce osteogenic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells and regulate mineral deposition in the vascular intima [7–9]. Currently, the identification of AAC mainly relies on lateral lumbar spine X-rays, which have drawbacks such as high cost and radiation exposure [10, 11].

The aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) is a novel inflammatory index derived from platelet, neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts. This index offers a comprehensive assessment of an individual’s systemic inflammatory status and can be easily obtained through routine complete blood count testing. Although several studies have explored the relationship between the monocyte-lymphocyte ratio and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio with AAC, no studies have examined the association between AISI and AAC [12, 13]. Compared to the monocyte-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), AISI combines four cell types, offering a more holistic evaluation of systemic inflammation. Existing research suggests that AISI may serve as a biomarker for mortality in patients with hypertension, stroke, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and cardiovascular diseases [14–17]. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) assessed AAC during the 2013–2014 cycle. In this context, the present study aims to investigate the relationship between AISI and AAC.

Methods

Study population

We downloaded data for this study from the 2013–2014 cycle of NHANES, a national survey approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. All participants provided informed consent. A total of 10,175 participants were involved in the NHANES 2013–2014 survey. After excluding participants with missing data on AAC assessment (N = 7035), platelet count (N = 84), neutrophil count (N = 9), and covariates (N = 317), the remaining 2,730 participants were included in our study (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of participant selection for this study. NHANES: National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey; AAC: abdominal aortic calcification

Calculation of AISI, SII, NLR, and PLR

NHANES used flow cytometry to perform complete blood counts on participants, with rigorous quality control of the process. According to previous reports, the calculation formulas for inflammatory markers are as follows: AISI = neutrophil count * monocyte count* platelet count/lymphocyte count; Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) = platelet count * neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; Platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) = platelet count/lymphocyte count. We performed a loge transformation on AISI (logeAISI), SII (logeSII), NLR (logeNLR), and PLR (logePLR) to maintain their normal distribution.

Assessment of AAC

NHANES assessed AAC status through lateral spinal X-ray scans and scored AAC using the AAC-24 semi-quantitative scoring techniques. By identifying the presence of calcification in the abdominal aorta at different lumbar spine segments, the AAC-24 semi-quantitative scoring technique is currently considered an effective method for evaluating AAC [18]. The scoring range is from 0 to 24 points, where a score greater than 0 indicates the presence of AAC, and a score greater than 6 indicates severe AAC (SAAC). NHANES implemented high-quality control measures for both the scanning and data collection processes related to AAC.

Covariates

To control for potential confounding variables, based on previous studies, the covariates selected in this study included age (years), gender (male/female), marital status (yes/no), race, education level, body mass index (BMI) (kg/m²), alcohol consumption (yes/no), smoking (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), coronary heart disease (CHD) (yes/no), total calcium (mmol/L), cholesterol (mmol/L), uric acid (mg/dL), creatinine (µmol/L), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (25(OH)D3)(nmol/L). Within the NHANES survey framework, race was classified into four groups: Mexican Americans, other Hispanics, non-Hispanics, and other races. Likewise, educational level was categorized as: college graduate or above, high school or equivalent, and under high school. The NHANES questionnaire provides information on age, gender, marital status, race, education level, alcohol consumption, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, and CHD. Physical measurements are used to determine BMI, and NHANES lab tests are the source of total calcium, cholesterol, uric acid, creatinine, and 25(OH)D3. Data with incomplete covariate information was not included.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the differences were assessed using a t-test or Mann-Whitney U test according to normal or non-normal distribution, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as proportions and analyzed using the Chi-Square test. We employed multiple regression models and trend tests to examine the relationship between logeAISI and AAC scores and SAAC. Prior to performing the regression analysis, we assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors and checked the linearity of continuous variables with the log-odds using the Box-Tidwell test. Model 1 was not adjusted for any covariates. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, marital status, race, and education level. Model 3 further adjusted model 2 for BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, CHD, total calcium, cholesterol, uric acid, creatinine, and 25(OH)D3. Smooth curves were plotted to visualize the relationships between AISI and AAC scores as well as SAAC. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests were conducted based on age, gender, race, BMI, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, and CHD to explore the relationships between AISI and AAC scores as well as SAAC across different populations. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to compare the diagnostic abilities of AISI, SII, NLR, and PLR for AAC scores and SAAC. Considering the sampling method of NHANES, sample weights were included in this study. All the data were statistically analyzed using R software (version 4.3) and EmpowerStats (version 4.2), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis

The smooth curve indicated a nonlinear correlation between AISI, AAC scores, and SAAC (Fig. 2). Further threshold effect analysis identified a breakpoint at logeAISI = 6.64. On the left side of this breakpoint, logeAISI was positively correlated with both AAC scores and SAAC. Specifically, for each one-unit increase in logeAISI, the AAC score increased by 0.37 points (β = 0.37, 95% CI = 0.17–0.57), and the risk of SAAC increased by 66% (OR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.29–2.12). No statistically significant differences were observed on the right side of the breakpoint, with likelihood ratio tests yielding values of 0.02 and 0.047, respectively (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis showed that the association between logeAISI, AAC, and SAAC was not consistent across all subgroups. Specifically, gender, race, BMI, alcohol consumption, and CHD did not influence the association between logeAISI and AAC. However, the correlation between logeAISI and AAC was more significant in patients with CHD, diabetes, and individuals aged 65 and above (P for interaction < 0.05). Meanwhile, the relationship between logeAISI and SAAC remained stable across age, gender, race, BMI, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and CHD, but was more pronounced in the diabetes group (P for interaction < 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Comparison of predictive abilities of inflammatory markers

By plotting the ROC curves for logeAISI, logeSII, logeNLR, and logePLR, we compared the predictive abilities of these inflammatory markers. The results showed that logeAISI had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.57 for predicting AAC and 0.61 for predicting SAAC, both higher than those of logeSII, logeNLR, and logePLR. This indicates that AISI has a stronger predictive ability for AAC and SAAC than SII, NLR, and PLR (Fig. 4).

Discussion

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 2,730 participants to explore the relationship between logeAISI and AAC. The results indicated a nonlinear association between logeAISI and both AAC scores and SAAC after fully adjusting for covariates. Specifically, on the left side of the threshold (logeAISI = 6.64), logeAISI was positively correlated with both AAC scores and SAAC, while on the right side, no statistically significant correlation was observed between logeAISI and AAC scores or SAAC. Additionally, ROC analysis revealed that AISI had stronger predictive ability for AAC and SAAC compared to other inflammatory markers (SII, NLR, PLR).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the association between AISI and AAC. AISI is a novel inflammatory index initially proposed to predict the length of hospital stay for patients undergoing thoracic surgery [19]. Subsequent research has identified AISI as a potential biomarker for mortality in stroke patients, hypertension, severity of COVID-19, and prognosis of CHD [15, 20–22]. A study on COVID-19 demonstrated that AISI had a higher predictive value for mortality compared to SII, NLR, and PLR [23]. Similarly, research by Angelo et al. showed that AISI is a useful prognostic biomarker for patients with pulmonary fibrosis, highlighting its potential in predicting outcomes for this condition [16]. Li et al. found that AISI has the same predictive value as SII in assessing the risk of CHD in patients with hypertension [24]. The occurrence of AAC is closely associated with inflammation, and traditional biomarkers such as IL-6, IL-1β, and hs-CRP have been shown to have significant correlations with AAC [25, 26]. Our findings indicate that AISI is positively correlated with both AAC scores and SAAC. Although the ROC curve analysis reveals that AISI does not have a high AUC value for predicting AAC and SAAC, in the context of the current lack of well-established predictors for AAC and SAAC, AISI complements these traditional inflammatory markers and may provide additional value in predicting AAC. Moreover, AISI, in combination with inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and hs-CRP, may enhance the predictive value for AAC, but further studies are needed to confirm this potential.

Inflammation plays a crucial role in the mechanisms underlying arterial calcification. Studies have shown that monocytes and macrophages can induce endothelial cell osteoblastic differentiation through the release of TNF-α and IL-1β, thereby promoting vascular calcification [27, 28]. Moreover, monocytes migrate to the arterial wall, adhere to damaged endothelial cells and trigger the onset of arterial calcification, further promoting its development by differentiating into macrophages, ingesting oxidized low-density lipoprotein, and forming foam cells [29]. Previous studies have confirmed that neutrophils primarily contribute to vascular calcification through the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, which promote vascular smooth muscle cell apoptosis via histone H4 and enhance vascular calcification through osteocalcin and TGF-β [30–32]. In addition, neutrophils release circulating microvesicles that activate NF-κB, inducing oxidative stress and further promoting vascular calcification [29, 33]. Studies have shown that by releasing platelet-derived growth factors, platelets can activate inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-6, which further promote vascular calcification [34]. Moreover, platelets contribute to the progression of AAC by releasing inflammatory mediators that stimulate the migration of vascular smooth muscle cells, thereby initiating and amplifying the inflammatory response [35].

Research has shown that chronic inflammation increases with age and is considered a significant risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and Alzheimer’s disease in the elderly [36, 37]. The prevalence of AAC increases with age [6]. Nicole et al. found that in the elderly population, an increase in inflammatory factors leads to higher oxidative stress responses, which directly contribute to an increased occurrence of arterial calcification [38]. The role of inflammation in CHD has been widely supported by research, with inflammation contributing to and exacerbating the development of arterial calcification in CHD patients [39]. Recent large-scale prospective studies have demonstrated that inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and lipoproteins are closely associated with CHD and can predict cardiovascular events over a 30-year period [40]. Additionally, it has been found that a higher number of inflammatory markers exceeding normal values correlates with an increased incidence of CHD [41]. It has been reported that diabetic patients have a 12% higher incidence of AAC compared to the general population, which may be related to the chronic pro-inflammatory state in diabetes, leading to vascular smooth muscle cell calcification and subsequently promoting arterial calcification [42, 43]. Our subgroup analysis results indicate that the correlation between AISI and AAC is more pronounced in the elderly as well as in patients with CHD and diabetes, which aligns with previous literature findings.

This study has several strengths. Firstly, we utilized nationwide, stratified sampling data from NHANES and employed weighted analysis, which enhances the reliability of our results. Secondly, in addition to performing validation and interaction tests, we compared the predictive ability of AISI for AAC with other inflammatory markers, which adds completeness to our findings. However, there are limitations to this study. Firstly, the NHANES data are derived from a cross-sectional study, which does not establish a causal relationship between AISI and AAC. Secondly, AAC assessments were only conducted during the 2013–2014 cycle of NHANES, leading to a lack of data continuity. Lastly, as the data are sourced from the U.S. population, the applicability of our results to other populations requires further investigation. Given these limitations, future interventional studies are needed to explore whether reducing AISI can lower the risk of AAC. For example, randomized controlled trials targeting AISI with anti-inflammatory or other treatments could assess its potential as a modifiable risk factor for AAC, which may have important clinical implications for its prevention and management.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that AISI is associated with both AAC and SAAC. Maintaining a lower AISI may help reduce the incidence of AAC. However, further large-scale studies are needed to validate these findings.
