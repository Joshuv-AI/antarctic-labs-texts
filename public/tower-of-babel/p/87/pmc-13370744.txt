Advances in green-synthesized quantum dot-based nanoplatforms for cancer treatment, photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy and cancer theranostics
Authors: Glory Kah, Heidi Abrahamse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The escalating global cancer burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, necessitates safer and more effective therapeutic strategies. The toxicity and environmental issues of traditional heavy-metal-based quantum dots (QDs) have been addressed by green-synthesised QDs, which have become a promising platform for nanomedicine. In photodynamic treatment (PDT), photothermal therapy (PTT), and theranostic applications, the anticancer effectiveness of green-synthesized QDs that are derived from plant extracts, microbes, biomolecules, and biomass waste is critically assessed. Green synthesis techniques, such as hydrothermal and microwave-assisted methods, provide biocompatible QDs with good photostability, tunable optical characteristics, and decreased cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, these QDs generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), induce mitochondrial dysfunction, produce localized hyperthermia upon near-infrared irradiation, and activate apoptotic pathways (such as p53, Bax/Bcl-2, and caspase cascade), leading to selective cancer cell death. Preclinical in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate potent tumor ablation through passive (EPR effect) and active targeting strategies. Despite these advances, some key setbacks hinder their clinical translation: lack of standardized synthesis protocols, batch-to-batch variability, limited long-term biosafety data, suboptimal targeting efficiency, and regulatory hurdles. Future perspectives include AI-driven optimization, smart theranostic platforms integrating multimodal imaging and therapy, and sustainable circular economy approaches using biowaste. Addressing these challenges through harmonized protocols and rigorous preclinical validation will be essential to realize the full potential of green-synthesized QDs as safe, multifunctional, and effective cancer nanomedicines.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global contributor to the burden of diseases, with incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise worldwide. Despite improvements in cancer treatment, diagnosis, and initiatives to address the cancer risk factors, the number of cancer diagnoses and fatalities worldwide increased dramatically between 1990 and 2023. It is predicted that 30.5 million people will be diagnosed with cancer and 18.6 million will die from the disease by 2050, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) expected to record more than half of new cases and two-thirds of deaths if immediate action and focused funding are not implemented.1 Chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, and hormonal therapy are examples of the conventional treatment approaches for cancer, yet they are frequently linked to nonspecific toxicity, adverse side effects, multidrug resistance, high likelihood of recurrence, and limited therapeutic efficacy.2 These limitations greatly highlight the critical need for safer and more effective treatment approaches. The rapidly emerging nanomedicine approach, ‘nanotechnology-based cancer therapeutics’, uses the special qualities of materials at the nanoscale to improve treatment selectivity, minimize side effects, and improve overall results.3,4 Furthermore, nanomaterials can facilitate multidrug loading and targeted distribution to the tumor microenvironment to overcome drug resistance in cancerous cells.4

Nanomaterials, such as nanoscale semiconductor materials known as quantum dots (QDs), which are usually less than 10 nm in size, have special size-dependent optical and electrical characteristics. Among NPs, QDs are notable and essential because of their applications across various industries, including quantum computing, sensors, environmental remediation, photovoltaics, catalysis, display technology, and light-emitting diodes (LEDs).5 Besides, they are very appealing for biological applications in cancer treatment, drug delivery, and bioimaging due to their great photostability, wide absorption spectra, adjustable fluorescence, and narrow emission bands.6–8 QDs have been investigated in oncology as therapeutic agents, fluorescent probes for tumor imaging, drug delivery vehicles, biosensors, photosensitizers in photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) agents. PDT is a less invasive treatment approach that explores the use of light-activated photosensitizers at a particular wavelength to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which cause tumor cell death. Likewise, QDs in PTT offer a non-invasive treatment, where they absorb light to generate localized heat that kills cancerous cells.9–12 Compared to traditional photosensitizers, QDs (as photosensitizers) have better photostability, adjustable excitation wavelengths, and the capacity to serve as both imaging and therapeutic agents, making theranostic cancer treatment possible.9–11 Despite the promising biomedical efficacy of QDs, conventionally formulated QDs, especially those containing heavy metals like lead and cadmium, pose significant concerns about cytotoxicity, environmental persistence, and bioaccumulation. These issues have severely hampered their clinical translation and regulatory approval.13 Nonetheless, green-synthesized QDs have gained prominence as biocompatible and sustainable substitutes. Green-synthesized QDs, which are made from natural biomolecules, microbes, or plant extracts, offer better biodegradability, low cytotoxicity, and increased biological compatibility while retaining desired therapeutic and optical properties.14,15 Current studies have reported the effectiveness of green-synthesized QDs in the treatment of cancer through a variety of mechanisms, such as the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial malfunction, photothermal conversion under light irradiation, and apoptosis induction.9,16 Green-synthesized QDs have further established themselves as potent platforms for cancer theranostics, allowing for image-guided therapy, real-time diagnosis, and individualized treatment plans due to their capacity to function as both imaging agents and therapeutic mediators at the same time.17,18 A thorough assessment of their anticancer efficacy spanning PDT, PTT, and theranostic applications seems lacking despite the increased interest. In order to assess the effectiveness of quantum dots in cancer treatment, cancer photodynamic therapy, cancer photothermal therapy, and theranostic cancer applications, this paper critically explores current developments in the green synthesis of quantum dots. Synthesis methodologies, mechanistic insights, therapeutic performance, biosafety considerations, and potential future translations are highlighted.

Review scope and literature selection criteria

This review was conducted through a systematic search of peer-reviewed literature published between 2004 and June 2026 in major scientific databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Most studies reporting original research on the synthesis, characterization, or therapeutic application of green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer models were included. Priority was given to articles providing mechanistic insights, quantitative therapeutic outcomes, and biosafety evaluations. The selection was intentionally broad to capture the diversity of green precursors (plant extracts, microorganisms, biomolecules, and biomass waste), synthesis routes (Fig. 1), and therapeutic modalities (PDT, PTT, and theranostics). Studies reporting on non-green QD synthesis or applications outside cancer therapy were not strongly considered. This comprehensive approach ensures that the review reflects the current state of the field while identifying critical knowledge gaps and translational barriers.

Positioning relative to prior reviews and novelty

Several comprehensive reviews have previously addressed aspects of quantum dot-based cancer therapy. In a meta-analysis of cellular toxicity for cadmium-containing quantum dots, Oh et al. developed a quantitative framework for comprehending the relationship between cytotoxic results and physicochemical characteristics, such as surface coatings, diameter, and exposure circumstances.19 Wang et al. reviewed multicolored carbon dots for tumor theranostics, highlighting the potential of carbon-based QDs for simultaneous imaging and therapy.20 Tade and Patil provided a focused review on the theranostic prospects of graphene quantum dots specifically in breast cancer, addressing the photophysical properties explored in photothermal therapy, hyperthermia therapy, and photodynamic therapy.21 Malavika et al. comprehensively examined biomass-derived carbon quantum dots for cancer theranostics, correlating fabrication methodologies with biomedical applications.22 The cytotoxicity of semiconductor QDs was first studied by Derfus et al., who showed that under some circumstances, CdSe-core QDs are acutely hazardous and that cytotoxicity is correlated with the release of free Cd2+ ions as a result of CdSe lattice degradation.23 While these prior reviews/studies have made significant contributions, the present review distinguishes itself in several key aspects. First, unlike the meta-analytical focus of Oh et al. or the material-specific focus of Tade and Patil, this review provides a comprehensive and integrated assessment of green-synthesized QDs across all major precursor categories (plant extracts, microorganisms, biomolecules, and biomass waste), as well as the therapeutic modalities (PDT, PTT, and theranostics). Second, this study provides a cohesive molecular framework that is lacking in previous studies by closely analyzing the mechanistic pathways through which green-synthesized QDs exercise their anticancer effects, such as ROS production, mitochondrial malfunction, and apoptotic signaling. Third, this review explicitly addresses the translational barriers that have hindered clinical progress, which include the lack of standardization, regulatory hurdles, and long-term biosafety concerns, providing a forward-looking perspective on how these challenges might be overcome. Fourth, this review explores the most recent advances (2023–2026) in the field, including AI-driven optimization, smart theranostic platforms, and sustainable circular economy approaches. By integrating these elements, the present review fills a critical knowledge gap and provides a timely, holistic resource for researchers and clinicians interested in the translational potential of green-synthesized QD-based cancer nanomedicines.

Quantum dots

Researchers may find the term “quantum dots” confusing, particularly if they are unfamiliar with quantum mechanics or nanotechnology. Depending on the context, it can be interpreted differently, necessitating more precise specifications. The word “quantum” suggests a small, distinct unit of any physical attribute. This appellation originates because quantum mechanics governs the optical characteristics of nanocrystals (NC) or “Dots”. Thus, QDs are semiconductor nanoscale particles with size-dependent bandgap quantum confinement, typically ranging in size from 2 to 10 nm.24,25 The arrangement of atoms in QDs is comparable to that in bulk materials; however, significantly more atoms and groups are exposed to the surface due to 3-dimensional truncation.24 The history of quantum dots may have begun in the early 1980s when scientists began examining the properties of semiconductor particles that were too small for quantum confinement demonstrations.26 The scientific community has acknowledged QDs as a significant breakthrough since their discovery in the 80s, and subsequent developments have created new opportunities for nanotechnology. A Nobel Prize in Chemistry was given to Moungi G. Bawendi, Louis E. Brus, and Alexei I. Ekimov in 2023 for their innovative research on the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots. The prize honors their substantial contributions to our knowledge of nanomaterials and the creation of novel technologies that take advantage of QDs' special qualities. These tiny nanoscale particles (QDs), whose characteristics are determined by their size, have greatly transformed nanotechnology and have several uses in consumer electronics, biochemistry, and medicine.27 Nevertheless, QDs can reach up to 100 nm depending on the thickness of the coating. QDs' surface chemistry controls their solubility, biological interaction, and stability, whereas their core structure controls their optical and electrical properties. The protective shell, typically made of ZnS, envelops the core, which is made of semiconductor materials, like CdSe, CdTe, InP, etc.10 The emission of light in QDs is strongly dependent on the chemical makeup of the QD core. Depending on the components incorporated in the QDs, various emissions can be customized.5,24

Compared to organic dyes, QDs have many benefits, including a narrow emission spectrum, a broad absorption spectrum, photostability, a wide range of excitation wavelength options, and high fluorescence emission.28 The tunable size, shape, and composition of QDs, which affect the emission wavelength, are other benefits.5,10 Quantum confinement is an essential characteristic of QDs and can be established when the size of QDs is less than that of the exciton Bohr radius. Certain energy levels are created as a result of this constraint. Some electrons in QDs (semiconductors) are stimulated to higher energy levels if a visible light photon strikes them. On returning to their ground state, they release material-specific light. The color of the light that is emitted depends on the size of the QD; larger QDs emit red light with relatively low energy, while smaller QDs emit blue light with high energy. An important feature of QDs is their size-dependent color emission. The “particle in a box” model, which Brus created as the underlying principle for this event in quantum mechanics, states that the energy level rises as the box size decreases. Since the electron and hole are usually restricted, the box's boundary is defined by the surface of the nanocrystal. This concept is consistent with the quantum mechanics governing the size-dependent optical characteristics of QDs.5,29 The bandgap energy of bulk semiconductors usually lies halfway between those of metal conductors and insulators, dividing the conduction band (CB) from the valence band (VB). The VB develops an empty hole (H+) when it is exposed to light because the electrons in the VB and CB are stimulated. Fluorescence is released when the electrons return to the VB. When light is absorbed, electrons and holes in bulk materials can travel independently. However, because of their small size, QDs (small-sized semiconductors) experience energy confinement as a result of electron–hole (e–h) pairs being compressed, creating a quantum confinement regime and increasing energy.5,29,30 Excitons (e–h pairs) are confined inside the grain boundary of nanocrystals, which gives them their distinctive optical properties. These photophysical characteristics include fluorescence intermittency, fluorescence quantum yields (QYs), broad absorption spectra, narrow tunable emission, significant Stokes shifts, photochemical durability, and great resistance to the photobleach effect. The visible, ultraviolet (UV), and near-infrared (NIR) spectral measurements are made easier by the ability to carefully control QDs' fluorescence over a broad wavelength range of 400–4000 nm. Higher-energy excitons and a rise in the bandgap energy result from the degree of confinement increasing as the QD size decreases. With shorter wavelengths, this significant bandgap energy causes emission to be focused in the blue area (that is, longer wavelengths (orange, red, or infrared) are emitted by larger QDs (5–6 nm), while smaller ones (2–3 nm) often emit light in the blue-wavelength region). Electrons move from a stable state to an excited state of higher energy when energy is introduced; the two states are separated by a band gap. The fluorescence emission and absorption properties change towards shorter wavelengths when the band gap energy increases with decreasing QD size and vice versa for bigger QDs. Strong signal-to-background ratios can be obtained thanks to these QD characteristics.5,10

Types of quantum dots

Based on their fundamental makeup, quantum dots can be divided into a number of different groups. II–VI semiconductor QDs (such as CdSe, CdTe, CdS, ZnS, and ZnSe), which are made up of elements from groups II and VI of the periodic table, are the most extensively researched. Elements from groups III and V make up III–V semiconductor QDs (such as InP, GaAs, GaN, and InAs), while elements from groups IV and VI make up IV–VI semiconductor QDs (such as PbS and PbSe). In order to lower surface imperfections and increase quantum yield, these traditional QDs usually have a semiconductor core surrounded by a broad band-gap semiconductor shell (such as ZnS).31–33

However, the main drawback of II–VI, III–V, and IV–VI QDs is their heavy metal concentration (cadmium, lead, arsenic, or selenium), which poses serious biosafety and toxicity issues (Phogat et al., 2023). Other QD kinds have been created to overcome these restrictions, including the following: (i) carbon-based quantum dots: similar photoluminescent characteristics to semiconductor QDs are provided by carbon quantum dots (CQDs) and graphene quantum dots (GQDs), but they are far less toxic and more biocompatible.31 GQDs are tiny pieces of graphene sheets with edge effects and quantum confinement, whereas CQDs are usually amorphous or nanocrystalline carbon particles with sizes smaller than 10 nm. (ii) Non-cadmium metal-based QDs: these include silver-based QDs (e.g., AgInS2 and Ag2Se), copper-based QDs (e.g., CuInS2 and CuInSe2) and zinc-based QDs (e.g., ZnS and ZnO). These materials offer low toxicity while preserving desirable optical properties (Lv et al., 2024).34 (iii) Silicon QDs: silicon-based QDs are widely used in the chip and semiconductor sectors and have good biocompatibility.32

Green nanotechnology in quantum dot synthesis

Physical, chemical, and biological methods can be used to create QDs. Different synthesis techniques will result in QDs with varying biocompatibilities and luminous characteristics.35 These methods are categorized into two main synthesis approaches called top-down and bottom-up approaches, which use various QD synthesis methods to produce QDs (Fig. 2). In some cases, the top-down and bottom-up approaches are integrated or combined to produce intricate colloidal semiconductor heterostructures. By utilizing the benefits of both approaches, seeded assembly techniques offer improved control over the composition, dimensions, and functional characteristics of anisotropic quantum dots and heterostructures.36

Quantum dot synthesis approaches and methods.

In top-down synthesis, bulk materials are reduced to nanoscale quantum dots. Significant energy is needed for bulk materials to be reduced to nanoscale sizes (1–100 nm) through physical and chemical methods, such as milling, X-ray lithography, ion implantation, and molecular beam epitaxy, which are the cornerstones of top-down methods.37 Other methods, such as laser ablation, arc discharge, chemical oxidation, and electrochemical oxidation, can be used during top-down synthesis. Techniques used in top-down approaches can offer precise control over particle size and shape. Also, the top-down method often produces crystalline quantum dots that can be suitable for uses such as near-infrared photoluminescence applications and allows control over particle sizes.38–40 However, due in part to impurities and surface flaws picked up during processing, top-down methods can also produce particles with greater size dispersity and wider emission spectra.41 Bottom-up synthesis, on the other hand, produces quantum dots in colloidal solution where chemical reduction and assembling of smaller building blocks occur molecule by molecule or atom by atom.37 Methods used in bottom-up synthesis include hydrothermal treatments, microwave irradiation, thermal combustion, template-based growth, ultrasonication, and heating. Bottom-up synthesis can enable the formulation of quantum dots with regulated surface functionality, shapes, and sizes through molecular design.41 Bottom-up methods are crucial in creating freestanding hybrid nanostructures that combine plasmonic nanoparticles and quantum dots. This method can result in nanostructures with fine structural and emission tunability.42 The advancement in bottom-up synthesis also highlights the utilization of tiny molecules as “building blocks”, enabling considerable control over the chirality, structure, and photoluminescence properties of QDs, such as carbon-based QDs.43

However, conventional methods (physical and chemical methods) used in bottom-up and top-down approaches for QD synthesis frequently rely on chemically toxic solvents, high temperatures, heavy metal precursors, and intricate post-synthesis surface modifications.37 This results in the translational potential of conventionally synthesized QDs being limited.36 Still, the different methods for synthesizing QDs and their different disadvantages and advantages are presented in Table 1. Among these methods, green-synthesized QDs are often accomplished using low-temperature pyrolysis, microwave-assisted, and hydrothermal methods (Fig. 1). For example, hydrothermal and microwave-assisted techniques enable quick reaction times and provide good control over the size and shape of QDs, which is essential for adjusting their optical characteristics.44,45 The hydrothermal methods are especially preferred because of their ease of use, scalability, and capacity to generate highly fluorescent QDs under controlled circumstances. During biosynthesis, the biological precursors can go through dehydration, polymerization, carbonization, and passivation, leading to the formation of QD cores with functionalized surfaces.46,47 In addition, green synthesis has several benefits, including using waste, natural, or renewable precursors; avoiding hazardous chemicals and unfavorable reaction conditions; using less energy; and having easier post-processing procedures.31,48

Green synthesis methods and reaction mechanisms

Precursor composition, pH, temperature, and reaction time are some of the crucial reaction parameters that control the environmentally friendly synthesis of quantum dots (QDs). Natural precursors, in particular, affect nucleation dynamics and kinetic development, producing QDs with improved biocompatibility and adjustable optical properties. The precursor composition creates the basic chemical environment and supplies the building blocks required for QD creation.54–56 Nucleation and growth kinetics are influenced by the pH of the reaction medium, which also modifies the ionization states of functional groups and the chemical environment. Increasing pH generally encourages particle formation and changes surface states, which in turn modifies optical properties like emission wavelengths and photoluminescence intensity, according to studies on comparable nanoparticle systems.57–59 Since harsh chemicals are often not utilized in green syntheses, making pH adjustments during synthesis could be an essential strategy for property tuning. Furthermore, the temperature controls the crystallinity and reaction kinetics. Mild to moderate temperatures are frequently used in green synthesis methods to reduce energy consumption and environmental impact. An example of a balance between thermal treatment and green chemistry principles is the hydrothermal carbonization of renewable natural precursors during synthesis.55 In a microwave-assisted green synthesis method, the photoluminescence attributes were improved through optimal exposure time to increased heat before the onset of degradation.60 Likewise, green CdSe QDs with good emission properties and a high product yield were produced in the open air without the use of hazardous ligands at relatively moderate temperatures (190–230 °C).61 The degree of QD growth and maturation is influenced by the reaction time. Longer times can allow for complete crystallization and carbonization or crystallization, leading to improved crystallinity, size, and photoluminescence quantum yield. However, excessively long reactions could result in emission quenching or agglomeration in the reaction mixture due to defects or overgrowth.60,62 As a result, during green quantum dot synthesis, the precursor composition establishes the chemical framework, pH regulates growth conditions, temperature regulates reaction kinetics and crystallinity, and the reaction time determines particle maturation. Particle size, crystallinity, emission wavelength, and quantum yield are all important physical and optical characteristics that are determined by the interaction of these factors, making them essential levers in the development of effective, sustainable quantum dots for a variety of applications.54,61,63

Biological or green synthesis techniques using inexpensive, eco-friendly, and biocompatible materials and processes are advantageous for both top-down and bottom-up approaches and have been widely used in the formulation of CQDs and GQDs. Green synthesis techniques have led to the expansion of the biomedical and biotechnological uses of quantum dots in bioimaging, biosensors, diagnostics, and drug delivery by providing benefits, including easier operations, renewable resources, and less harmful impacts.31,64 Likewise, green nanotechnology minimizes environmental effects, reduces toxicity, and improves sustainability without sacrificing functional performance by combining the concepts of green chemistry with the production of nanoscale materials. Green synthesis highlights the use of renewable precursors, low-energy procedures, non-toxic solvents, and ecologically safe reaction conditions.37,65 In the context of quantum dots (QDs), a conceptual foundation for green QD synthesis is provided by the principles of green chemistry, which include waste prevention, atom economy, the use of cleaner solvents, and energy efficiency.65,66 This can lessen the impact on the environment while making QDs more suitable for biological systems. Green methods commonly use biological materials that serve as surface passivators, reducing agents, and carbon sources, all at once, in the production of QDs. Bioactive functional groups on the QD surface are preserved, and energy consumption is decreased because these reactions frequently take place under mild conditions, such as at ambient temperature and pressure. Crucially, green-synthesized QDs naturally have surface functionalities like hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups that improve aqueous dispersibility, biological compatibility, and colloidal stability, essential conditions for use in cancer nanomedicine.65,67,68 This could help to limit the problems associated with the increased utilization of QDs in cancer nanomedicine, such as long-term toxicity, biocompatibility, and environmental sustainability related to traditional physicochemical production methods.

Biological precursors in green quantum dot synthesis

Green synthesis of QDs relies heavily on naturally and biologically derived precursors that serve as carbon sources, stabilizing ligands, and reducing agents. These precursors can be generically classified as biomolecules, microbial systems, plant-based materials, and biomass or its waste products (Fig. 1).37,69,70

Plant-derived precursors

Plant-derived precursors are largely used for the biosynthesis of quantum dots because of their cost-effectiveness, abundance, and rich phytochemicals (such as polyphenols, alkaloids, terpenoids, and flavonoids). These phytochemicals can be obtained from fruits, leaves, peels, seeds, and roots. They are noted to help stabilize nanoparticles, reduce metal ions, and achieve surface passivation, which enable the regulated creation of quantum dots with specific crystallinities, sizes, and fluorescence characteristics.37,71 The green synthesis of QDs, such as cadmium sulfide (CdS) QDs and carbon quantum dots (CQDs), using various plant extracts has been reported in a number of studies. The green synthesis of cadmium sulfide (CdS) QDs using waste-matured tea leaves as biosurfactants was reported. Mature tea leaves that had been rejected were gathered, cleaned of contaminants, cut, and combined with methanol in a 1 : 10 ratio to start the process. Following filtration, a phytochemical-rich, transparent tea leaf extract was produced. After that, this extract was mixed with sodium sulfide (Na2S) and cadmium sulfate (CdSO4), agitated, and left in the dark for a week. Cubic crystalline CdS QDs with a size range of 2.5 to 4 nm were achieved by adjusting the incubation duration. The effective production of the CdS QDs was verified by a color shift in the solution.72 Besides, a similar green synthesis technique was used to create CdS QDs utilizing plant extract from Camellia sinensis as a stabilizing agent. After gathering, washing, and shade-drying the Camellia sinensis leaves (tea leaves), methanol was used to obtain the tea extract. Sodium sulfide was first added to the extract and maintained for three days in the dark, after which a new portion of sodium sulfide was added again. The mixture was allowed to stand for another four days, and bright yellow-colored CdS QDs were confirmed in the reaction mixture. The mixture was centrifuged and lyophilized to purify the particles before they were characterized. This biosynthesis method uses the tea leaf extract (Camellia sinensis) as a non-toxic particle stabilizing agent to produce CdS QDs with particle sizes ranging from 2 to 5 nm.73 Similarly, species [ginger (Zingiber officinale) and green cardamom (Elettaria cardamomum)] were used in another method for CdS QD production. After being cleaned with distilled water, green cardamom pods were air-dried in the shade at room temperature for a day, then finely ground with an electric blender. The fresh ginger was finely grated to a homogeneous texture. The blended cardamom pods and grated ginger were each mixed with a solution (containing methanol and distilled water) in a 1 : 10 (w/v) ratio and kept in the dark at room temperature for 24 hours to enhance the effective extraction of the bioactive compounds. The filtrate was obtained using Whatman filter paper (grade number 41), and 30 mL of each extract was mixed with 2 mL of cadmium sulfate CdSO4 (0.25 M). The mixture was homogenized by shaking vigorously for 30 minutes on a rotary shaker, followed by the addition of 2 mL of sodium sulfide (Na2S) to each reaction mixture. The mixture was shaken for 6 hours and kept for 4 days in the dark for the synthesis of CdS QDs to reach completion. The suspension obtained was centrifuged and washed to eliminate impurities using methanol and distilled water, then dried for 7 hours at 50 °C in an oven. The formed QDs were confirmed with absorption peaks at 447 nm for ginger-extract-mediated QDs (CdS-Gi QDs) and 449 nm for green cardamom-pod-based QDs (CdS-Cm QDs). The band gap energies were validated at 3.06 eV for CdS-Gi QDs and 3.00 eV for CdS-Cm QDs.74 In addition, Opuntia ficus-indica fruit sap was used for the green synthesis of CdS QDs. The harvested fruits were washed with distilled water, the skin peeled, and the sap collected. Fresh extract was prepared using the sap, and the filtrate was obtained using Whatman filter paper (grade number 1). After that, a freshly made solution of cadmium chloride monohydrate [CdCl2H2O (0.5 M)] was added to 100 mL of the sap extract under magnetic stirring. A sodium sulfide monohydrate [Na2SH2O (0.5 M)] solution was then added drop by drop to the reaction mixture with continuous stirring for over 24 hours until the mixture turned orange-yellow, signifying the formation of CdS QDs. The resulting solution was centrifuged and diluted five times with distilled water, and the obtained clean CdS QDs were then dried at 110 °C for 4 hours. The UV-visible absorption peaks confirmed the presence of CdS QDs at 323 nm, which was matched to a 3.8 eV bandgap energy, with sizes ranging from 3 to 5 nm.75

Green-synthesized CQDs were obtained using an extract from Aloe barbadensis Miller (Aloe vera). Fresh, healthy Aloe vera leaves were gathered and repeatedly cleaned in double-distilled water. Fifty milliliters of double-distilled water was combined with 50 mg of crushed leaf fragments and microwaved using a power output of 80 W and a 2.45 GHz operating frequency. A water-cooled condenser was installed on top of the microwave oven to prevent the evaporation of the aloe vera extract. The reaction continued with a continuous “on” period of 1 minute and an “off” period of 30 seconds. The mixture was then left at ambient temperature to cool. To get rid of unwanted impurities, the solution was centrifuged for 20 minutes at 5000 rpm. Dialysis (MW: 12–14 kDa in Milli-Q water) was performed after the CQDs were separated using the silica gel column separation process. The UV-visible spectra confirmed that the biosynthesized CQDs had a maximum peak absorption at 301 nm, while the TEM analysis indicated that the CQDs had an average size of 3.2 nm.76 Similarly, Mesosphaerum suaveolens extract was used to produce CQDs via a microwave-assisted simple reflux method, thus ensuring a cost-effective and sustainable process. The microwave irradiation helps to facilitate the breakdown of organic compounds in the Mesosphaerum suaveolens extract into CQDs. Basically, hand-selected Mesosphaerum suaveolens specimens were cleaned several times in double-distilled water, allowed to air-dry for 15 days, and then ground into a powder using a mixer grinder. After that, 50 mL of double-distilled water and 2.5 g of powdered leaves were combined in a 250 mL beaker and constantly swirled for 1 hour. The mixture was heated for 10 minutes at 200 °C in a microwave oven with its operating parameters set at 2.45 GHz frequency and 200 W output power. A dark brown product was obtained following the irradiation process, then filtered and subjected to centrifugation for 10 minutes at 12 000 rpm to remove impurities. After filtering the supernatant solution through a syringe filter (0.22 µm) to remove any remaining particles, the filtrate was used for additional characterization. The CQDs were confirmed to have a spherical shape with an average size of 2.24 nm. The UV-vis spectra showed a wide absorption peak at 277 nm, which extends into the visible range. Five grams of fresh turmeric leaves was cleaned and mashed in a mortar and pestle with 40 mL of ultrapure water. After that, 20 mL of water was used to extract the leaf paste. The extract was heated at 180 °C for 10 hours in a hydrothermal cell and left to cool at room temperature. A deep brown solution was obtained and then centrifuged for 20 minutes at 8000 rpm. A cellulose syringe filter and a dialysis bag (MW cut-off = 500–1000) were used to filter and dialyze the resulting yellow solution. The finished product was freeze-dried, then dissolved in water. The TEM analysis confirmed the spherically shaped particles with a mean size of 2.6 nm, which, when exposed to UV light at 350 nm, showed strong blue luminescence.78 Also, carbon quantum dots were bioformulated using artichoke leaves by employing the hydrothermal technique. The artichoke leaves were chopped, thoroughly cleaned, rinsed with distilled water, dried in the sun for 2 days, and then milled into a powder. The artichoke leaf powder (0.4 g) was mixed with deionized water (50 mL) and stirred continuously for 4 hours before sonicating for 1 hour. Thirty-five milliliters of the mixture was poured into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, kept in a 240 °C hot air oven for 18 hours, and then allowed to naturally cool to room temperature. A brownish-yellow crude product was collected from the autoclave and filtered through a 0.22 µ syringe filter to get rid of big particles. Finally, a dialysis membrane (1000 MWCO) was used to collect the CQDs against deionized water for 72 hours. TEM analysis confirmed that the obtained quantum dots were semi-spherical in shape with a minimum size of 2.88 nm and a 37.31 V zeta potential value. This confirmed that the green-synthesized CQDs were in an exceptional colloidal state.79

Microorganism-mediated synthesis

Microorganism-mediated synthesis involves the use of fungi, bacteria, yeast, and algae for QD fabrication. This offers a biological, sustainable, economical, and environmentally friendly route for the biosynthesis of QDs.80 Microorganisms' biosynthesis utilizes both intracellular and extracellular metabolic pathways to promote QD nucleation and growth under ambient conditions, in contrast to conventional chemical techniques that frequently require hazardous precursors and high-energy inputs. Cadmium telluride (CdTe), cadmium sulfide (CdS), cadmium selenide (CdSe), and CQDs are among the compounds that bacteria, fungi, algae, and yeast have shown exceptional ability to produce. These microorganisms use a variety of processes, including metabolic transformation, biomineralization, and enzymatic reduction, to change metal ions into stable, fluorescent nanocrystals with regulated sizes and shapes.18,81,82

Pseudomonas putida KT2440 is one of the bacteria used to produce medium-chain-length polyhydroxyalkanoates (MCL-PHAs) and cadmium sulfide (CdS) quantum dots. The bacterial cells were inoculated in 100 mL of M9 culture medium in a 500 mL baffled Erlenmeyer flask and left for 48 to 68 hours, shaking on a rotary shaker, after which 180 or 360 µg mL−1 of cadmium chloride (CdCl2) was added. Afterwards, cysteine (1 mM final concentration) was added to the culture medium at 72 hours to boost the biotransformation of hazardous ions into CdS QDs by producing hydrogen sulfide (H2S). Fifty milliliters of the culture medium producing the QDs was centrifuged at 8000×g for 5 min to concentrate the QDs and washed two times in 50 mM TrisHCl at pH 7.4. Glass beads were used for cell lysis throughout three homogenization cycles. To completely break the cells, the suspension was subsequently subjected to ten cycles of sonication (15 seconds on and 30 seconds off). The resultant solution with the QDs was centrifuged for 10 minutes at 14 000 rpm, and the supernatant was put into a Sephadex column G75 for gel filtration. The CdS QDs' effective production was confirmed by their fluorescence when exposed to UV light. Interestingly, MCL-PHAs were noted to build up in the cytoplasm, while the CdS QDs were found in the periplasmic region, suggesting clear biochemical compartmentalization.83 In another study, Escherichia coli (E. coli) was used in the biosynthesis of CdS QDs. Luria-Bertani (LB) was used to culture E. coli. The E. coli at 4 hours of cultivation (early logarithmic phase), at 6 hours of cultivation (mid-logarithmic phase), at 8 hours of cultivation (late logarithmic phase), and at 10 hours of cultivation (stationary phase) were all collected and adjusted to the same absorbance based on the OD600 value. Cadmium chloride (CdCl2) was then introduced into the collected LB medium containing E. coli at concentrations ranging from 0.5 × 103 mol L−1 to 2.5 × 103 mol L−1. Following that, cultures were incubated for one to several days at 37 °C in the dark on a rotating shaker. Cells with CdS QDs with various fluorescent hues were then obtained. Cells with CdS QDs of various fluorescence colors were then obtained. The obtained mixtures were centrifuged, subjected to three rounds of washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove any unwanted chemical residue, and stored in PBS (pH 7.4) at 4 °C. The presence of CdS QDs was confirmed in the collected samples, validated by the appearance of a high fluorescence emission peak for Cd-treated E. coli at 470 nm, while the control sample with E. coli only showed no fluorescence. Also, the TEM image showed monodisperse small QDs, which were spherical in shape, with a size of about 10 nm. The presence of CdS QDs was demonstrated by the high fluorescence emission peak of Cd-treated E. coli at 470 nm, while the control E. coli showed no fluorescence. This implies that E. coli matrices successfully facilitated the bio-manufacture of CdS QDs.84

Furthermore, bacterial cells (E. coli strain NCTC 13216) can be used to synthesize CdTe QDs. Culture medium in a 25 mL Erlenmeyer flask was inoculated with the E. coli and allowed to continuously shake for 24 hours at 37 °C at 600 rpm on the shaker. One milliliter of E. coli culture (OD600 = 0.6) was diluted with 45 mL of LB medium. Following continuous stirring, 4 mL of cadmium chloride (CdCl2 0.04 mol L−1), 100 mg of trisodium citrate dihydrate, 1 mL of sodium tellurite (Na2TeO3 0.04 mol L−1), 50 mg of sodium borohydride (NaBH4), and 60 mg of mercaptosuccinic acid (MSA) were added, and the mixture was incubated for 24 hours at 130 rpm on a rotary shaker. The fluorescence micrographs confirmed the presence of CdTe QDs in E. coli cells exposed to Cd and Te, while no fluorescence was detected for the control and Cd-only groups. Also, a distinct fluorescence emission spectrum peak was noted at 480 nm, indicating the synthesis of CdTe QDs. However, no such peak was observed for the control groups.82 Similarly, the fungus Fusarium oxysporum was used in the extracellular bioformulation of cadmium telluride (CdTe) QDs. The fungus was cultivated in 100 mL of MGYP [malt extract, glucose, yeast extract, and peptone] medium at 25–27 °C in a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask that was maintained for 4 days in a shaker at 200 rpm. The culture medium (broth) was then centrifuged for 20 minutes at 5000 rpm at 10 °C to remove the fungal mycelium. Twenty grams of the wet mycelia was added to an aqueous solution (100 mL) containing 1 mM TeCl4 and CdCl and maintained for 4 days at 25–27 °C while shaking at 200 rpm. This process demonstrated an eco-friendly synthesis strategy by producing fluorescent CdTe QDs via fungal metabolism. The UV-Vis spectrum validated the formation of CdTe QDs having a broad absorption peak (between 400 and 450 nm). The particles showed a prominent emission peak centered at 475 nm (green luminescence) when excited at 400 nm, with a fluorescence energy peak centered at 2.61 eV that matches that of the bandgap.85

Moreover, a study by Mirseyed et al. reported the biosynthesis of phosphorus-doped CQDs (P-CQDs) using Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast). A 15% (w/v) suspension of the yeast was hydrolyzed at pH 7.0 using a mixture of Alcalase and Flavouzyme enzymes (0.5% v/v) at 55 °C for 48 hours under shaking. The enzymatic activity was then halted by boiling at 100 °C for 5 minutes, followed by centrifugation for 15 minutes at 5000×g to isolate the insoluble yeast cell wall fraction. This biomass serves as the carbon precursor for CQD synthesis via a hydrothermal process, where a 3% (w/v) suspension of the cell wall material in 0.5 M phosphoric acid was stirred for 2 hours and subsequently heated in a hydrothermal reactor at 250 °C for 5 hours. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged (7000 g for 30 min) to collect the supernatant containing the synthesized CQDs. To optimize the fluorescence properties, the CQD solution was adjusted to pH 4.0, diluted up to 1 : 100, and stored at 4 °C to enhance stability and emission intensity. This green and efficient method yields phosphorus-doped CQDs (P-CQDs) with strong blue fluorescence and multifunctional biological activities. The CQDs were spherical or quasi-spherical and had sizes ranging from about 3 to 7 nm. The UV-Vis spectrum had a maximum peak at 210 nm. The fluorescence spectrum showed an optimal excitation peak at 360 nm and an emission peak at 450 nm (blue fluorescence).86 Furthermore, Cheng et al. utilized a cost-effective method to formulate CQs using microalgae Euglena gracilis (E. gracilis) through a one-step hydrothermal green method. In this process, 4.8 g of dry E. gracilis powder was added to 60 mL of deionized water and continuously stirred for 10 minutes. The mixture was poured into a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and maintained at 220 °C for 2.5 hours. The solution was allowed to cool at room temperature and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane. The filtrate was freeze-dried, affording a brown powder of Euglena-derived CQDs (E-CQDs). Optimization studies determined that using an 8% E. gracilis powder concentration, a reaction temperature of 220 °C, and a reaction time of 2.5 hours yielded E-CQDs with the highest fluorescence intensity. The E-CQDs exhibited a spherical shape with a size range of 6.5 to 10.5 nm and a mean size of 9.2 nm. The UV-Vis spectrum showed a strong peak at 252 nm. The maximum fluorescence emission peak was validated at 416.8 nm for the 330 nm excitation.81

Biomolecule-assisted synthesis

A biomolecule, often known as a biological molecule or naturally occurring molecule, is any organic chemical made by living things that may be essential for life functions, like cell division, energy, and structure. The main categories of biomolecules include amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids. Due to their inherent molecular ability, biocompatibility, and structural variety, biomolecules have become reducing agents in the environmentally friendly production of QDs. Significantly, scalable, biocompatible, and highly luminous CQDs and GQDs can be created using biomolecules in ways that are simpler, more economical, and environmentally friendly compared with the conventional techniques utilizing artificial carbon-based sources.18,69 For instance, biomolecules such as amino acids, which are the fundamental building blocks of proteins, are abundant, affordable, biocompatible, and ecologically benign. The presence of a variety of functional groups, especially amino and carboxyl groups, makes them suitable chemical precursors for the bioformulation of QDs with anticipated biomolecule-based properties.51–53 Moreover, the assembly of superior biomolecule-derived QDs could be made more programmable by the inclusion of highly effective combinations of different functional groups and amino acids.18,87,88 Studies by Hesheng Victor 2018 89 document the utilization of 20 amino acids in the synthesis of amino acid-derived biodots (AA dots). The AA dots were produced through a green hydrothermal method, wherein individual natural α-amino acids were dissolved in deionized water at a concentration of 0.15 M and heated in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 180 °C for 12 hours. Afterwards, the solutions were centrifuged (for 30 minutes at 12 000 rpm), filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane, and dialyzed to obtain the final photoluminescent AA dots. Among the synthesized AA dots, those derived from serine (Ser) and threonine (Thr) exhibited exceptional photostability, retaining over 90% of their fluorescence intensity after continuous UV irradiation for 3 hours, and achieved high quantum yields of 30.44% and 23.07%, respectively. The AA dots' size and shape varied according to the amino acid precursor, as evidenced by characterization studies. For example, Asp-dot and Asn-dot formed monodispersed disc-like nanostructures with diameters of about 2.62 nm and 4.56 nm, respectively, while Ser-dot and Thr-dot also showed nanodisc morphologies with larger diameters of about 19.04 nm and 27.66 nm, respectively. AA dots from polar amino acids showed UV-vis absorption peaks at 240 and 270 nm, which were attributed to π–π* and n–π* transitions, respectively, while those from nonpolar precursors had a blue-shifted peak at 195 nm. The fluorescence emission spectra revealed that most AA dots emitted bright blue fluorescence with a maximum emission around 450 nm when excited at 360 nm, demonstrating their potential as tunable, biocompatible probes for cellular imaging applications.89 In another study, the synthesis of l-glutamic acid-derived GQDs employed a one-step, straightforward pyrolysis method. l-glutamic acid was heated to 210 °C using a heating mantle, where it melted and, within 45 seconds, turned from a colorless liquid to a brown one, indicating carbonization and GQD formation. The product was then homogenized in 10 mL of water, centrifuged, and the GQDs collected as a stable aqueous solution without the need for additional surface passivation agents, as the residual l-glutamic acid acted as a natural passivator. The resulting GQDs exhibited a high fluorescence quantum yield of 54.5% and demonstrated superior photostability compared to traditional dye fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). The GQDs were reported to be nearly spherical with an average size of 4.66 ± 1.24 nm and a lattice spacing of 0.246 nm, consistent with graphitic carbon. UV-Vis spectra showed two distinct peaks at 238 nm and 335 nm, attributed to the π–π* transitions of CC bonds and sp2 clusters, respectively. The fluorescence emission spectrum was excitation-dependent, with emission peaks shifting from 415 nm (violet) to 580 nm (red) as the excitation wavelength increased from 320 nm to 540 nm. It also featured a near-infrared emission around 815 nm upon 360 nm excitation.90

Furthermore, the biosynthesis of QDs can be done using biomolecules like proteins. Proteins possess different functional groups at specific points along the main chain of peptide bonds and can readily unfold into peptide polymers based on the surrounding circumstances and reaction. Also, proteins are a great source of ligands because they possess a variety of functional groups, such as carboxylate, thiols, and amine moieties. As a result, they are employed as templates (or stabilizing agents) for the formulation of different metal-based conventional QDs, and they also help in improving the surface of hydrophobic QDs that have already been synthesized. This helps to boost the biomedical significance of the QDs and improve their suitability for biological usage. Proteins and their denatured forms have been utilized in the production of biomolecule-derived QDs as carbon sources since the turn of the 21st century.44,69,91 The study by Chakraborty et al. confirmed the synthesis of carbon quantum dots termed “blood dots” (BD) using hemoglobin as a protein source. Hemoglobin is composed of four subunits of protein and a heme group, which has Fe2+ integrated into a porphyrin ring. The BD was formulated by dissolving hemoglobin powder (27 mg) in 2 mL of Milli-Q water and heating the mixture to 120 °C in a muffle furnace for 1 hour. Thereafter, the obtained sticky residue was rinsed in three rounds to get rid of unreacted hemoglobin using water. The obtained water-insoluble mixture was sonicated for 5 minutes in a solution containing NaOH. After that, the solution was centrifuged for 10 minutes at 12 000 rpm in order to extract the insoluble portion. The BD with a yield of around 75% was obtained by collecting and drying the supernatant. The BD had an average size of 4.0 nm, demonstrated exceptional photostability, and showed no photobleaching. The BD exhibited maximum emission shifting from 380 nm to 410 nm when the excitation wavelength increased from 230 nm to 340 nm, which is in line with the intense blue fluorescence observed upon exposure to UV light.92 Similarly, a study by Ghataty et al. utilized bovine serum albumin (BSA) for the biosynthesis of carbon quantum dots (BCQDs). BSA (3.6 g) was dissolved in deionized water (80 mL), sealed in a Teflon-lined autoclave, and heated for 6 hours at 200 °C. A yellowish-brown mixture was obtained, then purified by centrifugation, filtration, and dialysis to obtain a pale-yellow, fluorescent BCQD solution. The BCQDs exhibited excellent photostability, retaining about 90.5% ± 1.2% of their fluorescence intensity after 20 hours of continuous UV irradiation (365 nm). The BCQDs were monodispersed and spherical in shape, with an average diameter of 4.5 ± 0.3 nm. A distinct absorption peak at 280 nm was noted in the UV-Vis spectra, ascribed to the π–π* transition of CC bonds. The fluorescence emission spectrum displayed a strong, symmetric peak centered at 425 ± 1.2 nm when excited at an optimal wavelength of 320 ± 1 nm, indicating bright blue fluorescence under UV light. The BCQDs demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, low cytotoxicity, and synergistic activity. They also function as fluorescent nanocarriers in drug delivery. These attributes make them promising candidates for advanced therapeutic and diagnostic biomedical applications.93 The aforementioned experimental evidence shows that proteins and amino acids can be used in the green production of fluorescent quantum dots for biomedical applications, such as in oncology.

Still, in the past ten years, it has been shown that nucleic acids, especially DNA, can serve as the initial carbon source materials for the production of QDs derived from biomolecules, primarily C-QDs.69 Nucleic acids are naturally occurring biopolymers of nucleotides, made up of pentose sugar, phosphate groups, and nitrogenous nucleobases (purine and pyrimidine). Some of the nucleobases and the pentose sugar can be divided into two main categories based on structural differences: DNA, which has thymine and deoxyribose (without one hydroxyl group), and RNA, which has ribose (containing hydroxyl groups) and the base uracil.69,94 The chemical moieties of these nucleic acids make them essential for the stabilization and functionalization of various QDs. Moreover, DNA offers several advantages for bio-dots, including (i) the ability to create DNA-QDs with the appropriate particle size due to its natural dimensions matching those of regular QDs. (ii) DNA molecules have a diameter of only 2 nm, which offers favorable circumstances for creating unique nanostructures. (iii) It is possible to artificially create nucleic acids with varied sequences and functionalities, thus enabling the formulation of DNA-QDs with a variety of uses. (iv) Nucleic acids can be employed directly as biological functional QDs in biological samples for recognition and targeting. (v) Degradation products from nucleic acids are not harmful to the environment.95,96 Also, it will be crucial to prepare DNA-dots/nucleic acid-dots using molecules like DNA as the raw material, as this type of method could result in the synthesis of low-toxicity and high-efficiency bio-dots. For instance, studies by Ma et al. established that the aqueous synthesis of semiconductor quantum dots, like cadmium sulfide (CdS), can employ polycytosine DNA as a templating agent or ligand. The polycytosine oligonucleotides (polycytosine DNA) were reported to bind successfully to the surface of CdS QDs during their synthesis. This helped stabilize the CdS QDs (approximately 4 nm in size), resulting in water-soluble CdS QDs with good luminescence qualities within the visible range (500 to 700 nm). As a biological ligand, DNA minimized cytotoxicity, promoted colloidal stability, and made the produced QDs biocompatible. The nucleic acids were involved in coordinating the surface cadmium ions during the synthesis in aqueous buffer solutions, capping the developing CdS QDs nanocrystals and enabling excellent ionic strength stability. The resultant DNA-passivated CdS QDs were noted to be beneficial for bioimaging and biological applications because they exhibited little cell cytotoxicity and were taken up by HeLa cells.97

Another important biomolecule employed as a biological precursor in the synthesis of QDs is low-molecular-weight carbohydrates called sugars (including mono- and di-saccharides). The polymeric and oligomeric units of monosaccharides result in the development of polysaccharides and oligosaccharides, the two other groups of carbohydrates. The polysaccharides and sugars are an important class of biomolecules that can be used as starting materials for the creation of highly luminescent, biocompatible, and inexpensive QDs (including GQDs and CQDs) due to their availability, different functionalities, abundance, high water solubility, heterogeneity, low carbonization temperatures, lack of toxicity, and low cost. Therefore, using sugars (glucose, maltose, fructose, sucrose, glucosamine, lactose, and mannose and their derivatives) and polysaccharides (chitosan, starch, cellulose, glycogen, hyaluronic acid, dextran, and chitin) as carbon material for CQDs and GQDs has become a preferred “green” method by researchers.69,98,99 The study by Chen et al. confirms the synthesis of GQDs by a hydrothermal and eco-friendly process that utilized only water and cellulose, without strong organic solvents and acids. The synthesis of the GQDs is attributed to the fact that cellulose is hydrolyzed to glucose, leading to ring-closure condensation, hence producing GQDs. The GQDs obtained showed outstanding photostability, with a quantum yield of 19.4% when stimulated at 360 nm. The GQDs had a uniform size distribution of 1.0–5.0 nm. In addition to a peak at 282 nm, the UV-vis spectroscopy also showed absorption peaks at 199 nm and 236 nm that were linked to π–π* transitions. Because of their strong biocompatibility, good hydrophilicity, low cytotoxicity, and sustainable synthesis from renewable cellulose, the QDs obtained through a similar method could be a noteworthy and prospective candidate for applications in bioimaging, biosensing, and other biomedical domains.54 Similarly, nitrogen-doped CQDs (85% product yield) were prepared using chitosan through a green hydrothermal method. The nitrogen-doped CQDs in aqueous solution showed a bright blue fluorescence emission, and a 6.6% quantum yield (QY) was recorded. The nitrogen-doped CQDs were of sizes ranging from 2 to 10 nm, had excellent water dispersibility, precipitated in ethanol, and were used for the detection of Fe3+ ions. The study thus proposes a simple method to produce a high yield of nitrogen-doped CQDs from chitosan.100

Biomass- and its waste-assisted synthesis of QDs

Biomass and biomass waste play a crucial role in the green synthesis of quantum dots (QDs), particularly CQDs and GQDs, because biomass waste is cheap, plentiful, and renewable. Also, biomass-derived quantum dots have substantial economic and environmental advantages. By turning inexpensive biomass into useful nanomaterials, this reuse of waste resources not only solves waste management problems but also adds value.12,101,102 Various sources, including perennial grass, agricultural, organic household waste, forestry, fishing, animal husbandry, poultry, and associated industries' wastes, can provide biomass.69,103 Intrinsic carbon-rich substances found in biomass, such as lignin, cellulose, carbohydrates, and proteins, are great sources of carbon for CQD synthesis, promoting environmentally friendly and sustainable methods.103 Besides, biomass-based quantum dots have better chemical stability, biocompatibility, and photoluminescence characteristics.56,102,104 Studies by Zhang et al. confirmed the sustainable synthesis of bright-green-fluorescent nitrogen-doped CQDs using renewable biomass (alkali lignin) through a two-step hydrothermal method. The obtained nitrogen-doped CQDs presented excellent photoluminescence (bright green emission), low toxicity, good biocompatibility, and stability, making them suitable for intracellular ion detection and cell imaging. The work shows that using biomass sustainably to produce high-quality nanoproducts is feasible.105 The study by Reagen et al. green-synthesized GQDs using the biomass-derived precursor, cis-3,4-di(furan-2-yl) cyclobutene-1,2-dicarboxylic acid (CBDA-2). The process involved dissolving CBDA-2 in deionized water, adjusting the pH to 10.0, autoclaving (200 °C for 12 hours), followed by dialysis to obtain the GQDs. These GQDs were then integrated with hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (hMSNs) via sonication to form the composite GQDs-hMSNs. The GQDs-hMSNs maintained fluorescence stability across a pH range of 5.0 to 9.0, with quenching occurring under extreme acidic conditions. The GQDs act as photosensitizers capable of generating singlet oxygen (1O2) under laser irradiation, effectively inducing cytotoxicity in cancer cells. Combined with the drug-delivery capability of hMSNs, this platform (GQDs-hMSNs) enables dual-mode cancer treatment through simultaneous PDT and chemotherapy, highlighting its potential as a biodegradable, multifunctional theranostic agent.106

Characterization of green-synthesized quantum dots

Characterization analyses are required following the green-synthesis of QDs using various biological precursors. The characterization analysis helps to confirm the effective synthesis, structural integrity, optical characteristics, and stability of the green-synthesized QDs. The characterization techniques commonly involve a combination of different analytical techniques and are discussed as follows. The morphology, size distribution, and crystallinity of the QDs are usually observed using atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). AFM provides topographical height profiles, while TEM is critical for evaluating the shape and size, as well as for revealing the lattice spacing in the analysed QDs. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) provides a surface morphology image and reveals the elemental composition of the QDs, respectively, as well as the distribution within the analysed QD sample.107–109 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) are used to determine the functional groups of active compounds involved in the synthesis of QDs (e.g., hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino) and to establish the chemical bonding states. These are essential in understanding the QDs' biocompatibility and hydrophilicity. The QDs' crystalline structure and interlayer spacing are evaluated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). The optical attributes of the CQDs are assessed using fluorescence spectrophotometry to determine emission spectra, excitation-dependent behavior, and quantum yield, while ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry is used to assess the QDs' absorption spectra. The colloidal stability and surface charge of QDs are evaluated by zeta potential analysis, which provides information on the dispersion of QDs under physiological conditions and the resistance to aggregation.107–110

Toxicity of green-synthesized quantum dots

It is essential to distinguish between the toxicity profiles of different QD systems when evaluating their biomedical suitability (Table 2). Conventional cadmium-based QDs (CdSe, CdTe, CdS) are among the most toxic due to the release of Cd2+ ions, which can induce mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis even at low doses.34 Exposure to air and UV light increases the toxicity of CdSe QDs by accelerating the release of free Cd2+ ions and promoting surface oxidation. Lead-based QDs (PbS, PbSe) raise similar issues because of the systemic and neurotoxic effects of Pb2+ ions. In contrast, indium is far less dangerous than cadmium. InP-based QDs provide a less toxic substitute for CdSe; nonetheless, careful surface passivation is still necessary to avoid core deterioration. Compared to cadmium and lead, zinc-based QDs (ZnS, ZnO) and silver/copper-based QDs (AgInS2, CuInS2) offer less toxicity while retaining beneficial optical characteristics.34,37,111 The most advantageous biocompatibility characteristics are often shown by carbon-based QDs (CQDs and GQDs), with multiple studies demonstrating little cytotoxicity even at high concentrations.12,18 Additional environmental and biological safety problems arise from the use of hazardous organic solvents and harsh conditions in conventional organometallic synthesis techniques.57,65 Typically, the intrinsic physicochemical factors of QDs, as well as environmental factors, play a major role in determining the QD toxicity. These factors include surface charge, size, surface coating/bioactivity (capping material and functional groups), concentration, and stability (mechanical, photolytic, and oxidative stability). For example, smaller QDs may more easily enter cellular and subcellular spaces, which could result in unforeseen interactions with organelles like nuclei and mitochondria.

QDs at elevated concentrations or under extended exposure might cause oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions.37,111 However, approaches to green-synthesized QDs aim to mitigate toxicity risk. It is crucial to understand that being “green” may not mean being totally non-toxic. Surface changes, synthesis by-products, and residual contaminants from biological extracts can still affect biological reactions. Still, the toxicity risks of green-synthesized QDs are lessened through (i) using biocompatible capping agents, i.e., natural biomolecules (polyphenols, lipids, proteins, amino acids, and tripeptides such as glutathione) that help to prevent toxic core leaching and also stabilize green-synthesized QDs.37,111,120 (ii) Aqueous synthesis: getting rid of organic solvents and decreasing the toxic impact on the environment. (iii) Making use of less toxic core materials: transitioning from cadmium to compounds or materials like zinc-based compounds, carbon, and selenide (AISe) or silver indium selenide.18,34,111 However, it is crucial to recognize that the label “green” does not automatically imply total biosafety or clinical preparedness.121 Although green-synthesized QDs often show less cytotoxicity than their conventionally synthesized counterparts, their safety profiles are still context-dependent and necessitate careful, case-by-case analysis. Because aspects including biodistribution, clearance kinetics, protein corona formation, and chronic exposure effects are rarely adequately defined in current investigations, the biocompatibility seen in vitro does not necessarily translate directly to in vivo consequences. In addition, issues like batch-to-batch variability, limited scalability, and the presence of undefined bioactive compounds from biological extracts that may complicate therapeutic interpretations must be weighed against the translational benefits of green synthesis, such as decreased toxicity, environmental sustainability, and simplified production.37,122,123 Therefore, while green-synthesized QDs represent a promising advancement toward safer cancer nanomedicines, claims of safety and translational readiness should be appropriately moderated and supported by comprehensive long-term biosafety studies in relevant preclinical models.

Toxicity profiles of different green-synthesized quantum dots

Carbon-based quantum dots

Carbon-based QDs, including carbon QDs (CQDs) and graphene-based QDs (GQDs), consistently exhibit acceptable biocompatibility profiles, making them the most thoroughly researched class of green-synthesized QDs. A study using a hydrothermal green chemistry technique to manufacture CQDs from Caccinia macranthera revealed that the CQDs on CT26 cancer cells exhibited a concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect, with an IC50 value of about 1000 µg mL−1, showing comparatively moderate toxicity.124 Similarly, a microwave-assisted green method was used to produce graphene oxide QDs (GOQDs) using black carbon. The GOQDs showed outstanding biocompatibility, demonstrating no discernible abnormalities when tested on zebrafish embryo models in vivo, at up to 400 µg mL−1, while the in vitro test on CT26 cells revealed an IC50 of 200 µg mL−1.125 Furthermore, a comparison of traditional QDs versus green carbon highlights that these QDs have different safety profiles. While the traditional QDs (heavy metal-based QDs) show significant biocompatibility problems and high toxicity, the green CQDs are eco-friendly, biodegradable, and show low toxicity.17

Cadmium-based green quantum dots

Cadmium-based QDs are inherently toxic because of their heavy metal core. However, obtaining them through green synthesis techniques can substantially lessen their intrinsic cytotoxicity by passivating their surfaces with biocompatible coatings. Significant differences between chemically produced and green-obtained cadmium sulfide (CdS) QDs were established in a study by Calvo-Olvera et al.120 When compared to their chemically produced equivalents, the CdS QDs biogenically obtained using the fungus Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici demonstrated a 1.61-fold increase in cell viability on HT-29 cells and a 1.88-fold decrease in cytotoxicity at the IC50 dose. The organic coating from fungal secretions containing compounds that include proteins, lipids, amino acids, and nitrate groups was found to be responsible for the decreased toxicity. These compounds had coating interactions with CdS through –SH and –OH groups, thereby passivating the CdS toxic core.120 This shows that the CdS intrinsic toxicity may not be eliminated completely. The toxicity of cadmium-based QDs has been extensively studied, with a substantial body of literature establishing the mechanisms and magnitude of their cytotoxic effects.19,23 While green synthesis can mitigate some of this toxicity through biocompatible surface coatings, the inherent risk of Cd2+-ion release remains a fundamental limitation of cadmium-based QDs for biomedical applications.

Non-cadmium metal-based green quantum dots

Emerging research studies on green-synthesised non-cadmium quantum dots hold out hope for real biocompatibility, safety, and minimal toxicity. The production of zinc sulfide (ZnS) quantum dots using quercetin (flavonoid) is a prominent example. Many fruits and vegetables include quercetin, a naturally occurring flavonoid that acts as a biocompatible coating to stabilize the ZnS core, producing dots that are mostly safe for normal cells but selectively harmful to cancer cells.126 Similarly, ZnS QDs were synthesized using green tea extract. The ZnS QDs showed a high safety index (6.61) and were effective against Leishmania, thus indicating that green-synthesized ZnS QDs are safer substitutes to traditional antileishmanial medications for patients in clinical settings.117 Silver indium sulfide/zinc (AIS/Zn) dots with a quantum yield of 18% have been successfully produced by researchers in an “all-aqueous-phase” procedure employing biomineralization, a green method that eliminates organic solvents. These dots offer a non-toxic nanoplatform appropriate for targeted bioimaging in delicate cellular systems.127 Another safer substitute for cadmium-containing QDs is silver indium selenide (AISe) dots, which are produced by green aqueous electrosynthesis and stabilized with glutathione. The larvae of Drosophila melanogaster, when exposed to AISe@ZnS QDs at doses up to 300 µg mL−1, followed by toxicity assessment, revealed no signs of toxicity that affected morphology or survival, no oxidative stress generation (as determined by protein content and lipid peroxidation), and no genotoxic effects (primary DNA damage was not detected by the Comet assay).111 These findings confirm that well-designed green-synthesised QDs with non-toxic core materials can preserve functional qualities appropriate for biomedical applications while maintaining good biocompatibility. This subsection (Section 3.4.1.3) provides a detailed coverage of non-cadmium metal-based QDs to highlight emerging alternatives to traditional heavy metal QDs, and readers should note that carbon-based QDs remain the most extensively studied and best-characterized class of green-synthesized QDs.

Green-synthesized quantum dot cellular uptake and tumor targeting

In cancer cells, green-synthesized quantum dots (QDs), especially carbon-based and GQDs, show unique cellular absorption mechanisms that are primarily controlled by their surface chemistry, size, and charge. Usually, energy-dependent endocytic mechanisms, such as macropinocytosis, caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, allow these quantum dots to penetrate cancer cells. For example, clathrin-mediated pathways across cancer and other cell types contribute laterally to the internalization of GQDs produced using green techniques, which are mostly mediated by caveolae-mediated endocytosis.128,129 Also, a study by Sawalha and colleagues confirms that the sage leaf-derived CQDs infiltrated the Hep3B cells by an energy-dependent endocytosis (distinctively caveolae- and lipid raft-mediated pathways) and also by passive diffusion.130 Nanoparticle endocytosis might occur in cells through two main mechanisms, namely pinocytosis and phagocytosis. In phagocytosis, large particles are taken up by only certain specialized mammalian cells, such as neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages. Conversely, pinocytosis occurs in all cell types and is used for the uptake of fluid, solutes, and tiny particles. The four subcategories of pinocytosis are clathrin/caveolae-independent endocytosis, caveolae-mediated endocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and micropinocytosis. For the uptake of smaller particles, micropinocytosis is preferable because it creates endocytic vesicles of varying sizes, including clathrin (∼120 nm), clathrin/caveolae-independent (∼90 nm), and caveolae (∼60 nm). On the contrary, large solute macromolecules larger than 1 µm can be efficiently taken up by cells through macropinocytosis, which involves ruffling the cell surface.131

Moreover, when properly functionalized, CQDs made from plant biomass or extracts exhibit efficient uptake in cancer cells, primarily via receptor-mediated endocytosis mechanisms, such as folate receptor-mediated pathways.132 Green-synthesized QDs' intracellular destiny and efficiency in cancer cells are influenced by a number of factors: the interaction with endocytic machinery depends on the particle's size, which promotes cellular uptake.133 Through the promotion of receptor-mediated internalization, surface functionalization with targeted ligands like dopamine or folic acid can greatly increase absorption, boosting the cytotoxic efficiency and specificity of cancer cells.134,135 Furthermore, a balanced or slightly positive charge promotes cellular adhesion and endocytosis, whereas the surface charge regulates contact with the negatively charged cell membrane.133 These quantum dots primarily concentrate in the cytoplasm and membrane compartments after internalization, allowing for fluorescent labeling and therapeutic actions.136 Importantly, targeted cancer imaging and treatment are among the biological results of these uptake mechanisms. For instance, dopamine-functionalized CQDs from mango leaves demonstrate multifunctionality by promoting anti-cancer effects and neural development, in addition to exhibiting increased absorption by cancer cells. Importantly, the biological outcomes of green quantum uptake processes include targeted cancer imaging and therapy. For example, dopamine-functionalized CQDs from mango leaves not only exhibit enhanced cancer cell uptake but also promote anti-cancer effects and neuronal differentiation, highlighting multifunctionality.135 The therapeutic potential inherent in their cellular absorption methods was also demonstrated by carbon dots made from bamboo leaves that used folate receptor-mediated endocytosis to greatly improve drug delivery and cytotoxicity in HeLa cancer cells.132

Green-synthesized quantum dots in passive and active tumor targeting

To provide oxygen and nourishment, tumors constantly and indiscriminately create new blood vessels, but these vessels are frequently loose, leaky, and haphazard. Tumor blood capillaries have bigger capillary pores of at least 200 nm due to their aberrant structure, which makes it easier for macromolecules and nanoparticles like QDs to enter tumor tissue.137 Furthermore, tumors have poor lymphatic outflow, which keeps these molecules stuck inside the tumor longer and improves medication retention. Interestingly, the nanocarrier's size has a significant impact on the EPR effect.138 Small nanoparticles can pass into the leaky blood vessels of tumors. Larger particles are frequently absorbed by the mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS), which can lessen the efficacy of drug delivery, whereas particles smaller than 10 nm can be rapidly eliminated through renal clearance. This suggests a size-dependent EPR effect. This size-dependent EPR effect emphasizes how crucial it is to maximize the accumulation of QDs in tumors by maximizing their size.16,137 By exploring the EPR effect, QDs could passively collect at tumor sites. This method makes use of the distinct physiological characteristics of tumor tissue rather than relying on particular tumor markers or receptors. The advantage is that drugs can be passively and non-invasively delivered to tumors without the requirement for further functionalization.16,137,139 Nonetheless, when assessing the translational potential of the EPR effect, it is important to recognize its major limits. Tumor kind, stage, vascular permeability, interstitial fluid pressure, and lymphatic drainage status all significantly affect how successful EPR-mediated targeting is.140,141 Because of tumor heterogeneity, many cancers show inadequate or irregular EPR-mediated accumulation, especially in hypovascular areas or tumors with extensive fibrosis.140,141 Furthermore, compared to the subcutaneous xenograft models frequently employed in preclinical research, the EPR effect is frequently less noticeable in human tumors, which contributes to the frequent failure of nanomedicines to duplicate their encouraging preclinical efficacy in clinical trials.142 The mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), which can drastically lower the percentage of administered QDs that reach the tumor site, and the high interstitial fluid pressure inside tumors, which prevents deep penetration of nanoparticles beyond the perivascular space, are additional obstacles. DQDs may build up in organs like the liver and spleen that have fenestrated vasculature, raising the possibility of off-target damage. This is due to the lack of molecular specificity in passive targeting through the EPR effect and damage. Despite these drawbacks, passive EPR-mediated accumulation is still a crucial mechanism for the retention of nanoparticles in cancers, and it is often used in conjunction with active targeting techniques to improve treatment efficacy and specificity.140,141 However, CQDs green-synthesized from walnut oil demonstrated a significant ability to target and enter cancer cells, though this was achieved through passive accumulation and inherent cellular uptake. The study showed that the CQDs were readily internalized in PC3 and MCF-7 cancer cells, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy, which revealed bright intracellular fluorescence increasing in a dose-dependent manner. Once inside the cells, the CQDs exerted a potent cytotoxic effect by inducing apoptosis.133 This suggests that while the bioformulated CQDs successfully targeted cancerous tissues at the cellular level, leading to cell death, the targeting was passive and likely driven by the EPR effect and nonspecific endocytosis, as the quantum dots were not functionalized with targeting ligands.

Furthermore, biosynthesized sulphur quantum dots (SQDs) using Arabic gum exhibited efficient passive cellular uptake, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry, which showed that 97.3% of Y79 cells internalized the SQDs within 4 hours. The SDDs showed minimal cytotoxicity, and the internalization resulted in bright green fluorescence within the cytoplasm, highlighting their utility for cellular labeling and diagnostic imaging. The SQDs' accumulation in tumor cells relies on passive diffusion and the EPR effect, since they were not functionalized with any specific targeting ligands.143 In another study, bioformulated GQDs were synthesized using cow milk as a carbon source as a passive targeting drug delivery and bioimaging system. The GQDs exhibited excellent biocompatibility and were internalized by both normal (L929) and cancerous (HeLa and MDA-MB-231) cells through non-specific endocytosis, as confirmed by confocal and fluorescence microscopy. The passive cellular uptake was attributed to the ultra-small size of GQDs (5 nm) and favorable surface chemistry, which could facilitate passive diffusion and endocytic entry. For therapeutic application, the anticancer drug berberine hydrochloride (BHC) was conjugated to the GQDs using cysteamine hydrochloride (Cys) as a linker, forming the GQDs@Cys-BHC complex with a high drug loading efficiency (about 88%). This complex exhibited pH-responsive passive targeting, releasing BHC preferentially in acidic environments (pH 5.8), mimicking the tumor microenvironment and intracellular lysosomes, while showing minimal release at physiological pH (7.4). Pharmacokinetic modeling revealed that drug release followed Higuchi and first-order models, characteristic of diffusion-controlled, sustained-release systems. Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that the GQDs@Cys-BHC complex was significantly more potent against cancer cells than free BHC. Thus, the GQDs@Cys-BHC complex relies entirely on passive mechanisms for cellular uptake and drug delivery.144

However, in order to increase specificity, active targeting strategies are commonly coupled with passive targeting.145 The active strategies aim to improve drug delivery efficiency and specificity, which can be achieved with quantum dots by functionalizing them with ligands or antibodies that target overexpressed receptors or antigens on the surface of cancer cells. By using this strategy, the nanocarrier may immediately identify and attach to cancer cells, increasing treatment accuracy and reducing unintended side effects.137,146 In a study, cadmium-based (CdS) semiconductor QDs were synthesized using chitosan (natural biopolymer) in a green chemistry approach for active targeting of cancer cells. The active targeting strategy was achieved by functionalizing the obtained chitosan-capped CdS QDs with the RGD tripeptide (arginine-glycine-aspartic acid), which served as a high-affinity ligand for αvβ3 integrin receptors—transmembrane proteins that are selectively overexpressed on the surface of angiogenic endothelial cells and malignant tumor cells. The RGD peptide was covalently conjugated to chitosan via amide bonds using EDC/sulfo-NHS chemistry, and this RGD-chitosan bioconjugate was subsequently used as a capping ligand during the one-step aqueous synthesis of CdS quantum dots, resulting in CdS-RGD-CHI nanoconjugates. The targeting specificity was rigorously evaluated in vitro using two model cell lines: HEK 293T cells (non-cancerous human embryonic kidney cells with medium integrin expression) and SAOS cells (human sarcoma osteogenic cells, a bone tumor model with high αvβ3 integrin expression). Fluorescence microscopy imaging revealed that CdS-RGD-CHI nanoconjugates exhibited significantly higher binding and internalization in SAOS cells compared to HEK 293T cells, with fluorescence intensity greater than that exhibited by the non-targeted control (CdS-CHI without RGD). In contrast, minimal non-specific binding was observed in both cell lines when incubated with unconjugated CdS-CHI quantum dots, confirming that the targeting was mediated by specific RGD-integrin recognition rather than passive uptake. Cytocompatibility assays [MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] demonstrated that the nanoconjugates were non-toxic to both cell lines at the concentrations tested. Thus, this system exemplifies a fully active targeting nanoplatform, where the RGD moiety provides molecular specificity for cancer biomarkers, while the chitosan shell on the CdS ensures biocompatibility and colloidal stability.147

Anticancer efficacy and mechanism of green-synthesized quantum dots

The anticancer efficacy of green-synthesized quantum dots represents a convergence of sustainable nanotechnology and innovative cancer therapy. As detailed in this section, quantum dots derived from diverse natural sources have demonstrated potent and selective cytotoxicity against various cancer cell lines, such as breast cancer (MDA-MB-231 cells), hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2 cells), and B16F10 melanoma cells. The mechanisms underlying this efficacy are multifaceted and interconnected, involving (1) reactive oxygen species generation that overwhelms antioxidant defenses, (2) targeted mitochondrial dysfunction leading to metabolic collapse and release of pro-apoptotic factors, (3) DNA damage and activation of p53-mediated checkpoint responses, and (4) induction of apoptosis through Bax/Bcl-2 imbalance and caspase activation.148,149

A study by Salve and Bhinge, 2025 150 reports that Blumea eriantha-derived CQDs (BEDC-CQDs) exhibited a multi-faceted anticancer mechanism in HepG2 human liver cancer cells. BEDC-CQDs induced cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells with an IC50 value of 40.86 µg mL−1. In addition, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly elevated by 6.33 times when compared to untreated controls, suggesting that oxidative stress is the main cause of cell death. Western blot analysis showed that treated HepG2 cells had a dramatic 6.282-fold rise in p53 expression and a 3.836-fold increase in their negative regulator, MDM2, indicating that the significant ROS generation causes DNA damage and subsequently activates the p53 tumor suppressor pathway. The flow cytometry analysis verified the activation of apoptosis, revealing 54.70% overall cell death (including necrosis and late apoptosis).150 In another study, Dutta et al. bioformulated polyphenolic CQDs [dual-emissive green and yellow CQDs (G-CQDs and Y-CQDs)] using phloroglucinol, a nontoxic precursor. G-CQDs and Y-CQDs exhibited specific cytotoxicity against B16F10 melanoma cells, with strong cell death at 100 µg mL−1. The cytotoxicity was directly linked with the intrinsic potential of the CQDs to amplify oxidative stress. Particularly, the G-CQDs were found to produce abundant hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) and singlet oxygen (1O2), as confirmed by electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy and terephthalic acid assays. This led to a time-dependent increase in intracellular ROS levels detected by DCFDA staining. The remarkable mitochondria-targeting capability of the G-CQDs was confirmed through their colocalization with MitoTracker Red in the mitochondria. The biological TEM analysis showed severe mitochondrial damage, including reduced mitochondrial branching, decreased form factor, and disrupted cristae. This mitochondrial damage causes a dramatic drop in ATP synthesis, a considerable decrease in oxygen consumption rate (OCR), and a 78% reduction in both mitochondrial and cellular glutathione (GSH). A time-dependent rise in TUNEL-positive cells was noted, which was connected to the overexpression of p53 and verified by protein array analysis, and it was indicative of the significant nuclear DNA damage caused by the oxidative stress. Also, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio became dramatically unbalanced after the G-CQD treatment. The qRT-PCR revealed a 6-fold rise in pro-apoptotic Bax and a corresponding reduction in anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 mRNA expression. Alongside this, executioner caspases were activated, as evidenced by increased protein expression of SMAC, cytochrome c, and cleaved caspase-3, as well as 10-fold and 10.5-fold increases in the mRNA levels of caspase-3 (10-fold) and caspase-9 (10.5-fold). Collectively, this study thus demonstrated that polyphenolic G-CQDs serve as potent therapeutic agents in targeting the mitochondria, amplifying ROS, promoting DNA damage, and inducing caspase-mediated apoptosis through p53 activation and Bax/Bcl-2 dysregulation, leading to effective tumor suppression.149 Likewise, Dar et al. produced GQDs using an eco-friendly electrochemical method that exploited defect-rich graphene sheets, vitamin C (as a redox-buffered exfoliant), and sodium hydroxide (to control the ionic environment). The GQDs exhibited selective cytotoxicity against triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) via intracellular ROS generation. The cytotoxicity began following the internalization of GQDs in TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231 cells), leading to increased ROS concentration (such as superoxide anions (O2−˙), hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)). The ROS was linked to dose-dependent cytotoxicity, where GQDs at 50 µg mL−1 led to a decrease in viability of about 42% in MDA-MB-231 cells, while the L929 fibroblasts (normal cells) stayed largely unaffected (>80% viability). The mechanism linked to the selective cytotoxicity encompassed mitochondria targeting, where the GQDs (oxygen-rich GQDs) accumulated within the mitochondria. This led to the amplification of oxidative stress and the disruption of the electron transport chain. Also, the ROS production led to mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) disruption, hence activating mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). This promoted cytochrome c's release into the cytoplasm. Damage to the mitochondria could enhance DNA damage and p53 activation, as the ROS-mediated stress could trigger the p53 tumor suppressor pathway. Moreover, the GQD treatment led to the activation of caspase-9 and caspase-3, which are responsible for apoptotic cell death. The activation of these caspases, which is controlled by the Bax/Bcl-2 rheostat, resulted in chromatin condensation, fragmentation, and programmed cell death, as evidenced by the reduction in the viability of cancerous cells and the morphological alterations. Also, the in vivo fluorescent imaging in 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice verified the GQDs' renal clearance and preferential tumor accumulation.148

Green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer photodynamic therapy (PDT)

PDT is an appealing therapeutic technique that requires activating the photosensitizer (PS) at the tumor site by irradiating it at a specific wavelength. This leads to photochemical and photophysical reactions, resulting in cytotoxic ROS generation and ultimately eliminating the tumor cells (Fig. 3). Basically, the absorption of photons whose energy corresponds to the electronic transition states of the PS molecule initiates PS activation. This absorption causes the PS to transition from its ground singlet state (S0) to an excited singlet state (S1), which has a short lifespan (nanoseconds) and paired electron spins. The PS can either undergo non-radiative decay by internal conversion (IC) or radiative relaxation back to S0, generating fluorescence. Alternatively, the excited PS could proceed via intersystem crossing (ISC), a spin-forbidden but quantum mechanically allowed process that produces the excited triplet state (T1) and spin inversion. The T2 state has enough time to interact with nearby substrates because of its much longer duration (microseconds to milliseconds). The T1 state can take part in type-I (electron or hydrogen transfer) and/or type-II (energy transfer to oxygen, producing singlet oxygen, 1O2) photochemical processes if molecular oxygen (3O2) or other biomolecular targets are available. These reactions result in ROS generation, including superoxide anion, singlet oxygen, and hydroxyl radicals, which can cause oxidative damage to cellular constituents.151,152

Schematic of the principle of photodynamic treatment. The photosensitizer (PS) is stimulated from its ground state (S0) to a higher energy orbital (S1 or S2) excited state by light absorption. The PS either excites to the triplet state (T1) or reverts to the initial S0. ROS are produced when the PS at T1 combines with oxygen in a type I reaction, also known as the Type I energy transfer. When the PS at T1 directly combines with oxygen in its ground state (3O2) by transferring energy to produce singlet oxygen (1O2), a type II reaction (Type II energy transfer) may also take place. The produced ROS damages and kills malignant cells.

PDT offers many benefits over conventional anti-cancer treatments. For instance, (i) it can selectively induce tumor necrosis without causing damage to the surrounding healthy tissue. (ii) PSs used in PDT can be utilized in conjunction with other therapies because they are not particularly hazardous to the human body and do not interact with other medications. (iii) Normal tissues are often not severely harmed; PDT is less intrusive and leaves fewer scars. It works well for superficial lesions and lesions that optical fibers can reach. (iv) The drug (PS) used in PDT exhibits no cumulative effect, and it can be applied repeatedly. As early as the 1990s, PDT was employed in clinical settings without the need for surgery, employing photoproteins and derivatives of hematoporphyrin as photosensitizers. However, the practical use of classical PSs is significantly limited because of drawbacks such as complex synthesis methods, a brief blood circulation period, and poor solubility in water. The development of nanotechnology has made it possible to produce new PDT materials. These materials can be used to get around the drawbacks of the first and second generations of PSs and create a third generation of PSs that is safer and more effective.35

Ideally, good PSs have a number of important traits. (i) They maintain their stability in a composition. (ii) They can be easily synthesized. (iii) When exposed to no light, they exhibit non-toxicity. (iv) They can successfully localize to particular cells, such as cancerous cells, and have target specificity. (v) Their energy-transfer efficacy is sufficient for singlet-oxygen generation, and they possess a high triplet-state yield (Φt), with a triplet-state energy (ΔEt) that is above 94 kJ mol−1 = 0.97 eV (the energy of singlet oxygen). (vi) The body can effectively metabolize PSs to limit any adverse effects. (vii) Self-aggregation of PSs decreases Φt. Thus, avoiding or reducing PS aggregation in the body is very necessary. (viii) To guarantee long-term stability, PSs have to show insensitivity to degradation processes (e.g. photobleaching). QDs have the first five important traits of good PSs, making their application as PSs quite promising.35,153

Green-synthesized QDs as PDT photosensitizers and their advantages

QDs, including green-synthesized QDs, exhibit the first five important traits of good PSs, as mentioned above. This makes their application as PSs quite promising. Despite that, QDs also offer various important advantages over traditional PDT PSs, which can help to overcome key limitations linked to tissue penetration, stability, and functionality. The advantages are: (i) tunable emission: QDs' ability to be precisely tuned from the ultraviolet (UV) to the infrared (IR) part of the spectrum can be achieved by simply altering their composition and size during synthesis. Besides, the engineered QDs can specifically emit light at a particular wavelength. The emission characteristics are caused by quantum confinement processes in which QDs can be modified to emit in the near-IR region. In contrast, the majority of conventional PSs emit light in the visible region.7,65 (ii) Deep tissue penetration: compared to the visible light employed by most conventional PSs, light in the near-infrared spectrum penetrates more deeply into biological tissues because of less scattering of near-infrared light. Thus, low-intensity light can reach deep-seated cancers by penetrating tissue to a depth of several centimeters. This makes it possible for QD-based PDT to target and treat deep-seated cancers that traditional PDT treatment would not be able to reach.7,154 (iii) Two-photon absorption: various QDs can be stimulated by two low-energy NIR photons at the same time because they have a high two-photon absorption cross-section. This makes it possible to trigger the therapy using deeply penetrating NIR light.154,155 (iv) Exceptional photostability: QDs have exceptional photostability in contrast to traditional PS, which can be degraded by exposure to light (photobleaching). They are extremely resistant to enzymatic degradation and photobleaching, hence assuring a more dependable and long-lasting therapeutic impact.7,18,156 (v) Improved solubility: the efficiency of most conventional PS is hampered by their tendency to aggregate in the body's aqueous environment since they are hydrophobic. In contrast, QDs, especially those that are carbon-based, could be designed to be hydrophilic. This helps to enhance their transport and dispersion in biological systems.7,157 (vi) Active targeting: the surface of QDs can easily be modified or functionalized with ligands like antibodies or peptides. These various surface functionalizations enable QDs to actively bind to cancerous cells. This helps to increase selectivity and limit off-target effects. Conversely, selectivity of tumor cells is often difficult to achieve with most traditional PSs, as they are usually used alone in therapy.16,156 (vii) Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based activation: conventional PSs can be conjugated with QDs (through close steric proximity between the structures) to produce a hybrid system. The QDs function as an effective energy donor in the hybrid system in a procedure called FRET. The QD transmits its absorbed light to the attached PS, stimulating the PS to then generate singlet oxygen. This results in an amplified effect where the poor light absorption of some PSs is overcome and boosts the system's therapeutic output and photoluminescence effect.156,158,159 In addition to the aforementioned advantages of QDs as PDT PSs, green-synthesized QDs offer additional key advantages over traditional PDT PSs (Table 3). These advantages are critically helpful in addressing the limitations associated with biocompatibility and environmental impacts, as well as traditional PS drawbacks in PDT. These advantages could be fulfilled while maintaining the inherent superior optical attributes of QDs.

In vitro and in vivo evidence of green-synthesized QDs in cancer PDT

Green-synthesized QDs represent a promising development in tumor-selective PDT, employing eco-friendly production techniques to produce biocompatible, non-toxic nanoparticles that improve the accuracy of cancer treatment. They are used in PDT through active and passive mechanisms. When exposed to radiation, QDs produce localized ROS after preferential tumor accumulation through the increased permeability and retention (EPR) effect, reducing off-target damage.16,163,164 Green-synthesized QDs can also be surface-functionalized with tumor-targeting ligands and conjugated with PDT PSs to enhance cancer cell-specific targeting and improve intracellular ROS-mediated damage.165,166 Different in vitro and in vivo studies have explored the efficacy of green-synthesized QDs in cancer PDT.

In a study, a natural material (riboflavin (vitamin B2) was used as a carbon source to prepare CQDs (rCQDs) to ensure safety and biocompatibility. When exposed to light, these rCQDs efficiently produced singlet oxygen, which is the basic mechanism of PDT. The rCQDs showed a markedly increased capability for singlet-oxygen production while retaining the photosensitive characteristics of riboflavin. The singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) test revealed that extended irradiation intervals (from 1 to 5 minutes) gradually raised the fluorescence intensity of SOSG, which was directly connected with the 1O2 concentration. The rCQDs generated more than twice as much singlet oxygen as free riboflavin under the same irradiation conditions, and after five minutes, the production of 1O2 was 3.63 times higher. The rCQDs' computed 1O2 quantum yield was 0.716, which was much higher than that for riboflavin (0.51). The improved photosensitizing ability of these riboflavin-derived CQDs was attributed to their superior water solubility, which promoted greater production of singlet oxygen, and their surface chemistry, which makes it easier for molecular oxygen to efficiently transfer energy. In the in vitro anticancer analysis, the rCQDs exhibited exceptional biocompatibility. The cell viability analysis showed that the treated cells (CCK-8) remained viable above 95% even at doses as high as 400 µg mL−1, indicating minimal dark toxicity. The confocal microscopic results confirmed that rCQDs exhibited brilliant green fluorescence and readily infiltrated 4T1 breast cancer cells by endocytosis. The rCQDs caused the killing of cancer cells upon light activation (365 nm, 60 mW cm−2). This demonstrates that the cytotoxic effect was specifically mediated by the rCQDs in PDT. Similarly, the in vivo antitumor efficacy following PDT in the murine 4T1 breast tumor model resulted in a significant tumor growth inhibition. In contrast, the control groups (PBS, rCQDs alone, or light alone) exhibited rapid tumor progression. Histopathological analysis using H&E and TUNEL staining following PDT revealed the dissociation of cell nuclei and the formation of apoptotic bodies. Importantly, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) of the treated mice showed no appreciable changes in body weight or histological abnormalities, demonstrating the RCQDs' systemic biosafety.163 Likewise, aloe vera was used as a natural precursor for the production of CQDs. The obtained CQDs were combined with chromium-doped alumina (Al2O3: Cr) to produce a heterostructured nanocomposite (Al2O3: Cr@CQD NPs). The Al2O3: Cr@CQD NPs showed superior production of hydroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen when exposed to UVA light (320–400 nm). The nanocomposite had a synergistic photodynamic cytotoxic impact when paired with UVA irradiation, as demonstrated by in vitro experiments on C26 colon cancer cells that revealed an IC50 of 20 µg mL−1 without light activation, which dropped to 10 µg mL−1.160

Moreover, a simple and green approach was used for the production of pristine GQDs. The obtained GQDs were thiourea (GQDs-TU) and urea (GQDs-U). The GQDs-TU completely lost the capacity to generate singlet oxygen (1O2) following photoexcitation. On the other hand, the GQDs-U extensively improved the ability of GQDs to produce 1O2 under the same conditions. The nitrogen doping from urea enhanced the photosensitizing properties of the quantum dots. The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy analysis showed that upon light irradiation, the GQDs-U generated 50% more singlet oxygen (1O2) than the pristine GQDs. The singlet oxygen produced was similar to that for gamma-irradiated GQDs, which have been established to be quite successful in the past. Over the course of a 15 minute test period, GQDs-U actually demonstrated a distinctive, steadily rising production of singlet oxygen, indicating a highly effective and long-lasting photodynamic effect. The cytotoxicity study indicated that the GQDs-U treatment was non-toxic to HeLa (cervical carcinoma) cells in the dark at concentrations below 100 µg mL−1. However, low concentrations of GQDs-U (below 100 µg mL−1) in combination with light irradiation treatment induced cytotoxicity in HeLa cells. This indicates that GQDs-U is a promising candidate for photodynamic therapy applications.167 Also, manganese-doped carbon quantum dots (Mn-CQDs) were produced through a green synthesis method that uses waste green tea (a natural and sustainable carbon source). The Mn-CQDs then served as a targeted drug delivery system. The Mn-CQDs were functionalized with folic acid (FA) and chlorin e6 (Ce6), forming a multifunctional platform (Mn-CQDs@FA/Ce6 nanohybrid) for targeted PDT. The FA (ligand) can specifically bind to folate receptors, which are often strongly expressed on the surface of many cancer cells. This facilitates the targeted uptake of Mn-CQDs@FA/Ce6 by cancerous cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis. This was confirmed through the confocal microscopic image, which showed that the HeLa cells (which overexpress folate receptors) emit a stronger red fluorescence signal from Ce6 than B16F10 and HepG2 cells (which have lower folate receptor expression). The Mn-CQDs@FA/Ce6 treatment at 500 ppm in combination with laser irradiation treatment (671 nm, 1 W cm−2) for 5 minutes resulted in over 90% of HeLa cancerous cells being killed. This was the highest cytotoxic effect observed among the three cell lines examined, hence highlighting the advantages of active targeting. The improved therapeutic effect was associated with the successful uptake of active Ce6 by HeLa, leading to mitochondrial membrane depolarization, DNA damage, and apoptosis, as evidenced by the correlation between the ROS levels and cytotoxicity. These results highlight the green-synthesized Mn-CQDs as a biocompatible carrier in PDT, and the cell death is particularly caused by the light-activated Ce6 PDT process.165

Furthermore, tea polyphenol-derived carbon dots (T-CQDs) exhibited significant efficacy as a type I PS for PDT. The T-CQDs efficiently generate hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) under mild LED irradiation (400–500 nm, 15 mW cm−2, and for 12 minutes), as confirmed by electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy. In contrast, the ability to generate singlet oxygen (1O2) by the T-CQDs was minimal. In vitro experiments using 4T1 mouse breast cancer cells revealed that T-CQDs exhibit extremely low cytotoxicity in the dark (cell viability >85% at 300 µg mL−1). Conversely, the T-CQD in combination with LED irradiation induces a potent photodynamic effect, reducing cell viability to about 50% at the same concentration. This efficacy was validated in vivo using a 4T1 subcutaneous tumor model in mice, where intratumoral injection of T-CQDs, followed by LED irradiation treatment, significantly inhibited solid tumor growth over 14 days, compared to the results for the control groups. Notably, the treatment showed no adverse effects on major organs or mouse body weight, and histopathological analysis confirmed tumor-specific necrosis without damage to surrounding healthy tissues. The T-CQDs also demonstrated excellent biocompatibility. The MTT assays on normal (HUVEC) and cancer (HepG2) cells showed no significant cytotoxicity in the dark, with cell viability reaching over 85% at high concentrations. Hemolysis tests also confirmed no considerable hematological toxicity.168

In addition, CQDs formulated using curcumin and folic acid (CQDCF) acted as two-photon active PS in PDT to enhance the treatment of oral cancer. When exposed to two-photon near-infrared (NIR) radiation at 780 nm, the CQDCF strongly generates ROS, as revealed by the DCFH fluorescence assay, with a time-dependent rise in ROS production. A major advantage of CQDCF over non-targeting CQDs is that its folic acid (ligand) component allows for active nucleus-targeting capabilities. Confocal microscopy confirms CQDCF localization within the nucleus of H413 oral cancer cells. Folate receptor-mediated endocytosis helps the CQDCF to penetrate the nucleus, allowing for direct DNA damage upon photostimulation. Under the same irradiation experimental conditions, flow cytometry analysis showed that CQDCF produced significantly more intracellular ROS (62.1% of cells) than curcumin-derived CQDs (CQDC) (23.1%). The comet assays showed much higher DNA damage (32% for CDCF versus 11% for CDc). Higher DNA damage is translated as an enhanced photodynamic effect, resulting in increased cancer cell mortality. Electron spin resonance spectroscopy confirmed that singlet oxygen (1O2) was produced through energy transfer, following irradiation. The CQDCF exhibits remarkable photostability over 125 minutes of continuous irradiation and outstanding two-photon imaging capacity, allowing for simultaneous imaging and therapeutic activities within a single nanoprobe platform.166 In a similar study, carboxylated graphene quantum dots (cGQDs) were conjugated with curcumin to produce a nanocomposite (cGQDs-PEG-curcumin) that functioned as a highly efficient PS in PDT. Upon irradiation (414 nm, 5 J cm−2), it decreased cell viability, induced DNA double-strand breaks, disrupted nucleolar integrity in HeLa cancer cells, and caused cell death. Surprisingly, this impact occurred without the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), demonstrating a direct transfer of energy to biomolecules, leading to increased apoptosis, membrane damage, and DNA damage. This suggests a novel ROS-independent PDT mechanism.169

Green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer photothermal therapy (PTT)

The use of photothermal agents (PTAs) to transform absorbed light energy into heat, resulting in localized hyperthermia and tumor cell killing, has made photothermal therapy (PTT) a viable non-invasive cancer treatment approach (Fig. 4).170,171 Photothermal nanomaterials produce heat when exposed to light, especially in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum. This heat raises tumor tissues' temperature to cytotoxic levels, usually between 42 °C and 45 °C.170,171 Likewise, cancer cell death induced by PTT typically occurs in the temperature range of 42 °C to 60 °C. Protein denaturation starts at about 42 °C, which temporarily inhibits the activity of cancer cells. Temperatures that are above 45 °C result in fast necrosis, whereas oxidative stress and irreversible cellular damage happen between 43 °C and 45 °C. Temperatures above 48 °C can cause ischemia and microvascular thrombosis, which can cause instantaneous cell death.110 Moreover, high temperatures following PTT can interrupt the homeostasis of the cell through various mechanisms, including alterations in membrane permeability, membrane rupture, cellular swelling, protein denaturation, mitochondrial dysfunction and collapse, and cytoskeletal damage. In the end, these mechanisms set off necrotic or apoptotic cell death pathways.110,172,173 Photothermal heating can promote tumor vascular permeability and oxygenation, which will improve other therapeutic agents' delivery and efficacy. Likewise, heat stress may boost antitumor immune responses and might support systemic anticancer immunity by stimulating the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and tumor-associated antigens.172–174

Schematic of the principle of photothermal treatment. The photothermal agents in PTT are activated by NIR light and undergo a non-radiative vibrational relaxation, producing heat that induces damage to cancerous cells, resulting in cell death.

PTT offers a solution since cancerous cells usually exhibit poor heat tolerance. Compared to traditional cancer treatments, PTT presents benefits, including minimal invasiveness, reduced systemic toxicity, and spatial precision. External laser irradiation in PTT can use doses that may be adjusted to reduce damage to nearby non-cancerous tissue while selectively eliminating different types of cancer.170,171 Like PDT PSs, photothermal agents in PTT can be activated from the S0 to an excited S1 state. However, the photothermal agents will undergo a non-radiative vibrational relaxation to return to the ground state after being excited from the S0 to S1 state, producing heat that induces tumor damage and cell death (Fig. 4). The therapeutic application of PTT seems less advanced compared to PDT, yet different photothermal agents, including indocyanine green, gold nanoparticles, and carbon nanomaterials, have been studied. The nature of the photothermal agent thus determines its capacity to generate heat.10,173 Most conventional photothermal agents (metallic nanoparticles and organic dyes) have demonstrated efficacy in PTT; however, they are frequently hampered by their high synthesis cost, potential toxicity, poor water solubility, and limited biodegradability.110 In contrast to inorganic photothermal agents, which can produce high photothermal yield but are less degradable and may be stored for extended periods of time, organic photothermal agents often achieve lower photothermal conversions but are biocompatible and biodegradable. Different studies have focused on QDs as PAs, especially GQDs and CQDs, which have intriguing attributes for PTT.10,173

Biological or green-synthesized QDs are becoming effective photothermal agents for PTT. This is attributed to their unique optical properties, tunable absorption, photostability, biocompatibility, and versatility. Green-synthesized QDs can efficiently absorb NIR light. Since water and other endogenous biomolecules exhibit minimal absorption in the NIR region, this light penetrates more deeply into cancer tissues. The light energy or photon absorbed by QDs is then transformed into heat (photothermal conversion), causing cancer cells to become hyperthermic and eventually die, with little harm to nearby healthy tissues. Heat-induced cellular stress and structural damage are the sources of QD-mediated PTT's therapeutic benefits. Also, QDs' small sizes make it possible for them to effectively enter tissues with enhanced cellular absorption, making them perfect for targeted cancer treatment. Green-synthesized QDs can be designed to have a higher photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) by adjusting their surface chemistry, size, structural composition, and elemental doping. Green-synthesized QDs that are well-passivated and have a high sp2 carbon concentration often show better photothermal stability and efficient tumor ablation with few off-target effects. These designed attributes of green-synthesized QDs make them favorable agents for cancer photothermal therapy.18

In vitro and in vivo evidence of green-synthesized quantum dots in PTT

A study by Young et al. used bulk tungsten disulfide (WS2) to produce ultrasmall (∼3 nm) QDs (WS2 QDs) through a “green” process that involved physical grinding and ultrasonication without the use of hazardous chemical solvents. When exposed to laser irradiation at 800 nm, the WS2 QDs mediated cancer PTT by generating substantial hyperthermia. An elevated photothermal conversion (44.3%) was achieved, which was attributed to the WS2 QDs' strong NIR absorption and their tiny size. The WS2 QDs also served as radiosensitizers because of their high-Z tungsten atoms that caused DNA damage through Compton and photoelectric effects. This resulted in DNA double-strand breaks (γ-H2AX foci increased 2.1-fold) and increased apoptosis when the treatment was done in combination with radiotherapy. The clonogenic assays following in vitro cell treatment demonstrated that X-ray and NIR irradiation significantly decreased the 4T1 cell survival to 6%, as opposed to the individual treatments with 31% for RT and 39% for PTT. This shows a strong synergistic effect. In vivo investigation on BEL-7402 tumor-bearing mice revealed that the PTT/RT treatment in combination with WS2 QDs caused the complete killing of the tumor without recurrence. On the contrary, the monotherapies simply cause a delay in the growth of the tumor. Due to their ultrasmall size and green synthesis, WS2 QDs also allowed for dual-modal CT/photoacoustic imaging for accurate tumor localization, and toxicity tests verified acceptable biocompatibility and renal clearance.109

Alcea leaf extract was used as the carbon source in a one-step, environmentally friendly hydrothermal method for the green synthesis of copper-doped carbon dots (Cu-dCQDs), which produced monodisperse and spherical QDs below 10 nm in size, with a photothermal conversion efficiency of 39.3% when exposed to an 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser. The PTT efficacy relied on the Cu-dCQDs' stable and strong absorbance at 800 nm, which permitted them to produce localized hyperthermia when exposed to NIR, resulting in the death of cancerous cells, with little inherent cytotoxicity and minimum invasiveness. In vitro experiments on 4T1 cells showed that the cellular internalization of Cu-dCQDs, followed by NIR laser treatment, significantly reduced cell viability to 15% at 80 ppm, while flow cytometry revealed an elevation in apoptosis to 65.6% and reduced necrosis (12.6%). This was achieved without any targeting ligands because of the small nanoparticle size. Additionally, the improved cytotoxicity and apoptotic induction caused by the green-synthesized Cu-dCQDs were validated by the 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining, which revealed apoptotic hallmarks, such as nuclear fragmentation and extremely condensed chromatin, suggestive of DNA damage.175

Kim et al. used a cost-effective and straightforward hydrothermal process to create bioinspired sulfur-doped CQDs (S-CQDs) with significant NIR absorbance from the flowers of Camellia japonica. These green-synthesized QDs leverage the benefits of CQDs, such as their better photostability, minimal cytotoxicity, and great biocompatibility, while also using the sulfur doping to achieve substantial near-infrared (NIR) absorbance in order to overcome the common problem of insufficient photothermal conversion in PTT. Under 808 nm laser power (1.1 W cm−2), the S-CQDs (at a low dose of 45 µg mL−1) showed a high photothermal conversion efficiency of 55.4%, which is essential for safe and efficient therapy. This led to a reduction in the viability of the cancer cells.176

A study used natural biomass from Hypocrella bambusae (HB) to produce phototheranostic CQDs (HBCQDs). When exposed to a 635 nm laser, these HBCQDs produce hyperthermia and have a 27.6% photothermal conversion efficiency, making them dual-modal agents for cancer photothermal therapy (PTT). Mechanistically, the HBCQDs simultaneously create singlet oxygen species (1O2 quantum yield of 0.38) for photodynamic treatment (PDT) and heat for PTT under higher-power laser irradiation (0.8 W cm−2). This synergistic treatment effect overcomes the constraints of hypoxia in cancerous cells. In vitro investigation using HeLa cells under hypoxic conditions revealed that PDT alone (0.1 W cm−2 laser) led to about 30% cell viability, whereas the combination of PDT/PTT treatments (0.8 W cm−2) resulted in nearly 99% cell death, as verified by the Calcein AM/PI co-staining and MTT assays, indicating increased apoptosis. The effectiveness of HBCQDs was further confirmed by in vivo investigations on 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. A synergistic effect was established in the PDT/PTT group, resulting in complete tumor ablation and no regrowth. This was contrary to the partial efficiency of PDT alone. The H&E staining also showed widespread damage to the tumor tissue following PDT/PTT treatment.107 The natural leaves of Ayurvedic medicinal plants, including Azadirachta indica, Ocimum tenuiflorum, and Tridax procumbens, serve as renewable and biocompatible precursors for CQD production. These CQDs, which range in size from around 6 to 12 nm, mediate cancer PTT by inducing hyperthermia when exposed to 750 nm near-infrared (NIR) light. After 6 minutes, they achieve a therapeutic temperature of 43 °C, and after 10 minutes, they reach 46 °C, which causes cancer cell death. An effective photothermal ablation was confirmed by in vitro investigations on HeLa cancer cells, which showed that although the CQDs alone were biocompatible (over 85% viability on normal NIH3T3 cells), NIR light activation dramatically decreased cancer cell viability to about 45–59%. The fluorescence microscopy analysis showed that the green-fluorescent CQDs were localized in the HeLa cells and were subjected to NIR light activation, resulting in light-to-heat conversion that probably caused increased apoptosis and cellular damage. Additionally, the same platform demonstrated antibacterial phototherapy, killing roughly 80% of E. coli bacteria when exposed to NIR light, demonstrating the versatile therapeutic potential of plant leaf-derived CQDs.177

Moreover, a study by Liu et al. used the green hydrothermal method to produce CQDs using the fruit extract of Osman fragrans (carbon source) and ethylenediamine (nitrogen dopant). By using a nonradiative relaxation mechanism to transform near-infrared (NIR) light into heat, the CQDs facilitated PTT and achieved an elevated photothermal conversion efficiency of 46.7% at 808 nm, with outstanding photothermal stability. The CQDs were biocompatible, exhibiting negligible cytotoxicity when used alone on HeLa cells and L929 fibroblast cells up to 1.2 mg mL−1. Yet, the CQDs in combination with laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W cm−2, 10 min) induced substantial cancer cell death. A higher apoptosis ratio of 67.5% in HeLa cells after laser-light-activated therapy was validated by flow cytometry analysis and linked to the photothermal effect. Calcein-AM/PI co-staining showed bright red fluorescence in the treated group (CQDs + NIR group), signifying extensive cell death.178 A green, one-pot microwave method was used to create CQD nanocomposites utilizing soybean milk as the carbon source. In order to create a cocktail, methylene blue (MB) was added to the soybean milk and then microwaved. The obtained CD nanocomposites (MB-CQDs) function as PTT agents by generating hyperthermia upon 808 nm laser irradiation, resulting in a temperature of 19.2 °C and 25.3 °C in solution and simulated tissue, respectively. The induced temperature increase is sufficient for thermal ablation in cancerous cells. MB embedded in CQDs has a synergistic impact that improves photothermal conversion when compared to either free MB or CQDs alone. The MB-CQDs were absorbed by breast cancer cells (MDA-MB231) through passive membrane penetration, according to in vitro experiments. This allows for dual-channel fluorescence imaging at 640 nm and 405 nm for accurate cell tracking. Also, the in vivo photoacoustic imaging further validated the ability of MB-CQDs in tracing ocular blood vessels. This highlights the MB-CQDs' dual role as photothermal agents and imaging tracers for possible cancer therapeutic applications.179 Furthermore, biomass-derived CQDs were synthesized by egg yolk carbonization on a direct gas flame, which underscores the eco-friendliness, low cost, and convenience of the method. Polydopamine (PDA) was then coated with the obtained CQDs, forming a CQD composite (CQDs@PDA). The CQDs@PDA served as the primary PTT agent. When irradiated at 808 nm with the NIR laser, the CQDs@PDA efficiently converted light energy into heat, resulting in rapid elevations in temperature that triggered cancer cell death. For instance, in vitro studies using HepG2 cells showed that the CQDs@PDA (CQDs/PDA ratio of 4 : 1) treatment, combined with NIR laser treatment (2 W cm−2, 3 min), increased the temperature to 70 °C. This resulted in low cell viability (23%). Live-dead staining revealed widespread red fluorescence (a sign of dead cells) only in HepG2 cells treated with CQDs@PDA plus NIR irradiation, further supporting the photothermal impact. The CQDs@PDA lacked imaging capabilities, while the pristine CQDs demonstrated exceptional intracellular Fe3+ monitoring and cell imaging capabilities.180

Green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer theranostics

The term “theranostics” refers to the integration of treatment and diagnostics on a single platform. Combining treatment and diagnostic capabilities using nanoplatforms to enable real-time monitoring and individualized treatment represents a paradigm shift in oncology.181 Theranostics methods can directly address the shortcomings of traditional cancer treatments, which frequently cause substantial systemic toxicity, have low specificity, and lack real-time feedback on treatment efficacy.13,18

Quantum dots (QDs) have become particularly intriguing as theranostics agents, owing to their distinct optical properties and multifunctionality. Their remarkable brightness, photostability, and tunable fluorescence make them perfect for tracking tumor margins, high-resolution bioimaging, and drug delivery. The ability of QDs to be functionalized with various therapeutic targeting ligands enables QDs to serve as contrast agents in diagnosis, as well as transporters for therapeutic payloads and PSs for PDT or PTT.18,182,183 These advantages are extended by green-synthesized QDs, which can be used in repeated imaging and treatment cycles due to their decreased toxicity, improved biological compatibility, and environmentally sustainable production. This design of nanoplatforms is made simpler because green-synthesized QDs can function as both imaging probes and targeted therapeutic agents through ROS formation and photothermal and photodynamic effects, hence facilitating real-time monitoring of treatment response. This eliminates the need for numerous independent components.182,183 Researchers seem interested in exploring green-synthesized QDs as promising tools for cancer theranostics, by capitalizing on their imaging and therapeutic capabilities.

In vitro and in vivo evidence of green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer theranostics

Guan and colleagues successfully conducted a green synthesis of glycol-copper indium sulfide QDs (glyco-CuIn2S QDs) through a straightforward aqueous reaction of thiol-ending monosaccharides (derived from natural sources, such as honey, milk, and fruit peels). The obtained QDs (frutose-, galactose-, mannose-, and glucose-CuInS2) were water-soluble, monodisperse, and in the near-infrared (about 827 nm) and visible (500–590 nm) regions, in addition to exhibiting dual emission that is excitation-dependent. The glyco-QDs at 100 µg mL−1 against HeLa, A549, and MKN45 cancer cells were non-toxic, indicating their excellent biocompatibility. The confocal microscopic images revealed that the QDs particularly targeted the cell membrane in HeLa, A549, and MKN45 cancer cells because of their surface glycoclusters, thus facilitating a reversible dual-color (green and red) fluorescence labeling. In addition, the QDs were able to infiltrate the necrotic interior of three-dimensional multicellular tumor spheroids (MCTS) due to their high negative zeta potentials (−23.9 to −30.1 mV). This is a significant benefit over traditional QDs, which frequently only collect at the periphery. Strong green and red fluorescence signals with over 91% channel overlap were also seen in zebrafish in vivo bioimaging, highlighting the QDs' active absorption and minimal systemic damage. The green-synthesized glyco-CuInS2 QDs can offer a safe and valuable platform that can be used in whole-organism bioimaging, deep tumor spheroid penetration, and dual cancer cell imaging.182

Crystalline GQDs synthesized using Ficus racemosa waste withered leaves demonstrated multifunctional theranostic potential in breast cancer cells. The GQDs' great near-infrared (NIR) absorption, high photostability, and outstanding biocompatibility allowed for the simultaneous use of PTT, PDT, and cancer cell imaging. The GQDs exhibited a concentration-dependent photothermal effect when exposed to an 808 nm laser (0.5 W cm−2), increasing the temperature to about 49 °C at 1 mg mL−1. This caused hyperthermia-mediated death in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. DCFDA assay and electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis verified that the photoexcited GQDs simultaneously produced ROS, which resulted in further photodynamic cytotoxicity. Also, the GQDs functioned as multicolor fluorescent probes for tracking cells in situ. This was validated by confocal microscopy, which showed successful labeling of the cancer cells (Mia-PaCa-2, MDA-MB-231) and normal cells (L929, HaCaT) without changing the cellular morphology. Importantly, the GQDs maintained their fluorescence up to 24 hours after therapy and even after 30 minutes of continuous laser irradiation, enabling real-time monitoring of laser-induced morphological alterations and eventual cell death. A further cell cycle study showed that GQDs at 100 µg mL−1 inhibited DNA replication by stopping cells in the S and G2 phases. These green-produced GQDs hence offer an affordable and efficient theranostic nanoplatform for dual PTT and PTT, along with long-term, photostable cancer cell tracking.184 Similarly, star anise fruit was used in the green synthesis of fluorescent GQDs. Hyaluronic acid (HA) was conjugated with synthesized GQDs, forming a QD complex (HA-GQDs) for cancer theranostics. The HA-GQDs demonstrated a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect. At doses below 40 µg mL−1, the HA-GQDs showed good biocompatibility due to their low toxicity, whereas a decrease in cell viability was recorded when the cells were treated with HA-GQDs at doses between 60 and 100 µg mL−1. The intrinsic cytotoxicity of HA-GQDs at high doses might complement the effects of other anticancer drugs. In bioimaging, the HA-GQD administration at low doses against MCF-7 cells produced a bright multicolor fluorescence under excitation because of the enhanced cellular uptake through HA-mediated CD44 receptor targeting. As a result, the green-produced HA-GQDs served two purposes, including exhibiting dose-dependent anticancer cytotoxicity at higher doses and enabling safe bioimaging at low doses.183

CQDs were synthesized using milk thistle seed extract (Silybum marianum). The resultant Silybum marianum CQDs (SM-CQDs) have an average size of 4.6 nm, show blue emission with a quantum yield of 4.9%, and have a large number of surface functional groups (OH and COOH) that provide exceptional water stability (zeta potential: −22.24 mV). With an IC50 of 285.2 µg mL−1, the SM-CQDs showed anticancer efficacy against Caco-2 cells. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, the SM-CQDs also served as good sensitive fluorescent probes for H2O2 (detection limit 0.159 µM) and Fe3+ ions (detection limit 0.071 µM), as well as a nanothermosensor with a temperature range of 10–90 °C. This combination of thermal sensing, chemical sensing, and anticancer activity shows how green-synthesized CQDs could function as integrated theranostic platforms that concurrently deliver therapeutic effects and detect disease-related analytes.185 In addition, Wen and colleagues green-synthesized hydrophobic CQDs using pheophytin (a magnesium-free natural derivative of chlorophyll) as biomass for sourcing carbon. The obtained hydrophobic CQDs had a strong quantum singlet oxygen (1O2) of 0.62 and also produced a maximum emission of about 680 nm (NIR wavelength). These attributes make them appropriate for use in PDT. However, the hydrophobic CQDs were conjugated with DSPE mPEG2000, resulting in an efficient 1O2-producing hydrophilic CQD assembly. On the 4T1 cell line, the CD assembly demonstrated minimal dark cytotoxicity in vitro, with an elevated cell viability (above 90%) noted even at 250 µg mL−1. Yet, treatment using the CQD assembly in combination with laser irradiation (671 nm, 0.1 W cm−2 for 10 min) led to about 95% cell death at the same concentration. The light-activated toxicity was further established through AM/PI co-staining, which revealed widespread red fluorescence (dead cells). In bioimaging, the cytoplasm of 4T1 cells showed bright red fluorescence of the internalized CQD assembly. The in vivo analysis following the injection of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice validated the effective accumulation of the CQD assembly in the tumor through the EPR effect, with the maximal fluorescent signal occurring 8 hours after injection. The CQD assembly, in the mediation of PDT, stopped tumor growth, resulting in body weight loss and prolonging the survival of the mouse to 40 days, whereas the growth of the tumor was observed in the control groups. The histological H&E staining also showed substantial damage to tumor cells only in the treatment group. Therefore, pheophytin-derived CQD assembly demonstrated selective cytotoxicity only upon laser stimulation and served as a dual-mode theranostic agent, enabling NIR fluorescence bioimaging and light-triggered PDT without the need for additional drug-delivery components.186

Current challenges of green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer PDT, PTT and theranostics

Limitations in clinical translation

The ongoing discrepancy between encouraging preclinical findings and repeatable clinical efficacy is a major barrier to clinical translation. Several in vitro and in vivo investigations have demonstrated the substantial anticancer potential of green-synthesized QDs.18,187 However, the data from these studies are often obtained in controlled experimental environments that may not really reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of human tumors. Also, the lack of well-designed clinical trials and the rarity of large-animal research make it difficult to validate safety and efficacy in people. Moreover, the clinical translation of QD-based therapies is hampered by the lengthy and expensive preclinical and clinical validation procedure.13,187,188

Lack of standardization and regulatory barriers

Despite being eco-friendly, green synthesis techniques frequently experience unpredictability because of variations in the composition of precursors, extraction procedures, and reaction conditions.189,190 Standardized procedures for synthesis, characterisation, and performance assessment are still lacking in the field of green QD research. This hinders regulatory approval processes and makes it difficult to compare study data directly. Green-synthesized QDs have yet to satisfy regulatory bodies like the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), which demand precise physicochemical characteristics, repeatable production procedures, and extensive safety data.189,191,192

Scalability and reproducibility

One of the primary obstacles in green QD synthesis is scalability and reproducibility, or the capacity to move from laboratory-scale production to industrial-scale manufacturing while preserving constant quality. The chemical composition of natural biomass sources differs from batch to batch, which causes variations in QD features, such as size distribution, optical properties, and surface chemistry.191 Compared to conventional chemical synthesis techniques, which often provide exact control over the reaction parameters, green synthesis techniques commonly depend on plant-mediated, microwave-assisted, or hydrothermal processes, making it difficult to maintain reproducibility across batches and precursor sources.37

Long-term biosafety and toxicity concerns

Even though green QDs are typically thought to be less hazardous and biocompatible than their heavy-metal counterparts, long-term biosafety investigations are still very limited.193 There seem to be unanswered questions about their biodegradation, biodistribution, and possible build-up in critical organs after systemic treatment. It may be necessary to conduct extensive research on the breakdown products of green-synthesized QDs and how they interact with biological components, especially in situations involving prolonged exposure. Furthermore, metal-based green-synthesized QDs (such as zinc sulfide QDs made from plant extracts) help in lowering the concentration of heavy metals; they may eventually liberate metal ions, which could cause genotoxicity and oxidative stress.194 For regulatory approval, consistent toxicity assessment procedures for unique green-synthesized QDs are essential.37

Optimization of targeting efficiency and therapeutic specificity

Green-synthesized QDs exhibit an intrinsic passive tumor-targeting capability through the EPR effect. The EPR effect alone may be insufficient for precise tumor localization. This is because the EPR effect varies greatly among people and tumor types, frequently leading to less-than-ideal accumulation.18,195 This can be overcome through improved active tumor targeting by ligand conjugation. However, challenges such as ligand stability, immunogenicity, and off-target interactions must be carefully addressed. Designing the active tumor targeting agent may introduce additional complexity in synthesis and quality control.196–198

Technical limitations in PDT and PTT performance

Beyond the translational and regulatory challenges discussed above, green-synthesized QDs face several intrinsic technical limitations that constrain their therapeutic efficacy in PDT and PTT. These include (i) insufficient near-infrared (NIR) absorption: many green-synthesized QDs, particularly carbon-based QDs, exhibit limited absorption in the NIR region (700–1000 nm), which is the optimal optical window for deep tissue penetration.113,199 While some QDs can be engineered to absorb NIR light using surface modification or doping, the NIR absorption of green-synthesized QDs often remains suboptimal when compared to those of inorganic photothermal agents, such as gold nanoparticles or copper sulfide nanostructure. This restriction limits the depth of tissue that can be successfully treated and may call for greater laser strengths, which raise the possibility of harming nearby healthy tissues.173,200 (ii) Suboptimal photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE): the PCE of green-synthesized QDs varies widely depending on composition, size, and surface chemistry. Many green-synthesized QDs have PCE values below 30%, which is insufficient for efficient tumor ablation at clinically tolerable laser fluences; however, other QDs have attained PCE values surpassing 50%.176,200 It is still very difficult to improve the PCE without sacrificing biocompatibility. Although they are being investigated, techniques like heteroatom doping, core–shell engineering, and hybridization with plasmonic nanoparticles complicate the green manufacturing process.113,200 (iii) Limited ROS generation: for PDT applications, the quantum yield of singlet oxygen (1O2) and other ROSs is a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy.201 When compared to traditional photosensitizers like porphyrins or phthalocyanines, many green-synthesized QDs have comparatively low ROS quantum yields. Due to competitive non-radiative decay mechanisms, carbon-based QDs, in particular, frequently exhibit minor 1O2 production.153,168 While heteroatom doping and surface functionalization can enhance ROS production, achieving consistently high ROS yields across different QD formulations remains challenging. Furthermore, the hypoxic tumor microenvironment limits the efficacy of oxygen-dependent type-II PDT mechanisms, necessitating the production of type-I photosensitizers or an oxygen-independent therapeutic approach.167,202–204

Future perspectives for green-synthesized quantum dots in cancer PDT, PTT and theranostics

Advanced synthesis and surface engineering

Future research may focus on improving green synthesis methodologies to gain greater control over QD properties. Methods such as ultrasonic, microwave-assisted, and continuous-flow synthesis procedures can offer potential for reproducible and scalable production, resulting in precise surface chemistry, tuning of size, and unique optical properties.205 Also, advanced surface engineering techniques will further improve biocompatibility, decrease nonspecific protein adsorption, and extend in vivo circulation time. These techniques include ligand engineering, congeneric passivation, and zwitterionic polymer coatings.193 Heteroatom doping and co-doping methods (e.g., N, S, P, and B) could further enhance the fluorescence quantum yield and reinforce the multimodal imaging abilities of QDs.

Smart theranostic platforms

An attractive new area is the combination of several treatment modalities on a single QD platform, with green-synthesized QDs that can combine the following: (i) real-time imaging (fluorescence, photoacoustic, and magnetic resonance); (ii) targeted drug delivery with stimuli-responsive release (pH-sensitive, enzyme-responsive, redox-triggered); and (iii) combination therapy that integrates PDT, PTT, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy.18 Additionally, green-synthesized QDs could be integrated into hybrid nanocomposites to enhance their theranostic efficacy. Current developments in hybrid nanocomposites report QD-MoS2 and QD-gold nanostar systems showing exceptional performance in fluorescence-guided surgery and multimodal imaging193 (12), while designing CQDs and modifying them with catalase helped in reducing hypoxia in PDT, demonstrating the possibility of resolving issues linked with the tumor microenvironment.206,207

Integration with emerging technologies

Several emerging trends can shape the future of green-synthesized QD-based cancer nanomedicine. These can include the following: (i) the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning for the creation and optimization of nanomaterials, as well as sophisticated bioengineering techniques for targeted delivery. By predicting structure–property connections from vast datasets, AI-driven methods can speed up the search for and optimization of green-synthesized QDs, allowing for the logical design of QDs with desired optical and biological features.208–210 (ii) Integrating green-synthesized QDs into microfluidic devices (microfluidic and lab-on-a-chip systems) may facilitate a quick point-of-care cancer-specific biomarker detection with much specificity and sensitivity.194 (iii) Personalized theranostic (personalized medicine) techniques could be made possible by customizing green QDs made from patient-specific or locally accessible biomass to specific tumor attributes. Therapeutic precision can be improved by the ability to functionalize QDs with targeting ligands specific to the patient (such as tumor-specific antibodies).17,211,212

Clinical translation and regulatory pathways

Achieving clinical translation may need synchronized efforts from multiple fronts that may include: (i) the standardization and development of harmonized protocols for synthesis, characterization, and quality control in QD green synthesis processes; (ii) regulatory science through collaborative work with regulatory organizations to determine effectiveness and safety benchmarks for QD-based cancer nanomedicines; (iii) large-scale safety studies that may embody comprehensive pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies in suitable animal models; and (iv) adaptation of green synthesis processes to good manufacturing practice (GMP) criteria for pharmaceutical production.

Sustainable and circular economy approaches

The concepts of sustainable and circular economy could be enforced by making use of different biowaste streams (such as fruit peels, food processing waste, and agricultural residues) as precursors for QD synthesis. This method can produce useful nanomaterials for medical applications while lowering the cost of production and getting rid of waste management issues.189,194

Conclusion

This review indicates that green-synthesized QDs are effective in vitro and in vivo for cancer treatment, PDT, and PTT. These agents act by generating ROS, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, and enabling efficient, light-triggered tumor ablation. Their integration into theranostic systems further allows simultaneous cancer imaging and therapy. Importantly, green synthesis approaches mitigate the toxicity and environmental limitations associated with conventional QDs, enhancing their biocompatibility and translational relevance. Despite these advantages, several setbacks are linked to green-synthesized QDs, including variability in synthesis protocols, long-term biosafety concerns, suboptimal targeting efficiency, and regulatory concerns. Addressing these setbacks requires standardized production methods, more in vivo and clinical validation, and improved surface functionalization strategies. The convergence of green QDs with emerging fields, such as artificial intelligence and personalized theranostics, may accelerate innovation and therapeutic precision. Overall, green-synthesized QDs represent a perfect shift toward safer, multifunctional, and sustainable cancer treatment nanoplatforms. They hold strong potential for use in highly effective oncological interventions.
