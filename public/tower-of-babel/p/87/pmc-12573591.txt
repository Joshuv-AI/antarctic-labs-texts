Influence of demographic and pathological factors on the clinical course and outcomes of the arthroscopic frozen shoulder 360° release
Authors: Brandon Ziegenfuss, Kristine Italia, Sarah L. Whitehouse, Tristan Shuker, Kenneth Cutbush
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrozen shoulder (FS) is an inflammatory condition that affects the shoulder capsule and can cause significant pain and stiffness. The arthroscopic FS 360° release is indicated in patients with global active range of motion (AROM) limitation. The aim of this study is to determine whether various patient demographic and pathological variables influence patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following a complete capsule resection. The variables of interest include patient comorbidity status, conservative therapy prior to surgery, and time from symptom onset to surgical treatment.MethodsAn observational prospective cohort study was conducted assessing PROMs and AROM in patients undergoing the 360° arthroscopic release between July 2013 and January 2019. AROM and PROMs including the Oxford Shoulder Score; Western Ontario Shoulder Index; Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand; Constant–Murley Score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, as well as general measures of pain intensity (visual analog scale) and wellbeing (Euro Quality of Life Measure) were collected preoperatively, 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months postoperatively. Patient demographic variables including comorbidity status (diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders), preoperative therapy (physiotherapy, cortisone injections), and symptomatic timeframe prior to surgical treatment were also collected.ResultsFifty consented patients underwent the arthroscopic FS 360° release. The mean age was 52.1 ± 7.7 years (range 35-72), and mean body mass index was 27.1 ± 4.7 kg/m2 (range 19.5-37.5). There were no significant differences across all AROM and PROMs between patients with and without diabetes mellitus and thyroid disorders at 24 months postoperatively (all P > .05). There were no significant differences across all AROM and PROMs between patients that had undergone prior conservative therapy and those that had not at 24 months postoperatively (all P > .05). Finally, there was no statistical relationship found between symptomatic timeframe prior to surgery, and AROM and most PROMs at 24 months postoperatively (all P > .05). However, those with a shorter symptomatic timeframe (0-3 months) displayed a significantly better American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score compared to those with longer symptomatic timeframes at 24 months postop (P = .045). No complications or reoperations were reported.ConclusionThe arthroscopic 360° shoulder capsule release is a safe procedure with demonstrated improvements across all PROMs and AROM movements. Patient comorbidity status, prior conservative therapy, and symptomatic timeframe did not influence functional and symptomatic postoperative outcomes 24 months after surgery.

The anatomy of the shoulder affords the joint an extensive range of motion (ROM). Frozen shoulder (FS), also referred to as adhesive capsulitis, is a common pathology that significantly affects shoulder mobility due to its restrictive and painful stigmata. These symptoms contribute to sleep disturbance and limit activities of daily living.37 The condition affects 5.3% of the general population and is most common in women aged 40-60 year old.19,25 Despite its prevalence, the condition remains inadequately defined and its pathogenesis poorly understood.16,25,29,37,44

Reeves first described the natural history of FS in 1975 and identified 3 classical stages: the painful phase (10-36 weeks), a period of stiffness (4-12 months), and a gradual recovery and spontaneous return to ROM (5-26 months).3,34 The condition is a fibrotic, inflammatory contracture primarily affecting the joint capsule.31 Histological investigations have reported the presence of inflammatory biomarkers within surrounding tissues including cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, and interleukin-1 α and β.2,30,37 While the pathogenesis of the condition remains poorly understood, risk factors associated with its development have been identified.

FS is regarded as a self-limiting condition, though several studies have shown that complete resolution is not always achieved.19,26,34,38,41 To aid in the recovery process, first-line intervention is often conservative, including oral pain and anti-inflammatory medication, physical therapy, and intra-articular injections of cortisone with local anesthetic.18,28,39 The mechanism of injury and time from symptom onset to clinical presentation will further dictate whether a surgical option such as a capsular release is indicated. A recent study reported that there was a positive correlation with shorter symptomatic time period and postoperative AROM and strength compared to patients with longer symptomatic timeframes prior to surgery.36 However, to the best of the author's knowledge, the potential confounding effect of both prior conservative therapy and symptomatic timeframe on patient outcomes following the arthroscopic 360° shoulder capsule release up to 24 months postoperatively is yet to be explored.

FS is associated with metabolic disorders including autoimmune conditions such as diabetes mellitus and thyroid disorders. A recent meta-analysis reported diabetic patients have 3.69 times the odds of developing FS compared with nondiabetic patients,15 while patients with thyroid disorders have a 2.69 increased risk of developing FS.5 It is postulated that patients with diabetes mellitus are at risk of developing the condition due to the accumulation of advanced glycation end products and cross-linking of collagen contributing to the inflammatory contracture.22 However, the pathophysiology responsible for the deposition of dense collagen matrix and recruitment of proliferating fibroblasts in idiopathic FS remains unclear. Despite proposals reporting the same inflammatory-fibrotic cascade underpinning shoulder stiffness is shared with some thyroid disorders, the exact mechanism by which FS manifests in such patients is yet to be determined.7 While the pathophysiology of these disease groups remains elusive, a recent systematic review has suggested that patients suffering from such diseases may experience worse outcomes (including symptoms and stiffness) from FS than those without such disease.14 Furthermore, the effects of diabetes mellitus on health outcomes following surgery for FS has been explored.4,11,15,17,42 While both diabetic patients and nondiabetic patients net satisfactory clinical outcomes following a capsular release for FS, research has suggested that comorbid populations experience inferior PROMs and AROM at numerous timepoints postoperatively.4

The 360° capsular release is a surgical procedure and definitive means of treating FS as described by Cutbush et al.8 The outcomes of this procedure were explored by Ziegenfuss et al.43 This paper demonstrated the procedure to be safe, with no reports of postoperative complications and fast, favorable outcomes (including return to healthy ROM and functional scores). However, the impact of preoperative patient and pathological factors is yet to be explored and given the increased risk of developing FS in some aforementioned cohorts, such an investigation is warranted. Therefore, the aim of this study is to assess how patient demographic, pathological and preoperative treatment variables affect PROMs and AROM following the arthroscopic FS 360° arthroscopic release. Specific variables of interest include patient comorbidity status (diabetes, thyroid disorders), prior conservative therapy (physiotherapy, cortisone injections), and symptomatic time period prior to surgery.

Material and methods

Study design

This is a single-center, single surgeon, prospective observational cohort study. Written and informed consent was obtained from patients who underwent an arthroscopic FS 360° release between July 2013 and January 2019. Participants were 18 years or older and diagnosed with either idiopathic or secondary post-traumatic FS. A detailed outline of the study methodology, surgical technique and 3-phase postoperative rehabilitation guideline is provided in Cutbush et al8 and Ziegenfuss et al.43 This study was approved by the institutional review board of Brisbane Private Hospital (LREC/15/BPH/4).

Participants

The surgeries were performed by a fellowship-trained shoulder surgeon (K.C.). Surgical intervention was indicated in patients with debilitating functional restriction due to a contracture of the capsule, resulting in severely limited active and passive external rotation of <30° at 0° abduction, as well as failed previous conservative management. Patients were excluded from the study if they developed joint stiffness following a prior procedure, were indicated for an open shoulder release procedure, had been diagnosed with concomitant shoulder pathologies, or had current musculoskeletal injuries or neurovascular disorders of the ipsilateral shoulder resulting in weakness and poor function. Additional exclusion criteria included concomitant contralateral shoulder pathologies, pregnancy, central nervous system disturbance, alcohol/drug addiction impacting clinical evaluation, high medical dependency affecting clinical assessment, or inability to comply with the study protocol.

Demographic and functional outcome measures

Patient demographic information was collected preoperatively and included age, sex, height, weight, hand dominance, pathological shoulder side, patient occupation, past medical history (in particular diabetes mellitus and thyroid disorders) and history of presenting complaint (including symptom onset, mechanism of injury, prior conservative management). Body mass index and symptomatic time period, defined as the period of time between self-reported symptom onset and surgery, were calculated.

Preoperative assessment was conducted assessing shoulder active ROM (AROM) in both the pathological and healthy shoulders. ROM assessment included forward flexion, abduction, as well as external and internal rotation at 0° and 90° abduction. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs—all on a worst to best scale unless specified) were also used to assess function, symptoms, and patient wellbeing. For function, Oxford Shoulder Score10 (possible range: 0-48, minimal clinically important difference (MCID): 5.19), the Constant-Murley Score6,40 (range: 0-100, MCID: 8.09), and the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score35 (ASES, range: 0-100, MCID: 6.49) were assessed. For symptoms, a visual analog scale (range: 0-10 (pain scores are measured as no pain to worst pain), MCID: 1.379), and the shortened version of the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand21 (range: 0-100, MCID: 8.09) were assessed. For joint stability, the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index27 (range: 0-2,100, MCID: 60.79) was assessed. Finally, the Euro Quality of Life Measure1,13 (range: 0-1, MCID: 0.048524) was used as a quantitative metric for patient quality of life and well-being. Note MCID values were also compared with thresholds recently published by Pasqualini et al32 with similar values of 8.2 for ASES, 10.1 for Constant-Murley Score and 1.1 for visual analog scale. AROM and PROMs were repeated at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months postoperatively. Complications were recorded during follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS statistical software (version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Sample size was calculated for the initial outcomes paper (Ziegenfuss et al43) based on clinical experience in order to identify safety from inception of this technique with a minimum follow-up period of 24 months. Baseline demographic data were analyzed using means, standard deviations and range for continuous variables, and frequencies for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with comparisons between groups performed using analysis of variance or the Mann–Whitney U-test as appropriate for the data type. Frequencies were compared using the chi-squared, Fishers Exact test or Kendall's tau-c for ordinal responses, as appropriate. No adjustments were made for multiple testing. For all analyses, P ≤ .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographic characteristics

Fifty-three arthroscopic FS 360° release procedures were performed between July 2013 and January 2019 (Table I). Two patients declined participation and were therefore excluded. One patient developed FS in both shoulders, however the conditions were not concurrent, and symptom occurrence was separated by 22 months; therefore, 51 shoulders were included in this study. The mean age was 52.1 ± 7.7 years (range 35-72) and mean body mass index was 27.1 ± 4.7 kg/m2 (range 19.5-37.5). Most patients were female (58.8%), with the dominant side being affected in 19 (37.3%) patients. Seven (13.7%) patients had either type I or type II diabetes mellitus and 6 (11.8%) had a diagnosed thyroid disorder with 1 patient having both conditions. Six (11.8%) patients were smokers. Pathology characteristics

Forty-three (84.3%) patients were diagnosed with primary FS, and 8 (15.7%) patients were diagnosed with secondary FS (Table I). Twenty-one (41.2%) patients had a history of trauma (fracture, soft tissue injury, dislocation) to the affected shoulder; careful consideration by a fellowship trained subspecialist orthopedic surgeon determined if the history of traumatic injury had a temporal causative affect in the development of FS. Thirty-six (70.6%) patients were symptomatic for up to 12 months prior to surgery, while 15 (29.4%) patients experienced symptoms for longer than 12 months (range 0-3 months to >36 months). Thirty-six (70.6%) patients had received cortisone therapy (subacromial or glenohumeral joint) prior to surgery. Seventeen patients received 1 injection, 14 received 2 injections, and 5 patients received 3 injections. There was no association between the number of injections and time of symptoms (P = .834, Kendall's tau). Seventeen (33.3%) underwent physiotherapy preoperatively. Eighteen (35.3%) had undergone a previous upper limb surgical procedure, while 33 did not have a history of upper limb surgery. All patients underwent physiotherapy postoperatively.

Cohort results

The overall outcomes of the collective cohort are reported in Ziegenfuss et al.43 In summary, all AROM movements and PROMs significantly improved from preoperative to 24 months post operation (P < .001). No differences were found between primary and secondary cases of FS at any timepoint for AROM movements or ASES and shortened version of the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand scores across all timepoints (all P > .05). Importantly, there were no complications including axillary nerve injury, infection, recurring joint stiffness, gross dislocation or instability (as supported by Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index outcomes in Supplementary Appendix SI) throughout the 24-month follow-up period.

There were no significant differences across all functional, symptomatic and wellbeing PROMs between the cohort that received cortisone injection therapy and those that had not at any time point. Discussion

FS is an inflammatory contracture of the glenohumeral joint capsule of unknown etiology.2,16,25,29, 30, 31,37,44 Severe and/or recalcitrant cases of FS can be definitively treated with a complete capsule resection as described by Cutbush et al.8 This technique is safe, with no reported complications, while achieving fast and effective return to AROM and PROMs as reported by Ziegenfuss et al.43 However, the impact of various patient demographic factors is yet to be explored in the journey to recovery for patients. Therefore, to the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first paper to outline the clinical course and outcomes following the 360° FS release across multiple timepoints up until 24 months postoperatively for various pathological and demographic factors. The variables of interest in this study included patient comorbidities status, prior conservative management and chronicity of symptoms prior to surgical intervention.

Comorbidities

Literature reports there is a positive association between FS and some metabolic disorders, with diabetics and thyroid disorder patients having 3.69 times and 2.69 times the odds of developing FS compared to the healthy population, respectively.5,12,15,20,23,31 While the exact mechanism by which FS develops in the context of these diseases remains unclear, Dyer et al14 reported such patients may experience worse measures of function, pain and ROM. Cho et al reported that idiopathic FS patients and diabetic FS patients achieved similar functional score outcomes and ROM at 12 months follow-up, though ASES scores were significantly lower at this timepoint in the diabetic population compared to the healthy population.4 However, the authors also mentioned that while favorable outcomes were eventually achieved in both groups, the diabetic population had demonstrated a slower progression to recovery at 12 months, with significantly lower forward flexion, external rotation and internal rotation at numerous timepoints throughout4; similar findings were reported by El Deriny et al17 and Yanlei et al.42 In contrast, Virgilio-Salgado et al found diabetic patients did not demonstrate a significantly inferior forward flexion, external rotation, or worse pain intensity scores at 6-month follow-up.11 While a recent systematic review by Phua and colleagues33 found inconsistent evidence of variable clinical outcomes of FS in thyroid disorder patients, the outcomes following complete capsular release treatment in this population is, to the best of the authors' knowledge, yet to be reported in literature.

Our study found no significant differences in AROM movements or PROMs between the patients with diabetes or thyroid disorders and the healthy population at 24 months postoperatively. However, similar to previous studies,4,17,42 patients did experience a slower progression in regaining AROM for forward flexion, abduction and internal rotation at some timepoints leading to 24 months postoperatively. Importantly, these differences had no bearing on their functional outcome, given there were no significant differences in functional or symptomatic PROMs at any timepoints throughout follow-up. Evidently, the presence of these comorbidities has no bearing on the final outcome following complete capsular release, though patients ought to be forewarned about the potential (albeit small) delay in their AROM recovery relative to healthy patients undergoing the same procedure.

Prior conservative therapy

Physiotherapy and cortisone injection therapy are common techniques employed as first-line conservative treatment modalities of FS. The benefits in the acute-post conservative intervention period are well established and have been reported in original studies28 and systematic reviews by Favejee et al18 and Sun et al.39 However, to the best of the author's knowledge, the impact of these conservative treatment modalities on postoperative functional and symptomatic outcomes is yet to be explored. In response to this knowledge gap, a subgroup analysis was conducted to assess the impact of conservative therapy prior to complete capsular release. Patients that had undergone prior physiotherapy demonstrated similar PROMs and AROM at 24 months postoperatively and throughout their recovery journey to this point in time. However, while a significantly greater active forward flexion was found in the physiotherapy group at 12 months, this difference (10°) was not large enough to produce demonstratable functional or symptomatic outcomes at or after the 12-month timepoint.

Patients that had undergone cortisone injection therapy prior to surgery reported similar functional, symptomatic and wellbeing PROMs compared to those who had not had any injections at any timepoint throughout the study (regardless of the number of injections received). There were, however, significant differences in external and internal rotation at 90° of abduction (both at 2 weeks), as well as in forward flexion at 12 months. Note, these differences were minor, did not impact functional outcomes to produce significant differences at the same or later timepoints, and in some cases, represented clinically insignificant differences (eg 0.6°).

Symptomatic timeframe

The influence of the symptomatic timeframe prior to capsular release has been reported in literature.36 Rizvi et al36 found that patients with a shorter symptomatic timeframe (<10 months) demonstrated greater improvements in their ROM and strength at 6 months postoperative compared to patients with longer symptomatic timeframes (>10 months). Moreover, there were no significant differences for mean abduction, forward flexion and external rotation at 6 months between these groups; however, a significantly greater mean internal rotation was found in the shorter symptomatic timeframe group at 6 months.36 In our study, we divided our cohort into 4 symptomatic timeframes (0-3 months, 3-6 months, 6-12 months, ≥ 12 months). Similar to Rizvi et al, we found no significant differences in mean AROM across all groups; though, in our study we demonstrate this to be the case up to 24 months postoperatively. Note, we report no differences found in internal rotation, contrary to the findings of Rizvi et al in the acute postoperative period. Finally, there were no significant differences across all functional, symptomatic and wellbeing PROMs between various symptomatic timeframes at any timepoint up to 24 months postoperatively, with the exception of those symptomatic for 0-3 months demonstrating a significantly lower ASES score at 24 months compared to those with a longer symptomatic timeframe (which exceeds the MCID of 6.4 for this score). While a significant difference was found for this score at that timepoint, its clinical relevance ought to be put into question given no differences were found for any other functional, symptomatic or wellbeing score, nor for any AROM movement at this timepoint.

Limitations

This study conducted numerous subgroup analyses to assess the impact of pathological and demographic factors on functional and symptomatic outcomes following the 360° release. However, a number of these groups were comprised of relatively small sample sizes, for example no prior cortisone injection therapy (n = 11), acute symptomatic timeframes (0-3 months, n = 5); sample sizes for the subgroups which are observed in clinical practice for patients opting for the surgical intervention. As such, while the findings indicated no statistically significant relationship between these explanatory variables and patient outcomes, these findings ought to be considered with caution. This includes our combining of diabetic and thyroid disorder patients into a single comorbid group in the interest of maintaining an adequate sample size for subgroup analysis. Therefore, the results of thyroid disorder patients and diabetic patients may not be satisfactorily teased apart to assess the individual influence of each comorbidity on outcomes. Moreover, numerous patients had both diabetes and thyroid disorders, limiting the capacity to be allocated a single comorbidity group. The degree of successful management of associated chronic diseases such as diabetes mellitus and thyroid disorders may also influence the improvement trajectory of functional and symptomatic outcomes, and therefore warrants further research.

The results in this paper are derived from the experiences of a single-center and surgeon. This is an important consideration given that a specific population is represented in this study, which may exclude patients that do not have a similar access to services. Finally, while this study required multiple statistical comparisons to investigate the influence of various demographic factors on numerous postoperative outcomes, some of the statistically significant differences may have manifested due to chance. This includes the marginal significant P values which are likely a result of a type I error.

Conclusion

This study has investigated the postoperative outcomes of complete capsular release in FS patients. Herein, comorbid conditions (including diabetes mellitus and thyroid disorders), preoperative therapy strategies (including physiotherapy and cortisone injections), and condition symptomatic timeframe were found to have no influence on the functional and symptomatic postoperative outcomes. Furthermore, no complications were reported across all cohorts. Note the findings ought to be considered in the context of this single-center study which could benefit from a greater sample size.
