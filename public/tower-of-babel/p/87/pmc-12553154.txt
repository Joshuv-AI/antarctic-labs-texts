Possible involvement of spermidine and methyl jasmonate on sex expression and some physiological responses of cucumber
Authors: Ghasem Akhbarfar, Maryam Haghighi, Maryam Mozafarian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSex expression in plants is a complex process influenced by genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors. In cucumbers, a high male-to-female flower ratio significantly contributes to low yields. The application of exogenous plant growth regulators can modify the sex ratio and flowering sequence, potentially improving productivity. In this experiment, effects of plant growth regulators, Spermidine (Spd) at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 mmol/L and methyl jasmonate (Mj) at concentrations of 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mmol L− 1 on sex expression, chlorophyll content, antioxidant capacity, and fruit quality and yield of cucumber plants evaluated.Results Results indicate that, overall, female flowers, the female-to-male flower ratio, chlorophyll b, yield, and antioxidant capacity increased in autumn compared to spring. Among various growth-regulating treatments, the application of 0.75 mmol L− 1 Spd resulted in a 246% increase in the female-to-male flower ratio and a 125% increase in yield compared to the control in autumn. In contrast, the highest number of male flowers was observed in spring under the control treatment, leading to reduced yield during that season.ConclusionOverall, Spd at 0.75 mmol L− 1 had a more significant impact on improving most traits examined in both spring and autumn compared to other growth-regulating treatments. However, methyl jasmonate was less effective, particularly at lower dosages.Keywords: Cucumber, Methyl jasmonate, Sex expression, Spermidine, Yield

Background

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) is an important horticultural crop of the Cucurbitaceae family with monoecious flowering habit, staminate (male), pistillate (female) and rarely bisexual flowers [1]. Cucumber has become a model organism for studying floral biology, due to genetic and hormonal regulation of sex expression.

Floral sex regulation is influenced by tissue differentiation and hormonal balance, and auxins cytokinins, gibberellins and ethylene play a basic role. Gibberellins (GAs) and ethylene are the major antagonistic hormones, ethylene promoting female flower induction by inhibiting stamen formation and GAs inducing male flower by suppressing ethylene production [2]. Polyamines, including spermidine (Spd), are important for the regulation of plant growth and development, and for stress responses.

Application of Spd in cucumbers improves reproductive organ development and modulates ethylene biosynthesis to enhance floral sex expression. Spd stimulates pistillate flower formation by increasing ethylene production and controlling key gene expression pathways related to floral organogenesis. Furthermore, Spd has been demonstrated to increase pollen viablity [3, 4]. Cucumber plants respond differently to stress (Spd enhances stress tolerance) and sex expression (Spd affects sex expression). Gong et al. [5] reported that Spd modulates ethylene biosynthesis, promotes pistillate flower formation, and enhances reproductive organ development. In addition, Spd inhibits ethylene production under stress conditions and impacts sex expression by promoting female flower development [6].

Methyl jasmonate (Mj), is a jasmonic acid derivative, Mj application promotes pistillate flower formation through enhanced ethylene signaling and repression of GA induced male floral development [7]. In addition, Mj enhances physiological responses including photosynthetic efficiency, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging and secondary metabolite accumulation and is beneficial to plant health and reproductive success [8]. More recently, Mj has also been shown to modulate cucumber sex expression by controlling hormone pathways, specifically by enhancing ethylene signaling and repressing gibberellin induced male floral development [9]. The physiological response to Mj application is complex, including dose dependent changes in stomatal conductance and volatile emissions [10].

Variations in temperature, light intensity and day length determine the season during which cucumbers are cultivated and the sex expression of Cucumis sativus is highly dependent on such variations. Pistillate (female) flowers usually develop better in spring under longer photoperiods and moderate temperatures. Previous studies have demonstrated that increased light intensity, and light spectrum in the blue region, during spring promote ethylene biosynthesis and female flower formation. In contrast, short day lengths and cooler temperatures in autumn may reduce ethylene production, increasing the proportion of staminate (male) flowers. In addition, cool temperatures have been associated with a delay in the flowering development of pistillate flowers, probably due to a reduced efficiency of photosynthesis and hormonal activity [11–13]. High temperature also promotes pistillate flower development. For example, temperatures between 26 °C and 30 °C promote pistillate flower anthesis [14]. As well, temperature affects epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation patterns that are central to sex determination [11]. Additional modulation of sex expression can be attained via the combination of light and temperature, expressed as a photothermal ratio.

Light intensity has the tendency to increase pistillate (female) flower number and staminate (male) flower number with increasing and decreasing light intensity respectively. Under high light intensities, more pistillate flowers anthesis, and staminate flowers are more common under moderate light conditions [14]. The photothermal ratios are higher and the number of female nodes is increased and the number of male nodes is decreased, which implies greater photoassimilate translocation to reproductive organs [15].

Further, sex expression is influenced by light quality, especially the red to far-red ratio. Upregulation of ethylene responsive genes was required for pistillate flower development under spring conditions with a higher red light component. By contrast, autumn reduces the efficiency of floral differentiation and biases sex expression toward male flowers [16, 17]. Ethylene and gibberellins are upset by temperature extremes high or low and affect sex ratio. In transitional seasons such as spring and autumn, light and temperature conditions need to be well managed in controlled environment, such as greenhouses, for optimal flower sex expression and yield.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of varying concentrations of Spd and Mj on cucumber sex expression, chlorophyll content, antioxidant capacity, yield, and quality of cucumbers. The objective is to address existing gaps in understanding the physiological pathways influenced by these treatments and to develop strategies for optimizing floral sex ratios and yield under spring and autumn seasonal conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant material and treatments

The present study was conducted through in a plastic tunnel at the experimental farm of Isfahan University of Technology. The average temperature during spring was between 18 and 20 °C, while in autumn, it ranged from 13 to 15 °C. The average humidity levels during spring were approximately 60–70%, while in autumn, they were around 50–60%. The photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) was measured at 260 µmol m− 2s− 1. The day length during spring was about 12–14 h, and in autumn, it was approximately 10–12 h. The chemico-physical characteristics of the experimental soil are presented in Table 1.

Chemico-physical characteristic of experiment soil

This research used a factorial design within a completely randomized block layout with three repetitions, each consisting of three plants. The seasonal treatments included spring and autumn, while the growth-regulating treatments comprised Spd at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 mmol/L (Spd 0.25, Spd 0.50, Spd 0.75) and Mj at concentrations of 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mmol L−1 ((Mj 0.1, Mj 0.25, Mj 0.5) [18]. The phytohormones Spd and Mj were purchased from Sigma Company.

Seeds of cucumber cv. Rashid, obtained from Petro Seed Company, were soaked in water for 24 h and then planted in a soil and manure mixture at a density of four plants per 50 square centimeters.

Spd was applied at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 mmol L− 1, while Mj was used at concentrations of 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mmol L− 1 as a foliar spray. The spraying was done three times during the growth period, with intervals of seven days between applications. The first spray was applied at the two-leaf stage, followed by two consecutive weeks of spraying.

Irrigation was performed using a drip irrigation system, with water applied every three days during the growing season to maintain optimal soil moisture levels. During the growth period, no pruning was performed since the variety used was a non-greenhouse cultivar, not requiring heavy pruning like greenhouse varieties. Only side branches growing on the main stem were removed up to the seventh node. To nourish the plants before planting, 50 g of complete NPK fertilizer (20-20-20) per square meter was mixed into the soil prior to seeding.

Plant growth was monitored weekly, and any signs of disease or pest infestation were recorded, with appropriate measures taken as needed. To assess traits such as the number of male and female flowers, their ratio, and yield, we counted the total number of flowers per plant across each treatment with three replicates each consisting of three plants, then calculated the average for each treatment. For physiological traits, including chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and antioxidant capacity, we randomly selected three leaf samples from each replicate and averaged the results across all three replicates for each treatment.

Number of male flowers, number of female flowers, and male-to-female flowers ratio

From the first flower appearance, male and female flowers were counted. This counting was conducted every three days and continued for a duration of three months.

Days to the first flower and first flower node appearance

The number of days from seed planting to first flower appearance and first flower node were recorded for each treatment.

Plant yield

Fruits with an average length of 12 cm were harvested, counted, and weighed weekly from week eight to week twelve. The yield was expressed as the average weight of harvested fruit per plant over a 10-day period.

Antioxidant capacity

We used mature leaves from the middle part of the plant, which were fully developed at the time of measurement. Initially, 200 µL of the leaf sample was combined with 800 µL of Tris-HCl buffer in a test tube at pH 7.4. Next, we added 1 mL of DPPH solution and agitated the mixture using a test tube shaker for 10 s. After that, we let the combination sit at ambient temperature and kept it away from light. Thirty minutes after adding the DPPH solution, a spectrophotometer measured the light absorbance at 517 nm. The blank solution included 800 µL of Tris-HCl buffer and 1.2 mL of ethanol. The aforementioned UV spectrophotometer denoted the absorbance as As when the sample was introduced, and Ac when ethanol was present. The inhibition ratio was computed using Koleva et al. [19] method and reported as a percentage: inhibition ratio (%) = ((Ac - As)) / Ac) × 100.

Fruit firmness and total soluble solid

Fruit firmness and Total soluble solid (TSS) were measured using a penetrometer device (OSK-I-10576; Ogawa Seiki Co., Tokyo, Japan) and hand refractometer (Model K-0032, Cosmo, Japan), respectively. Fruit firmness was measured from cucumber fruits located on the middle parts of the plant to ensure data comparability. Measurements were taken at the mature green stage of cucumber fruit development.

Leaf chlorophyll content

The chlorophyll content of the leaves was measured three weeks after the last spraying, using the equations provided. We specifically used mature leaves from the middle part of the plant, which were fully developed at the time of measurement. The chlorophyll content was determined using the following equations:

(1) Chlorophyll a (mg g− 1) = (12.7 * A663) − (2.59 * A645).

(2) Chlorophyll b (mg g− 1) = (22.9 * A645) − (4.7 * A663).

(3) Chlorophyll total (mg g− 1) = (8.2 * A663) + (20.2 * A645).

where A663 and A645 are the absorbance measured from 663 nm to 645 nm, respectively [20]. The spectrophotometer was adjusted to zero using the acetone/ethanol mixture.

Statistical analysis

The research employed a factorial experimental design, specifically utilizing a completely randomized block design with three replications. The data were analyzed using Statistix 8 software, developed in Tallahassee, Florida, United States. A three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the data, and means were evaluated for significance using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of P < 0.05. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted utilizing Statgraphics Centurion, Version XVI. The circular, Sankey, and correlation diagrams among traits were generated using the website https://www.chiplot.online/.

The effects of growth-regulating treatments on number of female and male flowers, and female/male ratio of cucumber across spring and autumn seasons

In each of the growth-regulating treatments during the autumn season, the number of female flowers was greater than in the spring. The highest number of female flowers in autumn was observed in the Spd 0.75 treatment, while the lowest number was found in the control and Mj 0.1 treatments. In spring, there were no significant differences between the control treatment and all three concentrations of Mj (0.1, 0.25, and 0.75), with all showing fewer female flowers compared to the Spd concentrations (Fig. 1A). The highest number of male flowers in spring was recorded in the control and Mj treatments at all three concentrations (0.1, 0.25, and 0.5), while the lowest counts were observed in the Spd 0.25 and Spd 0.75 treatments. The highest ratio of female to male flowers in autumn was observed in the Spd 0.5 and Spd 0.75 treatments compared to the other growth-regulating treatments. Conversely, the lowest ratio of female to male flowers in autumn occurred in the control treatment, while in spring, the lowest ratios were found in the control and all three concentrations of Mj (0.1, 0.25, and 0.5) (Fig. 1C).

Discussion

The effects of growth-regulating treatments on the number of female and male flowers and female/male ratio of cucumber across spring and autumn season

The results of our study demonstrate that administration of Spd, particularly at 0.75 mM, significantly enhances the female-to-male flower ratio in cucumbers. Spd plays a crucial role in increasing the sensitivity of cucumber flowers to ethylene, which in turn promotes the development of female flowers [21]. This finding suggests that Spd not only contributes to the hormonal balance necessary for female flower induction but also amplifies ethylene signaling, leading to more pronounced differentiation of female flowers. Extensive documentation exists regarding ethylene-induced sex expression in cucumbers, where elevated ethylene levels are known to favor the formation of female flowers [22].

Previous studies have shown that manipulating the expression of ethylene receptor genes, such as downregulating CsETR1 or upregulating CsACO2 in transgenic Arabidopsis, can facilitate female flower development [23]. Further investigations have confirmed that CsACO2 is essential for initiating female flower formation and is regulated by the gene CsWIP1 in cucumbers [24]. In hermaphrodite flowers, ethylene is perceived by the ETR receptor, which activates a signaling cascade involving a calcium-dependent nuclease that inhibits primordial anther development [25]. This mechanism highlights the roles of CsACS, CsETR, and CsCaN in female flower differentiation. Specifically, when cucumber flowers, initially hermaphrodite, are exposed to ethylene, CsCaN acts as a specific nuclease targeting primordial anthers, thus halting stamen development and facilitating the transition to female flowers [26].

In contrast, Mj treatments did not significantly enhance the quantity of female flowers. This lack of effect may be attributed to the antagonistic interaction between jasmonates and ethylene in certain physiological processes, where jasmonates can inhibit ethylene activity at suboptimal concentrations [27]. The role of CsGL2-LIKE in regulating male flower development is particularly relevant here. CsGL2-LIKE, a member of the cucumber HD-ZIP IV subfamily, interacts with CsJAZ1, a transcriptional repressor from the jasmonate ZIM-domain family, to form a complex that regulates male flower development [9]. Thus, understanding the balance between Spd, ethylene, and jasmonate signaling is crucial for optimizing flower sex expression and enhancing fruit yield in cucumbers.

Our study indicates that Spd mM, improves cucumber flower development and increases the female-to-male ratio, especially in the fall. The observed decrease in male flowers during fall, particularly under the Spd 0.5 and Spd 0.75 mM treatments, suggests that Spd effectively regulates the ethylene pathway to promote female flower growth while inhibiting male flower production. The formation of male flowers is often enhanced by gibberellins and suppressed by ethylene; thus, the absence of growth-regulating treatments in the control group may have allowed gibberellins to exert a more significant influence [22]. Comparable results have been observed in pumpkin and zucchini, where the application of ethylene-releasing agents decreased male flower counts and improved the female-to-male ratio [28].

In summary, these results suggest that hormonal applications can be strategically employed to control reproductive characteristics in cucumbers, potentially enhancing yield by optimizing the sex ratio.

The effects of growth-regulating treatments on days to the first flower and first flower node appearance across spring and autumn seasons

This study demonstrates that Spd treatments significantly accelerate the time to the first blooming in cucumbers, with the Spd 0.75 treatment in autumn exhibiting the most pronounced effect. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that Spd acts as a signaling molecule in the flowering process. Kiełkowska and Dziurka [29] highlighted that variations in the levels of these signaling molecules activate hormone-responsive pathways crucial for floral development. Specifically, external application of Spd has been shown to up-regulate floral activator genes such as MdFT, MdFD, and MdSOC1, thereby facilitating accelerated flowering [30]. This underscores the positive signaling role of Spd and its integration within a targeted genetic network that regulates floral signaling.

In contrast, while Mj treatments had a smaller impact on flowering than Spd, they still resulted in reduced time to flower emergence compared to controls. This effect may be attributed to Mj’s influence on vegetative growth rates. Notably, the silencing of cucumber CsGL2-LIKE has been shown to decrease endogenous jasmonic acid levels, leading to delayed blooming of male flowers [9]. These findings suggest that while Mj contributes to flowering, its effects are less pronounced than those of Spd, indicating a potential hierarchy in the roles of these hormones in flower development.

Conversely, the warmer temperatures and extended daylight in autumn enhance the efficacy of Spd treatments, as cucumbers thrive in thermophilic environments [31]. Supporting this, Song et al. [32] found that weak light and insufficient day length can suppress flower initiation, particularly in cucumbers with deficient vegetative growth.

The results confirm that Spd can significantly accelerate flowering in cucumbers, regardless of the season, particularly at higher dosages. This highlights the importance of adjusting environmental conditions to optimize hormonal therapies for flowering. Moreover, the differential effects of Mj on blooming in cucumbers compared to other crops suggest that plant hormonal responses exhibit species-specific characteristics.

The effects of growth-regulating treatments on fruit yield and antioxidant capacity of cucumber across spring and autumn seasons

In our experiment, the administration of Spd significantly enhanced the vegetative development of cucumber plants, as evidenced by an increase in the number of stems, nodes, and overall plant dry weight. This improvement in vegetative growth suggests the potential for greater fruit output. Supporting this, research on fruit trees has shown that foliar application of Spd 90 days post-flowering can lead to improved fruit yield [33]. Additionally, other studies have indicated that elevated levels of Spd promote flower opening, early fruit development, and overall fruit yield, further corroborating our findings [34]. These results highlight the beneficial role of Spd in enhancing both vegetative growth and reproductive success in cucumbers.

Our study highlights a significant enhancement in the antioxidant capacity of cucumber plants following foliar applications of Spd and Mj. Consistent with our findings, Baek et al. [35] reported the highest antioxidant capacity in tomatoes treated with preharvest Mj. Similarly, López-Peñuelas et al. [36] found that the application of Mj at 1.5 mM significantly increased cellular antioxidant activity compared to the control in summer squash. Interestingly, our results indicate that the Spd 0.75 treatment exhibited superior performance in autumn compared to spring. By quantifying antioxidant levels, we can assess the effectiveness of foliar treatments in enhancing the plant’s defense mechanisms, which is vital for improving growth and yield. This understanding it allows us to tailor treatments based on seasonal conditions to achieve the best possible outcomes for cucumber cultivation.

Effects of the hormonal treatment on cucumber fruit firmness and TSS during the spring and autumn

The results of this study demonstrate that the application of Spd at 0.75 mM significantly enhances cucumber firmness compared to Mj treatments during the spring season. While, the study by Minh [37] showed that the highest retention of firmness in cantaloupe was observed with pre-harvest treatment of Mj at 1.5 mM, suggesting that specific concentrations of jasmonates can influence firmness retention in various fruits. According to Baek et al. [35], Mj application increased the activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and peroxidase (POD) enzyme that are involved in lignin biosynthesis which could, in turn, increase fruit firmness. This study found that spring fruits exhibited greater firmness than those harvested in autumn, likely due to the cooler temperatures in spring, which impede fruit growth while strengthening cell wall structure [31]. Notably, higher concentrations of Mj led to a marked decrease in firmness during spring, aligning with its role in increasing ethylene sensitivity, which accelerates ripening and results in softer fruit [38].

Additionally, the results indicate that Mj applications at all concentrations significantly increased TSS in cucumbers compared to Spd treatments. This observation aligns with findings from previous studies that reported increased TSS in tomatoes following Mj treatment [39]. Mj enhances sugar metabolism by elevating the activities of sucrose synthase and invertase, thereby promoting sugar accumulation in the fruit [39].

Overall, these findings highlight that hormonal interventions should be tailored to specific environmental conditions and quality objectives to achieve optimal outcomes.

The effects of growth-regulating treatments on chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll content across spring and autumn seasons

The results of the present study indicate that the application of Spd at 0.75 mM resulted in the highest concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll in cucumber leaves during both spring and autumn. Foliar application of exogenous Spd significantly enhances chlorophyll concentration, which is vital for optimizing photosynthetic performance [40]. Spd contributes to reducing chlorophyll degradation and improving light capture, thereby enhancing photosynthetic rates. For example, Lester [41] investigation into polyamines in honeydew muskmelon demonstrated that these compounds effectively delay chlorophyll degradation by reducing the hydrolytic activity of chloroplast thylakoid membranes [41]. The positive impact of polyamines on chlorophyll content can be attributed to their antioxidant properties, which help maintain the integrity of chloroplast membranes and prevent degradation [42, 43].

Since photosynthesis is crucial for plant growth and development [44], understanding how Spd and Mj influence chlorophyll content helps us assess their effects on the plants’ ability to capture light and convert it into energy. Enhanced chlorophyll levels can indicate better growth conditions, which may correlate with increased flower production. As shown in the trait correlation diagram (Fig. 8), there is a correlation between chlorophyll and the number of cucumber flowers.

In contrast, our findings indicate that the application of Mj did not result in a significant increase in chlorophyll levels compared to Spd. While Mj may stimulate the expression of key enzymes involved in the chlorophyll biosynthesis pathway through the production of 5-aminolevulinic acid, a precursor in chlorophyll synthesis, its overall effect on chlorophyll accumulation appears limited. Furthermore, previous research suggests that Mj can influence sugar metabolism, with sugars potentially acting as signaling molecules that promote chlorophyll production [45].

Conclusion

This research finds that Spd improves cucumber production and antioxidant capacity throughout the spring and autumn seasons, with Spd at 0.75 mmol L− 1 being the most effective. However, Mj was less effective and especially at lower dosages. The differences observed between these two treatments demonstrate that more research is needed to better understand the dose dependent and seasonal interactions of these hormones in cucumbers. Here, we emphasize the importance of tailoring hormonal application, such as Spd, to the specialized physiological and environmental conditions of cucumbers. Seasonal factors had a big impact on these findings, and Spd, particularly at high dosages, had a huge impact on yield and antioxidant capacity. These results indicate that Spd may prove to be a useful means of boosting crop yield and quality in a number of horticultural settings.
