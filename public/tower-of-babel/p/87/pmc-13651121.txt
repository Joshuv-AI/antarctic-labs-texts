Development and validity evidence of a knowledge assessment in genomics sequencing report interpretation for non-geneticist medical trainees
Authors: Stacy A. Johnson, Whitney S. Thompson, Robert A. Vierkant, Stephanie Brown, Filippo Pinto e Vairo, Stephanie C. Mavis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinicians are increasingly expected to interpret genomic sequencing reports, yet tools to assess the proficiency of non-geneticist medical trainees in this important skill are lacking. This study aimed to develop a multiple-choice test to evaluate non-geneticist trainees’ knowledge and skill in interpreting genomic reports and to systematically gather sufficient validity evidence supporting its use. An iterative, expert-informed yet practical approach was employed to design and refine the assessment, with validity evidence analyzed according to Messick’s framework. The final 27-item test demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.9), and significant differences were seen between participant groups, with test scores correlating with participants’ level of expertise. These findings support the utility of this assessment as a robust, low-stakes measure of genomic sequencing report interpretation skills among non-geneticist medical trainees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09898-x.

Introduction and background

Medicine is entering into a genomic era, with rapidly advancing genomics knowledge and technologies being applied across numerous medical subspecialties. However, formal education for medical professionals not formally trained in genetics and genomics has lagged, resulting in a gap between the knowledge available about the clinical relevance of genomic information and the ability of clinicians to include and use such information in their medical practice [1]. While significant effort has led to development of robust and evolving databases and clinical decisional support tools like Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) [2] and GeNotes [3] to help clinicians recognize, diagnose, and treat patients with suspected or known genetic diseases, relatively little attention has been paid to skills in interpreting complex test results, such as those from whole genome sequencing. Interpreting genomic test results is an important micro skill that allows clinicians to translate a test result into meaningful information for the patient, and while clinicians are increasingly expected to use genomics knowledge and skills in patient care, many feel inadequately prepared for such tasks [4].

Due to the frequency of genomic testing at our institution, especially in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), we observed a need for a practical and sustainable educational approach for pediatric trainees (medical doctors undergoing specialty training in pediatrics) to interpret genomic testing reports. Our search for existing education revealed two easily accessible resources on how to read genomic reports: a PDF toolkit from genome.gov [5] and a video from the Genomics Education Programme in the UK [6], neither of which included active learning strategies or evidence that knowledge or skill increases with such training. One additional study [7] leveraging adult-learning theory principles and a case-based design for non-geneticist medical trainees (physicians in training in fields other than genetics) used a programmatic evaluation approach and assessed Kirkpatrick level one (‘reactions’) outcomes such as learner confidence and self-assessed skill [8]. However, self-assessment of skill in novices is frequently poorly correlated with markers of objective skill development [9, 10].

Robust assessments are essential for defensible conclusions about to the degree of learning that has occurred and are informative in continuous educational programmatic improvement, yet establishing rigor in learner assessment is a frequently overlooked aspect of many curricula [11]. As tests in education have ‘little or no intrinsic meaning’ and educational assessments are never said to be either ‘valid’ or ‘invalid’, the modern conceptualization of test validity refers to the “evidence presented to support or refute the meaning or interpretation assigned to [any educational] assessment results” [12]. Stated simply, “validity describes how well one can legitimately trust the results of a rest as interpreted for a specific purpose” [13] and it is the interpretation and use of a score, not the test itself, that is ‘validated’ [14].

In the absence of reliable tools to guide teaching and assessment in our intended audience of non-geneticist medical trainees, we sought to address this critical gap by systematically developing the evidence that scores from our written assessment could be used to draw reliable conclusions about pediatric trainees’ skill in genome report interpretation.

The purpose of this project was to develop the validity evidence for a test assessing non-geneticist trainees’ knowledge and skills in reading and interpreting pediatric genome sequencing reports. The development of the test and the validity argument are described in this manuscript.

Methods

Our team of educators and subject matter experts (SMEs) developed the assessment and its validity evidence through a series of rational steps (Fig. 1) in constructing the validity argument as recommended by Cook et al. [11] We explicitly defined the purpose of the assessment, then developed the test itself and the validity evidence for it. To begin, the group confirmed that the knowledge domains to be learned and assessed were primarily cognitive and at the level of ‘knows’ and ‘knows how’ in Miller’s Pyramid [15]. Because the written assessment paradigm is well suited to assess knowledge at those levels, we selected a multiple-choice question (MCQ) format for our test.

Participants & study location

This study was conducted at Mayo Clinic Rochester, USA. The assessment was designed for trainees including resident physicians in our three-year graduate medical education program in pediatrics & adolescent medicine, as well as for neonatology fellows who had completed such general pediatrics training and were in their subsequent clinical three-year fellowship training program. ‘Interns’ had just graduated medical school and were tested before any clinical exposure in the training programs, while current residents and fellows included trainees from all three years were tested at the end of their current training year.

Validity framework

To construct our validity argument, we used Kane’s validity framework to guide logic and Messick’s framework to identify sources of evidence [13, 14, 16] Kane’s framework involves a deliberate focus on the purpose of the assessment (called the interpretation/use argument (IUA)), then examining which of four primary inferences are most important for the reliability of that particular assessment. Kane “does not encourage boundaryless validity investigation,” but rather encourages investigators to seek evidence of the most critical assumptions (for example, the weakest assumptions, the most controversial, or those that discriminate experts from non-experts) [16]. Scoring inferences relate to evidence that the single MCQ test score generated is a fair, accurate, and reproducible score. Generalization refers to how well the selected test items represent the universe sample of the testing domain. Extrapolation supports the inference that test performance reflects real-world performance, and implications supports the interpreted score to a specific use, decision, or action.

The IUA for our MCQ assessment is that trainee scores reflect their global knowledge and skill in reading and interpreting a genome report, such that scores can be used to identify non-genetic clinicians with a minimal level of knowledge and skill to read and interpret a pediatric genome report in clinical practice. Scores can also be used to distinguish trainees ready to discuss more advanced or nuanced concepts regarding genome reports or testing. Importantly, we did not seek evidence that the scores would identify those entrustable to discuss these results with families of children undergoing advanced genomic testing.

Guided by our IUA, we found the most critical assumptions related to scoring and generalization inferences. Next, we systematically developed the evidence related to Messick’s key validity sources, with particular attention to pieces of evidence supporting the scoring and generalization inferences.

Content evidence

Critical to both scoring and generalization inferences, content evidence refers to whether the material tested appropriately reflects the intended content in substance and distribution [16]. An iterative approach to written assessment was utilized to develop the MCQ test. As no current gold-standard exists for defining the educational needs for frontline, non-genetics and genomics clinicians who may request such advanced genetic testing, a team of experts, including a clinical geneticist (FPeV), a medical genetics fellow (WST), an education professional specializing in instructional design and eLearning development (SAJ), and pediatric clinicians with masters’ level education training (SB and SCM), iteratively defined the nine learning objectives most relevant to pediatric residents (Table 1). The team subsequently developed a 27-item test mapped roughly equally across the nine objectives. The test length was decided by team consensus, balancing desire for adequate psychometric performance with practical considerations such as the time commitment for a low-stakes test for clinical learners. Question stems and responses were then analyzed by two genomic specialists and the two education specialists to ensure clarity of questions, answers, and distractors, to reduce grammatical and stylistic errors, and to reduce risk of bias. The questions were adjusted based on expert feedback.

Response process

Response process evidence refers to evidence of data integrity such that errors associated with test administration are minimized [12].

To ensure interpretation of the stems and responses was congruent between test participants and the study team, cognitive interviews were conducted. Using an adapted cognitive interviewing test script developed by Krug [17], SAJ asked two field experts to evaluate the educational methods and content using a “think out loud” methodology and recorded the sessions. Experts included a genomics nurse with a Master’s of Science in Nursing Education and a former genetics professor with a Doctorate of Education (Ed. D.). The cognitive interviews began with an introduction clarifying the intent of the interview – to evaluate the educational methods and content and not the interviewee - and proceeded to capture stream-of-consciousness reactions to the content. Test quality control measures included use of a proctored testing environment, with explicit instructions to all groups not to use outside resources to answer the questions, as well as electronic scoring and score recording via the learning management system.

Relationship to other variables

Relation to other variables refers to the correlation of performance on this assessment with other indicators of knowledge and skills in the content area. We sought evidence of the test’s ability to discern between different levels of expertise and clinical experience of applied genomics. Therefore, we not only tested our trainees, but also those with formal genetics and genomics training whose primary profession centered on genetics and genomics, as well as board-certified clinicians with specialties outside of genetics/genomics but who regularly use genome reports in their clinical practice. We defined a priori different test-taking groups by which to evaluate test metrics (Table 2); these included experts (formally trained and practicing in the field of genetics/genomics), intermediate clinicians (practicing neonatologists not formally trained in but who have significant clinical exposure to genomics), intermediate learners (trainees also not formally trained in but who have some clinical exposure to genomics), and novices (trainees with no formal training and no clinical exposure to applied genomics). All study participants were volunteers solicited from the author’s professional and educational networks at our institution via email. To enhance receptivity of the project amongst all stakeholder groups, we developed two possible paths of exposure to the test based on the participant’s test-taking group. Experts and intermediate clinician volunteers completed the entire MCQ test, whereas learner groups were asked to complete a pre-test (composed of nine questions mapped 1:1 across the learning objectives), review a corresponding eLearning module we developed, and then complete a unique but parallel post-test (composed of the remaining 18 questions, mapped approximately 2:1 across the same learning objectives).

We chose this approach for two reasons. Presenting different questions in each test while maintaining equivalent content mapping across the learning objectives and similar item difficulty between the two tests guarded against immediate recall and practice effects and instead, tested application. Containing the entire learning and assessment experience to one hour using the split test approach and minimizing total time commitment was also important to education leadership given the busy clinical schedules of our trainees and already full educational calendar. Mean pre- and post-curriculum scores and standard deviations were calculated for learner groups, and mean scores for the entire test were calculated for clinician groups. Results were then compared within and across groups and effect size estimated using Cohen’s d.

Intervention for learner groups

The eLearning module presented to the learner groups (intermediate learners and novices) consisted of a mock-up 5-page Genome Report from Rady Children’s Institute for Genomic Medicine (RCIGM) converted into JPG files and loaded into Articulate Rise™. The team identified significant elements of the Genomic Report and used visual digital markers in the eLearning tool to mark those areas. The learner clicked the digital markers to learn more about each item and completed integrated learning activities throughout the learning module. Learning activities included scenarios, case studies, sorting activities, and basic recall, with feedback indicating where the learner could find where the original information is presented in the learning module. Two learning experts were interviewed to evaluate the quality of the eLearning design.

Internal structure

Internal structure includes evidence that scores within an assessment relate to one another as predicted by the intended use of the assessment. Internal consistency was measured using Cronbach’s alpha based on the expert and intermediate clinician responses only. Item analyses were conducted on data from these groups as well, including item difficulty. Discrimination index, D, and point biserial rpbis, were also examined as part of the quality control process. To reduce the effect of learning due to the educational intervention occurring between the pre- and post-tests scores, we did not use learner data in these analyses. Thus, we anticipated range restriction of the item analyses due to use of scores from more expert groups.

Consequences

Lastly, consequences refers to the evidence that “score interpretation and use leads to suitable positive and minimal negative consequences” [16]. We instructed all participants that this was a no-stakes test. Neither program leadership nor clinical rotation directors were granted access to test results, and the scores had no impact on trainees’ rotation evaluations. This test was not used to, and is not intended to, determine readiness for practice or to evaluate trainees readiness to practice or progress in their rotations, and the scores did not positively or negatively influence a trainee’s ability to participate in clinical care.

Results

Content evidence

Content evidence for the MCQ included an iterative development of course objectives defined by content experts attentive to the appropriate range and depth of skill and knowledge for the needs of pediatrics residents with no formal genomics training and little prior clinical exposure to reading genomic result reports [16]. Additional content evidence included test blueprinting to the objectives, item-writer qualifications, use of best-practice guidelines for MCQ questions, and iterative item refinement process to edit for clarity and cultural sensitivity.

Response process

Response process evidence supporting the scoring inference included quality control processes (electronic testing, proctored testing environments, written instructions provided to all test takers, and electronic scoring) and additional item refinement during cognitive interviews to improve item clarity of stems and responses [16]. One item (Q10) was modified from the original assessment due to expert feedback to improve its clarity. The sources of evidence serve as additional assurance that questions are interpreted as intended and are not subject to construct-irrelevant differences between examinees, such as differences in interpretation based on factors like language, age, or sex.

Relationship to other variables

Psychometric test data were analyzed by examinee groups (experts, intermediate clinicians, intermediate learners, and novices) (Table 2).

19 clinicians (experts and intermediate clinicians) took the full 27 question, whereas 34 learners (novices and intermediate learners) took the exam in two parts, with a 9 question pre-test and 18 question post-test of equivalent mapping to the objectives and similar in item difficulty (pre-test 0.86; post-test 0.88), but completely distinct in composition.

The test demonstrated good differentiation between both states of participant expertise and in pre/post-test differences, as shown in Fig. 2. Experts (n = 13) scored significantly higher than intermediate clinicians (n = 6) on the full exam (mean 94%, SD 5% vs. mean 70%, SD 15%, d = 2.56, 95% CI [2.51, 2.61]. Novice learners scored lower than intermediate learners in both the pre-test (novices 53%, SD 10% vs. intermediate learners 61%, SD 18%, d = 0.45) and the post-test (novices 81%, SD 16% vs. intermediate learners 85%, SD 8%, d = 0.71). Both learner subgroups showed large gains in pre-test to post-test scores (novices, d = 2.03, 95% CI [1.97, 2.09]; intermediate learners, d = 1.97, 95% CI [1.93, 2.01]).

Internal structure

Cronbach’s alpha was very high (0.9), suggesting a high degree of internal consistency. Item analyses are included in Supplemental Table 1. Of the 27 items included in the final assessment tool, item difficulty ranged from 0.63 to 1.0. 18 items (66%) had an item discrimination index above 0.20, indicating good ability to discriminate between high and low performing trainees. 24 items (88%) had a positive discrimination index and point biserial, and 16 items (60%) had a point biserial greater than 0.3, indicating good discrimination. The two items with poor discrimination (Q10: “True or False: Phenotype is an important factor for clinical interpretation of genome sequencing data” and Q18 “True or False: Genome re-analysis is indicated if a patient develops new significant phenotype findings”) were maintained in the final test despite their poor discrimination indices due to group consensus on the critical nature of their content.

Discussion

There is an increasing need for practitioners who are skilled in reading and interpreting genomic sequencing reports, but there are no published tools to sufficiently or reliably assess those skills. Guided by our interpretation/use argument (IUA) that the non-geneticist trainee’s score reflects their global knowledge and skill in reading and interpreting a genome report, our team of diverse experts created a new written assessment tool to advance the field of genomics education beyond self-assessment alone, introducing objective measures of trainee skill. In systematically seeking evidence for the most important assumptions underlying the defensible use of test scores, we have developed the validity argument that a non-geneticist trainee’s score on this written test accurately reflects their level of genomics sequencing report interpretation skill. The iterative process with subject matter expert and education expert guidance contributed to strong content and response process evidence, including robust learning objective development and test blueprinting, cognitive interviewing, and refining of the MCQ stems and responses. Evidence of internal structure was high with very high Cronbach’s alpha and strong test item statistics. Important evidence of total assessment score differences were found between participants with different levels of expertise and knowledge.

Without a validity argument appropriate for the stakes of the test, claims that learning has occurred are unsubstantiated. Whereas high-stakes assessments clearly demand substantial validity evidence, there is also value in developing the evidence for low-stakes, locally endorsed multiple choice tests to a reasonable degree. Failure to report validity evidence remains common in medical education research [18, 19] and is an ongoing barrier to advancing not only medical education, but that of education science. This may be because of differing mental models of validity amongst educators [20]. Yet, defining validity and constructing a validity argument need not be inaccessible to medical educators. Paul et al. have recently proposed a workshop strategy for teaching and learning how to construct a validity argument [21]. Our study shows that constructing a validity argument for a low-stakes test serves important functions in the assessment for learning paradigm, and, importantly, can be performed quickly without funding by following a series of logical steps in rational test design as described in Fig. 1.

Test score trends from this small validity study support the idea that for learners to become experts, they need both exposure to formal instruction and dedicated clinical training. Score differences were large between the most expert non-geneticist group and those in genetics/genomics (Cohen’s d = 2.56), confirming that even significant exposure in practice without foundational skill development is not a substitute for years of dedicated structured training and clinical mentorship. Large gains in scores were also apparent in the scores of the less experienced trainees. Together with item discrimination indices, these support the idea that this MCQ test can help educators truly distinguish amongst levels of trainee skill related to report interpretation [18].

In line with our study goal to develop sufficient evidence for our new tool, we utilized a split test design to evaluate expert clinicians differently than novices. From a pragmatic standpoint, we chose this approach to prioritize timely collection of data from participants across the spectrum of expertise and to be able to assess whether a brief content exposure would be associated with significant score changes from baseline in trainees. We valued trainees seeing an entirely new set of questions on their second pass, in order to attempt to avoid simple recall. To address practical constraints related to graduate medical education (GME) and enhance test security, we limited the assessment length, enabling completion of both the test and the learning module within a one-hour proctored educational setting. We attempted to mitigate risks measurement error related to the shorter assessments completed by the novice and intermediate learner groups by ensuring similar blueprinting and item difficulty between the pre- and post-tests. This approach may be adopted by others attempting to raise the bar in the quality of their own low-stakes assessments while maintaining a practical approach in GME.

Our study has limitations. The designer and participant groups were from a single institution, and the participant sample size is small. Adding additional content or education experts from other institutions may provide additional perspectives leading to modified learning objectives, which could influence test items or options. Conducting this study across institutions with different learner groups could provide additional evidence of generalizability beyond our local learner and clinician population. We calculated item discrimination based on the expert and intermediate clinician groups only, because 2/3 of the trainees’ data was confounded by the preceding learning intervention. While this makes the item difficulty and discrimination indices less, we felt it was an important practical consideration in facilitating this intervention in the real clinical learning environment. Finally, our study measures trainee abilities at the levels of Miller’s “knows” (fact recall) and “knows how” (interpretation and application) [15]. As such, this study is a foundational but incomplete step in improving frontline non-genetics providers training in such tasks. An important future direction for assessing knowledge and skill would be to expand the curricular goal and assessment focus to Miller’s level of “shows” (demonstrates in simulation) and “does” (perform independently in clinical practice), enabling assessment of trainee development in the outcomes of translating such knowledge into clinical care and conversations at the bedside.

Conclusion

Technological advances have changed what is possible in the field of genetics and genomics; such advances have also changed the frontline clinician’s role and educational needs. Reliable assessment underlies our abilities to draw defensible conclusions about our trainees. This work in developing the evidence for a tool that can reliably assess trainee skill in genome report interpretation represents a foundational first step towards improving non-geneticist training in this rapidly evolving field. Future work should expand this foundational assessment toward performance-based evaluations, ensuring clinicians can effectively translate genomics knowledge into patient care.
