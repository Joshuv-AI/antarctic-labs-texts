Lactiplantibacillus argentoratensis AGMB00912 alleviates diarrhea and promotes the growth performance of piglets during the weaning transition
Authors: Ki-Nam Yoon, Yo-Han Choi, Gi Beom Keum, Seo-Joon Yeom, Sang-Su Kim, Eun Sol Kim, Hyun Ju Park, Jo Eun Kim, Jong-Heum Park, Beom-Seok Song, Jong-Bang Eun, Seung-Hwan Park, Ju Huck Lee, Ju-Hoon Lee, Hyeun Bum Kim, Jae-Kyung Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreventing post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) in weaned piglets is a crucial challenge in the swine production industry. The stress of weaning, dietary shifts from maternal milk to solid feed, and environmental changes lead to decreased microbial diversity, increased pathogen abundance, and compromised intestinal integrity. We have previously identified Lactiplantibacillus argentoratensis AGMB00912 (LA) in healthy porcine feces, which demonstrated antimicrobial activity against pathogens and enhanced short-chain fatty acid production. This research aimed to evaluate the efficacy of LA strain supplementation as a strategy to inhibit PWD and enhance overall growth performance in weaned piglets.ResultsLA supplementation in weaned piglets significantly increased body weight gain, average daily gain, and average daily feed intake. It also alleviated diarrhea symptoms (diarrhea score and incidence). Notably, LA was found to enrich beneficial microbial populations (Lactobacillus, Anaerobutyricum, Roseburia, Lachnospiraceae, and Blautia) while reducing the abundance of harmful bacteria (Helicobacter and Campylobacter). This not only reduces the direct impact of pathogens but also improves the overall gut microbiota structure, thus enhancing the resilience of weaned piglets. LA treatment also promotes the growth of the small intestinal epithelial structure, strengthens gut barrier integrity, and increases short-chain fatty acid levels in the gut.ConclusionsThe study findings demonstrate the promising potential of LA in preventing PWD. Supplementation with the LA strain offers a promising feed additive for improving intestinal health and growth in piglets during the weaning transition, with the potential to significantly reduce the incidence and severity of PWD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03536-6.Keywords: Growth performance, Gut microbiota, Intestinal barrier integrity, Lactiplantibacillus argentoratensis, Post-weaning diarrhea, Short-chain fatty acid

Background

It is challenging to prevent post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) in the swine production cycle. During the weaning phase, piglets are abruptly separated from the mother sow, and maternal milk is replaced with solid feed containing cereals and high concentrations of crude protein [1]. These stressful conditions reduce microbial diversity and increase the proportions of Clostridium spp. and Escherichia coli [2–4], along with increased villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia, resulting in enhanced intestinal permeability [5, 6]. These intestinal disorders accelerate the infection potential by two major pathogenic microorganisms of PWD, enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) and Salmonella spp [7–9]. This reduces the feed intake efficiency, body weight (BW), and quality properties of the piglets, thus increasing mortality and economic losses in the modern pig industry [10–12]. Hence, it is critical to maintain a healthy intestinal environment, optimal epithelial barrier integrity, and balanced gut microbial composition during the crucial weaning phase for the ideal growth performance of the weaned piglets.

Antibiotics are commonly used in weaning diets to alleviate gut disorders and enhance growth performance by inhibiting the growth of harmful microorganisms. Nonetheless, since 2006, the European Union has mandated the use of alternative substances as growth promoters instead of antibiotics. This regulation was implemented to address the emergence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens and the presence of residual antibiotics in animal products [13]. Acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, known as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), are produced by gut bacteria through the fermentation of dietary fibers. These SCFAs play a crucial role in maintaining intestinal health in both humans and animals [14, 15]. The fermentation of dietary fiber can occur in either the upper or lower parts of the intestine, depending on the type of fiber and its solubility, which affects the fermentation time [16]. These SCFAs can modulate gastrointestinal pH and provide energy to the colon epithelial cells, resulting in epithelial proliferation [15, 17, 18]. They play a crucial role as regulators of host-microbiome interactions, contribute to defense against pathogenic microorganisms, and function in immunomodulation [19, 20]. The SCFAs produced can alter the intestinal environment, thereby directly and indirectly regulating intestinal pathogens [21]. Additionally, the adhesion and invasion of pathogenic bacteria are essential stages in the colonization of the host’s intestinal epithelial cells. However, SCFAs can weaken these processes by regulating specific genes of the pathogenic bacteria [22]. SCFAs have beneficial health effects in weaned piglets [23–25], and sodium butyrate may effectively ameliorate PWD in weaned piglets and enhance protein levels in tight junctions (TJs) of the colon [26]. In particular, SCFA-producing probiotics, such as Lactobacillus johnsonii L531, reduce the pathogenic microbial population and maintain intestinal SCFA levels in weaned piglets infected with Salmonella spp [27]. A probiotic mixture (Levilactobacillus brevis, Lactobacillus reuteri, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, Bacillus licheniformis, Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Enterococcus faecium, Saccharomyces cerevisiae) has also been shown to modulate gut microbial communities and improve SCFA levels in weaned piglets [28–30].

Lactiplantibacillus argentoratensis, previously classified as Lactobacillus argentoratensis, is a gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic bacterium known for its ability to perform both homo- and heterofermentation. This bacterium was initially isolated from sources such as cassava and maize and later reclassified under its current nomenclature in 2020 [31]. Notably, L. argentoratensis is capable of fermenting carbohydrates through the Embden–Meyerhoff–Parnas and phosphoketolase pathways, resulting in the production of important metabolites like SCFAs. These SCFAs are known to contribute to its probiotic effects [32]. We have previously isolated L. argentoratensis AGMB009122 (LA) from healthy porcine feces and demonstrated the presence of SCFA production-related genes using whole-genome sequencing. Our study findings further demonstrated that LA administration significantly improves SCFA production, enhances gut barrier function, and maintains the gut microbiota composition in weaning mice challenged with ETEC K88ac [33]. The current study aimed to evaluate the hypothesis that LA supplementation could alleviate diarrhea and promote growth performance by improving the SCFA levels and gut barrier integrity in piglets during the weaning transition.

Methods

Animal feeding and management

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Animal Science in Cheonan, Republic of Korea (approval No. NIAS 2019 − 408) and were conducted at the same institution. The composition and nutritional values of the basal diet are presented in Table 1. Eighteen crossbred piglets, aged 28 days and consisting of a mix of Duroc, Landrace, and Large Yorkshire breeds, were bred and housed at the National Institute of Animal Science. These piglets were weaned and housed indoors on rigid plastic slatted floors. The initial temperature in the nursery was set at approximately 27 °C and was consistently reduced by 1 °C/week. The relative humidity was maintained at approximately 65%, with a 5% variance, and the piglets were exposed to natural light conditions. Body weight and average daily gain (ADG) were recorded for each piglet at the beginning and end of the 14-day trial through individual weigh-ins. At the conclusion of the trial, the piglets were euthanized using T-61 (MSD, Rahway, NJ, USA) in a humane manner, following recommended protocols to ensure humane endpoints. Samples of tissue and gut contents were collected immediately after exsanguination. Experimental groups included a baseline diet control, and a group administered the same diet with the addition of 10 mL/day 1 × 10⁹ colony forming units/mL of the probiotic, LA, derived from the feces of rapidly growing pigs. The pigs had ad libitum access to food and water throughout the experiment. The diet was designed to fulfill or surpass the nutritional guidelines recommended by the National Research Council [34]. Furthermore, all procedures were conducted in accordance with the appropriate guidelines and regulations, in compliance with ethical standards for animal research. Each experiment was strictly conducted according to established guidelines, with a particular focus on adhering to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Diarrhea incidence

This scoring system was modified slightly from the method described by Oh et al. (2020) [35]. Diarrhea incidence was assessed using a fecal scoring system throughout the study duration. Scores ranged from zero for normal stool consistency, one for soft feces, two for mild diarrhea, and three for severe diarrhea. Each animal’s fecal score was assessed at 09:00 h for 2 weeks. The average of these daily scores was calculated for each piglet to establish the final cumulative diarrhea incidence.

SCFAs

A fresh stool sample was collected from each piglet via gentle rectal stimulation and immediately stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Consistent with the methodology described by Oh et al. [29], 1 g of each fecal sample was extracted, accurately weighed, and homogenized with 2 mL of sterile deionized water. This mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min, resulting in a clear supernatant. This supernatant was further processed by adding a metaphosphoric acid solution at a 9:1 ratio, followed by an additional centrifugation step at 3000 × g for 10 min. The resulting clear liquid was filtered using a 0.45 μm membrane filter before gas chromatographic analysis. To quantify SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, a YL 6500 gas chromatograph equipped with a TRB-G43 capillary column with an inner diameter of 0.53 mm, a length of 30 m, and a film thickness of 3 μm was employed, alongside a flame ionization detector. The thermal gradient was initiated at 70 °C and increased to 150 °C over 3 min, with the injector and detector temperatures maintained at 250 °C and the injection volume set at 1 µL.

Histomorphological analysis

For assessing intestinal morphology, samples of mucosal and histological tissue were collected from the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. These were then promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at − 80 °C. For histological examination, approximately 5 cm long tissue sections from each intestinal segment were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, preserved in 70% ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin, as per the protocol established by Lee et al. [36]. Slides prepared from these sections were examined to count and measure five representative villi and crypts per segment. Crypt depth was measured from the base of the villus to the deepest point of the crypt, while villus height was recorded from the tip of the villus to the junction with the crypt. Intestinal morphology was evaluated using an Olympus Vanox-S Microscope, and the SPOT imaging software was utilized for detailed image analysis.

Inflammatory cytokine and immunoglobulin levels

The levels of serum cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-10, and interferon (IFN)-gamma, and immunoglobulins (Ig), including IgA and IgM were determined using a porcine-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (R&D systems, Minnneapolis, MN, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antioxidant index analysis

The colon tissue was washed using cold PBS to remove any blood cells or clots. The colon tissue (100 mg) was then homogenized in 1 mL assay buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. Supernatants were collected and stored at − 80 ℃ for further analysis. Antioxidant indices, including catalase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione, in the colon were measured using commercial kits (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Blood hemato-biochemical profile analysis

The hematologic profile, including white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), and hemoglobin (Hb) levels in whole blood was analyzed using the Drew Hemavet 950FS analyzer (Drew Scientific, Oxford, CT, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Biochemical profiling related to serum levels of glucose, total protein, triglyceride, albumin, creatine, total cholesterol, blood urea nitrogen, glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase, glutamic pyruvic transaminase, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) was performed using a DRI-CHEM NX500i automated clinical chemistry analyzer (FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan).

RNA extraction and real-time qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from intestinal tissues using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoPhotometer (Implen, Munich, Germany). The isolated RNA was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the AccuPower® CycleScript RT premix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The RT-qPCR reaction mixture contained 10 µL of SYBR® Green PCR master mix, 2 µL of primers (5 pmol), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 6 µL of nuclease-free water in a 96-well plate. The RT-qPCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 40 s. The relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2–ΔΔCT comparative method. The expression data were normalized to the reference gene, β-actin. The primers used are presented in Additional file 1.

Metagenomic analysis

Fecal samples were directly obtained from the rectums of pigs on day 1 (feeding initiation) and day 14 (end of feeding). Mucosal samples were also obtained from day 14 piglets by scraping the inner side of the colon with a scalpel blade after the pigs were euthanized. Total DNA was extracted from 200 mg of each collected feces and mucosa samples following the manufacturer’s manual of the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen). Only DNA samples with an OD260/280 ratio between 1.85 and 2.15, measured using a Colibri Microvolume Spectrometer (Titertek-Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany), were used for further analysis. A V5-V6 targeting universal primer set (799 F-mod6/1114R) was used to prepare 16 S rRNA amplicons under the following PCR conditions: one cycle of 98 ℃ for 3 min, 30 cycles of 98 ℃ for 10 s, 57 ℃ for 5 s, 68 ℃ for 1 s, and one cycle of 72 ℃ for 5 min. After 16 S rRNA PCR, the amplicons were purified using Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Next-generation sequencing was performed using the Illumina MiSeq System at Cancerrop (Seoul, Republic of Korea). The MicrobiomeHelper software package was used to identify gut bacteria and Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2) was used to analyze microbial diversity from the 16 S rRNA amplicon sequences [37, 38]. The naïve Bayesian classifier-trained Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) was used as a reference for taxonomic assignment [39].

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance was calculated using the Student’s two-tailed t-test within the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS 22.0; IBM, Armnok, NY, USA). Results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and differences between means were considered significant at p < 0.05. The gut microbiome parameters were calculated using GraphPad Prism version 8.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). A Kruskal–Wallis H-test was used to confirm the data from alpha diversity analysis, including observed features, Shannon, Chao1, and Simpson indices. Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) in QIIME2 was used to determine statistical similarities in the microbial community compositions among the four groups. To identify significant differences in the relative abundance of microbial taxa among the four groups, the Tukey–Krammer multiple comparison test (two-way analysis of variance) was used in the Statistical Analysis of metagenomic Profiles (STAMP) software v2.1.3. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05.

Results

LA supplementation improves growth performance and alleviates diarrhea in weaning pigs

In this study, LA supplementation enhanced the overall performance of the weaning piglets. As shown in Fig. 1a–d, there was a significant increase in the final body weight (12.31 vs. 10.44 kg, p = 0.0109), body weight gain (4.26 vs. 3.01 kg, p = 0.0043), average daily gain (304.14 vs. 214.93 g, p = 0.0044), and average daily feed intake (ADFI, 436.00 vs. 337.39 g, p = 0.0029) in the LA-treated groups compared to those in the control groups. However, a significant difference in the gain-to-feed intake ratio (G: F) was not observed between the control and LA groups (p = 0.1809). The diarrhea scores were significantly decreased in the LA-supplemented group compared to the control group both during the periods of 1–7 days and 8–14 days (p = 0.0088 and 0.0054, respectively; Fig. 1g–h). Moreover, the frequency of diarrhea was effectively reduced (14.29 vs. 54.29% for 1–7 days and 0.00 vs. 15.71% for 8–14 days). These results indicate that LA alleviates the symptoms of diarrhea in weaning pigs, thus improving their growth performance.

The LA diet modifies the levels of immune cytokines and antioxidant activity in the blood of weaning piglets

WBC and RBC counts and Hb levels were similar between the control and LA supplementation groups during the weaning periods (p > 0.05; Fig. 2a–c). Moreover, in the blood biochemical profile, only LDH levels were significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the LA supplementation group than those in the control group; the other parameters did not exhibit significant differences between the two groups (Additional file 2). The serum cytokine and immunoglobulin levels of the weaned piglets are shown in Fig. 2d–g. The TNF-α levels were significantly decreased (p < 0.05) in the LA group compared to those in the control piglets after 14 days. However, there was no significant difference (p > 0.05) in IL-6, IgM, and IgA levels between the LA and control groups. IL-8, IL-10, and IFN-γ were not detected. The abundance of porcine beta-defensin proteins in the colon tissue was assessed using RT-qPCR (Fig. 3). Dietary LA supplementation significantly upregulated the relative mRNA expression levels of pBD2, pBD3, pBD114, pBD129, PG1-5, and PEP2C in colon tissues of LA groups (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the Toll-like receptor (TLR) 2 gene expression levels in the colon were upregulated in the LA groups. These results indicate that LA can modulate the immune response in the intestines of weaned piglets.

LA supplementation promotes SCFA production in weaning pigs

SCFAs produced by microorganisms via fermentation in the intestines play a crucial role in digestion and nutrient absorption [30]. We found that LA treatment promoted acetic acid (26.97 vs. 22.71 µmol/g; p = 0.0006) and butyric acid (9.91 vs. 6.06 µmol/g; p < 0.0001) production in cecum tissues (Table 2). Additionally, in fecal samples, LA treatment demonstrated significant increases in the acetic acid (27.25 vs. 22.83 µmol/g; p = 0.0003), butyric acid (9.95 vs. 1.19 µmol/g; p < 0.0001), and isovaleric acid (1.94 vs. 1.67 µmol/g; p < 0.0001) contents. However, the levels of propionic, isobutyric, and valeric acids remained similar in both the control and LA groups. Our results demonstrated that dietary supplementation with LA influenced SCFA synthesis in the cecum and feces.

LA supplementation enhances the gut barrier integrity of weaned piglets

Efficient nutrient absorption is influenced by the morphology of the small intestinal mucosa. In this study, we investigated the effects of LA on the morphology of the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum). Compared to the basal diet, LA supplementation significantly increased villi height of the duodenum (438.47 vs. 401.70 μm; p = 0.0423) and ileum (342.78 vs. 320.40 μm; p = 0.0130; Fig. 5). However, there was no significant difference observed in the villus height of the duodenum and crypt depth of all intestinal tissues between the control and LA groups (p > 0.05). Nevertheless, the villus height to crypt depth ratio (V: C) in the jejunum and ileum significantly increased on LA supplementation compared with the control group (p = 0.0031 and p = 0.0147, respectively). These results suggest that adding LA to the diet may help stabilize the structure of the intestines and improve their development. This, in turn, could enhance nutrient absorption and overall growth performance (Fig. 6).

Gut microbiota composition

A total of 8,783,278 reads were generated using Illumina MiSeq sequencing, ranging from 94,559 to 222,489 sequence reads per sample. To improve downstream statistical analysis, we filtered out sequences with an average Phred quality score of less than 27. The filtered sequences were denoized, and the chimera was removed after trimming with sequence length 200 using the deblur workflow. The alpha diversity indices, as indicated by species richness (Observed features and Chao1) and evenness (Shannon and Simpson), represented the bacterial diversity within the microbial communities of feces and colon mucus (Table 3). Observed features and Chao1 values of the colon mucus were significantly lower in the LA group (259.56 ± 33.74, 274.86 ± 39.47) than those in the control group (324.89 ± 70.49, 349.69 ± 82.14), indicating a significant difference in bacterial richness between the two groups. However, no significant difference in fecal microbial diversity was observed between the control and LA groups on days 1 and 14 (Table 3). ANOSIM was conducted using both Weighted and Unweighted UniFrac distance matrices, and the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plots were generated based on these matrices using R to represent beta diversity (Fig. 7). PCoA plots showed the distinct clustering of fecal microbial community structures from those of colon mucus (Fig. 7). Qualitative assessment showed age-based distinct clustering of microbial community structures in pig fecal samples (Fig. 7b), which were similar to the quantitative assessment between days 0 and 14 (Fig. 7a). The taxonomic classification of 16 S rRNA gene sequences was performed using the RDP classifier, to describe the composition and changes in the microbial community in the feces and colon mucus among weaning piglets (Fig. 8). At the phylum level, the phyla Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes were commonly dominant in feces and colon mucus, regardless of the LA treatment (Fig. 8a). The abundance of phyla Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes within the fecal microbiome changed over time. The phylum Bacteroidetes in feces decreased from 53.03 to 44.17% in the control group and from 60.09 to 47.04% in the LA group, whereas Firmicutes increased from 32.42 to 44.97% in the control group and from 31.38 to 46.51% in the LA group. In the colon mucus, the phylum Campilobacterota was the third most abundant in both the control (21.56%) and LA (10.71%) groups. In feces, the relative abundance of the phylum Spirochaetes decreased from 5.07 to 2.11% in the control group, whereas it increased from 0.21 to 1.23% in the LA group. Unclassified bacteria represented less than 1% of all the groups (Fig. 8a). At the family level, the predominant bacteria in both feces and colon mucus were Prevotellaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Bacteroidaceae, and Clostridiaceae 1. The families Prevotellaceae and Bacteroidaceae were the two most abundant families within the phylum Bacteroidetes (Fig. 8b). The relative abundance of these two families in the feces decreased as pigs aged, which coincided with the decreased abundance of Bacteroidetes at the phylum level. Two families, Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae, were abundant within the phylum Firmicutes in the pig gut microbiota. An increase in the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae in feces aligned with the overall increase in Firmicutes (Fig. 8a, b), whereas the relative abundance in the colon mucus of the LA group was significantly higher than that in the control group (p < 0.05). The families Helicobacteraceae, Campylobacteraceae, and Pseudomonadaceae were specifically dominant only in colon mucus samples of the control and LA groups (Fig. 8b). At the genus level, 104 and 106 genera were identified from feces and colon mucus, respectively (Fig. 8c). The genera Prevotella, Clostridium sensu stricto, Oscillibacter, Roseburia, and Eubacterium were dominant in all the samples, and the relative abundance of each taxon represented more than 1% of the total sequences. Prevotella was the most abundant genus within the family Prevotellaceae (Fig. 8c). The genera Clostridium sensu stricto, Oscillibacter, Roseburia, and Eubacterium belong to the phylum Firmicutes. STAMPs were used to analyze significant differences between fecal and colon mucus microbial compositions according to LA treatment (Fig. 8d–k). The genus Pseudoramibacter (control 0.05% vs. LA 0.17%, feces at day 14) and Anaerobutyricum (control 0.31% vs. LA 3.26%, colon mucus at day 14) showed significantly higher relative abundances in the LA group than those in the control group (Fig. 8e, f). In both feces and colon mucus, the genus Treponema was dominant in the control group, whereas Lactobacillus and Roseburia were more dominant in the LA group on day 14 (Fig. 8g, h,i). The genera Helicobacter and Campylobacter were mainly identified in the colon mucus and were less abundant in the LA group (Fig. 8j, k).

Discussion

We have previously identified the impact of LA isolated from healthy swine on growth performance and improving intestinal barrier integrity in weaning mice (data not published). These findings suggest LA as a potential alternative to antibiotics for alleviating PWD in piglets. Growth performance is a crucial indicator in swine production and is influenced by multiple stresses in the weaning phase [1, 28]. After separation from the mother sow, low feed intake and high diarrhea incidence decrease the body weight gain (BWG) and G: F, which negatively affects pig growth performance [40]. In our study, compared with the controls, the LA-supplemented weaning piglets showed significantly increased BWG, ADG, and ADFI (Table 2). The administration of probiotic supplements (such as Bacillus subtilis, Clostridium butyricum, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Lactobacillus casei, and Lactobacillus plantarum) effectively promotes growth performance by improving BWG, ADG, and ADFI [41]. PWD management significantly impacts growth performance throughout the pig’s lifetime. After weaning, piglets frequently encounter a decrease in feed intake, which can lead to anorexia [42]. Madec et al. [43] identified a strong correlation between low feed intake during the first week after weaning and the risk of disease occurrence during the post-weaning period. In this research, we found that LA supplementation can alleviate diarrhea symptoms by improving feed intake (Table 2). PWD-related mortality may reach 30% over 1–2 months among infected weaned pigs [44]. Therefore, these findings suggest that LA treatment during the weaning phase can reduce economic losses in the pig industry.

Furthermore, dietary supplementation with LA significantly decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine (TNF-α) levels and increased antioxidant indices (superoxide dismutase, glutathione) in weaning piglets. In general, the withdrawal of maternal milk supply in weaning piglets impacts the development of both innate and adaptive immunity [45] The upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β) disrupts gut barrier integrity and increases the permeability of gut epithelial cells during the weaning phase [46]. Furthermore, intestinal inflammation is related to oxidative stress. The secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines is attributed to the downregulated cellular redox status, which can further damage intestinal function [47]. Therefore, improving antioxidant activity plays a key role in the healthy growth and development of weaning piglets. In the present study, superoxide dismutase and glutathione levels increased in the LA supplementation groups, suggesting that LA treatments boost antioxidant activity and alleviate intestinal inflammation in weaning piglets. Consistent with our findings, Wang et al. [48] reported that Lactobacillus plantarum ZLP001 treatment improves antioxidant capacity in weaning piglets. TLR2 is an important superfamily of pattern-recognition receptors and plays a vital function in host immune regulation by probiotics [49]. TLR2 recognizes cell wall components of probiotics (lipoteichoic acid, peptidoglycan, and lipoproteins), which induce the production of host defense peptides (HDP), thereby improving the intestinal microbiota and promoting immune responses [50]. We observed increased mRNA levels of TLR2, pBD2, pBD3, PG1-5, and PEP2C genes upon LA treatment in the colon tissues of weaning piglets (Fig. 3). Our results support the proposition by Wang et al. [51] that Lactobacillus plantarum ZLP001 may induce porcine HDP expression and secretion through TLR2 recognition. Hence, our findings indicated that LA effectively alleviates post-weaning-induced intestinal inflammation as probiotics.

SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are metabolic products mainly produced through the microbial fermentation of dietary fiber, resistant starch, and protein in the small intestine, cecum, and colon [52]. Here, the acetate and butyrate levels in cecum and fecal samples significantly increased (p < 0.05) on LA supplementation. Lu et al. [28] reported that probiotic supplementation could upregulate SCFA levels in the fecal samples of weaning pigs. These results indicate that LA can promote SCFA production in the intestine. Acetate synthesized from acetyl-CoA during glycolysis can enzymatically convert into butyrate via butyryl-CoA: acetyl-CoA transferase [53], and acetates are essential for several biological activities, including lipid metabolism, energy homeostasis, and appetite modulation via regulating gut–brain neural circuits [54]. Furthermore, acetate produced by Bifidobacteria can protect from infection against Escherichia coli O157:H7 and modulate the permeability of intestinal epithelial cells in mice [55]; acetate also exhibits protective effects against inflammation by regulating the levels of TJ-related proteins in porcine intestinal epithelial cells [56]. Butyrate, another important SCFA, plays a vital role in providing energy to gut epithelial cells [57] through PPAR-γ signaling activation. Coordination with regulatory T cells stimulates epithelial cells and promotes the metabolism of colonocytes [58], thus improving immunomodulation and alleviating intestinal inflammation [59]. Similar to acetate, butyrate decreases intestinal permeability and enhances intestinal barrier integrity [60]. This result is consistent with our finding that LA treatment can improve villus height and V: C ratio in the small intestine of weaning piglets (Fig. 5).

The gut microbiome interacts with the host (i.e., pig) in various ways and maintains intestinal health, prevents infection, and improves growth performance [61]. Cell components and products of intestinal microorganisms, including exopolysaccharides, interact to influence the immune system and metabolism of the host. In addition, the fermentative metabolism of undigested food and host-derived products in the gut microbial community also significantly influences the host [62]. These microbial metabolites not only enhance host immunity but also induce overall changes in the gut microbial composition through alterations in the intestinal environment (such as pH) and cross-feeding interactions among different microorganisms [63, 64]. Metagenome analysis of the 16 S rRNA genes provided insights into the effects of LA on improving health in post-weaning piglets. In this study, the observed features and Chao1 index of microbial communities confirmed that LA supplementation significantly reduced species richness in the colon mucus relative to the control group while increasing the evenness of microbial communities (Table 3). This is consistent with the findings of Wang et al. that L. casei ZX633 supplementation improves intestinal health, reduces richness, and increases evenness [65]. Overall, these results suggest that LA increased bacterial evenness by selectively inhibiting the growth of certain bacteria. Beta diversity analysis showed differences in microbial community structures between colon mucus and feces, whereas there was no significant difference between colon mucus and feces regardless of LA supplementation (Fig. 6). Consistent with other studies, our study identified Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes as the most abundant phyla in pig feces (Fig. 7a). However, LA supplementation caused bacterial shifts at the genus level (Fig. 7c). The genus Pseudoramibacter was significantly abundant in the feces of the LA group compared to that of the control group (Fig. 7c). Although the exact role of the genus Pseudoramibacter has not been elucidated, genome analysis has revealed its potential to use trehalose, starch, and cellulose as carbohydrate substrates resulting in pyruvate production [66]. Pyruvic acid is an important cellular molecule; it is oxidized in the mitochondria and fuels the tricarboxylic acid cycle by donating electrons to the respiratory chain [67]. The genus Anaerobutyricum, which was significantly abundant in the colon mucus of the LA group compared to that in the control group, is a member of the family Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 7d). Anaerobutyricum can produce butyrate from acetate, D- and L-lactate [68]. Our data showed that the genera Lactobacillus and Roseburia were more abundant, whereas the genus Treponema was less prevalent in the LA groups than those in the control group (Fig. 7h, i, and g). Lactobacillus possesses antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects. Furthermore, although excessive lactate can cause acidosis, in a healthy colon, it is consumed through cross-feeding by bacteria that utilize lactate as an energy source [69]. Lactate stimulates SCFA production to improve intestinal health and growth [70], whereas Treponema can be a potential pathogen in skin lesions and dysentery in pigs [71]. Riboulet-Bisson et al. [72] showed that Lactobacilli can inhibit the growth of Treponema. The genus Roseburia, along with the genus Faecalibacterium, is a representative butyrate-producing bacterium [73]. It has been observed that Roseburia significantly correlates with decreased butyrate levels in feces when a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet is consumed [74]. It is a key member of the intestinal microbial community of pigs. The SCFA produced by the bacteria not only gets directly absorbed and utilized by the host cells but also directly impacts the microbial composition within the intestine. First, the reduction in intestinal pH due to SCFA production helps prevent the overgrowth of pH-sensitive pathogenic bacteria such as Enterobacteriaceae and Clostridia [75, 76]. Furthermore, SCFA production can inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria like Salmonella and Escherichia coli by regulating the expression of toxic genes and disrupting cell binding processes [77]. The genera Helicobacter and Campylobacter, which are present in the gastrointestinal mucosa of animals, were mainly detected in the colon mucus [78]. The genus Helicobacter can cause reduced weight gain and gastritis in pigs [79]. The genus Campylobacter, a commensal gut bacterium, is commonly transmitted through the fecal-oral route from sow to piglets and induces enteritis in colostrum-deficient piglets [80]. Our results showed that LA supplementation increased the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, Anaerobutyricum, and Roseburia, and reduced the abundance of harmful bacteria, including Helicobacter and Campylobacter species. Sodium butyrate, a major component of SCFAs, can inhibit Helicobacter pylori growth and reduce H. pylori-induced inflammation [81]. In summary, orally administered LA colonized the intestine, generating lactate and leading to an increase in lactate-utilizing bacteria through cross-feeding to produce SCFA. This resulted in a decrease in the relative abundance of pathogens and a shift in the overall composition of the intestinal microbial community. This suggests that LA could increase the abundance of intestinal SCFA-producing bacteria, resulting in anti-inflammatory effects and improving intestinal health [54].

LEfSe analysis was performed to verify the statistical significance of differentially abundant bacteria between the control and LA supplementation groups. In this study, LA treatment significantly decreased the abundance of Gammaproteobacteria, Epsilonproteobacteria, and Desulfovibrionia at the class level and the proportions of Helicobacter and Campylobacter at the genus level (p < 0.05). Gammaproteobacteria is a diverse class of gram-negative pathogenic bacteria, including Enterobacter, Escherichia, Klebsiella, Salmonella, Shigella, and Yersinia [82]. Additionally, Epsilonproteobacteria consist of a few known genera, mainly the Helicobacter and Campylobacter species [83]. Therefore, our study demonstrates that LA administration significantly reduces the abundance of these harmful microorganisms in piglets during the weaning transition. In addition, LA supplementation significantly increased the proportion of Actinomycetota, Lachnospiraceae, and Prevotella (p < 0.05). The Actinomycetota phylum, which includes the genus Bifidobacterium, is recognized for its role in the health of mammalian and human intestines. Members of this phylum enhance gut health and immunity, increase antioxidant activity, and reduce pathogen loads, particularly in weaned piglets. Actinomycetota can contribute to maintaining gut homeostasis and modulating the intestinal microbiota, thereby promoting growth performance in young animals [84, 85]. The Lachnospiraceae family, within which the Lacrimispora genus has been reclassified, is notable for its genetic capability to produce butyrate through the acetyl-coenzyme A pathway [86]. Holst et al. [87] reported that Lachnospiraceae positively correlates with increased levels of acetate and butyrate in feces, thus contributing to a healthy gut microbiome. Blautia, an anaerobic bacterium, plays an important role in alleviating inflammatory diseases, preventing gut inflammation, and maintaining the intestinal microbial community by producing SCFAs [88]. Minamida et al. [89] suggested that Blautia can decrease cecal pH and increase SCFA levels in rats. Our findings suggest that LA supplementation plays a crucial role in the gut microbiota of weaned piglets by decreasing the proportion of harmful microorganisms (Helicobacter and Campylobacter) and enriching host-beneficial bacteria (Lactobacillus, Anaerobutyricum, Roseburia, Lachnospiraceae, and Blautia).

Conclusions

In summary, our study has revealed encouraging findings, demonstrating that LA supplementation may not only prevent PWD but also enhance SCFA production. Additionally, LA potentially improves key growth metrics, such as body weight, while also contributing to intestinal integrity and a balanced gut microbiota. Thus, LA supplementation could be considered a viable strategy for enhancing the health and productivity of weaned piglets. The study findings have potential applications in improving animal welfare and reducing the economic impact of post-weaning disorders in the swine industry. However, further research will be crucial to fully elucidate the mechanisms by which LA influences gut health.
