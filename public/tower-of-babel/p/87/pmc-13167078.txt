Insights into the role of the shock index and its derivatives in triage
Authors: Da-Sen Chien, Tsung-Hsien Wu, Chi-Yuan Liu, Chien-Hsing Wang, Chia-Hung Tsai, Giou-Teng Yiang, Meng-Yu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTrauma is a considerable global public health concern. In Taiwan, the Taiwan Trauma Registry was established in 2005 through collaboration between the Formosa Association for the Surgery of Trauma and the government. However, the registry has not yet achieved full national integration, which limits its utility for research and policymaking. To address this gap, we integrated data from the four hospitals of the Tzu Chi Medical Foundation, which are located in northern, central, southern, and eastern Taiwan. This newly developed Tzu Chi Trauma Registry reflects the diverse characteristics of regional emergency medical service systems and provides a foundation for optimizing trauma care models and developing targeted injury prevention and clinical intervention programs. This review summarizes current tools for prehospital and in-hospital triage, focusing on the shock index and its derivatives. This review also discusses the predictive value of combining hemodynamic indices with neurological assessment tools with respect to clinical outcomes in patients with trauma, including predicting the need for transfusion, the need for massive transfusion protocol activation, and mortality. By performing a retrospective analysis of the Tzu Chi Trauma Registry, we aim to improve the accuracy of triage decision-making by reducing overtriage and undertriage, thereby enhancing the quality of trauma care and improving patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Trauma has become a critical public health concern worldwide. Unintentional injury is the seventh leading cause of death in Taiwan. In 2024, unintentional injuries accounted for 6924 deaths, with transport-related accidents being the leading cause, resulting in 2932 (42.3%) deaths, followed by falls, which caused 1654 deaths (23.9%) [1]. Most injuries are preventable, and the effectiveness of injury reduction efforts is mainly determined by the appropriateness of prevention strategies, timely access to medical care, the quality of trauma systems, and efficient resource allocation [234]. To effectively address the growing burden of injury, a clear understanding of its risk factors and the predictors of its incidence, associated disability, and associated mortality is necessary. However, most evidence in the trauma epidemiology literature is derived from high-income Western countries [5678], and therefore, it may not be applicable to Taiwan.

Trauma care systems differ substantially in Western and Asian countries, including with respect to resource distribution and challenges in timely access to care. In addition, emergency medical service (EMS) systems vary widely among nations and are often adapted to local conditions, which increases the difficulty of developing a one-size-fits-all approach. Gaining a comprehensive understanding of the trauma landscape in Taiwan is therefore essential to designing effective prevention strategies and improving outcomes for patients with trauma in the region.

TAIWAN TRAUMA REGISTRY: CURRENT STATUS

A trauma registry is among the fundamental components of any comprehensive trauma care system. In Taiwan, the Formosa Association for the Surgery of Trauma (FAST), in collaboration with the Bureau of Health Promotion of the Department of Health, launched the first version of the Taiwan Trauma Registry Software in 2005. A second version was released in 2006, with this version offering simplified data entry for external causes of injury (E-codes), a standalone operating system, and batch-upload capability to the bureau’s central server, and introduced with the goal of creating a national trauma database. Although the FAST made initial progress in standardizing trauma data collection, the system remains at an early developmental stage and lacks the breadth required in a fully integrated registry. For example, critical variables are missing, particularly those capturing the entire continuum of trauma care – from prehospital emergency response to definitive in-hospital treatment and long-term postdischarge outcomes, such as survival and functional status. In addition, the registry is not linked with data from other relevant governmental agencies, such as the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, the National Police Agency, and the National Health Insurance Administration. Therefore, hospitals maintain their own data systems, often focusing on accreditation requirements in maintaining the systems rather than on injury prevention or policy development. Furthermore, the Taiwanese government has not yet allocated dedicated resources to establish a truly national trauma registry. By contrast, countries such as Japan and the United States have established detailed, integrated trauma-related data systems. Because it has no such database, Taiwan lacks the comprehensive datasets necessary for epidemiological analyses and effective injury prevention planning.

TZU CHI TRAUMA REGISTRY

Against this backdrop, we integrated the trauma assessment databases of four hospitals under the Tzu Chi Medical Foundation to establish a standardized trauma registry supported by a newly implemented hospital information system. This registry incorporates additional key variables and includes data regarding patients with trauma from the northern, central, southern, and eastern regions of Taiwan, effectively covering diverse EMS systems nationwide. Through this registry, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview of trauma care in Taiwan and analyze regional disparities. Compared with single-hospital databases, the Tzu Chi registry provides a larger sample size and ensures broader geographic representation, enabling investigation of epidemiological trends, identification of risk factors, evaluation of clinical and policy interventions, and generation of evidence that can guide the development of an optimal trauma care model for Taiwan. Our long-term objective is to obtain evidence that can inform the formulation of strategic injury prevention policies and clinical intervention programs tailored to Taiwan’s specific needs.

TRIAGE

Triage classification is a critical component of prehospital trauma care and a key pillar of the EMS system [9]. To strengthen prehospital trauma care in Taiwan, the Ministry of Health and Welfare incorporated trauma care into the “Hospital Emergency Medical Capability Accreditation” program in 2009. This program evaluates hospitals on the basis of their emergency care capacity and the continuity of trauma care quality, classifying eligible hospitals as either advanced or intermediate emergency responsibility centers. Medical services are then delivered in accordance with each institution’s designated level to improve the quality of trauma care. The primary objective of this program is to ensure that patients with severe trauma are transported to the appropriate facility at the right time and treated by the appropriate team, reducing trauma-related mortality [1011]. Taiwan currently employs a binary prehospital triage system that classifies cases as either “critical” or “noncritical.” Upon arrival at the scene, emergency medical technicians (EMTs) examine vital signs, injury sites and mechanisms, and specific circumstances to determine whether the patient requires immediate treatment or emergency transport. On the basis of the urgency of the condition, the EMTs perform triage and transport the patient to an appropriate medical facility. However, this system frequently underestimates the severity of patient conditions. The current binary triage system in Taiwan lacks both sufficient discriminatory power and practical applicability, and consequently, it is inadequate to guide transport and referral decision-making [12]. Therefore, numerous triage scoring systems have been proposed to help EMTs perform more accurate severity assessments. These systems ensure that patients with trauma are classified appropriately, improving the quality of care and clinical outcomes.

SHOCK INDEX

Hemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable death in trauma, accounting for approximately 39% of all preventable or potentially preventable deaths. When hemorrhage occurs, aggressive transfusion resuscitation is crucial, particularly during the “golden hour.” In prehospital settings, rapid and accurate identification of patients requiring transfusion enables hospitals to prepare blood products in advance, minimize transfusion delays after arrival, and ultimately reduce mortality. Accordingly, the shock index (SI) has been proposed as one of the most fundamental triage scoring systems for detecting hemorrhagic shock.

The SI, defined as the ratio of heart rate (HR) to systolic blood pressure (SBP), is a simple physiological parameter that reflects the balance between circulatory demand and perfusion. Compared with the absolute value of HR or SBP alone, the SI is a more sensitive indicator of early hypovolemic shock, particularly in patients whose vital signs may initially appear normal because of compensatory mechanisms. A previous study observed that in healthy blood donors, the mean SI substantially increased after standing for 1 and 5 min. Although HR and SBP also changed markedly, their values remained within the normal range [13]. Moreover, even in patients with prehospital SBP of >90 mmHg, an abnormal SI was strongly associated with activation of the massive transfusion protocol (MTP). The risk of MTP activation increased 1.5-, 5.5-, and 8-fold when the SI value ranged from >0.9 to 1.1, from 1.1 to 1.3, and exceeded 1.3, respectively. A high SI is associated with hemodynamic instability and adverse outcomes in patients with trauma; thus, in clinical practice, a cutoff of 0.9 or 1.0 is used to identify patients with trauma requiring transfusion or at high risk of mortality [14].

The SI may underestimate the severity of occult shock in certain subgroups [15]. Although the SI was originally developed to detect hemorrhagic shock, variations in shock mechanisms can affect its predictive performance [16]. For example, spinal or neurogenic shock affects hemodynamic stability differently from hypovolemic shock. Although hypovolemic shock typically presents as hypotension accompanied by compensatory tachycardia, this response may be blunted or absent when spinal or neurogenic shock coexists because of autonomic disruption. In patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI), increased intracranial pressure or brain herniation can produce vital sign patterns that differ from those observed in hemorrhagic shock. TBI can cause neuronal death and cerebral edema, leading to increased intracranial pressure, with this often accompanied by hypertension and bradycardia, known as Cushing’s triad [17]. This phenomenon contributes to the bimodal relationship between SBP and mortality and reduces the discriminatory accuracy of SBP and the SI [18].

In patients with cardiovascular disease or those receiving long-term antihypertensive or antiarrhythmic therapy (e.g., β-blockers and calcium channel blockers), the physiological compensatory response to shock after injury is impaired [1920]. In such patients, the compensatory increase in HR and vasoconstriction required to maintain organ perfusion may be blunted, which can reduce the predictive accuracy of the SI. Consequently, several derivatives or modified indices have been developed to more accurately determine the risk of shock in diverse trauma populations to improve its discriminatory performance [21].

In the modified SI (mSI), the denominator of the SI is replaced; that is, SBP is substituted with mean arterial pressure (MAP), thereby incorporating the effect of diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Because DBP typically decreases earlier than SBP does in critically ill patients, the use of MAP enables a more comprehensive assessment of circulatory status. Several studies have demonstrated that the discriminatory ability of the mSI is comparable to or greater than that of the SI, with an optimal cutoff of >1.3 [222324]. The age SI (ASI) further accounts for the effect of age on hemodynamic responses and enables improved risk stratification among older patients with trauma [25].

The aforementioned findings indicate that the addition of variables to scoring systems can improve the accuracy of mortality prediction in patients with trauma. In addition, predictive models can be enhanced using simple calculation tools that incorporate repeated measurements at multiple time points, enabling continual monitoring of patient status and evaluation of treatment effects. The delta SI (dSI) has demonstrated substantially greater discriminatory power for predicting major injury, prolonged stay in an intensive care unit (ICU), and in-hospital mortality. Moreover, a potential J-shaped relationship has been observed between the dSI and mortality, particularly in patients with dSI values below −0.5 or at or above 0.5, both of which are strongly associated with increased in-hospital mortality [2627]. Overall, the SI and its derivatives provide practical, physiology-based tools for the early identification of high-risk patients with trauma. Similar to the SI, these indices exhibit a J-shaped association with mortality and exhibit higher specificity but lower sensitivity. Because of their simplicity and ease of application, these indices are considered useful adjuncts for prehospital clinical decision-making. However, their predictive performance may be less robust than believed to be because they are easily affected by specific patient subgroups.

COMBINATION OF THE SHOCK INDEX WITH NEUROLOGICAL ASSESSMENT TOOLS

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was first introduced in 1974 to provide a standardized method for describing coma [28]. This tool has since become one of the most widely used scoring systems for patients with TBI and remains a reliable predictor of patient outcomes [2930]. One study reported that the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values of the GCS for predicting 30-day mortality and poor functional outcomes among patients with trauma were 0.92 and 0.75, respectively [31]. Therefore, incorporating a consciousness assessment tool may be a means of addressing the limitations of the SI, particularly in populations with a high incidence of multiple trauma and TBI.

Building on this concept, Kimura and Tanaka [32] proposed a novel index that combines the SI and GCS, with it termed the reverse SI multiplied by the GCS score (rSIG or rSI-GCS) and calculated as 1/SI × GCS. The rSI-GCS has been validated as a predictor of critical outcomes in patients with trauma, including the requirement for immediate trauma care, massive transfusion, and in-hospital mortality [3334353637]. Uemura et al. [38] demonstrated that combining the SI with a neurological assessment tool improved the AUROC for predicting massive transfusion. Among the compared scoring systems – GCS, rSI-GCS motor (GCSM), ABC score [39], and FASILA score [40] – the rSI-GCS achieved the highest AUROC for massive transfusion prediction (AUROC: 0.846, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.832–0.859). Similar results were observed for predicting resuscitation, surgery, and emergency interventions, with rSI-GCS showing the highest AUROCs (AUROC: 0.777, 95% CI: 0.769–0.785, AUROC: 0.731, 95% CI: 0.720–0.741, and AUROC: 0.760, 95% CI: 0.753–0.768, respectively). Wan-Ting et al. [35] further evaluated the mortality predictive ability of rSI-GCS <14 using the optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index and compared it with SI >0.9, rSI <1, and GCS <13. The results showed that among patients with severe trauma, rSI-GCS <14 had the highest AUROC for predicting mortality (0.76, 95% CI: 0.71–0.82), whereas GCS <13 had an AUROC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.70–0.80). Data from the Pan-Asian Trauma Outcomes Study registry, which included 105,641 patients, demonstrated that the rSI-GCS achieved the highest AUROC values for short-term mortality (0.800; 95% CI, 0.791–0.809) and poor functional outcomes (0.596; 95% CI, 0.590–0.602). When a cutoff of 18 was used, the rSI-GCS exhibited a sensitivity of 0.668 for short-term mortality and 0.371 for poor functional outcomes, with corresponding specificity values of 0.805 and 0.813, respectively. Moreover, the rSI-GCS exhibited superior discriminative ability in subgroups of older patients, those with TBI, and those with nonpenetrating injuries [24]. To validate these findings, a retrospective cohort study included 1791 patients with TBI and compared the predictive accuracy of the rSI-GCS with that of the SI, mSI, and ASI for mortality. The results revealed that the rSI-GCS demonstrated the highest predictive performance in the overall TBI population (AUROC = 0.710), particularly among patients with moderate or severe TBI and isolated head injury. In addition, the rSI-GCS exhibited a superior discriminative ability in patients with major trauma (injury severity score ≥16), motor vehicle collision-related injuries, or fall-related injuries; in both nongeriatric and geriatric populations; and in those with and without cardiovascular disease [16].

The three individual components of the GCS represent distinct neurological domains, and each subscore has its own importance and weighting. Summing these components can dilute their individual meanings and lead to overemphasis on the total score. Compared with the total score, individual components may have higher reliability because normal values in some subscores can artificially increase the overall score. Moreover, the strong intercorrelation among GCS components enables independent use of single subscores for predicting intubation [4142]. Furthermore, although the full GCS is a detailed, multidimensional assessment, the evaluation process is complex and time-consuming. In prehospital settings, such granularity may not be necessary, and using a single subscore can improve assessment accuracy, shorten on-scene time, and potentially reduce delays in patient transport. A retrospective study performed using the Pan-Asia Trauma Outcomes Study registry demonstrated that in prehospital environments, the on-scene GCS and GCSM scores exhibited comparable predictive performance for 30-day mortality (AUROC 0.917 [95% CI, 0.887–0.946] vs. 0.907 [95% CI, 0.875–0.938]) and poor functional outcomes (AUROC 0.617 [95% CI, 0.597–0.637] vs. 0.613 [95% CI, 0.593–0.633]) among patients with trauma [31]. This finding suggests that the GCSM score conveys most of the predictive information for triage scoring in this population, whereas many eye and verbal components are absent or poorly represented [43]. Consequently, the total GCS score exhibits a weaker linear relationship with mortality, whereas the motor score demonstrates a stronger linear correlation with mortality than either the total GCS or its verbal and eye components. Alternative tools have been proposed to further simplify assessments, with these including the Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive (AVPU) scale and the three-point simplified motor score (sMS), which was derived from further categorization of the GCSM [444546474849].

Another cohort study performed using the Tzu Chi Hospital trauma registry determined that the full GCS achieved slightly higher AUROC values for all clinical outcomes compared with the GCSM and sMS, with these outcomes including 3-day mortality (0.899 vs. 0.894 vs. 0.890), 7-day mortality (0.871 vs. 0.864 vs. 0.861), in-hospital mortality (0.833 vs. 0.817 vs. 0.815), ICU length of stay ≥14 days (0.645 vs. 0.628 vs. 0.628), and hospital length of stay ≥30 days (0.607 vs. 0.587 vs. 0.587). By contrast, the GCS eye component (GCSE) demonstrated the poorest discriminative ability for all outcomes [48]. Although the full GCS demonstrates slightly higher accuracy than the GCSE, GCSM, and sMS do, its marginal advantage may not justify its time-consuming assessment process. In prehospital settings, accepting a minimal reduction in accuracy in exchange for faster assessment is often practical and efficient.

We combined the SI with the GCSM, sMS, and the AVPU scale to develop three derivative indices: RSI-GCSM, rSI-sMS, and SIAVPU, defined as follows: RSI-GCSM = 1/SI × GCSM, RSI-sMS = 1/SI × sMS, and SIAVPU = SI × AVPU [151920].

Using the Tzu Chi Trauma Registry, we compared the SI-derived indices with and without the integration of consciousness assessment tools, including the SI, mSI, rSI-GCS, rSI-GCSM, and rSI-sMS. Compared with the SI and mSI, the rSI-GCS demonstrated significantly higher AUROC values for predicting mortality (0.729 vs. 0.505 vs. 0.541), ICU admission (0.608 vs. 0.522 vs. 0.535), and prolonged ICU stay (0.623 vs. 0.527 vs. 0.545). Similar findings were observed for the rSI-GCSM and rSI-sMS, indicating that incorporating consciousness assessment tools can enhance the predictive accuracy of the SI. Furthermore, among the SI derivatives that integrate consciousness assessment, the rSI-GCS and rSI-sMS demonstrated comparable discriminative ability for predicting mortality (0.733 vs. 0.729), ICU admission (0.605 vs. 0.608), prolonged ICU stay (0.623 vs. 0.625), and prolonged hospital length of stay (0.616 vs. 0.619). However, compared with the rSI-GCS and rSI-sMS, the rSI-GCSM consistently yielded lower AUROC values for mortality, ICU admission, prolonged ICU stay, and prolonged length of stay. These findings suggest that the predictive accuracy of the rSI-sMS is comparable to that of the rSI-GCS and that the rSI-sMS has greater clinical utility [15].

Similar results were observed in the analysis of the SIAVPU; the predictive accuracy of the SIAVPU for mortality, ICU admission, and prolonged hospital stay was significantly higher than that of the SI, mSI, and ASI, with AUROCs of 0.738, 0.641, and 0.606, respectively. In the validation cohort, the SIAVPU also demonstrated superior predictive performance for both short-and long-term mortality to that of the SI, mSI, and ASI, with this including 24-hour mortality (0.845 vs. 0.533, 0.540, and 0.678, respectively), 3-day mortality (0.803 vs. 0.513, 0.524, and 0.688, respectively), 7-day mortality (0.755 vs. 0.494, 0.505, and 0.648, respectively), and in-hospital mortality (0.722 vs. 0.510, 0.524, and 0.667, respectively). The predictive ability of the SIAVPU for ICU admission was also superior to that of the SI, mSI, and ASI (0.635 vs. 0.547, 0.551, and 0.563, respectively) [1920].

CLINICAL APPLICATION OF THE RSI-SIMPLIFIED MOTOR SCORE

The Taiwan Triage and Acuity Scale (TTAS) is a reliable tool for triaging patients in emergency departments; however, most triage decisions are primarily based on chief complaints [50]. In the TTAS, physiological indicators are expressed as absolute thresholds (e.g. SBP < 90 mmHg), and consequently, the system does not fully reflect their relative clinical importance. Using data from the Taipei Tzu Chi emergency department information system, we conducted a retrospective analysis to evaluate whether incorporating the rSI-sMS could improve patient reclassification within the TTAS [51]. On the basis of previous studies, we adopted a cutoff of <4 for the rSI-sMS. The results revealed that across triage levels I–III, patients with an rSI-sMS value of <4 had more severe injuries and poorer clinical outcomes than did those with an rSI-sMS value of ≥4. For mortality, ICU admission, prolonged ICU stay, and prolonged hospital stay, the combination of the TTAS with rSI-sMS yielded higher AUROC values than the TTAS combined with the SI or mSI did. When an rSI-sMS value of <4 was used to predict mortality, its specificity was 79.2% in triage levels I and II and 87.4% in triage level III.

The rSI-sMS has been applied for different patient populations. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the use of the SI was limited because fever-induced tachycardia increased the SI value, leading to difficulty in distinguishing patients truly requiring medical intervention from those with mild disease, which led to a high false-positive rate. A study demonstrated that the predictive ability of the SI and ASI for in-hospital mortality was limited among patients presenting to the emergency department with COVID-19 pneumonia, with the study reporting AUROC values of 0.647 (95% CI: 0.58–0.71) for the ASI and 0.549 (95% CI: 0.49–0.61) for the SI [52]. To address this concern, we conducted a retrospective cohort study at the Taipei Tzu Chi emergency department to determine whether the rSI-sMS could identify high-risk mortality among patients with COVID-19. Compared with the SI, mSI, and rSI-GCSM, the rSI-sMS demonstrated superior performance in predicting sepsis, endotracheal intubation, and mortality, with AUROC values of 0.716 (95% CI: 0.661–0.770), 0.844 (95% CI: 0.763–0.925), and 0.804 (95% CI: 0.733–0.875), respectively. The optimal cutoff values of the rSI-sMS for predicting general ward admission, ICU admission, and mortality were 3.17, 3.45, and 3.15, with these corresponding to predictive accuracies of 86.83%, 81.94%, and 90.96%, respectively. These findings indicate that compared with the rSI-GCS and rSI-GCSM, the rSI-sMS is not only faster and easier to use but also demonstrates better predictive performance [53].

FUTURE DEVELOPMENT AND APPLICATION OF THE RSI-SIMPLIFIED MOTOR SCORE

Multiple studies have demonstrated that the rSI-sMS, which integrates hemodynamic and neurological assessments, outperforms the traditional SI and mSI in predicting mortality, ICU admission, and other major clinical outcomes among patients with trauma and those with COVID-19 [1553]. In addition to in-hospital applications, the rSI-sMS can be used for prehospital triage and can serve as a practical tool for emergency medical personnel to rapidly identify high-risk patients with trauma in time-sensitive and resource-limited settings. Compared with other scoring systems that require additional variables, the rSI-sMS is simple and objective, relying only on routinely collected parameters instead of ultrasound or laboratory data. In the future, integrating the rSI-sMS into prehospital electronic records for automatic calculation or embedding it within hospital triage systems may reduce the complexity of manual computation, enhance triage accuracy, and minimize both undertriage and overtriage. Such integration could improve prehospital decision-making, guide the transfer of patients with trauma to higher-level trauma centers, optimize the allocation of critical care resources, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

In prehospital and emergency department settings, generative artificial intelligence (AI) can be integrated with a clinical decision support system (CDSS) to improve triage accuracy and reduce both overtriage and undertriage. In time-critical, high-pressure environments, an AI-driven CDSS can assist physicians in making precise decisions within limited timeframes. AI algorithms, particularly deep learning models, enable accurate patient risk stratification that can guide targeted interventions and optimize resource allocation. They also predict clinical risks and support early interventions. By anticipating adverse events and issuing timely alerts, an AI-driven CDSS can enhance clinical decision-making efficiency and patient safety [54]. Such an approach has been applied for sepsis prediction. At the emergency department of Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital in Taiwan, we developed an AI-based, real-time sepsis prediction system – the Sepsis CDSS (S-CDSS). The S-CDSS demonstrated a higher predictive accuracy for sepsis than the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome, quick Sequential (Sepsis-related) Organ Failure Assessment, Sequential (Sepsis-related) Organ Failure Assessment, and National Early Warning Score did, achieving an AUROC value of 0.879 (AUROC: 0.832 vs. 0.736 vs. 0.830 vs. 0.842). Furthermore, high- and medium-risk alerts generated by the S-CDSS were significantly associated with all major clinical outcomes, namely, endotracheal intubation (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 5.758; 95% CI: 4.456–7.441), general ward admission (aOR: 12.338; 95% CI: 11.112–13.700), ICU admission (aOR: 6.314; 95% CI: 5.457–7.305), emergency department mortality (aOR: 3.082; 95% CI: 1.898–5.003), and in-hospital mortality (aOR: 14.507; 95% CI: 12.056–17.457) [55]. Therefore, integrating an AI-driven CDSS into trauma care can reduce reliance on manual interpretation and enable the timely prediction of disease progression, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Among the variables available in emergency settings, continuously monitored vital signs are the cornerstone of early detection. However, reliance on individual parameters such as HR, SBP, DBP, or the GCS can be confounded by various external factors. In this context, the rSI-sMS, which integrates hemodynamic and neurological assessments into a single index, appears to be a highly promising core feature for enhancing the predictive performance of AI-based models. The rSI-sMS is currently undergoing cross-population and cross-disease validation, including ongoing verification using the Japan Trauma Data Bank. Future research should expand these validation efforts to diverse populations (e.g. older adults and pediatric patients) and different clinical scenarios, such as those involving sepsis and cardiogenic shock. If validated through large-scale, multicenter studies, the rSI-sMS has strong potential to be adopted as an international standard tool for clinical triage and emergency care workflows.

CONCLUSION

The clinical manifestations of trauma are diverse, and achieving accurate triage in the shortest possible time remains a critical challenge for physicians and prehospital providers. This review summarizes current evidence regarding the SI and its derivatives in trauma triage, with particular emphasis placed on the rSI-sMS, which integrates hemodynamic and neurological assessments. The current study identified several key points. First, although the traditional SI and mSI indicate circulatory status, their performance can be compromised under extreme physiological conditions or in specific populations, leading them to have limited sensitivity and specificity. Second, combining the SI with neurological assessments, such as the GCS, AVPU, or sMS, can capture both circulatory and neurological features, thereby enhancing risk stratification accuracy. Studies have demonstrated that the rSI-sMS outperforms the traditional SI and mSI in predicting transfusion requirements, ICU admission, and mortality among patients with trauma. Finally, all variables required for the rSI-sMS are routinely collected in prehospital and emergency settings without reliance on ultrasonography or laboratory testing, and therefore, it is particularly suitable for time-sensitive and resource-limited triage scenarios. Although further multicenter and cross-population validation is warranted, the rSI-sMS exhibits strong potential as a standardized triage tool that can be used to reduce both overtriage and undertriage and can thereby optimize clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes.
