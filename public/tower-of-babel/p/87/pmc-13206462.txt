Effectiveness of Scientific Literacy and Communication E‐Modules in a Molecular Biology Course: A Qualitative and Quantitative Analysis
Authors: Meghan Kates, Alexa Fitzpatrick, Yupeng Zhang, Alison Gibbs, Qin Liu, Alan da Silveira Fleck, Leora Freedman, Stavroula Andreopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn previous work, we described the development and implementation of new pedagogical practices aimed at enhancing literacy, critical thinking, and communication skills development in a third‐year undergraduate molecular biology course. The online nature and large size of the course presented challenges regarding student engagement and access to adequate support. To address these issues, we created four assignments (Journal Article, TED‐style Talk, Reflective, and Concept Map) to build scientific literacy skills. To develop the intended skills, we designed interactive e‐learning modules with active learning opportunities and practice activities to provide effective feedback. Here, we present an evaluation of the outcomes of this work by comparing student engagement in the modules with learning outcomes and academic performance indicators. The majority of students indicated that the e‐modules were conducive to their learning and acquisition of scientific literacy skills. Additionally, most students found that all individual e‐modules and associated assignments supported their learning, with somewhat lower agreeance among students regarding the Journal Article assignment. This feedback and student performance on the assignment supported our hypothesis that students lack practice developing these skills earlier in their education. Overall, there was a trend showing that higher student engagement with the e‐modules was associated with higher assignment scores and final grades. Therefore, the e‐modules and assignments supported the goals of improving targeted skills and contributed to an overall positive learning environment for students. These data demonstrate the utility of online e‐modules for student learning and the usefulness of learning analytics data to better understand the student experience.Keywords: biochemistry, communication, e‐learning, literacy, science education

Introduction

While there is growing interest in cultivating integrated strategies dedicated to the major modalities of language in the sciences, namely scholarly reading, analytical writing and oral communication, traditional approaches to undergraduate science education still dominate in large, introductory courses. This traditional approach often uses exams that rely at least partially on information recall as a way of assessing learning [1]. Although the multiple choice‐style exams favored in these courses can test critical thinking and application of course concepts, they are usually lacking in evaluation of science communication skills. This is problematic because the learning outcomes of this approach do not match the skills valued by future employers in both academia and industry, the two main career streams for undergraduate students in the sciences [2]. In fact, effective science communication has been shown to contribute to higher quality decision‐making and improved collaboration results [3].

A growing body of literature is centered around adding scientific literacy skills as learning outcomes in undergraduate courses. However, most interventions only focus on the development of one scientific literacy skill, either scholarly reading [4, 5], analytical writing [6, 7] or oral communication [8, 9]. Some interventions focus on scientific argument [10, 11] which could be used as an organizing principle for reading, writing, and oral communication, but has been studied mainly in relation to reading. Flipped classroom models, where students review content ahead of class time while in‐person time is dedicated to discussion and problem solving, have shown success in developing scholarly reading and analysis skills [12, 13]. While this is an effective strategy, it has demonstrated success in relatively small classroom settings. Other work has shown that inquiry‐based laboratory curriculum increases students' problem‐solving, understanding of the scientific process, and application of material compared to standard lab curriculum with structured protocols [14, 15]. Although some analytical writing is performed in these interventions, the focus is mainly on the scientific process. Additionally, laboratory sessions dedicated to teaching scientific literacy have been developed to allow for iteration in scholarly reading skills [16]. Another set of interventions is dedicated to oral communication in undergraduate science courses, ranging from the integration of PowerPoint presentations to poster presentations to case‐based tutorials [17]. These represent an excellent set of interventions dedicated to teaching individual scientific literacy skills. However, they are largely suited to small to moderate class sizes, with both instructor and course time dedicated to their implementation.

In recognition of this unmet need in undergraduate science education, we restructured the evaluation model of a large (600 students), online Biochemistry and Molecular Biology undergraduate course to promote all three scientific literacy skills of scholarly reading, analytical writing and oral communication [18]. In 2019, 95% of student success in our molecular biology undergraduate course was measured using exam or quiz‐based evaluation, and only 5% was measured using assignments focused on scientific literacy skills. We transitioned this evaluation structure to be comprised of 60% exam or quiz‐based and 40% dedicated to the development of scientific literacy skills through four assignments.

During the restructuring of this course, the COVID‐19 pandemic forced us to shift to an online learning environment, making it essential to understand online pedagogy to support student learning and course development. Chat et al. have defined e‐learning broadly as a technology and internet‐reliant approach to education that permits students access to digital resources where they learn through interaction and collaboration [19]. As technology has become a day‐to‐day feature of life, it is difficult to provide a comprehensive review on its integration with education. E‐learning can encompass everything including online quizzes, polls, chat features in online courses, break‐out rooms for student interactions, asynchronous pre‐recorded classes, and uploaded course material [20]. Here, we focus on interactive e‐modules as one strategy for online learning. These modules are well suited to make use of the strengths of an online learning model including flexibility for self‐directed learning, engagement with interactive systems, and space for reflection on content [21]. Similarly, a literature review of almost 100 articles about e‐learning found the most important determinants of effective e‐learning to be interactivity with course instructors, a clear understanding of expectations, and the promotion of self‐regulated learning [22]. Therefore, research has shown that e‐learning can be a positive alternative to in‐person learning.

For these reasons, we supported our new course structure with the creation of four e‐modules with key information and practice activities for each of the assignments [18]. Additional support was provided through instructor‐guided discussions about each assignment synchronously during tutorials and asynchronously through online discussion boards. Specifically, the three major assignments (and their associated e‐modules) focused on building skills in scholarly reading, oral communication, and writing. These e‐modules were conceived of as an intentional use of technology where the online experience was curated for students in a way to make engagement clear and streamlined.

While the benefits of this new course design, including the use of interactive e‐modules, are supported by previous literature [22], we directly assessed the impact of these changes using qualitative surveys, participation data, and analyses of key performance indicators. Specifically, we evaluated student responses to the qualitative surveys to determine if the associated e‐modules facilitated acquisition of the associated scientific literacy skill and supported assignment completion. We also evaluated student satisfaction on assignment contribution to their learning. Finally, we used participation data and assignment scores to quantitatively associate e‐module access and completion with assignment success as a metric for skills development. All of these analyzed metrics displayed positive results indicating broad success of the e‐module and assignment implementation toward teaching scientific literacy skills in a large class size.

Methods

Details on Course, e‐Modules and Assignments

Students were enrolled in “BCH311: Nucleic acids and flow of information”, a large, third‐year biochemistry course within the Faculty of Arts and Science at the University of Toronto (U of T). The course is presented on the web‐based platform and learning management system Canvas (branded “Quercus” at U of T). The course is compulsory for students in several life sciences programs, including the Biochemistry major and enrolls up to 600 students.

During the Winter 2022 term, four assignments were designed and implemented to promote the acquisition of skills in scientific literacy including scientific reading, analytical writing, and oral presentation (Figure 1) [18]. The first assignment included the creation of a short, recorded TED‐style Talk to promote oral scientific communication skills. The second (Journal Article) assignment involved reading and responding to questions about a chosen journal article to promote skills of scientific reading. The third (Reflective) assignment involved writing a one‐page reflective piece on a recent scientific article, discussing the content and impact on a field to develop scientific reading and analytical writing skills [18]. There was also a fourth, minor, assignment at the end of the term to create a concept map linking together key topics from all of the lectures. See Appendix A for a more detailed description of the assignments and how they were graded (rubrics) as provided to students enrolled in BCH311. Collectively, these assignments accounted for 37% of a student's total course grade (Table 1).

Four e‐modules were designed to complement these assignments and help students acquire and practice the associated scientific literacy skills. The e‐modules consisted primarily of active learning opportunities to practice skills development. In addition, key information about assignment requirements and deadlines was communicated through interactive videos of the instructor explaining the rubric [18]. These e‐modules remained online even when lectures and tutorials returned in person following the easing of COVID‐19 restrictions. All e‐modules were available online from the start date of the course to allow students flexibility to complete them on their own time. However, to keep students on track with completion of the e‐modules, and to allow further chances for feedback, a minor assignment worth 1% of the student's total grade was completed with each e‐module (except the Concept Map). These minor assessments are not included in the analyses performed here. See Appendix B for further details on the e‐modules including their outline and an overview of the content included.

Survey Data Collection

Qualitative data was gathered during the final week of the course through an anonymous survey regarding student perceptions of the assignments, e‐modules and efficacy of skills development (Appendices C and D). The survey included questions to assess the students' experience of individual e‐modules and their assignments, as well as their development of scientific literacy skills. There were open‐ended questions around these themes asking students to explain which features of the e‐modules either facilitated their completion of the assignments or their development of scientific literacy skills. These open‐ended survey questions were independently analyzed for broad themes. Additionally, the survey included questions asking students to rate whether each assignment contributed to their learning experience using a Likert (5‐point) scale from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’. Responses to questions that used the Likert scale were summarized using stacked bar graphs.

Students were surveyed during the Winter 2022, 2023, and 2024 academic sessions of BCH311 (n = 3 cohorts) with 106 responses collected in total (56 survey responses from 2022, 29 from 2023, 21 from 2024). Participation was voluntary, and survey responses collected were anonymous through MS Forms.

Ethics

Students were invited via an announcement through the learning management system, Canvas, to participate in the anonymous survey. Informed consent was obtained from all participants (See Appendix C). This component of the study was approved by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board (REB #00041818 [Enhancing Science Literacy and Communication Among the Next Generation of Scientists], REB #00042912 [Investigating Learner Engagement through Quercus Student Activity Logs]).

Quantitative Analysis

Analysis of student activity in online course components has been gaining traction and has been successfully used to identify traits associated with successful student outcomes [23, 24]. To quantitatively measure student engagement with the e‐modules, student activity data for the Winter 2022 BCH11 academic term (n = 1 cohort) was collected from Canvas. The detailed log files contained individual student interactions, including timestamps of when specific course components were accessed. We focused on two engagement metrics that could be consistently and objectively derived from these data:

Timeliness: Defined as the number of days between the first recorded access of an e‐module and its associated assignment deadline. For example, a timeliness value of 10 indicates that the student first opened the e‐module 10 days before the assignment was due.

Timeliness: Defined as the number of days between the first recorded access of an e‐module and its associated assignment deadline. For example, a timeliness value of 10 indicates that the student first opened the e‐module 10 days before the assignment was due.

Regularity: Defined as the number of distinct calendar days on which the student accessed the e‐module. This metric captures the extent to which students spread their engagement across time, rather than concentrating it in a single session.

Regularity: Defined as the number of distinct calendar days on which the student accessed the e‐module. This metric captures the extent to which students spread their engagement across time, rather than concentrating it in a single session.

Assignment scores and final, overall course grades were also downloaded from Canvas grade reports and matched with engagement data to allow for performance comparisons. Students without corresponding assignment scores or final grades were excluded from analysis.

To group students according to their engagement patterns separately for each e‐module, we applied the K‐Medoids cluster analysis. This algorithm is often used in educational data mining because of its robustness to noise and outliers compared to k‐means [25]. This approach ensures that clusters are formed based on relative engagement levels within each e‐module, allowing differences in e‐module characteristics and activity distributions. To determine the optimal number of clusters, we used the elbow method, which involves plotting the total within‐cluster dissimilarity for increasing numbers of clusters and selecting the point at which additional clusters yield diminishing improvements in fit [26, 27]. Features (timeliness and regularity) were normalized using Min‐Max scaling to ensure comparability across variables.

To investigate whether assignment scores and final grades differed among the student engagement clusters, we conducted Kruskal–Wallis tests, a non‐parametric alternative to one‐way ANOVA that does not assume normality and compares distributions across groups [28]. Where statistically significant differences were observed, Dunn's post hoc tests were applied to identify which pairs of clusters differed significantly [29, 30]. A p‐value smaller than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Student Evaluation of e‐Module Contribution to Skills Development and Assignment Completion

In the end‐of‐course survey, students were asked to answer questions regarding their experience of the e‐modules and to comment on how these e‐modules facilitated their completion of the assignment and acquisition of targeted skills. To specifically evaluate the usefulness of the e‐modules for development of scientific literacy and science communication skills, students were asked to, “Please comment on how the e‐modules were useful for the development of scientific literacy, communication and writing skills in biochemistry.” In this case, scientific literacy was used to refer to reading while communication encompassed both written and oral communication. From the responses, it was evident that students found these e‐modules and their associated assignments to be useful for the development of these skills for two main reasons (Table 2). The first reason was by allowing students to practice scientific communication in multiple modalities as well as with different audiences. The TED‐style Talk assignment was targeted toward a more general audience through an engaging, oral presentation format, whereas the Reflective Assignment was a slightly more formal piece of written work. Students indicated that they enjoyed the variety, especially regarding being different from other courses. The second main reason students found the e‐modules especially helpful was due to the provided clarification of what was needed for the course assignments. Particularly, they found it helpful that the e‐modules discussed the rubric in more depth and provided examples of assignments, common mistakes, and good practices for communication. The second theme was also reflected in student responses to the end‐of‐course survey when asked to rate the following question using a Likert scale: “The interactive activities in the E‐modules provided an opportunity for you to practice your skills and make the completion of the assignments easier.” The majority of students agreed (52.1%) or strongly agreed (14.1%) with this statement (Figure 2A). Additionally, students were asked in the end‐of‐course survey to rate the following question using a Likert scale: “In general, the E‐modules (TED style‐talk, Journal Article, Reflective, Concept Map) were useful for the development of scientific literacy, communication and writing skills in biochemistry.” The majority of students felt that the e‐modules positively contributed to their education and learning experience in the course (Figure 2B). Specifically, 51.4% of students agreed and 9.5% of students strongly agreed with the question. Therefore, students considered the e‐modules to be useful for both assignment completion and development of scientific literacy skills.

Student Evaluation of Assignment Contribution to Learning and Skills Development

The BCH311 end‐of‐course survey also included questions to specifically assess the students' experience of the individual course assignments. In particular, students were asked to rate whether each assignment contributed to their learning experience using a Likert scale from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree (Figure 3A–C). The majority of students found each assignment to be conducive to their learning. Specifically, for the TED‐style Talk assignment, 39.6% of students agreed and 28.3% of students strongly agreed that this assignment contributed to their learning experience (Figure 3A). Only a small portion of students disagreed with this statement. This was also reflected in the quality of the video TED‐style Talk submissions where the majority were exceptional and achieved high grades, providing insight on the topic with good enthusiasm. Overall, students seemed to enjoy and learn from this assignment.

Student evaluation of assignment contribution to learning experience. Summary of student responses along the Likert 5‐point scale from “Strongly Agree” to “Strongly Disagree”. (A) n = 106 students, 3 cohorts, (B) n = 88 students, 3 cohorts, and (C) n = 106 students, 3 cohorts.

This is also true of the Reflective Assignment where 51.1% of students agreed and 11.4% of students strongly agreed that the assignment contributed to their learning experience (Figure 3B). However, more students were neutral about how the assignment contributed to their learning. This could also have been explained by the timing of the assignment as it was due in the last week of class and was therefore during a time of year when many assignments need to be completed. Quality of work and interest in learning the skills required for this assignment could have suffered due to the lack of time budgeted to complete the assignment.

However, for the Journal Article assignment, only 32.1% of students agreed while 16% of students strongly agreed that it contributed to their learning experience. A greater percentage (34.9%) of students were neutral toward the assignment or disagreed about its contribution to their learning (Figure 3C). This was also reflected in their performance on the journal article assignment, which was lower, on average, compared to the other assignments. Additionally, the format of this assignment was different as students answered questions on the provided journal article during their midterm.

Relationship Between e‐Module Engagement and Academic Performance Indicators

Although students overall indicated that the e‐modules and assignments were conducive to their learning and acquisition of scientific literacy skills, we also wanted to quantitatively evaluate these claims. Specifically, we performed a pilot (single‐cohort) study to examine the relationship between e‐module access and student grades both for the individual major course assignment and final overall grade in the course.

Cluster analysis grouped students into three distinct engagement clusters—labeled High, Medium, and Low—based on patterns of timeliness and regularity for viewing the e‐modules. These clusters were generated using K‐medoids clustering, with the optimal number of clusters determined by the elbow method applied to the within‐cluster variance (see Methods). Each cluster therefore represents a relative level of engagement within the dataset: students in the High engagement cluster accessed the modules earlier and on more days, the Medium group showed moderate levels of both behaviors, and the Low group showed minimal engagement, with fewer days of access and less advance use of the modules. Appendix E summarizes the distribution of student engagement across clusters, and Table 3 shows the mean values of timeliness and regularity for each cluster. For example, in the Journal Assignment e‐module, the High engagement group first accessed the e‐module an average of 21.6 ± 3.1 days before the deadline and returned on 3.3 ± 1.2 different days, compared to just 0.8 ± 0.9 days before the deadline and 1.1 ± 0.5 days of access in the Low engagement group. A similar pattern was observed across all other e‐modules.

Discussion

Our results indicate that the e‐modules created to accompany major course assessments supported the student learning experience and encouraged the development of scientific literacy skills. Students described that practicing scientific communication in multiple modalities was a benefit to this form of assignment design. Additionally, they thought that the provision of examples in the e‐modules was conducive to their learning. One of the most important predictors of e‐learning success is the incorporation of meaningful interactions with instructors and peers [31]. While likely not as powerful as in‐person interactions, the e‐modules still included videos of the instructors walking through the rubrics and modeling some of the assignment activities. Secondly, it provided additional opportunities for students to receive feedback without requiring additional time from course instructors, which is already limited due to the large size of the class. Accordingly, teaching assistants for the course reported a better ability to manage their time. The findings of this study are corroborated by other studies on writing practice and online learning.

Students considered the second (Journal Article) assignment to have contributed to their learning less than the other assignments. Notably, student performance on the Journal Article assignment was also lower than for the other assignments. This may be because the Journal Article assignment has a stronger focus on reading compared to the other assignments. While students read a primary article for their Reflection Assignment, it required more of a general understanding of the work as opposed to the Journal Article assignment, which required more in‐depth understanding and a grasp of technical details. This finding is consistent with recent data on what Horning calls the “don’t, won’t, can’t” problem of undergraduate reading [32]. The decline in reading level among students often leads to the avoidance of course reading and lower performance on assessments requiring reading. This decline in reading level is not just seen in undergraduate students, but can be traced through the education system. In the United States, only 51% of students taking the American College Test met the benchmark for college readiness for reading [33]. Additionally, in a survey of 23 million 15‐year‐olds in 41 countries, only a third of students reached the highest levels of literacy [34]. While issues with reading tend to be more easily concealed than those with writing, lack of clarity in writing can often be the tip of the iceberg with deeper reading comprehension issues underlying vague or confusing writing. It is therefore exceptionally important in this environment of declining literacy levels globally that we implement strategies to improve general and scholarly reading. It is essential that faculty across the disciplines embed instruction in scholarly reading in the same way that writing instruction has been normalized. However, this practice is still not widely accepted, as may be reflected in the responses of the students in our study.

Along with considerations of the decline in reading skills observed among undergraduate students, the results for the Journal Article assessment also must be interpreted based on the course context. Learning related to this assignment was assessed via a 35‐min take‐home quiz in the Winter 2022 academic year whereas it was assessed during the in‐person midterm exam in subsequent academic years. In contrast, the assessments associated with the other two assignments were completed within an extended time frame, allowing students to interact with the videos and iterate on any skills development.

Quantitatively, the pilot study statistical results above demonstrate that student engagement levels (as captured by the timing and regularity of e‐modules access) were associated with academic performance indicators to varying degrees. In general, non‐significant findings indicated that the more often and earlier the students viewed the e‐modules, the better their performance on the module‐based assignments and their final course grade. These findings were more strongly associated with final course grade when looking at student engagement with the Journal Article assignment and Concept Map. Looking at the reverse correlation associating students' overall final grade band, there was significant association with e‐module engagement for both regularity and timeliness. Therefore, this pilot study provides evidence for associations between e‐module engagement and improved final course grade and grade band.

This work is supported by data from several other studies that have indicated that engagement with online learning material is associated with academic achievement, course grade, and course completion [23, 35, 36]. In particular, a study conducted by Ozan and Ozarslan identified that learners who watched video lectures had higher final exam scores, but that shorter videos had a greater degree of complete watches compared to longer video formats [37]. It is therefore likely that students found the short e‐module video modality attractive. Additionally, they were likely motivated to engage with the e‐modules because they were linked to specific assessments and thus had definite timing in the progression of the course. This is especially important given that the main purpose of BCH311 is to teach biochemistry. If the videos on communication skills were perceived as a mere “add‐on”, engagement levels would likely be lower.

However, although the findings indicated associations between academic performance indicators, particularly final course grade band, and e‐module engagement, the interpretation of these results should be nuanced. Online learning can be more convenient for some students, but it tends to favor students who are already highly self‐motivated, and there are mixed reviews on whether it allows for better regulation of the pace of learning for each student [38]. The same could be true of the e‐modules here, where students who were already highly motivated to succeed in the course were more likely to regularly access the e‐modules and already more likely to succeed in the assignments and overall course. This does not negate the positive impact of the intervention of teaching students scientific literacy skills. In fact, studies have shown that although students have the capacity to learn at a relatively consistent rate, providing them with opportunities for practicing their learning allows for growth [39, 40]. Additionally, we designed the e‐modules to promote self‐directed learning for all students while still keeping them on track by setting deadlines for low‐stakes activities embedded in the e‐modules relevant to each assignment. Approximately 95% of students completed these low‐stakes post e‐module assignments, indicating that they did access these learning opportunities.

While student engagement was correlated to the timeliness and regularity of student access to the e‐modules, the strength of the associations varied among the modules. Two limitations in the regularity metric might contribute to inconsistencies in the observed results. First, the regularity metric reflects the number of days course material was accessed rather than the number of times e‐modules were specifically viewed. For example, a student may view an e‐module 10 times in 1 day compared to a student who only views the e‐module twice, but across 2 days. Second, the regularity metric does not capture the depth of cognitive engagement during those interactions. While the e‐modules were designed to have active learning components, as it has been shown that active learning improves academic indicators [41], the timeliness and regularity factors may not be fully representative of the extent of effort made toward each activity. To address these issues, regularity could be measured by the number of times the e‐module is opened, and a short survey could accompany the e‐module to ask about what the students did, such as note‐taking or rewinding to better understand content, while viewing the materials. Another way to improve the collection of student responses is by incentivizing participation in the course survey. Survey data is notoriously plagued by participation bias, where participants that have certain traits will disproportionally respond to surveys, an effect that can be particularly striking with low response rates [42]. This can skew the responses and make their interpretation challenging. Therefore, future investigation into the utility of the created assignment and e‐modules will focus on improving student participation.

Overall, the results of this study show that the e‐modules designed to support four course assignments were regarded by students as being supportive of their learning of targeted skills including scientific literacy, writing, and communication. Most students agreed that each assignment was conducive to their acquisition of the targeted skill. This was supported by positive associations in academic performance, particularly for overall grade band. The focus on discipline‐specific communication makes this instruction both appropriately challenging as well as relevant to the students' progress in their program. Previous evidence has shown that embedding language and writing instruction in credit courses is the gold standard for the development of students' academic literacy [43]. Thus, students learning in biochemistry courses that have reading, writing, speaking, and listening woven into the high‐level learning are more likely to become better scientific communicators. This improvement in scientific communication could also lead to improved performance in their future careers, as both academic and industry professionals have indicated communication skills as being vital to success in these fields [2].
