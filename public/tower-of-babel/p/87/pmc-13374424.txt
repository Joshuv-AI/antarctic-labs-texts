Preconception care health literacy among medical students at a Japanese medical school: a cross-sectional study of knowledge–behaviour gaps across academic years
Authors: Mika Sugihara, Kuniaki Ota, Erika Ota, Eri Maeda, Yumiko Morimoto, Ryo Matsumoto, Yoshiaki Ota, Naoko Arata, Koichiro Shimoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe preconception care health literacy among medical students and examine knowledge–behaviour gaps across academic years and by future parenthood intention.DesignCross-sectional online survey.SettingKawasaki Medical School, Japan.ParticipantsMedical students in years 1–6. A total of 613 students were included in the analysis.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPreconception care health literacy was assessed using a validated Japanese preconception care-specific scale comprising a knowledge domain (13 true/false/do not know items; total score 0–13) and a behaviour/skills domain (25 4-point Likert items; total score 25–100). Domain scores were compared across academic years and future parenthood intention groups. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed adjusting for age.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 22.53 years (SD 2.19). The mean knowledge score was 9.99 (SD 2.60) and the mean behaviour/skills score was 73.66 (SD 12.25). Knowledge scores increased with academic progression (year 1, 8.47 (SD 3.00); year 6, 11.14 (SD 1.34); p<0.001). In contrast, behaviour/skills scores did not differ significantly across academic years (p=0.092). Knowledge scores were similar across future parenthood intention groups (p=0.858). Behaviour/skills scores differed by intention (p=0.005), with higher scores among students who intended future parenthood than among those who were unsure or had not considered it.ConclusionsIntegrating skills-based training and reflective life-planning components into medical curricula may help strengthen preconception-related behaviours in medical students and may have indirect implications for their future readiness to support preconception care.

Introduction

Preconception care is the provision of biomedical, behavioural and social interventions to women and couples before conception to address modifiable risks and improve maternal, newborn and child health outcomes.13 Contemporary frameworks emphasise that preconception health is broad, spanning chronic disease management, optimal nutrition including folic acid use, healthy weight, lifestyle modification and appropriate vaccinations.4 5 Clinical guidance similarly recommends that prepregnancy counselling comprehensively address modifiable risk factors and preventive strategies to optimise health before conception.6

However, effective implementation of preconception care requires more than awareness of recommended practices. Individuals need the ability to obtain, understand, appraise and apply health information to everyday decisions and appropriate healthcare-seeking behaviours—core elements of health literacy.7 To support population-based risk assessment and behaviour-oriented interventions in this field, Suto et al developed a preconception care-specific health literacy scale in Japan.8 This scale was designed to capture both knowledge and behavioural/skill-based dimensions, reflecting the premise that knowledge does not necessarily translate directly into practice in preconception care.8

Medical students represent an important target population that has been less frequently examined for studying the knowledge–behaviour relationship in preconception care. As future healthcare providers, they will be expected to counsel patients on preconception health across diverse clinical contexts.6 Furthermore, progression through medical school entails cumulative curricular and clinical exposure to reproductive and women’s health topics, which may increase preconception-related knowledge. Nevertheless, whether such educational progression is accompanied by parallel improvements in behavioural and practical skills related to preconception care remains unclear. In addition, students’ future parenthood intention may influence the degree to which preconception knowledge becomes personally relevant and is translated into health-related behaviours.

Therefore, we aimed to describe preconception care health literacy among medical students at Kawasaki Medical School using the preconception care health literacy scale and to examine differences in knowledge and behaviour/skills domains across academic years. We further explored whether future parenthood intention was associated with these domains. By clarifying potential gaps between knowledge acquisition and behavioural/skill-based health literacy in a high-expected-literacy cohort, this study may provide insights for curriculum design and inform broader strategies for effective preconception care implementation.4 8

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey to evaluate preconception care health literacy among medical students at Kawasaki Medical School, Japan. The survey was administered between September 2025 and November 2025 using an online questionnaire.

Participants

All enrolled medical students (years 1–6) at Kawasaki Medical School were eligible to participate. A total of 794 students were invited to participate in the survey. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Students were informed that their decision to participate or not would not affect their academic evaluation. We excluded responses with missing data for the primary outcome measures (knowledge items or behaviour/skills items). The final analytic sample comprised 613 students, corresponding to a response rate of 77.2%.

Measures

Preconception care health literacy scale

Preconception care health literacy was assessed using the preconception care-specific health literacy scale developed by Suto et al.8 The scale consists of two subscales: a knowledge domain comprising 13 items presented in a true/false/‘do not know’ format assessing knowledge related to preconception care and a behaviour/skills domain comprising 25 items rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale assessing behaviours and practical skills relevant to preconception health.8

Response options for all knowledge items were true, false and do not know.8 Response options for all behaviour/skills items were 4=strongly applicable, 3=somewhat applicable, 2=neither and 1=not at all applicable.8 The full lists of items, response options and scoring rules for the knowledge and behaviour/skills domains are provided in online supplemental tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Scoring

For the knowledge domain, each correct response was scored as 1, whereas incorrect and ‘do not know’ responses were scored as 0, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 13.8 For the behaviour/skills domain, each item was scored from 1 to 4 as marked, and item scores were summed to generate a total score ranging from 25 to 100.8 Higher scores indicated higher preconception care-related behavioural/skill-based health literacy.

When domain-specific analyses were performed, subscale scores for the behaviour/skills domain were calculated according to the original six-factor structure. The factor labels and item assignments used in this study are presented in online supplemental table S3.8

Internal consistency in the present sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the overall knowledge and behaviour/skills domains.

Covariates

We collected demographic and educational characteristics including age and academic year. Future parenthood intention was assessed using the item: ‘Do you want to have children in the future?’ with response options of ‘yes’, ‘unsure/not considered’ and ‘no’.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for participant characteristics and outcome measures. Continuous variables were summarised using means and SD or medians and IQRs as appropriate and categorical variables using counts and percentages. To examine differences in knowledge and behaviour/skills scores across academic years and across future parenthood intention groups, we used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate based on data distribution and variance structure. When overall group differences were significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with adjustment for multiple testing using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test after ANOVA-based analyses and Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons after Kruskal-Wallis analyses, as appropriate.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate associations of academic year and future parenthood intention with the knowledge total score and the behaviour/skills total score. Age was included as an adjustment variable. Academic year 1 and intention ‘yes’ were used as reference categories. Model assumptions were assessed using residual diagnostics. The results of these multivariable analyses are presented in online supplemental tables S4 and S5. A two-sided p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Dual-axis plots were created to visually summarise mean knowledge and behaviour/skills scores by academic year (figure 1) and future parenthood intention (figure 2). All analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R.9

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 794 eligible medical students invited to participate, 613 completed the survey and were included in the analysis, corresponding to a response rate of 77.2%. The mean age was 22.53±2.19 years; 434 students (70.8%) were aged<25 years and 179 (29.2%) were aged≥25 years. Participants were distributed across all academic years: year 1 (n=135, 22.0%), year 2 (n=79, 12.9%), year 3 (n=115, 18.8%), year 4 (n=135, 22.0%), year 5 (n=70, 11.4%) and year 6 (n=79, 12.9%). Regarding future parenthood intention, 466 students (76.0%) responded ‘yes’, 112 (18.3%) responded ‘unsure/not considered’ and 35 (5.7%) responded ‘no’ (table 1).

The mean knowledge total score was 9.99±2.60 (range 0–13), and the mean behaviour/skills total score was 73.66±12.25 (range 25–100) (table 1).

The internal consistency of the scale in the present sample was acceptable to excellent, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.789 for the knowledge domain and 0.916 for the behaviour/skills domain.

Knowledge domain across academic years

Knowledge scores differed significantly across academic years (one-way ANOVA, p<0.001). Mean (±SD) knowledge scores increased with academic progression: year 1, 8.47±3.00; year 2, 8.90±2.92; year 3, 10.47±2.16; year 4, 10.61±2.27; year 5, 10.87±1.98; and year 6, 11.14±1.34 (table 2). This pattern indicates a clear upward trend in preconception care-related knowledge with advancement through medical school. Post hoc comparisons showed that knowledge scores were significantly higher in years 3–6 than in year 1 and in years 3–6 than in year 2, whereas differences among years 3–6 were not statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Behaviour/skills domain across academic years

In contrast to knowledge, the behaviour/skills total score did not show a statistically significant difference across academic years (one-way ANOVA, p=0.092). The mean (±SD) behaviour/skills scores were: year 1, 72.99±12.38; year 2, 74.19±12.95; year 3, 75.33±12.11; year 4, 71.27±13.07; year 5, 75.26±10.85; and year 6, 74.54±10.81 (table 2). Thus, academic progression was not accompanied by a parallel improvement in behavioural and practical skills related to preconception care.

Taken together, knowledge scores increased with academic progression, whereas behaviour/skills scores did not show a parallel increase (figure 1).

Knowledge and behaviour/skills by future parenthood intention

Knowledge scores did not differ by future parenthood intention (p=0.858): ‘yes’, 10.02±2.57; ‘unsure/not considered’, 9.93±2.63; and ‘no’, 9.80±2.96 (table 3).

Knowledge and behaviour/skills domain scores by future parenthood intention

By contrast, behaviour/skills scores differed significantly across intention groups (p=0.005). Students who responded ‘yes’ demonstrated higher behaviour/skills scores than those who were ‘unsure/not considered’ (74.57±12.04 vs 70.78±12.81), while the ‘no’ group showed a similar numerical trend towards lower scores (70.80±11.77) (table 3). Post hoc analysis indicated a significant difference between the ‘yes’ and ‘unsure/not considered’ groups, whereas comparisons involving the ‘no’ group were not statistically significant after adjustment. Taken together, future parenthood intention was associated with behaviour/skills scores but not with knowledge scores, as illustrated in figure 2.

Multivariable associations

In multivariable linear regression analyses adjusting for age and future parenthood intention, higher academic year was independently associated with higher knowledge scores. Compared with year 1, knowledge scores were higher in year 3 (β=1.81, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.52, p<0.001), year 4 (β=1.88, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.66, p<0.001), year 5 (β=2.11, 95% CI 1.22 to 3.01, p<0.001) and year 6 (β=2.36, 95% CI 1.44 to 3.28, p<0.001), whereas year 2 did not differ significantly from year 1. Age was not independently associated with knowledge after accounting for academic year (online supplemental table S4).

For the behaviour/skills domain, academic year was not significantly associated with total scores after adjustment. However, compared with students who responded ‘yes’, those who were ‘unsure/not considered’ had lower behaviour/skills scores (β = −3.53, 95% CI −6.05 to −1.00, p=0.006). The ‘no’ group showed a similar direction of association, although this did not reach statistical significance (β = −3.70, 95% CI −7.89 to 0.49, p=0.084) (online supplemental table S5).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 613 medical students at Kawasaki Medical School, we assessed preconception care health literacy using a validated scale that captures both knowledge and behavioural/skill-based domains.8 We observed higher knowledge scores in students in more advanced academic years, whereas behaviour/skills scores did not demonstrate a corresponding yearwise pattern. In addition, future parenthood intention was associated with the behaviour/skills domain but not with the knowledge domain. These findings suggest a potential knowledge–behaviour gap in preconception care health literacy even within a cohort expected to have high baseline literacy and progressive educational exposure during medical training.

Preconception care comprises a broad set of biomedical, behavioural and social interventions delivered before conception to address modifiable risks and improve maternal, newborn and child outcomes.13 Contemporary frameworks emphasise that preconception health spans chronic disease management, optimal nutrition including folic acid, healthy weight, lifestyle modification and appropriate vaccinations.4 5 Clinical guidance similarly recommends comprehensive prepregnancy counselling across diverse risk factors rather than a narrow focus on a single health issue.6 However, translating these recommendations into real-world practice requires more than awareness of guidelines. Individuals must be able to obtain, understand, appraise and apply health information in everyday decisions and appropriate healthcare-seeking behaviours—capabilities central to health literacy.7 To operationalise these constructs for preconception health, Suto et al developed a preconception care-specific health literacy scale in Japan that explicitly distinguishes between knowledge and behavioural/skill-based dimensions.8 Our findings in medical students provide empirical support for this conceptual separation and underscore that knowledge acquisition does not necessarily ensure parallel development of practical competencies. At the same time, the behaviour/skills domain should be understood as a broad and multidimensional construct rather than a single unitary behaviour, as it encompasses multiple preconception-related aspects of health-related decision-making and self-management. This conceptual breadth is consistent with the inherently broad scope of preconception care, which spans nutrition, lifestyle, preventive behaviours and health communication.48

Importantly, the behaviour/skills domain of the scale primarily reflects medical students’ own health-related behaviours and practical skills relevant to preconception health, rather than directly measuring professional counselling competence or clinical communication skills.8 Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as identifying potential educational targets at the level of students’ personal preconception-related behaviours and self-management. Although such behaviours may plausibly be relevant to later patient-centred counselling, this study did not directly assess professional competence, and any implications for future counselling readiness should be regarded as indirect and hypothesis-generating.10 11

International literature related to medical trainees further supports the need to evaluate and strengthen preconception and fertility-related competencies within medical education. Recent studies assessing preconception awareness in medical students have suggested that even early-stage trainees may have important educational needs and that structured curricula could help identify and address knowledge gaps.12 A recent cross-sectional study of Japanese medical students using the Cardiff Fertility Knowledge Scale likewise showed that fertility knowledge increased across academic years but plateaued thereafter, further supporting the need for structured curriculum-based fertility and preconception education in medical training.13 Surveys of premedical and medical students in other settings have also highlighted concerns about fertility decline during training and perceived insufficiencies in fertility-related education and institutional support, reinforcing the importance of integrating reproductive life planning into medical training.11 In addition, broader reviews of physicians and trainees indicate that formal education regarding fertility decline and ART remains insufficient across specialties, suggesting that systematic reproductive health education should begin earlier in training.10 This collective evidence aligns with our observation that a high-expected-literacy cohort may still experience incomplete translation from knowledge to applied behaviours and skills.

From an educational perspective, our results suggest that students in more advanced academic years had higher factual knowledge about preconception health, whereas behavioural/skill-based health literacy did not show a comparable pattern across years.6 8 These findings may indicate that knowledge acquisition and behavioural development do not necessarily progress in parallel during medical training. From this perspective, this gap may be more effectively addressed through skills-based educational approaches that explicitly incorporate preconception topics. For example, case-based counselling exercises, simulated patient encounters and structured role-play focused on preconception counselling (eg, folic acid use, weight optimisation, vaccination status, lifestyle risks and chronic disease management) may provide opportunities for students to practise translating knowledge into communication and decision-making. In addition, guided reflection or portfolio-based assignments that ask students to assess their own preconception-related risk factors and health behaviours may reinforce the link between knowledge and personal health-related decision-making, while also prompting reflection on the future clinical relevance of preconception care.468

Notably, future parenthood intention was associated with higher behaviour/skills scores, while knowledge scores were similar across intention groups. This pattern suggests that personal relevance may facilitate knowledge-to-action processes, consistent with health literacy frameworks emphasising motivation and application in daily life.7 8 Survey data among medical students also suggest that fertility and family planning are often perceived through the lens of training demands and life planning, which may shape whether knowledge is internalised as personally actionable behaviour.11 Qualitative and mixed-method evidence in reproductive-age populations likewise indicates that preconception beliefs, perceived relevance and readiness influence receptivity to preconception information and engagement in health behaviours.14 This gap may also reflect factors beyond knowledge itself. Preconception-related behaviours may depend on motivation, perceived opportunity, personal priorities and the extent to which such behaviours are experienced as personally relevant. In younger students, preconception care may remain conceptually important but not yet feel immediately applicable to their own lives. In addition, institutional stress and workload during medical training may influence health-related behaviours independently of knowledge or health literacy. Importantly, the ‘unsure/not considered’ and ‘no’ categories should not be interpreted as representing a single underlying orientation towards future parenthood. These groups may reflect distinct psychological and life-course contexts, including unresolved uncertainty, deferred planning or a more stable intention not to have children. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the broad single-item categorisation used in this study. From a life-course perspective, these findings underscore that preconception-related behaviours and skills should ideally be strengthened regardless of future parenthood intention, as they contribute to the long-term health of individuals and for those who do become parents, to the health of future offspring.2 4 5 These observations may have direct implications for curriculum design: embedding reflective life-planning components and self-assessment of preconception risk factors may help students connect emerging knowledge with personally meaningful actions, thereby strengthening behavioural/skill-based preconception health literacy in medical students.6 8 11

Our study has several strengths. First, we applied a preconception care-specific health literacy scale that simultaneously assesses knowledge and behavioural/skill-based domains, enabling a nuanced examination of potential knowledge–behaviour gaps in a high-expected-literacy population.8 Second, the sample size was substantial for a single-institution medical student survey, and participants were well-distributed across all academic years, allowing us to evaluate educational progression in relation to domain-specific outcomes. Third, by examining future parenthood intention alongside academic year, we explored a plausible motivational pathway that may influence behavioural/skill-based health literacy.7 8 14

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the cross-sectional design, causal inferences regarding the impact of medical education on preconception care health literacy cannot be made. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether curricular exposure directly drives knowledge gains and whether targeted interventions can enhance behavioural/skill-based domains.68 1012 Second, the study was conducted at a single medical school in Japan, which may limit generalisability to other institutions with different curricula or student demographics.81012 With a response rate of 77.2%, non-response bias and self-selection bias cannot be excluded; students with greater interest in reproductive health or preconception care may have been more likely to participate. In addition, institution-specific contextual factors, including institutional culture, may have influenced responses independently of individual health literacy. Third, the behaviour/skills domain was based entirely on self-reported responses and may therefore have been influenced by social desirability bias, recall bias and possible overestimation of healthy behaviours. This issue may be particularly relevant in medical students, who may be more aware of normative or socially desirable health-related responses. Fourth, we did not collect sex/gender information, which precluded evaluation of potential gender-related differences in health literacy and behavioural engagement. This is an important limitation given that prior literature in trainee populations suggests that reproductive life planning experiences and institutional challenges may differ by gender.10 11 In addition, the multivariable analyses were adjusted only for age, and other potentially relevant factors—such as relationship status, prior reproductive health education, personal or family health experiences and socioeconomic background—were not assessed. Residual confounding due to these unmeasured variables cannot therefore be excluded. Moreover, the subgroup of students who responded ‘no’ to future parenthood intention was relatively small, which may have reduced statistical power and limited the precision of comparisons involving this category. Findings related to this subgroup should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, future parenthood intention was assessed by a single item, and future studies might benefit from more granular assessment of life-plan context and perceived personal relevance of preconception health.14 Future multi-institutional and longitudinal studies are warranted to validate our findings across diverse educational settings, to disentangle the effects of academic progression from cohort-related or contextual influences and to examine how specific educational interventions influence trajectories of preconception-related knowledge and behaviour/skills over time.

Despite these limitations, our findings offer important implications for medical education and broader preconception care implementation. Even among medical students, knowledge acquisition alone may not be sufficient to support behavioural and practical capacities related to preconception health. Integrating competency-based, behaviour-oriented educational strategies—such as structured counselling practice, simulation-based training and reflective life-planning modules—may help bridge this knowledge–behaviour gap.468 10

In conclusion, among medical students at Kawasaki Medical School, preconception care knowledge increased with academic progression, whereas behavioural/skills-based health literacy did not show a parallel improvement. Future parenthood intention was associated with behavioural/skill-based scores, indicating that motivation and personal relevance may facilitate knowledge-to-action translation. These findings highlight the need for educational strategies that integrate practical, behaviour-oriented competencies—such as structured counselling practice, simulation-based training and reflective life-planning modules—with knowledge acquisition. Strengthening personal preconception-related behaviours and communication-oriented learning during medical training may not only benefit students’ own life-course health but may also have indirect implications for their future readiness to support preconception care in clinical practice.
