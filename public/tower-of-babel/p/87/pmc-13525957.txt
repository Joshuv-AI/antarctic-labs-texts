Clinical features and outcomes of patients with anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-positive systemic lupus erythematosus from a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Magdalena Morytko, Radosław Dziedzic, Lech Zaręba, Mariusz Korkosz, Joanna Kosałka-Węgiel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe role of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) remains unclear. ANCA positivity has been linked to more severe disease and possible overlap with ANCA-associated vasculitis, but available data are inconsistent.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single-center study of SLE patients treated at the University Hospital in Kraków (2012–2022). Patients fulfilled the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria. ANCA positivity (anti-MPO or anti-PR3) was confirmed by ELISA. Clinical features, laboratory findings, treatment, and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsAmong 1039 SLE patients, 18 (1.73%) were ANCA-positive (anti-MPO, 72.22%; anti-PR3, 27.78%). Most ANCA-positive SLE patients were female (88.89%), with a median age at disease onset of 35.5 years. The most common manifestations in ANCA-positive cases were hematological abnormalities (100%), constitutional symptoms (88.89%), and joint involvement (88.89%). Renal involvement was observed in 72.22% (n = 13) of ANCA-positive SLE patients; however, lupus nephritis was confirmed by kidney biopsy in only seven cases. Vasculitis was rare (5.56%). Anti-dsDNA (61.11%) and anti-SSA (50%) were the most frequent autoantibodies. No significant differences were found between anti-MPO and anti-PR3 subgroups. Most ANCA-positive patients received glucocorticoids (94.44%), cyclophosphamide (61.11%), and antimalarials (61.11%). No statistically significant differences were observed between the ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative groups (p > 0.05 for all parameters).ConclusionsANCA positivity in SLE is rare and predominantly associated with anti-MPO antibodies. It is linked to frequent renal involvement but infrequent vasculitis. No differences were observed between ANCA subtypes, suggesting no distinct clinical phenotype. Key Points• ANCA positivity was rare in this systemic lupus erythematosus cohort (1.73%) and was predominantly associated with anti-MPO antibodies rather than anti-PR3 antibodies.• No significant differences in demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, comorbidities, or autoantibody profiles were observed between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients. Similarly, no significant differences were identified between the anti-PR3–positive and anti-MPO–positive groups.• ANCA-positive SLE was commonly associated with the need for intensive immunosuppressive treatment, highlighting its potential relevance as a marker of severe disease course.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from cutaneous and musculoskeletal involvement to severe and potentially life-threatening complications such as lupus nephritis (LN) and central nervous system involvement [1, 2]. It is well established that the presence of specific antinuclear antibodies (ANA) may indicate an increased risk of particular disease manifestations; for example, anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies are strongly associated with LN [3]. Moreover, not only the presence but also the titer of these antibodies appears to be clinically relevant. As demonstrated in our previous study, an anti-dsDNA titer of ≥ 1:80 detected by indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) was associated with a 1.76-fold increased odds of LN [3]. Interestingly, anti-dsDNA antibodies appear to be more frequently detected in late-onset LN compared with early-onset LN [4]. However, data regarding the association between specific autoantibody profiles and organ involvement remain inconsistent. For instance, in our previous study, we did not observe differences in the prevalence of anti-Smith (Sm) antibodies between patients with and without LN, despite reports indicating that anti-Sm antibodies are more frequently detected in African American patients [3, 5, 6].

Beyond predicting organ involvement, specific autoantibodies may also suggest the presence of overlapping autoimmune conditions. The coexistence of additional ANA specificities accompanied by characteristic clinical features is a key element in the diagnosis of overlap syndromes. For example, anti-topoisomerase I antibodies are associated with skin fibrosis, while anti-Jo-1 antibodies are linked to interstitial lung disease and rhabdomyolysis, reflecting concomitant systemic sclerosis or inflammatory myopathy, respectively [7, 8]. Recognition of such antibody–phenotype associations is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management of patients with complex autoimmune presentations [9, 10].

Vasculitis may also occur in patients with SLE [11]. Cutaneous vasculitis has been associated with a more severe disease course, including heart failure and central nervous system manifestations [11]. Therefore, it is important to consider alternative causes of vasculitis, particularly autoimmune conditions such as anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV). AAV represents a group of severe autoimmune diseases that may share several clinical manifestations with SLE, including skin lesions, arthritis, and renal involvement [11]. In most patients, SLE and AAV can be distinguished based on demographic characteristics as well as laboratory and histopathological findings; however, some patients present with overlapping clinical and immunological features fulfilling the classification criteria for both diseases [11]. Galeazzi et al. [12] reported that among patients with SLE, 9.3% were positive for anti-myeloperoxidase (anti-MPO) antibodies, whereas anti-proteinase 3 (anti-PR3) antibodies were detected in only up to 1.7% of cases. Notably, overlap between SLE and AAV is associated with a particularly severe disease phenotype [13, 14]. More than 90% of reported cases present with serious manifestations, most commonly rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, requiring aggressive immunosuppressive therapy with high-dose glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide. Moreover, the coexistence of SLE and AAV has been linked to a high mortality rate [13, 15].

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the clinical course of a Polish cohort of SLE patients with ANCA positivity. Specifically, we assessed clinical manifestations of SLE, laboratory findings, immunosuppressive treatment strategies, and patient outcomes. Additionally, we performed a comparative analysis of patients with anti-MPO–positive and anti-PR3–positive antibodies to identify potential differences in clinical presentation and prognosis.

Patients and methods

Study population

This study was based on a retrospective analysis of medical records of patients diagnosed with SLE who were under care at the University Hospital in Kraków, Poland, between 2012 and 2022. Only patients fulfilling the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification criteria were included [16].

First, we identified all patients diagnosed with SLE, and within this cohort, we screened for the presence of ANCA (IIF), confirmed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for anti-MPO or anti-PR3 antibodies. The ANCA-positive subgroup was subsequently followed to assess SLE-related outcomes and the occurrence of cutaneous or organ vasculitis. When available, histopathological findings from skin and kidney biopsies were evaluated for features consistent with SLE and vasculitis.

For all included patients, detailed demographic characteristics (age and sex) as well as comprehensive clinical and laboratory data were collected. Collected data included the age at SLE onset, age at LN onset, total disease duration, time from SLE or LN diagnosis to the development of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), coexisting comorbidities, history of miscarriages in female patients, and therapeutic approaches applied during the disease. When applicable, causes of death and age at death were also recorded.

Clinical manifestations evaluated in this study comprised cutaneous involvement, arthritis, serositis, hematologic abnormalities, and organ-specific complications involving the kidneys, liver, nervous system, and respiratory tract, as well as Raynaud’s phenomenon and lymphadenopathy. A detailed description of the assessed clinical variables and evaluation methods has been published previously [4].

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Bioethics Committee of Jagiellonian University Medical College (Approval No. 118.6120.21.2023, dated June 15, 2023). All research activities adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Laboratory analysis

Standard laboratory methods were used to assess complete blood counts, lipid profiles, serum creatinine levels, and glomerular filtration rate (GFR), calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation. Urinalysis included 24-h urinary protein excretion and microscopic examination of urinary sediment. ANA were detected by IIF on HEp-2 cells, with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) or line-blot techniques applied for the identification of specific autoantibodies. Anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies were measured using the Crithidia luciliae indirect immunofluorescence test, and complement components were quantified by nephelometry. ANCA were screened by IIF, with determination of antibody titers and fluorescence patterns. Positive ANCA findings were subsequently confirmed by ELISA, enabling the identification of anti-myeloperoxidase (anti-MPO) and anti-proteinase 3 (anti-PR3) antibodies. Hypercoagulability assessment included lupus anticoagulant testing, as well as measurement of anti-cardiolipin and anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies (IgG and IgM).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA software (TIBCO Software Inc., version 13.3, Tulsa, OK, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Due to the small sample size, non-parametric methods were applied. Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic features

The presence of anti-PR3 (n = 5, 27.78%) or anti-MPO (n = 13, 72.22%) antibodies was identified in 18 of 1039 patients with SLE (1.73%) included in our database; this subgroup comprised 16 women and 2 men. The median age at SLE onset was 35.5 years (22.0–50.8 years), with no differences between subgroups according to ANCA status. The median disease duration was 9 years (4–14 years) and did not differ between the analyzed groups. During follow-up, one anti-PR3–positive patient died due to lung cancer. Clinical manifestations

The most prevalent clinical manifestations were hematological abnormalities (n = 18, 100%), constitutional symptoms (n = 16, 88.89%), and joint involvement (n = 16, 88.89%). Detailed analysis showed that certain symptoms were present in all anti-PR3–positive SLE patients, namely arthralgia and anemia, whereas all anti-MPO–positive SLE patients exhibited lymphopenia and anemia.

Most ANCA-positive patients frequently presented with renal involvement (n = 13; 72.22%). Specific renal findings included leukocyturia, erythrocyturia, and 24-h urinary protein excretion > 0.5 g/day (each n = 12; 66.67%), while urinary casts were observed in half of the patients. Histopathological confirmation of renal involvement was available in only seven cases, demonstrating class III LN in four patients, class IV in two patients, and class V in one patient, according to the ISN/RPS classification criteria. In the remaining cases, renal involvement was established based on abnormalities in urinary sediment and the presence of proteinuria. Among mucocutaneous manifestations, malar rash was the most prevalent (n = 8; 44.44%). Interestingly, only one patient presented with symptomatic cutaneous vasculitis (5.56%). Serositis was confirmed in four ANCA-positive SLE patients (22.22%).

Clinical features observed exclusively in anti-MPO–positive patients included pulmonary involvement (n = 3; 16.67%), neurological manifestations (n = 2; 11.11%), and lupoid hepatitis (n = 1; 5.56%). None of the ANCA-positive SLE patients developed urticaria, hemolytic anemia, macrophage activation syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, pericarditis, peripheral nervous system involvement, or pulmonary hypertension.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the study groups for any clinical manifestation. Comorbidities

The most common concomitant conditions were hypercholesterolemia (n = 11, 61.11%) and arterial hypertension (n = 10, 55.56%). Hypothyroidism was present in one-third of ANCA-positive SLE patients, while diabetes mellitus was diagnosed in 4 out of 18 patients (22.22%). Heart failure and peripheral artery disease were observed in only two ANCA-positive patients (11.11%). Interestingly, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) was identified in one patient (5.56%), whereas malignancy was diagnosed in three patients (16.67%). None of the patients had hyperthyroidism or end-stage kidney disease. Thromboembolic events were relatively uncommon and occurred in a minority of patients: deep vein thrombosis was observed in two patients (11.11%), while stroke, myocardial infarction, and another venous thrombotic event (upper-limb vein thrombosis) were each reported in one patient. No miscarriages were recorded among female patients. No statistically significant differences was observed between the study groups for any comorbidities. Detailed data are summarized in Table 3. Autoantibody profiles

All patients tested positive for antinuclear antibodies (ANA). In five patients (27.78%), the ANA titer was ≥ 1:20,480. Among ANCA-positive SLE patients, the most frequently detected ANA were anti-dsDNA (n = 11, 61.11%) and anti-SSA (n = 9, 50%). Anti-SSB antibodies were present in six patients (33.33%), anti-nucleosome antibodies in five patients (27.78%), and anti-histone antibodies in two patients (11.11%). Notably, anti-RNP antibodies were detected exclusively in anti-MPO–positive SLE patients (n = 4, 22.22% of ANCA-positive SLE patients), while anti-Sm antibodies were identified in one anti-MPO–positive patient (5.56% of ANCA-positive SLE patients).

Consistent with the study design, all patients were positive for ANCA. Half of the ANCA-positive SLE patients tested positive for anti-cardiolipin in class IgG and/or IgM antibodies. In contrast, lupus anticoagulant and anti–β2-glycoprotein I in class IgG and/or IgM antibodies were detected only in anti-MPO–positive patients (in one and two patients, respectively).

No statistically significant differences in autoantibody profiles were observed between the study groups. Detailed data are presented in Table 4. Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics and outcomes associated with ANCA-positivity in patients with SLE, as well as to identify distinguishing features between anti-MPO–positive and anti-PR3-positive subgroups. Although the prevalence of ANCA in SLE and its potential associations with patient outcomes have been explored in previous studies, the clinical implications remain incompletely understood. Emerging evidence highlights substantial heterogeneity in ANCA prevalence across SLE cohorts, with documented rates ranging from 0 to 53% [17–20]. In our cohort, ANCA was detected in 1.73% of patients, which falls within the wide range reported in the literature. Among ANCA-positive individuals, anti-MPO antibodies were identified in 72.22%, whereas anti-PR3 antibodies were detected in 27.78%. This distribution is consistent with previous reports indicating that anti-MPO antibodies are more frequently observed than anti-PR3 antibodies in patients with SLE [12, 18].

Such variability in reported ANCA prevalence in SLE may be attributed to differences in detection methods, population characteristics, and disease activity. Studies relying on IIF tend to report higher ANCA positivity rates than those using ELISA, likely reflecting the lower specificity and greater susceptibility to false-positive results associated with IIF [18]. As ANCA detection in our study was performed using ELISA, this may partly explain the relatively low prevalence observed. Beyond methodological considerations, ethnic and environmental factors may also contribute to heterogeneity across cohorts. Notably, a Tunisian cohort reported ANCA positivity in 53% of patients with SLE, highlighting the potential impact of genetic background and environmental exposures [20]. Disease activity represents another factor that may contribute to variability in autoantibody profiles and their clinical relevance. Several studies have suggested that ANCA positivity in SLE is associated with increased disease activity, reflected by higher SLEDAI scores observed in ANCA-positive patients compared with ANCA-negative individuals [21]. However, other reports have found no significant correlation between ANCA status and disease activity, reflecting heterogeneity in the literature, and underscoring the need for further research to clarify the clinical significance of ANCA in SLE [22].

Our demographic analysis revealed that 18 patients in our cohort were ANCA-positive, the majority of whom were women (88.89%). This finding aligns with the well-established female predominance of SLE, with epidemiological studies reporting a female-to-male ratio of approximately 6:1 [23]. In our cohort, the median age at SLE onset was 32 years in ANCA-positive patients and 35 years in ANCA-negative patients, consistent with prior research indicating that SLE predominantly affects individuals in early to mid-adulthood [24, 25]. Comparable demographic patterns have been described in previous studies evaluating ANCA-positive SLE cohorts, in which the distribution of sex and age closely mirrors that of the general SLE population [18]. In line with these observations, no significant differences in demographic characteristics were observed between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients, nor between the anti-PR3–positive and anti-MPO-positive groups in our cohort. These findings suggest that ANCA positivity is unlikely to define a distinct demographic subgroup within SLE.

The clinical course of SLE can differ markedly between individuals, reflecting heterogeneity in both organ involvement and symptom presentation. In the ANCA-positive group in our cohort, the most frequently observed clinical manifestations were hematological abnormalities, present in all patients, followed by constitutional symptoms and joint involvement, each observed in 88.89% of patients. Interestingly, Galeazzi et al. [12] reported a higher prevalence of arthritis in ANCA-positive patients, suggesting a possible association between ANCA status and musculoskeletal involvement. Most ANCA-positive patients in our cohort (72.22%) exhibited renal involvement, consistent with evidence suggesting that ANCA positivity may be linked to an increased risk of organ damage in SLE, particularly the development of LN [26, 27]. In a multicenter retrospective study, Lacetera et al. [17] reported that ANCA-positive LN was associated with a more severe disease course, characterized by proliferative histologic classes and a higher activity index. However, long-term renal outcomes, including chronic kidney function impairment, did not differ significantly between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients. This apparent discrepancy may be attributable to more aggressive immunosuppressive management in ANCA-positive patients, potentially mitigating the impact of greater baseline disease activity [28]. Several studies have suggested that elevated ANCA levels may be associated with poorer clinical outcomes, although the prognostic role of ANCA remains a subject of ongoing debate [19, 28]. Taken together, ANCA positivity may warrant closer clinical follow-up, particularly in patients with renal involvement [29]. Despite these observations, the overall distribution of clinical manifestations was comparable between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients in our cohort.

Several studies have examined the potential association between ANCA positivity and vasculitic manifestations in SLE. In a cross-sectional study by Nisihara et al. [18], ANCA-positive patients exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of peripheral vasculitis compared with ANCA-negative individuals. In contrast, earlier research by Pauzner et al. [22] did not demonstrate a consistent association between ANCA positivity and clinically evident vasculitis in SLE, highlighting substantial variability between studies. In our cohort, vasculitic involvement was relatively uncommon and occurred at comparable frequencies in ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients. Although our findings do not indicate a significantly higher frequency of overt vasculitic manifestations among ANCA-positive patients, evidence suggests that ANCA positivity in SLE may reflect partial overlap with AAV [30]. Although true SLE/AAV overlap syndrome appears to be rare, estimated to occur in approximately 2% of cases, several reports have suggested that some ANCA-positive SLE patients display clinical or histopathological features resembling those observed in AAV [14, 31, 32]. Notably, such overlap presentations are often associated with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and anti-MPO–positivity [33].

In addition to disease-specific manifestations, patients with SLE frequently present with comorbidities that negatively affect clinical trajectory and long-term outcomes [34]. Hypercholesterolemia, arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart failure are particularly relevant, as each of these conditions may independently contribute to the progression of renal impairment and further increase cardiovascular risk in this population [35–37]. Hansen et al. [38] demonstrated that diabetes mellitus in patients with SLE is associated with a significantly increased risk of progression to ESRD, highlighting the additive adverse effect of metabolic comorbidity on long-term renal outcomes. In our cohort, the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia, arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart failure among ANCA-positive patients was 61.11%, 55.56%, 22.22%, and 11.11%, respectively, with no significant differences compared with the ANCA-negative group. These findings are consistent with previous reports from other SLE cohorts, although variability across geographic regions and study designs should be acknowledged [39, 40]. Notably, no significant differences were observed in the prevalence of comorbidities between the anti-MPO-positive and anti-PR3-positive groups in our cohort.

The autoantibody profile of ANCA-positive SLE reflects the immunological heterogeneity of the disease rather than a distinct serological phenotype. In line with previous studies, ANCA positivity is commonly observed alongside classical lupus-associated autoantibodies [41]. In a retrospective study, Pan et al. [27] demonstrated a correlation between ANCA positivity and anti-dsDNA, anti-nucleosome, and anti-histone antibodies. In the ANCA-positive group in our cohort, all patients tested positive for ANA, with anti-dsDNA antibodies being the most frequently detected autoantibodies (61.11%), followed by anti-SSA antibodies (50.00%). Anti-nucleosome were present in a minority of patients in our cohort, detected in 27.78% of ANCA-positive individuals, respectively. Furthermore, Nishihara et al. [18] observed an association between anti-PR3 positivity and lupus anticoagulant, suggesting a potential coexistence of ANCA and antiphospholipid autoantibody profiles. In contrast, in our cohort, lupus anticoagulant and anti-β2-glycoprotein I were identified exclusively in anti-MPO–positive patients. However, we observed no statistically significant differences in autoantibody profiles between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients or between the anti-MPO–positive and anti-PR3-positive groups. Given the relatively small cohort size in our study, particularly within the anti-PR3-positive subgroup, these observations should be interpreted with caution, and further research is warranted to clarify the clinical significance of autoantibody profiles in ANCA-positive SLE, including potential differences between anti-MPO–positive and anti-PR3-positive patients.

Antimalarial agents, including chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine, play a pivotal role in the standard treatment protocols for SLE due to their broad immunosuppressive properties and established effects in managing disease activity, preventing relapses, and enhancing long-term outcomes [42]. Chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine were administered to 61.11% of ANCA-positive SLE patients in our cohort. Current evidence suggests that ANCA-positive SLE patients presenting with renal involvement often require intensified immunosuppressive therapy to achieve higher remission rates [21]. Accordingly, glucocorticosteroids in combination with cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate mofetil represent a standard induction regimen for proliferative LN [21]. Notably, Li et al. [43] demonstrated that mycophenolate mofetil may be associated with higher remission rates and more favorable outcomes than cyclophosphamide when used as induction therapy in patients with ANCA-positive LN. In our cohort, glucocorticosteroids were administered to nearly all patients, with 94.44% of ANCA-positive patients receiving this treatment. Cyclophosphamide was given to 61.11% of ANCA-positive patients, while mycophenolate mofetil was administered to 38.89% of patients, respectively, reflecting contemporary management strategies in SLE. In general, no marked differences in treatment patterns were observed between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative patients.

Potential differences in clinical presentation and long-term patient outcomes between anti-MPO-positive and anti-PR3-positive patients have been evaluated in numerous studies; however, the results remain inconsistent. In contrast to AAV, where disease phenotype and relapse risk differ significantly between anti-MPO-positive and anti-PR3-positive patients, the prognostic relevance of ANCA specificity in SLE has not been clearly established [44, 45]. Wang et al. [29] suggested that anti-MPO positivity may be associated with worse baseline renal function, although no significant difference in long-term outcomes was observed between the two groups. In contrast, Drooger et al. [46] reported a significantly lower survival rate in anti-PR3-positive patients compared with the anti-MPO-positive group. In our cohort, no significant differences were observed in demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, autoantibody profiles, or comorbidities between the anti-PR3-positive and anti-MPO-positive groups. The heterogeneity of evidence highlights the need for further studies to clarify potential clinical and prognostic distinctions between anti-MPO-positive and anti-PR3-positive patients.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduces a risk of selection and information bias. Second, the relatively small number of ANCA-positive patients, particularly in the anti-PR3 subgroup, reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses and may have limited the ability to detect significant differences between anti-MPO–positive and anti-PR3–positive patients. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. Third, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded, as ANCA testing was performed selectively rather than systematically at predefined time points, and longitudinal changes in ANCA titers were not assessed. Fourth, standardized disease activity assessment using instruments such as SLEDAI was not available. Given the retrospective design, the long mean disease duration, and the inclusion of patients at different stages of disease course and treatment, reliable comparison of disease activity over time was not feasible. Finally, histopathological data from kidney or skin biopsies were not available for all patients, which may have limited the precise differentiation between lupus-related and vasculitic organ involvement.

Conclusions

The present study provides valuable insight into the clinical characteristics of ANCA positivity in SLE and its emerging prognostic and therapeutic relevance. Although previous studies have suggested a potential association between ANCA positivity and organ damage, particularly renal involvement, such a relationship was not identified in our cohort. Nevertheless, close surveillance of ANCA titers in patients with SLE may still be warranted. ANCA-positive SLE patients often require aggressive immunosuppressive treatment to achieve optimal disease control and mitigate the risk of progressive kidney injury. Evidence regarding differences in disease phenotype and long-term outcomes between anti-MPO–positive and anti-PR3–positive patients remains inconsistent. Further prospective studies are needed to determine the clinical significance of ANCA and its potential utility in prognostic assessment and individualized management in SLE.
