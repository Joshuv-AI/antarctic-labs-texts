Prior cervical spine fusion impairs early clinical outcomes following total shoulder arthroplasty and may be associated with increased fusion burden: a matched cohort analysis
Authors: Jude Alawa, Alyssa D. Althoff, Ryan R. Thacher, Anthony Finocchiaro, Ryan Cheng, Robert Uzzo, Carl M. Cirino, David M. Dines, Michael C. Fu, Lawrence V. Gulotta, Francis C. Lovecchio, Gabriella E. Ode, Samuel A. Taylor, Christopher M. Brusalis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical spine pathology may influence outcomes following total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) through its biomechanical and neurologic effects on shoulder motion and function. This study compared post-operative clinical outcomes after TSA in patients with a history of cervical spine fusion vs. a matched cohort without cervical pathology.MethodsPatients who underwent anatomic or reverse TSA between 2016 and 2021 at a tertiary care institution were retrospectively reviewed. Nineteen patients with prior cervical spine fusion and complete pre-operative and post-operative patient-reported outcome measures were identified and matched 1:1 to controls without cervical pathology based on age, sex, laterality, diagnosis, and implant type. The American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function 10-item (PROMIS-PF 10) scores were analyzed. The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for ASES improvement was defined as ≥ 12 points.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant ASES improvement (P < .0001). ASES improved from 40.4 ± 20.6 to 66.3 ± 23.4 in the cervical fusion group and from 37.1 ± 22.5 to 85.2 ± 19.1 in controls (P < .0001 between groups). PROMIS-PF 10 improved slightly in the cervical fusion group (44.9 ± 7.1 to 48.5 ± 6.3) but remained stable in controls (54.1 ± 8.9 to 52.7 ± 7.5; P = .13). MCID achievement was comparable (68.4% vs. 89.5%, P = .23). Among fusion patients, single-level fusions had higher post-operative ASES and PROMIS-PF 10 scores than multilevel fusions, though MCID rates were similar.ConclusionPatients with a history of prior cervical spine fusion experience reduced clinical improvements following TSA at early follow-up compared to a matched cohort of patients without history of cervical spine pathology. Further studies are needed to assess whether this impairment is proportional or commensurate with the number of levels fused or their location within the cervical spine.

Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) remains a cornerstone in the management of advanced shoulder pathologies, including glenohumeral osteoarthritis, rotator cuff arthropathy, and proximal humeral fractures. In the United States, TSA utilization is projected to increase more than 120% by 2040, reflecting both expanded indications and favorable long-term outcomes.27 TSA has demonstrated substantial and durable improvements in various patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score and the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) upper extremity module.3,10 Nonetheless, complications following TSA and need for revision shoulder arthroplasty persist and prompt ongoing efforts to optimize clinical outcomes in primary TSA.4

Proper function of the shoulder girdle is influenced by both dynamic and static stabilizers. The interplay between the cervical spine, thoracic posture, and scapulothoracic rhythm each impact spatial orientation of the shoulder girdle.7,29 During overhead motion, cervical extension and axial rotation contribute to scapular upward rotation and posterior tilt, movements that optimize glenohumeral alignment and deltoid efficiency.15 Cervical spine pathology, such as spondylosis, radiculopathy, myelopathy, or prior arthrodesis, can disrupt this kinetic chain, leading to compensatory trunk extension, reduced scapular motion, and impaired shoulder elevation.13 These effects are particularly relevant in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA), where post-operative shoulder function is heavily dependent on deltoid mechanics and scapulothoracic motion.

Cervical spine disease is common among patients undergoing TSA. Griswold et al15 found that up to 32.6% of rTSA candidates presented with concurrent cervical pathology. In addition, recent studies have reported that patients with prior cervical spine fusion experience worse post-operative outcomes after shoulder arthroplasty, including lower functional scores, reduced range of motion, higher complication rates, and decreased satisfaction.5,30 Existing research, however, is limited by retrospective designs, heterogeneous cohorts, and a lack of matched controls. Moreover, no prior study has stratified outcomes based on fusion burden, a potentially important biomechanical factor.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to (1) assess the impact of prior cervical spine fusion on clinical outcomes after TSA and (2) evaluate whether greater fusion burden correlated with post-operative outcomes. We hypothesized that patients with a history of cervical fusion would have lower post-operative ASES scores and smaller functional gains compared to matched controls and that outcomes would worsen with increasing number of cervical fusion levels.

Methods

Study design and patient identification

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care orthopedic specialty hospital following institutional review board approval. Patients who underwent primary anatomic (aTSA) or rTSA between February 1, 2016, and December 1, 2021, were identified from an institutional registry of prospectively collected arthroplasty outcomes. Eligible indications included glenohumeral osteoarthritis and rotator cuff arthropathy. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision arthroplasty, surgery for fracture, fracture sequelae, or inflammatory arthropathy, or if baseline or post-operative ASES scores were unavailable.

Each patient was cross-referenced in the electronic medical record (Epic Systems, Verona, WI, USA) to identify those who had undergone a cervical spine procedure at any time. Operative notes were reviewed to confirm the procedure type; only cervical fusions performed for degenerative, nontraumatic pathology were included. These patients comprised the cervical fusion group, which was further stratified by fusion burden into single-level and multilevel subgroups. Patients without a history of cervical fusion served as the shoulder-only group.

Data collection

Demographic variables included age at shoulder arthroplasty, sex, body mass index, and race/ethnicity. Surgical variables included arthroplasty type (aTSA vs. rTSA), laterality, and operating surgeon. For patients in the cervical fusion group, the number of levels fused and the interval between cervical fusion and shoulder arthroplasty were recorded.

Because registry follow-up was incomplete, patients missing post-operative data were contacted through Research Electronic Data Capture and provided individualized survey links with automated reminders at 1 and two weeks. The analytic dataset included all patients with a baseline ASES score and a post-operative ASES score obtained at least 24 months after shoulder arthroplasty.

Outcome measures

The ASES score served as the primary outcome measure, and the PROMIS Physical Function 10-Item (PROMIS-PF10) was analyzed as a secondary outcome when available. Improvement was calculated as the post-operative minus pre-operative score for each measure. Achievement of the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) was defined as an improvement of ≥ 12 points in the ASES score.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized patient and surgical characteristics as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Paired t tests evaluated pre-operative to post-operative changes within each group. Independent t tests compared continuous variables between the cervical fusion and shoulder-only groups and between single- and multi-level fusion subgroups. Fisher exact tests were used for categorical comparisons, including MCID achievement. All analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance set at P < .05.26

Results

Cohort identification and matching

A total of 57 patients were identified who had undergone both cervical spine surgery and TSA during the study timeframe. Of these, 14 patients had cervical procedures other than fusion and were excluded. The remaining 43 patients had a history of cervical spine fusion prior to TSA. Among these, 19 patients had complete baseline and post-operative PROMs and were included in the final analysis (cervical fusion group).

A matched comparison cohort of 19 patients who underwent TSA without a history of cervical spine fusion (shoulder-only group) was identified from the institutional registry. Matching was performed based on age, sex, laterality, diagnosis, and implant type.

Patient-reported outcomes

Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in ASES following TSA (Table II). In the cervical fusion group, ASES improved from 40.4 ± 20.6 pre-operatively to 66.3 ± 23.4 post-operatively (P < .0001). In the shoulder-only group, ASES improved from 37.1 ± 22.5 to 85.2 ± 19.1 (P < .0001). Post-operative ASES and the magnitude of improvement were both greater in the shoulder-only group (P < .0001). For PROMIS-PF 10, scores increased modestly in the cervical fusion group (44.9 ± 7.1 to 48.5 ± 6.3), while they were stable to slightly lower in the shoulder-only group (54.1 ± 8.9 to 52.7 ± 7.5). Discussion

The principal finding is that patients with a history of cervical spine fusion had lower post-operative ASES scores and smaller improvements after TSA compared with a matched cohort without prior cervical fusion. Most patients still achieved clinically meaningful improvement based on ASES MCID, but absolute post-operative function remained lower in the fusion cohort. Within the fusion group, multilevel fusion was associated with lower post-operative scores than single-level fusion, although MCID achievement was similar between subgroups. While prior studies have identified an association between cervical spine disease and diminished outcomes after shoulder surgery, the present study uses a matched cohort design and introduces fusion burden (single- vs. multilevel) as a stratification variable to evaluate its impact on outcomes. These results support more personalized perioperative assessment in patients with dual pathology and provide a framework for future investigations into the interplay between spinal and shoulder disease.

Neck and shoulder pain represent 2 of the 3 most common musculoskeletal complaints in the United States, and their anatomic proximity contributes to a high rate of dual pathology.1,2,12,19,31 Up to 25% of patients over the age of 60 years with cervical spine disease have concurrent shoulder pathology, and roughly 1 in 25 orthopedic patients demonstrate a “crossover phenomenon,” in which pathology in one region manifests as symptoms in the other.19,28 Despite this clinical overlap, few studies have specifically examined how cervical spine disease—particularly cervical fusion—affect outcomes after shoulder arthroplasty. Tangtiphaiboontana et al30 found that patients with prior cervical fusion had significantly reduced post-operative forward elevation and higher reoperation rates following arthroplasty. Colasanti et al,5 in a cohort of 573 patients, reported that those with a history of cervical arthrodesis had lower ASES and Constant scores, diminished external rotation, higher complication rates, and reduced satisfaction compared to controls. Using a large national database, Griswold et al15 demonstrated that prior cervical fusion was associated with increased rates of emergency department visits, complications, and revision surgery. Together, these studies suggest a negative association between cervical spine pathology and shoulder arthroplasty outcomes. Our findings build on this limited literature by employing a matched cohort design and introducing fusion burden as a novel stratification variable, offering further insight into the impact of cervical pathology on functional recovery after arthroplasty.

However, a systematic review by Dekhne et al9 found that outcomes in patients with dual cervical and shoulder pathology were variable, with some studies showing worse outcomes and others showing no significant differences. Notably, few studies stratified by cervical pathology type, severity, or surgical history, and most treated it as a binary variable. Our study builds on this literature by shifting the focus to shoulder arthroplasty and introducing cervical fusion burden as a critical modifier of post-operative recovery. Rather than simply assessing the presence of cervical pathology, we demonstrate that the extent of prior cervical fusion may influence functional gains after shoulder replacement surgery. This distinction may have important implications for pre-operative risk stratification and rehabilitation planning.

The association between cervical spine pathology and diminished outcomes following shoulder arthroplasty is likely multifactorial, involving both biomechanical and neurologic mechanisms.11,18,18,19,21 Cervical malalignment—especially in the setting of thoracic kyphosis or sagittal imbalance—has been shown to alter scapulothoracic mechanics and restrict glenohumeral motion.8,23 Recent work using pre-operative planning software has demonstrated that accounting for scapulothoracic orientation significantly alters simulated range of motion in rTSA, underscoring the clinical importance of posture and global alignment in surgical planning and outcome prediction.24 These postural deviations may necessitate modified implant positioning in rTSA; for instance, increased humeral retroversion has been recommended in kyphotic patients to better align with altered scapular orientation.14,25 In addition, scapular dyskinesis, frequently observed in individuals with cervical pathology, has been associated with increased rotator cuff strain, decreased shoulder range of motion, and delayed recovery following surgical intervention.6,20,32 Cervical fusion may further impair post-operative outcomes by contributing to adjacent segment disease and progressive foraminal stenosis.16,22 The C5 and C6 nerve roots, responsible for innervating the suprascapular and axillary nerves, are particularly vulnerable.17,32 Even in the absence of overt symptoms, degenerative changes at these levels may compromise shoulder girdle function and impair recovery.

Our findings, in combination with existing literature, highlight the complex interplay between cervical spine disease and shoulder pathology, particularly in older adults with high rates of coexisting musculoskeletal conditions. While most prior studies have treated cervical involvement as a binary variable, our introduction of fusion burden as a stratification metric suggests that the extent of cervical pathology meaningfully impacts functional recovery following shoulder arthroplasty. These findings have important implications for pre-operative risk stratification, shared decision-making, and rehabilitation planning. Given these mechanisms, comprehensive pre-operative evaluation, including cervical imaging and neurologic assessment, should be considered standard in shoulder arthroplasty candidates with known or suspected cervical pathology. Future research should investigate whether pre-operative spinal alignment, degree of nerve root involvement, or presence of scapular dyskinesis can serve as prognostic indicators for arthroplasty outcomes.

Despite growing recognition of dual cervical–shoulder pathology, high-quality evidence to inform treatment sequencing, surgical decision-making, and long-term management remains limited. Future investigations should evaluate the comparative outcomes of treating cervical vs. shoulder pathology first, assess the influence of specific cervical fusion levels and constructs on post-operative shoulder function, and characterize the impact of cervical disease on recovery trajectories following shoulder intervention. Additional studies are warranted to explore the reciprocal effect of shoulder surgery on cervical symptoms and alignment. Moreover, clarifying the contributions of fusion burden, cervical alignment, scapulothoracic mechanics, and neurologic integrity to patient-reported outcomes will be essential for refining pre-operative risk stratification. These areas of investigation are critical for developing evidence-based care pathways for patients with coexisting cervical spine and shoulder pathology. Prospective, multicenter studies that incorporate both cervical- and shoulder-specific outcome measures, standardized rehabilitation protocols, and multidisciplinary collaboration will be essential to advancing clinical care in this population.

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the study is subject to the limitations of a retrospective analysis, including selection bias and reporting bias. We excluded 24 patients from the cervical fusion group due to lacking baseline and/or follow-up PROMs. As a study performed at an urban, tertiary care center, our findings may not be fully generalizable to other practice settings or broader populations. While we attempted to reduce confounding by comparing patients across cohorts with similar sex distribution and surgical indications, we did not perform exact 1:1 matching, and unmeasured confounders may remain. Variables such as baseline cervical alignment, severity of neurologic symptoms, duration since fusion, and use of physical therapy were not consistently available and may have influenced outcomes. We also did not collect cervical spine–specific outcome measures, limiting our ability to assess how shoulder arthroplasty affected concurrent cervical symptoms. Finally, the study group and subgroup with multilevel cervical fusion was relatively small, which may have reduced statistical power to detect certain between-group differences. Future prospective, multicenter studies with cervical-specific metrics and standardized evaluation protocols are warranted to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

Patients with a history of prior cervical spine fusion experience reduced clinical improvements following TSA at early follow-up compared to a matched cohort of patients without history of cervical spine pathology. Further studies are needed to assess whether this impairment is proportional or commensurate with the number of levels fused or their location within the cervical spine.
