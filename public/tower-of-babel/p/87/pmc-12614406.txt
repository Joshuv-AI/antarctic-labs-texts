Introduction

Traumatic injury is a common emergency presentation in both people and animals. It is one of the leading causes of death in both populations, particularly among younger individuals [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. It is crucial for emergency teams to recognize common trauma presentations, understand methods of trauma severity assessment, and have the confidence to implement first‐line treatments to optimize patient care and outcomes. Human trauma care systems have been established all over the world, leading to significant improvements in patient care and outcomes [1, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Many of these systems are supported by trauma registries that are vital to providing current data to improve the quality of trauma care. They form the basis for new approaches to patient evaluation and care, both at the individual and institutional levels [8, 9, 10, 11].

The American College of Veterinary Emergency and Critical Care Veterinary Committee on Trauma (ACVECC‐VetCOT or “VetCOT”) established the veterinary trauma registry in 2013 to serve as a foundation for a global veterinary trauma network [12]. Since the inception of the VetCOT registry, three iterations of registry summary reports have been published, spanning from 2013 to 2021 [12, 13, 14]. All summary reports share the goal of providing a descriptive overview of dog and cat trauma patients over a specific timeframe. In addition to providing an overview of sustained trauma with the variables provided in the registry, this report also describes trauma etiology as it relates to geographic region and human population density.

Materials and Methods

Twenty‐two Veterinary Trauma Centers (VTCs) contributed data for dogs and cats with evidence of trauma that presented to their facility between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2023. VetCOT defines trauma as “any tissue injury that occurs suddenly as a result of an external force” [12]. Data were entered into the trauma registry database, which uses the Research Electronic Data Capture (“REDCap”) system hosted at the Colorado Clinical and Translational Sciences Institute [15, 16]. Case data required for inclusion in the report were patient information (e.g., species, breed, age, weight, sex, and neuter status), injury‐related information (e.g., type, cause, and prehospital care), trauma severity (e.g., modified Glasgow Coma Scale, animal trauma triage score, and VetCOT score), and outcome (discharged, died prior to discharge, and euthanized; reason for euthanasia was indicated as due to grave prognosis, financial constraints, both, or other reason). Diagnostics and treatments were performed at the attending clinicians’ discretion and were considered “optional” data entry in the registry. Diagnostic data included point‐of‐care bloodwork parameters (packed cell volume; total plasma protein, glucose, lactate, and ionized calcium concentrations; and base excess) and point‐of‐care ultrasound descriptors. Treatment data included blood product administration, surgical procedures (whether in the emergency room or operating room), and mechanical ventilation.

Compared to previous summary reports, three major additions have been made to this iteration: (1) environmental trauma (including heat stroke, electrocution, smoke inhalation, and drowning) was included as a new mechanism of injury, (2) surgical procedures were subcategorized as “major” or “minor” surgery (major surgeries were defined as those ideally performed in operating rooms or involving entry into a body cavity, and minor surgeries were defined as those typically not performed in operating rooms), and (3) data were further categorized into trauma type as a function of geographic region and human population density.

To maintain confidentiality, the participating hospitals were not identified by name but were subcategorized according to practice setting (university or private), VTC level (I, II, or III, assigned based on the VTCs’ resources and the verification guidelines set forth by VetCOT), geographic region (Northeast, South, Midwest, West [all of the United States]; international), and human population density of the hospitals’ locations (rural, defined as human population <50,000; or urban, defined as human population >50,000) [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Any hospital that was the sole representative of a specific category (such as the sole level III VTC and sole VTC in the US South) was excluded from any subcategorization that would compromise anonymity.

Statistics were performed using commercially available softwarea, and stacked pie charts were created with Pythonb. Categorical data are reported as absolute number and percentage. Trauma cases often involve multiple types of trauma (combination trauma) as well as multiple types of interventions; therefore, reported case numbers and percentages often do not equal 100%. Continuous data are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Evaluation of data to identify outliers or nonsensical values resulting from data entry error was not performed.

Results

In calendar years 2022–2023, 9,820 trauma case records (8,130 dogs and 1,690 cats) were entered into the registry by veterinary hospitals in the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom (n = 22). Seven VTCs were university and 15 were private practices. There were four level I VTCs (three university and one private practice), 17 level II VTCs (four university and 13 private practices), and one level III VTC (private practice). Geographically, seven VTCs were located in the Northeast, one in the South, six in the Midwest, and four in the West of the United States, and four internationally. Nine VTCs were located in rural settings and 13 were urban.

The median age of dogs and cats was 4 years (IQR 6.6) and 3 years (IQR 7), respectively. The median weight of dogs and cats was 19.2 kg (IQR 21.6), and 4.4 kg (IQR 2.1), respectively. Discussion

The top three causes of trauma in dogs were penetrating bite wounds (35% of all dog trauma cases), vehicular strikes (17%), and lacerations (12%). In cats, unknown blunt trauma (22% of all cat trauma cases), penetrating bite wounds (20%), and falls from heights (14%) were the leading causes of trauma. Understanding these trends enables veterinary teams to better prepare for triage, stabilization, and care of these trauma patients. Additionally, this information should be disseminated to the general public to facilitate the development of prevention strategies, which may include promoting driver awareness and safety measures for pet owners living in high‐rise dwellings.

Importantly, this is the first VetCOT registry report to include environmental trauma. Although uncommon, cases of burns from fires, smoke inhalation, electrocution, and drowning are often serious, as indicated by their relatively higher fatality rates, and warrant careful attention when presenting to the emergency room and being managed in the intensive care unit.

Overall, survival to discharge remains high in both dogs and cats (93.1% and 83.7%, respectively), which is consistent with survival rates reported in previous registry reports [12, 13, 14]. However, also consistent with previous reports, there was variability in the survival for specific trauma subtypes. High‐mortality trauma events included vehicular trauma (whether vehicular strikes or animals injured inside of vehicles) and drowning (a subtype of environmental trauma). It is worth noting that a small proportion of patients that survived to discharge returned to the hospital for additional care (approximately 8% of total cases for both species). The most common reasons listed for return after initial discharge were planned surgical procedures and wound infections; other reasons for return included euthanasia (or were dead upon return arrival) and owners’ inability to manage the discharged patient (e.g., bandage care, esophageal tube management). These findings indicate that there are important aspects of client education that the emergency team must convey to owners regarding necessary case follow‐up and at‐home patient care.

The prevalence of trauma types across geographic regions was similar except for porcupine quilling, which was reported primarily in the Northeast of the United States. Vehicular strikes and porcupine quilling were commonly seen in rural VTCs, with falls from heights and nonpenetrating bite wounds commonly seen in urban VTCs. However, the rural versus urban classification was based on the human population density of the town or city in which the VTC was located and not necessarily where the traumatic injury occurred, introducing a potential source of misclassification.

As noted in previous registry reports, there are limitations in the structure and function of the VetCOT registry database and the associated reports, including complete, accurate, and timely data entry; selection and information bias; and variation in the number, VTC level, and VTC status of participating hospitals.

Conclusion

The VetCOT registry and summary reports provide a vital framework to support the advancement of veterinary trauma care. With each iteration, the trauma registry is becoming increasingly robust, allowing for further evaluation of detailed criteria. Continued refinement of data collection methods and broader participation of VTCs will enhance the registry's utility as a tool for improving trauma care and outcomes in veterinary medicine.
