Effect of six weeks of dynamic cervical PNF training on neck disability index in men with video display terminal syndrome: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Arash Joodaki, Seyed Sadredin Shojaedin, Amir Letafatkar, Behrouz Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic neck pain is increasingly common among digital device users due to prolonged static postures. Evidence is limited regarding interventions targeting both neuromuscular control and proprioception. Cervical Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation (PNF) with Hold-Relax is hypothesized to enhance neuromuscular control and proprioception, improving muscle relaxation and reducing joint position errors (JPE) through sensory-motor coordination. This study evaluated the effectiveness of six weeks of dynamic cervical PNF training on Neck Disability Index (NDI) in men with VDT syndrome. In this prospective, randomized controlled trial, 30 participants aged 18–25 years with VDT syndrome were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 15) or a control group (n = 15). The primary outcome, functional disability, was measured using the NDI. Secondary outcomes included cervical Range of Motion (ROM), Flexion–Relaxation Ratio (FRR), proprioception assessed via the JPE test, and pain measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Assessments were conducted before and after the six-week intervention using MyoMotion sensors, electromyography (EMG), and a laser-equipped helmet. Data were analyzed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and independent t-tests. Baseline demographics and outcomes did not differ significantly between groups. After six weeks, the experimental group demonstrated significant improvements in NDI (P < 0.05), ROM (P < 0.05), FRR (P < 0.05), and JPE (P < 0.05), along with a significant reduction in pain (P < 0.05) compared with controls. Six weeks of dynamic cervical PNF training significantly improved NDI, cervical mobility, neuromuscular control, proprioception, and reduced pain in young adults with VDT-related neck disorders. These findings provide novel evidence supporting the use of integrated PNF and proprioceptive exercises as a safe and non-invasive rehabilitation strategy, as well as a foundation for personalized therapeutic programs for digital device users.Trial registration: This study was retrospectively registered at https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/ with the registration number IRCT20240521061862N2 on 2024/09/26.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43349-3.

Introduction

In recent years, especially after the COVID-19 pandemic, increased use of computers and smartphones among university students has led to a rise in musculoskeletal disorders, with neck pain being highly prevalent1. Recent evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis in Ethiopia reported that approximately 65.9% of computer users experience musculoskeletal disorders, with the neck (38.2%) and lower back (44.4%) being the most affected regions2. Key factors include poor posture, prolonged device use without breaks, and inadequate ergonomics, particularly during remote work. Forward head posture and scapular dysfunction further contribute to neck pain development3. Psychological factors such as kinesiophobia, anxiety, and depression also play a role in chronic neck pain by promoting movement avoidance and functional decline4. Despite the high prevalence of neck pain among VDT users, there is a lack of clear evidence regarding the effectiveness of current interventions, which mainly focus on strengthening deep cervical flexors and cervical spine mobilization. Newer approaches combining strengthening and self-mobilization show promise5. Yet the complex interaction between posture, psychological factors, and musculoskeletal dysfunction remains insufficiently understood. This represents a critical scientific gap and forms the primary problem addressed in this study.

With the rapid advancement of digital technologies and the expansion of internet-based services, daily use of computers and smartphones has significantly increased3. This trend is driven by factors such as cultural adaptation to modern technologies, growth of service-based economies, sedentary work environments, and the widespread integration of smart devices in educational settings6,7. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong association between VDT use and chronic neck pain, identifying it as one of the most frequently reported musculoskeletal complaints among computer users1,7. Individuals suffering from neck pain typically exhibit increased activity in the cervical flexor muscles, anterior scalene, and upper trapezius, along with a reduced ability to achieve muscular relaxation5. Neuromuscular dysfunction and limited cervical mobility are commonly observed in this population8,9.

One valuable metric for assessing neuromuscular alterations is the FRR, which is a sensitive indicator of motor control impairments9,10. Forward head posture, prevalent during prolonged VDT use, places excessive mechanical stress on cervical ligaments and muscles, ultimately reducing mobility11. Prolonged static postures also increase joint loading and muscle fatigue, contributing to ROM limitations and pain. These changes disrupt postural reflexes and proprioception, negatively impacting neuromuscular coordination and joint flexibility12,13. Although ergonomic interventions such as adjusting monitor height, keyboard positioning, and improving sitting posture are commonly employed to manage VDT-related neck pain, they are not always sufficient14,15. Alternative approaches, including electrotherapy, proprioceptive exercises, stretching, and relaxation techniques, have shown promise in alleviating musculoskeletal discomfort among computer users12,16. Exercise therapy can improve posture control, coordination, and range of motion, thereby reducing pain and associated symptoms such as dizziness17,18.

PNF, particularly the Hold-Relax Technique (HRT) has recently gained attention as an innovative method for stimulating neuromuscular pathways and enhancing flexibility19. However, due to its relatively novel application, limited research has investigated its effectiveness in managing chronic neck pain20. A recent study by Lindegård et al. (2024) demonstrated that combining ergonomic education with biofeedback could effectively reduce pain intensity among computer users. Moreover, emerging evidence based on machine learning highlights the multifactorial and dynamic nature of neck pain in digital professionals, emphasizing the need for individualized, mechanism-based rehabilitation programs6. Despite the growing interest in PNF and proprioceptive interventions, few studies have comprehensively examined how such interventions simultaneously influence neuromuscular control, proprioception, and functional outcomes in individuals with chronic neck pain caused by prolonged VDT use, representing a critical gap in the literature. The novelty of the present study lies in implementing a six-week dynamic cervical PNF training protocol designed to target both mechanical and sensory pathways of the cervical spine, thereby addressing deficits in motor control and proprioceptive function concurrently.

Therefore, the main problem addressed in this study is the lack of evidence on how dynamic cervical PNF training affects neuromuscular control and proprioception in individuals with chronic neck pain associated with prolonged computer use.

The scope of this research is limited to assessing the effects of a six-week dynamic cervical PNF program on ROM, FRR, proprioception, and pain intensity in young adults with chronic neck pain.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sport Sciences Research Institute (Approval Code: SSRI.REC.2211.1933). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Trial registration

The trial was registered retrospectively on the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) at https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/ under the registration number IRCT20240521061862N2 on 26/09/2024, after completion of participant recruitment. Recruitment of participants meeting the inclusion criteria occurred between 06/12/2021, and 08/02/2022. The study protocol, eligibility criteria, intervention procedures, and primary outcome (NDI) were predefined and were not altered after the start of the trial.

Participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Recruitment was conducted through university-wide announcements under the supervision of the attending physician and by two of the researchers. Due to the nature of the interventions, participants were necessarily aware of their group assignment. Outcome assessors were intended to be masked to group allocation, though complete blinding could not be guaranteed. Data management and statistical analysis were performed by two investigators (A.L. and B.J.) who were blinded to group codes and were not involved in the randomization, exercise implementation, or test administration. All authors confirmed the accuracy and completeness of the data and adherence to the pre-specified protocol. Participant enrollment and randomization procedures followed the CONSORT 2010 guidelines. Participants

Initially, 38 male university students aged between 18 and 25 years, all diagnosed with chronic neck pain associated with VDT use, were recruited for the study. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of VDT-related neck pain, (2) forward head posture, (3) use of smart devices for at least 20 h per week, (4) a NDI raw score of ≥ 10 points (on the 0–50 scale) and (5) a VAS score of ≥ 4, indicating at least moderate pain and disability.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of cervical spine surgery or trauma (e.g., whiplash injury, fracture, or dislocation); (2) presence of medical “red flags” such as infection, tumors, rheumatoid arthritis, or cardiovascular disease; (3) current use of pain medications, ongoing physiotherapy, or participation in neck or shoulder exercise programs within the past 6 months; and (4) severe acute neck pain or diagnosed psychological disorders.

A licensed physician conducted the initial screening process. The study faced challenges in recruitment, failing to reach the initially planned sample size of 38. Ultimately, 38 individuals were assessed for eligibility. Eight of these were excluded based on eligibility criteria or due to voluntary withdrawal prior to randomization, resulting in a final sample of 30 participants. These 30 individuals were then randomly assigned to two groups (n = 15 per group): an experimental group receiving dynamic cervical PNF training, and a control group receiving no intervention.

Comprehensive medical history taking and physical examination were conducted to ensure accurate identification of eligible participants. To maintain methodological rigor and ensure consistency in participant selection, two experienced investigators (S.S.H. and A.L.), each with over 10 years of clinical experience, were responsible for overseeing the inclusion and exclusion procedures.

Participants in the intervention group performed dynamic cervical PNF training three times weekly for six weeks. Each session involved targeted dynamic movements designed to mobilize the cervical spine and activate deep sensory receptors. Interventions

Participants in the intervention group performed dynamic cervical PNF Exercise Program three times weekly for six weeks. Each session involved targeted dynamic movements designed to mobilize the cervical spine and activate deep sensory receptors. The intervention protocol was developed based on evidence from previous studies and established guidelines in the field of dynamic cervical PNF training (Table 1).

Before the intervention, an athletic coach (A.J.) with over five years of experience in manual therapy and soft tissue techniques provided participants with both theoretical and practical instruction to ensure correct execution of the exercises (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Sample exercises performed in this study. (a) illustrates left neck extension, while (b) depicts right neck flexion.

Sample exercises performed in this study. (a) illustrates left neck extension, while (b) depicts right neck flexion.

In addition, all training sessions were supervised by a licensed physiotherapist, who holds a professional clinical certification in physiotherapy and has more than 15 years of experience in musculoskeletal rehabilitation. The physiotherapist ensured correct performance of the PNF techniques, monitored participants’ safety, and adjusted exercise intensity when needed in accordance with institutional ethical standards.

The applied pressure during the intervention was adjusted to the maximum level tolerated by participants—pushing into discomfort but not pain—following guidelines from previous studies22. Subjective perceptions of effort were recorded and analyzed at the conclusion of the intervention. Although the training protocol was predefined, exercises were individually adjusted and progressively increased over the six-week period based on each participant’s tolerance and improvement. Cervical ROM, applied resistance during the Hold–Relax Technique, and exercise duration were gradually progressed under researcher supervision to optimize neuromuscular control and proprioception.

Participants in the control group had chronic neck pain but no progressive symptoms requiring urgent treatment. During the 6-week study period, they were instructed to maintain their usual daily activities. The research team monitored participants regularly to ensure safety and adherence. Any participant experiencing issues received immediate clinical care, and the intervention was offered to all control participants after the study period. Delaying the exercise program during the study posed no risk to participants’ health.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was functional disability, assessed using the NDI. This validated, self-administered questionnaire includes 10 items evaluating various aspects of daily activities affected by neck pain—pain intensity, personal care, lifting, reading, headaches, concentration, work, driving, sleeping, and recreation. Each item is scored from 0 (no limitation) to 5 (severe limitation). The total score is converted into a percentage to represent the overall disability level, classified as: 0–8% (no disability), 10–28% (mild), 30–48% (moderate), 50–64% (severe), and 70–100% (complete disability). The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was considered to be 6.4 points, consistent with previous studies5.

The secondary outcomes included pain intensity, cervical ROM, FRR, and JPE test. These measures were selected to comprehensively evaluate neuromuscular and biomechanical changes resulting from the intervention. Assessments were performed at baseline and after completion of the six-week training program.

Pain Intensity (Visual Analog Scale – VAS)

Neck pain intensity was assessed using the VAS, a well-established and reliable measurement tool23. The VAS comprises a 100 mm horizontal line with endpoints labeled “no pain” (0) and “worst imaginable pain”24. Only participants who reported a pain score of 4 or higher—indicative of moderate to severe pain—were included in the study. Those with scores of 3 or lower, reflecting mild or no pain, were excluded. The MCID for the VAS was defined as 2.76 points, based on findings from previous research25.

Cervical ROM

Standardized instructions on posture and movement were provided before assessment. Cervical ROM in flexion, extension, lateral flexion, and axial rotation was measured using the Noraxon MyoMotion system. Participants were seated, with sensors placed on the C7 vertebra and occiput. Kinematic data were recorded and analyzed using Noraxon MR3 software (v3.10.64) under standardized laboratory conditions to ensure accuracy and reproducibility (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Measurement of cervical flexion-relaxation ratio using MyoMotion sensors.

Measurement of cervical flexion-relaxation ratio using MyoMotion sensors.

Flexion-Relaxation Ratio (FRR) was measured using surface EMG (Noraxon). Electrodes were placed bilaterally at C4. The FRR was calculated as the mean EMG amplitude during full-flexion hold divided by the mean amplitude during quiet standing (baseline), where a higher ratio indicates better relaxation. The protocol followed SENIAM recommendations for validity and reliability26, and showed excellent intra-session reliability (ICC > 0.90) in our lab.

Cervical proprioception: JPE test

Cervical proprioception was evaluated using the JPE test, as described by Dinesh et al. (2022). A laser pointer was mounted on a headband worn by the participant, who sat in a relaxed posture with back support, facing a calibrated target board positioned 90 cm away27.

Participants first performed full head rotations to minimize vestibular input, then gradually returned to what they perceived as the neutral position, verbally indicating, “I feel my neck is in a neutral position.” The point where the laser beam hit the target was marked as the reference point (Fig. 4). Next, with eyes closed, participants rotated their heads to the right and left through their full available ROM in the transverse plane. After each rotation, they attempted to return to the initial neutral position. The resulting laser projection was marked as the target point. The distance (in centimeters) between the reference and target points was measured. This procedure was repeated three times in each direction, with each attempt held for two seconds. The average error across the three trials was calculated for both directions28,29.Fig. 4Joint position error test.

Joint position error test.

This method has demonstrated moderate to good reliability (ICC = 0.71). The minimal detectable change is approximately 0.51 cm, indicating that a change greater than this value reflects a true clinical difference beyond measurement error. A lower JPE value (in cm) signifies improved proprioceptive acuity and reduced cervical repositioning error30.

All devices were calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before each session. Outcome assessors were trained in standardized measurement procedures to ensure high reliability and consistency across participants.

Randomization and allocation

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the Control or Intervention group at the university clinic by the two corresponding authors (A.J. and S.S.H.). Random numbers were generated from a random number table and placed into sealed, opaque envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. Participants who received an odd number were assigned to the Control group, while those with an even number were assigned to the Intervention group. Participants, outcome assessors, and statistical analysts were blinded to group allocation; however, trainers administering the intervention were aware of the group assignment due to the nature of the study design.

Sample size determines

Sample size estimation was based on data from previous non-randomized, prospective, and retrospective studies15,31 and was calculated using SPSS (version 2021). Assuming a standard deviation of 3.5 for changes in the score and a 10% attrition rate over a 6-week follow-up period, a total sample size of 38 participants (19 per group) was estimated to provide 90% statistical power to detect a between-group difference of 6.4 points in NDI scores at a two-sided significance level of 0.05.

Due to recruitment constraints and participant availability during the study period, a total of 30 participants (15 per group) were ultimately included. A post-hoc power analysis based on the observed effect size for the primary outcome (NDI; Cohen’s d = 1.0) and the final sample size indicated a statistical power of 0.97, exceeding the conventional threshold of 0.80.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For continuous variables, independent t-tests were used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons across the approximately 10 outcome measures, the Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.005. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (P > 0.05). Since the test results were not significant across all experimental conditions, it was concluded that the distribution of all measured data was normal. Therefore, the mean was used as the measure of central tendency, and parametric tests were applied to analyze the study hypotheses. Additionally, the results of the independent samples t-test during the pretest phase indicated no significant differences between the control and experimental groups across all measured variables (Table 4), confirming that the groups were homogeneous before the intervention.

However, the independent samples t-test results revealed significant differences between the control and experimental groups in range of motion, flexion-relaxation ratio, proprioception, and pain variables. All reported p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons, with an adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.005.

Consequently, the null hypothesis was rejected, and the research hypothesis was supported. For all outcome measures, group differences were assessed using independent t-tests. Due to the unavailability of the original dataset, standardized effect sizes (SMD) and confidence intervals could not be calculated. Reporting these measures in future studies is recommended to strengthen the statistical interpretation and clinical relevance of the findings.

Discussion

This study evaluated the combined effects of dynamic cervical PNF training on neuromuscular control, proprioception, pain, and NDI in men with VDT-related neck disorders. The intervention significantly improved cervical ROM, FRR, JPE, pain, and NDI, highlighting enhanced motor control, reduced muscle tension, and meaningful gains in daily functional performance5,9,10,15,32–36. The primary outcome, NDI, decreased by 12.69 points in the Experimental group, exceeding the established MCID of 6.4 points, while VAS decreased by 3.8 points, surpassing the MCID of 2.76. These improvements were therefore not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, likely perceptible by participants themselves, whereas the Control group changes did not reach these thresholds. In addition, improvements in proprioception (JPE reduction of 1.1 cm, ~ 17% increase in accuracy) suggest enhanced cervical joint position sense, facilitating more prompt postural adjustments during prolonged computer use. Increases in FRR indicate greater relaxation capacity of cervical muscles, potentially disrupting the common muscle tension–pain cycle. Together with increased cervical ROM, these changes demonstrate a logical chain from sensory input (proprioception) to motor control (muscle relaxation) to functional output (ROM, pain reduction, and reduced disability).

The observed benefits likely result from decreased muscle stiffness, enhanced facet joint function, and post-isometric relaxation induced by the HRT2,10,20,32,33,37. PNF exercises also stimulate sensory neurons, improve neuromuscular coordination, reduce muscle tightness, and improve spinal biomechanics, thereby reducing mechanical stress and pain5,8,13. Compared with previous studies that focused on isolated interventions, this study demonstrates a practical, integrated approach addressing mechanical, sensory, and functional deficits simultaneously. A recent network meta-analysis demonstrated that PNF techniques were among the most effective components for reducing cervical pain (SMD = -1.45, 95% CI: -1.88 to -1.02), along with cervical traction and articular treatment. This finding reinforces the current study’s results, emphasizing that PNF approaches can play a pivotal role in pain reduction and functional recovery among individuals with cervical musculoskeletal disorders38.

Pain reduction and NDI improvement were key outcomes, stemming from better ROM, reduced muscle spasm, enhanced proprioception, and improved postural alignment5,9,36. These findings support the use of combined PNF and HRT as a safe, cost-effective, and evidence-based strategy for managing VDT-related neck dysfunction and functional impairment39,40.

Recent technological advances, including blockchain, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, and federated learning, have enhanced secure data management in healthcare. While promising, their application in personalized rehabilitation is limited. This study bridges these innovations with evidence-based neuromuscular and proprioceptive training, offering a foundation for future personalized interventions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The study population was highly selective, consisting only of young adult men (18–25 years) with severe neck disability, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other groups. The sample size was relatively small, and follow-up was short. Additionally, participants and therapists could not be blinded, which may have introduced performance and expectation biases (e.g., Hawthorne effect). Future studies with larger, more diverse samples and longer follow-up are needed to improve the external validity and robustness of the findings.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated that six weeks of cervical dynamic PNF training significantly improved functional disability (NDI), cervical ROM, FRR, proprioception, and pain in men with VDT-related neck disorders. Improvement in NDI highlights the clinical relevance and effectiveness of this integrated rehabilitation approach.

These findings provide evidence supporting the use of combined PNF and proprioceptive exercises as a safe, non-invasive strategy for managing neck pain and functional impairment associated with prolonged digital device use. Incorporating such exercises into therapeutic programs may enhance daily function, reduce pain, and improve quality of life. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples and longer follow-up are warranted to further validate and optimize this intervention (Supplementary Information).
