Identifying Optimal Acute Care Comparators to Inform the Evaluation of an Advanced Care at Home Pilot Program
Authors: Martin Elliott, Gina Rinetti-Vargas, Patricia Kipnis, Ariel R Herm, Kent Wong, Agnieszka Witkowski, Jesica Deputy, Vivian Reyes, Fernando Barreda, Laura C Myers, Vincent X Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHospital at Home (H@H) programs—which seek to deliver acute care within a patient’s home—have become more prevalent over time. However, existing literature exhibits heterogeneity in program structure, evaluation design, and target population size, making it difficult to draw generalizable conclusions to inform future H@H program design.ObjectiveThe objective of this work was to develop a quality improvement evaluation strategy for a H@H program—the Kaiser Permanente Advanced Care at Home (KPACAH) program in Northern California—leveraging electronic health record data, chart review, and patient surveys to compare KPACAH patients with inpatients in traditional hospital settings.MethodsThe authors developed a 3-step recruitment workflow that used electronic health record filtering tools to generate a daily list of potential comparators, a manual chart review of potentially eligible comparator patients to assess individual clinical and social criteria, and a phone interview with patients to affirm eligibility and interest from potential comparator patients.ResultsThis workflow successfully identified and enrolled a population of 446 comparator patients in a 5-month period who exhibited similar demographics, reasons for hospitalization, comorbidity burden, and utilization measures to patients enrolled in the KPACAH program.ConclusionThese initial findings provide promise for a workflow that can facilitate the identification of similar inpatients hospitalized at traditional brick and mortar facilities to enhance outcomes evaluations for the H@H programs, as well as to identify the potential volume of enrollees as the program expands.

Introduction

Hospitals are the default location for treating patients with acute illness; however, emerging evidence suggests that select acutely ill patients can be effectively treated at home. 1–3 Driven by capacity limitations in inpatient wards and emergency departments (EDs), advances in telehealth technology, an increasing focus on patient care experiences, and a desire to minimize hospital-associated complications, the value of hospital at home (H@H) programs has been under active investigation. 1–8 Early reports suggest that such programs can produce a diversity of beneficial outcomes, including the following: improved patient satisfaction; reduced adverse outcomes in appropriately enrolled patients; decreased iatrogenic events such as nosocomial infections, falls, and delirium; reduced lengths of stay; and even lowered acute care costs. 1–10 However, existing reports of H@H programs have demonstrated considerable variability in program design, patient enrollment criteria, and the services provided, and the benefits of different program designs remain uncertain. 3,5,9,11

Prior H@H program evaluations have included several outside the United States where health care delivery structure and financing vary. 2,5,8,9,11 A few randomized control trials have been performed in the United States, lending insight into the potential benefits of these H@H programs. However, because of the need to divide patients into intervention or control arms after eligibility is established, they often have small sample sizes and may limit insights into the costs and challenges associated with operating a H@H program at scale. 3,8,9,11 Given that reducing acute care costs is a motivating rationale for H@H programs and that the initial implementation costs are high and per-patient program costs are expected to decline as the scale of the program expands, 4 evaluation designs that maximize health system enrollment and also including contemporaneous brick and mortar comparators are needed.

The potential for beneficial outcomes for patients motivated the implementation of the Kaiser Permanente Advanced Care at Home (KPACAH) pilot program within the Kaiser Permanente Northern California integrated health care delivery system. An early consideration during implementation was the design of an evaluation strategy to enable outcomes comparisons of similar patients between those hospitalized in traditional settings (brick and mortar) and those enrolled into the KPACAH pilot program for quality improvement purposes.

The authors sought to develop such an evaluation strategy that maximized the number of patients enrolled and also identifying similar comparators undergoing contemporaneous traditional hospitalization. By leveraging a quasi-experimental design that included electronic risk adjustment indices, 12 manual chart review, and patient surveys, the aim was to minimize the bias that can arise from standard observational and retrospective evaluation designs. 7,8,10 This report describes the development of a comparator identification and assessment workflow for the KPACAH program and compares baseline characteristics between KPACAH-enrolled patients and comparators.

Methods

KPACAH program overview

The KPACAH program is designed to deliver a safe, high-quality, and convenient model to care for acutely ill patients in their home (see Patient Brochure, Supplemental Appendix A1). The project leverages an integrated care team and telehealth technology to provide a variety of hospital-level services and interventions, adapted for effective delivery in the home setting. In-home services offered by KPACAH include skilled nursing care, such as intravenous therapies, medication administration, wound care, Kaiser Permanente laboratory testing, mobile imaging services (including x-ray, ultrasound, and electrocardiogram), consultation with specialists, and specialized therapy services, such as physical, occupational, and speech language therapists (see Expanded Detail for Target Population, Target Population, Supplemental Appendix A2-A).

Despite the heterogeneity of H@H models presented in the literature, there is a general distinction drawn between programs that completely substitute for an inpatient hospital stay from the ED (ie, Acute Substitution or Admission Avoidance) and those that facilitate a reduction in inpatient length of stay (ie, Early Discharge). 4,7,8 Although Acute Substitution pathways have typically been shown to offer greater per-patient cost outcome benefits, both categories have been associated with positive effects on patient mortality, readmission rates, post-discharge utilization outcomes, and patient satisfaction. 2,5,8 Because of the more relaxed timeline for patient enrollment, Early Discharge models may facilitate increased program enrollment 8,9 and the expansion of treatment options because these patients have often already been medically stabilized in the hospital setting.

Kaiser Permanente Northern California Acute Substitution and Early Discharge models

In the KPACAH program, the Acute Substitution pathway targets patients in the ED prior to inpatient admission by identifying patients meeting standardized clinical and social criteria that would make safe and effective home care possible. KPACAH patients included those aged 18 years or older, living within the defined program catchment areas, holding Kaiser Permanente membership with commercial or Medicare insurance plans with a supplemental benefit for KPACAH (Medicare patients were approved for clinical enrollment in specific catchment areas starting January 1, 2022) and able to complete program eligibility interviews in English. Virtual KPACAH practitioners proactively screened patients in the ED for the appropriateness of the patient’s health, degree of need for home health support, and home accessibility using standardized clinical and social criteria (see Eligibility Screening Questionnaires, Supplemental Appendix A3A–A3C). Enrolled patients were then provided appropriate telehealth devices and durable medical equipment for care at home, with daily virtual clinician rounding and in-home visits from clinical staff to perform clinical interventions, therapies, and diagnostic studies. The process for the Early Discharge workflow was similar, except patients could be enrolled into the KPACAH program after hospital admission, based on ongoing daily assessment of the same criteria by inpatient teams. The KPACAH clinical program launched in 2 pilot facilities, serving their corresponding catchment areas, in September 2020.

KPACAH program evaluation design

The authors’ evaluation of the KPACAH program was designed to leverage a prospective natural experiment design, necessitating the recruitment of comparators undergoing traditional hospitalization at brick and mortar Kaiser Permanente Northern California hospitals who would have been potential KPACAH enrollees had the program been operational at their medical center. Working with the KPACAH clinical and operational teams, the authors defined the clinical and social criteria used to identify and characterize their eligible KPACAH patient populations. Then, to facilitate the identification of as many brick and mortar comparator patients as possible, a series of electronic and survey-based data collection tools were designed and incorporated into a 3-step workflow (see Comparator Recruitment Workflow with Criteria, Supplemental Appendix A6):

Each step is described in further detail below.

Step 1: Generation of daily EHR patient list through filtering tools

The brick and mortar comparator identification process began with an electronically generated daily list of patients at non-KPACAH facilities across Kaiser Permanente Northern California between Monday and Friday. To mimic KPACAH criteria, electronically screened patients were Kaiser Permanente Northern California members who had commercial insurance or Medicare plans, were aged 18 years and older, and were admitted to the hospitalist service from the ED during the previous 24 h. These screening lists were further refined using several clinical severity scores: Emergency Severity Index (ESI), 13 Laboratory-Based Acute Physiological Score version 2 (LAPS2), and Comorbidity Point Score version 2 (COPS2), 14 which have been validated as predictive for a patient’s outcome. 15 The ESI, LAPS2, and COPS2 eligibility cutoffs were established based on prior literature, EHR auditing and review of KPACAH patients over several weeks of iterative list generation, and feedback from KPACAH clinicians. Ultimately, patients who had ESI scores of 1 or 5 (who were likely to be too sick or too well, respectively, to qualify for KPACAH enrollment), ED maximum LAPS2 scores < 100 (a cut point reflective of patients often receiving care in a stepdown or Intensive Care Unit setting), and ED maximum COPS2 scores < 110 (a significant increased chronic comorbid disease burden) were excluded (see Electronic health record screening criteria for KPACAH comparators, Supplemental Appendix A4).

Step 2: Daily chart review: Criteria for identifying candidates

These EHR screening criteria produced daily lists of between 40 and 60 potential comparators who were individually assessed by a staff member (Martin Elliott) through a chart review of hospital encounters. Data were recorded for each patient’s clinical presentation, signs and symptoms, medical history, and pertinent social health data according to the criteria adapted from clinical and social screening questionnaires used by KPACAH program clinicians (see Eligibility Screening Questionnaires, Supplemental Appendix A3) in addition to criteria drawn from prior literature. 3,10,16 Data were collected in a standardized database developed for the project (see Expanded Detail for Inclusion / Exclusion, Supplemental Appendix A5). At the conclusion of each daily list review, patients who met both EHR-based clinical and social criteria were identified as potential candidates eligible for phone interview. These patients’ reasons for hospitalization were later identified by extracting encounter data, including the primary admitting diagnosis, from the EHR.

Step 3: Candidate phone interview process

Candidates who met initial EHR criteria were surveyed through phone calls to verify chart review findings, complete a social history survey, and assess whether their home environments could be appropriate for safe care delivery. A standardized survey script was developed in collaboration with the KPACAH clinical program and iteratively improved through practice interviews with KPACAH team members acting as simulated patients. Protocols for phone-based patient interaction, call schedules, and voicemails were adapted from existing Kaiser Permanente Northern California workflows and guidelines. Surveys were conducted no sooner than the patient’s third day after hospital discharge, and patients received a maximum of 4 call attempts, with no more than 2 attempts per week. Phone messages were limited to 1 per week. Surveyed candidates were given an overview of the KPACAH program and asked to participate in a survey about their home environment and potential interest in receiving home-based hospital care in the future. At the conclusion of this interview, candidates who were confirmed to be eligible and affirmed interest in considering KPACAH care in the future were identified as traditional hospital comparators for the KPACAH-enrolled patients.

Enrollment of comparators in different pathways

The comparator identification process began September 2, 2021. Initially, the authors identified a comparator population that mimicked the Acute Substitution clinical pathway (ie, patients who would have met KPACAH enrollment criteria in the ED). However, lower than anticipated KPACAH enrollment numbers in the Acute Substitution pathway prompted adjusting the comparator recruitment pathway to increase the number of potential comparators from an Early Discharge group (ie, patients who could meet criteria later in their hospitalization even if they did not meet eligibility in the ED). This expanded comparator recruitment workflow was initiated on November 4, 2021. In this report, to reflect actual practice within the KPACAH program, the Acute Substitution and Early Discharge patients were combined into a single comparator group.

A t-test or a Pearson’s chi-square test was used when comparing descriptive statistics of continuous or categorical variables between KPACAH and comparator groups. All of the evaluation workflows and analyses were conducted within the broader quality improvement activities to support the implementation and refinement of the KPACAH program and were determined to not qualify as research by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Research Determination officer.

Results

Between September 2, 2021, and February 4, 2022, a total of 5,065 Kaiser Permanente Northern California patients undergoing traditional hospitalization at non-KPACAH facilities were identified for daily screening lists (Figure 1) using standardized EHR criteria. From these, 706 (13.9%) met manual chart review eligibility and were identified as potential comparator candidates. The authors reached 513 (72.7%) of these by phone, with 451 (87.9% of those reached by phone) completing the interview. The vast majority of those completing the interview (n = 446, 98.9%) were confirmed as eligible KPACAH comparators, which represented 8.8% of the starting daily list sample. A total of 321 comparators (72.0%) were classified as Acute Substitution, and 125 as Early Discharge (Figure 2). Overall, an average of 15.5 comparators were identified per week using the initial Acute Substitution–only approach, which increased to an average of 19.5 per week after expanding to include Early Discharge comparators.

The characteristics of the KPACAH enrollees and the comparator groups were broadly similar (Table 2). The comparator group was older than the KPACAH clinical cohort (55.3 ± 15.4 years vs 48.4 ± 11.7 years) with a lower percentage of male patients (50.4% vs 57.1%). The comparator cohort also included 29.4% insured through Medicare, but the KPACAH cohort included only 2.4% Medicare patients, reflecting the fact that Medicare patients became eligible for the KPACAH program later in the implementation period than comparators. There were no significant differences between the groups in chronic comorbid disease burden (median COPS2 10 in both groups) and acute illness severity (median LAPS2 66 in both groups) metrics, as well as utilization of ED visits not ending in hospital admission (45.1% vs 43.6%) or nonelective hospitalizations (14.8% vs 12.6%) in the year prior to hospitalization. Nearly half of the patients in both the comparator group (49.6%) and the KPACAH treatment group (48.4%) were hospitalized with COVID-19. There were some differences in racial/ethnic categories, with a higher percentage of Black/African American (12.7% vs 7.2%) and Asian (15.0% vs 12.6%) patients in the KPACAH cohort.

Discussion

This report describes a screening workflow for identifying comparator patients to facilitate outcome evaluation associated with implementation of the KPACAH program in Northern California. By applying a combination of daily EHR screening lists, standardized chart review, and post-discharge surveys, the authors were successful in identifying a subset of comparator patients from an initial cohort of 5065 hospitalized patients who shared similar characteristics with KPACAH enrollees in hospitalization diagnoses, acute severity of illness, and chronic comorbid disease burden.

The approach detailed here exhibited some differences between comparators and enrollees. For example, electronic screening identified more comparator hospitalizations with abdominal diagnoses, which may reflect differences in services available in brick and mortar hospitals compared with care at home programs (eg, continuous wall suction, frequent abdominal imaging). The mean age of the comparator cohort was also higher than that of the KPACAH cohort, which was an expected finding given that the KPACAH program did not begin enrolling Medicare patients until January 2022, whereas the described comparator approach allowed eligibility for both commercial and Medicare patients in anticipation of Medicare expansion over time. However, patients were similar with respect to measures of acute and chronic severity of illness, as well as with prior ED and hospital utilization over the prior year. As KPACAH and other H@H programs continue to mature, the findings described here provide novel insights to inform program development, evaluation, and sustainability in the future.

One of the chief motivations in this evaluation design was to support increased KPACAH enrollment that would not be limited by the need to randomize patients to KPACAH intervention vs standard of care once program eligibility is confirmed. Although a randomized trial is the most robust method for assessing causal effects of an intervention, some past investigations have been limited by low overall sample sizes owing to such a study design. 10 Challenges in the ability to identify and enroll eligible patients in busy ED environments have also limited the expansion and sustainability of these programs. 17 As a result, the focus was on identifying contemporaneous comparators at hospitals without KPACAH programs to avoid cannibalizing enrollee volume from the actual KPACAH clinical program. Based on the authors’ estimates derived from KPACAH enrollment numbers (n = 126), a randomized approach would have yielded experimental and control groups of only about 60 patients over an 18-month period, which would severely limit the ability to evaluate the KPACAH program in a robust manner. This report describes the workflow for comparator identification and the authors anticipate their future outcomes evaluation to include roughly 1,000 patients in total.

A notable difference between prior literature and the present KPACAH enrollees and comparators has been the impact of COVID-19, with 49.6% of comparators and 48.4% of clinical KPACAH patients being diagnosed with COVID-19. The COVID-19 pandemic has placed unprecedented strain on hospitals, resulting in severe limitations in staffing and space during surge periods. Even beyond acute hospital strain, the pandemic has had other far-ranging effects on health care delivery by contributing to supply and staffing shortages across all health care domains. These results highlight a previously identified8 potential for KPACAH and similar programs to be used as a means of supplementing hospital capacity and/or helping patients avoid extended inpatients stays without sacrificing quality of care during future surges in hospital utilization due to COVID-19 or other illnesses. However, given that this program and its evaluation have been operating within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, further study will be needed to examine potential ways KPACAH and similar programs may have been affected during pandemic conditions and how they will operate in a post-pandemic paradigm. In addition to COVID-19, there is likely to be a substantial opportunity to manage other acute infections at home where patients can receive antimicrobial therapy when being monitored for clinical deterioration. Acute infections, other than COVID-19, comprised the largest diagnosis subgroup of both the comparator (43.6%) and KPACAH (42.0%) patients.

Data from prior research were instrumental in developing electronic exclusion criteria to identify the target patient pool. 1,3,7 Although prior enrollment criteria have substantial overlap, previous H@H programs have been quite heterogenous in model and target patients, making it difficult to draw useful and generalizable conclusions. For example, programs most frequently targeted diagnosis categories included skin and soft tissue infections, uncomplicated urinary or respiratory infections, or nonsurgical abdominal conditions. In a 2019 pilot randomized controlled trial study3 at 2 Boston-area facilities with a sample of n = 91 patients, 49% of patients were in the broad infection category. The breakdown of the remaining patient groups varied depending on the specific goals or services available in each program but broadly reflected common cardiorespiratory diagnoses seen in hospitalization. Many past investigations have identified the need for more specific definitions of H@H eligibility but have not yet gone on to propose a universal set of criteria.

The results described here also support the effectiveness of EHR electronic screening approaches to facilitate the early identification of patients who could meet eligibility criteria for KPACAH enrollment and enhance program enrollment. Again, given the challenges of enrolling patients within busy ED environments, enhanced severity measures and predictive tools could improve the capability to screen and work up patients for home care earlier in their ED course. A particular area for potential improvement is aiming to address lower than expected rates of patients meeting eligibility criteria. Only 13.9% of the daily list patients were deemed eligible candidates by chart review over a 5-month period, 84.5% of screened patients were ineligible due to needing services not currently available in KPACAH. Although some patients, such as those needing inpatient surgery, are unlikely to be able to be effectively treated at home, more granular examination of patients’ reasons for exclusion will be able to inform possible future program expansions.

Ultimately, 98% of respondents met the criteria for a safe home environment and affirmed their interest in the KPACAH program for future care, which is a novel finding that confirms large patient interest in care at home. The iterative generation of these lists from the EHR also creates an opportunity to examine future opportunities to target specific conditions that may most benefit from H@H programs and to identify novel indicators of care quality. Novel approaches that leverage detailed EHR data and machine learning could help refine patients screening algorithms for defining optimal patient cohorts who would most likely benefit from, and most likely choose, care at home, particularly in the Acute Substitution pathway.

Limitations

This evaluation does have several limitations. First, the KPACAH program and its evaluation were designed in the context of the Kaiser Permanente Northern California integrated health care system, which may limit generalizability beyond this care setting. This could impact both the types of patients selected for potential enrollment in the program, as well as the impact of the results. Further study of a similar approach in external settings are needed.

Second, the KPACAH clinical team began enrolling patients in September 2020, but comparators started recruiting in September 2021 once the program had reached maturity. Evaluation of KPACAH patients enrolled only after September 2021 did not show major differences. However, the authors’ future outcomes evaluation, including a larger number of Medicare patients enrolled in the KPACAH group, will be better able to address whether these timing differences impacted patient characteristics.

Third, the comparator patients were identified entirely remotely and were not identical to those identified in the clinical KPACAH program by bedside clinicians. Ongoing collaboration between the clinical and evaluation teams allowed for an approach to maximize similar comparators, but some differences are likely to persist because of subjective impressions or clinical gestalt that may not manifest in clear eligibility criteria during remote chart review. It also offers further motivation to create clear and standardized definitions of H@H and the patients who are most likely to benefit from these programs.

Finally, the authors identified some differences in racial and ethnic composition of patients between KPACAH patients and those hospitalized in traditional settings, although there were similar proportions of White patients across groups. Future implementation should work toward measuring and mitigating potential disparities in the enrollment and impact of H@H programs.

Conclusion

These initial findings provide promising support that the workflow developed for KPACAH and described in this article is an effective method for comparator patient recruitment. The 3-step process of electronic screening, manual EHR chart review, and phone-based patient interviews provides ample opportunity for iteration and dynamic adjustments to support pilot studies still in development. As this program and its evaluation continue to mature, a more in-depth examination of health, cost, and utilization outcome metrics will enhance the generalizability of these findings to inform the development of future programs.
