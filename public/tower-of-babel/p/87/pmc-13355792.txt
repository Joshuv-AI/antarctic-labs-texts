Astaxanthin and naringin synergistically promote proliferation and myogenic differentiation of piscine satellite cells via mTOR signaling
Authors: Ningning Jiang, Han Bao, Rui Yin, Qihe Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCultured fish meat requires efficient in vitro expansion of piscine satellite cells (PSCs). However, prolonged culture impairs proliferation and differentiation. Here, astaxanthin (Ast) and naringin (Nar) were evaluated as food-grade alternatives to synthetic modulators such as MHY1485. The combination of Ast and Nar promoted proliferation by accelerating cell-cycle progression and reducing apoptosis. Synergy analysis (Bliss, HAS, and ZIP) indicated a synergistic effect between Ast and Nar. Ast combined with Nar also improved mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis, as evidenced by increased mitochondrial membrane potential, decreased mitochondrial superoxide, and decreased intracellular ROS levels. Furthermore, co-treatment upregulated the expression of myogenic markers, including MyoD1, MyoG, and MyHC, and promoted myotube formation. Mechanistically, these effects of Ast and Nar were associated with mTOR activation, as indicated by phosphorylation levels, while inhibition of mTOR with rapamycin (Rap) attenuated these effects. Collectively, these results highlighted that Ast combined with Nar was an excellent supplement for large-scale expansion and differentiation of PSCs.Keywords: Astaxanthin, Naringin, Piscine satellite cells, mTOR signaling, Cell proliferation, Myogenic differentiations

Introduction

Cell-cultured fish meat has been considered as a promising alternative to conventional fisheries, offering a sustainable and controllable source of high-quality protein (Setthawong et al., 2026). It is produced through in vitro myogenesis, involving the culture and differentiation of muscle satellite cells, followed by the processing of mature muscle cells to achieve the desired flavor and texture (Xu et al., 2023). Large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea) is an economically important aquaculture species widely cultivated in East Asia. However, wild large yellow croaker resources are increasingly depleted due to environmental pollution and overfishing (Tang et al., 2008). Piscine satellite cells (PSCs) isolated from these species exhibit myogenic potential, including proliferation and differentiation into myotubes that give rise to muscle fibers (S. Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, they serve as an ideal cellular model for studying muscle growth and refining in vitro expansion strategies for cell-cultured fish meat production.

Current research priorities center on achieving mass expansion and efficient differentiation of muscle satellite cells in vitro (J. Wang et al., 2023). However, during prolonged cultivation, these cells progressively decline in both differentiation capacity and proliferation efficiency with each additional passaging cycle, severely limiting their large-scale applicability (Song et al., 2023). Safe and effective strategies that can simultaneously sustain proliferation, preserve differentiation potential, and maintain cellular homeostasis remain limited. The fate of muscle satellite cells is tightly regulated by intracellular signaling pathways and cellular metabolic status. The mTOR signaling pathway plays a central role in coordinating cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation by integrating nutrient availability and energy signals (Zoncu et al., 2010). In addition, mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis are critical for cell activity, as excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can disrupt proliferation and differentiation, and is reported to interfere with mTOR signaling (Holmström & Finkel, 2014; Li et al., 2024). Therefore, mTOR-mediated modulation of mitochondrial function and redox balance may be critical for promoting the in vitro expansion of muscle satellite cells.

In contrast to mammalian satellite cells, for which the regulatory networks governing growth and differentiation have been extensively elucidated, the regulation of PSC growth and development remains largely undefined. Current evidence indicates that the differentiation of PSCs depends on signaling pathway inhibitors, such as the TGF-βR1 inhibitor RepSox (Lou et al., 2024). However, the food safety of such synthetic additives remains uncertain. Consequently, it is essential to explore safe, cost-effective bioactive compounds that can maintain or enhance muscle satellite cell performance during long-term cultivation. Astaxanthin (Ast), a carotenoid derived from microalgae and crustacean shells, is widely recognized as safe for human consumption and is approved for use as a functional food additive (Ambati et al., 2014; Stachowiak & Szulc, 2021). Its potent antioxidant activity protects mitochondrial membranes from ROS-induced oxidative damage and can promote muscle growth in rainbow trout via activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Chen et al., 2024; Nishida et al., 2021). Naringin (Nar), a flavonoid predominantly found in grapefruit and other citrus fruits, exhibits multiple pharmacological effects (Shilpa et al., 2023). Nar promotes cell proliferation by accelerating cell cycle progression and counteracts apoptosis through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Nan et al., 2019; Ni et al., 2021). Our previous research also confirmed that Nar had the ability to enhance myoblast proliferation and myogenic differentiation (Jiang et al., 2025). Given that prolonged culture of PSCs is characterized by accumulated oxidative stress that suppresses mTOR activity, Ast and Nar may act synergistically. Ast alleviates the redox barrier via its antioxidant activity, thereby mitigating the inhibition of mTOR. Meanwhile, Nar directly drives downstream proliferative and differentiative signals. This dual mechanism is expected to yield stronger effects than either compound alone.

In this study, we systematically assessed the effects of three specific signaling pathway modulators, namely TGF-βR1 inhibitor SB431542, mTOR activator MHY1485, and GSK3β inhibitor CHIR99021, on PSCs proliferation and myogenic differentiation. Among these, MHY1485 (an mTOR activator) was identified as the most effective modulator. Molecular docking was performed to predict the binding modes of various natural products to mTOR. Based on the docking results and subsequent cell viability assays, two natural products, Ast and Nar, were selected as promising candidates capable of binding to mTOR and exhibiting positive effects on cell viability. Subsequently, we evaluated the feasibility of using Ast and Nar either alone or in combination, as alternatives to synthetic compounds. Furthermore, we evaluated the effects of Ast and Nar alone or in combination on PSC proliferation, myogenic differentiation, and mitochondrial function, and explored the involvement of mTOR signaling. In summary, this work proposes a safe and effective natural-additive-based strategy to address the limitations of in vitro expansion of PSCs, providing a theoretical basis for the large-scale production of cell-cultured fish meat.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of PSCs

PSCs were isolated from the epaxial muscle of three independent large yellow croakers (6-month-old) as described previously (Xu et al., 2023). Briefly, the epaxial muscle was sequentially digested with 0.1% type IV collagenase (1 h) and 0.1% trypsin-EDTA (20 min), followed by filtration through 70 μm and 40 μm sieves. The isolated cells were then cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F12 (DMEM/F12) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) at 27 °C with 5% CO₂. The identity of the isolated cells was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for Pax7 and MyoD1. The isolation protocol has been previously validated in our laboratory, yielding a highly enriched myogenic satellite cell population with 99.15% Pax7-positive cells and 98.31% MyoD1-positive cells (Lou et al., 2024).

Cell culture, differentiation, and treatment

For cell expansion, PSCs were cultured in growth medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS) at 27 °C with 5% CO₂. For cell differentiation, PSCs were first expanded in growth medium to full confluency for 48 h, and then the medium was replaced with differentiation medium (F12 supplemented with 2% horse serum (HS) and 1% PS) containing natural products or synthetic compounds. After 48 h, the growth medium and differentiation medium were added in the ratio of 1:2 to ensure the survival and continued differentiation of the myotubes. Following an additional 48 h of culture, the corresponding differentiation indicators were detected. For mechanistic inhibition studies, 100 nM rapamycin (Rap) (Topscience, China) as an inhibitor of mTOR was added for 24 h before treatment with Ast and Nar alone or in combination. Rap was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), the final concentration of DMSO in the culture medium was strictly maintained below a non-toxic threshold of 0.1% (v/v), and the control groups received the exact same concentration of vehicle treatment.

Cell viability

PSCs were planted in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and cultured overnight. Afterwards, the growth medium containing varying concentrations (10 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, 500 nM, 1 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM) of SB431542 (Topscience, China), MHY1485 (MedChemExpress, China), and CHIR99021 (Topscience, China), or the growth medium containing varying concentrations (10 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, 500 nM, 1 μM, 5 μM, 10 μM, and 50 μM) of Ast (Yuanye Biotechnology, China) and Nar (Yuanye Biotechnology, China), either alone or in combination were added into 96-well plates. All synthesized compounds and natural products were dissolved in DMSO, the final concentration of DMSO in the culture medium was strictly maintained below a non-toxic threshold of 0.1% (v/v), and the control groups received the exact same concentration of vehicle treatment. After culturing for 24 h and 48 h, 100 μL fresh complete medium and 10 μL CCK-8 solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) were added into each well according to the manufacturer's instructions, then they were incubated for 5 h at 27 °C with 5% CO₂. Finally, the optical density of each well was determined at 450 nm with a multimode microplate reader (SPARK, TECAN, Switzerland). Additionally, the synergistic effects of Ast and Nar were assessed using the SynergyFinder website (https://synergyfinder.aittokallio.group/). Synergy scores were then calculated based on the Bliss independence model, HSA model, and ZIP model (Sheng et al., 2023).

Cell cycle analysis

PSCs were planted in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and cultured overnight. PSCs were cultured in growth medium without serum starvation throughout the experiment. The cells were treated with SB431542 (10 nM), MHY1485 (10 nM), and CHIR99021 (10 nM) for 48 h, or treated with Ast (1 μM) and Nar (50 nM) alone or in combination for 48 h. According to the manufacturer's instructions, the PSCs were collected and fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C, and then treated with propidium iodide, RNase A, and staining buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. The flow cytometer (FACSCanto II, BD, USA) was used to analyze the cell cycle.

Immunofluorescence staining

PSCs were cultured in 24-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) overnight, then the complete medium containing SB431542 (10 nM), MHY1485 (10 nM), and CHIR99021 (10 nM) was added to incubate the cells for 48 h, alternatively, cells were treated with Ast (1 μM) and Nar (50 nM) alone or in combination for 48 h. After the cultivation periods, cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (YEASEN, China), and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (YEASEN, China). Subsequently, they were incubated with the primary antibody against Ki67 (Proteintech, USA), Pax7 (Proteintech, USA), and MyoD1 (Proteintech, USA) overnight in the 4 °C refrigerator. The next day, followed by incubating with fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark, and stained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 10 min in the dark. The laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSM900, Zeiss, Germany) was used to observe the protein expression. Six different fields of view were randomly selected for quantitative analysis using ImageJ software, and a fixed threshold (20–255) was applied uniformly across all images to determine mean fluorescence intensity.

Molecular docking

The mTOR protein structure (PDB ID: 4JSV) was downloaded from RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), while the 3D structures of Ast (ID: 5281224), Nar (ID: 442428), and MHY1485 (ID: 2834965) were downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Molecular docking between the ligands (Ast, Nar, and MHY1485) and the mTOR protein was performed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2019 (BIOVIA, USA) with the CDOCKER protocol, which employs a CHARMm-based molecular dynamics simulated annealing algorithm to generate ligand conformations and a grid-based binding energy scoring function (Adhikari et al., 2022). Prior to docking, the mTOR protein was preprocessed by eliminating water molecules and heteroatoms, followed by hydrogen addition. Ligand structures were subjected to energy minimization to optimize their geometry. A docking sphere with a radius of 33.6 Å was constructed, and its active center coordinates were x = 7.22242, y = −17.2908, z = −30.6169. The other parameters have been modified to their default settings, including Top Hits = 10, Random Conformations = 10, and Random Dynamics Steps = 1000. The –Cdocker Energy score (kcal/mol), which is a semi-empirical estimate of ligand-receptor binding affinity, was used to rank the docking poses. The resulting docking poses were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2019 and PyMOL software.

5-Ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining assay

PSCs were exposed to SB431542 (10 nM), MHY1485 (10 nM), and CHIR99021 (10 nM) for 48 h, alternatively, exposed to Ast (1 μM) and Nar (50 nM) alone or in combination. After 48 h, 10 μM EdU reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added to the well plants and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 12 min, incubated with click reaction solution for 30 min, and counterstained with Hoechst 33342 solution for 10 min at 25 °C in the dark. A laser scanning confocal microscope was employed to capture images from randomly selected fields. Six different fields of view were randomly selected to visualize the number of EdU-positive cells.

Cell apoptosis detection

The apoptosis rate of PSCs was detected using Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) based on the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, PSCs were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and cultured overnight. After indicated treatments, cells were collected and incubated with the solution containing 195 μL Annexin V-FITC binding buffer, 5 μL Annexin V-FITC, and 10 μL propidium iodide at 25 °C for 20 min in the dark. Subsequently, the sample was measured using a flow cytometer.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection

The JC-1 fluorescent probe was used to detect a change in MMP (ΔΨm). When the ΔΨm is high, JC-1 aggregates in the matrix of mitochondria to form polymers, which emit red fluorescence. Conversely, when ΔΨm is low, JC-1 cannot aggregate and remains in a monomeric state, emitting green fluorescence. After being treated with relevant conditions for 48 h, PSCs were collected and rinsed with PBS and incubated with JC-1 staining solution (Solarbio, China) at 37 °C for 20 min in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Thereafter, the cells were rinsed twice using 1 × JC-1 staining buffer and observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope and a flow cytometer. Six different fields of view were randomly selected for quantitative analysis using ImageJ software, and a fixed threshold (40–255) was applied uniformly across all images to determine red and green mean fluorescence intensity.

Mitochondrial superoxide (mtROS) detection

The change in mitochondrial superoxide was measured by the MitoSOX Red Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, PSCs were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX Red staining working solution at 37 °C. After incubation for 30 min, the cells were rinsed using PBS, and then the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 solution for 10 min at 25 °C in the dark. The relative fluorescence intensity of MitoSOX Red was evaluated using a laser scanning confocal microscope and a flow cytometer. Six different fields of view were randomly selected for quantitative analysis using ImageJ software, and a fixed threshold (20–255) was applied uniformly across all images to determine mean fluorescence intensity.

Intracellular ROS detection

The dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe was employed to quantify the level of overall ROS inside living cells. After corresponding interventions, PSCs were collected and incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) at 37 °C for 20 min, and then rinsed three times with serum-free medium. Finally, the treatment samples were detected using a flow cytometer.

Measurement of cell number and doubling time

PSCs were seeded in 6-well plates (1.2 × 104 cells/well) and cultured with growth medium overnight. After indicated treatments, cells were collected and counted using a hemocytometer at 2, 4, and 6 days. The doubling time was determined according to the exponential growth regression equation: DT = T ln2/ln (X1/X2), where T is the cultivation time, X2 is the cell number at the beginning of the cultivation, and X1 is the cell number at the end of the cultivation (Liu et al., 2023).

Giemsa staining

PSCs were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and induced differentiation into myotubes as described in section 2.2. Myotubes were fixed in 70% ethanol for 10 min and stained with 1 × Giemsa solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 20 min. The light microscope (DMi1, Leica, Germany) was employed to capture images from randomly selected fields.

Western blot

RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was employed to extract the total protein, then the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used to quantify the protein concentration. Equal protein quantities were electrophoresed on 10% or 6% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred onto 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. After being blocked with 5% non-fat milk or 5% bovine serum albumin at 25 °C for 2 h, the membranes were probed with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies included: anti-β-Actin (1:10000, Proteintech, USA), anti-Pax7 (1:1000), anti-MyoD1 (1:3000), anti-Cyclin A (1:1000, Upingbio, China), anti-Cyclin D1 (1:2000, Upingbio, China), anti-Cyclin E1 (1:500, Upingbio, China), anti-p21 (1:2000, Upingbio, China), anti-PI3K (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-p-PI3K (Tyr467/199) (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-AKT (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-p-AKT (Ser473) (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-mTOR (1:500, Abmart, China), anti-p-mTOR (S2448) (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-p70 S6K (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-p-p70 S6K (S371) (1:1000, Abmart, China), anti-MyoG (1:500, Proteintech, USA), and anti-MyHC (1:1000, Proteintech, USA). The membranes were then incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:5000–1:10000, LI-COR, USA) for 1 h at 25 °C in the dark. The target bands were visualized using the Odyssey® DLX Imaging System (LI-COR, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). All image capture and data analyses were performed in a blinded manner. Data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene's test. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, one-way analysis of variance followed by Duncan's post hoc test was performed to determine statistical significance. The experiments were three independent replicates. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Proliferative responses of PSCs to SB431542, MHY1485, and CHIR99021

Different concentrations of chemically synthesized compounds (10 nM-10 μM) were added to PSCs during their growth, and the CCK-8 method was used to assess the viability of PSCs. As shown in Fig. 1A-C, certain concentrations of three synthetic compounds enhanced the proliferation rate of PSCs. The viability of PSCs exhibited a significant increase following the treatment of 10 nM-1 μM SB431542 for 24 h, whereas no obvious stimulation was observed with the addition of SB431542 for 48 h (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the results showed that supplementation with MHY1485 and CHIR99021 at concentrations between 10 nM and 1 μΜ notably promoted the viability of PSCs, especially at 48 h (Fig. 1B and C). Consequently, the minimum effective concentrations (10 nM) and the optimal time (48 h) were employed in the subsequent experiments. The immunofluorescence results of the proliferation marker Ki67 demonstrated that MHY1485 was the most effective in stimulating the proliferation of PSCs, as evidenced by Ki67 positivity rates that were 4.89-fold and 2.79-fold higher than those of SB431542 and CHIR99021, respectively (Fig. 1D and E). The cell cycle distribution patterns were further detected by flow cytometry (Fig. 1F and G). Compared with the control group, the percentages of PSCs in the G1 phase significantly downregulated from 67.2% ± 2.77% to 57.7% ± 2.06% and 60.3% ± 3.58% after supplementation with MHY1485 and CHIR99021, respectively. On the contrary, the percentages of PSCs in the S phase dramatically upregulated from 17.8% ± 1.80% to 28.4% ± 1.37% after supplementation with MHY1485. The present investigation identified that MHY1485 exhibited a more potent stimulatory effect on the proliferation of PSCs than the other two synthesized compounds did.

Regulation of the myogenic potential of PSCs by SB431542, MHY1485, and CHIR99021

The levels of Pax7 and MyoD1 were detected using immunofluorescence staining and Western blot to clarify the myogenic potential of PSCs. Immunofluorescence showed that MHY1485 treatment markedly increased the expression of Pax7 and MyoD1 compared with the other two synthesized compounds (Fig. 2A-D). The fluorescence intensity of Pax7 in the MHY1485 group was 5.66-fold and 2.82-fold higher than in the SB431542 group and CHIR99021 group, respectively (Fig. 2A and B). The fluorescence intensity of MyoD1 in the MHY1485 group was 4.16-fold and 2.53-fold higher than in the SB431542 group and the CHIR99021 group, respectively (Fig. 2C and D). Consistently, a marked upregulation was observed in the protein levels of the satellite cell marker Pax7 and the myogenic marker MyoD1, following MHY1485 treatment compared with treatment with the other two synthesized compounds (Fig. 2E-G). The finding indicated that the mTOR activator MHY1485 exerted the strongest and most consistent effects in sustaining stemness and enhancing myogenic properties. To explore the structural basis of this activity, molecular docking analysis revealed that MHY1485 bound strongly (predicted binding energy: −26.9848 kcal/mol) to the mTOR protein within its active site, forming key hydrogen interactions through TRP1456, GLU1457, and ARG1665 (Fig. 2H). The predicted high binding affinity indicated a high potential for interaction between MHY1485 and mTOR.

Synergistic effect of Ast and Nar on promoting the proliferation of PSCs

The effects of Ast and Nar on the proliferation of PSCs were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. The results showed that 1 μM Ast and 50 nM Nar markedly enhanced the viability of PSCs at 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 3A and B). Among the tested concentrations, 1 μM Ast and 50 nM Nar exhibited the most pronounced effects and were therefore selected for subsequent experiments. Ast combined with Nar significantly increased cell viability compared with single-treatment groups (Fig. 3C). However, because the CCK-8 assay primarily reflects metabolic activity, it may not directly correspond to changes in cell number. To address this limitation and validate the proliferative effect of the combination treatment, growth curve analysis and population doubling time measurements were further performed. The cell growth curve analysis showed that the co-treatment group exhibited a significantly higher increase in cell numbers at 2, 4, and 6 days (Fig. S1A). Consistently, the population doubling time was significantly reduced in the Ast + Nar group (Fig. S1B). Thus, the combined evidence from metabolic activity and direct cell counting demonstrated that the combination of Ast and Nar exerted a pro-proliferative effect.

To quantitatively characterize the interaction between Ast and Nar, a synergistic effect analysis was performed using the SynergyFinder software based on three models, including Bliss, HSA, and ZIP. The corresponding synergy scores for these models were 11.092, 1.219, and 11.143 (Fig. S1C). According to the Bliss and ZIP models, the combination exhibited a synergistic effect, whereas the lower HSA score suggested a more modest interaction. This discrepancy likely arises from the distinct mathematical assumptions underlying these models, as Bliss and ZIP models evaluate synergy based on independence and dose-response deviation, while the HSA model is more conservative and defines synergy relative to the highest single agent effect (Vlot et al., 2019). Therefore, the synergy between Ast and Nar was supported by multiple complementary models rather than a single metric.

Meanwhile, we also conducted a comparative analysis of the effects of natural products and synthetic compounds on the proliferation of PSCs. According to Fig. 3D and E, the Ast + Nar group showed a notably higher Ki67-positive rate compared to the MHY1485 group, suggesting that the combination of Ast and Nar was more effective in promoting proliferation. EdU staining demonstrated that the combination treatment (Ast + Nar), but not Ast or Nar alone, prominently increased the ratio of EdU-positive cells (Fig. 3F and G). EdU assay data were consistent with the Ki67-positive rate assay, in which Ast + Nar exerted a greater effect on the proliferation of PSCs than either MHY1485 or individual compounds.

Combination of Ast and Nar synergistically accelerated cell cycle progression and delayed cell apoptosis of PSCs

Next, we further examined whether Ast and Nar could influence the cell cycle and thereby promote the growth of PSCs. The data suggested that Ast + Nar led to an apparent decrease in cell percentage in the G1 phase and an evident increase in cell percentage in the S phase, compared with Ast and Nar alone (Fig. 4A and B). In addition, Western blot was used to detect key regulators of the G1-S-G2 phase, including Cyclin A, Cyclin D1, Cyclin E1, and p21 (Fig. 4C). MHY1485, Ast, Nar, and Ast + Nar significantly increased the protein expression of Cyclin A, which regulated the S phase (Fig. 4D). MHY1485 and Ast + Nar stimulated Cyclin D1 levels to reach similar levels, but both were significantly higher than those of Ast and Nar alone (Fig. 4E). Similar results were found in Cyclin E1, indicating that the combination of Ast and Nar synergistically initiated DNA replication (Fig. 4F). However, the expression level of p21 showed the opposite trend, MHY1485 and Ast + Nar markedly inhibited p21 protein expression (Fig. 4G). The function of Ast and Nar on cell apoptosis was further evaluated. The apoptosis rate of PSCs markedly decreased in the Ast + Nar group compared with the control group (Fig. 4H and I). These findings confirmed that the combination of Ast and Nar could delay cell apoptosis and accelerate cell cycle progression to promote the proliferation of PSCs.

Combination of Ast and Nar synergistically reduced ROS and improved mitochondrial function of PSCs

Given the essential role of mitochondrial function in regulating cell growth and survival, we assessed several mitochondrial parameters, including MMP (ΔΨm) and mitochondrial superoxide. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, PSCs in the control group exhibited weak red fluorescence and strong green fluorescence, indicating PSCs experienced certain mitochondrial damage during the in vitro cultivation process. However, treatment of MHY1485, Ast, Nar, and Ast + Nar led to varying degrees of increase in MMP, while Ast combined with Nar was more effective than either treatment alone (Fig. 5A and B). MitoSOX Red staining suggested that MHY1485, Ast, Nar, and Ast + Nar significantly decreased the level of mitochondrial superoxide, with the MHY1485 and Ast + Nar showing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 5C and D). Additionally, we also detected the change in intracellular ROS level using a flow cytometer. Ast combined with Nar treatment and Ast alone treatment markedly decreased the level of ROS, implying that Ast further enhanced the ability of Nar to clear ROS (Fig. 5E and F). These data collectively demonstrated that Ast combined with Nar alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction by enhancing MMP and reducing the level of mitochondrial superoxide, and improved redox homeostasis by reducing ROS level.

Combination of Ast and Nar synergistically promoted myogenic differentiation

To assess the effect of Ast and Nar on the myogenic properties of PSCs, myogenic differentiation of myoblasts was induced. PSCs were cultured in growth medium for two days, after which differentiation was initiated by switching to differentiation medium supplemented with Ast and Nar alone or in combination. On day 4, the medium was refreshed with a 1:2 mixture of growth and differentiation medium. The differentiation culture was maintained until day 6 to allow terminal myogenic differentiation (Fig. 6A).

To further assess how Ast and Nar impacted the differentiation of myoblasts, we first investigated the expression of the early myogenic markers Pax7 and MyoD1. Immunofluorescence showed that treatment with Ast or Nar alone increased the fluorescence intensity of Pax7 and MyoD1, and the stimulating effect of the combination of Ast and Nar was even more potent (Fig. 6B-E). Western blot analysis further confirmed these observations, suggesting a significant elevation in the expression of Pax7 and MyoD1 proteins (Fig. 6F-H).

We further observed cell morphology under a microscope. In the Ast + Nar group and MHY1485 group, PSCs exhibited robust differentiation, leading to the formation of elongated and abundant myotubes. In contrast, cells exposed to Ast or Nar alone showed a significantly reduced number of myotubes, which were markedly shorter than those in the co-treatment group (Fig. 6I). To further visualize myotube formation, Giemsa staining was performed. The data revealed that MHY1485 promoted myogenic differentiation by increasing the number of myotubes, whereas the combination of Ast and Nar exerted a comparable effect (Fig. 6J). To assess terminal myogenic differentiation, we detected the expression of MyoG and MyHC using Western blot (Fig. 6K). Consistent with previous findings, these proteins were substantially upregulated under MHY1485 or Ast + Nar treatment. Taken together, these findings indicated that the combination of Ast and Nar sustained myogenic properties and enhanced myotube-forming capacity.

The effects of Ast combined with Nar were mediated by the mTOR signaling pathway

To comprehend the underlying mechanism by which Ast and Nar ameliorated mitochondrial dysfunction, promoted proliferation, and enhanced myogenic differentiation, we predicted the binding of Ast and Nar to the mTOR target protein using molecular docking. Ast exhibited an exceptionally strong predicted binding affinity (−48.7095 kcal/mol) stabilized by a hydrogen bond with LYS1662, whereas Nar displayed a moderate affinity (−15.1053 kcal/mol) with a hydrogen bond to ASP1632 (Fig. 7A). Docking of Ast, Nar, and MHY1485 was performed within the same predefined catalytic sphere of mTOR (radius 33.6 Å; center at x = 7.22242, y = −17.2908, z = −30.6169), and after structural alignment the ligand centroid distances were separated by 1.20 Å for Nar versus MHY1485 and 4.93 Å for Ast versus MHY1485, indicating that all three ligands occupy the same pocket with Nar essentially overlapping the reference pose and Ast residing in a nearby subsite. In binding-site analyses, residues within 4.0 Å of ligand heavy atoms were routinely considered part of the pocket, and centroid distances under 12.0 Å were generally interpreted as correct pocket localization (Khazanov & Carlson, 2013; Utgés & Barton, 2024). Together, the above results suggested that there may be a potential interaction of Ast and Nar with mTOR.

Next, the related proteins of the mTOR signaling pathway were detected using Western blot. The results showed that treatment with MHY1485 and Ast combined with Nar significantly increased phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR compared to the control group (Fig. 7B). Notably, the co-treatment of Ast and Nar produced greater p-PI3K and p-AKT upregulation than either compound alone, and the phosphorylation pattern of mTOR mirrored that of AKT (Fig. 7C-E). These results suggested that Ast and Nar synergistically enhanced the phosphorylation levels of key proteins in the mTOR pathway.

mTOR signaling is associated with the effects of Ast combined with Nar on promoting proliferation and differentiation in PSCs

To clarify whether the role of Ast and Nar in promoting proliferation involved the mTOR signaling pathway, we employed Rap (100 nM) to block this pathway in PSCs. We found that Rap reduced the protein expressions of p-AKT, p-mTOR, and p-p70 S6K in PSCs but not the total protein expression of AKT, mTOR, and p70 S6K (Fig. 8A). Moreover, the protein expression levels of p-AKT, p-mTOR, and p-p70 S6K were significantly lower in the Rap + Ast + Nar group than in the Ast + Nar group (Fig. 8B-D). Subsequently, we conducted a quantification of cell numbers and found that Rap significantly attenuated the increase in cell numbers induced by Ast and Nar (Fig. 8E). These results were consistent with the importance of the mTOR pathway in regulating proliferation. To further validate these results, the expression of the cell cycle proteins was analyzed, which indicated that Rap-induced downregulation of Cyclin D1 and Cyclin E1 was rescued by the addition of Ast and Nar (Fig. 8F-H).

To further confirm the effect of blocking the mTOR pathway on myogenic differentiation of PSCs, we observed cell morphology under a microscope. Bright-field and Giemsa staining results revealed that the Rap + Ast + Nar group showed markedly reduced myotube formation compared to the Ast + Nar group (Fig. 8I and J), suggesting that mTOR inhibition attenuated the promoting effects of Ast combined with Nar on the differentiation of PSCs. This finding was further confirmed by the protein expression analysis of myogenic differentiation markers (Fig. 8K-O). Therefore, the promoting effects of Ast combined with Nar on myoblast proliferation and differentiation are partially dependent on mTOR.

mTOR signaling was associated with the effects of Ast combined with Nar on improving mitochondrial function in PSCs

In order to clarify whether the ameliorative effects of Ast and Nar on mitochondrial dysfunction were related to the involvement of the mTOR signaling pathway, we conducted experiments using the specific mTOR inhibitor Rap. JC-1 results demonstrated that the aggregate-to-monomer fluorescence ratio of the Rap + Ast + Nar group dramatically decreased compared with the Ast + Nar group (Fig. 9A and B). The reduction of ROS levels resulting from Ast + Nar was partially reversed by the intervention of Rap (Fig. 9C and D). Similarly, Rap partially blocked the protective effect of Ast + Nar on mitochondrial function, as evidenced by reversing the decrease in mitochondrial superoxide level (Fig. 9E and F).

Discussion

Cultured fish meat represents an emerging industry offering a sustainable, high-quality protein source while mitigating challenges inherent to conventional fisheries, and its production critically depends on PSCs as seed cells. However, similar to mammalian stem cells, PSCs subjected to long-term in vitro cultivation frequently experience impaired stemness maintenance, reduced proliferation and differentiation efficiency, and even senescence or apoptosis due to frequent passaging and batch variations (Ding et al., 2017; Ding et al., 2018). Previous studies have attempted to address these limitations by supplementing culture media with synthetic small-molecule modulators targeting key signaling pathways, such as p38 MAPK, GSK3β, Notch, or TGF-βR1 inhibitors, which can enhance stemness maintenance or differentiation in mammalian and piscine models (Ding et al., 2018; Lou et al., 2024; Park et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2025). While effective, these chemically synthesized compounds pose potential food safety concerns, limiting their practical use in large-scale food production. In contrast, Ast and Nar, as naturally sourced functional compounds, are not only generally recognized as safe but also possess well-documented biological activities. These include potent antioxidant capacity, the promotion of cell proliferation and differentiation, and crucially, their potential to act via modulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway (Brendler & Williamson, 2019; Chen et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2018). These properties suggest that Ast and Nar may provide a potential strategy for enhancing the in vitro expansion of PSCs.

To explore this potential, the present study first focused on clarifying which signaling cascade exerts a dominant regulatory effect on the proliferative capacity and myogenic properties of PSCs. Therefore, we compared representative synthetic modulators targeting three key signaling pathways in PSCs, including TGF-βR1 inhibitor SB431542, mTOR activator MHY1485, and GSK3β inhibitor CHIR99021, and found that activation of mTOR played a more decisive role in regulating proliferation and myogenic properties than inhibition of TGF-βR1 or GSK3β. This aligns with the central role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis as a master regulator of cell cycle progression, growth, and survival (Sizek et al., 2019), yet our findings also underscore species-specific regulatory differences between fish and mammals. While mammalian myoblast proliferation is often regulated through Wnt (Jiang et al., 2025), PSCs appear to rely more heavily on mTOR activity, suggesting that strategies tailored to piscine systems may be required. Guided by this insight, molecular docking analysis indicated that Ast and Nar occupy the same mTOR binding pocket as MHY1485. Based on structural evidence, we speculated that Ast and Nar may exert their synergistic effects by interacting with mTOR.

As safe dietary supplements, Ast and Nar hold promise for scalable expansion of PSCs in future cultured meat production. mTOR signaling is a well-established driver of G1/S phase transition through transcriptional and translational control of cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (Cuyàs et al., 2014). This regulatory role is particularly important in muscle stem cells, which remain quiescent under homeostasis but re-enter the cell cycle upon activation to drive tissue growth and repair (Gattazzo et al., 2020; Mademtzoglou & Relaix, 2022). Natural flavonoids such as Nar have been reported to upregulate cyclin expression and modulate intracellular calcium to promote myoblast proliferation, while carotenoids like Ast enhance proliferation of mesenchymal and muscle progenitor cells (Kim et al., 2010; Ni et al., 2021; Zhang & Peng, 2019). In our study, Ast and Nar synergistically acted to reduce the G1 phase cell population, elevate S phase regulators (Cyclin A, Cyclin D1, and Cyclin E1), and suppress the cell cycle inhibitor p21, creating conditions favorable for rapid in vitro expansion of PSCs and highlighting the particular strength of combining natural bioactives rather than single-compound application. During skeletal muscle tissue formation, in addition to effective proliferation, mature myoblasts must undergo differentiation, migration, alignment, and fusion to develop into myotubes (Feng et al., 2024). This process is orchestrated by the sequential activation of Pax7 and myogenic regulatory factors. Pax7, as a transcription factor known to be indispensable in myogenesis, initiates the myogenic program and induces MyoD expression, which in turn activates the terminal differentiation marker MyoG (Rahman et al., 2023; Zammit, 2017). This cascade ultimately drives the expression of muscle fiber proteins, including MyHC (Hoh, 2020). In our investigation, it was observed that Ast and Nar synergistically promoted myotube formation and upregulated myogenic regulatory factors (MyoD1 and MyoG) and MyHC expression. These findings highlighted the potential of Ast combined with Nar to promote myogenic differentiation.

Maintaining mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis is essential for supporting myoblast proliferation and differentiation during muscle growth (Pozzi et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Accumulating evidence indicated that combining natural extracts with specific therapeutic agents could synergistically enhance mitochondrial function, with resveratrol, naringenin, and curcumin serving as representative examples (Gu et al., 2016; Ossikbayeva et al., 2021; D. Wang et al., 2024), yet Ast and Nar combinatorial effects in PSCs lack documentation. Prior studies indicated that Ast mitigated thermal stress-induced apoptosis via mitochondrial protection in C2C12 myoblasts, whereas Nar restored ΔΨm and blocked high glucose-induced cell apoptosis (Huang et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2019). Consistent with these properties, Ast and Nar in combination preserved ΔΨm, reduced mitochondrial superoxide and intracellular ROS levels, and these combination effects were more effective than either compound alone or the synthetic activator MHY1485, suggesting complementary protective actions that maintain organelle function during in vitro cultivation.

Skeletal muscle development is controlled by multiple signaling pathways, including mTOR, Notch, and Wnt (Li et al., 2024; Schmidt et al., 2019). Among them, mTOR acts as a key driver of myogenic commitment by amplifying extracellular signals to regulate cell fate decisions (Jin et al., 2025). mTOR is also an important regulator of mitochondrial function and myoblast differentiation (X. Wang et al., 2017). In this study, the combination of Ast and Nar synergistically activated mTOR signaling, which was associated with enhanced cell proliferation, myogenic differentiation, and improved mitochondrial function. To further investigate the involvement of mTOR signaling in mediating the effects of Ast and Nar, the mTOR inhibitor Rap was employed. The mTOR inhibitor partially reversed the impacts of Ast combined with Nar on proliferation, differentiation, and mitochondrial function. These findings indicated that mTOR signaling contributed substantially to the beneficial effects of Ast and Nar but might not fully account for all of the observed responses. The incomplete reversal by Rap further implied that additional signaling pathways might also participate in mediating these effects. Notably, our previous study demonstrated that Nar promoted the proliferation of murine satellite cells through activation of the Wnt signaling pathway (Jiang et al., 2025), raising the possibility that Wnt signaling may cooperate with mTOR signaling to regulate the behavior of PSCs. In addition, mTOR exists in two distinct complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2, which display differential sensitivity to Rap, and the potential contribution of Rap-insensitive mTORC2 signaling cannot be excluded (Jhanwar-Uniyal et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the present findings supported that Ast and Nar co-treatment could effectively balance self-renewal and differentiation in PSCs, consistent with evidence from mammalian systems showing that mTOR activation was indispensable for myogenesis (Fang et al., 2022).

In summary, the present study demonstrated that Ast combined with Nar activated the mTOR signaling pathway, which contributed to the beneficial effects observed in PSCs, including accelerating cell cycle progression via G1/S transition to promote proliferation, enhancing mitochondrial membrane potential and reducing ROS/mtROS levels to preserve mitochondrial function, and upregulating myogenic regulators (MyoD1, MyoG, and MyHC) to strengthen myogenic differentiation potential in PSCs (Fig. 10). Notably, the partial attenuation by rapamycin suggests that additional mechanisms beyond mTOR may also be involved, warranting further investigation. While this study delineates the effect of Ast and Nar on proliferation and differentiation of PSCs, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our study was conducted in conventional short-term two-dimensional culture, which does not capture the complex, multi-factorial environment of cultured meat production, including three-dimensional scaffolds, shear stress, and nutrient gradients. Moreover, the long-term safety and efficacy of Ast and Nar in scalable, serum-free bioprocessing systems remain to be established. Future studies should integrate these compounds into bioreactor models together with other food-grade additives to validate their translational potential. Nevertheless, this study is the first to present a safe, food-grade natural product combination capable of maximizing the early-stage proliferation and differentiation efficiency of PSCs, thereby providing crucial foundational data for establishing economically feasible cell-cultured seafood bioprocesses in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrated that the combination of Ast and Nar effectively promoted the proliferation and differentiation of PSCs, as well as improved mitochondrial function. Mechanistically, activation of mTOR signaling was involved in these effects, as inhibition of mTOR attenuated the beneficial effects of Ast combined with Nar. Collectively, these findings highlighted that Ast combined with Nar had the potential to serve as a functional, food-grade alternative for optimizing the culture system of PSCs, thereby providing a theoretical basis for future studies aimed at optimizing PSC expansion strategies for cultured meat production.
