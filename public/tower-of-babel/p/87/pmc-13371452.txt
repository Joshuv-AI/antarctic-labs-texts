A qualitative descriptive case study on the incentive strategies of hospice care volunteers
Authors: Yanming Wu, Rong Cheng, Yangchenchen Liu, Yi Yao, Youcheng Gu, Dejiang Sun, Maomin Jiang, Huiling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the operational and funding structures of hospice care vary significantly across the globe, volunteers remain an indispensable component of hospice care teams. Existing research has examined volunteers’ working experiences and training models; however, less is known about incentive strategies to promote sustained engagement.ObjectiveTo explore the incentive strategies for hospice care volunteers through this qualitative case study in China and thus provide evidence to enhance global volunteer retention.MethodsA descriptive qualitative case study was conducted using participant observation and semi-structured interviews to explore hospice care volunteers’ expectations for incentives, as well as administrators’ perspectives on strategies to motivate and retain volunteers. Data were analyzed through conventional content analysis.ResultsAnalysis yielded four themes and twelve sub-themes. Background

With the global aging population and the increasing burden of chronic diseases, the demand for hospice care had continued to rise [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that approximately 56.8 million people worldwide required hospice care each year, yet only 14% received such services [3].

A hospice is an independent, non-profit health care institution dedicated to the comprehensive, humanized end-of-life care for terminally ill patients and their families, emphasizing holistic support, symptom control, and psychological and spiritual comfort rather than curative treatment [4]. However, the hospice sector is under severe financial strain. According to Hospice UK (2026) [5], nearly 60% of hospices in England have implemented or are considering cutting frontline services due to insufficient funding. Meanwhile, a 2026 report by Marie Curie indicates that about 32% of people in England die with unmet palliative care needs, and this unmet need is projected to increase by 23% by 2050 [6]. Such funding shortages, coupled with unmet care needs, underscore the severe challenges confronting the hospice care system. Against this background, globally, while funding models and operational structures of hospice organizations differ substantially across regions, volunteers are universally recognized as an integral component of the hospice team [7]. Notably, the specific roles they undertake often vary according to country-specific healthcare contexts. For instance, in Western countries with established hospice movements, volunteers frequently engage in community fundraising and direct patient-care assistance; whereas in emerging hospice systems like China, their roles predominantly focus on emotional companionship, psychological support, and administrative assistance. Despite regional variations [8, 9], they universally provide essential non-clinical services and alleviate the immense pressure on formal healthcare systems.

Understanding the motivations of volunteers is crucial for effective hospice care [10]. Existing literature indicates that individuals who choose to serve as hospice volunteers are predominantly driven by complex intrinsic motivations [11]. These initial drivers frequently encompass altruism, a profound desire to give back to the community, deep empathy, or personal lived experiences navigating the grief of losing loved ones [12]. This strong sense of inner calling acts as the foundational catalyst that brings individuals into the emotionally demanding environment of end-of-life care [13].

However, initial motivations are rarely sufficient to sustain long-term commitment [14]. In practice, hospice organizations face a dual human resource challenge: the initial recruitment of volunteers and their subsequent retention [15]. Recruitment is inherently difficult due to pervasive societal taboos surrounding death and the prerequisite of exceptionally high emotional resilience for applicants. Yet, retaining those who do join presents a distinct and arguably more critical issue. Many recruited hospice volunteers struggle to continue their service over time [16]. The continuous exposure to patient mortality, coupled with the profound emotional burden of accompanying dying individuals, frequently precipitates compassion fatigue and psychological burnout [17]. Furthermore, a lack of adequate organizational support or psychological empowerment can exacerbate these emotional tolls [18]. Consequently, hospice organizations globally grapple with high attrition rates [19].

To reduce turnover and sustain a robust volunteer workforce, hospice organizations should move beyond reliance on volunteers’ intrinsic motivation and establish systematic, effective incentive strategies. Therefore, while acknowledging the overarching difficulties in recruitment, this qualitative case study specifically focuses on addressing the retention challenge of existing volunteers. By exploring the localized incentive mechanisms for hospice care volunteers in China, this study aims to provide evidence to enhance the broader global discourse on hospice volunteer management and retention.

Method

Study design

This study adopts a qualitative descriptive case study approach. This approach is suited for investigating a bounded, context-specific phenomenon within its real-life, complex practice setting while preserving the uniqueness and integrity of the case [20]. It enables in-depth exploration of the underlying logic, processes, and stakeholder perspectives embedded in a defined system or program [21]. This method is also suitable for eliciting nuanced, direct, first-hand, and contextually grounded accounts of a phenomenon when existing evidence is sparse [22]. Accordingly, the current study used this design to explore hospice care volunteers’ expectations regarding incentives and administrators’ perspectives on strategies to motivate and retain volunteers. By focusing on the case as a holistic, situated phenomenon, the study generates contextually relevant insights that can inform evidence-based strategies for volunteer management in end-of-life care globally. The research report follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

Study location

Fieldwork was conducted between August and December 2025 across four strategic urban centers in China, located in Suzhou, Nanning, Guangzhou, and Beijing cities. To capture diverse reginal characteristics and institutional infatuations, data were collected across hospital-based hospice wards, residential care homes, and community-level settings. This multisite configuration was designed to facilitate a comprehensive evaluation of incentive mechanisms across heterogeneous hospice care environments.

Research subjects

Study participants comprised hospice care volunteers and volunteer coordinators. Recruitment criteria for volunteers were as follows: ① aged 18 years or older with full legal capacity; ② completion of foundational hospice care volunteer training and possessing clinical service experience; and ③ demonstrated ability to engage in effective communication and interaction. Recruitment criteria for volunteer managers included: ① an administrative/management role or equivalent leadership experience within a hospice care organization; and ② the capacity to participate in clear communication and interviews. Data collection was guided by the principle of thematic saturation. In accordance with the recommendations of Hennink and Kaiser (2022), after preliminary coding saturation was reached, an additional 2–3 interviews were conducted to verify that no further novel codes or insights would emerge.

We conducted face-to-face interviews with hospice care volunteers (n = 25) and volunteer managers (n = 10) from Suzhou, Nanning, Beijing, and Shanghai, China. Due to personnel and budget constraints, online interviews were also conducted with volunteers (n = 2) and volunteer managers (n = 3) from Ningbo and Guangzhou. Each interview lasted between 45 and 120 min. General information about hospice care volunteers and managers were provided in Table 1.

Data collection

Data collection was undertaken by YW, who holds a postdoctoral background in nursing science with a research focus on hospice care. The researcher has undergone rigorous training in qualitative methodologies and possesses substantial expertise in hospice research and voluntary service delivery. This dual role, as a researcher and an active hospice volunteer, provided an “insider” perspective. It facilitated a profound understanding of the service environment and the intricate operational dynamics of volunteer systems throughout the inquiry. While the insider perspective enhanced contextual understanding, it may also introduce potential limitations such as desensitization to routine phenomena and bias from close relationships with participants.

The present study adopted a triangulated approach to data collection, including participant observation and semi-structured interviews. Participant observation was utilized to capture behavioral patterns and interpersonal dynamics between volunteers and managers within naturalistic service settings. Concurrently, semi-structured interviews were conducted to elicit participants’ lived experiences and subjective perceptions regarding their motivations and incentive mechanisms. These complementary methods ensured a multi-dimensional understanding of volunteers’ needs across both observational and experiential domains.

Participant observation

Researchers immersed themselves in hospice care services as ‘volunteers,’ conducting participant observation within clinical and community settings. Observation targets included both hospice volunteers and managers, with a specific focus on volunteers’ modes of engagement, interpersonal dynamics, and emotional trajectories during service delivery, as well as managers’ organizational strategies for supporting and motivating volunteers. These observations encompassed multi-layered interactions among volunteers, patients, families, multidisciplinary healthcare teams, and administrative staff, alongside the communicative and managerial exchanges between coordinators and volunteers. Observational data were documented in field notes through a combination of real-time jottings and post-service reflections, which were subsequently integrated with service logs and supplemental materials.

Semi-structured interviews

Building upon the insights gleaned from participant observations, semi-structured interviews were conducted with the hospice volunteers and the managers. (The interview guide is available in the supplementary file). The interview guide was developed to encompass several core thematic domains: (1) personal volunteering trajectories, (2) motivators, (3) critical appraisals of current incentive mechanisms, and (4) prospective recommendations for systemic improvement. During the interviews, open-ended probing was adaptively utilized to further elicit nuanced perspectives and allow for a more profound exploration of participants’ unique lived experiences.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using conventional content analysis to examine field notes, interview transcripts, and supplemental materials. Conventional content analysis is a data-driven, material-oriented inductive approach that prioritizes the generation of analytical categories and themes through a bottom-up process, particularly in research domains where established theoretical frameworks are nascent. This methodology is especially efficacious for conceptual exploration and the mapping of operational pathways [23].The analytical process adhered to the systematic stages of conventional content analysis. Initially, the primary researcher engaged in repeated, immersive readings of the entire dataset to achieve a “gestalt” understanding of the service context and participant narratives. Secondly, meaning units relevant to the research objectives were extracted from the text and subjected to line-by-line open coding. Subsequently, through the constant comparison of similarities and differences, codes with shared attributes were clustered into sub-categories, which were then synthesized into categories through a process of abstraction.

Rigor

Several strategies were employed to bolster rigor throughout the research process. Methodological and data triangulation were achieved through the continuous cross-validation of insights derived from participant observations and semi-structured interviews. During the analytical phase, we engaged in an iterative process of returning to the original transcripts to refine coding and categorization schemas. To minimize interpretive bias, peer debriefing was conducted with experienced qualitative researchers to critically scrutinize the analytical trajectory and emerging results. Furthermore, member checking was performed by sharing preliminary findings with a subset of participants to verify that the interpretations accurately reflected their lived experiences, thereby enhancing the credibility and resonance of the study.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the current study was obtained from the Soochow University Institutional Review Board (Approval number: SUDA20251204H08). Before initiating the inquiry, we provided comprehensive disclosures regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, and the intended utilization of findings to all participants. Participation was voluntary, and formal written informed consent was conducted from each participant prior to data collection. Participants were explicitly informed of their unconditional right to withdraw from the study at any stage without prejudice or any impact on their service delivery. To ensure confidentiality, all interview and observational data were anonymized through de-identification. Collected audio recordings and transcripts were stored in encrypted, password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Furthermore, we maintained a non-intrusive presence during participant observation, ensuring that data collection was smooth without disrupting routine hospice services or organizational workflows, thereby mitigating potential conflicts of interest.

Results

This study identified four core themes and twelve sub-themes through data analysis, namely material incentives, organizational incentives, spiritual incentives, and growth-oriented incentives. Figure 1 illustrates the overall composition and synergistic relationships of the incentive framework, emphasizing the holistic guiding role of multidimensional incentives in volunteer service management.

Multidimensional incentive framework for volunteer service management

Theme 1: Material incentives

Material incentives focus on supporting hospice care volunteers by providing tangible and visible resources that help reduce practical burdens during service delivery. Additionally, they convey organizational recognition by offering physical rewards and health benefits, demonstrating respect and care for volunteers’ contributions.

Theme1 subtheme 1: Basic subsidy relief

Offering financial subsidies to cover participation costs (e.g., travel and meals) can facilitate participation, given that these costs may pose a burden, especially for low-income groups.

"If it’s an all-day outing, perhaps appropriate allowances for meals, transport and such might be provided? This would ensure volunteers have no worries about their basic needs. (V18)"

Theme 1 subtheme 2: Tangible resources empowerment

Tangible tokens such as commendation certificates, awards, bespoke badges, and festive gifts demonstrating the organization’s recognition help foster volunteers’ sense of fulfillment and enhance their trust in organizations.

"For instance, rather than telling you I’ve received praise, showing you a certificate would carry more weight—it would be more tangible. That’s because objects serve as tangible bearers for our spiritual needs; they become tangible evidence, a tangible witness to that experience. (V14)"

Theme 1 subtheme 3: Health and welfare support

Many managers acknowledged that hospice care volunteering entailed significant physical and emotional demands. Organizations are recommended to customize health and wellbeing benefits based on their unique characteristics and resources for exceptional volunteers. For example, traditional Chinese medicine hospitals could offer Traditional Chinese Medicine Physiotherapy Services, such as cupping therapy, while tertiary hospitals could provide complimentary consultations to alleviate the physical and mental strain associated with volunteering.

"For instance, institutions like Fujian Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine can offer rewards tailored to their capabilities and institutional character. This might involve providing services such as traditional Chinese cupping therapy after accumulating a certain period of service. (M2)"

Theme 2: Organizational incentives

Volunteers required clear role definitions and a sense of team belonging. Through institutionalized design and relationship-building, organizational incentives can establish a stable and inclusive support system that sustains hospice volunteering services and enables volunteers to fulfil their supportive role.

Theme 2 subtheme 1: Engraving volunteer business cards

Organizations should strategically utilize important holidays and innovative tools, such as AI, to cultivate a positive public image of volunteers by systematically showcasing their value and contributions. This allows the public to more clearly recognize the compassion and impact of hospice care volunteers, thus reinforcing their sense of identity and perceived value.

"For instance, December 5th is observed as International Volunteer Day, and October marks Hospice Care Day. We can leverage these holidays to organize awareness campaigns promoting the spirit of volunteering, thereby enhancing public recognition of the value and significance of volunteers. Such initiatives would exert a profound motivational impact on volunteers. (V11)"

"Our organizers can leverage AI tools to enhance volunteers’ public image. For instance, using tools like Doubao or DeepSeek to create comics that showcase volunteers’ daily work, or employing AI voiceovers, to help people better ‘see’ our volunteers. (V5)"

Theme 2 subtheme 2: Fostering cohesive teams

Organizations may consider cultivating a stable, supportive, and inclusive team atmosphere for volunteers. Such an environment could foster emotional connections and collaborative support among volunteers and with professional team members, including healthcare staff. This sense of belonging can alleviate emotional exhaustion during service delivery and motivate volunteers through mutual trust and empowerment, thereby enhancing their commitment to sustained participation.

"Patients are mobile, while healthcare professionals are relatively fixed. Long-term interactions primarily involve collaboration with healthcare teams, including volunteers and medical staff, where teamwork is of paramount importance. (V9)"

"She said the positive atmosphere within the volunteer team was a significant reason she chose to volunteer—she simply wanted to see her fellow volunteers. My previous job was extremely demanding and draining. Despite being so exhausted, I still volunteered at weekends. Even my family couldn’t understand why I couldn’t just rest properly. The truth is, I gained immense energy from my fellow volunteers during these activities. (Observation Notes)"

Theme 2 subtheme 3: Flexible adaptive management

Hospice care volunteers provide services in various forms. Managers should identify each volunteer’s strengths and preferences to facilitate personalized matching and flexible scheduling. By aligning individuals with positions and tasks that best suit their capabilities and interests, organizations grant volunteers greater choice and autonomy. This sense of empowerment can help volunteers feel belonging and accomplishment through their service, thereby motivating their sustained participation.

"I excel at photography but am not adept at communication, so the doctors and nurses did not insist I enter the wards. Instead, they assigned me to document team activities with my camera and create life review videos for patients nearing the end of life. Witnessing my professional skills being valued and contributing in a way that suits my strengths. This sense of participation, tailored precisely to me. I felt truly rewarding. It also inspires me to continue serving this collective in my own way. (V10)"

"During my time serving alongside volunteers, I gained a profound insight: volunteers need not be confined to entering wards to provide direct care. In other words, not every volunteer must physically enter a ward. For instance, some volunteers participate in teaching as ‘simulated patients,’ assisting in the training and assessment of trainee doctors; others work behind the scenes writing popular science articles or producing videos to disseminate hospice care concepts… These roles are equally vital, forming indispensable components of the hospice care volunteer team. Managers should learn to flexibly manage and assign roles based on each volunteer’s strengths and preferences, allowing service formats to extend with adaptability. Such tailored arrangements not only enhance service effectiveness but also constitute the deepest form of respect for volunteers, serving as a sustained and gentle source of motivation. (Observation Notes)"

Theme 3: Spiritual incentives

Volunteers in hospice care are at risk of experiencing empathy fatigue and meaning depletion. Spiritual motivation addressed their fundamental need for value affirmation and emotional healing through multidimensional recognition and spiritual nourishment. This fostered the volunteers’ intrinsic motivation while providing spiritual guidance.

Theme 3 subtheme 1: Multi-dimensional value recognition

Organizations should establish a multi-dimensional recognition system to ensure that volunteers’ contributions are valued, acknowledged, and appreciated. Public acknowledgments, promotional displays, and annual commendation ceremonies can bring volunteers’ often-invisible efforts to light, reinforcing their sense of social value and spiritual belonging. This approach builds a strong foundation for sustained commitment.

"When providing care, the sense of being needed and appreciated that patients and their families convey is itself a multidimensional recognition of the volunteer’s value. However, not every family expresses this openly, so it falls to hospitals and organizing bodies to regularly articulate this appreciation, thereby fostering broader recognition of the vital role volunteers play. (V15)"

“Create a simple summary PPT or video showcasing their activity silhouettes, and they’ll feel truly seen and valued. (M2)”

Theme 3 subtheme 2: Nourishing the soul

Some volunteers were drawn to hospice care by personal experiences of bereavement, seeking self-healing through helping others. However, their frequent exposure to emotional challenges surrounding life, death, and separation might engender emotional burdens. To address this, managers should facilitate opportunities for volunteers to articulate their feelings and engage in peer listening, thereby cultivating emotional support grounded in mutual understanding and acceptance. This process can enable volunteers to reflect on their personal experiences and gain strength from the shared resonance of others’ life stories. It also provides sustained spiritual nourishment through emotional recharging and peer support, thus establishing a psychological foundation for their long-term commitment to hospice care services.

"Through participating in salons and group counselling sessions, we gain opportunities for profound exchanges with peers, listening to each other’s genuine feelings while receiving heartfelt encouragement from others. This sense of being understood and supported feels like a psychological recharge. As a group of like-minded individuals, we offer mutual support, finding reflections of ourselves in each other’s life stories and drawing strength to move forward. This serves as the nourishment sustaining us on our journey. (V13)"

Theme 3 subtheme 3: Intrinsic motivation enhancement

Motivations for volunteering in hospice care varied significantly among individuals, necessitating tailored engagement strategies. The core challenge lied in transforming externally driven participation into a self-sustaining motivation. Sustained volunteer commitment depends on cultivating this intrinsic motivation through the service experience itself. Such positive experiences, in turn, generated a spontaneous desire for continued involvement.

"Everyone has different motivations and indeed requires different forms of encouragement. But I believe the key lies in transforming external incentives into internal spiritual motivation. Many high school students at our center come to accumulate volunteer hours, which is one of the graduation requirements here. This makes it relatively harder for them to stay, as it’s more about external demands. However, many who have already started working do volunteer work out of genuine inner desire and can continue for years. I’ve been able to keep doing this primarily because I feel nourished by it—it strengthens the inner drive within me. So for me, it’s very natural to want to continue this work. (V26)"

Theme 4: Growth-oriented incentives

As volunteers enter the long-term engagement phase, growth-oriented incentives promote sustained participation through capacity-building initiatives, reflective practice, and role progression schemes. This approach not only supports personal development but also strengthens the overall service delivery framework.

Theme 4 subtheme 1: Knowledge and skill enhancement

Hospice care volunteers expressed a strong demand for professional development. Offering targeted training, in areas such as hospice care knowledge, communication skills, and aromatherapy, alongside access to external learning resources, enhanced their sense of service efficacy and served as a significant motivator for continued participation.

"Our hospital will open certain specialist training programs and academic conferences to volunteers. For those eager to pursue continuous learning and enhance their knowledge through service, such training addresses their need for personal development. This creates a dynamic pathway for advancing knowledge and skills, enabling learning through service and better service through learning. Having implemented this approach, I find it a rather effective way to motivate them. (V15)"

Theme 4 subtheme 2: Experience sharing and reflection

By reflecting on service experiences, including successes and challenges, volunteers identified strengths and areas for growth, informing their future engagement. This reflective practice builds confidence and supports continued participation.

"We can assist him(volunteer) in phases by summarizing his work—essentially helping him review his accomplishments. This serves two purposes: firstly, enabling him to recognize his strengths and areas for improvement through reflection; secondly, guiding him in drawing conclusions. This process equips him with clear objectives and fosters the confidence and motivation to persevere. (V10)"

Theme 4 subtheme 3: Character progression and development

Volunteers sought to transition from passive ‘executors’ to active ‘co-creators.’ To support this, organizations can assign volunteers to diverse roles, such as mentors, course collaborators, or project coordinators. Such role progression not only fulfills volunteers’ intrinsic need for personal influence but also strengthens the overall volunteer system development.

"For instance, having previously attended that hospice care training program, I transitioned from an ordinary volunteer to a volunteer instructor, sharing my experience and knowledge. This process not only facilitated my own growth but also equipped me with the skills to better fulfil my duties in hospice care. It has enriched me personally. (V10)"

Discussion

The findings indicate that hospice care volunteers require incentive strategies tailored to their different developmental stages to effectively stimulate their enthusiasm. These strategies encompass four motivational dimensions: material, organizational, spiritual, and growth-oriented. Material incentives aim to reduce burdens and empower volunteers; organizational incentives focus on optimizing management systems; spiritual incentives nourish the soul; and growth-oriented incentives promote role progression. Together, these strategies support sustained volunteer engagement and contribute to the healthy development of the service system. Consistent with previous research [24], this study emphasized that a single form of incentive struggles to address the diverse needs of volunteers from varied backgrounds. Existing research suggests that volunteer incentive strategies must be adapted to the diverse motivations of different groups rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach. However, no specific multi-dimensional adaptation schemes have been developed. The present study innovatively proposed a comprehensive incentive strategy that includes material, organizational, spiritual, and growth-oriented incentives.

Regarding material incentives, this study advocated for reducing volunteers’ financial burdens through basic subsidies and providing tangible support through in-kind care, consistent with previous research [25]. Building on this, we further recommend providing volunteers with health and well-being benefits that draw on the professional strengths of service organizations. For example, the hospice care department in traditional Chinese medicine hospitals could offer volunteers specialized therapeutic services, such as cupping therapy and ear acupressure. It is important to note that these health benefits should be strictly limited to non-invasive wellness practices, avoiding any invasive procedures to minimize potential risks.

In terms of organizational incentives, this study indicates that optimizing the management model of volunteer organizations helps foster a supportive environment for volunteers [26]. In line with prior research, the current study suggests that organizers can play a proactive role in helping volunteers develop a positive self-image, fostering a supportive team atmosphere, and reducing volunteer withdrawal associated with organizational alienation [27]. The study also highlights that adopting flexible management to achieve personalized matching can better meet volunteers’ expectations. For example, some volunteers preferred face-to-face care in hospital wards, while others favored non-contact services like community outreach or online support. This aligns with the global trend towards diversified hospice care service models [7]. Consequently, volunteer role deployment based on volunteers’ respective strengths and preferences facilitates flexible adaptation of service models. Many behind-the-scenes volunteers, who play a crucial role in organizational operations and resource allocation, are often overlooked. While their work may not involve direct patient contact, it is essential to the smooth functioning of hospice care services [7]. For example, these volunteers handle tasks such as fundraising, event organization, and advocacy, all of which provide critical support. Therefore, behind-the-scenes volunteers also deserve equal recognition of their efforts from managers, ensuring that every volunteer feels valued within the service system. Additionally, volunteers expect organizations to implement flexible feedback mechanisms, through which incentive strategies can be promptly adjusted in response to volunteer input and changing service needs [28]. For example, when volunteers report feeling exhausted or emotionally drained from frequent participation, managers may offer them a temporary break to recuperate or temporarily reassign them from frontline roles to less demanding administrative duties. This flexibility not only protects volunteers’ physical and mental wellbeing but also serves as an implicit motivator. It enables volunteers to recognize the organization’s humanistic care, thereby enhancing their sense of belonging and loyalty. Ultimately, this approach enhances the sustainability of hospice care volunteering.

Incentive strategies for hospice care volunteers should be contextualized and tailored to individual needs [29]. Research suggests that managers frequently employ monetary rewards and public recognition to motivate volunteers [30]. However, compared to external motivators such as material rewards or task completion incentives, the motivational needs of hospice care volunteers are more centered on ‘spiritual motivation’ and ‘growth-oriented motivation,’ emphasizing value recognition, emotional support, and the attainment of life meaning [31]. Extensive research and practice indicate that hospice care volunteers often bring diverse life experiences and emotional backgrounds to their service, including bereaved individuals, relatives of cancer patients, and those grappling with profound existential questions [32]. This demonstrates that volunteering is more than an altruistic act; it is also a process of self-healing and meaning reconstruction. Therefore, the design of incentive strategies should focus on guiding volunteers to engage in emotional awareness, life reflection, and value clarification while accompanying patients and their families. This approach assists volunteers in transforming personal trauma into empathic capacity and existential wisdom through systematic supervisory support, life education, and reflective practice. As volunteers progressively attain inner integration, spiritual growth, and affirmation of life’s meaning through service, their motivation evolves from initial emotional compensation or problem exploration into a stable and spontaneous intrinsic drive [33]. This motivation fosters the spiritual strength that sustains volunteers’ participation in service. In line with previous research, the current study found that hospice care volunteers generally exhibit strong learning and developmental needs [34]. To address these needs, this study introduces an innovative developmental motivation strategy that establishes clear pathways for skill enhancement and self-development. As a result, volunteers not only continuously enhance their professional competence through service but also, in some cases, transition from hospice care volunteers to professional hospice care social workers. This highlights that incorporating role progression into the volunteer incentive system stimulates long-term service motivation and strengthens for the professional development of hospice care services.

The dynamic evolution of patient needs, the continual adaptation of service content, and the variability in volunteers’ psychological states and capabilities demonstrate that volunteer motivation constitutes a fluid rather than static construct [35]. Finkelstein and colleagues [36] noted that volunteer motivation evolves throughout the service stages, yet such observations remain largely descriptive, lacking corresponding incentive strategies. To help volunteers maintaining long-term dedication despite changes in their initial motivations, the current study proposes four categories of motivational strategies—material, organizational, spiritual, and growth-oriented—tailored to the characteristics of hospice care services, addressing the dynamic shifts in volunteer motivation across multiple dimensions. Specifically, during the initial stages of volunteer participation, material incentives mitigate the practical costs of service engagement through basic subsidies, health coverage and essential in-kind support. These provisions provide fundamental safeguards for sustained volunteer commitment. As volunteers gain experience, organizational incentives help stabilize the workforce by fulfilling their needs for belonging and social recognition. This is accomplished through clearly defined roles, strengthened team cohesion, and enhanced organizational identification. During the service phase marked by long-term companionship and high emotional investment, spiritual motivation addresses volunteers’ intrinsic need for support when they experience emotional exhaustion. This support can be provided through multidimensional recognition, emotional guidance, and positive reinforcement on their sense of purpose. Additionally, growth-oriented incentives guide volunteers in evolving from service implementers to knowledge disseminators or project co-creators through skill enhancement, experience sharing, and role progression. This supports volunteers’ aspirations for self-actualization and continuous growth. Overall, the incentive framework developed in this study is dynamic, progressing from external support to intrinsic motivation and transitioning from short-term safeguards to long-term development. By fulfill distinct functions at various stages of volunteer service, these four incentive types provide practical guidance for the dynamic adaptation and refined management of hospice care volunteer incentive mechanisms.

Recommendations

Organizations should: (1) provide solid basic support and specialized health benefits to volunteers; (2) strengthen volunteers’ sense of spiritual identification and enhance their intrinsic motivation; (3) guide service coordinators in improving management practices and adopting flexible, adaptive models; (4) implement incentive strategies tailored to volunteers’ developmental stages to motivate hospice care volunteers effectively; (5) adaptively assign roles based on each volunteer’s strengths and preferences, allowing for flexible adjustments to service formats; (6) build a positive public image of volunteer service and improve team cohesion; and (7) create clear pathways for professional growth for volunteers seeking career development.

These measures aim to promote volunteer retention, support a sustainable hospice volunteer service system, and provide concrete guidance for evidence-based incentive practices in hospice care.

Limitations

Due to the social desirability effect, volunteers may overstate the importance of spiritual incentives while underreporting dissatisfaction with insufficient material subsidies, thus compromising data authenticity and preventing a full reflection of actual needs [37]. To address this, volunteers are encouraged to clearly articulate their true requirements in areas such as material support, emotional fulfillment, and professional training during motivational feedback sessions. Additionally, hospice care volunteering occurs in diverse settings, with considerable variations in service intensity and risk levels, which may require diverse incentive approaches [38]. However, the current study did not refine incentive pathways based on contextual stratification, which may limit its applicability to diverse hospice care settings. Future research should explore the variations in volunteer motivation across contexts. Furthermore, as the research is situated within a specific local cultural context under a global framework, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other cultural or geographical settings.

Conclusion

Managers should implement diversified and reinforcing incentive strategies based on the varied motivations of volunteers. Given that hospice care volunteers generally place greater emphasis on spiritual fulfilment and personal growth experiences, management practices should prioritize strengthening spiritual and growth-oriented incentives. This approach enhances volunteers’ intrinsic motivation and promotes sustained participation, thereby improving the sustainability of palliative care volunteering services. Future quantitative research may combine different service scenarios and volunteer groups characteristics to verify the effectiveness of the four-dimensional incentive strategies and to construct a dynamically adaptive incentive evaluation and optimization system for hospice care volunteers. Furthermore, cross-cultural research will be conducted in follow-up studies to validate and generalize the current research outcomes. Future research should also explore the variations in volunteer motivation across different contextual backgrounds.
