Disseminating cognitive-behavioral therapy for body dysmorphic disorder: Evaluating the efficacy of online provider training
Authors: Geneva K. Jonathan, Peyton E. Miyares, Susanne S. Hoeppner, Sara Velazquez, Ryan J. Jacoby, Jennifer L. Greenberg, Susan Sprich, Sabine Wilhelm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBody dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is a severe psychiatric condition affecting approximately 2% of the population, yet access to evidence-based cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is limited due to insufficient clinician training. Web-based training offers a solution for disseminating specialized treatment skills, but real-world effectiveness data are lacking. This study evaluated a brief, online CBT training program for BDD delivered under naturalistic conditions.MethodsBetween 2017 and 2023, 166 clinicians enrolled in a 4-week web-based training covering BDD assessment and CBT treatment techniques. The training included video lectures, case examples, discussion boards, and optional Q&A sessions. Participants completed surveys at baseline, post-training, and 6-month follow-up assessing self-reported confidence assessing and treating BDD, beliefs about CBT, self-reported implementation of evidence-based practices (EBPs), and perceived barriers. Mixed-effects models evaluated changes over time, and regression analyses examined predictors of dropout and outcomes.ResultsClinicians demonstrated significant improvements from baseline to post-training in self-reported confidence assessing BDD (EMM difference = 31.1), confidence treating BDD (EMM difference = 37.7), positive beliefs about CBT (EMM difference = 7.9), and EBP implementation (EMM difference = 6.2%; all p < .001). Gains were maintained at 6-month follow-up. Several barriers showed descriptive decreases, though none reached statistical significance. Baseline EBP use was the only significant predictor of outcomes: clinicians with lower baseline EBP use showed greater improvement in assessment confidence but smaller gains in CBT-related beliefs.ConclusionsBrief web-based training can enhance clinician self-reported confidence and readiness to deliver CBT for BDD, supporting dissemination of specialized skills. However, high attrition and reliance on self-report highlight challenges inherent to naturalistic training evaluation.

Introduction

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is a prevalent and debilitating mental health condition that affects approximately 2% of the general population, with higher rates observed in dermatology (21%) and cosmetic surgery (57%) settings (Minty and Minty, 2021). Individuals experience persistent preoccupations with perceived appearance flaws and engage in repetitive behaviors such as mirror checking, concealment, and social comparison (Fang and Wilhelm, 2015; Association, A.P., 2013). Onset typically occurs during adolescence (Bjornsson et al., 2013; Bjornsson and Phillips, 2017), and follows a chronic course marked by severe distress (Phillips et al., 2006), elevated suicide risk (Angelakis et al., 2016), significant social and occupational impairment (Phillips et al., 2008; Didie et al., 2008), high rates of psychiatric comorbidity (Gunstad and Phillips, 2003; Rautio et al., 2022), and frequent relapse without appropriate intervention (Phillips et al., 2013).

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most effective first-line treatment for BDD (Fang and Wilhelm, 2015). It targets the underlying mechanisms of BDD, including avoidance behaviors, distorted beliefs, and selective attention to perceived flaws (Jonathan et al., 2024; Wilhelm et al., 2012, Wilhelm et al., 2019). CBT has demonstrated effectiveness for adults and adolescents across face-to-face and digital treatment modalities (Jonathan et al., 2024; Harrison et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2024).

Despite its efficacy, many individuals with BDD do not receive CBT. Barriers include shame and fear of stigma, preference for cosmetic or dermatological care over mental health services, and skepticism about its efficacy (Buhlmann, 2011; Marques et al., 2011; Schulte et al., 2020; Weingarden et al., 2018; Bosbach et al., 2024). Only 15% of treatment-seeking individuals with BDD ever receive a formal diagnosis (Schulte et al., 2020; Veale et al., 2016). Among those who are diagnosed, rates of CBT receipt vary considerably—from approximately 17% in a U.S. study (Marques et al., 2011) to 69% in an Australian sample (McCausland et al., 2021), and essential components such as in-session exposure (e.g., completing exposure tasks with a therapist during session) are inconsistently implemented (McCausland et al., 2021). Many receive less effective interventions, such as supportive counseling (Wilhelm et al., 2019; McCausland et al., 2021), suggesting limited availability of training opportunities for providers to deliver BDD-specific CBT.

Web-based training programs have expanded access to evidence-based CBT education for anxiety and related disorders (Mikkonen et al., 2024; Kobak et al., 2013, Kobak et al., 2017). These interactive trainings, which feature transdiagnostic, principle-focused CBT, have demonstrated outcomes in skills acquisition comparable to in-person formats, particularly when they include quizzes, demonstration videos, and opportunities for feedback (Mikkonen et al., 2024; Henrich et al., 2023). Programs targeting disorders with overlapping mechanisms to BDD, such as obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), have focused on post-training skill development and evidence-based practice (EBP) implementation (Kobak et al., 2017; Jacoby et al., 2019). However, determining the most effective training methods to improve clinician confidence and ensure continued implementation is a significant challenge in the field.

Previous research on EBP training has been limited by methodological heterogeneity, including inconsistent outcome definitions, small sample sizes, and dependence on self-report measures (Mikkonen et al., 2024; Frank et al., 2020; Singh and Reyes-Portillo, 2020; Valenstein-Mah et al., 2020). Although studies demonstrate that active training improves short-term provider knowledge and self-efficacy compared to no training or self-study of treatment materials (Mikkonen et al., 2024; Frank et al., 2020; Singh and Reyes-Portillo, 2020; Valenstein-Mah et al., 2020), findings regarding the additive benefit of post-training consultation have been mixed (Valenstein-Mah et al., 2020; Ragnarsson et al., 2024), and no training modality has demonstrated clear superiority over another (Mikkonen et al., 2024; Ragnarsson et al., 2024). A recent supervised online training for clinicians treating pediatric BDD showed promise in improving clinician competence (Gumpert et al., 2025), but was conducted with intensive supervision (18 min per clinician per week) in controlled research settings, leaving questions about training effectiveness when implemented independently in real-world practice.

To address this gap, our team, in collaboration with the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Psychiatry Academy, developed a brief, web-based program to teach clinicians skills in diagnosing and treating BDD using an evidence-based treatment protocol (Wilhelm et al., 2011). The present study represents the first evaluation of CBT for BDD training delivered at scale in a naturalistic, real-world dissemination context with a geographically diverse sample of community clinicians.

This study had three aims. Aim 1 assessed the training's effectiveness in improving clinicians' knowledge and self-reported confidence in assessing and treating BDD at two timepoints: post-training and a 6-month follow-up. Aim 2 investigated factors influencing training dropout. Aim 3 explored baseline factors predicting improvements in clinician knowledge and confidence.

Method

Recruitment and eligibility

The course was openly accessible, with no prerequisites, and promoted via email advertisements, Google search, and the websites of the International OCD Foundation (IOCDF), Massachusetts General Hospital Psychiatry Academy, and Massachusetts General Hospital Center for OCD and Related Disorders. Enrollment was available three times per year (April, August, and October), with an unrestricted class size. All clinicians who enrolled in a Psychiatry Academy workshop between November 2017 and October 2023 were invited to participate in the study. Recruitment and data collection spanned the COVID-19 pandemic (2020−2022), which may have influenced clinicians' interest in and need for flexible, online training opportunities.

Psychiatry academy course

The training content was based on a well-established, evidence-based treatment protocol specifically developed for BDD (Wilhelm et al., 2011; Wilhelm and Neziroglu, 2002). This CBT approach targets the core maintaining mechanisms of BDD, including selective attention to perceived flaws, distorted appearance-related beliefs, ritualistic and avoidance behaviors. The training was designed to be brief and feasible (i.e., able to reach and engage community clinicians with minimal supervision) for dissemination while covering evidence-based skills for assessing and treating BDD. The ultimate goal of the training was to increase clinicians' comfort and confidence in using evidence-based skills so they could implement them in community practice settings.

The 4-week course was delivered entirely online. It was developed by a licensed clinical psychologist and CBT expert in BDD (SW), author of the evidence-based treatment manual for BDD (Wilhelm et al., 2012). The training was designed as a didactic program applicable to clinicians working with BDD clients of all ages. Participants were required to complete each weekly module in sequence. Each module included a one-hour pre-recorded video lecture with accompanying PowerPoint slides, assigned and optional supplementary readings, a discussion board where participants could post questions about course content, and two optional 30-minute live phone Q&A sessions with faculty (SW). Video lectures included case examples demonstrating the application of specific evidence-based assessment and treatment techniques. A licensed clinical psychologist with expertise in BDD (JG) monitored the discussion board and responded to participant questions asynchronously in writing within 48 h on weekdays. Discussion board questions were limited to didactic content and clarification of treatment techniques; participants were not permitted to seek consultation on their own clinical cases as part of this training program.

Week 1 covered diagnostic techniques, clinical features of BDD, a CBT treatment overview, the role of family, and motivational strategies. Week 2 focused on individualized case formulation, cognitive restructuring, and mindfulness. Week 3 emphasized practical CBT tools, including exposure planning, response prevention, perceptual retraining, and core belief modification. Week 4 addressed BDD-specific concerns such as skin picking, muscle dysmorphia, and cosmetic treatment and introduced relapse prevention strategies.

Participants were eligible to receive continuing education credits upon completion.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Review Board. Participation in the research surveys was optional. After completing informed consent, a survey was administered to training participants at three timepoints: (1) before the training began (pre-survey, PRE), (2) immediately after the training concluded (post-survey, POST), and (3) 6-months after the course (follow-up survey, 6MFU). All surveys were administered online via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure web-based data capture software platform (Harris et al., 2009) hosted at Massachusetts General Brigham. Participation in the research surveys was not required for CE eligibility. To incentivize participation, those who completed either the PRE or POST survey plus the 6MFU were offered a $25 discount on an IOCDF membership and a $100 discount on a future Psychiatry Academy course.

Measures

Personal and professional characteristics

At baseline (PRE), clinicians completed self-report questionnaires that collected demographics, including age, gender, race, ethnicity, and U.S. state or country of residence. Clinical variables included degree (e.g., doctorate, master's), years since earning professional degree (Orlinsky et al., 2001), current work setting (e.g., private practice, inpatient/outpatient hospital, community mental health, primary care), and prior training in CBT for BDD (e.g., textbook readings, workshops, consultation). Participants reported theoretical orientations used in their practice (percentages totaling 100%, e.g., CBT, mindfulness, family systems, supportive therapy) and their previous clinical experience treating BDD (number of child and adult cases, percentage of current caseload with BDD). At PRE and POST, they also rated their perceived importance of delivering EBPs (1 = not at all important to 5 = extremely important) and reported how often they received consultations for BDD-focused cases (e.g., “at least once a year”).

To evaluate barriers to implementing CBT skills, participants in the PRE and 6MFU groups identified reasons they might not use these techniques. The study team identified barriers based on over 20 years of clinical experience treating BDD and supervising trainees at an academic medical center. Items reflect concerns commonly raised by clinicians learning to implement CBT for BDD and included: (1) lack of competence, (2) limited experience or access to treating patients with BDD, (3) lack of supervision, (4) time constraints, (5) inability to leave the office for exposures, (6) discomfort with making patients anxious, (7) challenges generating exposure ideas, (8) not feeling supported by employer/colleagues, (9) perceived irrelevance of techniques for their patients, and (10) patients being resistant to the techniques. Participants could select multiple barriers or specify “Other” reasons. Barriers were assessed PRE and 6MFU, with yes/no responses. At 6MFU, participants were given the option to report that their barriers had resolved.

Clinical skills implementation

Feasibility was operationalized by enrollment, retention, and completion rates. At PRE and POST, clinicians self-reported the percentage of session time spent on 23 clinical skills for treating BDD (totaling 100%) drawn from prior research (Whiteside et al., 2016; Hipol and Deacon, 2013), divided into EBPs (n = 17) and non-EBPs (n = 6). EBPs included the following techniques considered essential to CBT for BDD: (1) structured diagnostic assessment, (2) case conceptualization, (3) perceptual retraining for mirror checking, (4) habit reversal training for BDD-related skin picking or hair pulling, (5) behavioral activation for BDD-related depression, (6) psychoeducation, (7) exposure hierarchy development, (8) in vivo and imaginal exposure with response prevention, (9) relapse prevention. Other CBT-based approaches, such as (10) cognitive strategies, (11) acceptance-based strategies, (12) mindfulness, (13) emotion regulation skills training, (14) motivational interviewing, (15) family-focused interventions, and (16) managing treatment resistance, were also included under EBPs.

Non-EBPs refer to non-specific methods unlikely to directly target BDD symptoms, such as (1) problem-solving and (2) reflective listening, as well as skills contraindicated with exposure therapy, like (3) progressive muscle relaxation and (4) diaphragmatic breathing. Additionally, non-CBT interventions, such as (5) dream analysis and (6) analysis of transference and countertransference, were categorized as non-EBPs.

Proportional recalibration was applied to responses whose totals did not equal 100%, whether exceeding or falling short (e.g., 80% or 150%). This approach maintains the intended relative emphasis across skills, regardless of whether the original values were equal or reflected different usage frequencies. Data were summarized as the percentage of implementation of EBP skills.

Comfort level assessing and treating BDD

Participants self-reported their comfort levels assessing and treating BDD on separate scales ranging from 0 (not at all comfortable) to 100 (completely comfortable).

Beliefs about exposure therapy and CBT

Participants responded to 10 items (rated 0–100; self-reported) regarding their perceptions of exposure therapy and CBT (Appendix A). Negatively-worded items were reversed (e.g., “I believe that purposely making my patients anxious through exposure therapy will make my patients' symptoms worse”). Then, all items were averaged to compute a total score, such that higher scores indicated more positive beliefs about exposure therapy.

Data management

A total of 386 clinicians were invited to participate in the evaluation study. Of these, 243 consented to participate. However, 52 participants were excluded due to fully or mostly missing baseline data, and an additional 25 participants consented but did not begin the baseline survey. This resulted in a final baseline analytic sample of 166 participants (43.0% of those invited). Of these, 148 (89.2%) completed the POST survey, and 73 (44.0%) completed the 6MFU survey (see Fig. 1 for participant flow). Data collection concluded on December 31, 2023. The final training cohort enrolled in October 2023 (n = 20), resulting in PRE and POST data but no 6MFU data for this cohort, as their 6-month follow-up would have occurred after the data collection end date. These participants were retained in analyses involving PRE and POST timepoints to maximize sample representativeness.

For each outcome, participants with missing or maximum (ceiling) baseline scores were excluded from that outcome's analysis, as they could not improve further or were missing baseline data. The number of exclusions varied by outcome: 8 for comfort assessing BDD, 11 for comfort treating BDD, 19 for beliefs about CBT, and 19 for EBP implementation.

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputations via fully conditional specification in SAS (Yuan, 2011). Analyses were conducted on multiple imputed datasets and pooled using Rubin's method (Rubin, 2018). Of the 166 participants who completed the baseline survey, 66 (39.8%) had at least one missing baseline data point, with no single variable missing for more than 27 participants (16.3%); at POST, 46.4% of participants (77/166) were missing at least one outcome variable, while at the 6MFU 69.3% (115/166) were missing at least one outcome variable. Detailed procedures for missing data imputation are provided in Appendix B.

Several demographic and clinical variables were recoded to address small sample sizes in specific categories and improve model stability. Recoding procedures, including categorizations for gender, race, ethnicity, degree, region, work setting, years post-degree, and percent of clinical practice devoted to CBT for BDD, are described in Appendix C.

Data analysis

Personal and professional characteristics

Descriptive statistics, including percentages, sample sizes, means, and standard deviations (as applicable), were used to summarize clinicians' demographics, clinical practice characteristics, and barriers to implementing CBT skills. To examine within-person changes in barrier endorsement from PRE to 6MFU, we used generalized estimating equations among participants who completed barrier items at both timepoints (n = 54). Of the 73 participants who completed the 6MFU, 19 were missing barrier data at one or both timepoints because these survey items were not required. Barriers marked “NA” were not assessed at both timepoints.

Clinician outcomes over time (Aim 1)

Hierarchical mixed models analyzed clinician outcomes over time (categorical; PRE, POST, 6MFU); repeated measures per participant over time were modeled with an unstructured covariance matrix. Outcome measures included (a) self-reported comfort levels in assessing BDD, (b) self-reported comfort levels in treating BDD, (c) beliefs about exposure therapy and CBT for BDD, and (d) percentage of session time devoted to evidence-based practices (EBPs). Comparisons between PRE and POST, and between PRE and 6MFU, were reported as adjusted estimated marginal means (EMMs), standard errors of EMMs, EMM differences with 95% confidence intervals, and sample sizes for each time point. Our primary analyses used all available data with methods appropriate for handling missingness (hierarchical mixed models for repeated measures outcomes; multiple imputation for predictor analyses in Aims 2–3). We also conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to completers (Appendix E). For primary outcomes, completers were defined as participants with outcome data at all three timepoints (PRE, POST, and 6MFU; n = 51); the same repeated measures mixed models were applied.

Predictors of clinician dropout (Aim 2)

Drop-out was defined as noncompletion of the 6MFU survey; however, all available data were included in imputed models. Logistic regression models were used to test predictors of assessment dropout (missingness) at POST or 6MFU. Predictor variables included categorical variables of gender, racial/ethnic minority status, highest degree, treatment setting, geographical region, enrollment cohort, years post-degree, percentage of evidence-based practices used, and baseline barriers, as well as the continuous predictors self-reported comfort level in assessing BDD, self-reported comfort level in treating BDD, test scores, and average beliefs about exposure, all assessed at baseline (PRE). All predictors were tested simultaneously. Due to the exploratory nature of these analyses, raw p-values are reported without family-wise error adjustment.

Predictors of post-training clinician outcomes (Aim 3)

Multiple regression models examined predictors of change scores of clinician outcomes at POST and 6MFU using multiply imputed data (Appendix B). Predictor variables included baseline scores of the modeled outcome, gender, racial/ethnic minority status (yes/no), categorized years post-degree, categorized percentage of evidence-based practices used, and categorized baseline barriers (see Appendix C). Given a high correlation (r = 0.75) between age and years post-degree, only the years post-degree categories were retained to avoid multicollinearity. All predictors were tested simultaneously. The significance of predictors was evaluated at a Bonferroni-adjusted p < .0125 (α = 0.05, adjusted for 4 outcomes).

Aim 2: predictors of survey noncompletion (clinician dropout)

Several predictors were examined to identify factors associated with clinician dropout from POST and 6MFU assessments (Appendix F). Non-female-identifying clinicians had higher odds of POST survey noncompletion than female-identifying clinicians (OR = 1.82, 95% CI [1.07, 3.09], p = .0278). Clinicians reporting three or more perceived barriers were significantly more likely to complete the POST survey than those reporting no barriers (OR for noncompletion = 0.30, 95% CI [0.10, 0.90], p = .0324). We were unable to identify any other demographic or clinical practice-related predictors of noncompletion of the POST survey. For 6MFU, we did not detect any demographic, professional, or cohort predictors of dropout.

Aim 3: predictors of changes in clinician outcomes at 6-month follow-up

Baseline use of evidence-based practices emerged as the only significant predictor of training outcomes at 6MFU (0–75% vs. 90% or more; Appendix G). Clinicians who used EBPs less frequently at baseline (0–75% vs. 90% or more) showed greater improvement in comfort assessing BDD (b = 9.63, 95% CI [2.68, 16.58], p = .0062) but less improvement in CBT-related beliefs (b = −9.87, 95% CI [−16.26, −3.48], p = .0025). No other demographic (gender, race/ethnicity) or professional characteristics (years since degree, number of baseline barriers) significantly predicted change in any of the four outcome measures at the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold (p < .0125).

Discussion

Participant characteristics and training context

The training reached over 300 clinicians across 8 countries and 32 U.S. states, including providers from states with documented mental health workforce shortages (Reinert et al., 2025), such as Alabama (740:1 population-to-provider ratio), Tennessee (530:1), and Indiana (500:1) (Reinert et al., 2025). This geographic breadth demonstrates the scalability of web-based training for specialized treatments beyond traditional academic settings (Singh and Reyes-Portillo, 2020; Bennett-Levy and Perry, 2009).

However, the sample reflected limited diversity, with most participants identifying as female (81%), White, non-Hispanic (90%), and working in private practice (61%). These demographics align with national workforce data (Lin et al., 2018, Lin et al., 2015) but highlight persistent challenges in reaching underrepresented groups, male clinicians, and providers in community mental health or primary care settings where BDD symptoms are underrecognized (Marques et al., 2011; Schulte et al., 2020). Our training attracted clinicians already familiar with CBT, potentially missing those who could most benefit from this introductory training.

Critically, participants demonstrated a significant gap between clinical need and specialized preparation. Nearly a quarter of participants reported no prior CBT for BDD training, and those who did typically cited passive learning such as reading textbook chapters (49%) or treatment manuals (45%), rather than formal workshops or supervised practice. BDD comprised just 4% of clinicians' caseload on average, yet two-thirds endorsed barriers to implementing evidence-based treatment, most commonly, lack of competence, limited case access, and difficulty generating exposure ideas. This profile, clinicians encountering BDD patients but lacking specialized confidence or knowledge, supports the need for accessible, practical training in this area.

Despite these gaps in BDD-specific preparation, participants were not novices to EBPs. Ninety-six percent valued evidence-based treatment, 72% of their clinical work was CBT-grounded, and 66% received consultation on evidence-based approaches. This foundation suggests the training reached an appropriate audience: clinicians with transferable CBT skills who needed specialized BDD content. Given the complexity of BDD and its elevated suicide risk (Angelakis et al., 2016), this brief training is best viewed as a specialized supplement to existing CBT skills rather than standalone preparation, and clinicians should have access to supervision before implementing BDD-specific interventions independently.

Training outcomes

This approximately four-hour program was associated with significant improvements in clinicians' self-reported comfort assessing and treating BDD, beliefs about CBT and exposure therapy, and reported EBP implementation, consistent with the training's primary goals and with research linking increased confidence and positive attitudes to greater evidence-based practice adoption (Mikkonen et al., 2024; Kobak et al., 2013, Kobak et al., 2017).

Although several barriers showed descriptive decreases from PRE to 6MFU, only one change reached statistical significance: more clinicians endorsed that CBT techniques were “not relevant” to their patient population after training (1.9% to 16.7%, p = .03). Concerns about patient appropriateness for a specific treatment are among the most commonly reported post-training barriers, even when clinician confidence and beliefs improve (Harned et al., 2021). However, with structured post-training consultation, community clinicians have been found to overcome such concerns (Pincus et al., 2022). The increase observed here may reflect improved familiarity with BDD diagnostic criteria that helped clinicians differentiate BDD from other appearance-related conditions. This finding warrants further investigation.

Consistent with the broader training literature, improved attitudes did not uniformly translate to practice change. Limited access to BDD patients continued as a common concern at follow-up, and prior research demonstrates that clinicians frequently use individual protocol components rather than full treatment protocols after training (Chu et al., 2015), particularly when organizational supports such as supervision and protected time are absent (Frank et al., 2023). Post-training support, such as consultation, peer supervision, or structured feedback, may be necessary to bridge this gap between training gains and routine clinical implementation (Frank et al., 2020; Beidas et al., 2012).

Predictors of clinician dropout/outcomes

Regarding study retention, few predictors emerged. Clinicians identifying as male or other genders had higher odds of not completing post-training assessments compared to female clinicians, warranting investigation to ensure equitable engagement across participant groups. Clinicians who endorsed multiple barriers were more likely to complete POST assessments, though this pattern did not persist at 6MFU.

Among demographic and professional characteristics, only baseline EBP use emerged as a significant predictor of training outcomes. Null findings should be interpreted cautiously given our sample size and the potential for ceiling effects. Consistent with prior work (Creed et al., 2021), clinicians with lower baseline EBP use showed the greatest improvements in comfort assessing BDD, but smaller changes in EBP-related beliefs. Attitude change regarding exposure use may require more than didactic instruction (Farrell et al., 2013, Farrell et al., 2016); training incorporating therapist-delivered exposure practice, self-reflective exercises, or ongoing live supervision may facilitate belief change (Frank et al., 2023; Beidas et al., 2012; Spencer et al., 2023; Kemp et al., 2023).

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, we assessed self-reported outcomes rather than objective clinician behavior. While self-report may not perfectly reflect actual practice, it provides valid information about clinician confidence and attitudes, factors associated with treatment adoption (Beidas et al., 2012). Future research should incorporate objective competency assessments. Second, high levels of missing data pose a risk of bias, particularly if data were not missing at random. Although dropout analyses found no evidence that baseline characteristics predicted 6MFU completion, we cannot rule out that unmeasured factors influenced retention. Third, without a control group, we cannot rule out the effects of external factors, such as self-directed learning. Fourth, the sample of clinicians who enrolled in this training was likely self-selected and skewed toward providers already interested in BDD. Finally, outcomes were measured only immediately after training and at 6 months, leaving questions about the long-term sustainability and real-world adoption.

Future directions

Future training initiatives should prioritize wider outreach to reach underrepresented professionals to improve equitable access. Efforts should also target settings where BDD symptoms emerge but are underrecognized, such as school counseling centers, college mental health services, pediatric practices, and primary care (Schulte et al., 2020). To support participation among providers with limited financial or time resources, subsidized training, or employer-supported protected time may improve accessibility. Implementation strategies such as train-the-trainer models (Wainberg et al., 2021) and embedding culturally responsive content may further improve reach and engagement.

Conclusion

This study supports the potential of utilizing brief, web-based training to enhance clinician readiness to deliver CBT for BDD, a condition that remains underrecognized and undertreated in many clinical settings. Expanding access to this type of training, particularly among generalist providers and those in early intervention settings, represents an important step toward closing the longstanding gaps in BDD care. For clinicians, these findings reinforce that specialized, evidence-based treatment for BDD is both learnable and implementable within standard practice frameworks.
