Effect of cabergoline on the management of diabetes mellitus: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shahrazad Khorasani, Tahereh Dara, Hamidreza Dehghan, Mohsen Zabihi, Maryam Saghafi, Fatemeh Saghafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the oldest diseases known to man. Type 2 diabetes is characterized by relative insulin deficiency due to pancreatic beta cell dysfunction and insulin resistance in target organs, which will reach 629 million people by 2045. On the other hand, dopamine and dopaminergic signals control some metabolic pathways. Cabergoline is a long-acting dopamine agonist with a high affinity for dopamine receptors.Materials and methodsIn the current systematic review and Meta-analysis, all research published in PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus databases investigating the effect of cabergoline on the control of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), postprandial glucose (PPG), and HbA1c has been included. The overall certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach. Comprehensive Meta-analysis Software was used for the statistical analyses using a random-effects model.ResultsA total of six articles (206 participants) were found from the mentioned databases. Data were extracted from six trials. Pooled Meta-analysis revealed that cabergoline significantly reduced FPG (SMD = -2.058; 95% CI: -3.269, -0.848), PPG (SMD = -1.786; 95% CI: (-3.035, -0.539), and HbA1c (SMD = -1.282; 95% CI: -2.283), but the analysis had extremely high heterogeneity (P-value-heterogeneity = 0.000).ConclusionThe results indicate that cabergoline may positively affect the control of type 2 diabetes, as almost all of the six reviewed articles revealed a significant reduction in FPG, PPG, and HbA1c. However, further studies with a larger sample size and on type 1 and 2 DM are required to confirm these results.Keywords: Cabergoline, Diabetes mellitus, Dopamine agonists, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

DM is seemingly one of the oldest known diseases and is a metabolic disease characterized mostly by increased blood glucose levels. DM is increasingly becoming an extensive non-communicable health problem, leading to significant morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Despite advancements in treatment, only 24.5% of patients achieve optimal glycemic control, defined by an HbA1c level below 6.5% [3]. According to the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 629 million people will be affected by 2045 [4]. Of these, approximately 80% of affected individuals live in low-income countries [5]. In particular, type 2 DM (T2DM) accounts for over 90% of all diabetes cases. The increasing burden is due to several risk factors such as sedentary lifestyles, obesity, unhealthy diet, lack of exercise, family history, and aging [6–8].

In 2015, DM was ranked as the sixth leading cause of disability worldwide, placing considerable social and economic burdens on individuals and healthcare systems. Cardiovascular disease remains the most significant cause of morbidity and mortality related to T2DM. Several large randomized controlled trials, including the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) and Action on Diabetes and Vascular Disease (ADVD), have shown the benefits of glucose management on microvascular complications, such as retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. Consequently, anti-diabetic drugs improve blood sugar and reduce cardiovascular risk [9–11]. Using natural treatments with antioxidant properties such as Okra and Corchorus olitorius, which exhibit anti-hyperglycemic effects with safety profile, may help reduce the burden of CVD in the DM population [12, 13].

Cabergoline as a dopamine receptor agonists is mostly used to treat disorders related to prolactin overproduction. However, recent studies have shown that cabergoline may have potential impacts on diabetes management [14, 15]. Its pharmacological impacts on dopamine receptors in various tissues, including the pancreas, liver, and skeletal muscle may affect glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and appetite regulation. There are ongoing studies on the use of cabergoline in managing diabetes, and some clinical studies and case reports have provided promising results. However, more studies are needed to fully understand its effectiveness and safety in this area [16, 17].

To our knowledge in September 2021, cabergoline was generally only available by physician’s prescription due to potential side effects and the drug’s-controlled status in some regions. However, drug regulations may change over time. Hence, it is essential to consult with a healthcare provider or the relevant authorities for the most up-to-date information on cabergoline prescription status in your region. In short, cabergoline is a versatile medication with a well-established history. It is rooted in the development of ergot alkaloids. Ongoing studies indicate its potential role in the management of diabetes, all of which are capable of capturing attention in endocrinology and neurology. Diabetes mellitus is widely regarded as one of the most oldest documented diseases. Type 2 diabetes is characterized by relative insulin deficiency due to pancreatic beta cell dysfunction and insulin resistance in target organs. Its rate will reach 642 million people by 2040 [16, 17].

Also, dopamine and dopaminergic signals control some metabolic pathways, such as the pathways in the central nervous system (CNS). Bromocriptine is a dopaminergic agonist that can affect serotonin turnover in the CNS. Also, it has been shown that treatment with dopaminergic agonists reduces hypothalamic stimulation, which increases during hepatic gluconeogenesis, lipid synthesis, and insulin resistance [18].

To our knowledge, no systematic review and Meta-analysis have examined the effect of cabergoline alone on the therapeutic indicators of DM. Therefore, a detailed review of recent clinical studies and an analysis of diabetes-related surrogate outcomes are essential for achieving a comprehensive and scientifically sound conclusion.

Materials and methods

Study design

Systematic literature review and Meta-analysis. The effect of cabergoline on the control of diabetes was utilized. The reviewed literature was analyzed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) [19]. A systematic literature search based on PICO-SD (participants, intervention, comparison, outcomes, study design) was conducted to select literature for analysis. Participants (P) were type 2 DM patients aged over 18. Intervention (I) was focusing on the glucose-lowering effects of cabergoline. The control group (C) consisted of patients who received usual care and self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG). The outcome (O) was glycemic control. Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included to ensure objective evidence on intervention effectiveness. All studies provided convertible statistical data (sample sizes, means, standard deviations, and effect sizes). Studies or theses not available as original text, survey research, and single-group comparative studies were excluded. The study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration code of CRD420251014465.

Literature search strategy

The literature search was conducted based on COSI (COre Standard, Ideal) provided by the National Library of Medicine (NLM) using the following core databases [20]: international databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and ISI Web of Science. The search was conducted for studies published up to 31 December 2024. The primary search terms used were (“cabergoline” OR “dopamine agonist”) AND (“diabetes mellitus, type 2” OR “diabetes mellitus” OR “type 2 diabetes” OR “T2DM”) AND (“glycemic control” OR “blood glucose” OR “HbA1c” OR “fasting plasma glucose” OR “postprandial glucose”) NOT (“Pediatrics” OR “Neonates” OR “Infant” OR “Children” OR “Animal Study” OR “rodents” OR “mice” OR “rats”). For domestic databases, the search was conducted using combinations of T2DM and glycemic control. In addition, manual searches were conducted for studies included as references, ongoing clinical trials, and the Google Scholar search engine was utilized as grey literature for related research topics.

Quality assessment of the selected studies

The quality of selected studies was assessed using the checklist for RCT studies included in Joanna Briggs Institute of Critical Appraisal Tools [21]. This checklist comprises 13 items and is as follows: random assignment, allocation concealment, treatment group similarity, blinding of participants, blinding of delivered treatment, blinding of outcome assessor, similar treatment, follow-up completion, intention-to-treat analysis, consistent method of assessing outcome measures in groups, reliability of outcome measures, appropriate statistical analysis, and appropriate trial design. Each item received a score of 0 (‘no’ or ‘unclear’) or 1 (‘yes’), and thus the maximum possible score was 13 points. One reviewer (SK) performed this assessment for each study, and a second reviewer (MS) confirmed the results. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus (HRD).

Selection process for the analyzed literature

Two independent investigators (SK and TD) extracted trial details using a standardized form, and any disagreements were resolved through discussions with FS and MZ. A list of identified studies from domestic and international databases was compiled using Microsoft Excel 2016, and duplicate studies were removed. Subsequently, titles and abstracts were reviewed to determine whether studies met the selection criteria. Finally, full texts were reviewed, and studies were selected for analysis.

Data analysis

Meta-analysisComprehensive Meta-analysis Software (version 2, USA) was used for the statistical analyses using a random-effects model [22]. For all study outcomes, Hedge’s g was utilized as the effect size, considering that many studies had a small sample size [23]. Hedge’s g values were interpreted as follows: an effect size of ≥ 0.2 but < 0.5 was categorized as small, an effect size of ≥ 0.5 but < 0.8 as medium, and an effect size of ≥ 0.8 as large [24]. The significance level for effect size was set at 0.05, and the confidence interval (CI) at 95%. The analysis was conducted using a random effects model because of the variances exhibited by study participants and study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I-squared (I2) statistic and was deemed absent when I2 was 0%, medium at 50%, and high at 75% [25]. Egger’s regression and Begg’s tests and the trim and fill method were used to confirm publication bias [26, 27].

Results

Literature selection

In this study, all domestic and foreign articles related to the desired keywords were searched and collected up to 31 December 2024. They were considered as the statistical population of the study. After reviewing and excluding contradictory articles (reviews, case reports, case series, editorials, observational, in-vivo, or in-vitro studies), the remaining articles (including 6 articles [28–33]) were reviewed. Among the remaining articles, some others were also removed after reviewing the titles and summaries of the articles that were unrelated or had less relevance, were not related to the effect of the cabergoline drug on diabetes control, or were conducted on animals. Finally, six cases (206 participants) were finalized and studied (Fig. 1). All the included articles were in English and four of the six studies were conducted in Iran. The administered dose of cabergoline was from 0.25 mg weekly to 0.5 mg twice weekly. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Meta-analysis

A pooled Meta-analysis including six studies revealed that cabergoline significantly reduced FPG but the analysis had extremely high heterogeneity (SMD = -2.058; 95% CI: -3.269, -0.848; P-value = 0.001; Cochrane Q test: I2 = 91.789%; P-value-heterogeneity = 0.000). Although PPG (SMD = -1.786; 95% CI: (-3.035, -0.539; P-value = 0.005) and HbA1c (SMD = -1.282; 95% CI: -2.283, -0.280; P-value = 0.012) were significantly greater in the cabergoline group compared to the control group, but the heterogeneity was high (P-value-heterogeneity = 0.000). Most trials showed a statistically significant advantage of cabergoline use. To test the robustness of the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by omitting each study in turn; the pooled outcome remained statistically significant across all iterations. Overall, Cabergoline intervention significantly decreased diabetes-related parameters (Table 3; Fig. 2A-C).

Publication bias

Although slight asymmetries were observed in Begg’s funnel plots, there was no significant evidence of publication bias in the studies examining the effects of cabergoline on HbA1c (Begg’s test, P = 0.221), FPG (Begg’s test, P = 0.463), and PPG (Begg’s test, P = 0.303), as determined by asymmetry tests (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a systematic review and Meta-analysis aimed at the effects of cabergoline on diabetes control. We aimed to improve the level of evidence for intervention methods and provide suggestions for future research. A total of six trials were analyzed and the intervention of cabergoline was determined to reduce the glycemic parameters. Based on our literature review, this is the first time that cabergoline was introduced as an agent to abate the progressive worsening of glycemic control from prediabetes to T2DM.

In 2010, the US Food and Drug Administration evaluated the results of cabergoline rapid-release formulation clinical trials and approved it as an adjunct agent in addition to main oral agents for T2DM. Cabergoline exerts its effects by acting on several receptors, including α2B-adrenergic, 5-HT2C, 5-HT2A, 5-HT1A, and dopaminergic receptors D2, D3, and D4 [34]. While the effect of dopamine on the secretion of pancreatic β-cells remains unclear, animal studies showed that dopaminergic receptor type 2 may be involved in this process [35]. Using this agent has been reported to increase dopamine levels and decrease noradrenergic activity in the hypothalamus, leading to increased insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, and decreased liver gluconeogenesis [36]. Furthermore, the results of another animal study indicated that prolactin administration could beneficially affected carbohydrate and adipose tissue metabolism (i.e., reduced adiponectin production from adipose tissue) [37].

Previous studies [30, 33] revealed that cabergoline treatment reduces FPG, HBA1C, and prolactin levels in individuals with type 2 diabetic and persistent hyperglycemia. Saadat et al. observed a significant reduction in FPG and prolactin levels (P < 0.001) after 4 months of treatment with cabergoline in pre-diabetic patients [28, 32]. Additionally, they reported a significant decrease in HOMA-IR (P = 0.04), indicating improved insulin resistance [28]. While some research suggests that cabergoline may contribute to weight loss, other studies have not observed this effect after six months of treatment [34]. In our study, prolactin levels in patients receiving cabergoline decreased significantly from baseline to the end of the treatment period, aligning with findings from previous research. A reduction in prolactin levels is an anticipated outcome in patients treated with cabergoline.

The results of a study by Saadat et al. show that cabergoline treatment may be effective in reducing FPG and OGTT levels in people with IFG/IGT stage [32]. Also, prolactin levels in the treated group decreased significantly after four months of treatment. This study was conducted on a small sample size with only 27 participants, limiting the generalizability of the results. However, this study provides preliminary evidence that cabergoline therapy may be a potential treatment option for people with stage IFG/IGT. This study also revealed that weight changes, triglyceride, fasting insulin, and A1c in both groups decreased during 16 weeks. However, the differences were not significant. Statistically significant changes were observed in 2-hour insulin levels, HOMA2%S, and HOMA2 IR. However, further studies with larger sample sizes, and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these results and evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of cabergoline treatment in subjects with stage IFG/IGT.

The results of the study by Ali Asgharzadeh [28] are significant in the area of type 2 diabetes management. This study selected a small sample size of 44 patients, but the results indicate that cabergoline may not be effective in reducing FPG levels in people with persistent hyperglycemia. This study also revealed a significant reduction in HbA1c levels in the case group after the intervention, while the control group showed only a slight reduction. This result suggests that cabergoline may have a positive impact on blood sugar control in type 2 diabetes patients.

Two recent systematic reviews and Meta-analyses have evaluated the effectiveness of dopamine agonists in managing individuals with type 2 diabetes [38, 39]. The study by Andersen et al., which included nine trials of moderate to low quality (six evaluating bromocriptine and three assessing cabergoline), demonstrated a significant reduction in HbA1c and FPG levels without serious adverse events. Subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference in efficacy between cabergoline and bromocriptine [39]. The findings of Dereje et al.‘s systematic review and meta-analysis support previous research, demonstrating that dopamine D2 receptor agonists can significantly lower HbA1c and FPG levels in individuals with type 2 diabetes, without causing serious adverse effects [38].

This study only considered certain parameters and did not consider other important factors such as lifestyle changes or drug use. Thus, further studies with larger sample sizes and more comprehensive analyses are needed to confirm these results. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into potential treatment options for type 2 diabetes patients with persistent hyperglycemia. Future studies can rely on these results by examining the optimal dose and duration of cabergoline treatment, and its potential side effects and long-term effects on blood glucose control. Generally, this study emphasizes the significance of continued studies in the area of new and effective treatments for type 2 diabetes and a growing public health worry worldwide. The factors identified in this study might serve as basic guidelines for setting the direction of future cabergoline intervention research and developing and implementing effective intervention programs.

The limitations of the included studies, such as small sample sizes, high heterogeneity, lack of consistent findings, geographic restrictions to a single country, variation in cabergoline dosing regimens and treatment duration, short study durations, limited use of blinding, and lack of comprehensive data on both type 1 and type 2 diabetes are either overlooked or addressed only superficially, so they require a more comprehensive critique.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings suggest that cabergoline may enhance the management of type 2 diabetes, with nearly all six reviewed studies reporting notable reductions in FPG, PPG, and HbA1c levels. Nevertheless, the current evidence is constrained by factors such as small sample sizes with high heterogeneity, geographic restrictions to a single country, variation in cabergoline dose and treatment duration, short study durations, limited use of blinding, and a lack of comprehensive data on both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. To confirm these promising results, more rigorous, large-scale studies with thorough safety evaluations are essential.
