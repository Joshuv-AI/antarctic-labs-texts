Structural and biochemical characterization of a novel DinG containing an endonuclease domain
Authors: Shen Li, Tianwen Gao, Wanshan Hao, Hang Chen, Xiaolei Hu, Rong Qi, Cheng Chen, Kaiying Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DinG-like proteins are members of the XPD-family SF2 helicases and are widely distributed in bacteria, yet they exhibit remarkable diversity in domain architecture and biological function. A distinct subgroup of DinG homologs harboring an N-terminal PD-(D/E)XK-type nuclease domain, designated EndoDinGs, has remained uncharacterized. Here, we report the first structural and functional characterization of this subgroup using Finegoldia magna DinG (FmaDinG) as a representative example. FmaDinG displays Mn2+-preferred endonuclease activity together with Mg2+-dependent 5′–3′ unidirectional helicase activity. We determined the crystal structure of the FmaDinG–ssDNA–ADPNP complex at 2.26 Å resolution. Structural and biochemical analyses reveal a direct role of the Arch domain in DNA engagement and suggest a gating mechanism within the nuclease domain that regulates DNA entry, a feature that may be specific to the EndoDinG subgroup. Key residues required for nuclease and helicase activities were identified by site-directed mutagenesis. Together, these results support a mechanistic model in which EndoDinGs such as FmaDinG function as coordinated nuclease–helicase machines for DNA processing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06346-3.

Introduction

Members of the xeroderma pigmentosum complementation group D (XPD) helicase family are evolutionarily conserved SF2 enzymes that move along single-stranded DNA in a 5′–3′ direction using an inchworm-like translocation mechanism [1, 2]. Despite functional diversification, these proteins maintain a conserved structural framework consisting of two RecA-like motor domains (MD1 and MD2) and two auxiliary elements, the iron–sulfur-cluster-binding (FeS) domain and Arch domains [3–6]. The FeS module typically binds a [4Fe–4S] cluster associated with redox-related roles, although this feature is absent in certain pseudo-FeS variants [7, 8]. Positioned within MD1, the FeS and Arch domains act in concert to promote strand separation during DNA unwinding [2, 9, 10].

The bacterial damage-inducible G-like (DinG-like) helicases represent a highly diversified group within the XPD family, with substantial differences in domain architecture and biological function [7]. Canonical members such as Escherichia coli DinG and YoaA retain the conserved four-domain framework and function in SOS-induced DNA repair pathways [11–13]. CasDinG proteins function in CRISPR-associated defense systems and retain the core helicase architecture of XPD-family enzymes, including the MD1, MD2, and Arch domains, while featuring a degenerated pFeS module that lacks iron–sulfur coordination [14–17]. In some variants, such as CasDinG–HNH, this framework is supplemented by a C-terminal HNH nuclease domain essential for interference activity [17]. ExoDinG proteins represent a distinct subgroup characterized by the presence of an N-terminal exonuclease domain with 3′–5′ polarity [7, 8, 18]. Biochemical analyses of the Staphylococcus aureus homolog have verified its exonucleolytic activity and demonstrated that DNA degradation is functionally coupled to helicase-driven translocation. This coordination is influenced by ATP availability and supports efficient processing of DNA crosslink-containing substrates [8].

A subset of DinG homologs identified in several bacterial lineages, including Bdellovibrionales, Eubacteriales, Tissierellales, Candidatus Uabimicrobiales, Acanthopleuribacterales, and Calditrichales, possess an additional N-terminal nuclease module with predicted 5′–3′ exonuclease or endonuclease activity, in addition to the conserved XPD-like core domains. These proteins have been grouped as EndoDinGs [7]. Despite their distinct domain architecture, their structural organization and biochemical functions have not yet been elucidated.

In this study, we selected Finegoldia magna DinG (FmaDinG), a representative EndoDinG, for structural and biochemical characterization. We purified FmaDinG, co-crystallized it with ssDNA and the non-hydrolyzable ATP analog ADPNP, and determined its complex structure. Both nuclease and helicase activities were investigated independently and in combination. Key residues required for these activities were confirmed by site-directed mutagenesis. Finally, we proposed a mechanistic model illustrating how EndoDinG proteins such as FmaDinG coordinate their nuclease and helicase functions during DNA processing.

Overall structure of FmaDinG-ssDNA-ADPNP complex

The structure of a nuclease-dead FmaDinG mutant (D75A), whose lack of nuclease activity was confirmed by biochemical assay (see below), was determined in complex with ssDNA and the nonhydrolyzable ATP analog ADPNP at 2.26 Å resolution using the molecular replacement method, with the E. coli DinG–ssDNA complex (PDB ID: 6FWR) as the search model (Fig. 1a). FmaDinG adopts the canonical domain organization of XPD-family helicases, consisting of the FeS, Arch, MD1, and MD2 motor domains, together with an additional N-terminal 5′–3′ exo/endonuclease domain (Fig. 1a). Consistent with the conserved cysteine motifs, the FeS domain of FmaDinG exhibits extra electron density corresponding to an Fe–S cluster. However, the cluster appears partially disordered and is coordinated by only three cysteine residues (Fig. 1b). The fourth cysteine residue and the nearby region (residues 285–293) are disordered in the present structure.

Structural analysis shows that FmaDinG retains the conserved XPD-like helicase core, comprising the FeS, Arch, and two RecA-like motor domains (MD1 and MD2), while additionally incorporating an N-terminal nuclease module with predicted 5′–3′ exo/endonuclease activity (Fig. 1a). Electron density observed within the FeS domain supports the presence of an iron–sulfur cluster, consistent with the conserved cysteine motifs. Notably, the cluster is only partially ordered in the current model and is coordinated by three cysteine residues. The fourth cysteine, along with an adjacent segment (residues 285–293), is not resolved in the structure (Fig. 1b).

Despite the addition of DNA (12-nt-length poly (dT)) in a three-fold excess relative to the protein during crystallization, DNA strand was observed bound only in the motor domains in the structure. The electron density clearly shows the 11 nucleotides plus the phosphate group of the 12th nucleotide bound across MD1 and MD2 (Fig. 1c). Each motor domain adopts the characteristic RecA-like fold comprising seven β-strands surrounded by multiple α-helices. A molecule of ADPNP is bound at the interdomain cleft between MD1 and MD2 lobes (Fig. 1d). The key residues required for ATP binding and hydrolysis are conserved (Fig. S1). Notably, two residues in MD2 that are expected to coordinate ATP are positioned far from the ADPNP molecule, suggesting that the ATP-binding pocket adopts an open conformation in the present structure. Although MgCl2 was included in the crystallization solution, no Mg2+ ion was observed at either the nuclease or the ATPase active site (Fig. 1d), likely due to the chelating effect of citrate ions in the crystallization buffer, which can form poorly soluble complexes with Mg2+.

The endonuclease activity of FmaDinG

While no crystal structure containing DNA bound to the N-terminal nuclease domain (NTD) was obtained, an AlphaFold3-predicted model of the FmaDinG–splayed duplex DNA complex with a high pLDDT score provides structural support for understanding the potential DNA-processing mechanism. Superimposition of this model with the crystal structure based on the motor domains revealed substantial conformational rearrangements within the NTD upon DNA engagement (Fig. 2a). In particular, α1 (residues 20–25) undergoes a “helix-to-loop” transition that opens the gate previously blocking DNA threading.

The NTD of FmaDinG exhibits a fold similar to that of PD-(D/E)XK-family 5′–3′ exo/endonucleases, comprising six β-strands and five α-helices (Fig. 2b). Four conserved residues—H40 (α2), E54 (β2), D75 (β3), and E88 (β4)—form the putative catalytic center for metal ion coordination (Fig. 2c). A conserved lysine (K90) at the C-terminus of β4, analogous to the catalytic lysine of canonical PD-(D/E)XK nucleases, is positioned to coordinate the scissile phosphate [19]. Two additional arginine residues, R10 (α1) and R73 (β3), appear to participate in substrate binding (Fig. 2c).

Structural similarity searches using DALI identified several nucleases with available DNA-bound structures, including Cas4 from Geobacter sulfurreducens (PDB ID: 7MI4) [20], mouse Dna2 (PDB ID: 5EAN) [21], Bacillus subtilis AddA in complex with AddB (PDB ID: 4CEJ) [22], and human MGME1 (PDB ID: 8XA9) [23, 24]. These proteins share limited sequence similarity with FmaDinG (≤ 17% identity) but adopt closely related folds within their 5′–3′ exo/endonuclease domains. Comparative structural analysis reveals that residues involved in metal ion coordination and substrate interaction are largely conserved across these enzymes (Fig. 2b). Notable differences are observed in specific positions: Cas4 lacks the residue corresponding to E54 in FmaDinG that is predicted to participate in metal coordination, whereas Dna2 and AddA do not contain the equivalent of R10, which is implicated in substrate binding (Fig. 2b, c). In contrast, MGME1 retains all corresponding residues found in FmaDinG, suggesting a closer conservation of catalytic features.

Biochemical assays revealed that FmaDinG cleaves both 3′- and 5′-FAM-labeled 20-nt ssDNA into multiple products rather than single-nucleotide products. Moreover, the cleavage products generated from 5′- and 3′-blocked DNA substrates were consistent with those from unmodified substrates, indicating endonuclease rather than directional exonuclease activity (Fig. 2d). Among various divalent cations tested, Mn2+ supported the highest nuclease activity, followed by Co2+ and Zn2+, whereas Mg2+ yielded weak activity and Ni2+/Ca2+ were ineffective (Fig. 2e). Using only the NTD to eliminate helicase interference, substrate preference assays showed that FmaDinG preferentially cleaves dA > dC > dT > dG sequences (Fig. 2f). The optimal reaction conditions were determined to be 100 mM NaCl, pH 8.0, and 45–50 °C (Fig. S2). Given that DinG homologs in S. aureus and L. monocytogenes have been reported to participate in RNA metabolism [18, 25], we tested whether FmaDinG also exhibits RNase activity. However, parallel activity assays revealed that FmaDinG cleaves DNA with substantially higher efficiency than RNA, suggesting that it is primarily involved in DNA metabolism (Fig. 2g).

Mutational analysis of catalytic and substrate-coordinating residues (H40A, E54A, R73A, D75A, E88A, and K90A) nearly abolished nuclease activity, confirming their essential roles in catalysis (Fig. 2h).

The helicase activity of FmaDinG

In the motor region, FmaDinG binds ssDNA along a groove spanning the MD1 and MD2 domains (Figs. 1c and 3a), resembling E. coli DinG-ssDNA (PDB 6FWR) [2] and Pseudomonas aeruginosa CasDinG-ssDNA (PDB ID: 7XF1) [15] complexes. An aromatic residue on MD2, F698, intercalates between the second and third nucleotides at the 5′ end of the DNA, likely functioning as a ratchet to prevent backward slippage during ATP hydrolysis (Fig. 3a). Apart from two regions rich in aromatic and hydrophobic amino acids, the bases of ssDNA strand are almost stacked on one another. The first region corresponds to the P-motif of EcDinG, located between β5 and α5 of the MD2 domain. The base of the third nucleotide was flipped out and sandwiched by an aromatic and a hydrophobic residue (F575 and I679). A proline residue (P677) in the P-motif formed stacking interaction with the sugar ring of the fourth nucleotide. The second region comprise the C-terminus of α4 of the arch domain. The F478 residue in that region formed stacking interaction with the eleventh base of the DNA strand. Besides, two residues on that region (K482 and D475) formed hydrogen bonds with the side chain of the bases, either directly or mediated by water molecule. Previous studies suggested that the arch domain assists in duplex unwinding through an uncharacterized mechanism. This is the first instance that interaction of this domain with DNA has been noted. However, these interactions appear specific to EndoDinG, as residues are not conserved in other DinG-like subgroups. The rest of the DNA strand are mostly coordinated by polar amino acids. Like EcDinG, there are also two conserved arginine residues located in FeS domain join in DNA binding (Fig. 3a).

Real-time fluorescence-based unwinding assays were performed to assess the helicase activity of FmaDinG. Using D75A to avoid nucleolytic degradation of labeled strands, FmaDinG displayed robust 5′–3′ helicase activity across various DNA substrates, including 5′ overhang, splayed duplex, double-flap, bubble, and gapped duplex structures (Fig. 3b), consistent with the known requirement of a 5′ ssDNA tail for XPD-family helicases. Interestingly, FmaDinG showed significantly higher unwinding efficiency for the 5′ overhang substrate than for the splayed duplex. We propose that an excessively long 3′ tail may hinder proper engagement of the 5′ tail within the motor domains, thereby reducing helicase activity. Similar to its nuclease preference, Mn2+ also proved to be the optimal metal ion for helicase activity (Fig. 3c).

To explore the interplay between the nuclease and helicase activities, DNA digestion assays were performed in the presence of ATP. For both splayed duplex and 5′ overhang substrates, ATP addition enabled FmaDinG to digest further into the duplex region and produce shorter products (Fig. 3d). In contrast, the ATPase-dead mutant (D378A) failed to generate such products (Fig. 3e). Strikingly, in the absence of ATP, FmaDinG only removed a few nucleotides (probably ≤ 4-nt) from the 3′ free tails of splayed or 3′ overhang substrates (Fig. 3d), suggesting that spontaneous threading into the nuclease domain is limited, and extensive digestion requires ATP-dependent translocation initiated from the 5′ tail.

Alanine substitution of DNA-binding residues in MD1 (K249), FeS (K278/K280), MD2 (R578, F698, and R733), and the Arch (F478) domain resulted in decreased activity toward the 5′ overhang substrate (Fig. 3f), confirming their critical roles in DNA engagement and ATP-driven strand translocation during resection.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates the structural foundation and functional features of EndoDinG, a previously underexplored subgroup within the DinG-like helicase family. Unlike the reported CasDinG–HNH and ExoDinG proteins, EndoDinGs have acquired an N-terminal PD-(D/E)XK endonuclease domain while retaining the canonical FeS domain. As a representative EndoDinG, FmaDinG reveals that its N-terminal nuclease domain undergoes a “helix-to-loop” transition to open the gate for DNA entry, a conformational switch that appears essential for 3′-end access to the nuclease active site. In the presence of ATP, FmaDinG is capable of digesting deeply into duplex regions, whereas the ATPase-defective mutant completely loses this capability, demonstrating that extensive nuclease processing requires ATP-driven 5′-end translocation. This mode resembles the coordinated helicase–nuclease actions observed in ExoDinG, AddAB, and Dna2, but the directional and structural dependencies uncovered here appear unique to DinG-like proteins.

In addition, we found that the Arch domain of FmaDinG directly participates in DNA engagement—an interaction not previously reported in other DinG structures. This observation suggests that EndoDinGs may have evolved a distinct structural regulatory method for controlling DNA translocation. Taken together, our data support a model in which FmaDinG initiates cleavage of 3′-ended substrates via its N-terminal nuclease, and the MD1–MD2 motor domains subsequently drive 5′–3′ unwinding in an ATP-dependent manner, feeding DNA progressively into the nuclease center for continued processing into duplex regions (Fig. 4). Compared with the “3′–5′ exonuclease + 5′–3′ translocation” strategy used by ExoDinG [8], EndoDinG employs an “endonuclease + 5′–3′ translocation” mechanism and displays a distinctive Mn2+ preference and strong selectivity for dA/dC sequences. These characteristics may enable EndoDinGs to efficiently process specialized substrates under specific physiological conditions, thereby driving functional diversification within the DinG-like family.

The N-terminal nuclease domain initiates cleavage at 3′-ended substrates, while the MD1–MD2 motor domains drive ATP-dependent 5′–3′ unwinding, directing DNA into the nuclease center for continued duplex processing

In this study, we also found that disrupting the Fe-S cluster of FmaDinG (C289A/C294A double mutant) severely compromises its stability, resulting in inclusion body formation and failure of in vitro purification (Fig. S3). FeS clusters are well known for their roles in redox sensing and for regulating enzyme activity in DNA replication and repair pathways [26]. In the DinG-like family, mutations in the FeS domains of EcDinG or its paralog YoaA abolish their in vivo functions [9, 27]. Likewise, the FeS clusters of human DinG-like homologs—including XPD, FancJ, RTEL1, and ChlR1 are sensitive to reactive oxygen/nitrogen, and disruption of which lost the helicase activity defects or associated with severe diseases [6, 28]. Previous bioinformatic analyses indicated that the host organisms encoding EndoDinGs are predominantly strict or facultative anaerobes [7]. This distribution raises the intriguing possibility that the FeS cluster in EndoDinGs may serve as a sensor of oxidative conditions, modulating protein stability and enzymatic activity in response to environmental stress.

F. magna is an important human commensal organism with emerging pathogenic potential. Due to current limitations in genetic manipulation of this species, the in vivo function of FmaDinG remains unknown. Future work should focus on identifying its physiological substrates, exploring its interactions with other genome maintenance factors such as SSB, and determining whether FmaDinG contributes to genome stability, DNA repair, resolution of transcription–replication conflicts, or defense against mobile genetic elements. From a structural perspective, the mechanisms underlying NTD conformational rearrangement, ATP-driven DNA threading, and the contributions of the Arch domain during unwinding warrant deeper investigations.

Materials and methods

Cloning and site-directed mutagenesis

All primers and oligonucleotides used in this study were purchased from Generay Biotech (Shanghai, China) and are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

The full-length gene encoding FmaDinG was codon-optimized, synthesized by TSINGKE (Beijing, China), and cloned into a modified pET28a expression vector (pET28T) containing an N-terminal 6×His tag followed by a TEV protease cleavage site. The resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli DH5α (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai) for amplification, selected on LB plates containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin, and verified by DNA sequencing. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange™ Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocol [29]. All mutant constructs were verified by sequencing and subsequently transformed into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells (TransGen Biotech, Beijing) for protein expression.

Protein expression and purification

Expression and purification of tag-free FmaDinG and its mutants were carried out as described previously [29], with minor modifications. Cells were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C until reaching mid-log phase, followed by induction with isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 18 °C for 16 h. After induction, cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Cell disruption was performed by sonication, and the lysate was clarified by high-speed centrifugation. The supernatant was applied to HisTrap Excel column (Cytiva), and bound proteins were eluted using an imidazole gradient. The protein was further purified by a Heparin HP column (Cytiva). The affinity tag was removed by TEV protease cleavage, followed by a second HisTrap Excel column (Cytiva) to separate the tag-free protein from uncleaved sample. The protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography in a buffer appropriate for subsequent biochemical and structural studies.

Crystallization and structure determination

Crystallization trials were carried out using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method at 289 K. To prevent DNA degradation during crystallization, a nuclease-deficient mutant, FmaDinG (D75A), was employed. The freshly purified protein was concentrated to approximately 10 mg/mL and clarified by centrifugation to remove insoluble material prior to crystallization. For complex formation, the protein was incubated with ssDNA at a molar ratio of 1:3 in the presence of 10 mM ADPNP. Following extensive screening and optimization, crystals of the FmaDinG–ssDNA–ADPNP complex were obtained from a reservoir solution containing 0.2 M ammonium citrate dibasic, 20 mM MgCl2, and 20% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 3350.

Crystals were cryoprotected by sequential transfer into reservoir solutions containing 20% and 30% (w/v) glycerol and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at beamline BL10U2 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility and processed with the Aquarium pipeline [30, 31]. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using the E. coli DinG–ssDNA complex (PDB ID: 6FWR). Model building and refinement were conducted in PHENIX [32] and COOT [33]. All residues reside in favored or allowed regions of the Ramachandran plot. Figures were generated with PyMOL, and statistics are provided in Table 1.

AlphaFold3 prediction of the EndoDinG–splayed duplex complex

AlphaFold3 was used to model the complex of EndoDinG with a splayed duplex DNA (formed by annealing the sequences 5′-TTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTT-3′ and 5′-AAAAAAAACCC-3′) and two Mg²⁺ ions. Default parameters were applied, and five seeds were used per run. The full-length EndoDinG sequence was used without any template bias. Model confidence was assessed using the predicted aligned error (PAE) and the local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores.

Nuclease activity assays

Nuclease activity assays were performed using a protocol adapted from a previously reported method [34]. All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Generay (Shanghai, China) and labeled with 6-carboxyfluorescein (6-FAM) at either the 3′ or 5′ terminus (sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1).

Standard reactions (10 µL) contained 100 nM DNA substrate, varying concentrations of FmaDinG (wild-type or mutant), and reaction buffer A composed of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 1 mM DTT, 3% (v/v) glycerol, and 10 mM MnCl2. Reaction mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and terminated by the addition of an equal volume of 2× stop buffer (10 mM EDTA, 98% formamide). Samples were subsequently heated at 100 °C for 10 min and rapidly cooled on ice. Reaction products were separated on 15% denaturing polyacrylamide gels containing 7 M urea and visualized by fluorescence imaging (FAM channel) using a ChemiScope 6100 system (Clinx Science Instruments, Shanghai).

To evaluate metal ion preference, MnCl2 in buffer A was substituted with 10 mM MgCl2, CaCl2, ZnCl2, NiCl2, CoCl2, or EDTA. Substrate sequence preference was assessed using 100 nM DNA substrates consisting of 14-nt poly dA, dT, dC, or dG, incubated with increasing concentrations of FmaDinG (0–400 nM) in the presence of 10 mM Mn²⁺ at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by analysis using denaturing PAGE.

For salt-dependence assays, reactions contained 100 nM 20-nt 5′ FAM-labeled poly dA and 100 nM FmaDinG in buffer A with NaCl concentrations ranging from 0 to 500 mM. To determine the optimal pH, reactions were performed in buffer A adjusted to pH values between 6.2 and 8.8. Buffers at pH 6.2–6.6 were prepared using 50 mM Bis-Tris, whereas pH 6.8–8.8 conditions were prepared using 50 mM Tris, with pH adjusted by HCl.

Temperature dependence was examined by conducting reactions at temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 70 °C. All assays were performed in three independent replicates. Digestion efficiencies were quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA) and plotted as line or bar graphs with GraphPad Prism 9 (San Diego, USA).

Helicase activity assays

Helicase activity was monitored in real time by measuring fluorescence changes using a previously described approach [8]. Six types of DNA substrates were prepared for these assays, including splayed duplex (Jb/Ja2), 3′ overhang (Jb/Ja), 5′ overhang (Jb2/Ja2), double flap (Jb/Ja2/Jc3), Holliday junction (Jb/Ja2/Jc3/Jd), and fully paired duplex (Jb/anti-Jb) (Table S1). Each substrate contained a duplex region in which one strand was labeled with a fluorophore (FAM) and the complementary strand carried a quencher (Dabsyl) at the corresponding terminus. Strand separation resulted in increased fluorescence intensity, allowing helicase activity to be quantitatively tracked.

Standard reactions (20 µL) included 25 nM DNA substrate and 500 nM FmaDinG (wild-type or mutant) in helicase buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 100 µg/mL BSA, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 5% glycerol. Reactions were initiated by the addition of ATP to a final concentration of 1 mM and monitored at 37 °C for 25 min using a SpectraMax M3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA) in fluorescence detection mode (FAM channel). To assess metal ion dependence, MgCl2 in the reaction buffer was substituted with MnCl2, CaCl2, ZnCl2, NiCl2, or CoCl2. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and fluorescence data were processed and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.
