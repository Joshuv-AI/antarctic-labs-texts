A survival prediction model for biliary tract cancer based on least absolute shrinkage and selection operator–cox regression
Authors: Shanshan Fan, Kexin Zhao, Ziwei Liang, Yang Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiliary tract cancer (BTC) is a digestive tract tumor with low incidence, high malignancy, and short survival times. Abnormal lipid metabolism may be related to the occurrence and development of tumors; therefore, we constructed a survival prediction model for patients with BTC using clinical data that included lipid indicators rarely considered in previous studies.Patients and MethodsClinical and pathological data were collected from 124 patients with BTC. Patients were divided into two groups according to the inclusion time. The training and validation cohorts included 70 patients from 2017 to 2021 and 54 patients from 2022 to 2023. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator–Cox regression analysis was conducted on the survival data. The resulting prediction model was evaluated using discrimination and calibration analyses performed in R Studio.ResultsTumor location, lipoprotein (a), carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and therapy type were identified as key predictors for constructing the nomogram. The consistency indexes for the training and validation cohorts were 0.677 and 0.655, respectively, indicating moderate discrimination. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test provided a p-value of 0.188 for the validation cohort, suggesting a good model fit. The calibration accuracy of the model in the two cohorts was further evaluated by drawing calibration curves based on the follow-up time. Patients were classified into high- and low-risk groups according to the nomogram risk scores. Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed significant differences between the training cohort (p = 0.00041) and the validation cohort (p = 0.0028). The risk score scatter plot provided visual verification of the model’s performance.ConclusionsThe predictive model constructed in this retrospective study shows potential for guiding the clinical identification of groups at high risk of BTC, adjusting treatment intensity, and improving follow-up management.Keywords: Biliary tract cancer, predictive model, survival, nomogram, prognosis

Introduction

Biliary tract cancer (BTC) is characterized by a poor prognosis [1], with a 5-year survival rate of less than 20% across all stages of the disease [2]. BTC is anatomically classified into intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and gallbladder cancer (GC) [3].

Cancer cells are heavily dependent on lipids, which serve as essential components of cell membranes and signaling molecules, and are vital for driving and sustaining energy-demanding tumorigenesis [4]. Notably, the liver plays an important role in lipid metabolism [5]; therefore, the link between abnormal lipid metabolism and the occurrence and development of tumors has received substantial research attention. Owing to the poor prognosis associated with BTC, identifying high-risk patients, implementing targeted adjustments to treatment plans, and optimizing patient follow-up management are critical. However, existing predictive models of patient survival often fail to incorporate key lipid indicators.

Therefore, the purpose of this retrospective study was to construct a survival prediction model for BTC by screening patient variables based on routine clinical data, including patients’ previous medical history of metabolic diseases, lipid metabolic-related indicators, pathological characteristics, tumor markers, and therapy type, using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)–Cox regression. The prediction model is designed to assist in the clinical screening of high-risk patients and improve survival prognosis assessments for patients with BTC.

Patients and methods

Participants and data collection

This study included 124 patients who were pathologically diagnosed with BTC at the Beijing Chao-yang Hospital from 2017 to 2023. The inclusion criteria included not having received any anti-tumor treatment, a pathological report of adenocarcinoma, and the presence of complete clinical, pathological, and follow-up data. Patients with pathological types other than adenocarcinoma and those lost to follow-up were excluded from the study. Survival follow-up was conducted via telephone or during outpatient visits, with the final follow-up in July 2024. The data for this study were obtained from the hospital information system of Beijing Chao-yang Hospital. All patients included in the study were divided into training and validation cohorts based on the period of their inclusion. The 70 patients included from 2017 to 2021 were designated as the training cohort, and the 54 patients included from 2022 to 2023 were set as the validation cohort (Figure 1). We constructed the model using training cohort data and verified its reproducibility using validation cohort data. A total of 16 variables were analyzed in the study, including general patient information, personal history, pathological characteristics, biochemical lipid indicators, tumor markers, and therapy type.

Flow chart of the study procedure. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 124 patients were included in this study. Patients were then assigned to training or validation cohorts according to their inclusion period (70 patients in 2017–2021 and 54 patients in 2022–2023, respectively).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chao-yang Hospital, Capital Medical University, and performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki (6th revision, 2008) (Ethical Approval Number: 2023-Science-514). As reflected in a priori approval by the institution’s human research committee, the need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Blood collection and laboratory analysis

Blood samples (3 mL) were collected after an overnight fast using red-top vacuum tubes within a week before diagnosis or surgery. These samples were used to assess biochemical lipid profiles and tumor markers. Serum total cholesterol and triglyceride levels were measured via enzymatic assays. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were quantified using a direct homogeneous assay. Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] concentration was determined using an immunoturbidimetric assay. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19-9 levels were analyzed using direct chemiluminescent immunoassays.

Nomogram construction and model validation

LASSO–Cox regression, which combines LASSO and Cox regression, is particularly suitable for dealing with small sample datasets. This method effectively performs feature selection and model sparsification by introducing L1 regularization (i.e. the LASSO penalty term) under conditions of small sample sizes, thereby avoiding overfitting and enhancing the model’s generalization ability [6].

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). First, we loaded and ran the ‘glmnet’ and ‘survival’ packages and then used the glmnet function in the ‘glmnet’ package for cross-validation, with the parameters set to alpha = 1, set nfolds = 5, and optimal lambda value = lambda.min to fit the LASSO–Cox model. We then loaded and ran the ‘rms’ package and used the nomogram function to construct the nomogram. The coxph function was used to calculate the consistency index (C-index) to determine the discriminative ability of the model. C-index values of <0.6, 0.6–0.75, and >0.75 indicate low, medium, and high model discrimination, respectively [7].

The Hoslem.test function in the ‘ResourceSelection’ package was then used to conduct the Hosmer–Lemeshow (H–L) test on the validation cohort to determine the goodness-of-fit of the prediction model. p > 0.05 indicates that the predicted values are consistent with the observed values, implying a good fit and a relatively high degree of calibration [8]. The calibrate function in the ‘rms’ package was used to draw calibration curves (bootstrap = 1000) to further determine model calibration accuracy.

Moreover, risk scores obtained from the nomogram were used to categorize patients into high- and low-risk groups according to the median survival time. The ‘survival’ and ‘survminer’ packages were used to estimate the survival function using the Kaplan–Meier (K–M) method, and K–M survival curves were plotted for each group. Finally, the aforementioned risk scores were used to draw risk scatter plots for each group using the ggrisk function in the ‘ggrisk’ package.

Statistical analysis

R Studio (version 4.4.0) software was used for all statistical analyses. Categorical variables were presented as percentages (%), whereas continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The chi-square test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups. Statistical significance was set to p < 0.05.

After constructing the prediction model, we evaluated model performance. First, the C-indexes of the training and validation cohorts were 0.677 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.587–0.767) and 0.655 (95%CI: 0.557–0.753), respectively, indicating moderate discriminative ability. Second, the H–L test and calibration curves were used to verify the calibration accuracy of the model. The H–L test revealed P values of 0.872 and 0.188 for the training and validation cohorts, respectively (p > 0.05). The median follow-up time of the training cohort was 58 months; therefore, calibration curves were plotted for 1 year and 3 years (Figure 3A and B). The median follow-up time of the validation cohort was 17 months; therefore, calibration curves were plotted for 0.5 and 1.5 years (Figure 3C and D). Based on the risk scores derived from the nomogram, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups, and K–M survival curves were plotted (Figure 4A and B). The K–M survival curves showed significant differences between the training (p = 0.00041) and validation (p = 0.0028) cohorts. Finally, the risk scores were ranked from small to large according to the nomogram, and scatter plots were generated for the training and validation cohorts, illustrating the risk scores (Figure 4C and D) and survival times of patients. Patients with higher mortality rates were more likely to be distributed in the high-risk group and have shorter predicted survival times.

Discussion

Recent research has consistently shown an association between abnormal lipid metabolism and the occurrence and development of tumors [9,10]. Therefore, in this study, we utilized clinical data, including lipid indicators rarely included in previous studies, to construct a survival prediction model for patients with BTC.

These clinical data were incorporated into LASSO analysis to identify five key variables: tumor location, Lp(a), CEA, CA19-9, and therapy type, which were then incorporated into Cox regression analysis. CA19-9 represents the only independent risk factor associated with overall survival. However, this study did not aim to identify independent risk factors; instead, it used LASSO to select variables related to prognosis to construct a predictive model. The quality of a model should be evaluated from two aspects: discrimination and calibration [7]. After external validation, the validation cohort demonstrated excellent discrimination and calibration.

We used data from the training and validation cohorts to construct and validate the prediction model, respectively. The calculated C-indexes suggested that the model had moderate discriminative ability. According to the visual verification results, which included calibration curves for both cohorts based on follow-up time and survival curves, as well as risk scatter plots for high- and low-risk groups based on nomogram-derived risk scores, the prediction model showed good generalization performance. Model efficacy was similarly observed in the validation cohort.

Regarding the impact of tumor location, our results showed that GC has a better prognosis than cholangiocarcinoma, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [11,12]. This may be because the cause of GC is related to chronic inflammation caused by cystic stones, and the symptoms caused by gallbladder inflammation are conducive to early detection of the disease and surgical removal [13], thus improving the prognosis of patients with GC. Conversely, the early symptoms of cholangiocarcinoma are atypical, leading to delays in early diagnosis. By the time jaundice caused by biliary obstruction is observed, the patient is often already in an advanced stage and cannot undergo surgical resection; therefore, the prognosis is poor [14].

The link between abnormal lipid metabolism and tumor occurrence and development is a highly studied and rapidly evolving area in cancer research [9]. Animal studies have demonstrated that high-fat diets can activate the lipid-ligand transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR)-δ. It then acts coordinately with other PPAR isoforms, primarily PPAR-α (predominantly expressed in the liver) and PPAR-γ (predominantly expressed in adipose tissue), to enhance fatty acid oxidation, thereby promoting tumor initiation [15]. Furthermore, lipids serve as intracellular and intercellular signaling molecules, acting as autocrine or paracrine mediators to regulate a wide range of cellular processes. These processes, including proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis, contribute to tumor development and metastasis [16]. Lp(a) is a special lipoprotein synthesized in the liver and predominantly determined by genetics. Currently, no effective drugs exist to control Lp(a) levels [17]. Moreover, Lp(a) is considered an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease [18]. Studies have shown that Lp(a) levels in patients with stage II–IV primary lung cancer are typically higher than those in patients with stage I lung cancer and healthy people [19]. However, the mechanism by which Lp(a) contributes to tumor occurrence and development remains unclear.

As is well known, serum tumor markers are related to the treatment and prognosis of malignant tumors. Moreover, CEA and CA19-9 have previously been associated with adverse outcomes in patients with BTC [20,21], as confirmed by the results of this study.

Regarding the impact of therapy type, most of the patients enrolled in this study underwent surgery, with a small portion receiving only local treatment (radiofrequency ablation and transarterial chemoembolization). According to the nomogram, surgery combined with chemotherapy can significantly improve prognosis, with surgery alone achieving the next best outcomes. This finding is consistent with existing results regarding the therapeutic efficacy of BTC [2].

The training (2017–2021) and validation cohort (2022–2023) comprised patients with BTC from distinct time periods. Due to continuous refinements in treatment approaches, the comparison is inevitably subject to confounding influences. Consequently, minor differences were observed in the C-indexes and H-L test P values between the two cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small. Owing to the low incidence of BTC and the inherent constraints of our patients inclusion criteria, further multicenter studies should be conducted to expand the sample size and verify the findings of this study. Second, the retrospective nature of this study can introduce inherent bias; therefore, a prospective study should be conducted in the future to verify the efficacy of our survival prediction model.

Conclusions

In this study, we applied the LASSO–Cox regression method to train and validate a new model for predicting the survival of patients with BTC. The results of this study indicate that the model has clinical significance for effectively identifying high-risk patients with BTC. Therefore, this research can contribute to guiding clinical decisions related to adjusting treatment intensity and strengthening follow-up management for patients with BTC.
