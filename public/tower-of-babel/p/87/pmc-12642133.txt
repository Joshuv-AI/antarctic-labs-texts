Recent advances in hydrogel-based delivery systems for the treatment of pancreatic cancer
Authors: Haibo Wang, Hongyuan Yu, Xue Bai, Xiaobo Wang, Hua Shao, Jun Liao, Xing Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a five-year survival rate below 10 %, largely due to late diagnosis, aggressive progression, and intrinsic resistance to conventional therapies. The dense desmoplastic stroma, hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment (TME), and profound immunosuppression severely limit drug penetration and efficacy. Hydrogels, as three-dimensional cross-linked polymer networks with high water content, biocompatibility, and tunable physicochemical properties, have emerged as versatile platforms for localized and sustained drug delivery in PDAC. Tailored designs enable hydrogels to respond to TME stimuli, achieving on-demand release and minimizing systemic toxicity. Recent advances demonstrate their utility in delivering chemotherapeutics, immunomodulators, photosensitizers, and gene cargos, as well as serving as scaffolds for tissue engineering and cell therapy. These multifunctional systems enhance intertumoral drug accumulation, remodel the TME, and synergize multimodal therapies to improve antitumor efficacy. Preclinical studies highlight significant tumor suppression and reduced recurrence with favorable safety profiles. However, challenges remain in long-term biosafety, scalable manufacturing, and precise in vivo delivery. This review summarizes current strategies, key advances, and translational challenges of hydrogel-based delivery systems for PDAC, offering insights into their potential to overcome therapeutic bottlenecks and advance toward clinical application.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, particularly PDAC, stands as one of the most aggressive and lethal malignancies worldwide, posing a severe threat to global health [[1], [2], [3]]. Its grim 5-year overall survival rate, consistently below 10 %, is primarily attributed to its insidious early clinical manifestations and the extreme diagnostic challenges, including non-specific symptoms that are easily overlooked and the pancreas's deep anatomical location, which hinders early screening and definitive diagnosis [[4], [5], [6]]. Consequently, the majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, missing the opportunity for curative surgical resection currently the only potential path to long-term survival. Although recent advances have been made in conventional treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and emerging modalities like targeted therapy and immunotherapy, their efficacy remains profoundly limited [[7], [8], [9]]. This is largely due to PDAC's unique TME, characterized by extensive desmoplasia, pronounced hypoxia, poor vascularization, and significant immunosuppression, compounded by high molecular heterogeneity and intrinsic drug resistance. Existing treatment regimens often yield suboptimal outcomes, are associated with severe adverse effects, and frequently lead to rapid tumor recurrence and progression [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop more effective, precise, and less toxic innovative therapeutic strategies to overcome the formidable challenges in pancreatic cancer treatment.

Against this challenging therapeutic landscape, hydrogels, as cutting-edge biomaterials, have emerged with vast application prospects in pancreatic cancer treatment, owing to their unique physicochemical properties and exceptional biological functionalities [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Hydrogels are three-dimensional (3D) cross-linked networks formed by hydrophilic polymers, distinguished by their high-water content, excellent biocompatibility, softness, and injectability [[20], [21], [22]]. These attributes make them ideal carriers for efficient encapsulation and delivery of various therapeutic agents or cellular components [23]. More importantly, precise material design enables hydrogels to achieve localized and controlled release, ensuring sustained therapeutic concentrations at the tumor site while circumventing systemic toxicities. Furthermore, intelligent responsive hydrogels can be engineered to react to specific TME stimuli, such as pH, temperature, enzyme activity, or redox status, facilitating “on-demand” and precise drug targeting [[24], [25], [26]]. This capability holds promise for overcoming the drug penetration and efficacy limitations imposed by PDAC's dense desmoplastic stroma and immunosuppressive milieu. In recent years, hydrogel-based multifunctional therapeutic systems have demonstrated preliminary efficacy in animal models of pancreatic cancer, showing potential in enhancing chemosensitivity, remodeling the immune microenvironment, and inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis, thereby offering novel avenues to circumvent the current therapeutic impasse [[27], [28], [29]].

This review aims to provide a systematic and comprehensive overview of the research progress and application strategies of hydrogels in pancreatic cancer therapy. We will first outline the pathogenesis of pancreatic cancer, its unique tumor microenvironment characteristics, and the limitations confronting current conventional treatments, thus elucidating the imperative for developing novel therapeutic modalities. Subsequently, this review will focus on the multidimensional applications of hydrogels in pancreatic cancer treatment, encompassing their roles as sustained-release carriers for chemotherapeutics, synergistic platforms for gene and immunotherapy, and their innovative uses in preventing postoperative recurrence and modeling disease. Finally, we will deeply analyze the key challenges and potential strategies for the clinical translation of hydrogels, alongside their future developmental directions. It is hoped that this review will provide a theoretical foundation and future guidance for in-depth research and broader application of hydrogels in pancreatic cancer therapy, ultimately accelerating the clinical transition of this cutting-edge technology to benefit a wider population of pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of a hydrogel-based drug delivery system synergizing multimodal therapies such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy and phototherapy for the treatment of PDAC.Fig. 1

Pancreatic cancer overview and molecular mechanisms

Epidemiology and pathophysiology

Pancreatic cancer is among the most aggressive and fatal malignancies worldwide, with both incidence and mortality continuing to rise [[30], [31], [32]]. According to recent WHO statistics, it ranks twelfth in global cancer incidence but sixth in mortality, reflecting its exceptionally high lethality. In 2021, approximately 508,000 new cases and 505,000 deaths were reported globally, underscoring its nearly one-to-one incidence-to-mortality ratio [[33], [34], [35]]. The age-standardized incidence rate is about 5.9 per 100,000 and shows a steady upward trend. Marked geographic variation exists: high-income regions such as North America and Europe exhibit incidence rate several times greater than those in low-HDI countries [[36], [37], [38]]. In the United States, pancreatic cancer accounts for ∼3 % of all cancers but contributes to nearly 9 % of cancer-related deaths, with cases projected to exceed 67,000 by 2025 [39]. The disease primarily affects older adults [40,41]. PDAC constitutes over 85 % of cases and is characterized by extreme malignancy and poor prognosis, with a global 5-year survival rate below 10 % [42]. In the U.S., the overall 5-year survival is 13 %, decreasing sharply with disease stage—from 44 % in localized to only 3 % in metastatic cases [43]. Late diagnosis remains the key obstacle, as symptoms are often nonspecific and reliable early biomarkers are lacking. Consequently, only 15–20 % of patients are eligible for curative resection, and even then, recurrence rates approach 80 % [[44], [45], [46]]. The pathophysiological characteristics of pancreatic cancer are equally complex and unique, primarily manifested by high tumor heterogeneity, rapid progression, and a distinctive TME [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. PDAC tumor tissue typically exhibits severe desmoplasia, predominantly mediated by activated pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), which form a dense fibrotic stroma. This significantly increases interstitial tumor pressure and restricts the penetration of therapeutic agents and immune cells. Furthermore, the pancreatic cancer microenvironment is often characterized by pronounced hypoxia and an acidic extracellular environment [51,52]. In addition, hypoxia and acidosis within the TME promote invasiveness, resistance, and immune evasion. These pathological and biological features collectively explain the poor clinical outcomes and underscore the urgent need for improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Pancreatic cancer pathogenesis and molecular markers

The initiation and progression of pancreatic cancer constitute a highly intricate process involving multiple steps, diverse molecular events, and cumulative alterations in key signaling pathways [53]. Pancreatic cancer, predominantly PDAC, typically originates from precancerous lesions of pancreatic ductal epithelial cells, specifically pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias [54]. These lesions progress into invasive carcinoma through the gradual accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations.

Among numerous molecular events, activating mutations in the KRAS gene are considered one of the earliest and most pervasive genetic events in PDAC, with a mutation rate reaching up to 90 % in PDAC cases [[55], [56], [57]]. It drives cell proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion, thereby serving as a crucial initiating mutation. TP53 mutations, present in about half to three-quarters of patients, result in the loss of tumor-suppressive functions and gain of oncogenic properties that promote metastasis and therapeutic resistance [[58], [59], [60]]. These TP53 mutations typically lead to the stable expression of mutant p53 protein, which subsequently loses its original tumor-suppressive functions and often acquires novel oncogenic properties that actively promote tumor metastasis and cell survival, thereby further accelerating disease progression [61,62]. Furthermore, mutations or deletions in the SMAD4 gene also play a crucial role in PDAC initiation and progression. As a core component of the Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway, SMAD4 mutation or loss leads to the inability of cells to effectively respond to growth inhibitory signals, consequently promoting tumor cell invasion, metastasis, and the development of therapeutic resistance [63,64]. Mutations or deletions in the CDKN2A gene are likewise common genetic events in PDAC, strongly associated with adverse patient prognosis.

Recent advancements in molecular biology and genomics have elucidated the complex molecular heterogeneity of pancreatic cancer, driving the accelerated development of diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive molecular biomarkers [[65], [66], [67]]. Non-invasive methods such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and liquid biopsies show promise for early detection and real-time disease monitoring. Activation of pathways such as STAT3 under hypoxic and inflammatory conditions, along with expression profiles like “STRESS,” are associated with tumor progression and therapeutic response [[68], [69], [70]]. Collectively, these molecular insights not only deepen understanding of PDAC pathogenesis but also provide potential therapeutic targets. Hydrogel-based systems, by enabling localized and controlled drug delivery toward specific molecular pathways or tumor biomarkers, hold promise to overcome conventional treatment limitations and improve therapeutic precision in pancreatic cancer [71].

Microenvironment characteristics

The distinctive TME of pancreatic cancer is a primary contributor to its inherent resistance to conventional therapies, while simultaneously providing crucial insights and opportunities for the rational design of novel treatment strategies [72]. The TME of PDAC is exceptionally complex, composed of malignant cancer cells, diverse stromal cells, various immune cells, and a dense, intricate extracellular matrix [73]. The most prominent feature of the PDAC microenvironment is its extensive desmoplasia. This fibrosis manifests as a highly dense network of collagen fibers and other extracellular matrix components, predominantly mediated by activated PSCs [74]. This fibrotic stroma markedly increases interstitial pressure, impairs vascular perfusion, and limits the penetration of chemotherapeutic and immune agents, leading to profound drug resistance [[75], [76], [77]]. Another defining feature is immune suppression, mediated by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which inhibit antigen presentation and effector T-cell activity, resulting in an “immune-cold” phenotype with poor response to immunotherapy [78]. Hypoxia is also a core and pervasive characteristic of the PDAC tumor microenvironment [79]. The resulting low oxygen tension and acidic milieu promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), genomic instability, and reduced drug bioavailability, further reinforcing therapeutic resistance [[80], [81], [82], [83]]. Pancreatic cancer exhibits high molecular and cellular diversity at both levels [84]. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis reveals that multiple genetically distinct tumor clones can co-exist within a single pancreatic tumor, with each clone exhibiting different transcriptional states and biological characteristics. Notably, up to 63 % of somatic mutations may display heterogeneous distribution within a single tumor. This high degree of heterogeneity dramatically increases the risk of treatment failure and drives the rapid emergence of drug resistance by providing a reservoir of adaptive clones [85].

In summary, the intricate microenvironmental characteristics of PDAC not only constitute formidable physical and biological barriers to conventional therapies but also provide specific design targets and opportunities for emerging treatment strategies. For instance, smart hydrogel drug delivery systems responsive to TME features hold significant promise to overcome drug penetration limitations and enhance overall therapeutic efficacy. Concurrently, hydrogel platforms loaded with immunomodulators may effectively reverse the immunosuppressive state of the tumor microenvironment, thereby fundamentally improving the therapeutic outlook for pancreatic cancer.

Traditional therapeutic strategies for pancreatic cancer

Despite aggressive therapeutic interventions, traditional pancreatic cancer treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, typically yield limited efficacy and are frequently accompanied by significant side effects [[86], [87], [88]]. These limitations are further exacerbated by the disease's unique biological characteristics. Surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment for pancreatic cancer. However, as previously noted, only 15 %–20 % of cases are amenable to surgical resection at the time of diagnosis. Even after curative pancreatoduodenectomy, the tumor recurrence rate is extraordinarily high, with up to 80 % of patients experiencing disease recurrence. A study focusing on long-term survivors surviving at least 60 months post-surgery indicated that 29.4 % of these patients still experienced tumor recurrence, with a median time to recurrence of 49 months [[89], [90], [91]]. These recurrences are frequently asymptomatic and can manifest as local recurrence or distant metastasis. While chemotherapy offers some survival benefits, its overall improvement is limited, and it is associated with significant systemic toxicity [[92], [93], [94]]. Resistance mechanisms are diverse and complex, encompassing genetic and epigenetic modifications, altered drug metabolism and efflux, and dynamic changes within the tumor microenvironment [95]. Specific examples include incomplete gemcitabine uptake due to issues with the hENT1 transporter, increased inactivation by cytidine deaminase, and the action of efflux pumps such as the ABC transporter family. Radiotherapy is also part of the therapeutic paradigm, but its efficacy is constrained by the pancreas's anatomical location and its proximity to radiosensitive gastrointestinal organs [[96], [97], [98]]. Historically, gastrointestinal toxicity has been a primary limiting factor for dose escalation in pancreatic cancer radiotherapy [[99], [100], [101]]. More severe but less common side effects include bleeding and ulceration, while perforation is exceedingly rare (<1 %). The high toxicity associated with radiotherapy further restricts its application, particularly when combined with other therapeutic modalities [[102], [103], [104], [105], [106]].

Despite the revolutionary advancements of immunotherapy in treating various cancers, characterized by high efficacy and tolerable toxicity profiles, PDAC is generally considered relatively insensitive to immunotherapeutic approaches [[107], [108], [109]]. PDAC's average 5-year survival rate is below 10 %, and it exhibits “significant resistance” to existing treatment modalities such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. This limited response is primarily attributed to PDAC being an “immunologically cold” tumor, lacking an abundance of mutations that would render tumor cells recognizable by the immune system. This deficiency results in insufficient cancer antigen presentation, weak or absent immune responses, and inadequate T-cell infiltration [[110], [111], [112], [113]]. This paradox indicates that simply extrapolating successful immunotherapeutic strategies from other cancers to PDAC is insufficient, as pancreatic cancer's unique TME poses profound barriers to immune cell infiltration and activation (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic representation of traditional strategies for the treatment of PDAC and their limitations.Fig. 2

A major impediment to effective treatment is the aberrant TME of PDAC. This TME is characterized by extensive desmoplasia, forming a dense fibrotic tumor stroma. This desmoplastic reaction generates high interstitial tumor pressure, compromises vascularization, and creates a physical barrier that impedes drug delivery and induces chemoresistance [[114], [115], [116]]. The TME also exhibits poor vascularization, leading to significant tumor hypoxia [[117], [118], [119]]. The cellular composition of PDAC tumors also reveals its complexity: approximately 35 % are cancer cells, 26 % are fibroblasts and PSCs, and 12 % are ECs. More importantly, extreme cellular heterogeneity both among patients (inter-tumoral) and within individual tumors (intra-tumoral) significantly increases the likelihood of treatment failure in clinical trials. Single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed the co-existence of multiple genetically distinct tumor clones within a single tumor, exhibiting diverse transcriptional states and subtype characteristics [[120], [121], [122], [123], [124]]. This complex biological landscape renders “one-size-fits-all” therapeutic approaches largely ineffective, urgently necessitating personalized treatment strategies capable of adapting to or exploiting this inherent heterogeneity.

Hydrogels in pancreatic cancer therapy

The profound limitations of traditional therapies and the complex biological barriers presented by pancreatic cancer underscore the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies [[125], [126], [127]]. Hydrogels, with their unique physicochemical properties and versatility, are rapidly emerging as a promising platform to overcome these challenges and achieve more effective, targeted pancreatic cancer therapy [[128], [129], [130], [131]].

Hydrogels are soft, three-dimensional network materials composed of numerous hydrophilic monomers, capable of absorbing and retaining significant amounts of water and biological fluids [[132], [133], [134]]. Hydrogels can be engineered to respond to specific triggers within the TME, such as pH, temperature, or enzymes, thereby enabling targeted drug delivery and reducing systemic toxicity [[135], [136], [137]]. Injectable hydrogels offer a minimally invasive approach for in situ chemotherapeutic drug delivery, representing a novel cancer therapeutic method by increasing drug concentrations at the tumor site while minimizing systemic side effects [[138], [139], [140]]. Hydrogel nanocomposites can further improve drug penetration and retention time within tumors through a combination of active transcytosis and passive diffusion. These properties directly counteract the formidable TME barriers and address the systemic toxicity issues associated with traditional therapies, offering novel possibilities for pancreatic cancer treatment [141]. Hydrogels are being extensively explored for various advanced therapeutic modalities, including small molecule drug therapy, immunotherapy, and gene therapy [142]. They are capable of encapsulating and controlling the release of immunomodulatory molecules, immune cells, and environmental modulators, thereby enhancing the overall response rate of immunotherapy, which is particularly crucial for “cold” PDAC tumors [[143], [144], [145]]. The development of nanohydrogels and other functionalized materials represents an emerging trend aimed at improving the loading and release efficiency of targeted drugs, thereby addressing the challenges of drug penetration and distribution within the dense PDAC stroma. The integration of nanotechnology with hydrogels provides novel approaches for drug delivery systems by improving drug targeting and release efficiency. This integrated approach, combining the unique properties of hydrogels with the specific challenges of PDAC, offers a powerful avenue for future therapies [146]. Hydrogels, acting as “smart” solutions, can respond to specific signals within the TME and enhance drug penetration into tumors, affording them distinct advantages in overcoming PDAC's physical and biological barriers [[147], [148], [149]]. Furthermore, hydrogels' capacity for delivering immunomodulators positions them as a key strategy for converting “immunologically cold” tumors into “immunologically hot” tumors, thereby amplifying the efficacy of immunotherapy in PDAC. This versatile platform is poised to significantly improve pancreatic cancer treatment outcomes by enabling targeted and localized delivery, coupled with precise modulation of the tumor microenvironment [150].

Classification and preparation of hydrogels

Hydrogels are typically classified based on their compositional origin and their responsiveness to external stimuli [151]. From a compositional perspective, hydrogels can be broadly categorized into natural polymer hydrogels and synthetic polymer hydrogels. Natural polymer hydrogels, such as hyaluronic acid, alginate, chitosan, collagen, and gelatin, are derived from biological sources and typically exhibit excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low immunogenicity. Some natural polymers also possess intrinsic bioactivity, which supports cell growth and function maintenance [152]. However, they may present limitations regarding mechanical properties and batch-to-batch consistency. Synthetic polymer hydrogels, including polyethylene glyco, polyacrylamide, and polyvinyl alcohol, are composed of artificially synthesized polymers [153]. Their primary advantage lies in the precise tunability of their physicochemical properties through controlled polymer structure and polymerization conditions, offering enhanced reproducibility and functional versatility. To reconcile the biological advantages of natural materials with the tunability of synthetic materials, hybrid natural-synthetic hydrogels have emerged, combining the strengths of both [154].

In addition to their compositional classification, the intrinsic physicochemical properties of hydrogels play a decisive role in pancreatic cancer therapy. Key features such as porosity, stiffness, degradability, and responsiveness to stimuli directly influence drug loading capacity, diffusion, and release kinetics. For example, collagen-based hydrogels with defined pore structures have been shown to modulate drug penetration and retention in tumor-like matrices. Similarly, synthetic polymer hydrogels offer highly tunable degradation rates, enabling precise control over the duration of therapeutic exposure. Stimuli-responsive hydrogels, in particular, are designed to undergo structural transitions in the acidic and hypoxic PDAC microenvironment, achieving on-demand release with minimal systemic toxicity. Based on their responsiveness to external stimuli, hydrogels can be classified as non-stimuli-responsive and stimuli-responsive (“smart”) hydrogels [156,157]. For pancreatic cancer therapy, where the TME is typically characterized by acidity, hypoxia, high enzymatic activity, and distinct temperature gradients, smart hydrogels can be engineered to swell, degrade, or release drugs selectively under these pathological conditions. This enables on-demand drug release and precise spatial targeting, markedly improving therapeutic specificity while minimizing off-target toxicity [[158], [159], [160]]. Hydrogel preparation methods are diverse, encompassing radical polymerization, ionic crosslinking, chemical crosslinking, physical crosslinking, enzyme-catalyzed crosslinking, and advanced click chemistry techniques. By precisely controlling preparation parameters such as monomer and crosslinker concentrations, initiator type, reaction temperature, pH value, and polymerization time, researchers can finely tune the hydrogel's pore structure, swelling capacity, mechanical strength, degradation kinetics, and drug encapsulation efficiency and release profile [[161], [162], [163]]. Hydrogel-mediated chemotherapy

An important and highly promising application direction for hydrogels in pancreatic cancer therapy is their use as local delivery systems for chemotherapeutic agents and radiosensitizers, facilitating effective synergistic enhancement of chemotherapy and radiotherapy [[164], [165], [166]]. Due to its unique biological characteristics, such as a dense tumor stroma, hypoxic microenvironment, and high invasiveness, pancreatic cancer often exhibits significant resistance to single-modality radiotherapy or systemic chemotherapy, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes. The introduction of hydrogels offers an innovative solution to overcome these formidable challenges [167]. By encapsulating chemotherapeutic drugs or specific radiosensitizers within hydrogels, these active substances can be precisely delivered for sustained, prolonged release directly at the tumor site. This strategy of localized high-concentration delivery, compared to conventional systemic administration, significantly increases intratumoral drug exposure, more effectively eradicating tumor cells while maximally reducing drug toxicity to normal tissues and systemic adverse reactions [[168], [169], [170]]. Specifically, hydrogel-mediated delivery of chemotherapeutic radiosensitizers can synergistically enhance the effects of radiotherapy through multiple mechanisms. For instance, certain chemotherapeutic agents can inhibit tumor cell DNA repair mechanisms, rendering cancer cells more susceptible to radiation-induced DNA damage; alternatively, they can arrest the cell cycle, trapping cancer cells in phases more sensitive to radiotherapy [[171], [172], [173]]. Furthermore, hydrogels can deliver substances capable of ameliorating the tumor microenvironment, such as reducing hypoxia or altering stromal architecture, thereby improving effective radiation penetration and tumor cell killing. This “two-pronged” strategy aims to overcome the inherent therapeutic resistance of pancreatic cancer, significantly enhancing the depth and breadth of treatment through localized high-concentration drug action and multiple synergistic mechanisms, thereby improving patient prognosis.

Yang et al. [152]. Developed a degradable thermoresponsive hydrogel drug delivery system based on poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid)-poly (ethylene glycol)-poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA-PEG-PLGA, hereafter referred to as PPP) and ingeniously integrated it with endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle injection (EUS-FNI) technology, successfully achieving local sustained chemotherapy for pancreatic cancer. This system utilized gemcitabine (GEM) as the core chemotherapeutic agent, leveraging the hydrogel's thermoresponsive properties to form a long-acting, sustained-release drug reservoir at the tumor site. This strategy significantly enhanced chemotherapeutic efficacy while effectively reducing systemic toxicity, laying a potential foundation for future combination therapies involving chemotherapy and other modalities. The PPP hydrogel system exhibited a liquid state at room temperature (25 °C), allowing for convenient injection via a syringe; upon entering the human body (37 °C), it rapidly underwent a sol-gel phase transition, forming a semi-solid drug reservoir (with a phase transition temperature of 37.3 °C, very close to human body temperature), thereby effectively preventing rapid drug diffusion. Regarding drug release performance, in vitro experiments demonstrated that GEM achieved sustained release from this hydrogel: in a neutral environment (pH 7.4), the cumulative release rate over 7 days reached as high as 85.49 %; even in an acidic environment (pH 5.0), the 7-day cumulative release rate was 79.51 %. In vivo experiments, validated by Cy5.5 fluorescence labeling, confirmed that the drug retention time within the hydrogel was significantly longer than that of free GEM (fluorescence was still detectable at the tumor site after 168 h, whereas free drug almost completely disappeared after 72 h). Furthermore, in vivo degradation studies indicated that the hydrogel remained stable subcutaneously in mice for 28 days, with no apparent inflammation or tissue damage during degradation, and its degradation products also demonstrated good biosafety. Coupled with precise injection facilitated by endoscopic ultrasound guidance, researchers were able to monitor drug distribution within the tumor in real-time: after injection, GEM/PPP hydrogel formed a localized and layered hyperechoic region within the tumor, with no needle clogging or drug extravasation observed during injection, successfully addressing the issue of rapid drug diffusion in conventional local injections. Regarding in vitro anti-tumor effects, the system exhibited significant inhibitory effects on PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, with cell viability only 34.75 % of the blank control group after 72 h, and an apoptosis rate as high as 51.49 %. Concurrently, the hydrogel system also effectively suppressed cell migration (24-h migration rate of only 6.83 %) and invasion (number of invading cells was only 55.81 % of the free GEM group). In a pancreatic cancer xenograft mouse model, following precise EUS-FNI injection, the system significantly reduced tumor weight by 75.96 %, and the average survival period of mice was extended by 14.4 days, reaching a median survival of 42.4 days. More importantly, mice exhibited no significant systemic toxicity during the treatment, and no notable damage was observed in major organs, demonstrating its favorable safety profile. This study not only offers an efficient new method for precise chemotherapy of pancreatic cancer but also provides crucial EUS imaging data that serve as an important reference for the future translation of this technology into clinical trials (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Schematic diagram of the sustained release of gemcitabine from a biodegradable thermosensitive hydrogel. Hou et al. [174]. Developed a novel carrier-free hydrogel (DA-gel), uniquely formed by the molecular co-assembly of natural small molecules, adenosine (Ado) and 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB), aiming for highly efficient local chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer. This hydrogel departs from traditional exogenous carriers or complex chemical modifications, relying solely on intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions to self-assemble into a stable three-dimensional network. It simultaneously possesses excellent injectability, thermo responsiveness, and biocompatibility, offering a highly promising novel platform for localized sustained release and synergistic enhancement of chemotherapeutic drugs. In terms of preparation, DA-gel was rapidly formed by heating Ado (0.1 M) and DHB (0.2 M) in water at a 2:1 M ratio until dissolved, followed by cooling to 4 °C. The gelation time was only 1 h, a 50 % reduction compared to the hydrogel formed by Ado alone (Ado-gel). Scanning electron microscopy analysis revealed that DA-gel exhibited a tightly intertwined fibrous network structure with a fiber diameter of approximately 37 nm, demonstrating greater stability compared to the fractured fibrous structure of Ado-gel. Rheological tests further confirmed that DA-gel's storage modulus (G′) was 10 times higher than that of Ado-gel, and it maintained its gel state under 5 % strain (whereas Ado-gel was disrupted at 1 % strain), showcasing excellent mechanical strength and stability. In vitro release experiments indicated that DA-gel could sustain Ado release for up to 16 days at 37 °C, with a cumulative release rate as high as 90.8 %, and the release rate was 20 % faster compared to Ado-gel, ensuring the maintenance of persistently effective drug concentrations at the tumor site. DA-gel significantly enhanced chemotherapeutic efficacy through a dual mechanism. Firstly, the hydrogel effectively suppressed tumor cell autophagy: after 24 h of treatment, the autophagy marker LC3-II/LC3-I ratio decreased to 0.32 (compared to 0.65 for the mixed solution group), while KRAS protein expression was reduced by 60 %, thereby effectively blocking the energy recycling and survival mechanisms of tumor cells. Hydrogel-mediated immunotherapy

Another cutting-edge application of hydrogels in pancreatic cancer therapy is their use as a co-delivery platform for chemotherapeutic agents and immunotherapeutic drugs, aiming to achieve synergistic activation of chemotherapy-induced tumor cell death and anti-tumor immune responses [[176], [177], [178]]. The pancreatic cancer TME is renowned for its highly immunosuppressive nature, which often renders single-modality immunotherapy ineffective. Hydrogel-based strategies can effectively overcome this formidable barrier. Hydrogels can precisely deliver chemotherapeutic drugs locally to the tumor, where these agents not only directly kill cancer cells but, more importantly, can also induce immunogenic cell death (ICD). During ICD, dying cancer cells release various danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as ATP, HMGB1, and calreticulin [[179], [180], [181]]. These DAMPs act as “danger signals,” capable of recruiting and activating antigen-presenting cells like dendritic cells (DCs), thereby promoting tumor antigen uptake and presentation [[182], [183], [184]]. Concurrently, hydrogels can co-deliver immune adjuvants such as TLR agonists or STING agonists, or immune checkpoint inhibitors like PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA-4 inhibitors. Immune adjuvants can further enhance immune cell activation and the efficiency of tumor antigen presentation, thereby initiating robust innate and adaptive immune responses. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, on the other hand, can release the tumor's suppression of T cells, restoring their cytotoxic function [185]. Through the localized and sustained-release properties of hydrogels, this combined chemo-immunotherapy strategy can exert its effects at a single site for prolonged periods: on one hand, chemotherapeutic agents continuously eliminate tumor cells and induce immunogenic death; on the other hand, immune adjuvants or checkpoint inhibitors continuously stimulate and activate local immune cells, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive state of the pancreatic cancer microenvironment [[186], [187], [188], [189]]. This “two-pronged” approach aims to convert “cold tumors” (immunologically unresponsive) into “hot tumors” (immunologically responsive), thereby eliciting durable and robust anti-tumor immune memory to effectively inhibit pancreatic cancer growth and metastasis [190].

Hydrogel-mediated photothermal/photodynamic therapy

Another innovative application of hydrogels in pancreatic cancer therapy is their role as delivery vehicles for photothermal agents (PTAs) and photosensitizers (PSs), enabling highly efficient and precise photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) [192]. Pancreatic cancer is deeply seated within the body, and conventional surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy often struggle to achieve complete tumor eradication [193]. In contrast, PTT and PDT, as minimally invasive local therapeutic modalities, can specifically kill cancer cells by activating hydrogel-encapsulated drugs with an external light source to generate heat or reactive oxygen species within the tumor site [194]. Hydrogels can efficiently encapsulate various photothermal agents, such as gold nanorods, copper sulfide nanoparticles, and black phosphorus nanosheets. When these PTAs are delivered to the tumor region and irradiated with near-infrared (NIR) light of a specific wavelength, they efficiently convert light energy into heat, rapidly elevating the local tumor temperature to cytotoxic levels. The biocompatibility and injectability of hydrogels ensure uniform distribution and prolonged retention of PTAs within the tumor, thereby enabling precise thermal ablation of tumor tissue while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues [195]. Similarly, hydrogels can effectively encapsulate PSs such as porphyrin derivatives and phthalocyanines. When PSs are excited by light of a specific wavelength, they react with oxygen through energy transfer or electron transfer processes to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as singlet oxygen and superoxide anions. These ROS possess potent cytotoxicity, capable of inducing apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy in cancer cells, thereby achieving therapeutic objectives [196]. As a carrier, hydrogels protect the stability of PSs, enhance their accumulation within tumors, and extend therapeutic effects through sustained-release mechanisms, ensuring the efficacy of PDT. Importantly, the integration of hydrogels enables the combined application of PTT and PDT. By co-encapsulating PTAs and PSs within a single hydrogel, synergistic killing of tumor cells through both thermal effects and ROS generation can be achieved in a single treatment, yielding a “1 + 1>2″ therapeutic outcome. Furthermore, the malleability and controlled-release properties of hydrogels also provide a platform for combining PTT/PDT with other therapeutic strategies, thus facilitating the development of more efficient and synergistic comprehensive treatment regimens for pancreatic cancer.

Tissue engineering scaffolds and cell therapy carriers

Beyond their role as drug delivery systems, hydrogels, with their exceptional biocompatibility, tunable physical properties, and three-dimensional network structure, demonstrate unique value in pancreatic cancer research and therapy within the fields of tissue engineering scaffolds and cell therapy carriers [197]. In vitro pancreatic cancer research has long been constrained by the limitations of traditional two-dimensional cell culture models, which fail to adequately mimic the complex tumor microenvironment and cell-cell interactions. Hydrogels, serving as three-dimensional culture scaffolds, provide an ideal platform for constructing more physiologically relevant in vitro pancreatic cancer models. Hydrogels can mimic the physical and biochemical properties of the extracellular matrix, offering a suitable three-dimensional growth environment for pancreatic cancer cells including primary tumor cells and cell lines. Pancreatic cancer organoids cultured within hydrogels can better recapitulate tumor heterogeneity, proliferation patterns, gene expression profiles, and drug responses, thereby becoming powerful tools for drug screening, such as novel chemotherapeutics and targeted drugs, and for disease mechanism research [198]. In combination with microfluidic technology, hydrogels can be used to construct complex “tumor-on-a-chip” models. By integrating tumor cells, stromal cells, and vascular networks within the hydrogel microenvironment, these chips can simulate tumor growth, invasion, metastasis processes, and drug permeability, providing a more precise platform for high-throughput drug screening, personalized treatment regimen development, and mechanistic studies of TME-drug interactions.

Cell therapy, particularly immunocyte therapy and stem cell therapy, shows great potential in pancreatic cancer, but their efficacy is often limited by cell survival rate, homing efficiency, and local immunosuppression. Hydrogels, serving as cell carriers, can significantly ameliorate these issues. Hydrogels can serve as local delivery carriers for immunocytes such as CAR-T cells and NK cells. By encapsulating immunocytes in injectable hydrogels, large numbers of active immune cells can be directly engrafted at tumor sites or surgical margins, ensuring high local concentrations and sustained function of immune cells, thereby overcoming the problems of low homing efficiency and systemic dispersion after systemic administration. Concurrently, hydrogels can also enhance the survival time, proliferative capacity, and anti-tumor activity of immune cells, and even remodel the local immunosuppressive microenvironment, by co-loading immunostimulatory molecules or sustained-releasing nutritional factors. In some cases, stem cells may be utilized for repairing postoperative complications of pancreatic cancer or mitigating radiotherapy/chemotherapy-induced damage. Hydrogels can provide a protective microenvironment for these stem cells, improving their in vivo survival rate and engraftment efficiency, and supporting stem cell proliferation, differentiation, and tissue repair functions through the sustained release of bioactive factors, thereby enhancing the safety and efficacy of stem cell therapy. In summary, hydrogels, as tissue engineering scaffolds and cell therapy carriers, open new avenues for both basic research and clinical applications in pancreatic cancer, with the potential to play a crucial role in drug development, mechanism elucidation, and enhancement of cell therapy efficacy.

Clinical translation and challenges

In recent five-year studies, hydrogel-mediated drug delivery has demonstrated good safety and significant anti-tumor efficacy in pancreatic cancer animal models. By directly delivering chemotherapeutic agents or immunomodulators to the local pancreatic tumor site, hydrogel systems can achieve high drug concentrations at the tumor while significantly reducing systemic exposure, thereby minimizing side effects [[199], [200], [201], [202]]. For instance, a gemcitabine sustained-release hydrogel implant achieved localized drug delivery for over 3 weeks in a pancreatic cancer mouse model. Its drug concentration within tumor tissue was significantly higher than that in the intravenous administration group, while drug levels in normal tissues such as plasma, heart, liver, and duodenum were substantially reduced [203]. This localized delivery strategy effectively enhanced anti-tumor effects: studies showed that hydrogel-mediated drug delivery augmented tumor cell killing and delayed tumor growth, with significantly reduced systemic toxicity [138]. Hydrogel formulations exhibited good biocompatibility and safety in animal experiments [204]. Many biodegradable drug-loaded hydrogel systems achieved sustained drug release without causing severe adverse reactions in experimental animals [205,206]. Representative studies indicated that co-encapsulation of gemcitabine and cisplatin in a thermoresponsive copolymer hydrogel enabled sustained drug release in a pancreatic cancer xenograft mouse model, significantly inducing intratumoral cell apoptosis and inhibiting tumor growth without observable damage to normal tissues [207]. Similarly, loading gemcitabine and paclitaxel nanoparticles into a thermoresponsive hydrogel allowed for localized, long-acting combined administration, effectively killing pancreatic cancer cells in vitro and reducing dosing frequency; in vivo experiments, mice in this hydrogel group exhibited high anti-tumor activity with only mild systemic toxicity [193]. Furthermore, some studies explored the application of hydrogels in postoperative local therapy: for instance, Delitto et al. [208]. Reported loading neoantigen peptide vaccines and adjuvants into a hyaluronic acid hydrogel and applying it to pancreatic tumor resection margins, successfully stimulating anti-tumor T cell immunity and thereby preventing residual pancreatic cancer recurrence in a mouse model. Overall, these animal experimental results indicate that hydrogel delivery systems can achieve sustained, targeted drug release in pancreatic cancer models, enhancing anticancer efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity, thus providing promising options for local treatment of pancreatic cancer. Notably, to overcome the dense stromal barrier characteristic of pancreatic cancer, researchers have designed various novel hydrogel administration forms to enhance tissue penetration and drug distribution efficiency. For instance, 3D-printed hydrogel drug patches can adhere closely to the tumor surface, achieving localized sustained drug release: an alginate hydrogel patch with an adhesive coating loaded with gemcitabine consistently inhibited pancreatic cancer cell proliferation in vitro for over two weeks and effectively delayed tumor growth in orthotopic pancreatic cancer mice without causing significant toxicity [209]. Another example is the microneedle array hydrogel patch inspired by the structure of octopus arms, which enhances adhesion and permeability in pancreatic tumor tissue by incorporating microscopic suction cup structures on its surface [210]. This gelatin methacrylate microneedle hydrogel can carry high concentrations of gemcitabine, penetrating the dense tumor stroma to deliver drugs deep into the tumor; mouse experiments showed that it significantly inhibited pancreatic tumor proliferation, with an anti-tumor effect superior to the combined effect of two direct intratumoral injections, even with only 40 % of the conventional full dose of drug loaded. These innovative delivery methods indicate that by improving the morphology and physicochemical properties of hydrogels, it is possible to further improve drug distribution and retention in pancreatic tumors, thereby overcoming the therapeutic obstacles posed by the highly desmoplastic matrix of pancreatic cancer.

Long-term biosafety and controlled degradability

The long-term in vivo fate and degradation behavior of hydrogel materials directly correlate with their biosafety. Currently, a major challenge is ensuring that hydrogels degrade as needed after fulfilling their drug release function, without inducing chronic adverse reactions [211]. Non-degradable or poorly degradable base materials may remain in the body long-term, potentially eliciting chronic inflammation or fibrotic responses [212]. However, even for biodegradable hydrogels, their degradation kinetics are often influenced by the complex in vivo environment, making precise control difficult—traditional hydrolysis or enzymatic degradation methods suffer from unpredictable degradation rates and poor spatiotemporal controllability. Therefore, a critical task is to achieve programmable degradation through material design to ensure that hydrogels safely inactivate or are absorbed after drug delivery is complete. Furthermore, the byproducts generated from hydrogel degradation must be non-toxic and harmless; ideally, degradation products should be biocompatible and even promote beneficial biological effects. In summary, achieving a match between hydrogel degradation rate and therapeutic requirements still requires in-depth research, which is crucial for their long-term implantation safety and efficacy.

Standardization of large-scale production and quality control

To successfully translate laboratory-prepared hydrogels into clinical use, issues of consistency and quality in industrial-scale production must be addressed [213]. Hydrogels derived from natural polymers, while possessing good biocompatibility, often suffer from batch-to-batch variability and poor reproducibility, posing challenges for large-scale manufacturing. Conversely, synthetic polymer hydrogels have well-defined compositions and high tunability, but still require strict control of reaction conditions during large-batch production to ensure consistent network structure and mechanical properties across every batch. Regulatory agencies mandate production in accordance with Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP), which implies rigorous standards and monitoring for critical quality attributes such as raw material purity, degree of crosslinking, water content, and sterility levels. However, the high-water content and delicate structure of hydrogels make process scale-up especially difficult in synthesis, sterilization, and storage. For example, parameters like temperature, pH, and reaction time, which are easily controlled at the laboratory scale, can cause hydrogel performance fluctuations with minor changes at industrial scale [214]. Addressing these issues requires establishing systematic quality control systems and production standards covering all stages from monomer raw materials to the final formulation. Currently, a lack of unified standards remains a major obstacle to hydrogel product consistency and controllable mass production. Additionally, the complexity of large-scale production and quality testing processes also drives up costs; it is estimated that bringing a novel hydrogel delivery system from R&D to market approval could cost tens to hundreds of millions of USD.

Technical bottlenecks in pancreatic localized injection/implantation and requirements for in vivo stability

Due to the pancreas's deep retroperitoneal location and proximity to vital blood vessels and nerves, precise delivery of hydrogels to pancreatic tumors is challenging. This typically requires image-guided techniques such as endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) or CT guidance for accurate injection or implantation into the tumor [215]. However, even with guidance, the complex anatomy of the pancreas and variations in tumor location increase procedural uncertainty. In preclinical and clinical trials, EUS-guided needle injection of hydrogel spacers into the space between the pancreatic head and duodenum has been explored to protect normal tissue from radiotherapy damage [216]. Yet, this process demands extremely high technical skill from physicians and advanced imaging equipment; if localization is imprecise and the hydrogel is inadvertently injected into critical areas like large blood vessels, it could lead to severe complications such as embolism. Therefore, improving the precision and safety of hydrogel delivery is one of the major technical bottlenecks. Furthermore, whether hydrogels can stably remain locally within pancreatic tumors after in vivo injection is a critical consideration. Pancreatic tissue is soft and constantly subject to erosion by digestive fluids, making it difficult for conventional hydrogels to fix in the moist and perpetually peristaltic pancreatic environment, posing risks of displacement or premature loss. To address this, novel hydrogels are being designed with tissue adhesiveness and rapid gelation properties [217]. For instance, incorporating adhesive functional groups like dopamine can enable hydrogels to firmly adhere to the slippery tissue of pancreatic resection margins, thereby maintaining their in-situ position without being flushed away and continuously releasing drugs [218]. Additionally, hydrogels need to resist degradation by biological factors like pancreatic enzymes, maintaining structural stability for a predetermined duration. Therefore, achieving high-precision, minimally invasive delivery of hydrogels to the pancreas, along with stable retention and reliable drug release after implantation, are challenges that must be overcome prior to clinical application.

Tissue penetration capability required to overcome the dense stromal barrier of pancreatic cancer

The tumor microenvironment of PDAC is characterized by dense fibrosis, with abundant collagen and matrix making the tumor tissue abnormally compact and rigid. This typical sclerotic stromal barrier not only limits the penetration of chemotherapeutic drugs into the tumor but also hinders immune cells from entering and functioning within the tumor [219]. Consequently, in preclinical research, it has been increasingly recognized that mere localized sustained release is insufficient; hydrogel delivery systems must also possess the ability to distribute deeply into tumor tissue for drugs to truly reach every cancer cell. To this end, researchers have adopted various strategies: one is to combine stroma-targeting therapy, i.e., incorporating drugs that can remodel the tumor matrix into the hydrogel to weaken the stromal barrier and enhance subsequent drug diffusion [220]. For example, some studies have loaded matrix-degrading enzymes into pH-responsive Penta block polypeptide hydrogels to enable selective gemcitabine release in the acidic tumor environment, more effectively killing pancreatic cancer cells while reducing impact on healthy pancreatic tissue [221]. Another strategy is to optimize hydrogel administration forms, such as the aforementioned microneedle patches, which can physically penetrate the tumor stroma to deliver drugs directly into deeper tumor tissue, thereby significantly increasing local drug concentration [222]. Preclinical results indicate that applying microneedle array hydrogels leads to more uniform drug distribution and more significant therapeutic effects within tumor tissue. In summary, the pancreatic cancer stromal barrier remains a formidable obstacle for hydrogel therapies, requiring an integration of materials science and tumor biology approaches to improve tissue penetrability [[223], [224], [225]]. Future directions may include: developing smaller-sized or more compliant hydrogel nanogels to self-infiltrate stromal micropores; introducing tumor microenvironment-responsive designs that enable hydrogels to release matrix-dissolving factors at the tumor site; and combining with immunotherapy to soften the tumor matrix by reprogramming CAFs. Only by breaking through this barrier can hydrogel-mediated drug delivery fully exert its therapeutic efficacy in pancreatic cancer.

Regulatory approval challenges for biomaterial-drug combination products

Advancing hydrogel drug delivery systems to clinical application is also constrained by complex regulatory pathways [[226], [227], [228]]. Such products often contain both a drug and a biomaterial carrier, and are thus regulated as “combination products,” requiring simultaneous fulfillment of approval requirements for both medical devices and drugs [229]. Regulatory agencies in different countries and regions have established stringent review procedures for combination products, often involving coordination among multiple departments and comprehensive consideration of product safety, efficacy, and material quality. This makes the approval process significantly longer than for standalone drugs or devices. It has been reported that such products can take 7–12 years from laboratory research to obtaining regulatory marketing approval. The main difficulties include: firstly, the diverse composition of hydrogels themselves leads to classification challenges, as regulatory bodies must determine the product's primary mode of action (drug-led or device-led) and apply different regulatory pathways accordingly. Secondly, during the review process, regulators must not only evaluate the efficacy and safety of the encapsulated drug but also scrutinize biosafety issues related to the hydrogel material's in vivo metabolism, degradation, and tissue compatibility [230]. This series of requirements means developers need to conduct extensive additional pharmacological, toxicological, material biocompatibility testing, and process validation. Furthermore, the preparation of GMP manufacturing and quality system documentation is also more complex, requiring compliance with both drug and device regulations. Due to high regulatory requirements and large-scale clinical trials, the R&D costs and risks for combination products are also significantly increased. For example, a review indicated that hydrogel implants encapsulating drugs or cells require additional submissions such as 510(k) or Biologics License Applications (BLA), costing hundreds of millions of USD on average to obtain market approval in the United States [231]. Therefore, clarifying product attributes with regulatory bodies and formulating reasonable clinical trial and approval strategies are also significant challenges for hydrogel-based pancreatic cancer therapies. Overcoming this difficulty may require further refinement of relevant regulations and clearer guiding principles, as well as close collaboration among researchers, industry, and regulatory agencies, to accelerate the demonstration of safety and efficacy for such products and facilitate their early clinical translation. The application of hydrogels in pancreatic cancer treatment is at a critical stage of transition from laboratory to clinic. Preclinical research has fully demonstrated its immense potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce side effects through localized controlled release, but it has also exposed various challenges related to material degradation, safety assessment, manufacturing processes, delivery methods, and regulatory approval. Future research needs multidisciplinary collaboration to achieve smarter and more controllable hydrogel designs in materials science, to overcome challenges in precise delivery and large-scale production through engineering technology, and to accumulate more evidence and guidelines in clinical research and regulatory science. Only by simultaneously addressing these issues can hydrogel-drug delivery systems truly move from the laboratory to the clinic and become a new powerful tool for improving the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients.

Conclusion and future perspectives

PDAC remains a formidable malignancy, characterized by its aggressive nature, late diagnosis, and a uniquely complex and immunosuppressive TME that collectively render conventional therapies largely ineffective. In this review, we have comprehensively summarized the burgeoning role of hydrogels as a versatile and promising platform to overcome these profound challenges. Hydrogels, with their tunable physicochemical properties, superior biocompatibility, and inherent ability to form three-dimensional networks, offer distinct advantages for localized, controlled, and sustained delivery of therapeutic agents. We have highlighted their successful applications in mediating chemotherapy, enhancing immunotherapy, facilitating photothermal/photodynamic therapies, and serving as sophisticated tissue engineering scaffolds and cell therapy carriers. Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that hydrogel-based systems can significantly increase intratumoral drug concentrations, reduce systemic toxicity, remodel the fibrotic and immunosuppressive TME, and ultimately improve anti-tumor efficacy and survival rates in various PDAC models. These achievements underscore hydrogels' potential to transform the therapeutic landscape for this devastating disease.

Despite the remarkable progress, the clinical translation of hydrogel-based therapies for pancreatic cancer faces several critical challenges, including ensuring long-term biosafety and controlled degradation, standardizing large-scale production and quality control, overcoming technical bottlenecks in precise in situ injection/implantation, and enhancing tissue penetration through the dense stromal barrier. Addressing these multifaceted hurdles will necessitate concerted, multidisciplinary efforts in future research. Future research should focus on designing highly sophisticated hydrogels capable of integrating multiple therapeutic modalities and responding to a wider array of complex TME cues. Such multi-stimuli responsive systems could enable more precise, “on-demand” drug release and synergistic therapeutic effects, moving beyond single-agent or dual-agent approaches. Leveraging advanced computational tools, including artificial intelligence (AI) and big data analytics, will be crucial for designing more precise and individualized hydrogel therapeutic regimens. AI-driven approaches can accelerate material discovery, predict hydrogel performance in vivo, optimize drug loading and release kinetics, and potentially guide patient stratification based on individual tumor characteristics and predicted responses. A deeper understanding of the intricate interactions between hydrogels and the dynamic PDAC microenvironment is paramount. This includes elucidating how hydrogel properties influence stromal remodeling, immune cell infiltration, and drug distribution at the cellular and molecular levels. Such insights will enable the rational design of hydrogels that actively engage with and favorably reprogram the TME, thereby optimizing delivery strategies and therapeutic outcomes. The ultimate goal is to translate these promising laboratory findings into clinical benefits for pancreatic cancer patients. This requires rigorous preclinical validation in more complex, patient-relevant models and a streamlined pathway for regulatory approval. Collaborative efforts among academia, industry, and regulatory bodies are essential to establish standardized manufacturing processes, robust quality control measures, and clear regulatory guidelines for these complex combination products. Overcoming these translational barriers will pave the way for hydrogel-based therapies to become a new cornerstone in the fight against pancreatic cancer. By addressing these future directions, hydrogel-mediated therapeutic strategies hold immense promise to significantly improve the prognosis and quality of life for patients battling pancreatic cancer.
