Comprehensive assessment of incidence, mortality, and lifetime risk of ovarian cancer: a global study with forecasts to 2050
Authors: Yanping Jiang, Qian Xu, Yanjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to update the latest epidemiology of ovarian cancer.MethodsIncidence and mortality data of ovarian cancer were extracted from 2 databases, the GLOBOCAN 2022 and the Global Burden of Disease 2021, to analyze age-standardized rates across 185 countries. The Age-Mortality-Population method and average annual per cent change (AAPC) were employed to calculate the lifetime risk of incidence (LRI) and lifetime risk of mortality (LRM), and to evaluate temporal trends (2000–2021) for ovarian cancer, respectively. Incidence cases and deaths of ovarian cancer were projected to 2050.ResultsThe number of incidence cases and deaths of ovarian cancer is projected to rise from 300,000 and 200,000 in 2022 to 477,000 and 337,000 by 2050. Low sociodemographic index (SDI) countries are projected to experience increases of 153% in incidence and 165.2% in mortality, while high SDI countries are expected to see modest increases of 21.8% and 36.4%. The overall trend of LRI and LRM decreased from 2000 to 2021. However, LRI in high SDI countries declined from 1.6% to 1.2%, LRM from 1.1% to 0.85%; LRI in low SDI countries increased from 0.46% to 0.63%, LRM from 0.37% to 0.49%. AAPCs for LRI and LRM both increased by >1% in low and low-middle SDI countries, but declined by >1% in high SDI countries.ConclusionThe incidence and mortality rates for ovarian cancer are expected to rise, but this burden is distributed unevenly across regions and SDI levels. The stark divergence underscores an urgent need for coordinated efforts to ensure equitable cancer care globally.Keywords: Ovarian Cancer, Incidence, Mortality, Epidemiology

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer remains one of the most lethal gynecologic malignancies worldwide, primarily due to its insidious nature and frequent late-stage diagnosis [1]. The 5-year survival rate remains low, and research has shown that the 5-year cause-specific survival for cases diagnosed at stages III and IV, which make up 80% of total incidents, is only 41% and 20%, respectively [2]. Despite advances in surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies such as poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors, the overall prognosis for ovarian cancer has seen only modest improvement over the past few decades [3]. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, ovarian cancer accounted for over 300,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths globally [4], posing a substantial challenge to women’s health.

Multiple risk factors contribute to the genesis and development of ovarian cancer. Research has demonstrated that genetic factors, such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 gene mutations, significantly increase the lifetime risk of developing ovarian cancer [5,6,7], supplemented by factors including family history, nulliparity, endometriosis, and hormone replacement therapy. Conversely, factors such as oral contraceptive use, full-term pregnancy and breastfeeding, and tubal ligation are associated with risk reduction [8,9,10]. Emerging evidence has also highlighted the role of lifestyle, such as diet and physical activity, in modifying ovarian cancer risk [11,12].

The global burden of ovarian cancer is not evenly distributed. High-income regions and countries such as Northern Europe usually report higher incidence rates, likely due to population aging and better diagnostic capabilities [13,14]. Studies have reported that the incidence and mortality rates of ovarian cancer are strongly correlated with the human development index, standard of living, educational level, lifestyles, cultural beliefs, and environmental exposures [15]. Socioeconomic disparities further compound these challenges due to limited healthcare infrastructure, insufficient access to early diagnosis and treatment, and a lack of awareness of symptoms and risk factors for ovarian cancer.

Given the substantial burden of ovarian cancer, this study leverages GLOBOCAN 2022 data to evaluate the current incidence, mortality, and distribution patterns of ovarian cancer worldwide. It further seeks to analyze the lifetime risk and future trends of ovarian cancer across regions and socioeconomic contexts, thereby providing critical evidence to guide public health planning and decision-making.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data sources

We obtained ovarian cancer incidence and mortality data from 2 major global databases: GLOBOCAN 2022 and the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. From GLOBOCAN 2022 (International Agency for Research on Cancer, IARC) [16], we downloaded country-level age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR) and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) in 2022, as well as the projected new cases and deaths for ovarian cancer among women of all ages. From the GBD 2021 database [17], accessed via the Global Health Data Exchange, we extracted age-band data on ovarian cancer incidence and deaths for females across standard 5-year age groups (5–9, 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, …, 80–84, and 85+ years), along with corresponding all-cause mortality rates for the same age ranges.

The study encompassed data from 185 countries and territories, supplemented with country-level metadata, including World Bank income classifications and regional groupings (https://www.worldbank.org/), to facilitate comparative analysis. The datasets were used to generate both descriptive and analytical insights. First, we visualized ovarian cancer incidence and mortality patterns across countries, stratified by World Bank region, sociodemographic index (SDI) level, and age group (<40 and ≥40 years). Second, the data were used for further analyses, including lifetime risk estimation, temporal trend assessment, and future burden projection.

2. Lifetime risk estimation

In this study, we estimated two key metrics: the lifetime risk of incidence (LRI) for ovarian cancer, defined as the probability of being diagnosed with the disease during a person’s lifetime (excluding multiple primary cancers), and the lifetime risk of mortality (LRM) for ovarian cancer, defined as the probability of dying from the disease over a lifetime. Traditional methods for estimating these risks included the cumulative rate, cumulative risk, and the life table approach. We adopted the Age-Mortality-Population (AMP) method [18,19], a life table-based approach that further adjusts for the occurrence of multiple primary cancers in the population, offering more accurate and realistic estimates.

Mathematically, for each 5-year age group i, the AMP-lifetime risk algorithm takes as inputs: Ri, the age-specific cancer incidence rate (number of new cancer cases per 100,000 persons per year); Mi, the all-cause mortality rate (deaths from any cause per 100,000 persons per year); Di, the cancer-specific mortality rate (deaths from the same cancer per 100,000 persons per year); and wi, the width of the age interval (usually 5 years).

At the start of each interval, the probability of being alive and cancer-free, denoted Si, is computed recursively as the product of the survival probabilities from all preceding intervals. The total exit rate from the cancer-free state is given by λi = (Ri +Mi − Di)/100,000, representing the combined hazard of developing cancer or dying from other causes. This formulation accounts for competing mortality, ensuring that individuals who die of other causes are removed from the population at risk in subsequent age intervals. When Ri is replaced by Di, the same framework yields the lifetime risk of mortality. The AMP method, therefore, provides internally consistent and life table–based estimates of the probability that an individual will develop or die from ovarian cancer during her lifetime, under the assumption that the age-specific incidence and mortality rates of the reference year remain constant over time. To quantify uncertainty, we recomputed the AMP estimates using the lower and upper bounds of the GBD-reported 95% uncertainty intervals for all three rate inputs (incidence, ovarian cancer-related mortality, and all-cause mortality). These runs yielded the lower and upper uncertainty limits of the lifetime risk estimates presented in this study. The resulting 95% uncertainty intervals reflect the propagation of uncertainty from the underlying GBD estimates.

4. Projections to 2050

We obtained estimates and projections for ovarian cancer incidence and mortality from the GLOBOCAN database for the period 2022–2050. GLOBOCAN projections were generated by applying the age-specific incidence and mortality rates observed in 2022 to population projections from the United Nations World Population Prospects, under the assumption that age-specific rates remain constant over time. Thus, these projections reflect expected demographic changes, principally population growth and ageing, rather than changes in the underlying cancer risk or survival. We visualized the temporal trends in the estimated and projected numbers of new cases and deaths, and calculated the percentage change between years using the formula %Δ = (Nt2−Nt1)/Nt1 × 100%, where Nt1 and Nt2 denote the numbers of cases or deaths at the beginning and end of the comparison period, respectively. We stratified analyses by SDI and age group (<40 vs. ≥40 years) to capture variations in the projected burden of ovarian cancer across different socioeconomic and demographic profiles.

5. Statistical analysis and visualization

All data processing, lifetime risk estimation, and visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The analyses employed key packages including dplyr, ggplot2, patchwork, and readr. To facilitate geographic or socioeconomic comparisons, country names were harmonized across datasets and mapped to relevant group classifications.

6. Ethics statement

This study employed previously published, de-identified human data from the GBD study, where ethics approval and consent to participate were not required for secondary data analysis.

RESULTS

1. The current global and regional burden of ovarian cancer

There were over 300,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths of ovarian cancer globally in 2022. Fig. 1 reveals significant regional disparities in ASIR (Fig. 1A) and ASMR (Fig. 1B). The highest incidence rates were clustered in Eastern and Northern Europe, North America, and parts of Asia (>8.2 per 100,000 women), and the lowest in parts of Africa and Latin America (0.7–4.1). Mortality was most pronounced in Eastern Europe and Central Asia (4.5–6.3). Notably, the data highlighted a critical disparity: sub-Saharan Africa endured high mortality rates against a backdrop of low incidence, while Western Europe and Northern America achieved lower mortality (0.8–3.3) despite higher incidence.

To further contextualize these distribution differences in ASIR and ASMR, we stratified countries based on geographic region (as defined by the World Bank) and SDI levels (Fig. 2). Europe and Central Asia recorded the highest average incidence rates (8.28), followed by North America (8.27), Southeast and East Asia and Oceania (7.66), South Asia (5.72), North Africa and the Middle East (5.55), Latin America and the Caribbean (5.31), and Sub-Saharan Africa (4.81) (Fig. 2A). In addition, mortality was disproportionately different relative to incidence across regions (Fig. 2B). Despite a lower incidence rate, regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia exhibited a comparatively higher mortality rate. Conversely, North America, and Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania demonstrated a lower average mortality relative to their incidence rate. Specifically, the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR) of the Sub-Saharan African region was 0.77, while North America showed a MIR of 0.50, as shown in Table S1. Notably, variations were seen in incidence and mortality rates across intra-regions (Fig. 2A and B). Take Europe and Central Asia as an example, Tajikistan’s incidence rate was under 4, while several other countries reported rates exceeding 12. Moreover, the average mortality rate in this region was 4.85, yet two countries diverged sharply, with one below 2.5 and another above 7.5. A similar disparity was evident in Sub-Saharan Africa, where the average mortality was 3.71, but one country reported mortality rates exceeding 7.5. These findings indicate that the disparities of ovarian cancer burden may not only be determined by incidence but also by inequalities in early detection and treatment. When stratified by SDI (Fig. 2C and D), high-middle SDI countries endured the highest average incidence (8.21) and mortality (4.96) rates, followed by high (7.93 and 4.51), middle (5.83 and 3.95), low-middle (5.12 and 3.72), and low (4.62 and 3.59) SDI countries. Of note, 30 of 41 (73%) high SDI countries had ASMR below 5, which was lower than the corresponding ASIR (around 8). In contrast, mortality rates in low SDI countries were relatively higher compared with their incidence rates, reflected by an MIR of 0.78, as shown in Table S2. These variations likely reflected differences in diagnostic capacity, life expectancy, and data quality.

This list closely mirrored that of the women of all ages group, but showed more pronounced disparities compared to the women under 40 years of age group. These findings suggest that survival disparities are most evident in older women, directly pointing to inequalities in access to advanced diagnostics and quality of medical care.

2. The refined lifetime risk estimates using the AMP method

Fig. 4 illustrates the overall trend of LRI and LRM decreased from 2000 to 2021, and these trends varied considerably across the study period, global regions and SDI levels. At the global regional level (Fig. 4A and B), the LRI and LRM remained highest throughout the period in North America, and LRI decreased from approximately 1.79% in 2000 to 1.26% in 2021 and LRM from roughly 1.23% to 0.89%. Followed by Europe and Central Asia, the LRI decreased from 1.48% to 1.25%, and the LRM decreased from 1.06% to 0.92%. Latin America and Caribbean (LRI, 0.86% to 0.89%; LRM, 0.65% to 0.63%), and Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania (LRI, 0.66% to 0.69%; LRM, 0.43% to 0.41%) exhibited a moderate risk among the seven global regions, with a relatively stable LRI and LRM over the past 20 years. In contrast, South Asia (LRI, 0.54% to 0.72%; LRM, 0.41% to 0.53%), Sub-Saharan Africa (LRI, 0.47% to 0.65%; LRM, 0.37% to 0.51%), as well as North Africa and Middle East (LRI, 0.44% to 0.56%; LRM, 0.33% to 0.41%) demonstrated a rising LRI and LRM over the same period, highlighting a growing ovarian cancer burden. Similarly, when stratified by SDI (Fig. 4C and D), the highest LRI and LRM were observed in high SDI countries, despite a decline in both measures (LRI, 1.6% to 1.2%; LRM, 1.1% to 0.85%). High-middle SDI countries followed a similar declining trend (LRI, 0.99% to 0.88%; LRM, 0.70% to 0.62%). In contrast, middle (LRI, 0.58% to 0.68%; LRM, 0.41% to 0.44%), low-middle (LRI, 0.53% to 0.70%; LRM, 0.38% to 0.50), and low (LRI, 0.46% to 0.63%; LRM, 0.37% to 0.49%) SDI countries experienced gradual increases in both LRI and LRM. Notably, the substantial overlap of 95% confidence intervals (The shaded ribbons in Fig. 4), particularly after 2015, suggests that differences across regions and SDI levels were less pronounced and should be interpreted with caution.

3. Future projections of burden

Based on projected demographic shifts, the global burden of ovarian cancer is expected to increase substantially from 2022 to 2050, with 477,000 new cases (a 46.9% increase) and 337,000 deaths (a 62.7% increase) projected for 2050 (Fig. 6). Of note, both incidence and mortality are expected to rise across all SDI levels, but the magnitude varies dramatically. The largest increases are anticipated in low SDI countries (incidence: +153.8%; mortality: +165.2%), followed by moderate increases in low-middle (+88.6% and +104.9%) and middle (+60.0% and +81.2%) SDI countries. In contrast, high-middle (+14.3% and +35.0%) and high (+21.8% and +36.4%) SDI countries are projected to see more modest changes (Fig. 6A and B). When stratified by age, for women under 40 years old, increases are expected in low (+103% and +102.3%), low-middle (+20.8% and +21.1%), and middle (+2.1% and 3.7%) SDI countries, and declines are projected in high-middle (−24.2% and −21.9%) and high (−10.9% and −9.9%) SDI countries (Fig. 6C and D). For women aged 40 years and older, the projected increases are pronounced in low (+174.4% and +177.0%) and low-middle (+102.9% and +112.3%) SDI countries. In contrast, modest increases are anticipated in high-middle (+18.5% and +36.7%) and high (+23.9% and +36.9%) SDI countries (Fig. 6E and F).

DISCUSSION

Ovarian cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed gynecologic malignancies, with incidence and mortality rates varying greatly across countries and regions. This study describes the latest epidemiology of ovarian cancer, including incidence, mortality, and lifetime risk, and projects its burden to 2050 at the global, regional, and national levels. We observed that developed countries bear a higher incidence burden, yet developing countries face a disproportionately higher mortality burden, indicating critical disparities in healthcare access. While the overall LRI and LRM from ovarian cancer have declined over the past two decades, this trend varies by regions and SDI levels. Alarmingly, we project a substantial increase in the future global burden of ovarian cancer, underscoring the urgent need for global action.

According to the GBD data, the global incidence and mortality associated with ovarian cancer increased from 1990 to 2019, reaching 294,422 and 198,412 cases, respectively. These numbers rose to over 300,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths in 2022 [4,20], and are projected to grow substantially to 477,000 and 337,000 by 2050. This trend indicates a huge increase in the global burden of ovarian cancer in the future. Analysis of age-standardized rate (ASR) reveals a similar pattern. The ASIR increased from 152.1 per 100,000 in 1990 to 286.1 in 2017, and the ASMR rose from 95.5 to 176.0. However, the distribution of these rates is highly uneven across regions: countries in Western Europe experience the greatest decreases, while nations in Latin America and the Caribbean have seen the most pronounced increases [21]. These findings were consistent with the results of our analysis. Besides, our analysis showed these disparities were strongly associated with SDI levels, with the highest ASR observed in high SDI countries. This correlation can be explained by variations in healthcare infrastructure. Extensive research has established that access to early screening, advanced cancer treatments, nutritional support, and rehabilitation services is essential for improving the survival of ovarian cancer patients. Developed countries tend to provide better comprehensive medical care, which may explain their relatively higher incidence rate but lower mortality rate. In contrast, the limited health care access in developing countries often results in later diagnosis and higher MIRs [14,22,23].

Global trends in ovarian cancer have shown a rise in incidence and mortality over the past 2 decades, while the overall trends of LRI and LRM were decreasing, and they varied hugely across regions and SDI levels. For instance, high SDI countries saw a marked decline from 2000 to 2021, whereas low SDI countries experienced a slight increase in LRI and LRM. This pattern aligns with the observed AAPCs in ASIR and ASMR, which decreased in developed countries like those in Western and Northern Europe but increased in many developing regions, including South Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East. These disparities are driven by multiple factors. Beyond unequal access to cancer care, genetic susceptibility may play a role. For example, the prevalence of BRCA1/2 mutations is higher in women of African descent (15.6%) compared to Western populations (12.2%) [24], which may contribute to the rising trend of AAPCs in some African regions. Moreover, the stark difference in outcomes is further supported by survival data: the 5-year survival rate in developed countries was 36%–46% (2010–2014) [25,26], while this rate in developing countries can be as low as 18.6% (India) to the mid-30s (Brazil, Thailand), with an overall estimated range of 20%–35% [26]. Notably, improvements in survival rate for ovarian cancer in developed countries ranged from 4.4% to 9.8% from 1995–1999 to 2010–2014, and were much lower compared to that of rectal cancer (9.5% to 21.0%) during the same period [25]. The high mortality rates and slow improvements for ovarian cancer were largely attributed to the lack of effective early screening methods, leading to most cases being diagnosed at a late stage. Our study also demonstrated that LRI and LRM for ovarian cancer rose more rapidly after age 40, highlighting the need for the development of effective screening methods.

Projections based on 2022 data indicate a sharp rise in the global burden of ovarian cancer by 2050, with a 46.9% increase in incidence and a more pronounced 62.7% increase in mortality. Critically, this burden is strongly correlated with SDI levels. Low SDI countries are projected to bear a disproportionately higher share of new cases and deaths, a trend consistent with other malignancies [27,28], highlighting widening disparities in the future. Consequently, it is necessary to call for global attention to reduce the disparities of ovarian cancer burden and facilitate access to quality cancer care and treatment.

There were several limitations of this study. First, the accuracy of lifetime risk estimates is contingent upon the quality and completeness of the underlying cancer registry data. However, the reliability of these registries varies substantially across regions, with data from areas such as Africa and South America being particularly limited. Second, the observed associations between ovarian cancer metrics (incidence, mortality, lifetime risk) and SDI levels may reflect international disparities in diagnostic capacity and access to medical care. Therefore, underdiagnosis of ovarian cancer in lower-resource settings may be an important factor that compromises the precision of lifetime risk estimations. Third, GLOBOCAN and GBD databases provide age information, but lack data on histology and clinical stage. This precludes an aggregated analysis by these factors and limits the clinical interpretability of our findings.
