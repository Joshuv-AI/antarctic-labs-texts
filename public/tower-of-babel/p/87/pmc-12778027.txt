Complexity and Health Care Utilization in Infant ESKD
Authors: Ashna R. Pudupakkam, Michael C. Braun, Catherine Joseph, Poyyapakkam R. Srivaths, Sarah J. Swartz, Shweta S. Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract are the leading causes of ESKD in the pediatric population. This is particularly evident in the neonatal population, where congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract and genetic cystic disorders are the dominant causes of neonatal ESKD. Unfortunately, although ESKD in the general pediatric population is associated with a low mortality rate, neonatal ESKD is not.1 Historically, mortality rates of neonates with ESKD have been near 20%–30%.1 Although the causes of this high rate of mortality are complex and multifactorial, over the past 2 decades, there have been a number of initiatives designed to reduce mortality associated with neonatal ESKD. These include improvements in antenatal diagnostic precision through fetal imaging and genetic testing and prenatal therapeutic interventions such as vesico-amniotic shunt placement in fetuses with lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO). With concurrent improvements in neonatal care and genetic diagnostic capture rates, innovative approaches to dialytic care of the newborn such as the Aquadex system (off label use for children weighing <20 kg), and more recently with the cardio-renal pediatric dialysis emergency machine (a renal replacement modality specifically designed for neonates, US Food and Drug Administration approved in 2020 for children weighing 2.5–10 kg), survival rates for newborn infants with ESKD has begun to improve.2,3

Although, appropriately so, the principal focus has been on improving outcomes of this patient population, what remains largely unexamined is the impact on the health care system of improved survival of infants and neonates with ESKD. Chronic dialysis in neonates and infants with ESKD can be especially challenging because of multiple comorbidities and the need for more intensified dialysis regimens with closer biochemical monitoring. In addition, dialysis in this patient population is often associated with a high risk of malnutrition, infection, and access complications.4,5 Although there are data available on health care expenditures for pediatric patients in general (0–18 years of age), there are little to no data available that examine the impact of neonatal ESKD on the health care system in terms of expenditures, hospitalizations, and subspecialty utilizations. These data are important not just for health care policy experts to ensure that resources are allocated to support this vulnerable population, but also to help inform and educate the caregivers of these patients of anticipated care needs. The primary aim of this study was to examine health care resource utilization from birth to age 2 years in neonates and infants with ESKD who were initiated on chronic KRT before discharge from the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).

Methods

We conducted a retrospective review of patients with ESKD cared for at Texas Children's Hospital (TCH) from 2011 to 2022. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (protocol H-42122). The primary inclusion criteria were patients who initiated chronic dialysis in the NICU at TCH and survival to discharge. We excluded patients who initiated dialysis before transfer to TCH or who initiated dialysis after discharge from the NICU. Patients were identified for inclusion using an internal database at TCH. ESKD was defined using US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services criteria including submission of a 2728 form. Data abstracted included patient demographics, initial length of hospital stay, comorbidities (including seizure disorders, cardiac involvement, and pulmonary disorders), subsequent admissions and LOS, pediatric subspecialist care consultations, and ambulatory subspecialty visits. Comorbidities were defined by subspecialty involvement and diagnosis codes during the initial admission. Medical complexity was further evaluated by the need for a gastrostomy tube, mechanical ventilation, physical or occupational therapy, and medication burden. Data were also collected on gross hospital charges related to the initial hospitalization, dialytic care, and facility/hospital charges after NICU discharge up to 2 years of age. Dialysis charges were identified using dialysis-specific cost centers during hospitalizations and cost centers assigned for our hospital-based chronic outpatient dialysis unit. Dialysis charges after neonatal discharge were aggregated using both acute inpatient and chronic outpatient dialysis cost centers. Cost data were calculated in 2023 dollars derived from gross charges adjusted for hospital specific cost-charge ratios (published by the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) for inpatient care, outpatient care, acute dialysis, and chronic outpatient dialysis and then adjusted for inflation using the annual consumer price index (published annually in the Federal Register). Initial hospitalization costs were converted into daily charges based on initial LOS for each patient. Postneonatal discharge costs were calculated using aggregate gross charges associated with subsequent admissions up to age 2 years. The postneonatal discharge costs were converted into average monthly care cost on the basis of the time from initial hospitalization discharge to 2 years of age. These cost data are a reflection of facility costs only and do not include physician fees.

Results

Clinical Data

In total, 19 patients met inclusion criteria, of whom 68% were male, 79% had a gestational age ≥37 weeks, and 90% had a birth weight ≥2500 g. The most common etiology of ESKD was LUTO, followed by genetic disorders (Table 1). A gastrostomy tube was placed in 95% of patients before NICU discharge; 79% of patients required some period of mechanical ventilation with an average duration of ventilator support of 27 days (interquartile range [IQR], 1.5–54.8). Infectious complications related to dialysis were frequent; 58% of patients had an episode of peritonitis, 21% had an exit site infection, and 21% had a central line-associated bloodstream infection documented. Significant comorbidities were common (Table 2) and included seizures/neurologic involvement (42%), bronchopulmonary dysplasia/pulmonary disorders (68%), and cardiac disease (37%).

Patients who did not meet initial screening criteria after chart review were excluded from the study. Initial screening was performed using an internal database at TCH and included patients with ESKD who started dialysis in the NICU. Two patients were excluded because of initiating dialysis at an outside hospital before transfer to TCH because we were unable to capture data related to non-TCH care. The patients who were excluded were transferred to TCH for higher level of care; however, they were comparable in terms of medical complexity, need for coordination of care with subspecialists, and LOS.

The average LOS for the initial hospitalization was 200 days (SD of 48 days) with a median of 192 days (IQR, 150–240) and an average of eight subspecialists consulted (excluding neonatologists) during their NICU admission (Table 2). The most common subspecialists consulted during the NICU admission included nephrology, urology, cardiology, pediatric surgery, and interventional radiology.

After NICU discharge, there was an average of 4.9 hospital admissions (IQR, 2–6.5) per patient before 2 years of age. The aggregate duration of inpatient care includes an average of 200 days (IQR, 150–240) for the initial hospitalization and an average of 42 days (IQR, 12–62) for subsequent hospitalizations up to age 2 years. Patients averaged 15 ambulatory clinic visits (IQR, 8.5–20), with an average of six subspecialists involved in subsequent care up to 2 years age (Table 2). The most common non-nephrology subspecialists involved in outpatient care included urology, pediatric surgery, pulmonology, neurology, and genetics. This translated on average to one clinic visit every 22 days when the patients were not hospitalized. These encounters are exclusive of routine dialysis clinic visits, chronic dialysis treatments, physical therapy, or occupational therapy, and 84% of patients qualified for physical or occupational therapy and 32% of patients required home health care after discharge.

On average, nine enteral medications were prescribed at discharge from the NICU, and 84% of patients required a catheter/access related intervention during either the initial or the subsequent hospitalizations.

The dialysis modality at discharge was heavily weighted toward peritoneal dialysis (84%), with fewer patients requiring chronic hemodialysis (16%). Overall, 89% (n=17) of patients discharged from the NICU with ESKD survived to age 2 years; the two patients who did not survive were noted to have complications related to sepsis and died at age 10 months and 12 months, respectively. One patient (5%) received a living donor kidney transplant at age 20 months.

Financial Data

The median cost of care for the initial hospitalization was $1.2 million ($1 million nondialysis costs [84%] and $190,000 dialytic costs [16%]). The median cost of care for the initial hospitalization that did not include dialysis was $1,032,633 (IQR, 722,690–1,288,345), with a median daily cost of care of $6224 (IQR, 5142–6993). The dialytic cost associated with the initial hospitalization was $190,757 (IQR, 145,200–311,773) or 15.6% of the total cost (Table 3).

Discussion

Pediatric ESKD is a condition that can be associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Our study highlights the significant medical complexity and substantial health care utilization in infants with ESKD. We found that infants with ESKD have complex care needs that can be associated with higher financial expenditures compared with older children with ESKD on the basis of United States Renal Data System data.6

In our cohort, the median cost of care for infantile ESKD was $1,223,389 for the initial hospitalization of which $190,757 or 15.6% was related to dialytic care. Other neonatal disorders with high cost and morbidity, such as surgical necrotizing enterocolitis, severe retinopathy, or severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia, are reported to have much lower median NICU cost: $430,860, $413,825, and $399,495 respectively.7 In fact, the index NICU stay for patients with infantile ESKD was two to three times more costly compared with other medically complex conditions with one of the factors being the higher LOS in the ESKD population.8

One surprising finding in our study is that, although the contribution of dialytic costs was only 15% for the initial hospitalization, subsequent median dialytic care costs were $15,920 per month or more than $190,000 per person per year on an annualized basis. This is dramatically higher than both Medicare expenditures for patients with ESKD and dialytic care for older children. In 2021, for children 0–17 years with ESKD, the inflation adjusted per person per year fee for service expenditures was $54,482 for Medicare only and $99,743 for both Medicare and Medicaid.6

It should be recognized that there are limitations to this study. First, these data represent a single-center experience and thus institutional care practice patterns and cost data may not be reflective of those across the United States as a whole. However, TCH is the largest children's hospital in the United States with an ethnically, racially, and economically diverse patient population, and these data may provide foundational information that will help foster additional more broadly based studies, as well as inform both resource allocation and public policy discussions nationally. Second, although the cost data for the initial period of patient care is likely comprehensive, financial data after discharge likely underrepresents costs as some patients may have subspecialty care or primary care outside of the TCH system. Patients who initiated dialysis at an outside hospital were also excluded as we were unable to capture data related to non-TCH care. In addition, some health care resources such as physical therapy, immunizations, pharmaceuticals, and laboratory and diagnostic testing that were obtained outside of the TCH system are also not captured. Similarly, physician fees or professional charges are not captured as the primary focus of this study was on nonphysician health care resource utilization. Thus, it is likely that the data related to gross charges in this study underrepresent the total cost of care delivery for this highly vulnerable and complex patient population.

Infants with ESKD often have nonrenal comorbidities necessitating coordination of care with multiple subspecialists.9 Most patients in our cohort had comorbidities requiring follow-up with multiple subspecialists both in the inpatient and outpatient settings. Seizures or neurologic involvement was noted in 42% of patients. Other common comorbidities included pulmonary disorders or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (68%) and cardiac involvement (37%).

Infants with ESKD also require multidisciplinary care including collaboration with physical therapists, occupational therapists, child life specialists, and social workers. Home health services are frequently needed after discharge. Furthermore, infants on dialysis require increased resources like recombinant growth hormone injections to promote growth.4 Interventions such as gastrostomy tube placement are routinely indicated, and formula prescription requires frequent assessments by a specialized dietician to ensure adequate intake to meet nutritional needs.9 This level of medical complexity was highlighted in our study with 95% of patients requiring gastrostomy feeding assistance. A vast majority (84%) required occupational and/or physical therapy services after discharge. A third of our patients (32%) were discharged with home health nursing support.

In addition, infants with ESKD require coordination with a specialized team of dialysis nurses and physicians to ensure optimal dialytic care.2 Peritoneal dialysis was the most common dialysis modality at discharge for patients in our cohort. This is comparable with previous reports which note that 85%–92% of infants with ESKD worldwide were prescribed peritoneal dialysis as the initial dialysis modality.9 Peritoneal dialysis is generally considered the preferred modality for younger children as it is associated with improved preservation of residual kidney function and allows for more liberal fluid and sodium restrictions.10 Advantages of peritoneal dialysis include avoidance of vascular access which is an important consideration as vascular access complications are seen more frequently in younger patients with ESKD.11 Preservation of vasculature is also essential for future access and potential kidney transplantation. In addition, peritoneal dialysis provides the option to provide dialysis at home which decreases disruptions to daily activities.10 Hemodialysis was used less frequently in our cohort with only 16% of patients discharged on this modality. Hemodialysis in infants with ESKD requires increased resources with more intensive dialysis regimens (four times/week) and nursing expertise for care delivery.9

Previous studies have demonstrated a higher rate of infectious complications and catheter dysfunction in this patient population with 47% of patients requiring vascular access replacement.11 Most of our patients (85%) required dialysis associated catheter revisions. Collaboration with interventional radiologists and pediatric access surgeons is required to address these complications. Patients in our cohort were also noted to have a significant number of catheter-associated infections and peritonitis (Table 1). Reports on the basis of data from the Standardizing Care to Improve Outcomes in Pediatric End Stage Renal Disease Collaborative found that infants with ESKD on peritoneal dialysis have an average of 0.76 episodes of peritonitis per patient-year.12 Episodes of peritonitis were associated with longer initial hospital stays and decreased overall survival.12 Frequent catheter-associated infections can lead to further complications and an increased need for resources related to dialysis.

Previous studies investigating pediatric ESKD in European and Asian countries also highlight the medical complexity associated with this patient population. Jiang et al. performed a cross-sectional survey of children with CKD in China including 44 patients <1 year old and noted significant comorbidities and resource utilization associated with dialysis.13 Hattori et al. investigated pediatric ESKD including survival outcomes in a Japanese cohort and noted similar findings.14 A survey of the Italian pediatric dialysis registry also outlines the complexity of these patients including significant comorbidities, infections, and vascular access complications.15,16 Although there have been multiple national and multinational studies investigating pediatric ESKD, there are little to no cost data available for nondialysis resource utilization. It can also be difficult to interpret these data and compare cost of health care because of significant variation in health care models in different countries. In addition, these studies demonstrate an inherent low population number for infants with ESKD.

It is critical to acknowledge the impact of infantile ESKD on caregivers beyond the financial cost. The duration of the initial hospitalization averaged 200 days, with an additional five hospitalizations encompassing on average 42 additional days of hospitalization. This translates into on average 242 days of inpatient care during the child's first 730 days of life. Outpatient care burden is also quite heavy with an average of 15 subspecialist visits per patient after discharge in our cohort. For the infants on peritoneal dialysis, there are at minimum monthly peritoneal dialysis clinic visits, but often more frequent appointments. For patients on hemodialysis who are dialyzed typically 4 days a week because of their metabolic demands and fluid restrictions, this translated into 16 hours a week on dialysis in addition to travel time. On average, there was one additional pediatric subspecialty clinic visit a month. Most patients required additional outpatient visits for physical therapy/occupational therapy and routine primary care. Scheduling follow-up with subspecialists and interdisciplinary team is especially important for infants with ESKD to address the unique challenges associated with their medical, physical, and cognitive comorbidities.

Caregiver responsibility is an important consideration given the time-consuming nature of administering home peritoneal dialysis, frequent outpatient hemodialysis visits, and complicated feeding and medication regimens, including growth hormone and erythropoietin stimulating agent injections. Most of our patients (>67%) did not qualify for supplemental home nursing support and hence the care burden on parents and families is substantial. Previous studies have also demonstrated that parents of children with CKD and ESKD frequently experience severe caregiver burden including significant mental stress in addition to the financial burden related to catastrophic health care expenditure.17

In summary, infants with ESKD who survive to NICU discharge require significant health care utilization including multidisciplinary care, hospitalizations, dialytic care, and increased financial expenditures. It is important to focus on resource allocation required to address this specific cohort's complex care needs. Our findings highlight the need for additional resources including optimized skilled nursing to patient ratios, access to infant tailored biomedical devices and dialysis access systems, transportation services, early intervention services, and home health nursing. In addition, the significant health care charges noted for infants with ESKD may represent the need for increased charge reimbursement and can be used to help guide health care policy. Additional studies focusing on parent/caregiver-reported outcome measures can be used by nephrologists and policymakers to help guide cost-effective resource allocation to ensure optimal patient outcomes and improve quality of life.18
