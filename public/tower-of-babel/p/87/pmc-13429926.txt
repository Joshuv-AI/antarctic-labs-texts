PAX8 controls proximal tubule epithelial identity and stress response through epigenetic modification of distal regulatory elements
Authors: Asha C Telang, Ma Wan, Steven K Orban, Jenna Stahlecker, Jenna T Ference-Salo, Don A Delker, Jonathan M Shillingford, Madison C McElliott, Ann M Laszczyk, Rohan N Vashi, Abhijit Parolia, Austin L Nguyen, Ryan J Snyder, Gregory R Dressler, Jason A Watts, Jeffrey A Beamish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe kidney proximal tubule undergoes dramatic cell-state transformations during recovery from injury, serving as a unique model to study the dynamic transcriptional regulation of cell identity. The DNA-binding transcription factor paired box-8 (PAX8) contributes to proximal tubule development and recovery from injury. Here we report a mouse proximal-tubule-derived Pax2-null, Pax8-conditional cell line that enables assessment of the genome-wide effect of PAX8 on transcription and cellular function. PAX8-depleted cells lose the differentiated proximal tubule phenotype. Re-expression of Pax8 rescues the epithelial phenotype and restores expression of genes lost following PAX8 depletion. This model recapitulates the expression patterns and stress-resistance of PAX8-deficient proximal tubule cells in vivo, justifying its use to study the molecular mechanisms of PAX8 function. Across the genome, the changes in transcription caused by PAX8 loss are linked with PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements, where PAX8 can either activate or repress transcription. Among PAX8-activated genes, expression is tightly linked to PAX8-dependent H3K4me1 and H3K27ac at distal regulatory elements. In contrast, PAX8-repressed genes showed little change in these marks. These results indicate that PAX8 regulates enhancer histone marks dynamically and reversibly across the genome to control proximal-tubule-specific gene expression patterns.Keywords: gene transcription regulation, transcription factor, epigenetics, kidney, proximal tubule epithelial cell, cell identity

The highly specialized epithelia of the proximal tubules are responsible for most of the solute reabsorption required for normal kidney function. These cells are vulnerable to a range of ischemic and toxic insults, such that their dysfunction contributes to acute kidney injury, a clinical syndrome complicating up to 20% of hospital admissions (1). The proximal tubule lacks a stem cell population, and most proximal tubule cells retain the capacity to regenerate after injury. During this process, genes needed for highly specialized transport and metabolic functions are repressed to enable cell proliferation, then restored during recovery (2, 3). These transformations provide a unique opportunity to investigate the molecular and genetic mechanisms used to establish and maintain specialized epithelial phenotypes.

Recovery of specialized gene expression during proximal tubule epithelial cell regeneration resembles the transformation of the metanephric mesenchyme into the nephron’s epithelium during kidney development (4, 5). These observations suggest that developmental lineage-determining transcription factors may contribute to cell state changes during regeneration (4, 5), including the paired box (PAX) DNA-binding transcription factors PAX2 and PAX8 (6, 7). In the mature proximal tubule, PAX8 is the dominant family member expressed (8). During proximal tubule regeneration, PAX8 expression increases (9, 10). Computational analyses that associate motif-containing cis-regulatory elements with gene expression have linked activation of PAX8 gene targets with cell fate after injury (2, 11, 12). Furthermore, pre-injury proximal tubule knockout of Pax8, along with Pax2 to prevent compensation, reduces susceptibility to ischemic injury and facilitates recovery (9, 13). PAX8 is also involved in cystogenesis in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease and in renal cell carcinoma, underscoring its fundamental importance to the function of kidney epithelial cells (14, 15). Thus, determining the mechanisms of PAX8 function not only will clarify ways that gene expression is controlled during proximal tubule regeneration but also may reveal general processes that maintain cell type specificity across many tissues.

PAX8 shares close homology with PAX2, which has been studied extensively in the kidney, suggesting shared mechanisms of function. Both proteins contain an N-terminal DNA-binding domain, an octapeptide domain, and a C-terminal domain rich in serine and threonine. When phosphorylated, these C-terminal residues facilitate interaction with the adapter PAX transactivation-domain interacting protein (PTIP), thus linking PAX2 and PAX8 with the lysine methyltransferase 2C/D (KMT2C/D) complex to activate transcription at selected gene promoters (16, 17, 18). In contrast, the transducin-like enhancer protein 4 (TLE4) can bind to the octapeptide domain present in PAX family members where it dephosphorylates the PAX c-terminal domain by recruiting the protein phosphatase PPM1B (19). These changes both disrupt the PTIP-mediated link to the KMT2C/D complex and recruit the Polycomb Repressor Complex to deposit H3K27me3 silencing marks (20, 21, 22, 23). Therefore, PAX proteins can both activate and repress target gene transcription by altering epigenetic marks. These mechanisms have been well-established at selected promoters and synthetic PAX-reporter constructs, especially for the PAX2 protein, and contribute to lineage determination during developmental transformations (24). However, the genome-wide transcriptional responses orchestrated by PAX8 in mature kidney cells and their functional significance remain poorly understood.

Here we report a Pax2-null, Pax8-conditional proximal tubule epithelial cell line that retains features of the renal proximal tubule and enables detailed assessment of the genome-wide effect of PAX8 on transcription and its functional consequences. PAX8-depleted cells lose the differentiated proximal tubule phenotype, which can be rescued by PAX8 re-expression. Mechanistically, PAX8 depletion alters enhancer histone marks at PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements that are linked to changes in transcription. These results indicate that PAX8 can function reversibly at enhancer elements to control proximal-tubule-specific gene expression patterns.

Results

PAX8 maintains the epithelial morphology of confluent proximal tubule epithelial cells

To study the function of PAX8, we generated a line of Pax2-null proximal tubule epithelial cells with inducible genetic deletion of the wild-type Pax8 gene (Fig. 1A). Cells were derived from mice with a constitutive tamoxifen-responsive CreERT2 and with LoxP sites flanking exons that encode the paired domains of Pax2 and Pax8, as previously described (8). In the native proximal tubules of wildtype mice, there is little expression of Pax2 (Fig. S1). However, proximal tubule cells upregulate Pax2 during primary culture (25). To avoid potential compensation by PAX2 in conditional cells derived from the transgenic mice, we identified clones harboring spontaneous recombination at both Pax2 alleles, which, for unknown reasons, is highly sensitive to Cre-mediated excision, yielding Pax2-null, Pax8-conditional cells (Fig. S1).

At baseline, the cells develop confluent cobblestone epithelial monolayers. Treatment with 4-hydroxytamoxifen for 2 to 3 days induces a 1000-fold reduction in wildtype Pax8 transcript and loss of PAX8 protein (Fig. 1, B–D). Concurrently with the loss of PAX8, cells lose their cobblestone morphology (Fig. 1E) and flatten (Fig. S3). Changes in cell morphology were not observed in ethanol vehicle-treated controls after 6 days nor in the mouse proximal tubule cell line BUMPT, which does not harbor a conditional Pax8 allele, treated with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (Fig. S2). These observations indicate that PAX8 is necessary to maintain the epithelial morphology of proximal tubule cells in culture. We expanded two clonal cell lines that shared these features, and selected one line, hereafter called “PT-22,” as the basis for further detailed studies of PAX8 function in proximal tubule cells.

PAX8 maintains the differentiated gene expression signature of proximal tubule epithelial cells

To characterize the molecular targets of PAX8, we measured the bulk transcriptomes of the PT-22 line 2, 6, and 12 days after Pax8 deletion (Fig. 2A). We detected 520 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, 2-fold up or down, Padj ≤ 0.05) 2 days following tamoxifen administration. The number of DEGs increased to 1884 by 12 days. For many genes, the magnitude of change was increased (Fig. 2B). For example, Acta2 (encoding alpha smooth muscle actin) increased from 4.3-fold at 2 days to 94-fold at 12 days, relative to untreated controls. The principal component (PC) projections of the transcriptional signatures followed a smooth progression, suggesting a genome-wide transition in the transcriptional program after PAX8 loss (Fig. 2C). We also performed transcriptional profiling of an independent Pax8-conditional cell line, generated in parallel with the PT-22 line, and found the two lines cluster tightly before and after PAX8 depletion, confirming that these changes were not cell line-specific (Fig. S4).

We next identified pathways activated and repressed by PAX8 loss by performing Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of differentially expressed genes in the PT-22 line using the library of Molecular Signatures Database Hallmark gene sets (26). This analysis revealed activation of inflammatory and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition pathways, suggesting a loss of proximal tubule identity (Fig. 2D). Epithelial-specific genes, including Slc6a18 and Cdh16, were lost, whereas genes associated with injured proximal tubule cells, such as Vim (encoding Vimentin) and Acta2, were induced (Fig. 2E) (27, 28). Transcript and protein products of Cdh16 and Acta2, followed the same pattern when compared with ethanol vehicle controls at 6 days (Figs. S2 and S5). Transcription of Cdh16 and Acta2 was unchanged in tamoxifen-treated BUMPT cells (Fig. S2). Overall, these changes recapitulate the early response of proximal tubule cells to injury in vivo (2, 11, 29) and suggest PAX8 inactivation contributes to this process. Furthermore, PAX8 depletion resulted in reduced expression of a gene set previously validated to define differentiated proximal tubule cells (25, 30, 31, 32). Decreased expression of these genes became more pronounced with time following PAX8 depletion, consistent with a time-dependent loss of proximal-tubule identity (Fig. S6).

We also observed repression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling and oxidative phosphorylation. Proximal tubule cells perform most of the solute transport in the kidney, which has high energetic demands. We next assessed if decreased expression of metabolism genes following PAX8-depletion resulted in a measurable change in cellular energetic flux. We found both basal oxygen consumption and glycolysis were significantly lower following PAX8-depletion (Figs. 3, A and B and S7). However, oxidative spare capacity was unchanged, suggesting decreased flux rather than an impairment in electron transport chain function (Fig. 3C). Consistent with that hypothesis, we also observed a decrease in activation of the mTORC pathway, which is a master regulator of global metabolic flux. We measured phosphorylated ribosomal protein S6 (pS6), a marker of mTORC activity, and found the pS6/S6 ratio was significantly lower following PAX8-depletion (Fig. 3, D and E). Combined, these findings functionally validate our differential gene expression analyses and indicate that PAX8 is necessary for maintaining gene expression that defines mature proximal tubules.

PAX8 depletion in PT-22 induces resistance to both ferroptotic and oxidative stress

Previously, we observed that the depletion of both PAX2 and PAX8 from the proximal tubule induces resilience to subsequent ischemic kidney injury (9, 13). The sets of up- and down-regulated genes that characterize proximal tubule PAX2/8-depletion in the native proximal tubule S3 segment were also enriched among differentially expressed genes in PAX8-depleted PT-22 cells (Fig. S8). These similarities were evident despite the known transcriptional and metabolic differences of cultured proximal tubule cells compared with native proximal tubules (25, 31, 33). Therefore, we asked if loss of PAX8 in PT-22 similarly leads to resilience against stress. To test this hypothesis, we treated cells with either RSL-3, a glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inhibitor that induces ferroptosis, or with buthionine sulfoximine (BSO), which depletes glutathione and causes redox stress. PAX8-depleted PT-22 cells exhibited significantly greater resistance to these stressors compared with PT-22 control cells (Fig. 4, A–C). These results indicate that PAX8-loss in the PT-22 cell line causes gene expression and phenotypic changes that recapitulate salient features of proximal tubule cells in vivo.

PAX8 re-expression rescues epithelial phenotype

Next, we asked if the transcriptional changes following PAX8 depletion could be rescued by re-expression of PAX8. To test this hypothesis, we generated a modified PT-22 line transduced with a Tet-on expression system linked to a hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged Pax8a transgene (Fig. 5A). We selected a mixed population of transduced cells with random integration of the transgene construct, then used it to synchronize depletion of PAX8 for 6 days, followed by re-expression of PAX8a for 6 days. In the mixed bulk population, expression of the Pax8a transgene reached 11 ± 1% of baseline Pax8 transcript levels on average (Fig. S9), and PAX8 protein was replaced with HA-tagged PAX8a (Fig. 5B). Remarkably, de-differentiated PAX8-depleted cells regained cobblestone epithelial morphology (Fig. 5C). Doxycycline treatment of untransduced PT-22 cells did not show these effects (Fig. S9). The level of PAX8 re-expression varied from cell-to-cell in this mixed transduced population, enabling us to localize restoration of epithelial morphology only to islands of cells with strong PAX8a-HA re-expression (Fig. S10). This indicates that PAX8 function in maintaining epithelial morphology is cell-autonomous.

Next, we asked if PAX8 re-expression can reverse gene expression changes. Many genes that are differentially expressed following PAX8 depletion in vivo (9) and in the PT-22 line, including Aadat and Cp, showed reversal of expression after PAX8 rescue (Fig. 5D). Across the genome, the Z-score-averaged expression of all genes downregulated after PAX8 loss was restored (Fig. 5E), and proximal-tubule-selective genes were re-expressed (Fig. S9). In contrast, genes upregulated by PAX8 depletion, on average, revealed a slight expression increase even after PAX8 rescue (Fig. 5F). However, some reversal of the upregulated genes was apparent when only genes with the strongest dependence on PAX8 were considered (>4-fold after PAX8 depletion, Fig. S9). These results indicate that PT-22 cells recapitulate the gene expression changes that occur following PAX8 loss in vivo and demonstrate that PAX8 can reversibly regulate the gene expression that governs epithelial phenotype.

PAX8 regulates gene expression through epigenetic modification at distal regulatory elements

PAX8 functions in lineage specification, maintenance of proximal tubule identity, and tuning metabolic activity, yet the mechanism by which these factors regulate gene expression remains unclear. Prior work in non-renal culture models has indicated that PAX2 and PAX8 alter transcription via trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3), which is an active promoter mark, or lysine 27 (H3K27me3), which is an inactivating mark, depending on the available cofactors (16, 20, 21, 22). Our initial hypothesis was that loss of PAX8 from PT-22 cells would reduce the level of H3K4me3 signal and lead to accumulation of H3K27me3. There were a few genes, such as Cdh16, where promoter H3K4me3 marks were lost, and transcription was decreased after PAX8 depletion (Fig. S11). However, when evaluated genome-wide, there was minimal change in H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 histone marks before and after PAX8 depletion (Fig. S11), including specifically segregating H3K4me3 abundance at PAX8-bound promoters compared with promoters with minimal PAX8 binding (Fig. 6A). The observation of widespread gene expression changes in the absence of substantial changes in H3K4me3 patterns suggested transcription regulation by PAX8 in proximal tubule cells is largely independent of epigenetic regulation at promoters.

To investigate alternative possibilities, we used Chipseeker (34) to categorize each PAX8 binding site according to the nearest gene and whether the site was promoter-proximal (TSS ± 3 kb) or distal (TSS ± 3–100 kb). Then, we binned expressed genes as being associated with proximal or distal PAX8 binding and compared expression changes following PAX8-depletion (Figs. 6B and S12). Both up- and downregulated genes following PAX8 depletion were more likely to be associated with distal PAX8 binding sites (χ2 P = 1 × 10−95). Genes with PAX8 occupancy only at the promoter were disproportionately unchanged (Fig. S12). These findings indicate that PAX8 regulates transcription in proximal tubule cells primarily through distal regulatory elements. We next identified enriched sequence motifs at distal PAX8 peaks, grouped according to the expression change of the nearest gene following PAX8 depletion. Distal PAX8-bound sites associated with up- and downregulated genes were enriched with PAX8 motifs, though these motifs appeared in only a minority of loci. Motifs for AP-1, TEAD, HNF1b, and RUNX family transcription factors were also present at many PAX8-bound loci regardless of the expression change of nearby genes (Supplemental Data).

Distal enhancer elements are marked by histone modifications that are distinct from promoters. The lysine methyltransferase 2C/D (KMT2C/D) complex, which can interact with PAX8 via PTIP, can mediate monomethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me1) and indirectly facilitate acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27ac) at active enhancers (35, 36, 37). Therefore, we measured the abundance of the enhancer marks H3K4me1 and H3K27ac following PAX8 depletion using cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag). This approach revealed loss of both H3K4me1 and H3K27ac at PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements following PAX8 depletion (Fig. 6C). One example is Aadat, a gene involved in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and lysine metabolism that is downregulated following PAX2/8 depletion in proximal tubules in vivo and in PT-22 cells. PAX8 binds both the Aadat promoter and a distal enhancer. Following PAX8 depletion in PT-22 cells, there was no change in H3K4me3 at the Aadat promoter, whereas H3K4me1 and H3K27ac were lost at the distal regulatory element concurrently with loss of PAX8-dependent transcription (Fig. 6D). These data demonstrate that PAX8-depletion leads to changes in enhancer-associated histone modifications more than promoter-associated histone marks.

Next, we asked how altered histone marks at distal regulatory elements were linked to changes in gene expression. We found that PAX8 depletion results in nearly the same number of up- and downregulated genes, and both are associated with PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements. For example, the gene Cp, which is involved in iron metabolism, was strongly upregulated in the proximal tubule in vivo and in PT-22 cells following PAX2/8 depletion. Cp has a PAX8-bound element approximately 3 kb upstream of its promoter, but, in contrast to Aadat, PAX8 depletion causes a small increase in H3K4me1 and H3K27ac signals at this site and increased transcription (Fig. S13). We subdivided distal PAX8-bound loci into three groups according to change in expression of the nearest gene (up, down, or no change) and determined the abundance of histone marks (Figs. 7 and S14). This analysis revealed relative preservation of H3K4me1 and H3K27ac at PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements near genes upregulated after PAX8 loss and significant loss of these enhancer marks near downregulated genes (Fig. 7, A–D). Furthermore, distal PAX8-bound loci associated with downregulated genes showed a distribution skewed toward a strong depletion of H3K4me1 and H3K27ac marks following PAX8 loss (Fig. 7, A and C). More than half of PAX8-bound distal elements near downregulated genes were depleted of H3K27ac more than 2-fold (Fig. 7D).

We next assessed if PAX8 regulates transcription by altering chromatin accessibility, which is a key mechanism that defines cell states. Early following PAX8 depletion, accessibility was lost at previously PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements but not promoters (Fig. S15). However, changes in accessibility weakly correlated with expression of nearby genes. PAX8-targeted distal regulatory elements near genes upregulated after PAX8 loss retained a slightly higher relative accessibility compared with unchanged genes (at 3 days following PAX8 depletion relative to baseline, median 0.86 versus 0.80, Padj = 1.6 × 10−10, Wilcoxon rank sum test, Fig. 7E). There was no significant difference in accessibility at peaks near downregulated genes compared with unchanged genes (median 0.78 versus 0.80, Padj = 0.3, Wilcoxon rank sum test, Fig. 7E). Later after PAX8 depletion, accessibility at PAX8-bound distal elements decreased further overall (Fig. S15) but was similar for all loci regardless of the expression of the nearest gene (median 0.60 versus 0.59 upregulated versus unchanged, Padj = 0.005, Wilcoxon rank sum test, and 0.57 versus 0.59 downregulated versus unchanged, Padj = 0.40, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that while PAX8 contributes to enhancer accessibility, short-term PAX8-dependent activation or repression of transcription is mediated by other mechanisms.

Discussion

How cells maintain their identity through DNA-binding proteins and epigenetic modifications at distal enhancers is a fundamental question in biology that remains to be clarified. Our report highlights how one such protein, PAX8, regulates the identity of proximal tubule epithelial cells via epigenetic modifications. We developed a Pax2-null, Pax8-conditional cell line, PT-22, which retains key features of proximal tubule epithelial cells in vivo and thus serves as a model to study the mechanisms of PAX8 function in the proximal tubule. These studies revealed that, at baseline, PAX8 simultaneously activates proximal-tubule-specific genes and represses stress-response genes. PAX8 reversibly controls proximal tubule epithelial identity primarily through regulation at distal elements rather than at gene promoters of lineage-selective genes. PAX8 functions by regulating active enhancer histone modifications. Combined, these findings highlight how a single transcription factor could dynamically contribute to guiding epithelial cells through injury-recovery processes by modulating enhancer epigenetics.

These studies provide new insights into the functions of PAX-proteins in the maintenance of epithelial phenotypes. Prior work on the PAX8 homologue PAX2 has focused on a small set of synthetic expression constructs to deduce key epigenetic events that can activate or repress target genes (16, 18, 20, 21). These studies highlighted how PAX2 mediates deposition of H3K4me3 activating marks or H3K27me3 silencing marks that are essential to imprint the repertoire of cell-type-specific gene expression in the developing organism (24). PAX5, which is highly homologous to PAX2 and PAX8 but not expressed in the kidney epithelium, also can use the same mechanisms interchangeably in vitro and can support kidney development in PAX2-deficient mice (7, 18, 22, 38, 39).

Our work highlights a complementary role for PAX8 in maintaining epigenetic modifications at enhancer elements. PAX8 is linked with KMT2C/D (also known as MLL3/4) via PTIP. This interaction can increase H3K4me3 at PAX-bound promoters (16, 22). However, the KMT2C/D complex can also deposit H3K4me1 marks at active enhancers and indirectly facilitate H3K27ac deposition (35, 36, 37). In post-developmental proximal tubule cells, our data strongly suggest that the proximal-tubule-specific repertoire of expressed genes depends on PAX8-mediated activation of distal enhancer elements. Both PAX8, in the context of renal cell carcinoma, and PAX5, in the context of B-cell development, have been linked with prominent enhancer-mediated regulation of gene expression (14, 38, 40, 41, 42), suggesting this mechanism of action is conserved among PAX2/5/8 family members.

Several additional observations suggest that in mature cell types, PAX8 function can be dynamic, especially for genes that depend on PAX8 for ongoing expression. The robust and near-universal re-expression of PAX8-dependent genes following restoration of ∼10% of baseline PAX8 levels indicates these genes are highly sensitive to PAX8. Second, changes in accessibility of PAX8-bound loci are not strongly linked to gene expression. Rather, expression is linked to enhancer-associated histone modifications at these sites. These observations suggest that PAX8 does not regulate gene expression by modifying the repertoire of possible enhancer sites but instead by tailoring the activity of each site via histone modifications. Such a model would allow cells to rapidly downregulate the genome-wide pattern of proximal tubule expression by inactivation of PAX8 yet retain the ability to restore the transcriptome by reactivating PAX8. This pattern follows the cell-state changes that occur in successful regeneration following proximal tubule injury (43). Although computational models have implicated PAX8 in proximal tubule regeneration (11, 12), whether PAX8 functions in this process remains an area of active investigation.

Surprisingly, we also discovered a near symmetric upregulation of many genes following PAX8 depletion, suggesting PAX8 can simultaneously function as an activator at some loci and a repressor at others. While these changes may represent a secondary response to PAX8 loss, there are several observations that suggest that these genes are PAX8 targets. Genes induced upon PAX8 depletion were just as likely to have PAX8-bound distal regulatory elements as genes where expression is lost upon PAX8 depletion. Also, recent work using the auxin-inducible degron system, which depletes the related-PAX5 protein within 6 h, in developing B-cells also finds near-equal up- and downregulation of target genes (40).

In some contexts, PAX2/5/8 transcriptional repression can occur when TLE4 binds to the octapeptide sequence (19) and dephosphorylates the c-terminal domain of PAX proteins by recruiting PPM1B (20, 21). This process disrupts the PTIP-mediated link to the KMT2C/D complex and recruits the Polycomb Repressor Complex leading to deposition of H3K27me3 silencing marks (20, 21). This model likely has developmental significance in settings where an entire repertoire of gene targets is silenced, such as the transformation of developing tubular epithelium into podocytes (24). Our work suggests there must be complementary mechanisms of PAX8-mediated transcriptional repression, since we observed no changes in H3K27me3 following PAX8 loss, nor are symmetric up and down changes in gene expression consistent with a model controlled by available cofactors that displace PTIP across all loci. While ongoing work seeks to better define this mechanism, it is unlikely that H3K27ac or H3K4me1 enhancer marks or chromatin accessibility are responsible since neither was increased following PAX8 loss. Furthermore, the reversal of genes induced by PAX8 depletion was less robust following PAX8 rescue. This observation suggests that repression of these genes either requires higher levels of PAX8 or that PAX8 loss induces epigenetic changes that PAX8 reexpression cannot rescue.

These studies also have important implications for the modeling of transcriptional networks during proximal tubule regeneration. First, despite the homogenous nature of the cell line, paired-domain motifs were present in only a minority of empirically measured PAX8-loci. This is consistent with the well-known degeneracy and complexity of PAX-family DNA binding (44, 45, 46). Since most transcriptional network models depend on binding motifs, our data suggest that PAX8-associated predictions are incomplete. Second, PAX8-bound loci can have either a positive or negative influence on target gene expression, which is complex and not currently predictable. Many target genes in our cell line and in vivo (9, 13) display opposite PAX8-dependency in renal cell carcinoma (14), highlighting how context can define PAX8 function. Furthermore, it is notable that our GSEA studies for proximal tubule genes are markedly polarized, with few targets ranking in the middle of the expressed gene list. This suggests PAX8 targets most of these genes, but its function is dichotomous. While newer strategies correlate transcription factor binding sites with gene expression to predict the effects of each locus (12), data in our study help to further refine our approach to modeling PAX8 function in the regenerating kidney and other PAX-expressing cells.

One key limitation of our model is that it depends on cell culture. Proximal tubule cells rapidly downregulate the expression of most proximal-tubule-selective genes and upregulate injury-response genes in culture concurrently with a profound shift away from oxidative metabolism (25, 33, 47). During the derivation of the PT-22 line, undoubtedly the epigenetic landscape and the PAX8 cistrome were altered compared with proximal tubules in vivo. Despite these limitations, our cell culture model recapitulated both up- and downregulated transcriptional features of PAX2/8-depleted S3 proximal tubules in vivo (9) and retained resistance to ferroptosis and altered metabolism. These features indicate that this cell line is a physiologically relevant model of PAX8 function in vivo that enables synchronized and homogenous PAX8 depletion, which cannot be achieved in mouse or organoid models. Furthermore, our work uses the proximity between distal regulatory elements and gene promoters to infer generalized relationships between PAX8 chromatin localization and transcription. Though proximity is a strong predictor of the gene regulated by an enhancer, the factors that influence enhancer-gene relationships remain an area of active investigation (48, 49, 50, 51). Therefore, distance-based inferences about the regulatory relationship between enhancers and their nearby genes may not be accurate at all locations.

Together, these findings highlight the critical role PAX8 plays in orchestrating gene expression in proximal-tubule-derived cells, suggests roles for PAX8 in normal physiology and injury response, and highlights the key epigenetic function of PAX8 at enhancers.

Experimental procedures

Animals

All studies involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Michigan and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. This study used female mice with conditional Pax2 and Pax8 alleles and heterozygous for the Rosa-CreERT2 construct, as previously published (8). The conditional alleles in these mice were designed to remove key exons in the DNA-binding paired domain of each protein and induce a frame shift, resulting in a non-functional, non-DNA-binding protein.

Cell line generation

The kidney cortex, which is predominantly comprised of proximal tubules, was harvested from female mice, was minced finely with a razor blade, and was transferred into 25 ml of DMEM, with Ca+ and Mg + lacking phenol red. The slurry was digested for 30 min with 300 μl of Liberase (1 mg/ml) at 37 °C while stirring. The slurry was decanted through a 250 mm filter and collected on ice. The flow-through was washed 3× with cold DMEM without Ca+ and Mg+. Cell pellets were then collected at 1200 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Cells were then plated in UltraMDCK media (Lonza) containing 60 mg/ml EGF, 0.5X ITES (Gibco A4000046401), and penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml). Epithelial cell islands were observed growing out of isolated tubule segments. Cells were then immortalized using a temperature sensitive SV40-T-Antigen expressing retrovirus and selected with G418 at 32 °C. Surviving cells were re-plated at 1 to 2 cells per well in 96-well plates in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 400 μg/ml G418, and Pen-Strep. Clones harboring spontaneous recombination at both Pax2 alleles, yielding Pax2-null, Pax8-conditional cells, were selected for further expansion. Clonally derived lines were tested for their ability to delete Pax8 with 1 μM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT). Genotypes were confirmed by PCR. (Table S1).

Cell culture

Following derivation, cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 3% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml) at 37 °C,5% CO2 unless otherwise specified. Early passages and some experiments used 10% FBS, as indicated. Medium was changed every 2 to 3 days, and cells were passaged every 4 to 7 days with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA. PAX8 was depleted from cells with the addition of 1 μM 4-OHT (administered from a 1000× concentrate in ethanol) for 1 to 6 days. Ethanol alone was used as a vehicle control. Clonal lines were expanded from single-cell clones and cryopreserved in aliquots. Subsequent experiments were performed within 12 passages of thawing.

The immortalized mouse proximal cell line BUMPT was obtained from Dr Wilfred Lieberthal (52) and were cultured under the same conditions as PT-22.

PAX8 re-expression

The mouse Pax8a transgene with an in-frame c-terminal addition of a hemagglutinin tag was cloned into the pTRIPZ plasmid (Addgene #206981) downstream of the tetracycline responsive element. A blasticidin selection cassette and an mCherry reporter were also cloned downstream of the rtTA3 gene. This plasmid was used to create a lentiviral vector. Subconfluent PT-22 cells were transduced for 12 h, then after 2 days selected with 5 μg/ml blasticidin. Surviving cells were subcultured then used for experiments. Culture medium for subsequent experiments retained blasticidin. Pax8a-HA was induced by addition of 1 μg/ml of doxycycline to the culture medium, which was exchanged every 2 days.

Treatment with redox stressors

Cells were cultured to confluence, treated with 4-OHT or ethanol vehicle control for 3 days, and then serum-starved (0.5% FBS) overnight for 16 h. Medium was replaced with reduced serum (0.5%) medium supplemented with RSL-3 (1–10 μM), buthionine sulfoximine (BSO, 500 μM), or dimethylsulfoxide vehicle control. Cell survival was determined at 24 h for RSL3 and 5 days for BSO. For BSO experiments, medium was replaced every 2 days.

Cell survival assays

Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS, Promega). MTS was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions in phenol-red-free DMEM. Cell survival was also assessed using crystal violet. For the crystal violet assay, cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 10% v/v methanol/10% v/v acetic acid for 10 min, and stained with 1% wt/v crystal violet in methanol, followed by repeated gentle washes in distilled water until rinses were clear. Plates were dried overnight, then resolubilized in methanol with 0.1% w/v sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for 2 h, and then absorbance at 595 nm was measured. In both assays, relative survival was normalized to untreated 4-OHT-treated or ethanol-treated controls without redox stressors.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were grown in chamber slides (Ibidi or Nunc Permanox) with treatments as for standard culture. Samples were fixed in 4% w/v paraformaldehyde in PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton-X 100 in PBS, blocked with 20 mg/ml bovine serum albumin, and incubated for 2-12h with primary antibodies (Table S2) in 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS (PBS-T). Samples were gently rinsed with PBS-T and incubated with species-specific fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies in PBS-T for 2 h at room temperature, rinsed with PBS-T, then covered with storage medium (Ibidi) or mounting medium and a coverglass (Permanox). Confocal or epifluorescence images were obtained on a Nikon A1 Confocal microscope. For quantification of vimentin, phospho-S6, and total S6, images were collected at fixed excitation strength and exposure. Relative fluorescence was determined across 10 non-overlapping fields using ImageJ and normalized to untreated controls.

RT-PCR

qPCR was performed using a 7500 Real Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo) or a Bio-Rad Opus 394 (Bio-Rad) with SYBR Green master mix according to manufacturer instructions with primers listed in Table S3.

RNA-sequencing

For the experiment in Figure 2, cells were cultured for 0, 2, 6, and 12 days following treatment with 4-OHT. For these experiments, 0 days samples served as the reference control. For experiments in Figure 5, cells were cultured for 0, 6, and 12 days with indicated additions and compared relative to baseline controls. RNA was collected and purified using RNeasy kits (Qiagen), including optional DNase treatment. Samples were submitted for routine poly-A library preparation and subjected to 150 bp paired-end sequencing according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina NovaSeq), targeting 20 to 40 million reads per sample at the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core. Reads were aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38/mm10). Differential expression was determined using DESeq2 (v1.38.2) (53). GSEA was performed using the fgsea package in R (54) against all differentially expressed genes with Padj < 0.05 regardless of magnitude of expression change, as determined by DESeq2.

Western blotting

Cells were rinsed with PBS, then ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer with protease inhibitors (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and 5 mM sodium fluoride was added to the cells and incubated for 20 min on ice. Lysate was collected by scraping and pipetting. Insoluble material was collected by centrifugation at 17,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo). Samples were then mixed with 6X sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 5 min before use. For each sample, 10 to 20 μg of protein was loaded in 4 to 15% gradient SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels (Bio-Rad) followed by electrophoresis for 1 to 1.5 h. Protein was transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore Sigma). Membranes were blocked with dehydrated milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After rinsing, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-labeled secondary antibody. Signal was detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Thermo) and autoradiography film or a digital detector (Bio-Rad).

Seahorse Assay

100,000 cells/well were seeded onto Seahorse XFe24 Cell Culture Microplates in volumes of 500 μl cell media. After 24 h, cells were treated with 1 μM 4-OHT or vehicle for 72 h. Seahorse assay medium was prepared by supplementing Seahorse XF DMEM with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 11 mM glucose. Cells were incubated at 37 °C and 0% CO2 in Seahorse assay medium for 1 h prior to the assay. The Mito Stress Test protocol was carried out to measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification (ECAR) using a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer. Final well concentrations for oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide-4 (trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and rotenone and antimycin A (Rot/AA) were 2.5 μM, 2.0 μM, and 0.5 μM, respectively. Three sequential measurements of O2 and pH were taken after injection of each compound. After each assay was completed, the plate was immediately fixed with 4% w/v paraformaldehyde for 10 min, then stained with DAPI for ∼30 min. Each well was imaged and nuclear density determined using a Cytation 5. OCR and ECAR were normalized to cell density and values in replicate wells averaged for each time point.

ChIP-seq

To delete Pax8, cells were treated with 4-OHT or vehicle for 6 days (2 days 4-OHT or vehicle pulse followed by 4 days washout). Cells were crosslinked for 10 min with 1% w/v formaldehyde, quenched in 0.125 M glycine, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, scraped, and harvested by centrifugation. Cell pellets were sent on dry ice to Active Motif, Inc. for ChIP-Seq analyses as fee-for-service. Briefly, chromatin was isolated by adding lysis buffer, followed by disruption with a Dounce homogenizer. Lysates were sonicated and the DNA sheared to an average length of 300 to 500 bp with Active Motif’s EpiShear probe sonicator (cat# 53051). Genomic DNA (Input) was prepared by treating aliquots of chromatin with RNase, proteinase K and heat for de-crosslinking, followed by SPRI beads clean up (Beckman Coulter) and quantitation by Clariostar (BMG Labtech). Extrapolation to the original chromatin volume allowed determination of the total chromatin yield. An aliquot of chromatin (30 μg) was precleared with protein A agarose beads (Invitrogen). Genomic DNA regions of interest were isolated using 4 μg of antibody against PAX8 (Table S2) or specific Histone modifications. Complexes were washed, eluted from the beads with SDS buffer, and subjected to RNase and proteinase K treatment. Crosslinks were reversed by incubation overnight at 65 °C, and ChIP DNA was purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation.

Illumina sequencing libraries were prepared from the ChIP and Input DNAs on an automated system (Apollo 342, Wafergen Biosystems/Takara). After a final PCR amplification step, the resulting DNA libraries were quantified and sequenced on Illumina’s NextSeq 500 (75 nt reads, single end). Reads were aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38/mm10) using the BWA algorithm (default settings). Duplicate reads were removed, and only uniquely mapped reads (mapping quality ≥ 25) were used for further analysis. Alignments were extended in silico at their 3′-ends to a length of 200 bp, which is the average genomic fragment length in the size-selected library, and assigned to 32-nt bins along the genome. Samples were normalized by reducing the reads in all samples for each target by random sampling to the number of reads present in the smallest sample. The resulting histograms were stored in bigWig files. Peak locations were determined using the MACS algorithm (v2.1.0) (55) with a cutoff of p-value = 1e-7. Peaks that were on the ENCODE blacklist of known false ChIP-Seq peaks were removed. Signal maps and peak locations were used as input data to Active Motifs proprietary analysis program, which creates Excel tables containing detailed information on sample comparison, peak metrics, peak locations and gene annotations. Data was further visualized using deepTools (56) and Integrated Genomics Viewer (IGV) (57).

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag was performed as described previously (58). PT-22 cells were washed with wash150 buffer (final concentrations: 20 mM HEPES (Na+) pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1X complete protease inhibitor) and added to BioMag Plus Concanavalin A Beads (Bangs Laboratories) pre-activated with cold bead activation buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MnCl2) for 10 min on a rotator at room temperature. Wash buffer was removed and cold antibody buffer (final concentrations: Wash150 buffer, 100× Protease Inhibitor, 0.05% Digitonin (Sigma), 2 mM EDTA, 1% BSA (Sigma) was added. Antibodies against H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K27ac, or H3K27me3 (Table S2) were added to each reaction and incubated overnight with orbital mixing at 4 °C. Reactions were washed with Digitonin150 buffer (final concentrations: Wash150 buffer, 100xPI, 0.05% digitonin) three times. 1 μg of secondary antibody Guinea pig polyclonal anti-rabbit IgG (ABIN101961) or Rabbit Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (ab46540) was added and incubated on an orbital rotator at room temperature for 1 h. Reactions were washed with Digitonin150 buffer three times. 2.5 μl of pAG-Tn5 fusion protein (Epicypher) was diluted with Digitonin300 buffer (20 mM HEPES pH7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 100× Protease inhibitor, 0.01% Digitonin), then added to each reaction and incubated for 60 min at room temperature. After reaction was washed with Digitonin300 buffer three times, tagmentation buffer (Digitonin300 buffer, 10 mM MgCl2) was added to reactions and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. To stop tagmentation, buffer containing 4.2 μl 0.5 M EDTA, 1.25 μl 10% SDS, 1.1 μl Proteinase K (20 mg/ml) was added to the reaction and incubated for 1 h at 55 °C. DNA was purified using a MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) as per manufacturer’s instructions. The product was PCR amplified using barcoded primers (See supplementary table) and NEBNext HiFi 2× PCR master mix (NEB). After PCR, libraries were purified using AMPure XP (Beckman) at 1:1 ratio and 80% ethanol. Libraries were eluted in DNase/RNase free H2O. Libraries were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer and dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermofisher) and then analyzed using Agilent Tapestation and the D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent).

Paired-end fastq files were aligned to the mouse GRCm38/mm10 genome with Bowtie2 (59) after removing low quality reads with fastp. The alignment parameters were (bowtie2 -p 8 -X 700 --local --very-sensitive-local --no-unal --no-mixed --no-discordant --phred33 -I 10). Aligned.sam files were converted to coordinate sorted binary.bam files with picard “SortSam”. Normalized bigWig read coverage files were created using the deepTools “bamCoverage” application using reads per genomic content normalization. Peaks were called with MACS2 (55) using parameters (-p 1e-5 -f BAMPE -g mm) for paired-end reads.

Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin (ATAC)-seq and analysis

Confluent PT-22 cells were depleted of PAX8 for 0, 3 or 24 days. Samples were prepared and analyzed as previously described (60, 61). Briefly, samples were washed with PBS, and nuclei were prepared using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents Kit (Invitrogen). Nuclei were resuspended in 50 μl of 1 × TD buffer, then incubated with 2 to 2.5 μl Tn5 enzyme for 30 min at 37 °C (DNA Library Preparation Kit, Nextera). Samples were immediately purified by Qiagen minElute column and PCR-amplified with the NEBNext High-Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix (NEB). qPCR was used to determine the optimal PCR cycles to prevent over-amplification. The amplified library was further purified by Qiagen minElute column and SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter). ATAC-seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 (125-nucleotide read length, paired end), trimmed, and aligned to the GRCm38/mm10 mouse genome as above.

Quantitative analysis of ChIP-seq, CUT&Tag, and ATAC-seq

Normalized read density was integrated over each PAX8 peak summit ± 500 bp for all PAX8 peaks then subdivided by the expression status of the nearest transcription start site (TSS) following PAX8 depletion determined using ChIPSeeker using its default definition for promoters (TSS ± 3 kb) (34). Distal elements, outside of the promoter, could be inter- or intragenic. A small number of sites were greater than 100 kb from any TSS and were excluded. The ratio of the measured signal after PAX8 depletion relative to that prior to depletion was determined. Loci with no measured signal at baseline were excluded. Outlier samples, defined as greater than 10 times the median absolute deviation greater above the relative signal ratio median, were also excluded. Because changes did not follow a normal distribution, data were analyzed using Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Lost H3K4me1 or H3K27ac peaks were defined by a 2-fold or greater decrease and were analyzed using χ2. Motif analysis was determined using HOMER2 (62) using parameters (-size 250 -mask) for only distal PAX8 peaks categorized by expression change in the nearest gene following PAX8 depletion. De novo motifs were reported.
