Management and long‐term haematological and neurological outcomes of Immunoglobulin M and Waldenström's macroglobulinaemia‐related neuropathy: A single‐centre experience
Authors: Maria Gavriatopoulou, Ioannis Ntanasis‐Stathopoulos, Charalampos Filippatos, Panagiotis Malandrakis, Sotirios Manganas, Foteini Theodorakakou, Irene Solia, Evangelos Eleutherakis‐Papaiakovou, Ilias Katsadouros, Panagiotis Boutsikos, Despina Fotiou, Magdalini Migkou, Nikolaos Kanellias, Evangelos Terpos, Efstathios Kastritis, Meletios‐Athanasios Dimopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmunoglobulin M‐related peripheral neuropathy (IgM‐PN) is typically demyelinating and associated with anti‐myelin‐associated glycoprotein antibodies; however, limited data on clinical outcomes are available. We retrospectively analysed consecutive patients with IgM‐PN assessed prospectively with the Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (RODS) and the Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment (INCAT) questionnaires. Twenty‐eight patients were retrospectively enrolled. The median follow‐up was 5.54 years. Treatment was initiated in all solely due to neuropathy symptoms: 19 (67.9%) dexamethasone‐rituximab‐cyclophosphamide, 6 (21.4%) rituximab and 3 (10.7%) ibrutinib. The baseline median INCAT score was 2.0 and improved to 1.0 at further time points; within‐patient improvements compared to baseline were significant (p < 0.005). A clinically relevant improvement was evident in 13 (46.4%), 18 (64.3%) and 17 (60.7%) patients at 6, 12 and 24 months respectively. IgM reduction was associated with improvement in INCAT scores at 6 (p = 0.041) and 12 months (p = 0.030). At baseline, the median RODS score was 38.0, improved to 41.5 at 6 months, then to 43.0 and 43.5 at 1 and 2 years (all p < 0.001). A clinically relevant improvement was evident in 5 (17.9%), 7 (25%) and 9 (32.1%) patients at 6, 12 and 24 months respectively. Patients with IgM‐PN, treated mainly with rituximab‐based regimens, showed significant improvement in functional and neurological ability over a 2‐year follow‐up.

INTRODUCTION

Immunoglobulin M (IgM)‐related peripheral neuropathy (IgM‐PN) is a persisting, mostly demyelinating, peripheral neuropathy associated with the presence of monoclonal IgM gammopathy that often targets myelin‐associated glycoprotein (MAG). 1 Both diagnosis and optimal treatment are challenging since IgM‐PN shares symptoms with several other neurological diseases and underlying haematological conditions. 2 Approximately 50%–60% of patients with IgM‐PN have increased levels of anti‐MAG antibodies at diagnosis. The spectrum of IgM‐PN also includes patients with non‐anti‐MAG polyneuropathy. These patients often present with a different clinical course and may show signs of inflammation and demyelination that are similar to chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy. 3 , 4

Genetic studies have demonstrated that somatic mutations, like myeloid differentiation primary response 88, leucine‐to‐proline substitution at position 265 (MYD88 L265P), which are often linked to Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (WM), are also present in a large number of these patients. This underlines a potential strong association between the underlying haematological malignancy and the neurological entity. 5

Due to the rarity of the disease, data from large phase 3 clinical trials are currently missing. Rituximab, a B‐cell‐depleting anti‐Cluster of Differentiation (CD)20 monoclonal antibody, has shown significant efficacy, particularly in anti‐MAG‐positive cases. 6 , 7 Nonetheless, responses may vary, and the ‘IgM flare’ phenomenon accompanied by a flare in neurological symptoms has been reported following rituximab administration, which necessitates vigilant monitoring. 8 Other treatment options, including immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide and covalent Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors like ibrutinib and zanubrutinib, are currently being investigated, particularly in the relapsed and/or refractory setting. 1 , 9

Outcome measures and tools to quantify symptoms, function and disability in patients with IgM‐PN are evolving, with tools like the Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (RODS) and the Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment (INCAT) score being increasingly implemented in both everyday clinical practice and clinical trials. 10 , 11 Due to the low incidence of the disease and the spectrum of variable symptoms, long‐term clinical outcomes are limited as well.

In this context, we retrospectively evaluate longitudinal neurological and haematological responses to rituximab‐based regimens and targeted therapies in a single‐centre cohort of patients with IgM‐PN.

METHODS

Study design and population

This was a retrospective, single‐centre study enrolling consecutive adult patients with a definite diagnosis of IgM‐PN associated with either IgM monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) or WM, who received treatment with either (chemo)immunotherapy or targeted agents. For patients with non‐anti‐MAG neuropathy, the diagnosis of IgM‐PN was determined by the concurrent presence of the pathogenic monoclonal IgM clone, clinically apparent sensory or sensorimotor neuropathic symptoms, and electrophysiological evidence of a demyelinating process on electromyography and nerve conduction studies, ruling out alternative aetiologies. Included patients were diagnosed and treated between 1 January 2012 and 31 July 2025. All data were extracted from patients' charts. This study was approved by the institutional review board (approval number 521/30‐10‐2025).

Treatment response

Patients with measurable disease, defined as a serum IgM concentration ≥500 mg/dL, were eligible for haematological response assessment according to the 11th International Workshop on Waldenström Macroglobulinemia criteria. 12

Neurological assessment

Neurological function was evaluated using two standardized and validated scales: RODS, which quantifies activity and physical limitations in patients with neuropathies 10 ; and INCAT, which assesses functional impairment of the upper and lower limbs. 11

RODS scores range from 0 to 48 (higher scores indicate better function), and INCAT scores range from 0 to 10 (0 indicating no disability, 10 indicating full disability). Clinically relevant neurological improvement was defined as an increase of ≥4 points in the RODS or a decrease of ≥1 point in the INCAT compared with baseline. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Moreover, Anti‐MAG antibodies were tested via enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and the cut‐off value was ≥1000 Bühlmann titre units (BTU). All patients were assessed clinically; neurophysiological assessment was not conducted in all individuals.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians (range), and categorical variables as absolute counts and percentages. Comparisons of paired data were conducted with the non‐parametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, due to the non‐normal nature of small samples and results were reported as medians (range). Correlations between RODS and INCAT scores, as well as between IgM reduction and neurological improvement, were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value <0.05.

All statistical analyses were performed with R/R‐Studio version 2024.04.2 (Posit Software, PBC).

RESULTS

A total of 28 consecutive patients (16 [57.1%] with WM and 12 [42.9%] with IgM MGUS) were retrospectively enrolled; the median age was 65.5 years and 17 (60.7%) were males. The median IgM and M‐spike levels were 627.0 mg/dL (range 139.0–2490.0) and 0.8 g/dL (0.4–1.7) respectively. Seventeen patients (60.7%) had anti‐MAG [12 of whom were also anti‐sulfated glucuronyl paragloboside (SGPG) positive] and 11 (39.3%) had non‐anti‐MAG demyelinating neuropathy. Neuropathy patterns included isolated lower limb involvement in 21 patients (75%), both upper and lower extremities in 6 (21.4%), and isolated upper limbs in 1 (3.6%). Treatment was initiated for all patients solely due to neuropathy symptoms; 19 (67.9%) were treated with dexamethasone‐rituximab‐cyclophosphamide (DRC), 6 (21.4%) with rituximab monotherapy and 3 (10.7%) with ibrutinib. The median time from the onset of neuropathic symptoms to the initiation of therapy was 16 months (range 2–60) (18 months [range 6–60] in the anti‐MAG and 9 months [range 2–20] in the non‐anti‐MAG cohorts). Haematological response and survival

In patients with measurable disease (defined as IgM ≥500 mg/dL, n = 17), best haematological responses were as follows: 3 (17.6%) very good partial response (VGPR), 9 (53.0%) partial response (PR), 3 (17.6%) minor response (MR) and 2 (11.8%) stable disease (SD). After a median follow‐up of 5.54 years, 2 (12.5%) deaths were recorded; both among patients with WM, one due to disease transformation to high‐grade lymphoma and one due to reasons unrelated to WM. Moreover, three WM patients (18.8%) relapsed post first line treatment. The 5‐ and 9‐year overall survival (OS) rates among all patients were 92.3% and among WM patients 90.9% at both time points (Figures S1 and S2). The 5‐ and 9‐year progression‐free survival rates among WM patients were 78.7% and 68.9% respectively (Figure S3). Table 2 summarizes treatment responses and patient outcomes.

Neurological functional outcomes (INCAT and RODS questionnaire‐based assessment)

At baseline, the median INCAT score was 2.0 (range: 0.0–7.0). Post‐treatment, median scores significantly improved over time, dropping to 1.0 at 6 months (p = 0.001), 1 year (p < 0.005) and 2 years (p < 0.005) (Figure 1A; Table 3). A clinically relevant improvement was evident in 13 (46.4%), 18 (64.3%) and 17 (60.7%) patients at 6, 12 and 24 months respectively. Among evaluable patients, IgM reduction was associated with improvement in INCAT scores at 6 months (p = 0.041) and at 1 year (p = 0.030).

At baseline, the median RODS score was 38.0 (range: 7.0–47.0). Post‐treatment, median scores significantly improved over time, reaching 41.5 at 6 months, 43.0 at 1 year and 43.5 at 2 years (all p < 0.001; Figure 1B; Table 3). A clinically relevant improvement per RODS was evident in 5 (17.9%), 7 (25%) and 9 (32.1%) patients at 6, 12 and 24 months respectively.

Individual patient trajectories both for INCAT and RODS scores can be found in the Supporting Information (Figures S4 and S5).

Furthermore, RODS and INCAT scores were strongly correlated at all‐time points (baseline ρ = −0.77, p < 0.001; 1‐month ρ = −0.80, p < 0.001; 6‐months ρ = −0.79, p < 0.001; 1‐year ρ = −0.78, p < 0.001; 2‐years ρ = −0.85, p < 0.001).

A clinical complete neurological response was observed in three patients (10.7%), defined as attaining a score of 0 in the INCAT scale and a score of 48 in the RODS scale at the same time point. No flare events in terms of IgM levels or neurological symptoms were observed at the 1‐month time point, based on both evaluation tools. There was one event of gradually progressive neurological deterioration 2 years post‐initial treatment with rituximab, which was managed effectively with DRC. Finally, three WM patients needed salvage treatment for disease symptoms unrelated to neuropathy; two were treated with bendamustine‐rituximab and ibrutinib for WM and one received rituximab‐cyclophosphamide‐doxorubicin‐vincristine‐prednisone (R‐CHOP) due to transformation to high‐grade lymphoma.

Exploratory subgroup analyses

Non‐anti‐MAG versus anti‐MAG neuropathy

Eleven patients (39.3%) had non‐anti‐MAG demyelinating neuropathy and 17 were diagnosed with anti‐MAG neuropathy. No significant differences were found between the two groups, except for a non‐significant trend of WM prevalence in the non‐anti‐MAG arm (Table S1; p = 0.054). Consequently, patients with non‐anti‐MAG neuropathy had numerically higher median IgM levels (778 mg/dL) compared to those with anti‐MAG (600 mg/dL), but this difference was not significant (p = 0.519). At baseline, the median INCAT scores were 1.0 (range: 1.0–7.0) and 2.0 (range: 0.0–7.0) respectively. Among non‐anti‐MAG and anti‐MAG neuropathy patients, clinically relevant improvement was evident in 3 (27.3%) and 10 (58.8%), 5 (45.5%) and 13 (76.5%) and 6 (54.6%) and 11 (64.7%) patients at 6, 12 and 24 months respectively. Furthermore, the median RODS scores at baseline were 38.0 (range: 10.0–46.0) and 37.0 (range: 7.0–47.0) for patients with non‐anti‐MAG and anti‐MAG neuropathy respectively. Between the non‐anti‐MAG and anti‐MAG neuropathy groups, clinically relevant improvement was evident in 1 (9.1%) and 4 (58.8%), 2 (18.2%) and 7 (41.2%) and 2 (18.2%) and 5 (29.4%) patients at 6, 12 and 24 months respectively. Finally, the median INCAT scores were equal across all time points (median, 1.0) while no clinically relevant differences were observed between the median RODS scores of the two groups at any time point (all median differences between the two groups were <4).

Baseline INCAT >2 vs. ≤2

Seven patients (25.0%) had a >2 INCAT score at baseline. These patients had a median pre‐treatment score of 4.0 (range: 3.0–7.0), compared to 1.0 (range: 0.0–2.0) among others. At the 6‐month time point, the median INCAT score was 3.0 (range: 3.0–7.0) and 1.0 (range: 0.0–2.0) between the two groups respectively. At the 12 and 24‐month time points, the median INCAT scores were 3 (range: 2.0–6.0) and 1.0 (range: 0.0–2.0) between the two groups respectively.

Baseline RODS <38 vs. ≥38

Eleven patients (39.3%) had a baseline RODS score <38. Their median pre‐treatment score was 33.0 (range: 7.0–37.0) vs. 43.0 (range: 38.0–47.0) in the higher cohort. This subgroup maintained a lower physical trajectory across all time points (6‐months, 35.5 vs. 45.0; 12‐months, 39.5 vs. 46.0; 24‐months, 36.5 vs. 45.5, respectively), albeit no statistically significant differences (for all time points, 0.214 < p < 0.297).

Patients aged >65 vs. ≤65 years

Fifteen patients (53.6%) were 65 years or older at baseline, with median INCAT and RODS scores of 2.0 and 38.0, compared to 2.0 and 43.0 among younger counterparts (p = 0.290 and 0.518 respectively). At 6‐months, both scores were similar (INCAT median 1.0 for both, RODS 40.5 and 43.0, respectively) and remained as such at 12‐months (INCAT: 1.5 and 1.0, RODS 41.0 and 45.5 respectively). At 24‐months, the median INCAT scores were 2.0 and 1.0 and the median RODS scores were 42.0 and 46.0. No significant differences were noted at any time point.

DRC versus rituximab

In our cohort, 19 (67.9%) patients were treated with DRC and 6 (21.4%) with rituximab monotherapy. The median INCAT and RODS scores for the two groups prior to treatment were similar (INCAT 2.0 and 1.5; RODS 41.0 and 38.0 respectively). These scores remained comparable across the 6‐month (INCAT: 1.0 and 1.0; RODS 42.0 and 40.0, respectively) and the 12‐month time points (INCAT: 1.0 and 1.0; RODS 43.5 and 41.0 respectively). At 24‐months, INCAT scores were again similar (medians, 1.0 and 1.0, respectively) but there was a small discrepancy between the median RODS scores (medians, 44.5 and 39.5 respectively).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective single‐centre study, including 28 patients with IgM‐PN, rituximab‐based therapies were associated with a significant neurological improvement over a 2‐year follow‐up period in a significant proportion of patients. Although the severity of symptoms was not substantial, these results point to the benefit associated with clone‐targeting therapies in IgM‐PN and to the safety of rituximab‐based approaches, suggesting that treating earlier such patients may be associated with better outcomes and a low risk of complications.

Importantly, treatment was initiated in all patients due to neuropathy symptoms. When neurological function was evaluated over time using validated tools such as RODS and INCAT, the functional and neurological improvement was both statistically and clinically significant but this was more pronounced after 6 months and mostly at 1 and 2 years. Thus, the evaluation of patients with IgM‐PN receiving anti‐clonal therapy should be evaluated after at least 6 months from the start of treatment. Although the validated RODS and INCAT scales demonstrated varying levels of clinical improvement across time points due to differing sensitivities, both captured consistent trends towards functional recovery. Notably, improved INCAT scores were associated with a decrease in serum IgM levels but this was not shown with RODS. This modest correlation is expected given that low‐level clonal IgM can exert significant neurotoxicity, and current therapies rarely eliminate monoclonal protein production entirely.

The inclusion of patients with a baseline INCAT score of 0 highlights the clinical utility of incorporating multifaceted evaluation tools like the RODS. While an INCAT score of 0 implies an absence of severe upper or lower limb disability, it lacks the sensitivity to capture finer restrictions in activities of daily living. These subtle impairments were clearly captured by the baseline RODS scores in our cohort. This divergence underscores that clone‐targeting therapies may be appropriately initiated based on real‐world functional difficulties and patient‐reported limitations before gross, irreversible neurological disability becomes evident on traditional staging scales.

The wide range of IgM‐PN, particularly the clinical variations seen in patients with or without anti‐MAG antibodies, has been highlighted by several studies. A study by Bardel et al. described the whole spectrum of IgM‐PN, including anti‐MAG but also ‘neuropathies of uncertain relationship with the IgM’ (NURIM) with mainly axonal subtypes. 4 Thus, there is a need for more accurate diagnostic categorization to predict outcome and guide therapy; nonetheless, we only included patients with demyelinating IgM‐PN, since we still lack adequate criteria to identify IgM‐related axonal neuropathies [excluding IgM‐related AL amyloidosis, cryoglobulinaemia, CANOMAD (chronic ataxic neuropathy, opthalmoplegia, monoclonal protein, cold agglutinins, and disialosyl antibodies) and CANDA (chronic ataxic neuropathy with disialosyl antibodies).] To meet the need for longitudinal clinical data and standardized outcome measures in this patient population, the global prospective registry IMAGiNe research was launched and is currently ongoing. 17

The clinical significance of genetic profiling in this setting has been increasingly clarified by larger real‐world cohorts. Castellani et al. evaluated 75 patients with anti‐MAG neuropathy and reported a MYD88 L265P mutation prevalence of 66.7%. 18 Similarly, Guérémy et al. identified the mutation in 48.3% of their 79‐patient cohort. 19 Aligning with our own observations, both of these large‐scale studies confirmed that MYD88 mutational status does not act as a prognostic factor for baseline neuropathy severity, nor does it predict clinical responsiveness to rituximab‐based therapies.

The treatment of demyelinating IgM‐PN is not standardized and current data come from small perspective and retrospective studies, often lacking standardized assessments. However, there are several available anti‐clonal treatments for IgM‐PN. Our findings support the efficacy of rituximab‐based regimens in the real‐world setting. Furthermore, our findings align with prior studies indicating clinical benefit when serum IgM levels decrease post‐rituximab, cyclophosphamide and prednisolone in IgM‐PN. 20 It has also been shown previously that rituximab monotherapy may improve neurological symptoms, as assessed by INCAT, in patients with IgM anti‐MAG demyelinating polyneuropathy. 21 There is also a pathophysiological rationale that rituximab acts by reducing the autoreactive pathogenic antibodies and promoting the expansion of immune‐regulatory T cells. 21 , 22 However, the randomized RIMAG trial found that rituximab monotherapy did not significantly improve primary 1‐year INCAT sensory scores 23 or long‐term clinical outcomes, 24 though secondary end‐points showed modest improvements likely owing to varying end‐point sensitivities. In our study, a small proportion of patients received ibrutinib, while the majority received either rituximab monotherapy or DRC. No significant differences regarding patient outcomes emerged among the treatment subgroups. Long‐term follow‐up suggests that there were durable improvements in the RODS and INCAT scales in a significant proportion of patients, but most of our patients were treated with DRC.

Similar findings have been reported by a Cochrane systematic review on the role of immunotherapy for anti‐MAG neuropathy, which noted that the available data are limited and of low quality, with few studies being sufficiently powered or with the required follow‐up to detect meaningful effects. 25 Our results highlight the necessity for trials employing specific disease outcomes measures on a prospective basis in larger populations. Additionally, the frequent occurrence of MYD88 L265P mutations in individuals with anti‐MAG neuropathy, as described by Vos et al., 5 presents novel opportunities for targeted therapy, especially via BTK inhibition with either covalent or non‐covalent inhibitors. This therapeutic rationale is strongly supported by recent literature; Castellani et al. reported an encouraging overall clinical response rate of 81.8% (9 of 11 patients) when utilizing novel targeted agents tailored to the patient's specific genomic profile. 18 Both cohorts emphasize that in patients demonstrating resistance, early relapse, or an unfavourable risk profile to standard rituximab regimens, mapping the MYD88 and C‐X‐C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) pathways offers a highly valid, biomarker‐driven rescue strategy.

Our study comprises one of the most extensive single‐centre cohort analyses of IgM‐PN patients with an extended follow‐up incorporating specific tools to quantify neurological response. However, it also carries limitations, mainly including the retrospective design and the absence of a control group regarding treatment disposition. The sample size was relatively small; however, this is expected for rare diseases. For several individuals, genetic profiling (e.g. MYD88, CXCR4 mutations) was not thorough, and longitudinal anti‐MAG titres were not available for all patients at all time points. Regarding the treatment disposition, not all patients were treated with the same regimen, most patients were treated either with rituximab monotherapy (four weekly infusions) or DRC and very few with BTK inhibitors. Despite these drawbacks, our results support the use of rituximab‐based treatment in individuals with symptomatic IgM‐PN, regardless of anti‐MAG status.

In conclusion, our study indicates that among patients with IgM‐PN undergoing rituximab‐based regimens a significant proportion can achieve significant and durable responses in neurological function and disability indices. The benefit lasts for at least 2 years and is consistent in both anti‐MAG and non‐anti‐MAG subtypes. Prospective studies in the future should focus on larger scale, prospective clinical trials, using standardized functional outcome measures and explore the role of all novel targeted immunotherapeutic regimens in a more individualized and biomarker‐driven, personalized way.
