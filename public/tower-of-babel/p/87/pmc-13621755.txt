Distinct SCFA profiles drive contrasting impacts of whole versus refined grains on metabolic health and body composition beyond inflammation in older adults: a secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zhiwen Ge, Cheng Li, Jia Wang, Bingjie Ding, Yaru Li, Nan Wang, Yanxia Bi, Yuxia Wang, Yisi Wang, Zebin Peng, Xinli Yang, Congcong Wang, Zhongxin Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough whole grains (WG) are widely recommended for health, the divergent impacts of WG versus refined grains (RG) on metabolism, body composition, and inflammation, mediated by distinct short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles, require deeper investigation in older adults. This secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial aimed to elucidate these differential effects and explore the specific mediating roles of SCFA changes.MethodsWhile maintaining their habitual non-staple food intake, 102 participants were randomly assigned to either the WG or RG groups and provided with standardized ingredients for staple food preparation. Fecal SCFAs, blood pressure, blood glucose, renal function markers, body composition, and systemic immune-inflammation markers were assessed at baseline and post-intervention, with further exploration of inter-variable correlations and mediation pathways among these factors.ResultsWG favored butyrate percentage, while RG increased acetate proportion. WG consumption improved post-prandial diastolic blood pressure (DBP), an effect not observed in the RG group, and yielded a significantly greater reduction in serum creatinine. Conversely, RG intake resulted in statistically significant short-term body composition changes, reducing fat mass and increasing lean mass. Mediation analysis revealed that changes in butyrate and acetate acted as suppressors in the pathways linking dietary grain type to post-prandial DBP and blood urea nitrogen changes, respectively. Systemic immune-inflammation markers did not differ between groups.ConclusionIn our study, we observed that WG and RG consumption elicited distinct SCFA profiles and divergent metabolic and body composition responses in middle-aged and older adults. WG intake preferentially benefited blood pressure and renal function, potentially through SCFA-related pathways, while RG intake unexpectedly improved short-term body composition. These findings highlight the complexity of grain-microbiota-host interactions and suggest that the grain consumed substantially influences metabolic outcomes through SCFA-mediated mechanisms.Clinical trial registration number: ChiCTR2300072978.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12986-026-01165-6.

Introduction

Metabolic disorders, including cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and chronic kidney disease (CKD), pose a substantial global health burden [1]. The latest epidemiological data reveal that approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years worldwide are living with diabetes (over 90% of whom have type 2 diabetes), with projections indicating this number may surge to 853 million by 2050 [2]. Concurrently, hypertension, a key risk factor for both cardiovascular and kidney diseases, affects over 1 billion individuals globally [3]. These chronic conditions collectively contribute to premature mortality, diminished quality of life, and significant pressure on healthcare systems [1].

Given their non-invasive nature and cost-effectiveness [4], dietary interventions remain important in managing metabolic diseases, despite challenges in long-term adherence [5]. Evidence increasingly highlights that the quality of carbohydrates, not just the quantity, influences metabolic outcomes [6, 7], leading to a greater emphasis on consumption of whole grains (WG). Whole grains are defined as the intact, ground, cracked, or flaked caryopsis with its principal anatomical components—the starchy endosperm, germ, and bran—present in their original relative proportions [8]. Common examples include whole wheat, brown rice, oats, barley, corn, and quinoa. Compared to refined grains (RG), whole grains usually exhibit superior nutritional profiles due to the enriched content of dietary fiber, antioxidants, vitamins, and minerals, essential components largely depleted during the refining process [9, 10]. Epidemiological evidence consistently links higher whole-grain intake with lower risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality [11, 12]. This scientific consensus has led to the incorporation of whole grain consumption as a key recommendation in national dietary guidelines worldwide [13, 14].

Over the last decade, the gut microbiota has emerged as an important mediator between diet and host metabolism [15, 16]. The fermentation of dietary fibers by gut microbes produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), mainly acetate, propionate, and butyrate. These SCFAs have been shown to play roles in regulating energy balance, metabolic and inflammatory responses [17, 18]. For instance, butyrate is noted for its multi-faceted roles in reducing inflammation, maintaining intestinal barrier function, and regulating immune responses [19]. Acetate and propionate are involved in energy metabolism and blood pressure regulation [20, 21].

While it is well-established that WG and RG diets differentially modulate gut microbial fermentation [22, 23], many questions remain worthy of investigation. Specifically, it is still necessary to characterize the dynamic shifts in overall SCFA profiles during intervention, considering the heterogeneity in individual responses [24–27]. Furthermore, the mechanisms through which these distinct SCFA patterns translate into divergent systemic effects on metabolism, body composition, and inflammation deserve further exploration, especially in metabolically vulnerable middle-aged and older populations. The insufficiency of studies that comprehensively assess these interconnected factors, coupled with inconsistent findings, underscores the need for more real-world evidence [28].

The primary trial [29] from which our data are derived provided insights into the immunomodulatory effects of whole grains, linking them to SCFA changes. However, the specific impact of these distinct SCFA profiles on a broader range of metabolic outcomes (e.g., blood pressure, renal function) and body composition, especially the mediating role of SCFAs, was not the focus. Therefore, this secondary analysis was designed to extend the initial investigation. Our objectives were: 1) to comprehensively characterize the differential effects of WG versus RG consumption on SCFA profiles, metabolic health, body composition, and systemic immune-inflammation in older adults; and 2) to explore the specific mediating roles of SCFAs changes in the pathways linking grain intake to these outcomes. In doing so, we also aim to corroborate the foundational SCFA-related findings of the parent study, thereby enhancing the overall robustness of the observations.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data were obtained from a randomized, single-blind, parallel-group controlled trial enrolling adults aged ≥ 45 years from Zhangfang community, Fangshan district, Beijing. The study design included participant recruitment from community health records, matching procedures for hypertensive and non-hypertensive individuals, randomization process using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., USA), and overall study procedure (Fig. 1). Detailed methodology, sample size calculation, and pre-survey procedures have been published previously [29].Fig. 1Flow chart

Flow chart

Briefly, matched pairs were randomly assigned to either the WG or RG groups. Standardized daily grain rations (≤ 300 g per participant: 150 g rice and 150 g wheat flour) were provided in anonymized packaging. The WG group received brown rice and whole-grain flour, while the RG group obtained refined rice and wheat flour. Participants were instructed to use these ingredients to prepare their staple foods according to their habitual cooking practices (e.g., steamed rice, steamed buns, flatbreads, or noodles). To enhance compliance, a multi-faceted strategy was implemented: 1) community staff performed blinded food deliveries; 2) licensed dietitians provided cooking tutorials via online videos and offered continuous dietary counseling through WeChat (Tencent, China) support groups; 3) each participant received an electronic scale and a detailed food diary for recording their daily consumption of the provided materials. Regular checks of the food diaries and active communication within the support groups were used to monitor and encourage adherence throughout the study [29].

The study protocol followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Beijing Friendship Hospital Ethics Committee (2023-P2-172–01). The trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300072978). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Of the 144 individuals originally recruited, 9 were lost to follow-up and 33 had incomplete critical information, leaving 102 participants for this analysis (Fig. 1).

Baseline data collection

Baseline data collection took place in June 2023 at the local community center. Procedures for informing participants and the centralized investigation site setup were followed as previously detailed [29]. Trained investigators collected standardized data, including age, gender, physical activity levels, dietary preferences and patterns, and medical histories of prevalent non-communicable diseases (NCDs). A validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was used to establish baseline dietary patterns for assessing intervention efficacy.

The study evaluated seven NCDs or metabolic abnormalities: hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia, gout/hyperuricemia, CKD, overweight/obesity, and central obesity. Hypertension diagnosis was determined by participant self-reporting with verification from community health records. T2DM, dyslipidemia, gout/hyperuricemia, and CKD were identified through self-reporting. Overweight/obesity and central obesity were defined using anthropometric measurements. Body mass index (BMI) stratification followed age-specific Chinese criteria [14]: Middle-aged and elderly adults (45–64 years): underweight < 18.5 kg/m2, normal weight 18.5–23.9 kg/m2, overweight/obesity ≥ 24.0 kg/m2; older adults (65–79 years): underweight < 20.0 kg/m2, normal weight 20.0–26.9 kg/m2, overweight/obesity ≥ 27.0 kg/m2. Central obesity [30] was defined as waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for males and ≥ 85 cm for females.

Metabolic parameter measurements

Anthropometric parameters including height (m), weight (kg), and waist circumference (cm) were measured using standardized protocols, with BMI calculated as kg/m2.

Blood pressure and glucose were evaluated under fasting and post-prandial conditions. Participants arrived at the testing site after an overnight fast. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured using ACCU-CHEK glucometers (Roche Diagnostics, Germany), followed by blood pressure assessment (systolic blood pressure [SBP] and diastolic blood pressure [DBP]) using OMRON HEM-907 digital sphygmomanometers (Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Japan). After a standardized breakfast consisting of 100 g steamed buns, one boiled egg (approximately 50 g), 150 g vegetables, and 250 mL of milk, post-prandial blood glucose (PBG) and blood pressure were measured again two hours later using the same protocols and investigators.

Renal function indicators, complete blood count, and systemic immune-inflammation indices

Certified nurses collected fasting venous blood samples. Serum creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and uric acid (UA) were measured by using Toshiba TBA40FR automated analyzers (Toshiba Medical Systems Corporation, Japan). Reagents were supplied by Beijing Beijian-Xinchuangyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Complete blood count was performed using automated hematology analyzers. Venous blood was collected from antecubital veins, and analyzed by using Mindray BC6600PLUS automated hematology analyzers (Mindray Medical International Limited, China). Core parameters included leukocyte subset differential counts, erythrocyte count, hemoglobin concentration, and platelet count. From these results, six systemic immune-inflammation indices were calculated as follows: the Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR; neutrophil count/lymphocyte count); the Monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR; monocyte count/lymphocyte count); the Neutrophil-monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (NMLR; [neutrophil count + monocyte count] / lymphocyte count); the Systemic inflammation response index (SIRI; neutrophil count × monocyte count/lymphocyte count); the Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII; platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count); the Aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI; neutrophil count × monocyte count × platelet count/lymphocyte count).

Body composition

Body composition was assessed via multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis using InBody 770 devices (InBody Co., Ltd., Korea). Participants removed metal accessories, wore light clothing, stood barefoot gripping electrodes, and stood still for 5 min before measurement to stabilize fluid distribution. Parameters included body fat mass (BFM), percentage of body fat (PBF), visceral fat area (VFA), soft lean mass (SLM), fat-free mass (FFM), and skeletal muscle mass (SMM). Devices were calibrated before each measurement session.

Fecal short-chain fatty acid quantification

Participants were provided with standardized aseptic collection kits and detailed instructions for at-home fecal sample collection at the start of the study. Each kit contained a sterile fecal collection tube with an integrated sampling spoon, a pair of disposable gloves, a tin foil container to aid collection, sealable plastic bags, and two pre-freezable gel packs. Participants were instructed to prepare the ice packs in advance and label the tube with their ID and collection date, and then, using the spoon, to collect a pea-sized (approximately 1–2 g) sample from the middle portion of a fresh stool specimen, carefully avoiding contact with water or other contaminants. Immediately after collection, the sealed tube was to be placed in a bag with the frozen gel packs and stored in their home freezer. Samples were transported to the central facility within 24 h, where they were promptly transferred to a -80 °C freezer for long-term storage prior to analysis. Fecal SCFA concentrations were quantified using a high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry method. Briefly, frozen fecal samples were thawed at 4 °C and homogenized (approx. 50 mg). The sample was then extracted with 0.5 mL of methanol and 0.5 mL of water by vortexing for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. For derivatization, 50 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 50 μL of a propionic acid isotope internal standard (5 μg/mL), 50 μL of 3-nitrophenylhydrazine, and 50 μL of N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride, and incubated at 30 °C for 30 min. The reaction was quenched by adding 50 μL of 2,6-di-tert-butyl-p-cresol in methanol (2 mg/mL) and 250 μL of 75% aqueous methanol. After a final centrifugation step (12,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C), 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on distribution, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages). For SCFA analysis, relative percentages were used instead of absolute concentrations to focus on the overall modulation of the microbial fermentation profile and to minimize individual variability [24–27].

Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for intergroup comparisons of categorical variables. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) compared baseline continuous variables between groups. Post-intervention group differences were assessed using ANCOVA with baseline values as covariates. Paired t-tests compared pre-post intervention values within groups. Variables not normally distributed were natural logarithm (ln) transformed; the ln(x + 1) transformation was used for variables ranging from 0 to 1. For these non-normally distributed variables, all descriptive statistics in text and tables reflect the original, untransformed data to maintain clinical interpretability, whereas the corresponding test statistics and p-values from ANCOVA and intra-group paired t-tests were derived from the transformed data.

Intervention-induced changes were compared between groups using independent-sample t-tests or non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U tests) based on data distribution. Pearson or Spearman methods were used for correlation analysis according to variable distributions. For change variables with p < 0.05 in normality tests but approximately normal P-P/Q-Q plots, parametric tests were applied based on sample size.

Mediation analyses were performed using the SPSS PROCESS macro (Version 4.1, Model 4; 5,000 bootstrap resamples). The study tested whether the effect of group assignment (X: RG = 1, WG = 2) on key metabolic outcomes (Y: change in post-prandial DBP, change in BUN) was mediated by changes in specific SCFAs (M1: change in butyrate percentage, change in acetate percentage) or inflammatory indices (M2, explored but not presented as primary findings due to lack of significant mediation). Models were adjusted for relevant covariates, including gender, age, baseline BMI, baseline levels of the mediator (M), and baseline levels of the outcome variable (Y). The result for each path of the mediation model was reported as a regression coefficient (β) along with standard error (SE). Indirect effects were estimated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

Differential modulation of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles

Comparative analysis revealed distinct shifts in the fecal SCFA profile (Fig. 2). After adjusting for baseline values, the WG group showed a significantly higher percentage of butyrate post-intervention compared to the RG group (WG: 21.8 ± 6.3% vs. RG: 16.9 ± 5.6%, p < 0.001). In the RG group, acetate percentage increased significantly (7.2 ± 8.0 pp, p < 0.001), while reductions were observed in butyrate proportions (-6.5 ± 6.2 pp, p < 0.001). In contrast, the WG intervention elicited a rise in propionate percentage (2.5 ± 6.7 pp, p < 0.05), accompanied by decreases in butyrate percentages (-3.1 ± 7.4 pp, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Between-group comparisons of the intervention-induced changes highlighted these divergences (Fig. 2). The increase in acetate percentage was significantly smaller in the WG group compared to the RG group (WG: 0.7 ± 8.3 pp vs. RG: 7.2 ± 8.0 pp, p < 0.001). Conversely, the reduction in butyrate percentage was significantly less pronounced in the WG group than in the RG group (WG: -3.1 ± 7.4 pp vs. RG: -6.5 ± 6.2 pp, p = 0.012).

In addition to these major shifts, we observed minor but statistically significant differences in some less abundant SCFAs. For instance, a directional difference was observed for valerate, which decreased in the RG group but increased in the WG group (RG: -0.3 ± 1.9 pp vs. WG: 0.5 ± 1.7 pp, p = 0.043). While within-group analysis showed a slight reduction in isobutyrate in both groups (RG: -0.6 ± 2.0 pp; WG: -0.7 ± 2.2 pp; both p < 0.05), this change was not significantly different in the between-group comparison. Given the small magnitude and large variability of these minor SCFA changes, they are interpreted with caution as secondary, exploratory findings.

Caproate is not shown in Fig. 2 because it accounted for a very small proportion (about 0.1%-0.2%) and showed no significant differences during the intervention in either group. Detailed data are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Differential effects on metabolic indicators

Both dietary interventions demonstrated beneficial effects on key metabolic parameters (Fig. 3). Significant improvements were observed in fasting blood glucose (FBG) (RG: -0.8 ± 1.2 mmol/L; WG: -0.9 ± 1.1 mmol/L; both p < 0.001) and post-prandial blood glucose (PBG) (RG: -1.7 ± 2.0 mmol/L; WG: -1.5 ± 2.6 mmol/L; both p < 0.001). Reductions in blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (RG: -0.7 ± 1.2 mmol/L; WG: -0.9 ± 1.4 mmol/L; both p < 0.001) and serum creatinine (Cr) (RG: -10.1 ± 16.2 μmol/L; WG: -27.1 ± 21.4 μmol/L; both p < 0.001) were also evident post-intervention. However, only the WG group experienced a significant reduction in post-prandial diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (-3.4 ± 9.7 mmHg, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the decrease in serum creatinine was significantly greater in the WG group compared to the RG group (p < 0.001), with post-intervention creatinine levels being significantly lower in the WG cohort (WG: 70.3 ± 15.1 μmol/L vs. RG: 71.4 ± 31.2 μmol/L, p < 0.001). Divergent effects were observed for body composition (Fig. 4). While the WG group showed non-significant trends towards improvement, the RG group experienced significant changes: decreases in body fat mass (BFM) (-0.8 ± 1.7 kg, p < 0.05), percentage of body fat (PBF) (-1.2 ± 2.2 pp, p < 0.001), and visceral fat area (VFA) (-4.4 ± 11.2 cm2, p < 0.05), alongside increases in soft lean mass (SLM) (0.9 ± 3.2 kg, p < 0.05) and fat-free mass (FFM) (0.9 ± 3.3 kg, p < 0.05). Despite having lower absolute PBF than the RG group after 6 weeks (WG: 33.6 ± 8.3% vs. RG: 33.9 ± 7.4%, p < 0.05), the WG group exhibited smaller reductions in PBF (-0.2 ± 1.2 pp) and VFA (-0.9 ± 4.9 cm2) compared to the RG cohort (both p < 0.05). Since the changes in FFM aligned with SLM in both groups with no significant change in SMM, only SLM data are shown in Fig. 4, with the assumption that bone mass remained unchanged during the 6-week intervention. Discussion

This secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial extends our previous findings [29] by shifting the focus from immune-mediated inflammation to the differential impacts of whole versus refined grains consumption on metabolic health and body composition. We found that WG intake was linked to improvements in post-prandial diastolic blood pressure and renal function, while RG consumption was unexpectedly associated with short-term improvements in body composition. These distinct outcomes are potentially underpinned by our findings on SCFA modulation, which are consistent with the primary report: WG consumption relatively preserved the butyrate percentage, whereas RG intake promoted a distinct SCFA profile characterized by a significant increase in acetate proportion. By linking these specific SCFA profiles to the metabolic and body composition changes, our analysis serves to explore the plausible hypothesis that different grain types drive distinct physiological effects via the modulation of microbial metabolites. The contrasting roles of butyrate and acetate, explored through mediation analysis, further highlight the complexity of these microbial-host interactions.

Divergent modulation of short-chain fatty acid profiles

In this study, we found that WG and RG diets divergently regulated the acetate-butyrate axis, which likely reflects the distinct fermentation of complex versus simple carbohydrates [31]. The complex matrix of fibers (non-starch polysaccharides, resistant starches, oligosaccharides) in whole grains provides substrates favoring butyrate-producing microbes (e.g., key members within Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae) [32, 33], explaining the relative preservation of butyrate percentage in the WG group despite a modest overall decline. Conversely, easily digestible carbohydrates in refined grains may favor microbial communities or metabolic pathways predominantly yielding acetate [31, 34, 35]. Beyond these major SCFAs, we also noted subtle, statistically significant differences in branched-chain SCFAs like isobutyrate and minor SCFAs like valerate between the groups. Based on the small magnitude of changes, the physiological relevance remains to be established. Therefore, we consider these secondary findings as preliminary and hypothesis-generating for future studies on protein fermentation dynamics or specific fiber fermentation networks [28]. Taken together, our study provides evidence that dietary grain qualitatively alters the gut microbiota’s metabolic output in a real-world setting. These findings reinforce a shift in focus from mere SCFA quantity to the compositional balance of the SCFA profile, highlighting the divergent impact of WG versus RG intake on the functional acetate-butyrate relationship.

Whole grains, butyrate dynamics, and post-prandial vasoregulation

The exclusive improvement in post-prandial DBP in the WG group aligns with evidence linking WG intake to cardiovascular health [36]. Butyrate is a potent signaling molecule associated with vascular homeostasis, which may act synergistically in multiple pathways to achieve antihypertensive effects through activation of G-protein-coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43 on vascular cells, promotion of vasodilatation, inhibition of sympathetic and RAS activity, anti-inflammation, and enhancement of the intestinal barrier [37]. Mediation analysis intriguingly revealed a significant positive indirect effect of the WG intervention on DBP change via butyrate percentage change, operating as a statistical suppression effect [38]. This means that the relative preservation in butyrate percentage in the WG group was associated with less DBP reduction than the overall (direct) effect of the WG intervention itself. This does not contradict the observed net DBP reduction in the WG group but points towards a complex interplay. It may imply that while butyrate itself contributes to blood pressure regulation, the relative percentage change within the overall SCFAs pool might not fully capture its net effect, or that other WG components (e.g., magnesium, potassium, phytochemicals) may provide additional antihypertensive contributions that are partially masked when only considering the butyrate percentage pathway [39–41]. It is also important to consider that the physiological effects of butyrate may be complex and inconsistent, potentially depending on other variables, including its levels in both fecal and circulating systems [42] as well as interactions with host factors [24, 43]. Therefore, future studies are warranted to fully dissect these intricate relationships.

Superior renoprotection with whole grains

The greater reduction in serum creatinine observed in the WG group likely involves the gut-kidney axis, where microbial metabolites like SCFAs play a regulatory role [44]. Butyrate, in particular, may ameliorate renal injury by suppressing inflammation (partially supported by the inverse correlation between butyrate change and inflammatory indices in the WG group), mitigating oxidative stress, and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity [45]. Improved gut integrity can attenuate the translocation of pro-inflammatory molecules (e.g., LPS) and nephrotoxic gut-derived uremic toxins (e.g., indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate) into circulation, lessening stress on the kidneys [46, 47]. However, the positive correlation between propionate change and creatinine reduction observed specifically in the WG group adds a layer of complexity to the controversial metabolic and renal effects [16, 48]. While previous evidence indicated an inverse correlation between fecal propionate and creatinine levels [47], the context of the entire SCFA ecosystem reveals a more complicated picture: a pronounced increase in the proportion of propionate likely means the reduction in other beneficial SCFAs, such as butyrate, via competitive microbial pathways, potentially offsetting some renal benefits and leading to a less marked decrease in creatinine [48].

The more pronounced decrease in serum creatinine observed in the WG group (approximately 27% from baseline) suggests a clinically relevant renoprotective advantage for several reasons. First, a change of this magnitude might improve a patient’s estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), potentially reclassifying them into a lower-risk category for CKD progression [49]. Second, studies have consistently confirmed that even modest changes in renal function markers are strongly associated with long-term prognosis for mortality and kidney failure [50, 51]. Finally, given the initial hemodynamic-mediated "dip" in eGFR (or a rise in creatinine) often observed with potent agents like SGLT2 inhibitors, and despite this dip being part of the long-term renoprotective benefit [52], the dietary intervention directly reduced serum creatinine levels within a relatively short period, highlighting its potential to serve as a complementary strategy to pharmacological treatments.

Additionally, although both groups demonstrated reductions in BUN, mediation analysis revealed that the WG group achieved greater BUN decline through a smaller increase in acetate percentage compared to the RG group. This mediated effect counterbalanced the non-significant direct effect of WG intervention, which might theoretically maintain higher BUN levels due to WG’s relatively higher protein content [53]. Refined grains enhance acetate production, which reduces the demand for protein catabolism as an energy source by affecting glucose metabolism [18], thereby lowering BUN. In contrast, whole grains promote the production of propionate and butyrate, with a lower proportion of acetate. Propionate is a significant gluconeogenic precursor in the liver, which can also spare amino acids from catabolism [18]. Concurrently, propionate and butyrate indirectly contribute to BUN reduction by alleviating systemic inflammation, improving renal function, and enhancing urea excretion [48]. These combined effects of whole grains likely offset their lower acetate-related BUN modulation.

The paradox of refined grains and body composition

An unexpected finding of our study was a modest but statistically significant improvement in body composition within the RG group, including reductions in body fat and gains in lean mass. Although this finding aligns with our team’s prior research [29], it presents a seeming paradox given the relatively high glycemic nature of refined grains [54]. Thus, we must interpret this result with considerable caution due to the small magnitude of the observed changes and the short duration of the intervention. While seemingly counterintuitive, with other dietary habits remaining unchanged (Supplementary Table 4), we can offer several speculative hypotheses that warrant future investigation. Firstly, it may be attributed to the differences in macronutrient composition between the two grain types. Despite being rich in dietary fiber and micronutrients, whole grains also possess a relatively higher content of fat and protein compared to their refined counterparts [53]. Within the timeframe of a short-term intervention, this higher energy density and altered macronutrient profile might have partially counteracted the potential benefits of whole grains on satiety and metabolic health, thereby resulting in a lack of significant net advantage for body composition [55]. Another possibility relates to the parallel shift observed in microbial fermentation, specifically the significant increase in acetate percentage in the RG group. Acetate is known to influence host energy metabolism, with some evidence suggesting it may play a role in regulating energy expenditure, substrate metabolism, appetite, and inflammation [56, 57]. Future studies with longer durations and more detailed metabolic tracking are needed to confirm these findings and to disentangle the complex metabolic effects of different carbohydrate sources on host-microbiome interactions.

In addition, this study did not detect significant differences in the systemic immune-inflammation indices between groups, which differs from our team’s prior work showing WG significantly reduced specific pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-22, IL-23) in a similar population [29]. Composite indices based on blood cell counts may be less sensitive than specific cytokines in capturing pathway-specific immunomodulatory effects induced by dietary interventions. The 6-week duration might be sufficient to affect certain fast-responding cytokine pathways but insufficient to alter composite indices based on blood cell ratios, which may require longer intervention or be more pronounced in populations with specific baseline inflammatory states. Some studies [58, 59] showed that improvement in inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, etc.) could be seen in a few weeks (6 weeks or 8 weeks) of whole grain intervention. However, there are few reports on the improvement of systemic immune-inflammation markers. Some studies have shown no significant change or only a slight decrease in NLR after 12 weeks of dietary intervention [60].

Strengths and limitations

Performing a secondary analysis on data from a well-designed RCT provides an opportunity to investigate the mechanisms underlying whole-grain benefits with a relatively lower risk of bias. The comprehensive assessment encompassing SCFAs, metabolic parameters, body composition, and inflammatory markers, coupled with mediation analysis, offers a multi-dimensional perspective. Standardization of grain provision and structured dietary counseling enhanced intervention fidelity. However, limitations temper the interpretation: the 6-week intervention may not fully capture long-term adaptations; findings from this specific middle-aged and older Chinese cohort may not directly extrapolate to other populations; reliance on self-reported food diaries and some disease histories introduces potential bias; and the possibility of unmeasured confounding variables cannot be entirely dismissed. Furthermore, the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons across the various evaluated outcomes represents an important statistical limitation. Consequently, the risk of Type I errors may be inflated. Therefore, some of the statistically significant findings in this study should be interpreted with caution as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future large-scale, confirmatory studies are warranted to validate these observed associations. Additionally, our dietary assessment was based on the FFQ. While this is a standard tool for large-scale studies [61], it did not capture all sources of energy, notably cooking oils, leading to an underestimation of total energy and fat intake [62]. However, the primary goal of the dietary assessment was to confirm that background dietary patterns were similar between groups, and any systematic underestimation would have applied equally to both the WG and RG groups, thus not biasing the observed differences in our outcomes, which were driven by the controlled intervention. Based on the consideration of minimizing individual variations in SCFAs and investigating overall changes in the SCFA profile, our analysis focused on the relative proportions of fecal SCFAs rather than their absolute concentrations, an approach that has also been adopted in several previous studies [63–66]. Future studies incorporating absolute concentrations as well as circulating levels of SCFAs would provide a more complete understanding of SCFA-mediated host-microbe interactions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our analysis demonstrates that the consumption of whole versus refined grains is associated with distinct microbial and host metabolic profiles in middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, we observed that whole grain intake was linked to improvements in post-prandial blood pressure control and renal function markers, while refined grains were unexpectedly associated with modest, positive changes in body composition, accompanied by different shifts in gut-derived short-chain fatty acids. These findings highlight the complexity of diet-microbiome-host interactions and contribute to a more multifaceted understanding of the health effects of whole grains. The next steps should involve large-scale, prospective, long-term intervention trials to validate the durability of these effects. Furthermore, mechanistic studies integrating metagenomics and metabolomics are needed to establish causality between specific microbial shifts, SCFA production, and the observed host metabolic responses. Such research will be pivotal for developing personalized dietary strategies that leverage an individual’s microbiome or inflammatory state to optimize metabolic health.
