Lemierre-like syndrome and severe descending mediastinitis in an Infant with retropharyngeal abscess due to methicillin susceptible Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Shreya Chandran, Wimwipa Mongkonsritragoon, Ji-Yeon Kim, Nirupama Kannikeswaran, Nahed Abdel-Haq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An 11-month-old girl presented with fever, respiratory distress and septic shock. She had no neck stiffness, swelling, difficulty swallowing, or drooling. An echocardiogram that was done to evaluate cardiac function showed compression of the innominate vein. Subsequent cross-sectional imaging revealed retropharyngeal abscess (RPA) with extensive descending mediastinitis and abscesses as well as septic thrombophlebitis involving the left internal jugular vein, subclavian vein, and innominate vein, consistent with Lemierre-like syndrome. Blood cultures grew methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA). The patient had surgical drainage of the RPA, followed by mediastinal catheter placement. However, she eventually required a cardiopulmonary bypass for a complete mediastinal exploration and drainage with thymectomy, left lung decortication, and an allograft patch repair of superior vena cava (SVC) erosion under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. Despite resolution of infection with drainage and antibiotics, the clinical course was complicated by bilateral diaphragmatic paralysis, and the need for a tracheostomy and ventilatory support. The case highlights the potential for life-threatening complications of RPA due to S. aureus in infants. RPA and mediastinitis may develop in infants without the usual clinical findings of deep neck infection. Early recognition, prompt imaging, and aggressive multidisciplinary management are crucial for improving outcomes.

Introduction

Retropharyngeal abscess (RPA) predominantly affects children under 4 years old due to lymphogenic spread of infection from the oropharynx into the retropharyngeal lymph nodes, which regress in older children [1]. Deep neck infections (DNIs) are potentially life-threatening in young children, with complications including airway compromise, Ludwig’s angina, and sepsis [2]. Descending mediastinitis is more common following an RPA due to the anatomical correlation of the neck spaces. Most cases occur in infants and are caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [3]. Septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, or Lemierre’s syndrome (LS), is another rare but potentially fatal complication often caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum [4]. We report an infant with RPA due to methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), complicated by septic shock, extensive descending mediastinitis, Lemierre-like syndrome (LLS) involving major blood vessels, and injury to bilateral phrenic nerves causing diaphragm paralysis.

Case report

An 11-month-old girl, who was previously healthy, presented to the emergency department (ED) with low grade fever, nasal congestion and cough for 4 days, associated with increasing respiratory distress. She had a past history of wheezing and was given nebulized albuterol treatment without improvement. On presentation, her vital signs included a temperature of 39.5°C, heart rate of 200/minute, respiratory rate of 45/minute, and blood pressure of 97/56 mm Hg. She was in moderate respiratory distress with nasal flaring, diffuse crackles, chest retractions, and hypoxia, requiring immediate noninvasive positive pressure ventilation. A detailed examination of upper airways including the ears, nose and throat by the emergency room physician and later by an ear/nose/throat (ENT) specialist revealed symmetric normal tympanic membranes, intact palate, and the throat with no injection or exudates. The neck was supple with no tenderness or lymphadenopathy. The remaining physical examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory studies showed a white blood cell count of 18,000/mm3 (neutrophils 72 %, lymphocytes 11 %, monocytes 16 %, eosinophils 1 %), C-reactive protein (CRP) 373.9 mg/L, procalcitonin 58 µg/L, sodium 129 mg/dL, d-dimer 12 mg/L FEU, troponin 167 ng/L, fibrinogen 605 mg/dL. Prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time were normal. A chest radiograph showed mild cardiomegaly with bilateral diffuse airspace opacities. Blood culture was obtained, and intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and clindamycin were started. An echocardiogram showed a small pericardial effusion with normal cardiac anatomy and function, but the innominate vein could not be visualized, suggesting external compression by a mediastinal mass. On the second day of admission, the patient underwent a neck exploration revealing a large abscess tract deep to the sternocleidomastoid within the carotid and prevertebral spaces, which was surgically debrided. She became more clinically stable and was weaned off pressor support, but remained febrile, and blood cultures continued to grow MSSA with persistent mediastinitis and left side empyema on repeat CT imaging. Mediastinal and chest tubes were replaced by interventional radiology, with minimal drainage. The patient had persistent MSSA bacteremia for 6 consecutive days, followed by 3 days of negative blood cultures, and then MSSA bacteremia recurred. Due to prolonged bacteremia, clindamycin was discontinued and ertapenem was added for synergy with oxacillin and to provide additional Gram negative and anaerobic coverage. Subsequently blood cultures were negative.

The patient continued to have fever, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers. She underwent mediastinal exploration via sternotomy with cardiovascular surgery. Intraoperative findings revealed extensive mediastinal and left pleural infectious burden involving bilateral thymic glands with abscess extending into the pericardial reflection and near each phrenic nerve. There was an anterior mediastinal abscess with liquefactive exudate resulting in thrombosis of the innominate vein and erosion of the superior vena cava (SVC) as well as left lung empyema. She required cardiopulmonary bypass for complete mediastinal exploration with thymectomy, left lung decortication, and SVC allograft patch repair under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest.

Following surgery, the patient improved clinically. She completed 3 weeks of ertapenem and 6 weeks of oxacillin. The infection resolved, but she failed multiple extubation attempts. Bilateral diaphragmatic paralysis was confirmed by fluoroscopy. Repeat fluoroscopy four weeks after surgery showed continued diaphragm paralysis. Surgical options, including bilateral diaphragm plication, diaphragm pacing, and tracheostomy, were discussed with the family. The patient subsequently underwent a tracheostomy and gastrostomy tube placement and was discharged with a home ventilator. A workup for underlying immunodeficiency revealed normal immunoglobulin levels, T-cell subsets, B-cell subsets, and NK cell counts. Oxidative burst assay was normal. HIV antigen/antibody test was negative.

Discussion

Our patient presented with sepsis and respiratory distress without typical RPA symptoms like difficulty swallowing, drooling, neck movement limitations or mass. The diagnosis of RPA and mediastinitis was confirmed via cross-sectional imaging. Pediatric DNI complications occur at a rate of 5 % [5], with retropharyngeal or multiple space abscesses posing the highest risk [6]. Wilson et al. reported that 16 of 19 cases of descending mediastinitis were due to RPA and all were under 18 months old [3], emphasizing risk of mediastinal extension in this age. Most infections were caused by MRSA [3].

The spread of DNI is limited by the fascial planes. However, when resistance is weakened, infection can spread through these planes. DNI can spread to the mediastinum via three routes: The pretracheal route leading to the anterior mediastinum; the lateral pharyngeal route leading to the middle mediastinum and the retropharyngeal route leading to the posterior mediastinum [7]. The “danger space” is located posterior to the retropharyngeal space and prevertebral fascia. It is named so due to the tendency of infections in this space to spread inferiorly to the thorax because of the loose areolar contents leading to complications such as mediastinitis and empyema [8]. Infection in the danger space may result from retropharyngeal, parapharyngeal or prevertebral abscesses. The most common signs and symptoms of DNI are fever, dysphagia, odynophagia, drooling, trismus, neck pain or swelling, neck stiffness and torticollis. Patients with infections the retropharyngeal space present with fever, systemic toxicity, odynophagia, dysphagia, dyspnea and limitation of neck movements [7]. Pharyngeal examination may reveal bulging of the retropharyngeal space. Protrusion in that space may lead to compression of the supraglottic area and subsequent respiratory distress. Extension of infection into the danger space and prevertebral space can lead to involvement of the pleura and mediastinal spaces causing chest pain as well as neck pain and stiffness [7]. However, the most critical complication of retropharyngeal abscess extending is acute bacterial mediastinitis which can affect spread to the posterior mediastinum through the danger space and prevertebral fascia or can reach the anterior mediastinum and parietal pericardium via the pretrachial fascia [7], [9]. The infection evolves rapidly and has a high mortality rate [9].

LS typically involves pharyngeal infection, sepsis, and internal jugular vein thrombosis, usually caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum. If other aerobic organisms are involved, it is termed LLS [4]. Our case is notable for thrombophlebitis involving multiple major blood vessels and erosion of the superior vena cava. In a retrospective review by Van-Hoeke et al. of pediatric and adult patients with LLS due to Staphylococcus aureus, 7/25 (28 %) were 1 year or younger, which suggests that infants are particularly at risk of this complication [10]. Thrombosis of the internal jugular vein occurred in nearly all cases, with pulmonary abscess and empyema being the most common metastatic infections [10].

Retropharyngeal abscesses are polymicrobial, including both aerobic and anaerobic organisms. S. aureus and group A beta-hemolytic streptococcus are the most common aerobes. Most RPA cases due to S. aureus including MRSA have been reported in children younger than 2 years, with a particularly high incidence in those younger than one year [3]. Immaturity of the immune system and bacterial invasiveness may contribute to severe infections. Panton-Valentine leucocidin (PVL) has been linked to severe community-acquired MRSA infections; however, some MSSA strains can also produce PVL [11]. The MSSA strain isolated from our patient was not tested for PVL. Reports in the early 2000s indicated rising MRSA prevalence in pediatric neck abscesses. However, data from 2005 to 2014 have shown a decline in the overall proportion of MRSA in pediatric infections, with an increase in clindamycin resistance in S. aureus isolates [12]. Thus, treatment should be guided by regional susceptibility patterns.

Prolonged bacteremia in our patient may be due to extensive endovascular infection and inadequate abscess drainage. However, ertapenem has been reported as an adjunct to oxacillin or cefazolin in beta-lactam combination therapy for patients with persistent MSSA bacteremia, with a proposed mechanism of complementary binding to penicillin-binding protein sites. Clinical success with this combination regimen has been reported in adults and in a preterm neonate with osteomyelitis and persistent MSSA bacteremia [13], [14], [15].

Meta-analyses have shown that early, aggressive surgical interventions for adult mediastinitis yield better outcomes than conservative approaches [16]. The role of surgical intervention of mediastinitis due to RPA in children is unclear. In cases when clinical improvement occurs following RPA drainage, mediastinal fluid collections may be managed conservatively [3]. Our patient had partial clinical improvement with RPA drainage and mediastinal tube thoracostomy and required aggressive surgical debridement of mediastinal structures including a patch allograft repair of the SVC. Mortality rates of mediastinitis due to RPA remain low with current management practices and suggested to be < 10 % in pediatric case series [3].

In conclusion, infants with RPA and mediastinitis may present without typical symptoms such as neck stiffness, swelling, or drooling. Although mediastinitis is a recognized complication of RPA, this case uniquely describes significant morbidity with LLS, extensive mediastinal involvement and bilateral phrenic nerve paralysis caused by MSSA RPA. In patients with extensive intrathoracic infection, clinicians should suspect diaphragmatic injury, which can be overlooked in prolonged ventilation. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion of deep neck infection and the potential of life-threatening complications. Early recognition, prompt imaging, and multidisciplinary management are essential to improving outcomes.
