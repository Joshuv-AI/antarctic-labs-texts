Unveiling masturbatory sexual behaviours in Nigeria: insights into the prevalence and factors associated with self- and mutual masturbation among the sexually active population
Authors: Yusuf Bello, Deborah Oke, Temitope Ilori, Sikiru Adekola Adebayo, Tope Olubodun, Isaac Adewole, Suzanna Francis, Deborah Watson-Jones, Imran O Morhason-Bello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMasturbation is a common practice across various demographics worldwide. However, its prevalence among the general population in Nigeria remains unknown. This study investigates the prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation and their associated factors among sexually active Nigerians.MethodsData were obtained from the Sexual Behaviour and HPV Infection in Nigerians in Ibadan cross-sectional study, including males and females aged 18–45 years. After obtaining consent, participants were asked during face-to-face interviews whether they had ever touched their genitals or inserted fingers into the vagina or anus for sexual pleasure (self-masturbation) or whether they and their sexual partners had ever touched each other’s genitals by hand for sexual pleasure (mutual masturbation). The prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation was reported using percentages, while their associations with participants’ demographic, lifestyle, and biological characteristics were examined using chi-square tests. Poisson regression with robust variance was performed to identify associated factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsMutual masturbation (83.7%) was more common than self-masturbation (64.9%) among participants. The prevalence of self-masturbation was significantly higher among males than females (69.9% vs. 62.4%, p = 0.022), while mutual masturbation was significantly higher among females than males (86.6% vs. 78.1%, p = 0.001). Occupation, smartphone ownership, and alcohol consumption were significantly associated with self-masturbation, while ethnicity, location, number of penile-vaginal sex partners, and receiving oral sex were associated with mutual masturbation.ConclusionSelf- and mutual masturbation are highly prevalent among sexually active Nigerians, with distinct factors influencing each behaviour.Keywords: Self-masturbation, Mutual masturbation, Sex, Male, Female, Nigeria

Background

Masturbation is a sexual behaviour that involves stimulating one’s genital organs, usually the breast, vulva, clitoris, and penis, for the aim of sexual pleasure or gratification [1]. Masturbation can involve an individual exploring their own body, referred to as self-masturbation, or it can occur between two individuals, known as mutual masturbation [2]. Across the globe, debates about masturbation signify differences in cultural, religious, medical, and psychological views, reflecting varying beliefs and attitudes toward the practice. In many societies, masturbation has traditionally been regarded as taboo due to moral and cultural reasons [3]. However, modern research recognises it as a natural and healthy sexual behaviour that can improve self-awareness and well-being [1, 4].

Despite this, concerns persist about excessive masturbation, its effects on relationships, and its inclusion in sexual education, particularly in conservative settings where myths and misconceptions still dominate. In Nigeria, as in many parts of Africa, masturbation is similarly viewed negatively, primarily because of cultural and religious beliefs that classify it as immoral or harmful [5]. This perception of masturbation, especially in Africa, is mainly influenced by traditional narratives that associate it with physical or spiritual harm and stigmatize those who engage in it. Despite these generally held beliefs, existing studies have revealed that the practice of masturbation is common among various age groups and demographics. According to a survey conducted in the United States, it was revealed that 38% of women and 61% of men aged 18 to 60 had masturbated within the past year [6]. Similarly, a national survey of the British population aged 16 to 44 years indicated that 73% of men and 37% of women had engaged in masturbation in the previous month [7]. A similar prevalence was found in Australia and Portugal [8, 9]. In Africa, a study conducted in Ghana to explore adolescents’ attitudes towards masturbation in selected senior high schools reported that 83.7% of males and 16.3% of females categorically admitted to masturbating [10]. Also, a study conducted among 484 male medical students in Egypt to assess their perspective and practices of masturbation revealed a prevalence rate of 75.2% [11].

Existing studies have also identified the reasons for engaging in masturbation, with the most common motivation for both sexes being its role as a simple and safe way to explore one’s body and understand personal preferences for stimulation and arousal [12]. For women, the primary reasons include seeking physical pleasure and satisfaction, relieving sexual tension, aiding sleep, and compensating for a partner’s lack of interest [13, 14]. Among men, common motivations include protection against Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs), enhanced sexual pleasure, increased self-esteem, reduced sexual difficulties, and overall greater sexual satisfaction [15, 16]. Other identified reasons for engaging in masturbation among both sexes include higher pornography use, sexual boredom, and low relationship intimacy [17, 18].

Given that masturbation is a typical sexual behaviour across different age groups and is essential to sexual functioning, it is surprising that there is little to no knowledge of its prevalence in Nigeria. In a scoping review that examined masturbation in Africa, only five studies were found to have focused solely on masturbatory practices, and none was from Nigeria [19]. Only one study on the ethical implications of masturbation and the sexual revolution crisis among Nigerian youths was identified in our local literature database search. This study was conducted among members of the Baptist Association Conference in Ibadan, where 61.7% reported engaging in masturbation [20]. However, findings from faith-based studies are not generalizable to the broader Nigerian population, and there little empirical evidence to measure prevalence and identify associated factors from representative and general population samples. Furthermore, most published research on masturbation focuses on it as a solitary sexual activity, and it is unclear whether self or mutual masturbation was considered in some studies.

Against this backdrop, it is evident that there exist significant gaps in understanding the prevalence and factors associated with masturbatory behaviours in Nigeria. Therefore, this study sought to investigate the prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation, their associated factors, and potential associations with STIs, including Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and Human Papillomavirus (HPV), among the general population in Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria.

Methods

Study design, population, and study sites

This study adopted a quantitative, cross-sectional design to examine the prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation, their associated factors, and potential links with STIs. Data were collected between 2016 and 2018 through a study on Sexual Behaviour and HPV Infection in Nigerians in Ibadan (SHINI). Details of the SHINI study have been published in previous studies [21–23]. The study population was sexually active males and females aged 18–45 years. The research was conducted in Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria, and the specific study locations were Mokola, a densely populated urban community in the Ibadan North Local Government Area (LGA), and Moniya and Sasa, peri-urban communities in Akinyele LGA. The full description of the study design and the process of selecting study sites has been previously published [22, 23].

Sampling and enrolment of study participants

The participants were recruited using a two-stage sampling technique. Four enumeration areas (EAs) were randomly selected from the Moniya and Mokola residential communities and one EA from Sasa using the 2006 National Population Commission Census EAs [24] identification codes. Each house in the selected EAs was assigned a unique identification number, followed by a listing of males and females in each household who met the study’s age criteria. The house listing data from each study site were classified into two age groups: young adults (18–24 years) and older adults (25–45 years), to serve as a sampling frame. In the second stage, a systematic random sampling of eligible household members was conducted from the sampling frame until the desired sample size for the study was achieved.

The selected participants in each household were visited at home by the research assistants, who fully explained the study objectives and an information leaflet in English or a local language. Consenting male and female residents were invited to the clinic individually to participate in the study. Potential participants were contacted 72 h and 24 h before the clinic date, and a short message service (SMS) reminder was sent on the morning of the clinic appointment. At the clinic, a gender-matched research assistant discussed the study’s objectives again and provided a copy of the information leaflet to complement the discussion. Participants then signed a written or witnessed informed consent form, which included consent for the collection of biological samples for HPV and HIV testing.

Afterward, a face-to-face interview was conducted in the participant’s preferred language (either English or a local language) using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was pilot-tested to identify questions that required refinement for clarity and conciseness. Feedback from the pilot test was used to enhance the reliability of the main study and to ensure that participants clearly understood the questions. The information collected included participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and smoking, and sexual behaviours, including non-penetrative sexual activities. Specifically, participants were asked if they had ever touched their genitals or inserted fingers into the vagina or anus for sexual pleasure, as well as whether they and their sexual partners had ever touched each other’s genitals by hand for sexual pleasure since their sexual debut. Participants’ awareness and knowledge of HPV and its vaccination were also recorded. A gender-matched nurse then collected a blood sample for HIV testing and other biological samples for HPV testing. A detailed description of the sample collection procedure for both males and females at each anatomical site (oral, cervix, anal, vulva, and penis), along with information on storage and transportation, has been previously published [21–23].

The rapid diagnostic test (RDT) HIV kit was used for HIV testing. The HIV testing was performed according to the National Guidelines for HIV Prevention, Treatment, and Care, Federal Ministry of Health, Nigeria [25]. Participants who tested positive for HIV were referred for management following the national protocol. Other biological samples were shipped to the Catalan Institute of Oncology in Spain for HPV genotyping. The Anyplex™ II HPV28 assay (Seegene, Seoul, South Korea) was used for HPV genotyping. DNA was extracted from oral samples using the Maxwell® 16 LEV Blood DNA Kit (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA) and from anal, vulvar, cervical, and penile swabs using the Maxwell® 16 Buccal Swab LEV DNA Purification Kit (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA). Details of the DNA extractions from various biological samples have been previously published [21, 22].

Data entry and analysis

The data analysis was performed using STATA 15.0 (Stata 2019. Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). The prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation was reported using frequency and percentage. A bar plot was used for the graphical representation of these behaviours. The distribution of self- and mutual masturbation across participants’ socio-demographic, sexual behaviours, and biological characteristics was presented using frequency and percentage for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range for continuous variables, depending on the nature of the variables. The association between self- and mutual masturbation with participants’ characteristics was examined using the chi-square test of association. Risk factors associated with self- and mutual masturbation were assessed using Poisson regression with robust variance to ensure accurate and reliable estimates. Poisson regression with robust variance was chosen due to the high prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation; logistic regression odds ratios have been found to overestimate the prevalence ratio when prevalence is high [26, 27]. Crude and adjusted relative risks, 95% confidence intervals, and associated p-values were reported for each participant’s characteristics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 941 participants were recruited for the SHINI study, of which 625 are females (including 315 female sex workers) and 316 are males. Of the total participants, 611 (64.9%) had self-masturbated at some point since their sexual debut, while 787 (83.7%) reported having engaged in mutual masturbation (Fig. 1).

Prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation among sexually active males and females in Ibadan, Nigeria

Self-masturbation prevalence was significantly higher among males than females (69.9% vs. 62.4%, p = 0.022). Divorced, separated, or widowed participants (72.8%) engaged in self-masturbation than single (66.8%) and married participants (58.5%) (p = 0.004). Alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and illicit drug intake were significantly associated with self-masturbation (p < 0.001). The results showed that the prevalence of self-masturbation was higher among participants who practiced other non-penile-vaginal sexual behaviours, such as receiving or giving oral sex and anal sex. Of those who engaged in penile-vaginal sex, the highest prevalence was among those with 5–10 partners compared to those with one partner. Awareness of HPV and its infection in oral or genital sites, or in any anatomical site, was not associated with self-masturbation.

The results indicated that mutual masturbation was significantly more prevalent among females than males (86.6% vs. 78.1%, p = 0.001). Participants aged 18–24 reported the lowest rates of mutual masturbation at 76.0% compared to other age groups (p < 0.001). Additionally, the findings revealed that both ethnicity (p < 0.001) and religion were significantly associated with mutual masturbation, with the highest prevalence observed among Igbo participants (96.8%) compared to other ethnic groups and among Christians (89.3%) compared to Muslims (75.3%). The prevalence of mutual masturbation also significantly increased with higher monthly income among participants (p < 0.001). Married participants reported the lowest rates of mutual masturbation (77.9%) compared to single and divorced or widowed participants. Occupation (p < 0.001), study setting (p < 0.001), and lifestyle characteristics, including alcohol use (p < 0.001), tobacco or cigarette use (p = 0.002), and illicit drug use (p = 0.024) were significantly associated with mutual masturbation.

For participants’ sexual behavioural characteristics, the prevalence of mutual masturbation significantly increased with the number of penile-vaginal sex partners, with the lowest prevalence among those with single penile-vaginal sex partner (p < 0.001). The results also revealed that mutual masturbation was significantly higher among those who practiced safe sexual behaviour through condom use (89.2%) compared to those who did not use condoms (78.6%) (p < 0.001). Also, the prevalence of mutual masturbation was significantly higher among those who had received or given oral sex (p < 0.001); however, mutual masturbation was not significantly associated with anal sex (p = 0.282). In addition, the results showed that participants who had hrHPV infection at any anatomical site had a significantly higher prevalence of mutual masturbation compared to those without hrHPV infection at any site (87.2% vs. 78.9%, p = 0.001). Genital hrHPV infection was significantly associated with mutual masturbation (p < 0.001). Nearly all HIV-positive participants (98%) practiced mutual masturbation.

Table 3 shows the crude and adjusted relative risks for self- and mutual masturbation across participants’ sociodemographic, sexual behaviour, and biological characteristics. In the crude model, males had a significantly higher relative risk of self-masturbation compared to females (Crude Relative Risk (CRR) = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.23). For divorced or widowed participants, the results showed that they had a higher relative risk of self-masturbation [CRR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.97–1.23] compared to single participants, while married participants had a significantly lower relative risk of self-masturbation [CRR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.78–0.98] compared to single participants (p = 0.004). Alcohol consumption, cigarettes smoking habits, and use of illicit drugs were associated with higher risks of self-masturbation (p < 0.001). Other significant sociodemographic characteristics associated with the risk of self-masturbation included occupation (p = 0.030) and ownership of a smart phone (p = 0.003). Having multiple penile-vaginal sex partners was significantly associated with a higher risk of self-masturbation compared to having a single sexual partner (p = 0.012).

Crude and adjusted model of factors associated with risk of self- and mutual masturbation among sexually active males and females in ibadan, Nigeria

After adjusting for other variables in the model, sex was no longer significantly associated with self-masturbation. The result also showed that participants who owned a smart phone had a 45% higher relative risk of self-masturbation compared to those without a smart phone [Adjusted Relative Risk (ARR) = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.14–1.85]. Participants who had consumed alcohol significantly had 1.49 (95% CI: 1.02–1.23) times higher relative risk of self-masturbation compared to those who had never consumed alcohol. In addition, participants’ occupation was significantly associated with self-masturbation (p = 0.020).

In the crude model for mutual masturbation, males had a significantly lower relative risk of mutual masturbation compared to females [CRR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.85–0.97, p = 0.003]. Participants from rural settlements had a lower relative risk of mutual masturbation compared to those from urban settlements [CRR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.77–0.88]. The analysis notably showed that all sociodemographic characteristics were significantly associated with mutual masturbation, except for education (p = 0.342) and ownership of a smartphone (p = 0.570). Likewise, the number of penile-vaginal sex partners and safe sexual practices through the use of condoms were significantly associated with a higher risk of mutual masturbation. Also, the practice of oral sex, either received [CRR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.19–1.30] or given [CRR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.14–1.25], was associated with a higher risk of mutual masturbation (p < 0.001).

After adjusting for other variables in the model, participants with multiple penile-vaginal sex partners had a higher relative risk of mutual masturbation compared to those with a single partner (p = 0.010). Participants who had received oral sex had an 11% higher risk of mutual masturbation compared to those who had not received oral sex. The analysis showed that ethnicity (p = 0.004) and study setting (p = 0.029) were associated with the risk of mutual masturbation after controlling for other variables in the model.

Discussion

In this study, mutual masturbation was more common than self-masturbation among the participants. The results of the study revealed gender differences in the prevalence of self-masturbation and mutual masturbation. Self-masturbation was significantly more prevalent among males than females, while mutual masturbation was significantly more prevalent among females than males. The gender differences in the reported prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation in our study were consistent with previous research, which reported a higher prevalence of self-masturbation among males than females and a higher prevalence of mutual masturbation among females than males [7, 28, 29].

The analysis of the study results indicates a higher prevalence of mutual masturbation (83.7%) than self-masturbation (64.9%). This result contrasts with the findings of the U.S. National Survey of Sexual Health and Behaviour (NSSHB) conducted among participants aged 18–49 years between 2009 and 2018. The NSSHB reported a higher prevalence of self-masturbation (74.4%) compared to mutual masturbation (37.8%) [30]. Considering that the age groups in NSSHB and SHINI are nearly the same, the difference in the results indicates a significant difference in the perception and acceptance of self- and mutual masturbation between Western countries and Nigeria, with mutual masturbation generally being more acceptable than self-masturbation among the Nigerian population. Societal and cultural norms in Nigeria place great emphasis on partner-involved sexual activities and stigmatize self-masturbation.

Furthermore, the results showed that participants’ occupation was significantly associated with the risk of self-masturbation, with a higher risk among skilled and semi-skilled workers compared to unskilled workers and those without paid jobs. This indicates an established relationship between education and occupational status, with individuals with higher education levels more likely to engage in skilled labour than those with little or no education [31, 32]. Evidence in existing literature in Nigeria have also identified that occupational status signify social class. The higher risk of self-masturbation among skilled workers may, therefore, be attributed to greater access to information, sex education, and public discourse, which may reduce the fear and guilt associated with masturbation and increase the willingness to report it. In tandem with this, a study that examined sexual activity shared with a relationship partner or self-masturbation, and how pleasure specifically predicts well-being and work outcomes among skilled workers, reported that pleasurable sexual activity, such as self-masturbation, is related to perceived recovery from work stress, job satisfaction, work engagement, and life satisfaction [33]. The significant risk of self-masturbation increases with social class and occupational status, which also supports findings reported in the British National Probability Survey [7].

Another key finding of this study is that smartphone ownership was associated with a higher risk of self-masturbation compared to those without a smartphone. This reiterates findings in existing studies that have explored the relationship between smartphone use, pornography consumption, and self-masturbation [34–36]. This relationship is mainly attributed to free access to internet pornography, particularly through smartphones, which has led to a high prevalence of self-masturbation, especially among the youths. While many countries have laws prohibiting pornography, including still images, videos, or films, with or without the consent of those depicted, as well as the printing, distribution, publication, sale, supply, or exhibition of such materials, some countries also regulate access to pornography by introducing age restrictions and other measures. However, Nigeria lacks national laws prohibiting or restricting pornography, except for the transmission of child pornography. This may explain the higher risk of self-masturbation among those with smartphones due to unrestricted access to pornography.

The study found alcohol consumption to be associated with the risk of self-masturbation, with participants who reported ever consuming alcohol having a higher risk of engaging in self-masturbation. While there has not been formal research specifically investigating the relationship between alcohol consumption and self-masturbation, there is evidence of alcohol consumption influencing sexual behaviours [37–39]. Alcohol affects the central nervous system by increasing impulsivity and reducing anxiety [40, 41]. This may lead to lower social constraints related to expressing or exploring sexual behaviours such as self-masturbation. Alcohol consumption can stimulate dopaminergic pathways linked to pleasure or reward-seeking behaviours, which may encourage activities associated with personal gratification, such as self-masturbation.

An important finding of this study is that the prevalence of self-masturbation was highest among divorced, widowed, or separated participants, followed by single participants, and lowest among married participants. However, while the majority of studies conclude that self-masturbation is a substitute for partnered sexual activity, our data do not provide sufficient evidence to support this claim, as marital status was not significantly associated with the risk of self-masturbation after adjusting for other participant characteristics in our study. Therefore, it is essential to consider self-masturbation not solely as a substitute for partnered sex but also as an expression of sexual autonomy, stress relief, or personal preference among our study population. The findings indicate that divorced, widowed, or separated participants had a higher risk of self-masturbation compared to single and married participants, which may be attributed to reduced or no access to a regular sexual partner or changes in sexual habits following significant life events.

The study found a significant difference in the prevalence of mutual masturbation among the major ethnic groups in Nigeria, with almost all Igbo participants (96.8%) reporting ever engaging in mutual masturbation, followed by Hausa/Fulani participants (89.2%). However, Hausa/Fulani participants had a higher risk of engaging in mutual masturbation compared to Igbo participants, and both Hausa/Fulani and Igbo ethnic groups had a higher risk of engaging in mutual masturbation than the Yoruba ethnic group. These findings signify the different socialization patterns that characterize the various ethnic groups in Nigeria. Similar trends have been documented in existing studies, which report a high prevalence of self- and mutual masturbation among Igbo participants as trends in various sexual behavioural studies have shown that Igbo people are more liberal and expressive about their sexual activities and behaviours compared to other ethnic groups in Nigeria [42–44].

Additionally, the study findings revealed that participants who reside in rural areas were less likely to engage in mutual masturbation compared to participants who reside in urban areas. This is because rural areas tend to have stronger religious and traditional beliefs that discourage or condemn behaviours perceived as non-normative or outside the scope of procreative sex. In contrast, participants who reside in urban areas were generally more exposed to diverse perspectives on sexual behaviours due to greater access to information through media, education, and social networks. These factors may contribute to the lower engagement in mutual masturbation observed among rural participants.

The analysis of the study found that sexual behavioural characteristics are significantly associated with the risk of mutual masturbation. Participants with multiple sexual partners had a higher risk of engaging in mutual masturbation compared to participants with a single sexual partner. This finding is consistent with earlier studies that reported individuals who frequently masturbate with their partners to also engage in intercourse more frequently as well as have more sexual partners [45, 46]. This could be attributed to the fact that people with multiple sexual partners are often more open to various forms of sexual expression to satisfy their sexual fantasies. Mutual masturbation was also relatively low risk in terms of STIs and unwanted pregnancies. This implies that people with multiple sexual partners might engage in mutual masturbation as a safer alternative, especially in relationships where STI prevention is a priority.

Another evident key finding in this study is the existence of a higher risk of engaging in mutual masturbation among participants who engage in other non-penile-vaginal sexual activities, such as receiving oral sex from their partner. These findings align with previous studies that reported an association between mutual masturbation and oral sex among both males and females [47]. In tandem with this, another study found that mutual masturbation was a prerequisite activity for oral sex [48]. This finding indicates that similar to individuals with multiple sexual partners, those engaging in diverse sexual activities other than penile-vaginal sex, such as oral sex, might adopt mutual masturbation as a safer sexual practice to reduce the risk of STIs and pregnancy. Likewise, participants who receive oral sex from their partners tend to show greater openness to exploring diverse sexual activities, may view sexual pleasure as multifaceted, and are more inclined to engage in mutual masturbation as part of their sexual activities.

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this study includes the inclusion of both adolescents and adults age groups, as well as the explicit and separate definitions of self- and mutual masturbation, allowing for a more complete and nuanced view of these behaviours compared to other sexual behavioural studies that do not distinguish between self- and mutual masturbation. Another key strength is the use of gender-matched research assistants to conduct live, in-person interviews to collect the data to reduce the bias often associated with reporting delicate subjects such as self- and mutual masturbation behaviours.

Some of the potential limitations of the study include non-documentation of the reasons for engaging in self- and mutual masturbation, making it difficult to understand why there is a very high prevalence of these behaviours despite the religious and cultural beliefs against them, particularly self-masturbation. Therefore, future research studies could address this gap by employing validated scales to assess factors such as sexual autonomy, guilt, and social norms, which may provide more insight into why participants engage in these behaviours. Another limitation is the lack of data on the frequency of self- and mutual masturbation. Thus, there is a need to develop a more detailed questionnaire that captures the frequency, context, and variation across gender and age groups. This would help clarify the role of these behaviours in people’s sexual lives. Another limitation is because the cross-sectional design of the study limits our ability to infer causal relationships between the identified associated factors and engagement in self- and mutual masturbation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed that mutual masturbation is more prevalent than self-masturbation generally, and it is associated with significant sex differences that are influenced by cultural, social, and behavioural factors. To address this, future studies could adopt a longitudinal or experimental design to understand the directionality of these relationships better and examine how the frequency of these behaviours changes over different seasons of the year. It is therefore recommended that further research should explore the frequency and reasons for engaging in self- and mutual masturbation, as well as the role of certain personality traits, to assess the impact of these behaviours on people’s sexual lives, particularly among those at high risk for STIs. The misconceptions surrounding masturbation should be addressed within sexual health education to improve public sexual health outcomes.
