Deep learning for high-resolution material texture enhancement in 3D environments
Authors: Kenneth Alonso, Gustavo Patow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTextures are central to the perceived realism of 3D environments, yet their generation and upscaling often rely on methods that produce low-quality or repetitive results. These limitations are particularly evident in high-fidelity applications, such as films, animations, video games, and virtual reality, where diverse material properties must be represented consistently at high resolution. Here, we introduce a texture-enhancement framework that improves the resolution and quality of multiple material channels, including color, displacement, metalness, normal, and roughness maps. The method integrates a batch-based processing pipeline, a material decompression-recompression strategy, and a “scalable tiling” (e.g., dividing the texture into patches that can be processed and reassembled seamlessly), enabling efficient handling of large textures while preserving spatial coherence. The model is trained on a curated dataset of relevant materials spanning surfaces such as stone, metal, wood, tile, and fabric, allowing it to reproduce fine-grained and material-specific characteristics. Quantitative and qualitative evaluations demonstrate that the proposed approach outperforms existing techniques in terms of clarity and fidelity, offering a practical solution for enhancing texture quality in both real-time and offline rendering contexts. The dataset and code are available on GitHub1.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Realistic visual appearance in interactive 3D environments relies heavily on the quality of material textures, which encode both color and a range of physical properties such as displacement, metalness, normal variation, and surface roughness. These maps determine how virtual objects interact with light and are essential for conveying material identity and spatial detail. As modern applications increasingly demand high-fidelity assets, the ability to generate and process high-resolution textures has become a central challenge.

Existing texture enhancement and upscaling techniques often face limitations when applied to large materials or multi-channel maps. High-resolution inputs can create computational bottlenecks, exceed memory constraints, or produce inconsistencies such as loss of detail and visible seams during scaling. These issues restrict the use of detailed textures in graphics pipelines that must support both real-time and offline rendering workflows.

Recent studies in visual perception and image understanding have demonstrated the effectiveness of deep learning models in enhancing visual fidelity under challenging conditions. For instance, approaches such as WaterCycleDiffusion leverage visual-textual fusion to improve underwater image enhancement, while studies on underwater color disparities explore perceptual cues to restore natural color consistency. Beyond conventional imaging, learning-based perception frameworks have also been applied to compact high-frequency surface wave radar (HFSWR) data, including spectrum-to-graph modeling with graph convolutional networks (S2G-GCN) and joint detection-and-tracking paradigms based on reinforcement learning. Although these methods target different domains, they collectively highlight a growing trend toward perceptually informed, data-driven enhancement pipelines that aim to preserve structural coherence and semantic consistency across complex visual representations2–5.

We hypothesize that a modular pipeline that breaks textures into tiles, enhances them, and recombines them to preserve details can mitigate these constraints. A batch-based architecture capable of splitting materials into smaller units for processing may enable resolution increases beyond conventional limits while preserving local and global texture coherence.

To address these challenges, we introduce a deep-learning approach for material texture enhancement that distributes computation across manageable patches, allowing efficient processing of high-resolution textures (Fig. 1). The training pipeline integrates decompression, modification, and recompression stages, and incorporates pixel-wise, perceptual, and adversarial losses to guide optimization (Fig. 2). During inference, textures are segmented, processed through the model, and reassembled to produce super-resolved outputs, which are evaluated against ground-truth materials using standard image quality metrics (Fig. 3).

Previous work

Enhancing texture resolution for digital materials has seen significant advances, with methodologies evolving to meet the complex demands of modern rendering and simulation pipelines. This section outlines key developments, focusing on multi-channel texture enhancement, performance optimization, scalability, and the integration of custom datasets6–8.

Enhancing the resolution of texture maps is a critical task in improving the visual fidelity of materials used in computer graphics, gaming, and virtual reality. Traditional super-resolution techniques often focus solely on color images, neglecting essential material properties such as displacement, metalness, normals, and roughness. To address this limitation, we propose a novel approach leveraging state-of-the-art models with custom adaptations designed to optimize material upscaling29,30,9.

Enhanced Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Networks (ESRGAN)10,11 is a technique (Fig. 5) that improves upon conventional super-resolution methods by leveraging adversarial training and perceptual losses to generate high-quality, high-resolution images. ESRGAN utilizes a deep convolutional neural network comprising a generator and a discriminator, where the generator aims to produce visually plausible, high-resolution images, and the discriminator differentiates between real and generated images31,32,12.

Overview of the ESRGAN pipeline. A schematic representation illustrating the key components and workflow of the ESRGAN architecture.

ESRGAN has subsequently been extended in several influential works to address specific limitations, including handling real-world degradation, enhancing perceptual fidelity, and incorporating semantic consistency. ESRGAN-DP (ESRGAN with Dual Perceptual Loss)13 focuses on improving visual quality and reducing artifacts by employing a novel Dual Perceptual Loss (DP Loss). This loss function moves beyond reliance on a single pre-trained network by extracting complementary perceptual features from both the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) network and the Residual Network (ResNet). A dynamic weighting mechanism is utilized within the DP Loss to stabilize the relative magnitude between the VGG loss and the ResNet loss, ensuring that the network effectively learns both sets of features. Meanwhile, Real-ESRGAN14 extends the capability of ESRGAN to address real-world blind super-resolution by training with pure synthetic data. To accurately simulate complex real-world degradations, it introduces a high-order degradation modeling process and uses sinc filters during synthesis to model common ringing and overshoot artifacts. To maintain stable training dynamics and improve discriminatory capability when dealing with this large degradation space, Real-ESRGAN utilizes a U-Net discriminator integrated with spectral normalization. Finally, SU-ESRGAN (Semantic and Uncertainty-Aware ESRGAN)15 was proposed specifically to enhance the reliability of super-resolution in critical applications, such as geospatial analysis of satellite and drone imagery. SU-ESRGAN addresses the issue of hallucinated features in GAN outputs by integrating semantic segmentation loss to ensure class and object preservation. It differs from Real-ESRGAN by incorporating semantic guidance to ensure that class details are preserved and not merely sharpened into “hallucinated” details. It also incorporates Bayesian uncertainty estimation via Monte Carlo Dropout within the generator architecture to produce pixel-wise confidence maps.

The generator architecture is based on Residual-in-Residual Dense Blocks (RRDB)16, which eliminates batch normalization to preserve feature flexibility and enhance fine texture details. Meanwhile, the discriminator is designed using a Visual Geometry Group (VGG) architecture to assess the realism of high-resolution outputs. The interplay between these networks enables ESRGAN to achieve superior image quality compared to traditional methods17.

Multi-channel Texture Enhancement approaches often focus solely on enhancing color textures, overlooking other crucial material properties. Recent work, such as CTE-Net, emphasizes enhancing multiple channels, including displacement, metalness, normals, and roughness, to ensure comprehensive material fidelity. Unlike Real-ESRGAN’s focus on general artifact removal, CTE-Net utilizes Local Binary Patterns (LBP) to guide its decision logic specifically toward texture recovery. This approach has enabled more realistic material rendering by capturing finer surface details and lighting interactions across different channels18–20.

The computational intensity of texture super-resolution demands efficient resource management. Batch processing has emerged as a solution, significantly reducing processing time and memory usage. Techniques like those employed by Reyes-Saldaña and Rivera19 illustrate how dividing tasks into smaller, parallelizable units can enhance throughput without compromising output quality19–21.

A critical advancement in texture workflows is the development of unified pipelines for material compression and decompression. These pipelines streamline the handling of high-resolution textures, optimizing storage and retrieval processes. By maintaining high fidelity during compression, these methods facilitate seamless transitions between storage and active usage states22,20.

Handling large textures has traditionally been constrained by memory and processing power. To overcome this, methods such as SyncMVD23 and TexFusion11 employ tiling strategies that split textures into smaller, manageable sections. While Real-ESRGAN operates on single 2D images, TexFusion and SyncMVD ensure global consistency across 3D objects by performing denoising diffusion iterations in multiple camera views. These sections are processed independently and later merged, ensuring global consistency while bypassing input size limitations33,34,22.

The importance of dataset specificity in training models for texture enhancement cannot be overstated. Models trained on generalized datasets often fail to capture the unique characteristics of specialized materials. Leveraging custom datasets enables tailored learning processes that enhance the model’s ability to generalize across various material types19,24.

Maintaining consistency across different views or sections of a texture is crucial. Approaches such as those adopted in TexFusion utilize synchronized multi-view denoising processes to mitigate inconsistencies, resulting in smoother transitions and coherent textures even across large-scale materials22,20.

Recent advancements have also incorporated gradient-based and structural similarity losses into their training regimes. These loss functions enhance the network’s ability to reconstruct fine details and maintain structural coherence, resulting in improved perceptual quality19,20.

Enhanced Render Pass Integration. The integration of specialized render passes, including normal and position maps, further enriches the learning process. By providing additional geometric and lighting context, these render passes enable models to better predict and enhance textures under varying lighting and view conditions22,25.

Addressing the challenge of representing high-fidelity materials in real-time rendering, Zeltner et al.26 introduced a comprehensive system designed to render film-quality materials previously reserved for offline use. This approach achieves real-time execution by baking complex, multi-layered shading graphs into a compact unified neural representation, utilizing a learned hierarchical texture interpreted by neural decoders. Complementing this work in managing rendering resources, Vaidyanathan et al.27 proposed a novel neural technique for the low-bitrate compression of material textures. This approach significantly reduces texture storage requirements by treating the entire texture set (such as diffuse color, normal maps, and roughness) as a single entity.

These advancements lay a strong foundation for future research in texture resolution enhancement, which could lead to more efficient, high-quality material workflows in both real-time and offline rendering.

Method

Our method introduces several enhancements over the state-of-the-art in this field, including batch processing to improve computational efficiency, a decompression-recompression pipeline for material restoration, and a “scalable tiling” framework for processing large textures based on dividing the texture into patches that can be processed and reassembled seamlessly. Additionally, our model has been trained on a custom material database to improve generalization and accuracy. In the following subsections, we describe the fundamental ESRGAN model (Sect. 3.1), our custom modifications (Sect. 3.2), and the combination loss function that optimizes our approach (Sect. 3.3).

Enhanced model

To address the unique challenges of texture super-resolution, we developed a modified pipeline in the ESRGAN framework, incorporating several key architectural and methodological enhancements. Our goal was to create a robust system that not only increases texture resolution but also preserves critical material properties and maintains high visual fidelity.

The Generator is based on the Residual-in-Residual Dense Block (RRDB) architecture16, which significantly improves the model’s ability to learn complex texture patterns. The configuration is 64 feature maps (nf = 64) and 23 RRDB blocks (nb = 23), amounting to about 16.7 million trainable parameters.

The structure consists of approximately 350 convolutional layers, composed of an initial convolutional head (3→64), followed by the trunk of 23 RRDBs (each comprising 15 convolutional layers with dense connections), a trunk output convolution, two PixelShuffle-based upsampling stages for 4× super-resolution, and a final convolutional layer that reconstructs the RGB (or multi-channel) output. A notable departure from conventional architectures is the complete omission of batch normalization, which avoids texture distortion and allows more flexible feature distributions. Residual scaling is also employed to stabilize training by constraining the magnitude updates. This technique is widely used in deep residual networks where a constant parameter is used to scale down the residual (the output of the convolutional layers) before it is added back to the main identity path.

The Discriminator adopts a VGG-inspired design (DiscriminatorVGG128)16, configured to operate on 128 × 128 pixel input patches. It consists of approximately 10 layers (8 convolutional and 2 dense), yielding around 2.6 million parameters. Through progressive convolutional blocks with LeakyReLU activations, followed by fully connected layers, it distinguishes between authentic and generated textures. By optimizing a relativistic adversarial loss, the discriminator encourages the generator to produce textures that are perceptually indistinguishable from real-world material samples.

Overall, the enhanced model pipeline comprises about 360 layers and 19.3 million trainable parameters across the generator and discriminator. Figure 6 illustrates this architecture, highlighting the convolutional trunk of the generator and the VGG-style discriminator.

A key innovation of our framework is its extension of capabilities beyond traditional RGB images. We have enabled multi-channel material upscaling, allowing the model to simultaneously process and enhance various material maps, including displacement, metalness, normals, and roughness. This comprehensive approach ensures that all material properties remain consistent and physically plausible across different resolutions, which is essential for realistic rendering.

To handle large-scale textures efficiently, our pipeline integrates several performance-enhancing strategies. Large textures are processed in batches to optimize both memory usage and computational speed. A decompression-recompression preprocessing sequence further preserves fine details that might otherwise be lost. For textures exceeding memory limitations, the system divides them into smaller patches, which are individually super-resolved and seamlessly merged without visible artifacts.

Finally, to ensure the model performs optimally on material-specific data, it was trained on a custom, curated database of materials. Unlike general photographic datasets, this collection captures the unique statistical properties of materials, enabling the model to generalize effectively across diverse material types and intricate structures. As a result, the enhanced ESRGAN outperforms conventional approaches in producing high-resolution textures that are both visually and physically realistic.

Combination loss functions

To ensure high-quality texture upscaling, we employ a combination of loss functions tailored for material textures. The framework is based on a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) architecture, consisting of a generator that produces super-resolution textures and a discriminator that distinguishes between real and generated textures. The loss functions are grouped according to whether they guide the generator or the discriminator.

Generator losses

The generator is optimized using a weighted combination of pixel-level, perceptual, and adversarial objectives:

Pixel Loss: Measures the pixel-wise difference between the generated super-resolution image (sr) and the high-resolution ground truth (hr). We use either L1 (Mean Absolute Error) or L2 (Mean Squared Error) loss. This term is multiplied by a weighting factor that controls how strongly the generator prioritizes exact pixel-by-pixel accuracy.

Pixel Loss: Measures the pixel-wise difference between the generated super-resolution image (sr) and the high-resolution ground truth (hr). We use either L1 (Mean Absolute Error) or L2 (Mean Squared Error) loss. This term is multiplied by a weighting factor that controls how strongly the generator prioritizes exact pixel-by-pixel accuracy.

Content Loss (Perceptual Loss): Utilizes feature maps extracted from a pre-trained VGG network to preserve perceptual quality and structural integrity. It is computed as the difference between the feature representations of hr and sr. A feature-level weight determines the emphasis on high-level content and structural similarity.

Generator Loss (GAN Loss): Evaluates how well the generator fools the discriminator. Let sr_output denote the discriminator’s prediction for a generated image. This loss penalizes the generator when sr_output is classified as “fake” and encourages the generation of textures that are indistinguishable from real high-resolution textures.

This loss penalizes the generator when sr_output is classified as “fake” and encourages the generation of textures that are indistinguishable from real high-resolution textures.

Discriminator losses

The discriminator is trained to differentiate between real and generated textures using the following components:

GAN Loss: Takes the discriminator’s output for both real images (hr_output) and fake images (sr_output). The discriminator is encouraged to classify real images as “real” (e.g., values close to 1) and generated images as “fake” (e.g., values close to 0). This loss penalizes misclassification in either case.

GAN Loss: Takes the discriminator’s output for both real images (hr_output) and fake images (sr_output). The discriminator is encouraged to classify real images as “real” (e.g., values close to 1) and generated images as “fake” (e.g., values close to 0). This loss penalizes misclassification in either case.

Regularization Loss: Represents the sum of any internal regularization terms defined within the discriminator model (e.g., L2 weight decay) to reduce overfitting and improve training stability.

Discriminator Loss: Guides the discriminator in differentiating between real and generated textures, refining adversarial training. This is a sum of any regularization losses defined within the generator model itself, such as L2 regularization on the weights, to prevent overfitting.

Total losses

The total generator loss is computed as a weighted sum of the pixel loss, content loss, and generator adversarial loss. This formulation balances pixel accuracy, perceptual realism, and adversarial fidelity to produce high-quality texture upscaling results.

The discriminator’s total loss, denoted as total_loss_D, is the sum of its GAN loss and regularization loss. Unlike the generator, it is not a weighted combination of multiple perceptual or pixel-based terms and is focused solely on the adversarial classification task.

The generator and discriminator are optimized using independent optimizers, leading to the standard adversarial training process in which the generator continuously improves its texture synthesis while the discriminator becomes progressively better at detecting generated textures. By combining these loss functions, our approach achieves superior texture fidelity while maintaining essential material properties across different texture maps.

Dataset and evaluation

Our Dataset has been constructed by picking 300 open-source textures (https://opengameart.org/textures/), ensuring diversity in material properties such as metal, fabric, wood, stone, and synthetic surfaces. Each texture included multiple channels: color, displacement, metalness, normals, and roughness, which allowed our model to learn a more comprehensive material representation (Fig. 7). The dataset was split by texture into 210 training textures (1,050 images), 45 validation textures (225 images), and 45 test textures (225 images). All quantitative and qualitative evaluations were performed exclusively on the held-out test set. For training, we processed these textures at various resolutions to obtain corresponding low-resolution (128 × 128) imagery for the high-resolution imagery (512 × 512), ensuring that our model could generalize across different scales.

Data augmentation techniques, including random cropping, scaling, and slight perturbations, were employed to enhance the diversity of the training data and improve the model’s generalization capability. Random cropping helps the model learn to recognize objects or textures even when only partial information is available, which can be especially valuable for handling occlusions or varied framing in real-world scenes. Scaling simulates different viewing distances, allowing the model to adapt to variations in object size and perspective. Slight perturbations, such as small rotations, brightness adjustments, and noise injection, introduce subtle variability to the dataset without distorting the underlying features, making the model more robust to minor environmental changes and imperfections in input data.

The dataset itself was carefully curated to ensure high fidelity and realistic representation of materials commonly used in rendering and game development, such as wood, metal, fabric, stone, and composite surfaces. This curation process involved filtering out low-quality or unrealistic samples, balancing material categories, and ensuring that textures exhibited physically plausible properties. By combining rigorous curation with targeted augmentation strategies, the training set provided both rich diversity and realistic detail.

We employed both quantitative and qualitative evaluation methods. The primary metric used was the delta error, defined as the mean pixel-wise difference between generated and ground truth textures. We also computed the standard deviation of the error to capture performance variability, thereby assessing how consistently the model performs across different material types. In addition to numerical metrics, we evaluated the perceptual quality of the results using NVIDIA’s FLIP algorithm, which assesses the visual similarity between the ground truth and generated textures. This enabled us to capture high-level perceptual differences beyond pixel-wise errors and provided a more meaningful assessment of texture fidelity28.

Training

All training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX 2060 with 6Gb GDDR6 SDRAM. With a database size of 1500 images, split into 1050 for training, 225 for validation, and 225 for testing, and a batch size limited to 4 due to hardware memory constraints, we obtain 400 training steps per epoch. The training loops were initially set up for 50 epochs with an equivalent of 20,000 training steps to prevent overfitting. After evaluating the results, we continued the evaluation for 40k and 80k training steps. The model from the best epoch was saved and loaded for evaluation. Discussion

Our approach has broad applications in computer graphics, gaming, and digital content creation. One key application is in real-time rendering for video games and virtual reality, where high-resolution textures significantly enhance visual realism without increasing storage and memory requirements.

Additionally, our method can be used in offline rendering and film production to upscale legacy textures while preserving material properties. By improving texture resolution across multiple material maps, our approach ensures consistency and accuracy in physically-based rendering (PBR) workflows.

While our method offers significant improvements in texture resolution, there are some limitations. First, our model requires a well-curated training dataset to achieve optimal performance; textures that deviate significantly from the training distribution may exhibit minor artifacts. This could be mitigated in future work by introducing a lightweight fine-tuning stage for new material categories or deploying a small adapter network that specializes the generator for unseen texture domains. Second, the batch-based processing method, while improving performance, introduces minor seams in extreme cases, which may require post-processing adjustments. This could be mitigated by applying a lightweight edge-smoothing or seam-removal CNN to the stitched output as a final cleanup step. Lastly, although our technique efficiently bypasses resolution constraints, extreme upscaling (e.g., 6x enhancement) can lead to diminishing returns in detail preservation. This could be mitigated by injecting learned or procedural texture priors to hallucinate plausible fine details at extreme scales. Future work will focus on refining our model to address these challenges and further improve texture fidelity.

In addition, while we compare against ESRGAN using its official pretrained weights as a representative general-purpose super-resolution baseline, retraining ESRGAN (and other generic SR models) on the same texture dataset would further strengthen the fairness and completeness of the experimental validation. Such a domain-adapted baseline would help disentangle the performance gains attributable to architectural and methodological differences from those arising purely from domain specialization. Moreover, evaluating all methods on additional external texture datasets with broader material diversity would provide a more rigorous assessment of cross-dataset generalization. We consider both retraining competitive baselines on our dataset and expanding evaluation to larger and more diverse texture collections to be important directions for future work.

To enhance texture fidelity under extreme upscaling, one option to pursue could be integrating a multi-stage refinement framework. First, the model would generate an initial high-resolution reconstruction using the existing pipeline. Then, a dedicated texture-aware refinement network would process overlapping local patches, using attention mechanisms trained on high-frequency residual maps to selectively restore fine-grained structures. This refinement stage could be informed by adversarial training with a multi-scale discriminator focusing specifically on local texture statistics, thereby reducing artifacts and over-smoothing.

Conclusions

This work introduces a novel texture enhancement technique that significantly improves the resolution and fidelity of material properties for game assets. Our approach addresses key challenges in texture processing, including handling high-resolution data, complex material maps, and diverse material types. The batch-based processing pipeline, coupled with the decompression-recompression framework and scalable texture segmentation, enables efficient and high-quality enhancement of large textures. The custom-trained model, leveraging a specialized database of game-relevant materials, allows accurate reproduction of nuanced surface details across multiple material channels. Our results, validated through quantitative and qualitative metrics, perceptual evaluations, and user studies, demonstrate a substantial improvement in texture quality compared to existing methods. This enhanced fidelity translates to more realistic and immersive in-game environments, benefiting both developers and players. While limitations exist, such as potential artifacts with significantly different material distributions and diminishing returns at extreme upscaling factors, future work will focus on refining the model and addressing these challenges. Ultimately, this technique offers a powerful tool for enhancing texture resolution in gaming, virtual reality, and other graphics-intensive applications, paving the way for more visually compelling and engaging digital experiences.
