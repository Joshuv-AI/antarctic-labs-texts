Optimizing acid-related disease management: insights into potassium-competitive acid blockers and proton pump inhibitors
Authors: Huihui Shang, Huijie Mao, Jiawei Wang, Xiumei Tian, Kaifu Zheng, Jing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis narrative review evaluates the clinical value of key globally marketed potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs) for acid-related diseases (ARDs) based on current evidence-based medical research to inform clinical practice.MethodsWe compare P-CABs and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) in terms of their mechanisms of acid suppression, speed of onset, metabolic pathways, and long-term safety. We present a detailed comparison of the clinical efficacy of individual P-CABs and PPIs for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), peptic ulcers, and Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) eradication, synthesizing findings from recent clinical trials and meta-analyses.ResultsThis highlights the advantages of P-CABs, such as rapid acid control and the achievement of maximal efficacy with the first dose, as well as their reduced susceptibility to CYP2C19 polymorphisms. Evidence suggests that P-CABs have potential advantages in the initial and maintenance treatment of erosive esophagitis (EE). P-CABs demonstrate non-inferiority to PPIs in ulcer healing. For H. pylori eradication, P-CABs are comparable to PPIs in standard regimens; however, P-CAB-based dual therapy (e.g., P-CAB + high-dose amoxicillin) is emerging as a promising first-line treatment option.ConclusionThis review emphasizes the evolving role of P-CABs in optimizing management strategies for ARDs.

Introduction

Acid-related diseases (ARDs) are a group of diseases affecting various digestive organs, including the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum. They are all related to the secretion of gastric acid. The most common diseases are gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), peptic ulcers, Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection [1]. ARDs are common chronic diseases that are prone to recurring episodes and have a serious impact on quality of life, as well as increasing the economic burden. Without treatment, complications such as esophageal stenosis, Barrett’s esophagus, esophageal adenocarcinoma, gastrointestinal bleeding, and perforation may occur [2]. Acid plays a predominant role in the pathophysiology of mucosal damage and/or symptoms in ARDs [3]. The percentage of time intragastric pH > 4 (pH > 4 holding time ratio) is important for healing erosive esophagitis (EE), while a pH > 6 holding time ratio is critical for eradicating H. pylori infection since bacterial replication is active at this pH and antibiotic effectiveness is optimized [4]. The main treatment for ARDs is acid suppression, which can significantly improve outcomes [5]. Appropriate medication must be selected as early as possible to relieve symptoms in patients with ARDs. Options include acid blockers, H2-receptor antagonists (H2RAs), proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs). P-CABs are a new class of acid inhibitors that can block gastric acid secretion by competitively binding to the potassium ion-binding site of gastric H+/K+-ATPase [6], and they are widely used in clinical practice. This article reviews the clinical applications of P-CABs for major ARDs, as well as their efficacy and safety.

Search terms in the review

The search terms used in the review were: “acid-related diseases,” “ARDs,” “gastroesophageal reflux disease,” “GERD,” “erosive oesophagitis,” “EE,” “gastritis,” “peptic ulcer,” “Helicobacter pylori eradication,” “H. pylori eradication,” “potassium-competitive acid blocker,” “vonoprazan,” “revaprazan,” “tegoprazan,” “keverprazan,” “fexuprazan,” “proton pump inhibitor,” and “PPIs.”

P-CABs overview

Representative drugs

Five P-CABs have been approved for marketing and are still in use globally: revaprazan, vonoprazan, tegoprazan, keverprazan, and fexuprazan. Revaprazan was the first P-CAB launched in South Korea, but further development for treating GERD was discontinued due to its non-inferiority compared to PPIs. However, it remains on the market for treating peptic ulcers [7]. Vonoprazan, a first-in-class P-CAB, has been available in Japan since February 2015 for the treatment of EE [8] and is now recommended as the primary medication for treating GERD [9]. It has been launched in several other Asian countries and has demonstrated superior pharmacological properties to conventional PPIs, including effective, consistent, and long-lasting acid suppression [10]. Tegoprazan was approved for marketing in South Korea in July 2018 for the treatment of non-erosive reflux disease (NERD), making it the first P-CAB available for this condition [11, 12]. It was successfully marketed in China in 2022. Fexuprazan has also been approved for treating GERD in South Korea. Various experimental and clinical studies have indicated that it provides superior, longer-lasting acid suppression than PPIs, covering both daytime and night-time, thereby improving NERD symptoms [13]. Moreover, studies involving several ethnic populations, including Koreans, Caucasians, and Japanese people, have shown no significant differences in sensitivity or metabolism related to the CYP2C19 genotype, with no notable variations in acid suppression among the three groups [14]. Keverprazan was approved for clinical use in China in February 2023. It is well tolerated and provides significant, stable, and lasting inhibition of intragastric acidity at a dose of 20 mg [15]. The current status of P-CABs

Clinical pharmacology

Although both PPIs and P-CABs interact with the parietal cell H+/K+-ATPase, they differ in their mode of action (see Table 2). PPIs require activation in an acidic environment to be effective. They have a short half-life, which can result in inadequate control of gastric pH and lead to therapeutic failure. Their efficacy is also affected by genetic polymorphisms, resulting in a high degree of variability [16]. On average, PPIs must be taken orally for two to three days to achieve maximal acid suppression, with stabilization lasting three to five days [17]. High-frequency, high-dose administration can induce or exacerbate H. pylori-associated atrophic gastritis following infection [18]. PPIs can result in nocturnal heartburn and esophageal reflux with uncontrollable symptoms [19]. P-CABs act as a K⁺ exchange channel for H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, leading to a rapid and reversible decrease in acid secretion from gastric lining cells [20]. They can inhibit both resting and activated H+/K+-ATPase activities, thereby reducing gastric acid production and mitigating mucosal damage in the upper gastrointestinal tract. They have a long plasma half-life and remain stable in acidic environments, enabling them to persist in the secretory tubules for extended periods [21]. They do not require activation in oxidizing cells and have a faster onset of action, and provide almost complete inhibition of gastric acid secretion [22]. They are stable in acidic conditions and are not degraded by gastric acid. Therefore, it can be used in an immediate-release oral formulation to rapidly inhibit gastric acid secretion, eliminating the need for an enteric formulation [23]. P-CABs are metabolized via the CYP450 pathway, with CYP3A4 acting as the primary metabolizing enzyme. This enzyme is unaffected by polymorphisms in the CYP2C19 gene and exhibits fewer individual differences [24–26]. Some reports that, the CYP2C19*2 allele is most common in Oceanian (60%) and South and East Asian (28%) populations, but less so in sub-Saharan Africans (16–18%) and Europeans (15%). CYP2C19*3 is more frequent in Oceanians (15%) compared to East Asians (7%) and Near Easterners (2%), and is rare elsewhere. Other alleles like CYP2C19*9, *15, and *35 are more prevalent in Africans. Conversely, CYP2C19*17 is common in Europeans (22%), Near Easterners (19%), and sub-Saharan Africans (17%), but rare in East Asians (2%). Overall, Oceanian and East Asian populations have the highest frequencies of poor metabolizer CYP2C19 alleles [27]. In conclusion, P-CABs have the following unique advantages: 1. They are effective without requiring activation in a strongly acidic environment. They inhibit both resting and activated H+/K+-ATPase, achieving full efficacy with the first dose and thereby avoiding nocturnal acid breakthrough (NAB) [28]. 2. They can be taken on an empty stomach or after a meal, making them more convenient to use [29]. 3. They are primarily metabolized by the CYP3A4 enzyme and unaffected by CYP2C19 or genetic polymorphisms [30]. Safety of P-CABs

Safety of short-term application

More recent literature indicates that the short-term safety profiles of P-CABs and PPIs are comparable [31, 32]. Revaprazan is used less frequently in clinical practice due to its limited range of applications. A study investigating the impact of PPIs on bone metabolism in elderly patients found that serum calcium and urine deoxypyridinoline (DPD) levels increased in the PPI group after the 8-week treatment period, but not in the revaprazan group [33]. Recent clinical trials have generally reported that vonoprazan is well tolerated, with most adverse events (AEs) being mild to moderate in severity [34]. In addition to the AEs listed in the specifications, headaches occurred infrequently during maintenance therapy [35], hypomagnesemia [36, 37] and increased serum gastrin were reported in a few cases of AEs [38]. These returned to normal after the drug was discontinued. Vonoprazan was found to significantly reduce the incidence of diarrhea and loose stools in patients with ARDs compared to PPIs [39]. The most serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs), occurring in < 2% of cases, included interstitial nephritis, pneumonia, dementia, chronic kidney disease, hematological disorders (anemia and neutropenia), bone fractures, and cardiovascular abnormalities with QT prolongation [40, 41]. Elevated blood gastrin levels (hypergastrinemia) are a common AE associated with tegoprazan. Across randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses, the most frequently reported treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) for tegoprazan were diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting. The majority of these TEAEs were mild and liver-related TEAEs occurred at a low incidence [42–45]. Only one case of severe nausea and vomiting was reported, with no significant drug-related AEs or new safety concerns identified [46]. For fexuprazan, TEAEs occurring in ≥ 2% of patients included hyperuricemia, liver dysfunction, and dizziness [47]. The most frequently reported AEs with keverprazan were abdominal pain, diarrhea, headache, and rash. While the severity of these AEs was not defined, one patient withdrew due to moderate abdominal pain [48], and one serious AE (gastric polyp) was observed [49]. No deaths were associated with keverprazan. Hypergastrinemia is higher for vonoprazan than for tegoprazan and fexuprazan [50].

Safety of long-term application (≥ 24 weeks)

Long-term use of PPIs may lead to AEs, including small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, opportunistic infections, osteoporosis-related fractures, chronic kidney disease, vitamin and mineral deficiencies, and dementia. Therefore, the use of PPIs should be justified [51]. There is limited data on the long-term use of P-CABs, primarily concerning vonoprazan. Only one study has reported on the AEs associated with 25 mg of tegoprazan compared with 15 mg of lansoprazole over a period of 24 weeks. The results confirmed that the TEAEs profile of tegoprazan was similar to that of lansoprazole and that it did not cause a significant increase in gastrin [52]. A 12-month prospective observational study analyzed patients with a history of gastric or duodenal ulcers who were prescribed vonoprazan once daily alongside a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) for pain relief. The overall incidence of ADRs was 0.71% (9/1268). The most commonly reported ADRs were gastrointestinal (0.32%), nervous system (0.16%), and hepatobiliary system (0.16%) disorders [53]. One year of vonoprazan therapy did not exacerbate constipation or diarrhea [54]. In a 52-week esophageal healing maintenance study in patients with reflux esophagitis, vonoprazan (10 or 20 mg once daily) induced a Striking and progressive increase in serum gastrin. However, there were no significant effects on gastric neuroendocrine cells at 24 and 52 weeks, nor ang changes in pepsinogen levels. Therefore, vonoprazan (10 or 20 mg once daily) was considered safe [55].

This manuscript summarizes data from controlled studies on the long-term use of vonoprazan and PPIs. Nine literature sources have reported on controlled studies of AEs associated with the long-term use of both [56–64], and these are shown in Table 3. Most of the literature compares the AEs of vonoprazan 10 mg and lansoprazole 15 mg in EE maintenance therapy for over 24 weeks. The most common AEs were diarrhea, constipation, dyspepsia, nasopharyngitis, bronchitis, influenza, cystitis, gastroenteritis, hypertension, and dizziness. Overall, no serious drug-related TEAEs were observed during the clinical trials. Vonoprazan 10 mg once daily was found to have a similar safety profile to lansoprazole 15 mg once daily. Four literature reported endoscopic changes to the gastric mucosa caused by the long-term use of PPIs and P-CABs [59–61, 64]. The most common changes were fundic gland polyps, gastric hyperplastic polyps, multiple white elevated lesions, cobblestone-like mucosa, stardust gastric mucosa and gastric mucosal redness. There were no neoplastic changes in either group. No new safety issues were identified. Concerns have been raised about the long-term application of gastrin-induced neuroendocrine tumors (NETs). Seven literatures reported gastrin hyperintensities induced by long-term use of vonoprazan at levels significantly higher than those of PPI [56–58, 61–64]. However, no cases of gastric NETs were observed in any of the studies, particularly during the five-year maintenance treatment period. Nevertheless, one adenoma was observed in each treatment group [64]. In conclusion, the safety profiles of vonoprazan and PPIs were similar in patients with healed EE and NSAID-induced peptic ulcers.

Clinical applications of P-CABs in ARDs

Effect of P-CABs for GERD

Changes in lifestyle and dietary habits mean that GERD is becoming increasingly prevalent. A meta-analysis showed that GERD has a prevalence of approximately 10–20% in Western countries [65]. GERD significantly impacts patients’quality of life and places a substantial financial burden on healthcare systems. Without effective treatment, frequent episodes can cause serious complications, such as airway obstruction and aspiration pneumonia, which can even endanger patients’lives [66]. The two main phenotypes of GERD are EE and NERD, the latter is the most common, affecting around 70% of patients [67]. Current pathophysiological studies using modern 24-h impedance-pH monitoring have demonstrated that the NERD population is greatly heterogeneous from a pathophysiological point of view and can be subdivided into several subgroups [68]: Around 40% of patients have excess acid in their esophagus (true NERD), while the remaining 60% have normal esophageal acid exposure. The latter group can be further distinguished as having either reflux hypersensitivity (RH) to both acidic and weakly acidic reflux (approximately 40%), or functional heartburn (FH) (approximately 20%). Patients with RH have a positive correlation between symptoms and reflux episodes, whereas patients with FH do not. According to Rome IV criteria for functional esophageal disorders, patients with RH and FH are no longer considered to be in the GERD population [69]. Currently, several treatments can be prescribed for the management of GERD, ranging from the oral administration of antacids or acid lowering drugs to antireflux surgery and, recently, to endoscopic procedures, depending on the severity of the disease and the clinical characteristics of the patients [70]. PPIs reduce gastric acid secretion, alleviating symptoms by inhibiting H+/K+-ATPase [71]. However, up to 40% of GERD patients given PPIs fail to respond effectively to this treatment, either symptomatically or endoscopically, and instead belong to the category of PPI-refractory GERD [72, 73]. PPIs provide only 50–60% relief from heartburn after 4 weeks of treatment in NERD patients, which is lower than for EE patients [74]. Various mechanisms may induce this phenomenon in addition to incorrect dosage or timing of PPI intake, such as weakly acidic or bile reflux, hepatic CYP450 mutations, esophageal hypersensitivity to physiological reflux, and the presence of FH [75]. NERD patients, who include both RH and FH (two clinical conditions in which the pathogenetic role of acid is reduced or absent), are the main population responsible for PPI refractoriness [76]. RH and FH are due to visceral hypersensitivity. The therapeutic approach to these patients is complex. Increasing the dosage of PPIs or adopting surgical therapy should be considered for patients with inadequate control of excess acid [77, 78]. Several controlled studies have shown that add-on therapies combined with PPIs, particularly those combining mucosal protection and acid inhibition, are effective. In patients who did not respond well to 8 weeks of PPI treatment, switching to vonoprazan (20 mg/day) resulted in enhanced symptom control and faster healing of mucosal lesions, probably due to the P-CAB’s longer-lasting acid suppression [79, 80]. For patients for whom acid-suppressing drugs were ineffective, neuromodulator use has produced positive results [81, 82]. As ovel acid inhibitors, P-CABs are widely used to rapidly relieve GERD symptoms and overcome the limitations of PPIs in improving NAB [83]. Tegoprazan is particularly effective at suppressing night-time gastric acid compared to vonoprazan or esomeprazole [84]. P-CABs have become widespread in Asia and in some places of South America, where they have been shown to successfully treat GERD by overcoming some PPI pitfalls. However, they are rarely used in Western countries. Some are still in Phase III trials due to their late availability [85].

Three RCTs on 24 weeks of EE maintenance therapy confirmed that vonoprazan was non-inferior to and superior to the lansoprazole in healing and maintenance the healing of EE [56, 62, 63]. A meta-analysis was conducted to analyze the efficacy of P-CABs (containing vonoprazan, fexuprazan, and tegoprazan) in the initial and maintenance treatment of erosive reflux disease (ERD) and NERD. The results showed that: in nine RCTs evaluating the initial treatment of ERD, the risk ratio for healing with P-CABs vs. PPIs was 1.09 (95% CI, 1.04–1.13) at 2 weeks and 1.03 (95% CI, 1.00–1.07) at 8 weeks, respectively. P-CABs exhibited a significant increase in both initial and sustained healing of ERD compared to PPIs in RCTs, particularly in severe ERD (Los Angeles grade C/D). In three NERD RCTs, P-CABs were superior to placebo in proportion to days without heartburn. Observational studies on PPI-resistant symptomatic GERD reported symptom frequency improvement in 86.3% of patients, while 90.7% showed improvement in PPI-resistant ERD across five observational studies [86]. Several recent studies have confirmed the non-inferiority of keverprazan to PPIs. A systematic evaluation and reticulated meta-analysis comparing the efficacy of P-CABs and PPIs in treating EE showed that keverprazan (20 mg, once daily) achieved the best healing results after 8 weeks of treatment. The healing rate of most P-CABs was higher than that of PPIs, particularly in patients with severe EE [87]. A network meta-analyses (NMA) was conducted using a Bayesian random-effects model. This model included three P-CABs (vonoprazan, tegoprazan and keverprazan) and six PPIs (lansoprazole, esomeprazole, omeprazole, rabeprazole, pantoprazole and dexlansoprazole). The analysis demonstrated that 20 mg of vonoprazan once daily was more effective than 15 mg of lansoprazole once daily in initial therapy. It reduced the risk of treatment failure compared to other PPIs and was relatively safe in both the short and long term. Vonoprazan was also an effective treatment for grade C/D esophagitis, performing better than PPIs in the treatment of severe GERD. Tegoprazan performed better in terms of long-term safety but had poorer maintenance efficacy. Keverprazan is efficacious, but it has a poor long-term safety profile, with a high rate of serious AEs after 24 weeks of use [88]. Vonoprazan at 10 and 20 mg was superior to 15 mg of lansoprazole in maintenance the healing of EE. Any TEAEs were greater with vonoprazan 20 mg [89]. P-CABs are effective at relieving heartburn symptoms and can be recommended for as-needed treatment. However, current evidence remains insufficient and requires confirmation through substantial, evidence-based research [90]. Five prospective RCTs compared P-CABs with placebo or PPIs for the treatment of extraesophageal symptoms and signs (e.g. gastroesophageal reflux-related cough, laryngopharyngeal reflux disease). The results indicated that tegoprazan 50 mg once daily, fexuprazan 40 mg once daily, and vonoprazan 20 mg once daily are similar in effectiveness to PPIs in the treating extraesophageal symptoms and signs of GERD. However, they are more rapid and effective in patients with severe symptoms [91–95]. A recent Japanese guideline for GERD recommended vonoprazan as the initial/maintenance treatment for severe esophagitis. It also recommends vonoprazan or a PPI as the initial treatment for mild reflux esophagitis and a PPI or vonoprazan for maintenance treatment [9]. In conclusion, P-CABs have advantages in both the initial and maintenance phases of GERD treatment and are as effective as PPIs at alleviating extraesophageal symptoms. Effect of P-CABs for peptic ulcers

Peptic ulcers are a common disease worldwide. In 2019, the global incidence was approximately 8.09 cases per million, reflecting a 25.82% increase since 1990 [96]. The most commonly used drugs to inhibit gastric acid secretion in clinical practice are PPIs and H2RAs. PPIs are more effective at inhibiting gastric acid than H2RAs and have a longer-lasting effect, making them the preferred treatment for peptic ulcers. P-CABs are also widely used in the treatment of peptic ulcers because they inhibit gastric acid for longer, creating a favorable environment for ulcerated surfaces to heal and damaged mucosal tissue to repair.

A recent meta-analysis showed that 30 mg of keverprazan may be the most effective dosage for enhancing the healing rate of gastric/duodenal ulcers [97]. Systematic and NMA revealed that vonoprazan, tegoprazan, and keverprazan were effective in treating peptic ulcers. Vonoprazan demonstrated superior efficacy in treating peptic ulcers, particularly in patients with H. pylori-positive ulcers. However, the risk of AEs associated with vonoprazan should be noted. Keverprazan also showed good therapeutic outcomes, performing better in terms of safety and providing more effective pain relief [98]. A Bayesian and frequentist NMA of revaprazan, vonoprazan, and tegoprazan in gastric ulcers (GU) showed that: P-CABs were not inferior to PPIs in terms of GU healing rates. Furthermore, P-CABs, particularly tegoprazan and vonoprazan, were ranked highest in terms of probability [99]. A meta-analysis of 20 studies involving 6,551 participants confirmed that the healing rate of P-CABs was comparable to that of PPIs for treating artificial and peptic ulcers. There was no difference in the incidence of TEAEs, and the delayed bleeding rate of artificial ulcers was RR 0.35 (0.16–0.80). For patients taking NSAIDs or low-dose aspirin (LDA) long-term and with a history of peptic ulcers, P-CABs were more effective than PPIs in preventing ulcer recurrence (RR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.25–0.81), which is advantageous [100]. Vonoprazan (at the dose of both 10 and 20 mg) produced similar results to PPIs in patients taking long-term NSAIDs, with no severe AEs, providing a more rapid and effective treatment of ulcers induced by endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) [101]. The non-inferiority of vonoprazan (at doses of 10 and 20 mg) was verified in patients receiving long-term NSAID treatment for more than 1 year [57]. In conclusion, P-CABs were as effective as PPIs at alleviating ulcer symptoms and healing artificial ulcers. In terms of preventing ulcer recurrence, they were more effective. When it came to safety, keverprazan was superior to other options.

Effect of P-CABs for H. pylori eradication

H. pylori remains a significant global health concern, causing substantial morbidity and mortality through peptic ulcer disease and gastric cancer [102]. Recent guidelines recommend considering eradication therapy for all individuals with confirmed H. pylori infections [103, 104]. The current diagnostic and therapeutic status of H. pylori can be summarized as follows: a high infection rate, high pathogenicity, high drug resistance, and low eradication rates [105]. Various treatment regimens have been used to date. The current treatment combinations for eradicating H. pylori are as follows: 1. Dual therapy, consisting of a PPI and amoxicillin. 2. Triple therapy, consisting of a PPI and two antibiotics. 3. Non-bismuth quadruple therapy, consisting of a PPI, amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and metronidazole. Which is also known as sequential, concomitant, and hybrid therapy. 4. Bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT), consisting of a PPI, bismuth and two antibiotics. P-CABs have significant acid-suppressive effects and are widely used to eradicate H. pylori, vonoprazan, tegoprazan, and keverprazan are particularly prevalent.

Most international guidelines or consensuses favor BQT as the first-line treatment in areas with high clarithromycin resistance [106, 107]. A recent study compared the efficacy of quadruple therapy with amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and bismuth potassium citrate for H. pylori eradication, when based either on keverprazan or esomeprazole. It demonstrated that, as part of a BQT, keverprazan (20 mg twice daily) was non-inferior to an esomeprazole-based regimen for H. pylori eradication [108]. BQT containing vonoprazan was as effective as a regimen containing PPIs for the eradication of H. pylori, was more economical, and was well tolerated. [109, 110]. A meta-analysis showed that tegoprazan-containing therapy is superior to PPI-containing therapy for first-line H. pylori eradication, with comparable safety profiles [111]. There were 100% eradication rates of H. pylori in clarithromycin (CAM)-sensitive 7-day triple therapies containing P-CABs [112]. Both vonoprazan and tegoprazan have demonstrated high eradication rates and a favorable safety profile when used in triple therapy for H. pylori eradication [113, 114]. A recent meta-analysis confirmed that vonoprazan -based triple therapy is more effective than conventional PPI-based triple therapy at eradicating H. pylori, making it a highly effective first-line regimen. Tegoprazan-based triple therapy was non-inferior to classical PPI triple therapy [115, 116]. Triple therapy involving vonoprazan or revaprazan is as efficacy as second-line treatments for eradicating H. pylori infection [117]. In the context of salvage therapy, there is no significant difference in the efficacy of P-CAB-based triple therapy compared to PPI-based triple therapy [118, 119]. Vonoprazan and high-dose amoxicillin dual therapy was considered a useful eradication regimen for H. pylori infection [120]. Meta-analysis showed that 14-day tetracycline/vonoprazan dual therapy was an effective treatment option for eradicating H. pylori in patients allergic to penicillin. First-line treatments for those without a penicillin allergy can include 14-day mono-antibiotic regimens such as high-dose amoxicillin/PPI dual therapy, high-dose amoxicillin/vonoprazan dual therapy, and tetracycline/vonoprazan dual therapy [121, 122]. A real-world, retrospective clinical study conducted in China demonstrated the effectiveness and safety of a 14-day dual regimen of vonoprazan and amoxicillin as rescue therapy for treating Helicobacter pylori infection, achieving an eradication rate of over 90% [123]. A cost-effectiveness analysis revealed that P-CABs incur lower overall costs than PPIs due to their higher first-line eradication rates [124]. A NMA of the comparative efficacy and safety of P-CAB-based dual, triple, and quadruple regimens for the initial treatment of H. pylori infection revealed that P-CAB-based dual therapy offered the most favorable efficacy-safety profile [125]. In conclusion, P-CABs are effective in eradicating H. pylori when used in dual, triple, and quadruple regimens, with P-CAB-based dual therapy being the preferred first-line treatment option. Limitations

Although this article provides an overview of reported clinical trials and meta-analyses, it is imperative to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, the majority of available data on P-CABs relates to vonoprazan, with limited data available on tegoprazan, keverprazan, fexuprazan, and revaprazan. Therefore, the conclusions may not be representative of all P-CABs. Second, the long-term effectiveness and safety of P-CABs are largely based on vonoprazan clinical data. However, there is a lack of reports on other P-CABs, which makes it difficult to assess the long-term safety of all P-CABs. Most of the articles referenced used lansoprazole as the comparator drug, and there is a lack of comparative data for other PPIs. This is not representative of all types of PPIs. Third, PPIs have been extensively utilized in clinical practice for an extended period, resulting in a comprehensive understanding of their side effects. In contrast, P-CABs represent a novel class of drugs that have only recently been introduced into clinical practice. Consequently, additional long-term research is required to elucidate the potential risks associated with P-CABs. Fourth, P-CABs are effective in healing gastric ulcers; however, there are no studies on their ability to prevent these lesions in patients taking NSAIDs chronically.

Conclusions

PPIs are widely prescribed to treat ARDs on a global scale. Current P-CABs are non-inferior to and/or superior to PPIs in the treatment of various conditions, including GERD, gastric ulcers, duodenal ulcers, ulcers induced by LDA and NSAID. They are also effective in treating post-ESD ulcers and H. pylori infections. However, further studies are needed to monitor the long-term efficacy and AEs of P-CABs, particularly in therapeutic areas where different types of P-CAB offer advantages in terms of drug interactions, genetic polymorphisms, and other factors. Future clinical trials could investigate the efficacy of this class of drugs in the prevention of NSAID-associated ulcers, thereby contributing to evidence-based medicine that supports the safer and more effective clinical application of these novel antisecretory agents.
