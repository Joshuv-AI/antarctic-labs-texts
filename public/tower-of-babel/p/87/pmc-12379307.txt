Association between body mass index and the prognosis of community-acquired pneumonia in patients with nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease: a retrospective cohort study using a nationwide inpatient database
Authors: Goh Tanaka, Shotaro Aso, Taisuke Jo, Hirokazu Urushiyama, Akira Yokoyama, Hiroyuki Tamiya, Hiroki Matsui, Kiyohide Fushimi, Hideo Yasunaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is a chronic, debilitating infection that predominantly affects patients with a low body mass index (BMI) and leads to progressive destruction of lung parenchyma. Although pneumonia is a common cause of death among patients with NTM-PD, information on its prognosis is scarce. We evaluated the association between BMI and outcomes in patients with NTM-PD who were hospitalized for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP).MethodsUsing the Japanese Diagnosis Procedure Combination database, we identified patients with NTM-PD hospitalized for CAP between April 1, 2012, and March 31, 2020, prior to the coronavirus disease 2019 epidemic in Japan. We evaluated in-hospital mortality as the primary outcome, and length of hospital stay and readmission due to CAP within 1 year as secondary outcomes. Restricted cubic spline regression models were used to evaluate the impact of BMI as a continuous variable on the study outcomes, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsWe included 13,302 patients (median age: 79 years [interquartile range {IQR} 72–85]; women: 63.7%; median BMI: 17.6 kg/m2 [IQR 15.6–19.9]). The median hospital stay was 14 d (IQR 9–25), and in-hospital mortality was 10.9%. In the restricted cubic spline model, being underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2) was associated with a sharp increase in both in-hospital mortality and length of hospital stay. In the overweight group (25–30 kg/m2), high BMI was not associated with adverse outcomes. Being underweight was also associated with a modest increase in readmission because of CAP within 1 year.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that assessing BMI may play a critical role in stratifying risk and optimizing management of CAP in patients with NTM-PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11426-z.

Background

Nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is a chronic pulmonary infection that predominantly affects middle-aged and older women. The progression of bronchiectasis or cavitary lesions leads to destruction of lung parenchyma in these patients.

Low body mass index (BMI) is frequently observed among patients with NTM-PD, and has been reported to contribute to disease development [1, 2]. A clinical finding of BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 has also been identified as a strong predictor of reduced survival time and is incorporated into the BACES score—a mortality prediction tool for NTM-PD that evaluates BMI, age, cavity presence, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and sex [3]. Using a nationwide database in Japan, we previously reported that low BMI is a risk factor for in-hospital mortality in patients with NTM-PD who underwent unscheduled hospitalization [4]. We also found that pulmonary infection was the most common comorbidity among patients who died in hospital, consistent with a retrospective study that identified pneumonia as one of the major direct causes of death in patients with Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease [5].

Although BMI has repeatedly been identified as a prognostic factor for NTM-PD [3, 5], its precise impact on prognosis—particularly in patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)—remains unclear.

We hypothesized that a low BMI has a strong influence on the mortality of patients with NTM-PD hospitalized for CAP. In this study, we analyzed a national inpatient database in Japan to determine the effect of BMI on the prognosis of CAP in patients with NTM-PD.

Methods

Data source

We used data from the Japanese Diagnosis Procedure Combination database [6]. This inpatient database contains information on the disease causing the admission, as well as the primary and comorbid diagnoses, which were recorded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. The Japanese Diagnosis Procedure Combination database also includes the following information: age, sex, height and weight, smoking status, and discharge status. In this database, documentation of each A-DROP parameter and pneumonia classification (CAP or hospital-acquired) is required for patients hospitalized with pneumonia. The A-DROP system [7], a CURB-65 modification for pneumonia severity classification, shows correlation with CURB-65. The A-DROP components comprise: (i) Age (men ≥ 70 years, women ≥ 75 years); (ii) Dehydration (blood urea nitrogen ≥ 21 mg/dL or clinical signs of dehydration); (iii) Respiratory failure (SpO₂ ≤ 90% or PaO₂ ≤ 60 Torr); (iv) Orientation disturbance (confusion); and (v) Pressure (systolic blood pressure ≤ 90 mmHg).

Study population and data

We selected patients with NTM-PD (ICD-10 codes: A31.0 and A31.9) aged ≥ 18 years who were hospitalized for pneumonia (J10.0, J11.0, J12–18, and A48.1) between April 1, 2012, and March 31, 2020. This period, which preceded the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic in Japan, was chosen for analysis. During the COVID-19 pandemic, it has been difficult to accurately assess the prognosis of CAP owing to insufficient medical care, including restricted access to health facilities for patients presenting with respiratory symptoms and fever. We excluded patients with human immunodeficiency virus infection (B20.0) and Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial diseases (D84.8), both conditions predisposing to disseminated NTM infection. Additionally, we excluded patients with missing A-DROP parameters.

We analyzed the first hospitalization during the study period as the index hospitalization. The following comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes: pulmonary aspergillosis (B44); chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (J43, J44.0, J44.1, and J44.9); interstitial lung disease (J84.1, J84.8, and J84.9); congestive heart failure (I11.0, I50.0, I50.1, and I50.9); cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69); renal disease (N00–N08 and N10–N19); autoimmune disease (M05, M06, M08, and M30–M35); diabetes mellitus (DM; E10–E14); and malignant neoplasm (C00–C97).

Category of BMI

We categorized patients into four BMI groups according to the World Health Organization classification: severely underweight (BMI < 16 kg/m2), mild-to-moderately underweight (16–18.4 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and overweight–obese (≥ 25.0 kg/m2).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. The secondary outcomes were the length of hospital stay (excluding discharges due to death) and readmission for CAP within 1 year after discharge from the index hospitalization, identified using diagnostic codes.

Statistical analysis

We calculated summary statistics of patient characteristics by BMI category. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test.

To ascertain any potential linear or non-linear association in the regression model and allow for flexible interpretation of the relation between BMI as a continuous variable and study outcomes, we evaluated continuous changes in BMI using shape-restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models. All models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking history, clinical parameters included in the A-DROP system, and comorbidities. We placed three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of BMI values [8]. For missing data on BMI and smoking history, we used multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption, which assumes that missingness depends only on observed variables. Multiple imputation introduces less bias than complete case analysis [9]. Missing data were imputed using the chained equations technique with 20 iterations [10], incorporating all variables. The results for each outcome were obtained using Rubin’s rules [11]. We conducted multivariate analysis for each outcome using generalized estimating equations to account for within-hospital clustering. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for in-hospital mortality and readmission for pneumonia within 1 year were calculated for each BMI value, with a reference value of 22.0 kg/m2. For the RCS curve assessing the length of hospital stay, reference categories for binary variables were female sex and the absence of comorbidities or risk factors.

For sensitivity analysis, we performed a complete-case analysis, excluding patients with missing data. Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/SE software (version 18.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tokyo and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of data anonymity.

Results

Patient characteristics

We initially identified 15,663 patients with NTM-PD who were hospitalized for pneumonia, from whom we selected 13,373 with a CAP diagnosis. We excluded 22 patients with a human immunodeficiency virus infection, 68 patients with Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial diseases, and 75 patients with missing A-DROP scores, yielding 13,302 patients, including 1,163 (8.7%) and 1,139 (8.6%) patients with missing BMI and smoking history data, respectively.

Overall, the median age of the study patients was 79 years (interquartile range [IQR], 72–85), and 8,478 patients (63.7%) were women. The median BMI was 17.6 kg/m2 (IQR, 15.6–19.9), with 18.3 kg/m2 (IQR, 16.1–20.6) for men and 17.3 kg/m2 (IQR, 15.3–19.5) for women. Among study population, 7,347 patients (55.2%) were below normal weight (< 18.5 kg/m2), and 368 patients (2.8%) were above normal weight (≥ 25.0 kg/m2), of whom 41 (0.3%) were obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m2).

The numbers of patients with A-DROP scores of 0 to 1, 2, 3, and 4 to 5 were 7,268 (54.6%), 3,812 (28.7%), 1,633 (12.3%), and 589 (4.4%), respectively. Readmission for CAP within 1 year

Among the patients who were discharged alive, 1,324 (11.2%) were readmitted within a year. The proportion in the underweight group was higher than that in the normal-weight and overweight/obese groups (Table 2). Underweight was associated with increased readmission for CAP within 1 year, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.55 (95% CI, 1.35–1.77) at BMI of 16 kg/m2, compared with the reference BMI of 22 kg/m2 in the RCS model (Fig. 3). A sensitivity analysis was conducted using complete cases. These results were similar to those of the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

We evaluated the association between BMI and in-hospital mortality as the primary outcome in patients with NTM-PD who were hospitalized for CAP. We also examined secondary outcomes, including the length of hospital stay and readmission for CAP within 1 year. Our results showed that being underweight was significantly associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality and readmission for CAP within 1 year. The restricted cubic spline model demonstrated a non-linear association on a log scale, with an accelerating increase in mortality as BMI decreased, particularly in the severely underweight range. Additionally, being underweight was associated with a longer hospital stay.

Previous studies on the general population have consistently demonstrated that being underweight—an indicator of malnutrition—is associated with increased mortality risk in patients with CAP [12–15]. For instance, a study using administrative healthcare databases in the United States found that underweight patients with pneumonia (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2) had a significantly high 90-d mortality risk (OR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.14–1.73) [13]. Similarly, a single-center study in Korea identified severe underweight (BMI < 16.0 kg/m2) as an independent risk factor for 30-d mortality, even after adjusting for the pneumonia severity index and other clinical factors (OR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.09–3.26) [14]. Malnutrition has been shown to impair immune function through the dysregulation of adipokines and other mechanisms, thereby increasing susceptibility to pneumonia-related mortality [16]. While these studies have established an association between underweight status and CAP-related mortality, our findings suggest that this risk may be even greater in underweight patients with NTM-PD. This increased risk may result not only from malnutrition but also from disease progression in NTM-PD, leading to destruction of lung parenchyma.

NTM-PD is closely related to bronchiectasis in terms of pathophysiology, regardless of whether NTM infection is a cause or consequence of bronchiectasis [17, 18]. In bronchiectasis, impaired mucociliary clearance and accumulation of airway secretions undermine normal host defense mechanisms, leading to increased susceptibility to infections and their progression [19, 20]. Additionally, in patients with Mycobacterium avium complex pulmonary disease, poor nutritional status is associated with radiological severity [21] and the presence of cavitary lesions [22]. Given these factors, severely underweight patients with NTM-PD may have a substantially elevated risk of CAP-related mortality resulting from both malnutrition-induced immune dysfunction and the progressive deterioration of local defense mechanisms.

By contrast, an increase in BMI within the overweight range (25–30 kg/m2) was associated with improved in-hospital mortality and a reduced risk of readmission for CAP within 1 year. A meta-analysis evaluating the relation between elevated BMI and pneumonia in the general population showed that overweight and obesity were associated with an increased risk of pneumonia but a decreased risk of pneumonia-related mortality [23]. This phenomenon is often referred to as the “obesity paradox.” The findings of this meta-analysis are further supported by a subsequent prospective observational study conducted in the United States [15]. Moreover, a previous multicenter study on patients hospitalized for CAP [24] showed that being overweight was not associated with a longer length of stay in patients with NTM-PD hospitalized for CAP. Although the existence of the “obesity paradox” remains controversial [24], overweight status may be a protective factor in patients with NTM-PD hospitalized because of CAP.

Limitation

A key strength of this study is its ability to analyze the association between BMI and CAP in patients with NTM-PD while adjusting for confounding factors using a large database. However, this study had several limitations. First, we could not evaluate patients’ baseline pulmonary characteristics, including cavitary lesions, pulmonary hypertension, or pulmonary function test results. Because cavitary lesions are an independent prognostic factor in NTM-PD [3, 25], and pulmonary hypertension is associated with advanced lung disease, this limitation could affect our CAP prognosis findings. Second, detailed clinical data were unavailable for NTM-PD (treatment status and disease activity) or CAP (causative pathogens and antimicrobial susceptibility). The gap in clinical data may affect clinical outcome interpretation. Third, this study was conducted in a Japanese population, and the distribution of BMI and age in patients with NTM-PD may differ across racial and ethnic groups.

Conclusion

This study suggests that BMI may strongly influence CAP prognosis in patients with NTM-PD. Proper management of systemic conditions and comorbidities is essential to improve the prognosis of patients hospitalized for CAP. Further studies are required to clarify the association between BMI and the prognosis of patients with NTM-PD.
