Proteomic Signatures in Extracellular Vesicles as Predictive Markers of Neural and Cardiac Differentiation from Human iPSCs
Authors: Mei Mikami, Eri Katsuno, Shunsuke Hoshina, Kansei Takashita, Daisuke Takakura, Nana Kawasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neural cells (NCs) and cardiomyocytes (CMs) show significant promise for clinical applications in regenerative medicine. Because the manufacturing of iPSC-derived products faces challenges in quality reproducibility, cost, and manufacturing time, closed automated culture systems have been developed. Recently, an efficient and noninvasive cell monitoring system based on extracellular markers secreted by cells represents a new strategy for closed systems. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), secreted by cells, contain proteins involved in stem cell differentiation; however, EV proteins in the cell culture medium are difficult to analyze because of their low abundance. Thus, their changes during cell differentiation, and the relationship between EV and cell proteins, remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the changes in EV proteins during iPSC differentiation into NCs and CMs using data-independent acquisition LC/MS/MS (DIA-MS), a highly comprehensive and quantitative approach. We observed significant positive correlations between the EV protein abundance and cellular protein expression levels during differentiation on days 0–7, and identified signature proteins characteristic of NCs and CMs. Among these, trophoblast glycoprotein (TPBG) for NC differentiation and keratin, type I cytoskeletal 19 (KRT19) for CM differentiation were particularly increased in both cells and EVs. Moreover, TPBG and KRT19 levels at day 7 showed similar trends to the differences in differentiation observed among the cell lines, as determined by flow cytometry. These findings suggest that these EV proteins may serve as predictive markers for monitoring differentiation into NC and CM.

INTRODUCTION

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be differentiated artificially into tridermic lineages.1) They hold promise for clinical applications for the treatment of various diseases that have been previously considered difficult to treat. In fact, clinical studies have advanced using iPSC-derived neural cells (NCs) derived from iPSCs for spinal cord injury2–4) and Parkinson’s disease,5) and iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (CMs) for ischemic cardiomyopathy,6) with therapeutic effects reported. However, iPSC-derived products have several issues. They exhibit heterogeneity across culture conditions and among individual cells, and changes in this heterogeneity may affect safety and efficacy.7–9) To maintain this heterogeneity within an acceptable range, it is necessary to establish appropriate manufacturing processes and quality control strategies. Until now, iPSC-derived products for clinical research have been cultured, maintained, and differentiated on a small scale by manual operations. However, for large-scale manufacturing and stable supply, a closed automated cell culture system is required, and efforts are underway to develop such equipment.10–15) For practical implementation, an efficient method to evaluate iPSC differentiation during the manufacturing process is indispensable.

Currently, differentiation into target cells is typically assessed by morphological evaluation and the detection of intracellular molecular markers in many studies.16–19) Conventional morphological evaluation relies on technical skill; therefore, there is poor reproducibility and robustness. In recent years, image analysis technologies using artificial intelligence and neural networks to monitor cell proliferation and colony formation for quality evaluation have advanced.2–23) On the other hand, issues remain in the detection of intracellular molecular markers, including complex operations and the consumption of cell samples. Consequently, evaluation methods using the cell culture medium discharged during the manufacturing process have been developed.24–26) This approach can overcome conventional challenges and is suitable for noninvasive cell quality monitoring within closed systems.

The cell culture medium contains extracellular vesicles (EVs) that are capable of specific enrichment. EV is a collective term for lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles that are secreted by cells. They contain proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids and play an important role in cell–cell communication and signal transduction,27–29) and are involved in stem cell differentiation through a synchronized process.30) Moreover, multiple studies suggest that EVs from iPSC-derived cells contribute to their therapeutic effects.31–34) Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of EV biomolecules secreted by iPSC-derived cells may enable the identification of molecular markers predictive of cell differentiation. However, EV proteins in the cell culture medium are in low abundance and are overwhelmed by medium-derived proteins, making it difficult to comprehensively analyze.35–38) Consequently, compared with studies focused on microRNA, there are relatively few reports of EV proteomics. Although there are a few reports comparing EV proteins with their parent cell proteins, their relationship remains unclear. While studies comparing several differentiation lineages and cell lines exist at the cellular level,39–41) few studies have focused on EV proteins.

In this study, we conducted quantitative proteomics on EV and cellular proteins derived from three iPSC lines differentiating into NCs or CMs by data-independent acquisition LC/MS/MS (DIA-MS), a highly quantitative and comprehensive approach,42) which improved the stable and reproducible identification of signature proteins characteristic of NCs and CMs. By analyzing expression trends across the three iPSC lines in relation to established NC and CM differentiation markers, we explored EV proteins as predictive markers for monitoring iPSC differentiation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. iPSC culture and differentiation

The human iPSC lines, iPSC 201B7 (HPS0063; RIKEN BRC, Ibaraki, Japan), iPSC 610B1 (HPS0331; RIKEN BRC), and iPSC 1231A3 (HPS0381; RIKEN BRC) were cultured in 5% CO2 on iMatrix-511-coated (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan) 10-cm dishes (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Corning, NY, USA) in StemFit AK02N medium (AJINOMOTO, Tokyo, Japan) at 37°C.

Differentiation of iPSCs into NCs was based on the methods of Chambers et al.43) Differentiation into neural stem cells (NSCs) was done on 70% confluent iPSCs cultured in medium A (47.5% DMEM/F12 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 47.5% Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 1% N2 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2% B-27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% nonessential amino acids (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 2 mM l-alanyl-l-glutamine (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 100 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 100 nM LDN193189 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), and 10 μM SB431542 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical)) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 0–7 days. The medium was changed every 24 h. NSCs were differentiated into NCs by culture in medium B (96% Neurobasal medium, 2% B-27 supplement, 1% GlutaMAX supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% CultureOne supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 200 μM ascorbic acid (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical)) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 14 days.

Differentiation of iPSCs into CMs was carried out using the method described by Tohyama et al.44) For CM differentiation, 70% confluent iPSCs were cultured in medium C (98% RPMI-1640 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) containing 2% B-27 supplement, minus insulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific)) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 0–7 days. The medium was changed every 24 h. On day 0, the cells were cultured in medium C containing (8 μM CHIR99021 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) and 1 ng/mL BMP4 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical)). On days 3–6, the cells were cultured in medium C containing 5 μM IWR-1 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). On day 7, the cells were cultured in medium D (95% α-MEM (Nacalai Tesque Inc., Kyoto, Japan) containing 5% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific)) for 5 days. The medium was changed on day 10. For cardiac purification, the cells on day 12 were cultured in medium E45) (99% RPMI-1640 no glucose (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 1% CDM3 supplement with human recombinant albumin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), and 4 mM L-lactic acid (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical)) for 6 days. The medium was changed on day 15.

2. Immunofluorescence

The cells were seeded in a 12-well plate (TPP Techno Plastic Products AG Int., Klettgau, Switzerland), washed twice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline without calcium and magnesium (DPBS (−); Nacalai Tesque Inc.), and the supernatant was removed. Methanol (KANTO CHEMICAL CO., INC., Tokyo, Japan) was added to the cells and incubated for 10 min at −30°C. The solution was replaced with DPBS (−) containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Nacalai Tesque Inc.) and incubated at 20°C for 1 h. The supernatant was removed and the cells were incubated with the following primary antibodies at 4°C for 16 h: R-10G (1/500 diluted; 397602; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for iPSC, anti-Nestin antibody (1/100; MAB1259; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for NSC, anti-tubulin β3 antibody (Tuj1; 1/100; 801201; BioLegend) for NC, anti-Nkx2.5 antibody (1/500; PA5-85215; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for cardiac progenitor cell, and anti-cardiac troponin T antibody (1/100; MA5-12960; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for CM. The supernatant was removed, and the cells were rinsed three times with DPBS (−) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich Int., St. Louis, MO, USA) (DPBST) and incubated with Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1/2,000; A-11001; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor 488 (1/1000; ab150077; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 20°C for 1 h. After rinsing three times with DPBST, the cells were stained with 1 μg/mL 4ʹ,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) at 20°C for 3 min and washed with DPBST. The cells were seeded into 12-well plates and examined under a microscope (Eclipse Ts2; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) at 488 nm for differentiation-specific markers and 385 nm for the nucleus. Images were pseudo-colored and merged using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

3. Cell protein extraction and EV enrichment

The cells and cell culture media on days 0 and 7 from each differentiation were collected. The cells were washed twice with DPBS (−) and mixed with buffer46) consisting of 12 mM sodium deoxycholate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 12 mM sodium lauryl sulfate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.0 (Sigma-Aldrich), Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA-free (1/100; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% O-GlcNAcase inhibitor, Thiamet-G (1/2000; Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C. The harvested cells were sonicated to reduce viscosity on ice.

The cell culture media (10 mL) were filtered (0.45-μm pore size; Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to remove debris. For improving EV protein yield or removing interfering soluble components, the samples were applied to an Amicon Ultra-15 3-kDa centrifugal filter unit (Merck Millipore) and centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 100 min. The supernatant was collected, and Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA-free (1/100) was added. EV enrichment was conducted by the phosphatidylserine (PS) affinity method. A MagCapture Exosome Isolation Kit PS (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) was used based on the manufacturer’s instructions. After EV enrichment, the supernatants were vacuum dried in a centrifugal evaporator (Sakuma Int., Tokyo, Japan), dissolved in 0.2% Protease MAX Surfactant (Promega Int., Madison, WI, USA), and incubated while vortexing (WAKENBTECH CO., LTD., Kyoto, Japan) at 20°C for 30 min. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit47) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). EV size distribution was determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a NanoSight LM10 (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK) instrument, and morphology was evaluated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM); both were performed using an external service provider.

4. Trypsin digestion

Cell proteins (50 μg) or EV proteins (1 μg) were diluted to 100 μL with 500 mM ammonium bicarbonate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). The diluted cell proteins were precipitated with a threefold volume of cold acetone (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), incubated at −30°C for 16 h, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The EV proteins were not used to prevent sample loss. The cell and EV proteins were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 56°C for 30 min. Alkylation was performed by incubating the samples with 10 mM acrylamide (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) at 37°C for 1 h in the dark. The proteins were digested with 1 μg of Trypsin/Lys-C Mix (Promega) at 37°C for 16 h with continuous shaking at 800 rpm. The tryptic peptides were desalted using GL-Tip SDB (GL Science, Tokyo, Japan) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the samples were vacuum dried in a centrifugal evaporator and redissolved in water containing 0.1% formic acid (KANTO CHEMICAL CO., INC.) to a final concentration of 0.1 μg/μL for cell protein and 0.02 μg/μL for EV protein.

5. LC/MS/MS

DIA-MS was performed using an LC/MS/MS instrument consisting of a Q-Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Nanospray Flex Ion Source (Thermo Fisher Scientific), an EASY-nLC 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Acclaim PepMap C18 LC column (3 μm, 75 μm × 20 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and an NTCC-360/75-3-125 (75 μm×125 mm, 3 μm, C18; Nikkyo Technos, Tokyo, Japan). The peptides were eluted at a flow rate of 300 nL/min and operated in positive ion mode. The Xcalibur 4.4 workstation (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for MS control. Solvent A consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid.

For EV sample analysis, the separation was achieved using a 0–35% linear gradient of solvent B from 0 to 120 min, 35–100% of B from 120 to 121 min, and 100% solvent B from 121 to 125 min. The sample injection volume was 4.5 μL, the spray voltage was set at 2.0 kV, the capillary temperature was maintained at 320°C, and the S-lens (stacked ring ion guide) RF (radio frequency) level was 50. Full mass spectra were acquired using an m/z range of 400–1,000 with a resolution of 70,000. Product ion mass spectra were acquired using a DIA method with a resolution of 35,000, a normalized collision energy of 27%, an isolation window of 25 m/z, and a loop count of 24.

For cell sample analysis, the separation was performed using a 0–35% linear gradient of solvent B from 0 to 150 min, 35–100% of B from 150 to 151 min, and 100% of solvent B from 151 to 155 min. The sample injection volume was 1.0 μL. The spray voltage was set at 1.8 kV, the capillary temperature was maintained at 250°C, and the S-lens (stacked ring ion guide) RF level was 50. Full mass spectra were acquired using an m/z range of 495–905 with a resolution of 35,000. Product ion mass spectra were acquired using a DIA method with a resolution of 17,500, a normalized collision energy of 27%, an isolation window of 20 m/z, and a loop count of 10. DIA-MS experiments were performed using three biological replicates.

6. Quantitative proteomics

Raw DIA files were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard (3.0.21) (ProteoWizard Software Foundation, Palo Alto, CA, USA).48) Database searching and quantitative analysis were performed using DIA-NN49) (v1.9) (Vadim Demichev, Berlin, Germany) against the UniProt human proteome (May 2024). The mzML files were converted to DIA files using DIA-NN. The DIA-NN parameters were set as follows: library-free research and deep learning-based spectra and retention time (RT) prediction. Mass accuracy was set to 10 ppm (1e−05) for both MS1 and MS2. Remove likely interferences: ON. Protein interference: ON. Cross-run normalization: RT-dependent. The protease was Trypsin/P and the missed cleavage was 1. The boxes for the N-term M excision and C carbamidomethylation were checked. The peptide length range was 7–30. The precursor charge range was 1–4. The precursor m/z range was 400–1000 for EV samples and 495–905 for cell samples. Minimum unique peptides were 0. The fragment ion m/z range was 100–1800. The precursor False Discovery Rate (FDR) was 1.0%.

7. Statistical analysis

Data sorting was performed using Microsoft Excel. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard error. The data were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. A log2 fold-change was plotted against the −log10 of the p-value. Statistical significance was declared at an FDR LogWorth (−log10 p-value) of 1.3 (equivalent to a p-value of 0.05). The bar chart was created using Excel. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was done using Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1). Principal component analysis (PCA) plots, volcano plots, and correlation analysis were performed using JMP Student Edition 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). For normalization, missing data were assigned values below the quantified minimum (3,000 for cell samples, 1,500 for EV samples).

8. Immunoblotting

The EV-enriched proteins (0.2 μg per well) were solubilized in EzApply (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) and denatured for 5 min at 95°C. After separating by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using SuperSep Ace, 10–20% gels (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) under a constant current of 21 mA for 40 min, the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer Pack (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The PVDF membranes were blocked with Block ACE (KAC Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) for 1 h. After washing three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS; Sigma-Aldrich) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T), the membranes were incubated for 1 h at 20°C with primary antibodies against CD9 (1/1,000; 014-27763; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), CD63 (1/1,000; 012-27063; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), or CD81 (1/2,000; 011-27773; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical). The membranes were washed three times with TBS-T and incubated with an horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1/2,000; SA00001-1; Proteintech, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h. The antibodies were diluted in Kiwami Setsuyaku-Kun antibody dilution buffer (DRC, Tokyo, Japan). The PVDF membrane was washed three times with TBS-T, and the target proteins were detected using Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and visualized with a C-DiGit Blot Scanner (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). Images were acquired using Image Studio Software (LI-COR).

9. Flow cytometry

On day 21 of NC differentiation or day 18 of CM differentiation, the cells (1.0 × 106) were washed twice with DPBS (−) and incubated in Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at 37°C for 3 min. The detached cells were centrifuged at 700 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. The cells were washed twice with DMEM/Ham’s F-12 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), and the supernatant was removed. Methanol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) at −30°C was added to the cells and incubated for 10 min. After removing the solution, the cells were washed twice with 0.5% BSA/DPBS (−), and the supernatants were removed. NC-differentiated cells on day 21 were incubated in 0.5% BSA/DPBS (−) containing purified anti-Tuj1 antibody (1/50; 801201; BioLegend) at 4°C for 16 h. Similarly, CM-differentiated cells on day 18 were incubated in 0.5% BSA/DPBS (−) containing cardiac troponin T monoclonal antibody (1/100; MA5-12960; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were rinsed twice with DPBST and incubated with Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1/2000) at 20°C for 1 h. The cells were washed twice with DPBST and incubated in DAPI solution (1/200) at 20°C for 3 min. The cells were washed twice with DPBST, and the aggregates were removed by filtration through a cell strainer (BD Falcon, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The cells were examined using a CytoFLEX-S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) at 488 nm and a flow rate of 30 μL/min. 20,000 events were recorded for each sample. Data analysis was performed using CytExpert (Beckman Coulter).

10. Proteomics data availability

The mass spectrometry proteomics data generated in this study were deposited in the jPost repository (Japan Proteome Standard Repository) under the dataset identifier PXD072745. The data can be accessed at https://jpostdb.org.

RESULTS

1. Overview of the experimental workflow

The iPSCs were differentiated into two lineages, and comparative quantitative proteomics was performed on the cellular and EV fractions from iPSCs and the differentiated derivatives. This enabled the identification of lineage-specific signature proteins, followed by an evaluation to determine which of these proteins is a suitable predictive marker for monitoring differentiation during manufacturing (Fig. 1). Specifically, three donor-derived iPSC lines (201B7, 610B1, and 1231A3) were induced to differentiate into NCs and CMs. To identify predictive markers of differentiation, the cell culture media and cells were collected on days 0 and 7 for each differentiation in triplicate. The cell culture media were collected after 24 h. After removing the floating cell debris by filtration of the cell culture medium, the EVs were concentrated by PS affinity purification using magnetic beads. The cellular protein was extracted from the cell lysates by sonication. The EV and cell proteins were reduced, alkylated, and cleaved into peptide fragments by Trypsin/Lys-C digestion. For quantitative proteomics, spectral data were acquired by DIA-MS and analyzed using DIA-NN software.

Fig. 1. Overview of the experimental workflow. Cells and the culture medium were collected at days 0 and 7 during iPSC differentiation into NCs and CMs. EVs were concentrated from the medium by affinity purification using magnetic beads, and proteins were extracted from the cell lysates, followed by trypsin digestion and DIA-MS. Quantitative proteomic data were used to extract EV protein signatures associated with NC or CM differentiation. Differentiation efficiency was validated across three cell lines. Finally, EV protein signatures were evaluated as predictive markers. 2. Immunofluorescence characterization of iPSC-derived neural and cardiac cells

The differentiation stages at days 0 and 7 of differentiation into NCs and CMs in three iPSC lines were characterized by immunostaining using common differentiation stage-specific markers (Fig. 2). The late differentiation stages (day 21 for NC and day 18 for CM) were confirmed to differentiate into target cells (Fig. 2). The iPSCs on day 0 were confirmed to be undifferentiated by staining the cell surface with R-10G. Nestin in the cytoplasm was stained on day 7 of NC differentiation. Nkx2.5 in the nucleus was stained on day 7 of CM differentiation. These results indicate that differentiated iPSCs on day 7 may be classified as NSCs or early-stage cardiac progenitor cells. In addition, on day 21 of NC differentiation, Tuj1 was stained in the cytoplasm and axons. On day 18 of CM differentiation, cardiac troponin T, which is specific for myofibrils, was stained. These observations indicate that all three iPSC lines can differentiate into NCs or CMs.

During CM differentiation, Nkx2.5 is present in the nucleus at day 7 (second from left), and troponin T is present in the myofibrils at day 18 (far left). Scale bars: 200 μm. 3. Characterization of EVs in iPSCs by size distribution and marker expression

Characterization of EVs from the iPSC culture medium was done using TEM and NTA. Particles with approximate diameters of 200 nm were observed in the EV fractions by TEM (Fig. 3A). NTA indicated that the mode particle size was 200.0 ± 12.00 nm, with an average particle size of 256.9 ± 9.40 nm. The distribution included a wide range of sizes from small to large EVs (Fig. 3B). These observations suggest that the integrity of EVs was preserved throughout the EV preparation procedures. In addition, immunoblot analysis of the EV markers CD81, CD63, and CD9 was performed on the EV samples from days 0 and 7 of NC/CM differentiation for all cell lines (Fig. 3C). For comparison with the EV fractions, flow-through fractions (FTs) of the supernatant remaining after EV enrichment were used as a negative control. As a result, EV marker bands were detected in all EV fractions, whereas no bands were detected in the FT fractions. In addition, 93 of the top 100 most frequently reported EV proteins listed in the Vesiclepedia database (version 5.1) were identified in our EV protein dataset by DIA-MS, further supporting the validity of our EV isolation and proteomic analysis.

4. Comparative proteomics for correlation analysis between EVs and iPSC-derived cells

To confirm whether EV proteins and cellular proteins can distinguish iPSC differentiation, PCA was performed for NC (Fig. 4A) and CM (Fig. 4B) differentiation using the quantitative values obtained by DIA-MS. In EV samples on days 0 and 7 of NC differentiation, 1,469 proteins were quantified, whereas 5,646 proteins were quantified in the cell samples. Similarly, for CM differentiation, 1,476 proteins were quantified in the EV samples, whereas 5,304 proteins were quantified in the cell samples. PC1 accounted for 40% of the variance, clearly separating day 0 and day 7 for both EVs and cells. PC2 explained 10–14% of the variance, distinguishing clusters by cell line.

The correlations of the changes between cells and EVs during differentiation were analyzed. The fold-changes in quantitative values from days 0 to 7 were plotted, and the correlation coefficients were calculated for NC (Fig. 4C) and CM (Fig. 4D) differentiation. A total of 1,200 proteins were common to both EVs and cells during NC differentiation, and 1,217 proteins during CM differentiation. A correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations, with r = 0.528 for NC differentiation and r = 0.507 for CM differentiation. These results indicate positive correlations between EVs and cells based on fold-change values for differentiation. Therefore, analysis of the characteristic EV proteins derived from differentiated cells suggests their potential as predictive markers for cell differentiation.

Furthermore, representative markers showed significant alterations in both cells and EVs. In NC differentiation, NC adhesion molecule 1, 2ʹ,3ʹ-cyclic-nucleotide 3ʹ-phosphodiesterase, and stathmin-2 were significantly upregulated in both cells and their EVs. These proteins are associated with neuronal development, including cell adhesion, axonal growth, and neuron–glia interactions.50–52) In CM differentiation, myosin light chain 4 and platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha were significantly increased in both cells and EVs, reflecting processes related to cardiac lineage development and mesenchymal/cardiovascular differentiation.53,54) These results showed that proteins associated with differentiation and lineage-related functions exhibit concordant changes in both cells and EVs.

5. Identification of EV protein signatures for neural and cardiac cells

To identify significantly increased EV proteins at day 7 of differentiation, volcano plots were generated for NC (Fig. 5A) and CM (Fig. 5B) differentiation. The proteins were extracted based on the following criteria: increased more than 5-fold and a p-value less than 0.05. As a result, 217 proteins showed a significant increase in NC differentiation, whereas 174 proteins showed a significant increase in CM differentiation. Next, a comparison of the significantly increased EV proteins between NC and CM differentiation revealed 61 proteins common to both. In addition, 156 proteins were specifically increased during NC differentiation, and 113 proteins were specifically increased during CM differentiation (Fig. 5C).

DISCUSSION

We performed a DIA-MS-based quantitative proteomic analysis of EVs and iPSC-derived cells during the differentiation of three human iPSC lines into NCs and CMs. We identified lineage-specific EV protein signatures and predicted their potential usefulness as markers for monitoring differentiation during manufacturing processes (Fig. 1). All three iPSC lines, which were capable of differentiation into NCs/CMs were stained by NSC markers and CM progenitor markers on day 7 of each differentiation. This suggested that the cells analyzed on day 7 represent intermediate cells (Fig. 2).

In the present study, EV secretion and enrichment were confirmed in all iPSC lines at days 0 and 7 (Fig. 3). The mode size of the EVs derived from the iPSCs was 200 nm, whereas previous reports25,55–57) indicated sizes of 70–150 nm. This suggests that the PS affinity method used for EV enrichment in this study may concentrate different sizes of EVs compared with ultracentrifugation.

PCA based on quantitative values indicated that EVs and cells exhibited characteristics clearly differentiated by differentiation stage (Figs. 4A and 4B). Notably, the greater separation of the clusters at day 7 compared with day 0 suggests that differentiation induces differences between the cell lines in both EV-mediated secretion and cellular protein expression levels. Significant positive correlations were observed between EV protein secretion and cellular protein expression levels during differentiation (Figs. 4C and 4D). Although classical neurotransmitter and ion channel-related markers were not comprehensively correlated, the significantly upregulated NC- and CM-associated proteins in cells and EVs suggest that EV proteins reflect the cellular differentiation state.

We also identified EV protein signatures that were significantly increased during NC/CM differentiation from days 0 to 7 (Figs. 5A–5C). GO analysis of these proteins revealed that terms related to cell morphogenesis were highly enriched in NC differentiation, with terms characteristic of NCs, such as axon guidance (Fig. 5D). CM differentiation exhibited enrichment in terms related to ECM proteoglycans, which are important for maintaining tissue structure, skeletal function, and metabolism. Muscle-specific terms, such as “cytoskeleton in muscle cells” and “miR-targeted genes in muscle cells,” were also observed (Fig. 5E). This suggests that EV proteins secreted by cells at day 7 of differentiation may functionally contribute to cell differentiation.

From the EV proteins specifically increased during differentiation, EV protein signatures were identified based on their correlation with cells, reproducibility, and robustness (Tables 1 and 2). Although some of these proteins have already been shown to exert functions in differentiated cells,58–67) this study revealed that their secretion levels into the extracellular space increased during differentiation. Of the EV protein signatures for NC differentiation markers, TPBG was shown to be involved in morphogenesis, including cell morphogenesis, regulation of cell junction assembly, and regulation of cell projection organization by GO analysis. TTYH1 and CLU are involved in morphogenesis, such as cell morphogenesis and multivesicular body assembly, and in EV production (Fig. 5D). Previous studies have reported that CD63 expression in EVs during the differentiation of iPSCs into neural lineage cells correlates with the proportion of CORIN-positive cells.25) In contrast, our study not only utilized three independent iPSC lines but also compared two distinct differentiation lineages, NC and CM, thereby enabling the identification of novel, differentiation lineage-specific EV protein signatures.

Similarly, among EV protein signatures for CM differentiation markers, VIM and KRT19 were shown to be involved in ECM proteoglycans, embryonic morphogenesis, and VIM specifically in the cytoskeleton of muscle cells (Fig. 5E). Because these are clearly involved in differentiation, they are particularly suitable as markers.

A comparison of the positive rates of existing markers between cell lines by flow cytometry using late-stage differentiated cells revealed that 610B1 showed relatively low NC differentiation rates, whereas 1231A3 showed low CM differentiation rates (Fig. 6A). We assessed whether the fold-change values for the EV protein differentiation markers at day 7 indicated differences between the cell lines. Based on the significant differences in differentiation levels of the target cells, TPBG, SLC44A2, and TSPAN14 were considered predictive markers of NC differentiation (Fig. 6B). TPBG is considered a suitable marker because of its functional involvement based on GO analysis, and it showed the greatest significant difference. TPBG is a membrane protein that inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by binding to low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 6, which is a Frizzled co-receptor.59,60) The levels of TPBG were increased during NC differentiation of iPSCs at day 7, and its knockdown promotes cell proliferation, rather than differentiation into NCs. Regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by TPBG may be important for NC differentiation.58)

Only KRT19 was identified as a marker reflecting differences in CM differentiation (Fig. 6C). KRT19 is an epithelial protein that is present in intermediate filaments and is involved in muscle fiber organization.68–70) Although keratins are commonly considered contaminants in proteomics, KRT19 is rarely expressed in skin keratinocytes or hair; thus, it was unlikely to be a contaminant.71,72) In addition, previous studies have identified it as a novel marker for cardiac epithelial cells that transiently appear during early cardiac development, suggesting its essential role in heart development.67)

Both TPBG and KRT19 play important roles in the differentiation and development of target cells and are abundant in cells. Because these proteins are increased in EVs during differentiation, their secretion via EVs may affect the differentiation of iPSCs. Although TPBG is a membrane protein and may be exposed on the surface of EVs, KRT19 is a cytoskeletal protein that lacks a transmembrane domain. The detection of these proteins as markers may require careful treatment, such as solubilization of the EV membrane. However, because the localization of these proteins within EVs remains unclear, the development of methods to detect EV proteins in culture medium is an important challenge for their application as markers. While the present study focused on the early differentiation period to identify predictive EV markers, the reproducibility and applicability of EV-based signatures at later differentiation stages and across different differentiation protocols remain to be addressed in future studies.

In conclusion, we identified predictive EV protein markers for differentiation that were associated with the differentiations of cell lines, including: TPBG for NC and KRT19 for CM. It will be necessary to develop a quantitative evaluation system for iPSC differentiation by flow cytometry or ELISA with anti-TPBG and anti-KRT19 antibodies and to correlate the levels of these EV-based predictive markers with the final differentiation (%) of iPSCs. In addition, cell monitoring by EV proteins may facilitate efficient production of iPSC-derived products.
