Ultrafast Cooling With Total Liquid Ventilation Mitigates Early Inflammatory Response and Offers Neuroprotection in a Porcine Model of Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Yara Abi Zeid Daou, Naoto Watanabe, Fanny Lidouren, Antoine Bois, Estelle Faucher, Hélène Huet, Alice Hutin, Ali Jendoubi, Mathieu Surenaud, Sophie Hue, Mathieu Nadeau, Sandrine Perrotto, Mickaël Libardi, Bijan Ghaleh, Philippe Micheau, Patrick Bruneval, Alain Cariou, Matthias Kohlhauer, Renaud Tissier
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain injury is one of the most serious complications after cardiac arrest (CA). To prevent this phenomenon, rapid cooling with total liquid ventilation (TLV) has been proposed in small animal models of CA (rabbits and piglets). Here, we aimed to determine whether hypothermic TLV can also offer neuroprotection and mitigate cerebral inflammatory response in large animals.Methods and ResultsAnesthetized pigs were subjected to 14 minutes of ventricular fibrillation followed by cardiopulmonary resuscitation. After return of spontaneous circulation, animals were randomly subjected to normothermia (control group, n=8) or ultrafast cooling with TLV (TLV group, n=8). In the latter group, TLV was initiated within a window of 15 minutes after return of spontaneous circulation and allowed to reduce tympanic, esophageal, and bladder temperature to the 32 to 34 °C range within 30 minutes. After 45 minutes of TLV, gas ventilation was resumed, and hypothermia was maintained externally until 3 hours after CA, before rewarming using heat pads (0.5 °C–1 °C/h). After an additional period of progressive rewarming for 3 hours, animals were euthanized for brain withdrawal and histological analysis. At the end of the follow‐up (ie, 6 hours after CA), histology showed reduced brain injury as witnessed by the reduced number of Fluroro‐Jade C‐positive cerebral degenerating neurons in TLV versus control. IL (interleukin)‐1ra and IL‐8 levels were also significantly reduced in the cerebrospinal fluid in TLV versus control along with cerebral infiltration by CD3+ cells. Conversely, circulating levels of cytokines were not different among groups, suggesting a discrepancy between local and systemic inflammatory levels.ConclusionsUltrafast cooling with TLV mitigates neuroinflammation and attenuates acute brain lesions in the early phase following resuscitation in large animals subjected to CA.Keywords: cardiac arrest, hypothermia, inflammation, liquid ventilationSubject Categories: Cardiopulmonary Arrest, Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care, Basic Science Research, Animal Models of Human Disease

Following cardiac arrest (CA), most surviving patients present multiple complications including cardiac and neurological injuries, leading to a post‐CA syndrome. 1 , 2 In animal studies, temperature reduction and mild hypothermia (32–34 °C) were shown to provide potent benefits against brain injury occurring after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). 3 , 4 This was demonstrated in many experimental conditions and recently emphasized by a meta‐analysis of experimental studies in small and large animals. 3 , 4 Conversely, therapeutic hypothermia (33 °C) did not seem to improve the outcome in patients when compared with subnormothermia (36 °C) or strict normothermia (37 °C) after ROSC. 5 , 6 The recent guidelines co‐issued by the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) recommendations suggested that targeted temperature management with active fever prevention could be sufficient in most patients resuscitated after CA. 7 This apparent discrepancy between animal and human studies might be explained by different windows of application of hypothermia with a short time to target temperature in bench studies (<1 hour after ROSC in most studies) 8 versus delayed hypothermia in most clinical reports (>6–8 hours after ROSC). 7 The recent ERC‐ESICM guidelines also state that “the therapeutic window within which temperature control with hypothermia may be effective in the clinical setting is unknown.” 7

In this context, our team has investigated total liquid ventilation (TLV) as an innovative technique for rapid cooling induction. This technique consists of lung filling and tidal ventilation with temperature‐controlled breathable liquids, 9 enabling body cooling with a core target temperature of 32 to 34 °C within <30 minutes in both small and large animals. 4 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Importantly, hypothermic TLV was further demonstrated to be potently cardio‐ and neuroprotective in rabbits and piglet models of CA. 4 , 11 For instance, hypothermic TLV instituted 15 minutes after ROSC decreased IL (interleukin)‐6 blood levels rapidly after CA and attenuated brain injury on both clinical outcome and histopathological markers after 72 hours of follow‐up. 10 However, the relationship between systemic and local cerebral inflammation was not properly established, and this was only observed in small animals until now. 10 Accordingly, the goal of the present study was to evaluate the neuroprotective effect of TLV in a large animal porcine model of CA, as well as its ability to mitigate local inflammatory response in the brain.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

Data are available upon request to the corresponding author.

Board Statement

All experiments were approved by the French ethical board for animal research number 16 (Comité d'Ethique Anses–ENVA–UPEC; APAFIS #32855‐2 021 090 315 125 530 v2) and fully complied with French and European regulations on the use of laboratory animals.

Animal Preparation

Female swine (Lebeau, Gambais France) were fasted overnight. Before the procedure, animals were sedated using a mixture of tiletamine and zolazepam (10 mg/kg each, IM). Anesthesia was induced by propofol (2.5 mg/kg IV and 10 mg/kg per hour, IV, for induction and maintenance, respectively). Analgesia was provided by an administration of methadone (0.75 mg/kg, IM). After endotracheal intubation, animals were mechanically ventilated (FiO2=30%, tidal volume=6–8 mL/kg, positive end‐expiratory pressure=5 cm H2O, respiratory rate=20–25 cycles/min; Monnal T60; Air Liquide Medical Systems, Antony, France), and the ventilation parameters were adjusted to optimize oxygenation and target normocapnia. Temperature probes were inserted into the bladder, ear drum, and esophagus. At baseline, all swine were placed on a heating pad to maintain their body temperature at 38.0±0.5 °C.

Two sheaths were introduced into the femoral vessels using the Seldinger technique to introduce central catheters and monitor arterial and right atrial blood pressures using pressure transducers (SPR‐524; Millar, Houston, TX). To compensate for fluid loss, animals received a standardized administration of Ringer Lactate (4 mL/kg).

CA and Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation Protocol

After a period of stabilization, ventricular fibrillation was induced by an electrical stimulation (A/C 10 V) with a pacemaker probe introduced into the right ventricle through the venous femoral sheath. Mechanical ventilation was simultaneously interrupted. After 14 minutes of no flow, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated with continuous chest compressions using an automated device (100 compressions/min; Lucas) and resumption of mechanical ventilation (FiO2=100%, respiratory rate=10 cycles/min, tidal volume=6–8 mL/kg). Following 3 minutes of CPR, epinephrine (0.5 mg) was administered, and external defibrillations were attempted until ROSC. After ROSC, norepinephrine was administered to maintain mean arterial blood pressure at 75±5 mm Hg if needed. Ventilatory parameters were set at similar levels as baseline and readjusted, if required, to target normocapnia and prevent hyperoxia. In the absence of ROSC after 20 minutes, experiments were interrupted. In resuscitated animals, a second administration of methadone was performed 4 hours after ROSC (0.75 mg/kg, IM).

Experimental Groups

As described in Figure 1A, resuscitated pigs were randomly subjected to normothermia or hypothermic TLV after ROSC (control and TLV groups, respectively; both n=8). In the control group, animals underwent conventional gas ventilation throughout the procedure, and body temperature was maintained at ~38 °C throughout follow‐up. In the TLV group, liquid ventilation was implemented within 15 minutes after ROSC to achieve a core body temperature of 32 to 34 °C. TLV was only used for cooling induction, after which gas ventilation was resumed and hypothermia maintained externally until the end of the third hour following the onset of CPR. Then, a slow rewarming phase was initiated with heating pads during an additional period of 3 hours (+0.5–1 °C per hour). Animals were euthanized in both groups after a total period of 6 hours of follow‐up after CA.

Beyond the animals included in the main experiments and subjected to CA, 6 additional animals were anesthetized to sample cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with neither CA nor TLV (sham group). This allowed the evaluation of usual cytokine concentrations in the CSF as a comparison with values obtained in the control and TLV groups after CA (see below).

TLV Procedure

In the TLV group, animals received rocuronium (3 mg/kg, IV) after ROSC. Then, liquid ventilation was instituted using a dedicated TLV device (Figure S1) with perfluorooctyl bromide, as breathable liquid. At the onset of the procedure, the endotracheal tube was disconnected from the conventional ventilator to be connected to a remote Y piece of the TLV device. This piece evaluates breathable liquid temperatures and pressures throughout the procedure. Once connected, TLV was started with an initial lung filling with a residual liquid volume of 12 mL/kg. In case of insufficient lung filling, this target could be increased within the 12 to 18 mL/kg range and further maintained throughout the procedure by an embedded algorithm. Respiratory rate and tidal liquid volume were set at 6 cycles/min and 8 mL/kg, respectively. The fraction of oxygen infused in the disposable kit was also adjusted to target pulsed oximetry between 94% and 98%. Finally, perfluorooctyl bromide temperature was automatically controlled to maximize thermal exchange while preventing temperature undershoot. After 45 minutes of TLV, the procedure was ended by a dedicated mode allowing to collect from the lung as much of the breathable liquid as possible. Then, animals were reconnected to the conventional gas ventilator until the end of the procedure using the same ventilator parameters as previously mentioned for control animals.

Investigated Parameters

During follow‐up, heart rate and mean arterial blood pressure were continuously monitored. Blood samples were collected regularly to assess arterial blood pH, O2 and CO2 partial pressures, and lactate and bicarbonate levels. Additional blood samples were collected at 2 and 6 hours after CA for further analyses, as well as CSF, as previously described. 15 These additional samples were stored at −80 °C before evaluation of inflammatory mediators or neurofilament light chain (NFL). The CSF samples of sham animals (see above) were also analyzed for the determination of usual cytokine levels in the CSF. Briefly, cytokine concentrations were assessed using a dedicated porcine multiplex evaluating of TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor‐α), IL‐1ra, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐18, MMP1 (matrix metalloproteinase‐1), and cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31) (8‐plex, number LXSAPM‐08, L151732; R&D Systems; Bio‐Plex 200; Bio‐Rad). NFL was also evaluated by a dedicated porcine kit (Neuro 2‐Plex B Advantage Kit number 103520; Simoa SR‐X; Quanterix).

End of Protocol

At the end of the follow‐up period, animals were euthanized, and brain and lung samples were extracted and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analyses. Fluoro‐Jade C staining was used to spot degenerating neurons in nonoverlapping fields within the cortex (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA). Infiltrated T cells into the brain were detected and counted using a monoclonal anti‐CD3 antibody (anti‐CD3, number A0452; Dako). Four lung samples were also prepared and stained with hematoxylin–eosin for histological analyses (2 samples from ventral left and right diaphragmatic lobes and 2 samples from the dorsal parts of the same lobes). Extension and severity of the lung lesions, which consist of cardiogenic edema, congestion, interstitial inflammation, or necrosis (pulmonary infarct‐like pattern), were blindly assessed on a 0 to 5 scale for each sample (0: normal appearance, 5: extended and severe lesions). The mean value was calculated for each animal for each type of lesion.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean±SEM. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Continuous variables were compared between groups using a 2‐way ANOVA for repeated measures, followed by Sidak multiple comparison test if needed. Post hoc analysis was only made between groups but not among the different time points, except for cytokines and NFL, for which we aimed at investigating variation as compared with baseline. In the CSF, we did not compare values between time points, because we did not have baseline values. Accordingly, we compared values to sham animals. The number of degenerating neurons in the brain and the score of lung lesions at histology were compared between groups using the nonparametric Mann‐Whitney test. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Thirty animals were subjected to CA, among which 16 were successfully resuscitated and allocated to the control or TLV groups (both n=8). Body weight was not different between groups (34±1 versus 31±1 kg in TLV versus control, respectively). Targeted Temperature Management

As illustrated in Figure 1, tympanic, esophageal, and bladder temperatures were similar between groups at baseline. After CA, these temperatures remained within the normal range in the control group and decreased <34 °C within 15 to 30 minutes in the TLV group. During the rewarming period, temperatures increased by +0.5 to 1 °C per hour. Full rewarming was not achieved in the TLV group before the end of the protocol (ie, 360 minutes after CA).

Hemodynamics

At baseline, mean arterial pressure and heart rate did not differ between the TLV and control groups. Following CA, heart rate significantly decreased in the TLV versus control group (127±4 versus 168±10 bpm at 30 minutes after CA, respectively). Mean arterial pressure was significantly increased in the TLV versus control group at 240 minutes after CA (84±2 versus 67±4 mm Hg, respectively), despite reduced doses of norepinephrine (Figure 2).

As shown in Table 2, there was no difference in biochemical parameters between groups at baseline. Inflammatory Markers

At baseline, both groups had similar blood cytokine concentrations. As illustrated in Figure 3, blood cytokine concentrations did not significantly differ after CA between the control and TLV group. Conversely, most cytokine concentrations in animals subjected to CA increased in the CSF (ie, control and TLV groups) when compared with sham animals, as illustrated in Figure 4. IL‐1ra and IL‐8 CSF levels were significantly reduced in the TLV versus control group at 6 hours after CA. For instance, IL‐8 concentration achieved 147.19±71 versus 735.83±243.28 pg/mL in the CSF in the TLV versus control group, respectively. In addition, IL‐6 concentrations in the CSF tended to decrease in the TLV versus control group without reaching statistical significance (9.7±5.05 versus 1669.61±1093.53 pg/mL at 120 minutes after CA, respectively).

The total number of degenerating neurons was also significantly reduced in the cortex of TLV animals versus control animals (10±1 versus 27±4 neurons per field, respectively). The number of CD3+ cells counted at brain immunohistochemistry was also significantly lower in the TLV versus control group (Figure 5).

Lung Histology

As illustrated in Figure 6A through 6C, the morphological appearance of the lung samples at histology revealed alveolar serous cardiogenic edema, congestion, and interstitial inflammation. Foci of pulmonary infarct‐like lesions were also observed in 5 out of 8 swine in the control group versus 0 out of 8 in the TLV group (Figure 6D). Overall, histological lesional scores were significantly decreased for cardiogenic edema, congestion, and pulmonary infarct‐like lesions in the TLV versus control group (Figure 6A). Scores of inflammation were not different between groups. In 1 lung sample from the TLV group, we also observed few macrophages with foamy appearance, suggesting clearance of the breathable liquid residue (Figure 6E).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the impact of hypothermic TLV on the inflammatory response and brain injury after CA in pigs. Remarkably, TLV attenuated local immune response in the brain through reduced cytokine concentrations in the CSF and cerebral infiltration by CD3+ cells. TLV also attenuated brain injury on histopathological markers. This was not associated with a systemic effect on circulatory cytokine blood levels.

This study extends previous results obtained with TLV on its ability to induce rapid hypothermia in large animals, providing a strong translational insight. Target temperature (<34 °C) was achieved in all body compartments within 30 minutes, corresponding to a cooling rate of ~−8 °C/h in body core. This was associated with a significant reduction in the overall number of degenerating neurons in the brain, and a trend toward reduced NFL blood levels. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of the neuroprotective effect of TLV in large animals, because it was mostly documented in rabbits 4 , 10 , 12 , 14 and piglets 13 until now. Importantly, a short hypothermic phase was sufficient to provide neuroprotective effects, whereas patients are generally treated with hypothermia for at least 24 hours when instituted in the clinical arena. 7

Importantly, the neuroprotective effect of TLV was observed with a procedure that started within 15 minutes after ROSC, allowing achievement of the target temperature within 45 to 60 minutes after the onset of CPR. This corresponds to an early therapeutic window of application when compared with clinical trials on target temperature management requiring >6 to 8 hours to achieve target temperature after CA. 5 , 6 , 16 However, TLV was instituted post‐ROSC, which differs from the intra‐arrest cooling that provides the most potent protection in small animals. 8 , 17 This also differs from other small animal studies in which hypothermia is achieved virtually immediately after cooling initiation, due to their low body mass, even when induced after ROSC. 8 In the present study, the cooling protocol also differs from most reports performed in large animals (ie, pigs and dogs). In a recent meta‐analysis of experimental studies in the field, Arrich et al demonstrated that among 17 large animals studies, 11 investigations started cooling at ROSC and 6 studies from 5 to 60 minutes after ROSC. 8 Among the latter studies, 1 study achieved target temperature within <60 minutes, 18 2 others required 60 min19, 20 and 3 >90 minutes. 21 , 22 , 23 Interestingly, this meta‐analysis demonstrated that cooling rate was an independent predictor of the benefit of therapeutic hypothermia in large animals, along with hypothermia duration and target temperature, which was not the case for the time to target temperature. This suggests that cooling rate could be more important than the moment of cooling induction after ROSC among the included investigations. Importantly, we should again consider that most experimental studies evaluated a short period of hypothermia, as we did here, with only 3 hours of hypothermia before starting rewarming, which is a major difference with clinical practice.

This study also highlights the cerebral anti‐inflammatory effects of TLV. These anti‐inflammatory effects were observed not only during the brief hypothermic period but also throughout the rewarming phase, suggesting that despite the shortness of the hypothermic episode, the effect of hypothermia was sustained beyond this period. These interesting results are in accordance with a previous study that demonstrated that mild therapeutic hypothermia resulted in a decrease in inflammatory cytokine expression in the cortex of swine subjected to CA. 24 Conversely, we did not observe any difference in blood cytokine levels in TLV versus control animals. This differs from our previous study in rabbits, in which IL‐6 blood levels were reduced during the hypothermia episode in TLV animals versus controls, 10 suggesting that swine are less likely to develop a systemic rather than cerebral immune response after CA. Interestingly, our results are consistent with clinical reports demonstrating no effect of hypothermia in patients on the cytokine blood levels after CA. 10 , 25 , 26 , 27

Nevertheless, the key finding is that the local effect of TLV on cytokine concentrations in the CSF was ultimately associated with reduced infiltration by CD3+ lymphocytes, as a marker of neuroinflammation. This is consistent with previous experimental reports in animal models of focal cerebral ischemia showing that hypothermia not only decrease cerebral proinflammatory cytokine concentrations 28 but also infiltration by circulatory leukocytes. 27 , 28 The mechanism of the action of hypothermia on lymphocyte migration has not been fully investigated but could involve, at least in part, the reduction in proinflammatory cytokine levels in the CSF, reduced brain–blood barrier opening, 12 and a direct effect of temperature reduction on leukocytes reactivity. 10

In addition to brain injury evaluation, the present study also investigated the effect of TLV on lung recovery after cardiac arrest. Importantly, we observed a trend toward moderately lower blood oxygenation at the end of the follow‐up in TLV versus control animals, despite reduced lung lesions. We could speculate that TLV actually preserved lung morphology but could temporarily favor atelectasis during the evaporation phase of the breathable liquid from the lung. In the present study, positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP) was limited to 5 cm H2O after cardiac arrest, which is likely insufficient to completely prevent some atelectasis. This could explain mildly reduced blood oxygenation despite reduced lung lesions after TLV as compared with control animals.

This study presents some limitations. First, the hypothermic period was limited to 3 hours, which is interesting to evaluate the early action of hypothermia but not comparable to the clinical practice. Second, animals were also not completely rewarmed by the end of the follow‐up period. Third, due to a small sample size and sex selection, the results can hardly be extrapolated to a wider population. In addition, when the sample size is small, the P value is not a great criterion for identifying significant differences. For instance, a power analysis demonstrated that at least 84 animals would be required to evidence a significant group effect for NFL blood levels at the end follow‐up, considering a hypothetical group effect similar to that observed in the present study with β=80%. It also suggests that histopathological parameters of neurological injury, which were less variable, might be more appropriate for experimental studies with small sample size. Finally, the follow‐up duration was limited to 6 hours after resuscitation, because we focused on acute brain injury and assessed early local cytokine production in the CSF. A longer period of follow‐up would be necessary to assess delayed brain injury.

CONCLUSIONS

The rapid induction of hypothermia by TLV attenuates acute brain injury and reduces the local inflammatory response occurring in the early phase following resuscitation in swine subjected to CA. These findings support further evaluation in patients resuscitated after CA to investigate potential neuroprotection.
