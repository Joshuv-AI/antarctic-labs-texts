A Patient With a Left Ventricular Assist Device Requiring Emergency Surgery for a Strangulated Umbilical Hernia: A Case Report

Introduction

The European Hernia Society classifies umbilical hernias as midline abdominal wall hernias located within the M3 zone of the abdomen (from 3 cm above to 3 cm below the umbilicus) [1]. Studies providing coherent, worldwide applicable data on incidence and complication rates of umbilical hernias are scarce, and providing reliable statistics requires referencing studies such as one conducted by Kokotovic et al, wherein 61.6% of 789 adult patients with an umbilical or epigastric hernia required surgical intervention within 5 years, 1.4% of which were acute [2]. Strangulation of an umbilical hernia is defined as compromised blood flow to the contents of the hernia (usually omentum or small bowel) [3], leading to necrosis if left untreated. Tissue necrosis can lead to bowel perforation, peritonitis, and sepsis.

It was recently estimated that patients with advanced heart failure comprise approximately 1% to 10% of the overall heart failure population [4]. The standard treatment for advanced heart failure is heart transplantation, but this has substantial limitations, primarily the significant shortage of donor organs. Mechanical circulatory support (MCS), mainly via left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), has become an alternative form of treatment for patients with advanced heart failure [4].

Our patient was implanted with a HeartWare HVAD System (HW, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) device. The HW is a continuous-flow device, which consists of a few key components. The inflow cannula directs blood from the left ventricular cavity to the blood pump. The pump contains a centrifugal rotating impeller connected to the ascending aorta via an outflow graft. The driveline is a cable subcutaneously tunneled from the pump exiting the body at the lower abdominal wall to the external controller and the energy supply [4]. The pump assists the left ventricle in pumping blood to the aorta, thus improving the survival rate of patients with advanced heart failure and can serve either as a bridge to transplantation or as a destination therapy [5]. Implantation of the LVAD requires an individualized anticoagulation and antiplatelet regimen due to the increased risk of thromboembolic events [6].

Between 2013 and 2022, isolated continuous-flow LVADs were implanted in 27 493 patients in the United States alone [7]. In recent years, few studies have attempted to estimate the number of LVAD patients who required emergency abdominal surgery. A study by Sanaiha et al used the 2005–2015 National In-patient Sample to identify all adult LVAD patients in the United States who had required emergency general surgery (EGS). Interestingly, the annual incidence of EGS in LVAD patients decreased from 5.4% to 3.3% within this time period. Nevertheless, LVAD-EGS patients had significantly higher odds of mortality compared with non-EGS patients, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.5 [8]. To the best of our knowledge, no comparative data regarding the incidence of emergency abdominal surgeries in LVAD patients in other parts of the world besides the United States have been published. International registry analyses, such as those from the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (IMACS) Registry, describe geographic differences in LVAD recipients characteristics and focused mainly on device-specific complications [9]. Consequently, the actual worldwide scale of the problem is unknown. Furthermore, no applicable guidelines for perioperative management of LVAD patients in an emergency setting have been prepared thus far and the available data are mainly anecdotal. Lack of proper preparation of the patient in terms of anticoagulation and antibiotic treatment lowers the survival chances drastically. Thus, preoperative communication with the appropriate LVAD center is advised to reduce the risk of perioperative complications [4].

This report describes the case of a 43-year-old man with an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) and a left ventricular assist device (LVAD) on long-term anticoagulation with warfarin and clopidogrel who required emergency abdominal surgery for an irreducible, strangulated umbilical hernia and a right-sided, non-strangulated inguinal hernia.

Case Report

In 2014, a 43-year-old man presented to the General, Oncological, and Endocrine Surgery Clinic of the Provincial Hospital in Kielce, Poland with a 2-day history of abdominal pain and vomiting. He had undergone an ICD implantation over 3 years prior as a sudden cardiac arrest primary preventive measure and HeartWare LVAD implantation in the Silesian Center for Heart Diseases in Zabrze, Poland 9 months prior to admission due to advanced heart failure – New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III/IV – caused by dilated cardiomyopathy. He had a medical history of hypertension, hypothyroidism, infection of the driveline exit site treated with cloxacillin, an inguinal hernia, and an umbilical hernia that was scheduled to be treated surgically 7 days after admission to our hospital. He had been receiving clopidogrel, warfarin, losartan, carvedilol, eplerenone, levothyroxine, and cloxacillin.

Upon physical examination, the abdomen was soft with generalized tenderness and normal bowel sounds. He also had an irreducible umbilical hernia (8 cm in diameter) and periumbilical erythema. Further inspection revealed a right-sided inguinal hernia. Localized pain was present upon attempting to reduce the inguinal hernia.

The initial laboratory diagnostic workup results are presented in Table 1. The hemoglobin (HGB) concentration was 11.2 g/dl (reference range: 13.0–17.2 g/dl), hematocrit (HCT) was 34.1% (reference range: 37–49.5%), prothrombin time (PT) was 24.8 s (reference range: 9.7–14.5 s), prothrombin index (PI) was 47% (reference range: 70–130%), international normalized ratio (INR) was 2.0 (reference range: 0.8–1.2), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was 47.8 s (reference range: 26.0–40.0 s), and serum glucose concentration was 105 mg/dl (reference range: 65–100 mg/dl). Radiography of the chest and the abdomen were conducted. Lack of intestinal obstruction signs notwithstanding (Figure 1), his symptoms continued to worsen, requiring urgent surgery.

Because this case was managed as an emergency during the night, the feasibility of guideline-driven, elective anticoagulation strategies was significantly limited. The decision to operate was preceded by a consult with the on-call cardiology consultant from the reference center where the LVAD implantation had taken place. In accordance with the provided advice, 2 units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and 3 units of packed red blood cell concentrate (pRBC) were administered to maintain the INR within the range 1.5 to 2.5, which was required for proper functioning of the LVAD. Moreover, all the other perioperative decisions were taken in consonance with the LVAD implantation center and were continuously reevaluated, considering the dynamic balance between the risk of pump thrombosis and bleeding.

Prior to surgery, an INR range of 1.5 to 2.5 was intentionally maintained in lieu of complete anticoagulation reversal. This decision was dictated by the need to reduce the risk of bleeding to an acceptable level, concomitantly considering the increased risk of pump thrombosis in this dehydrated patient on continuous-flow support with imminent intestinal ischemia.

Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) is generally preferred over fresh frozen plasma (FFP) due to its more predictable factor profile, faster INR correction, and lower infusion volume. These advantages notwithstanding, PCC was unavailable at our hospital during nighttime emergency hours, whereas FFP was readily accessible. Because he was dehydrated, fluid overload was not a major concern. Therefore, FFP was selected as a safe alternative to PCC in an emergency setting. This approach allowed for timely intervention, while also preserving our patient’s safety. No pump thrombosis or ischemic complications occurred despite temporary anticoagulation interruption.

Intraoperative management was adjusted with the requirements of the LVAD in mind. Because the LVAD is preload-dependent, special attention was paid to avoiding hypovolemia and maintaining an adequate preload. Because the reliability of non-invasive methods of blood pressure measurement is limited in LVAD patients, invasive hemodynamic monitoring was utilized.

Throughout the procedure, the mean arterial pressure (MAP) was sustained within the LVAD-appropriate range (~70–90 mmHg), and normovolemia was ensured. Anesthesia considerations included minimizing sudden changes in systemic vascular resistance and swiftly addressing hypotension. With eventual hemodynamic instability or pump alarms in mind, echocardiographic support and immediate access to LVAD parameter assessment were constantly available. All intraoperative precautions and targets were established following consultation with the LVAD implantation center.

An open laparotomy was conducted, which revealed a strangulation of the small intestine within the sac of the umbilical hernia. A partial resection of the small intestine was performed with an end-to-end, hand-sewn double-layer anastomosis. In addition to bowel resection, the coexisting right-sided inguinal hernia was repaired during the same surgery. The repair was limited to a simple closure of the hernia defect without mesh implantation and without extensive dissection to reduce the risk of bowel incarceration in another hernia, which could have necessitated re-operation in an extremely high-risk LVAD patient. The procedure involved only peritoneal suturing; therefore, the operative time and the bleeding risk were not meaningfully increased, and this intervention was considered appropriate in this scenario. The surgery lasted 2 h 40 min. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was 600 ml and the postoperative drain output was about 500 mL. The histopathological examination of the specimen revealed hemorrhagic necrosis of the intestinal wall and the mesentery.

In the first 2 days of hospitalization, the patient started to develop symptoms of intraperitoneal bleeding, which corresponded to the rising INR (Figure 2). An abdominal ultrasound revealed free fluid in the intraperitoneal cavity, mostly on the right side – a 70-mm layer of fluid located below the inferior margin of the liver. Thus, a decision was made to totally withhold anticoagulant and antiplatelet medication and administer 2 units of pRBC and 1 unit of platelet concentrate (PC). The patient had become hemodynamically stable by the 5th day of hospitalization and on that day we decided to administer 40 mg of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) subcutaneously (s.c.) every 12 h. On the 6th day, we administered 60 mg of LMWH s.c. every 12 h. Finally, on the 7th day of hospitalization, the dose of LMWH was increased to 80 mg s.c. every 12 h until the last day of hospitalization. We recommenced 75 mg of clopidogrel per os (p.o.) on the 8th day and 5 mg of warfarin p.o. on the 9th day. Clopidogrel and warfarin administration continued until the last day of hospitalization. The following days of hospitalization were uneventful. On the 11th postoperative day, he was transferred to the LVAD implantation center to continue cardiological treatment.

Discussion

This case report provides a detailed description of perioperative management in a patient with an ICD and an LVAD who required emergency abdominal surgery for a strangulated umbilical hernia. Emergency hernia repair surgeries in LVAD patients were discussed in a single-center retrospective study by Beetz et al, but individual cases have not been described in detail [10]. Most available case reports describe emergency abdominal surgeries performed due to indications other than intestinal ischemia, such as acute appendicitis [11], cholecystitis [12], or a ruptured spleen [13]. Our case report is one of the few touching upon the perioperative anticoagulation management in an LVAD patient requiring intestinal anastomosis surgery.

Implantation of the LVAD entails an increased risk of thromboembolic incidents [5], which requires introducing adequate pharmacotherapy. The manufacturer of the HeartWare LVAD recommends introducing warfarin within 4 days after implantation, with the target INR range between 2.0 and 3.0 as part of anticoagulant therapy. A feasible antiplatelet treatment option is introducing a 300-mg loading dose of clopidogrel with a subsequent decrease of the dose to 75 mg per day [14].

Managing anticoagulation in patients who have been taking oral vitamin K antagonists long-term and require an urgent procedure with high bleeding risk usually involves withholding the anticoagulant medication for an equivalent of 4 to 5 of its half-lives, which in the case of warfarin is 5 days [15]. Our patient required immediate surgical intervention, which forced us to follow a different strategy of preoperative management.

Guidelines presented by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) classify anastomosis surgery as a procedure with high risk of bleeding [16]. According to the latest information available at the time, the choice of preoperative management strategy for patients taking oral vitamin K antagonists who also require an urgent surgery was dependent on the patient’s INR and allowed the use of a combination of several solutions, such as withholding vitamin K antagonists, administering a vitamin K infusion intravenously, and administering prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) or FFP. Thus, in our case with INR 2.0, the adopted strategy was acceptable [15]. Interestingly, a 2015 study by Goldstein et al, found PCC to be superior to FFP due to more rapid INR reversal, smaller infusion volume, and lower risk of volume overload, [17] which is a crucial concern in patients with advanced heart failure.

Nevertheless, the perioperative management of patients on continuous-flow LVAD support in elective and emergency surgical settings differs substantially and these differences must be acknowledged when interpreting decisions taken in the presented case. In elective scenarios, time allows for modification of anticoagulation treatment and optimizing hemodynamics. Emergency surgery, especially in out-of-hours nighttime coverage, is very different, requiring swift decision-making that prioritizes treatment of the life-threatening pathology while maintaining LVAD safety.

In an elective scenario, it is common to discontinue the administration of warfarin approximately 5 days before surgery to normalize INR and to introduce bridging anticoagulation once INR decreases below a defined threshold. A practical approach involves starting the patient on therapeutic LMWH, such as enoxaparin 1 mg/kg subcutaneously twice daily once INR reaches ~1.6 to 1.7. At this point therapeutic LMWH is discontinued at least 24 h before the planned procedure. If hemostasis is preserved, full anticoagulation can typically be recommenced around 48 h postoperatively using warfarin with therapeutic LMWH bridging, while earlier administration of standard venous thromboembolism prophylaxis (eg, enoxaparin 40 mg once daily) may be used according to institutional protocols. Bridging LMWH can be stopped once INR exceeds ~1.8, with continuation of warfarin. Reintroducing full oral anticoagulation is preferably completed in monitored in-patient conditions. Moreover, elective planning allows for optimization of medication used in advanced heart failure therapy (eg, temporarily withholding sacubitril/valsartan or ACE-I/ARB for ~24 h before surgery), with structured reintroduction after resumption of oral intake and stabilization of blood pressure.

In an emergency setting, however, usually there is no time to implement the stepwise strategy. The opportunity for gradual INR correction or bridging schedules is limited when patients present with dehydration and hemodynamic instability. Therefore, emergency management of LVAD patients should rely on: (1) immediate communication with the LVAD implantation center to establish device-appropriate targets and risk tolerance; (2) rapid anticoagulation adjustment performed with locally available methods; and (3) conscientious hemodynamic control tailored to LVAD physiology.

Regarding anticoagulation reversal, PCC is generally favored over FFP, as it rapidly delivers concentrated vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X), provides faster and more predictable INR correction, and requires lower infusion volumes, thus reducing the risk of fluid overload. However, emergency after-hours logistics may preclude the availability of PCC in some centers, whereas FFP can be accessible around the clock. Such circumstances render the use of FFP a resource- and time-driven compromise rather than a deviation from best practice, especially in a clinically dehydrated patient in whom volume expansion is not contraindicated. Importantly, in this setting the objective is not to completely reverse to normal INR; rather, the goal is to achieve a balance that permits safe surgery while minimizing the risk of LVAD thrombosis, an approach guided through real-time consultation with the LVAD implantation center.

Elective surgery allows for comprehensive, LVAD-specific operative planning. Recommended precautions involve invasive hemodynamic monitoring (arterial pressure monitoring and, when appropriate, central venous pressure assessment), because non-invasive methods of blood pressure monitoring may be unreliable in patients on continuous-flow support. Hemodynamic goals typically include avoiding hypovolemia given the preload dependence of the LVAD and targeting LVAD-appropriate ranges of blood pressure. Importantly, elective conditions enable ICD interrogation and programming before surgery to prevent inappropriate therapies during electrocautery, and verification of uninterrupted LVAD power supply (backup batteries and/or a stable main power source available in the operating theater).

An important issue that requires explanation is the absence of LVAD pump thrombosis or ischemic complications despite complete, albeit temporary, discontinuation of anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy in the postoperative period. The outcome might have resulted from multiple factors.

Firstly, the period wherein complete discontinuation of anticoagulation took place was brief and involved close monitoring. Although warfarin and clopidogrel were discontinued following the intraperitoneal bleeding, anticoagulation with LMWH was gradually resumed once hemodynamic stability and adequate surgical hemostasis had been achieved. This relatively short period of complete discontinuation might not have been sufficient to develop clinically significant pump thrombosis.

Secondly, the preserved LVAD flow and thorough perioperative hemodynamic management might have had a protective effect. Precautions were taken to avoid low-flow conditions, which predispose to pump thrombosis, including maintaining adequate preload and normovolemia and target MAP appropriate for the LVAD. Moreover, device-related factors may have influenced the final outcome. Because the HeartWare HVAD is a continuous-flow centrifugal device, maintaining stable pump parameters throughout the pump-off period might have provided persistent and favorable flow dynamics despite the absence of systemic anticoagulation.

Furthermore, patient-specific factors should be taken into consideration. Our patient was relatively young, with no prior history of pump thrombosis, and was managed in close collaboration with the LVAD implantation center, allowing for early detection of potential complications. All these factors combined may have prevented pump thrombosis, but this favorable outcome should not be perceived as evidence that discontinuing anticoagulation is innately safe in LVAD patients. The dynamic nature of thrombotic risk should be acknowledged because it is influenced by multiple factors, such as the duration of anticoagulant medication interruption, pump performance, hemodynamic conditions and the patient’s individual treatment regimen.

There are few currently available guidelines for perioperative management of LVAD patients requiring non-cardiac surgery, and they apply the same principles to all continuous-flow devices without discriminating between different LVADs [18]. Nonetheless, due to limited evidence, further research is required to claim with absolute certainty that applying these principles to continuous-flow devices other than the HVAD carries no additional risk.

Appropriate modification of anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy as a part of preoperative management for urgent surgery poses a significant clinical challenge for medical professionals. The role of surgeons and anesthesiologists involves identifying potential difficulties in implementing the necessary treatment to minimize the risk of complications. Due to the relatively small number of LVAD patients, there is insufficient evidence to develop definitive guidelines for anticoagulation management in patients requiring emergency surgery.

The presented case highlights the importance and necessity of multidisciplinary collaboration. General surgery specialists often face the issue of limited time to properly optimize anticoagulation and antiplatelet treatment, especially in cardiologic patients who have high risk of complications. LVAD recipients require an individualized approach and perioperative management in urgent settings. Thus, communication with a specialist from the LVAD center proved to be invaluable, owing to their experience in optimizing pharmacotherapy in this distinct group of patients. Therefore, it is vital to seek the expertise of other specialists. A rational, thoughtful, and above all individualized approach to LVAD patients requiring urgent surgical intervention is a key element of achieving favorable outcomes. Given the risk of complications and possible treatment failures, consulting specialized centers helps to ensure the highest standard of care and minimizes adverse effects associated with surgical treatment.

Based on our experience, several practical conclusions may be drawn from this case report regarding emergency non-cardiac surgery in LVAD recipients. Firstly, if an intra-abdominal hemorrhage occurs, immediate bleeding control should be prioritized over therapeutic anticoagulation because uncontrolled hemorrhage is an immediate life-threatening event, whereas the risk of pump thrombosis is more likely to increase with prolonged interruption. Provided that the LVAD function remains stable and anticoagulation interruption is kept as short as clinically feasible, anticoagulation and antiplatelet cessation is considered acceptable when required for hemostasis.

Secondly, throughout the whole period of anticoagulation interruption, minimal monitoring should include continuous clinical assessment and invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring. Regarding hemodynamic balance, hypovolemia should be avoided due to preload dependence of the LVAD and LVAD-appropriate perfusion targets (typically MAP ~70–90 mmHg) should be maintained. We also recommend early availability of bedside or operating-room echocardiography, providing appropriate measures for reviewing LVAD parameters in the event of hemodynamic instability or pump alarms, and ensuring uninterrupted power supply (backup batteries or stable main power source).

Thirdly, thresholds for reinitiation of anticoagulation therapy ought to be individualized and reassessed daily, but in our experience the prophylactic dose of LMWH may be resumed once hemodynamic stability and surgical hemostasis are achieved, followed by escalation of therapeutic anticoagulation conducted in a stepwise manner: bridging strategies used electively (eg, therapeutic LMWH once INR decreases below ~1.6–1.7 and discontinuation once INR >1.8) may be considered a helpful reference framework, even in emergency settings.

Finally, we explicitly accentuate conducting a preoperative device check/programming in patients with an ICD or a pacemaker. Additionally, bipolar electrosurgery should be used whenever possible; if monopolar cautery is unavoidable, it should be applied in short, intermittent bursts (in our practice less than 2 s per activation) with pauses between activations. This is consistent with perioperative CIED safety recommendations aimed at reducing inappropriate sensing or ICD therapy [19].

Limitations

Importantly, conclusions regarding the safety of anticoagulation interruption drawn from this single case cannot be generalized, as outcomes may vary based on device type, patient-specific factors, and the duration of anticoagulation interruption. Furthermore, publication bias toward favorable outcomes must be acknowledged.

Conclusions

The actual number of LVAD patients who will require emergency surgical treatment will probably remain unknown. To ensure the highest standard of care for these patients, a multidisciplinary approach to their management is imperative. Early consultation with the LVAD center is essential and general surgeons should be familiar with LVAD-specific physiological considerations. Anticoagulation must be individualized and dynamically titrated. It is also advisable to consult the specialized center overseeing the patient’s ongoing care and to implement a medication regimen appropriately tailored to their condition. While limited to a single experience, this report highlights practical management considerations and underscores areas of uncertainty that may inform clinical awareness and generate hypotheses for future investigation into the emergency surgical care of patients with LVADs.
