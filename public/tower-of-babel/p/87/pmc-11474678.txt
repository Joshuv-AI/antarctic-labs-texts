Targeted inhibition of the IL5 axis for immune checkpoint inhibitors eosinophilic-induced adverse events
Authors: Limor Rubin, Aviv Talmon, Yaarit Ribak, David Lavie, Hovav Nechushtan, Nadia Caplan, Michal Lotem, Oded Shamriz, Irit Adini, Yuval Tal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the broad implementation of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) for cancer therapy, we encounter a variety of immune-related adverse events (irAE) including immune-related blood eosinophilia. Eosinophilia demonstrated a potential positive predictive marker for a beneficial clinical response to ICI. However, there are reports of eosinophil-induced adverse events (Eo-irAE) with organ dysfunction requiring initiation of oral glucocorticoid therapy and discontinuation of ICI.We aim to assess the efficacy and safety of interleukin (IL) 5-axis inhibition in Eo-irAE secondary to ICI therapy.We present three cases of Eo-irAE referred to our allergy and clinical immunology unit at Hadassah Hebrew University Medical Center following therapy with pembrolizumab and nivolumab, monoclonal antibodies that target the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) receptor, for two cases of melanoma and one metastatic non-small cell lung carcinoma. Following informed consent and committee approval, two patients were treated with 1–3 doses of mepolizumab, 100 mg, monoclonal IgG1 kappa anti-IL-5 antibody, and one patient received up-to-date 9 doses of benralizumab, 30 mg, monoclonal IgG1 kappa antibody directed against the alpha chain of the interleukin-5 receptor, both administered subcutaneously. Patients were carefully followed and treatment response was assessed by physical examinations and laboratory tests.Hypereosinophilia at the level of 2300–8000 K/UL was observed 8–12 months following therapy accompanied by symptoms of dyspnea, arthralgia, myalgia, fasciitis, ‘morphea’-like lesions, fatigue, abdominal discomfort, pruritus, and chest pain. ICI discontinuation did not improve symptoms, two patients were resistant to glucocorticoids and therefore biological treatment was initiated to inhibit the IL5 axis. Patients demonstrated rapid clinical response and a decrease in peripheral blood eosinophil levels with long-term symptoms remission. There were no signals of negative impacts, such as tumor progression following IL5 axis inhibition.Eosinophilia secondary to ICI therapy can lead to organ dysfunction. Discontinuation of ICI might not be effective and symptoms may be refractory to steroid therapy hence targeted inhibition of the IL5 axis should be considered.

Clinical insight

We report the successful outcome of IL5 axis inhibition with both mepolizumab and benralizumab for eosinophil-induced adverse events secondary to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) in three oncological patients, two with malignant melanoma and one with non-small cell lung adenocarcinoma. These cases present a proof of concept for future implementation of anti-IL5 monoclonal antibodies for eosinophil-induced adverse events in oncological patients in which symptoms are refractory to steroid therapy and to the discontinuation of ICIs.

Introduction

Eosinophils, a subset of white blood cells, mature and differentiate in the bone marrow in response to cytokine and chemokine signals, predominantly interleukin 5 (IL-5) that plays a key role in eosinophil expansion, survival, and trafficking of eosinophils to different tissues including to the tumor microenvironment (TME).1 Eosinophils are well known for their role in the development of type 2 inflammation in allergic diseases, autoimmune disorders, and primary immunodeficiencies, and in combating helminth infections. However, there is scarce evidence regarding their precise role in cancer, and hence eosinophils’ protumorigenic and antitumorigenic properties are under investigation. Transcriptomic and proteomic analysis identifies the existence of different subsets of eosinophils whose properties are determined by cytokines and lymphocyte interaction within the TME.2 Peripheral absolute eosinophil count (AEC) varies in different eosinophilic disorders, patient with atopic disorders present with AEC of 0.2–1×109/L, hyper-eosinophilic syndrome (HES) diagnostic criteria require AEC >1.5×109/L, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) diagnostic criteria require an eosinophil count ≥1×109/L and the highest count ≥10×109/L may be observed in myeloproliferative disorders.3 4 Peripheral eosinophilia in patients with cancer was reported more than 100 years ago and the presence of tumor-infiltrating eosinophils and/or peripheral blood eosinophilia has been associated with both good and poor prognosis, resulting in controversy regarding their precise role in the TME.1 5 In addition, eosinophilia is frequently observed as a direct adverse effect of immunotherapy and has been described following therapy with IL2, IL4, granulocyte macrophage-colony stimulating factor, anti-PD-1, anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (PDL1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) antibodies, representing a potential predictive marker for a beneficial clinical response to immunotherapy.6 7 Nevertheless, eosinophilia secondary to immunotherapy is also associated with the development of non-severe toxicity.8 Moreover, eosinophil-induced adverse events (Eo-irAE) with organ dysfunction were reported in 57% of patients with eosinophilia secondary to ICI, 38.5% required ICI discontinuation due to Eo-irAE. However, high remission rates were obtained on corticosteroid initiation.9 In addition, increased AEC was associated with the severity of cutaneous ICI adverse events and with refractory response to corticosteroids.10

Previous studies demonstrated that cancer cells undergo immunoediting, including overexpression of inhibitory molecules such as PDL1 to evade immune surveillance. ICI therapy overcomes these immune escape mechanisms enabling a sustained proinflammatory anti-cancerous activity due to robust T cell activation in parallel to decreased suppression by regulatory T cells. Unfortunately, ICI therapy can result in the development of autoreactive T cell clones that either interact with B cells and result in autoreactive plasma cells and antibody-mediated immune responses such as myasthenia gravis or under different cytokine profiles differentiate to a different subclass of T helper cells resulting in a variety of irAE. Our proposed mechanism for Eo-irAE is an abundant release of eosinophil chemotaxis cytokines, mainly IL5, secondary to a Th2 autoreactive clone (figure 1).

(A)Two-phase activation of naive T cells. The first phase involves antigen presentation by MHC molecules on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), followed by a second phase, in which the costimulatory molecules interact: CD28 on the T cells binds to CD80/86 (B7-1, B7-2) on the APCs. (B) T cell regulation is mediated by immune checkpoint proteins. The binding of PD-1 molecules on T cells to PD-L1 on APC cells leads to the inhibition of intracellular signaling of both the TCR and the costimulation receptors. Activated T cells express the CTLA-4 molecule, which binds to CD80/86 on the APC, leading to its internalization and thus disrupting the costimulatory process. Conversely, the CTLA-4 molecule expressed on regulatory T cells binds to CD80/86 on APCs, leading to the activation of regulatory T cells and hence the suppression of activated T cells. (C) The use of antibodies against CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1 molecules removes the inhibitory signals, thus allowing for the sustained activation of T cells. These antibodies also bind to the same molecules expressed on regulatory T cells, resulting in a dual effect: a decrease in their suppressive function and targeting these cells to antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) by NK cells. The net effect of this treatment is a proinflammatory, anti-cancerous effect. (D) Unleashed autoreactive T cells cause checkpoint-induced immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Different cytokine profiles lead to a predominance of a specific t cell (Th1, Th2, Th17) in different irAEs. (E) The interaction of autoreactive T cells with B cells in the lymph nodes results in the activation of autoreactive B cells and the generation of autoreactive plasma cells that produce autoantibodies. (F) Checkpoint inhibitors can lead to hypereosinophilic syndromes, “Eosinophilic irAE,” most likely due to the release of cytokines from activated T helper 2 cells. Treatment with antibodies targeting IL-5 or the IL-5 receptor can lead to resolution of the hyper-eosinophilic syndromes and to enable the continuation of the culprit checkpoint inhibitor. (G-H): Cancer cells express inhibitory molecules (PD-L1) to inhibit T cells. Treatment with antibodies targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 opposing this inhibition and activates T cells against the cancerous cell. In concordance with hypereosinophilic disorders, Eo-irAE may result in multiorgan damage and may be resistant to ICI discontinuation and glucocorticoid therapy. Hence, in the present study, we aimed to demonstrate the efficacy and safety of monoclonal antibodies targeting the IL5 axis by antagonizing IL-5 and its receptor. The IL-5 receptor is a heterodimer comprising of one alpha subunit (IL-5Rα) and one beta subunit (IL-5Rβ) upon IL5 stimulation it signals through the Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription proteins (STATs) pathway and is selectively expressed on eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells.11 Currently, there are three agents targeting the IL5 axis: (1) Mepolizumab, a humanized IgG1κ monoclonal antibody targeting IL5 that is Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved for the treatment of severe eosinophilic asthma, HES, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis and EGPA12 (2) Reslizumab, a humanized IgG4κ antibody anti-IL5 with a FDA approval for severe eosinophilic asthma13 (3) Benralizumab, a humanized fucosylated IgG1κ anti-IL-5Rα antibody approved by the FDA for the treatment of severe eosinophilic asthma.12 Benralizumab, in comparison to other anti-IL-5 agents, has the ability to induce antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) with resultant eosinophil apoptosis and eosinophil tissue depletion14 15 in addition to IL5 signaling blockade. Up to date, there are abundant ongoing clinical trials that study the implementation of IL5 axis blockade in eosinophil-driven disorders such as eosinophilic esophagitis, Gleich syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation with eosinophilia, eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, bullous pemphigoid and others.16 Moreover, successful off-labeled administration of IL5 axis blockade was presented by our center for patients with drug reactions with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, eosinophilic cystitis, and others.17 18

Here, we report three cases referred during the years 2020–2023 to our Allergy and Clinical Immunology Unit at Hadassah Hebrew University Medical Center due to Eo-irAE following treatment with nivolumab and pembrolizumab, humanized monoclonal anti-PD1 antibodies that are extensively implemented in numerous malignancies.19 Following informed consent and committee approval of off-label drug administration, two patients were treated with 1–3 doses of the anti-IL-5 antibody, mepolizumab, 100 mg administered subcutaneously and one patient received benralizumab, 30 mg administered subcutaneously. The patients were carefully followed and treatment response was assessed by physical examinations, blood tests, and long-term follow-up of 1–3 years. IL5 blockade treatment duration was personalized according to recurrence of Eo-irAE symptoms and the decision for ICI rechallenge was assessed according to oncological treatment protocol and clinical response.

Case reports

Patient 1

An adult patient in their 50s was diagnosed in December 2019 with nail bed malignant melanoma, her medical record consisted of atopic dermatitis treated with ultraviolet B light therapy and topical steroids, inactive in the past 5 years with baseline absolute eosinophil count (AEC) of 400–600 cells/µL. Two months following diagnosis, she underwent an amputation of her right distal phalanx with axillary lymph node dissection positive for two small (0.3 mm) subcapsular metastases. In March 2020, adjuvant therapy was initiated with pembrolizumab, 400 mg every 28 days, with good clinical response and no evidence of disease in a positron emission CT (PET-CT) performed 3 months following treatment. However, the patient developed a progressive peripheral eosinophilia with peak AEC levels of 7000 cells/µL 8 months following ICI initiation (figure 2A). In parallel to eosinophilia, the patient had exertional dyspnea improving on inhaled corticosteroid therapy despite unremarkable pulmonary function tests and transthoracic echo. In addition, she progressively developed extreme fatigue, non-pitting edema of upper limbs, hyperpigmented rash on abdomen and breast, proximal myalgia with polymyalgia rheumatica-like symptoms, and elevated C reactive protein levels of 9 (normal range 0–0.5) mg/dL. There was no laboratory evidence of rhabdomyolysis, troponin, and CPK levels, thyroid, kidney, liver, and adrenal function were within normal limits, and electromyography did not demonstrate muscle pathology. At this point, the patient underwent a profound HES workup that included a blood smear, parasite stool cultures, immunological serology, protein electrophoresis, whole-body CT scan, and bone marrow aspiration with molecular testing for the biomarkers breakpoint cluster region-Abelson murine leukemia fusion transcript, JAK2, and factor interacting with PAP/platelet-derived growth factor receptor α fusion transcript (FILP1-PDGFRA) that all turned out negative. The patient did not improve upon ICI discontinuation and a 7-day course of non-steroidal agents, naproxen 500 mg two times per day. Therefore, 4 months later, prednisone 40 mg was administered (0.6 mg/kg) with partial clinical improvement, unfortunately, while tapering down steroids to 20 mg she displayed a clinical flare with re-elevation of inflammatory markers and AEC. Following informed consent and institutional review board (IRB) approval of off-label drug administration, the patient was treated with one dose of, mepolizumab 100 mg, administered subcutaneously. A week following treatment, muscle pain and range of motion improved, edema decreased, and the patient reported better general well-being. AEC declined to 200 cells/µL (figure 2A). There was no relapse of symptoms following a single dose of mepolizumab. Since pembrolizumab was administered as adjuvant therapy and there was no evidence of the oncological disease, treatment was not renewed. Outpatient clinic follow-up for 2 years following treatment was unremarkable with no clinical symptoms, CT was performed with no evidence of malignancy and the patient demonstrated a persistent level of AEC 230 cells/µL.

Patient 2

An adult patient in their 70s, a former smoker, was diagnosed with non-small cell adenocarcinoma of lung origin. PET-CT demonstrated mediastinal, lung, and peritoneal masses, and MRI detected 11 brain metastases. Molecular analysis results decreased expression of PDL <1%, negative analysis for p53, and a serine mutation in KRAS G12S. Therapy consisted of chemotherapy, carboplatin, gemcitabine and pemetrexed, irinotecan, radiation, and bevacizumab, followed by administration of pembrolizumab therapy at 200 mg every 3 weeks, with good clinical response and no evidence of brain metastases on sequential MRI. The patient had a baseline AEC of 200–450 cells/µL. Unfortunately, 2 months following ICI therapy, the patient developed peripheral eosinophilia of AEC 8000 cells/µL, (figure 2B) followed by extreme fatigue, anorexia, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea, and pruritus unresponsive to topical therapy with corticosteroids. HES workup including parasite stool culture was unremarkable. Following informed consent mepolizumab 100 mg was administered subcutaneously with rapid clinical improvement, manifesting in complete resolution of pruritus and abdominal symptoms. Additional doses of mepolizumab were administered only during relapse of symptoms in concordance with eosinophil incline. Hence 3 months following the first dose a second dose was administered, and a month later, a preventive third dose was administered as the patient continued treatment with pembrolizumab. However, there were no additional relapses. Unfortunately, a year from ICI initiation, new evidence of brain metastases and pulmonary progression was detected, and therefore, pembrolizumab was discontinued and the patient underwent lung resection and head radiotherapy. A 2-year follow-up was negative for eosinophil-related symptoms, and laboratory tests demonstrated a stable AEC level of 350 cells/µL.

Patient 3

An adult patient in their 70s was diagnosed in 2017 with melanoma in the right forearm and underwent surgical removal. PETCT in August 2019 demonstrated an oligo-metastatic disease with multiple small lung nodules, the largest up to 8 mm. In November 2019, nivolumab treatment was initiated, and the patient received 11 doses overall. A year following ICI initiation, the patient developed morphea-like skin lesions on the abdomen and lower limbs corresponding with a peak AEC levels of 2380 cells/µL (figure 2C) from a baseline AEC of 100–200 cells/µL. Corticosteroids were administered, with a maximal dose of 80 mg prednisone (0.8 mg/kg) followed by methotrexate 12.5 mg once a week. Despite the decrease in peripheral eosinophil count and ICI withdrawal, there was no clinical improvement observed. In October 2022, due to deterioration in the painful infiltrative erythematous rash and edema of the lower limbs (figure 2D) a skin biopsy was undertaken. Pathology results displayed marked thickened dermal collagen, absent adnexal structures, and septa fibrous thickening in the subcutaneous fat; findings consistent with scleroderma. The patient was referred to our immunological clinic and our clinical impression was eosinophilic fasciitis (EF). An MRI was performed and confirmed the diagnosis (figure 2F,G). Since the patient’s AEC was within normal limits, we chose to administer an anti-IL5 receptor antibody aiming to deplete tissue infiltrating eosinophils. Following informed consent, benralizumab 30 mg was administered subcutaneously with rapid clinic improvement (figure 2E). The patient has received a total of nine doses of benralizumab to date and continues to receive therapy every 4 weeks, as increasing the interval between injections still results in a recurrence of clinical symptoms. One-year follow-up, the patient is with ongoing benralizumab therapy with complete remission of clinical symptoms, normal levels of AEC, and stable oncological disease on PETCT.

Discussion

Recognition of eosinophilia secondary to ICI therapy is important for diagnosis of Eo-irAE which can lead to severe organ dysfunction and negative effect on quality of life. Current guidelines recommend ICI therapy suspension and corticosteroid initiation for grade 2–4 toxicities.20 However, discontinuation of ICI might not be effective, and patients may become steroid-dependent as presented in patient 1. Moreover, some cases, as presented in patient 3, may be refractory to steroid and immunomodulatory therapy, and for others, as presented in case 2, ICI rechallenge might be mandatory for malignancy control. Considering FDA-approved treatment with mepolizumab, an anti-IL5 humanized IgG1κ antibody for eosinophil-driven diseases such as severe eosinophilic asthma, HES, and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis12 and our clinical experience of efficient off-label implementation of IL5 modulating therapy for rare eosinophil-driven disease17 18 we administered mepolizumab for the first two cases. The assumption was that IL5 plays a crucial role in the development of Eo-irAE. Lazzari et al were the first to publish the successful use of mepolizumab 100 mg, subcutaneously administrated in 1–2 doses, for the therapy of immune checkpoint-induced HES,21 and therefore, the present report further supports this novel approach. The third case was unique as EF secondary to ICI is a rare side effect. EF should be suspected in patients presenting with cutaneous sclerosis. On clinical suspicion of EF, an MRI is a useful diagnostic tool that provides guidance for deep skin biopsy and serves as a marker for disease activity and response to treatment.22 Only 15 cases of EF secondary to immunotherapy were reported in the literature, most requiring ICI discontinuation and in similarity to our patient, several cases showed resistance to GC and methotrexate therapy.23 To date, there is only one reported case of Eo-irAE EF which was successfully treated with an anti-IL5R antibody, benralizumab in addition to intravenous immunoglobulin.24 This is the second case, and the first to assess benralizumab sole effect in EF secondary to ICI.

Biological therapies, targeting the IL-5 axis, are advantageous due to their steroid-sparing effect and their good safety profile. Accordingly, these biological agents should be taken into consideration even as first-line therapy for Eo-irAE to prevent corticosteroid toxicity, as presented in case 2. Moreover, IL-5 targeting therapy could be administered concomitantly with ICI for patients with Eo-irAE that require ICI rechallenge.25 Considering the importance of eosinophils’ antitumorigenic role in the TME, a concern is raised regarding the implementation of eosinophil depletion therapy in oncological patients. Hence, in the first two patients, mepolizumab was chosen over benralizumab, an IgG1 kappa a fucosylated humanized anti-IL-5Rα antibody that in addition to IL5 signaling blocking induces ADCC which amplifies eosinophil apoptosis.16 Moreover, eosinophil biology is complex, different clinical trials demonstrated the presence of tissue eosinophilia despite low circulatory eosinophil level26 and TME factors may promote local eosinophilopoiesis in an IL5-independent pathway. Patient 3 demonstrated a severe tissue eosinophilic inflammation with low circulatory eosinophil count and hence benralizumab was chosen with successful clinical results. Despite eosinophilic depletion, all patients demonstrated a stable oncological status. In concordance, long-term follow-up on recipients of eosinophil-depleting therapies did not demonstrate an alarming sign of increased prevalence of neoplasia.27

In conclusion, we report the successful outcome of IL-5 axis inhibition using both mepolizumab and benralizumab for Eo-irAE. We propose the implementation of IL5 axis inhibition by biological agents for Eo-irAE cases in which the discontinuation of ICI might not be satisfactory and symptoms refractory to steroid therapy. This study is limited due to the small sample size and the diversity among the patients, however, it is a proof of concept. Future studies with a larger cohort of patients are required to study the efficacy and safety of IL5-modulating therapy in oncological patients characterized by Eo-irAE.
