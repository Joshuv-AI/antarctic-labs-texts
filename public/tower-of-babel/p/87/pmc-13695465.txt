Exploring the efficacy and safety of perampanel in epilepsia partialis continua: A case series
Authors: Setareh Lahsaee, Kristin M Ikeda, Stephanie Woodroffe, Benjamin P Whatley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpilepsia partialis continua (EPC) is a form of focal motor status epilepticus (SE), which is commonly drug‐resistant requiring treatment with multiple antiseizure medications (ASM). There are no established guidelines for pharmacological management. Perampanel (PER), an AMPA receptor antagonist, has gained attention in treating SE based on limited case reports. This study evaluates the efficacy of PER in treating EPC.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the treatment response and adverse effects of PER in patients with EPC at our local centre between January 2024 and March 2025. Eight patients with EPC were included. The loading dose of PER ranged from 6 mg to 20 mg. A clinical response was defined as seizure freedom within 72 h of initiating PER, with PER being the last ASM added.ResultsPER resulted in seizure termination in eight out of eight patients. Time to response ranged from less than 24 h to 72 h. Oral PER, up to a dose of 16 mg, was well‐tolerated in conscious patients. Common side effects included sedation, delirium, agitation, and nausea.ConclusionsOur real‐world data suggest that PER is an effective and well‐tolerated early adjunct therapy in EPC, particularly at higher oral loading doses in conscious patients.Keywords: case series, epilepsia partialis continua, focal motor status epilepticus, perampanel, safety

INTRODUCTION

The earliest description of epilepsia partialis continua (EPC) dates to 1894, when Kozhevnikov reported on four patients presenting with continuous myoclonic jerks. 1 The recognition of EPC as a distinct entity relied on three features: origin in the motor cortex, lack of spread, and non–self‐limiting course. 2 Although the ILAE has broadly classified EPC as a subtype of focal motor status epilepticus (SE), 3 there is a lack of a universally accepted refined definition, and the conceptualization of EPC has evolved over time. EPC has been defined based on either semiology, namely a seizure lasting for a minimum of 1 h with muscle twitches recurring at intervals of less than 10 s, 4 or electrophysiological evidence of epileptiform activity originating in the motor cortex temporally coupled to muscle jerks. 5

EPC has various etiologies, including structural brain abnormalities, infections, metabolic, inflammatory, and genetic disorders. Although the prognosis is variable and depends on the underlying etiology, EPC is often refractory to first‐line antiseizure medications (ASMs), with seizure durations ranging from hours to years. 6 Internalization of inhibitory postsynaptic gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors and upregulation of excitatory receptors, including N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) and alpha‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA), may play a role in treatment resistance in EPC. 7

Perampanel (PER) is a selective, noncompetitive AMPA receptor antagonist currently approved as adjunctive therapy for focal‐onset and primary generalized epilepsy in patients aged 12 years and older. Its unique mechanism of action has prompted investigation into its potential as a therapeutic option for SE. A systematic review of patients with various SE subtypes treated with PER reported response rates ranging from 17% in larger cohorts to 100% in smaller case series. 8 This variability may reflect the heterogeneity of patient populations, both in terms of SE subtype and underlying etiology. Notably, some authors have reported higher responsiveness to PER in focal motor SE compared with other non‐convulsive SE. 9 The preferential effectiveness of PER in terminating focal motor seizures was also recently reported in a pediatric case series. 10

Currently, no established guidelines exist for the optimal treatment of EPC. Patients with EPC often require multiple ASMs and frequently experience side effects related to polypharmacy. Aside from a few case reports, no observational studies have specifically evaluated the efficacy of PER in EPC. In this study, we report real‐world outcomes regarding the efficacy and safety of PER in patients with EPC treated at our institution.

METHODS

This is a retrospective, single‐center, real‐world study of adult patients with EPC who were treated with PER during their hospital stay. Patients admitted to or evaluated by the neurology consultation service at the QEII Health Sciences Centre between January 2024 and March 2025 were identified through regular review of admission and consultation lists, searching for those with suspected EPC and new use of perampanel. Discharge summaries were then reviewed, identifying 10 patients treated with PER for suspected EPC. Two were excluded; one due to unclear etiology of jerk‐like movements and one with a final diagnosis of focal SE with visual aura. The remaining eight patients met inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

In this study, we defined EPC clinically, based on its semiology and duration as a motor seizure causing continuous or quasi‐continuous repeated muscle twitches lasting at least 1 h. We allowed inclusion of patients with both preserved consciousness or those with minimally fluctuating consciousness (drowsy and still rousable and answering simple questions). This definition aligns with that proposed by Maméniškienė and Wolf (2017), excluding pure sensory seizures. 6

All patients were treated with PER during their clinical course. Decisions regarding the type or order of ASMs and the initial dose of PER were at the discretion of the treating neurologist. The loading dose of PER ranged from 6 mg to 20 mg. Routine 30‐min scalp EEG was performed selectively in 5 patients. The decision to perform EEG was at the discretion of the treating physician and was generally limited to cases where it was expected to influence management (e.g., to exclude seizure mimics). Continuous EEG was not performed in any patient. Follow‐up EEG was not performed after the resolution of the clinical syndrome of EPC.

Due to the retrospective design and the lack of EEG data following PER initiation, treatment response was assessed based on clinical seizure resolution. A clinical response was defined as cessation of motor twitches/jerks within 72 h of initiating PER, with PER being the last ASM added. Although some case series have used longer definitions of treatment response, up to 4 days, 9 we adopted the 72‐h definition as it aligns most consistently with the majority of previous reports. 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 When available, concurrent medical therapies that could influence seizure termination, such as steroids or antibiotics, were also documented. This research was reviewed by the Research Ethics Board of the Nova Scotia Health Authority and was granted an REB review exemption.

All patients had imaging abnormalities located either in close proximity to or directly involving the central sulcus. Baseline EEG prior to PER initiation was available in five patients and showed epileptiform abnormalities—either discharges or recorded seizures—that were concordant with the location of injury identified on neuroimaging (Table S1, Figure S1). Seizure and admission outcomes are detailed in Table 2. PER induced seizure cessation in eight of eight patients, with time to response ranging from less than 24 h to 72 h. Notably, no loss of efficacy was observed in patients with prolonged treatment delays. Oral PER doses up to 16 mg were well‐tolerated among conscious patients, with common adverse effects, including sedation, agitation, and nausea.

One patient (Participant #6) was discharged seizure‐free after a 12 mg loading dose of PER but experienced recurrence of EPC 3 days later, coinciding with multiple episodes of emesis due to a large glioblastoma. This recurrence was likely related to missed doses of ASMs, including PER; therefore, he was classified as a responder based on the initial episode of seizure termination. The second episode of EPC was successfully treated in the intensive care unit (ICU) with administration of his prior ASMs, propofol, and a 20 mg loading dose of PER.

All patients except three were discharged home. One patient (Participant #1) died from respiratory complications secondary to vasculitis 5 months after admission. Two patients transitioned to palliative care: one (Participant #5) with a subdural hematoma and suspected aspiration pneumonia 1 week after seizure termination, and another (Participant #8) with an anterior circulation stroke causing upper airway obstruction and paradoxical epiglottis 1 month after seizure termination.

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first case series to report on the efficacy of PER in EPC. In our cohort of eight patients, all obtained EPC termination within 72 h of PER initiation, and seven of eight had enduring seizure termination following the first loading dose. Although the exact time from PER administration to seizure termination was not recorded for all patients, termination as early as 12 h was observed. Our overall response rate is notably higher than expected based on prior reports of PER efficacy in SE case series. 8 , 11 , 12

Our findings are in keeping with similar case reports of PER in EPC. Two separate case reports have reported successful long‐term termination of EPC in two pediatric 13 and one adult patient. 14 Another larger case series of PER in refractory SE reported an overall response rate of 36.4%; however, two patients with focal motor seizures in that cohort had a 100% response rate. 9 The possibility of PER being more efficacious specifically for EPC is a plausible hypothesis which is difficult to test clinically given the natural heterogeneity of data and assorted pathological causes of EPC.

One possible explanation for the variability in reported efficacy across studies is the differing definitions of treatment response. Some studies defined response as seizure termination within 4 days, 9 while others applied a narrower time window of 72 h. 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 Perhaps unsurprisingly, studies with more liberal, extended time windows report higher rates of seizure cessation. This suggests that PER may still result in seizure termination beyond the 72‐h window in some patients.

A second source of variability stems from variable dosing of PER. There is a lack of certainty regarding the optimal dose of PER in SE. The recommended standard maintenance dose of perampanel is 8 to 12 mg once daily and loading doses of 16 to 32 mg have been used in SE. 12 In our study, conscious patients with EPC tolerated oral doses of up to 16 mg per day. There are also reports of oral doses as high as 32 mg being well‐tolerated, without adverse cardiorespiratory events. 15 Using higher loading doses than standard regimens may be supported by the pharmacokinetics of PER. PER has an average half‐life of 105 h, and steady‐state levels are typically achieved within 3 weeks. Therefore, insufficient high loading doses can be expected to lead to a delayed onset of therapeutic response. 16

No single ASM or combination has been consistently proven superior in terminating EPC. Current practice is variable but generally follows an escalation approach similar to SE treatment algorithm guidelines. A recent systematic review of 51 patients reported pharmacological management strategies for EPC and highlighted that benzodiazepines were commonly used as first‐line therapy but often failed to fully control seizures. Additional ASMs, including fosphenytoin, clobazam, valproate, levetiracetam, topiramate, and lacosamide, were subsequently used with variable success, achieving seizure control in 23% to 60% of patients across different case series. 17

PER has rarely been used as a first‐ or second‐line agent in the treatment of SE, including EPC, largely due to the lack of an intravenous formulation and uncertainty regarding its efficacy. Currently, an intravenous formulation of PER is available in Japan and has been evaluated in small, uncontrolled, open‐label studies, demonstrating a safety profile comparable to that of the oral formulation. 18

This study has several limitations. The narrow inclusion criteria for EPC resulted in a very small sample size, and the retrospective design limited our ability to control for confounding variables, such as the timing of treatment and concurrent medical therapies. Despite this, our cohort represents a relatively homogeneous patient population compared with other published, larger case series, as all individuals had the same clinical seizure subtype—EPC—and shared a common underlying etiology of structural brain lesions.

In all patients, perampanel was the last ASM added prior to clinical seizure termination, providing reasonable grounds to attribute the response to perampanel. Nevertheless, potential bias may arise from other factors, including changes in seizure threshold caused by concurrent medical therapies, prior ASMs, or steroid use in patients with brain tumors. This is particularly relevant in patients who received perampanel early in the disease course and achieved clinical seizure termination within a few days, as we cannot predict whether EPC would have become super‐refractory in the absence of perampanel. In addition, the absence of prolonged EEG monitoring limited our ability to accurately determine EPC resolution, as patients may have continued to experience subclinical seizures after their visible muscle twitches stopped.

We were unable to perform a meaningful comparison between early and late initiation of PER. No clear difference in efficacy was observed between patients who received PER shortly after seizure onset and those who were treated later. Nevertheless, given the critical role of prolonged AMPA receptor activation in the progression of SE, it is plausible that earlier administration of an AMPA antagonist may facilitate more rapid seizure termination. 19

CONCLUSION

Our findings suggest that PER, particularly when initiated with relatively higher loading doses, may be a safe and effective adjunctive treatment in adult patients with EPC secondary to structural brain lesions. Early seizure control in conscious patients may reduce the need for further therapeutic escalation, including ICU admission and intubation, which may be particularly relevant in resource‐limited settings. These observations should be interpreted with caution, as no causal relationship can be inferred. Further studies are needed to better define PER's efficacy and its role as a second‐ or third‐line treatment for EPC. Future prospective studies should include continuous or post‐treatment EEG monitoring to better quantify treatment response.
