Relationship Between ICU Delirium and Change in Quality of Life, Mood, and Cognition Over 12 Months in Survivors of Acute Respiratory Failure
Authors: Haley Ferguson, Adriano Sanjuan, Anthony J. Perkins, Maya Elias, Sophia Wang, Suneetha Naidu Mekala, Brendan Devine, Zachary Koehler, Rosalyn Chi, Sujuan Gao, Babar Khan, Sikandar Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Both acute respiratory failure (ARF) and ICU delirium are risk factors for postintensive care syndrome but whether quality of life (QoL) differs between delirium and subsyndromal delirium (SSD) over time is not well quantified.RESEARCH QUESTION:In ARF ICU survivors, are delirium and SSD associated with longitudinal changes in QoL, mood, and cognition up to 12 months postdischarge?STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS:In this secondary analysis, delirium (exposure variable) and SSD were assessed 2 times per day using the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale and Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU. QoL was the primary outcome; 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) Physical Component Summary, SF-36 Mental Component Summary (MCS), Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, and Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) scores were collected at discharge, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months. Mixed effects models were used with delirium group (delirium vs SSD vs no delirium), time, and an interaction term for group × time as independent variables.RESULTS:Of 456 ARF ICU survivors, 227 (50%) experienced delirium, 110 (24%) had SSD, and 121 (26%) had no delirium. The 3 groups did not significantly differ by pattern of change in SF-36 Physical Component Summary, SF-36 MCS, Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, or RBANS scores over time. Delirium survivors had significantly lower SF-36 MCS scores vs SSD (predicted mean difference, −2.81; 95% CI, −5.03 to −0.59; P = .013) and no delirium (predicted mean difference, −2.93; 95% CI, −5.17 to −0.69; P = .011). Delirium survivors had significantly lower RBANS z scores than individuals with SSD (predicted mean difference, −0.19; 95% CI, −0.35 to −0.03; P = .023) and no delirium (predicted mean difference, −0.20; 95% CI, −0.36 to −0.03; P = .019). All outcomes showed improvement over time in all 3 groups.INTERPRETATION:Delirium, rather than SSD, was associated with lower SF-36 MCS and RBANS scores up to 12 months. Further research is needed to identify ideal time points for interventions aimed at these postintensive care syndrome-related outcomes.CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATION:ClinicalTrials.gov; No.: NCT03053245; URL: www.clinicaltrials.gov

Acute respiratory failure (ARF) accounts for nearly 30% of ICU admissions in the United States annually, and delirium occurs in 50% to 80% of ICU adult patients receiving mechanical ventilation.1–4 Delirium is associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, higher health care costs, and new cognitive dysfunction after critical illness.5–8 ARF ICU survivors frequently develop new or worsening cognitive, physical, or mood impairments, collectively termed postintensive care syndrome (PICS),9 which could lead to severely reduced quality of life (QoL).10–13 Prior studies have reported conflicting results on the association between delirium and QoL in ICU survivors, attributable to their heterogeneity in methodology (timing and choice of assessments).14–16 As a result, the relationship between ICU delirium and longitudinal changes in QoL after ICU discharge remains an area of clinical and scientific uncertainty.

In a large cohort study (n = 448) assessing outcomes in US ICU survivors, delirium duration was not associated with impairments in mental health, functional status, or QoL at 3 months, but was associated with worse depression and mental health QoL by 12 months.15 By contrast, cross-sectional studies in European cohorts have found lower QoL scores in ICU delirium survivors than patients without delirium.17 Separately, in a study of surgical ICU survivors, delirium was not significantly associated with differences in QoL at 12 months.17 Prior studies that have assessed the relationship between delirium and QoL up to 24 months17–21 have not primarily enrolled ARF survivors, an ICU population at the highest risk for both delirium and low QoL.9,22 Finally, whether subsyndromal delirium (SSD), an encephalopathy that does not fulfill all diagnostic features of delirium, but increases risk of institutionalization, is associated with reduced QoL is not well understood.23

Therefore, we conducted this study to assess the relationships among ICU delirium, SSD, and changes in QoL, mood, and cognitive outcomes from ICU discharge to 12 months postdischarge in a cohort of ARF ICU survivors. We hypothesized that patients with delirium or SSD would experience persistently lower QoL at all time points compared with those without delirium. We also hypothesized that ARF ICU survivors with delirium or SSD would have more severe mood and cognitive symptoms at each time point.

Study Design and Methods

This is a secondary analysis of data obtained from the Mobile Critical Care Recovery Program (mCCRP) trial.12,13 ARF ICU survivors were enrolled in a 2-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial conducted from March 2017 to October 2022 (e-Appendix 1). The trial tested the efficacy of a multi-disciplinary collaborative care intervention delivered through a nurse care coordinator vs phone-based attention control for the primary outcome of QoL at 12 months after ICU discharge. There were no statistically significant effects of the intervention on the primary (QoL) or secondary (mood and cognition) outcomes of the trial.13 The study received ethical approval from the institutional review board at Indiana University (protocol No. 1605950074; ClinicalTrials.gov No.: NCT03053245). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards of the local Institutional Review Board and Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Study Population

The mCCRP trial enrolled 466 ICU survivors. We limited this secondary analysis to participants enrolled in the trial who were assessed for ICU delirium and had outcomes of interest available (n = 456).

Assessments:

The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) and Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU)24,25 were administered by trained research assistants or trained bedside nursing staff twice daily until ICU discharge (e-Appendix 1). Patients with an RASS score of −4 (no response to voice; movement only to physical stimuli) or −5 (no response to voice or physical stimuli) were characterized as comatose. Patients with an RASS score range of −3 to 4 were considered eligible for CAM-ICU assessments, consistent with the validation of CAM-ICU and methodology used in high-impact ICU delirium trials.2,4,26,27 Three groups were defined according to their delirium status. Patients with any instance of a CAM-ICU-positive assessment during the index ICU admission were categorized as the delirium group.4 Patients with no positive delirium screenings (ie, CAM-ICU negative with all 4 CAM-ICU features normal) throughout admission were categorized as the no delirium group. Patients who did not screen as CAM-ICU positive at any time during the hospitalization but had at least 1 abnormal feature on the CAM-ICU (features included altered/fluctuating mental status, inattention, altered level of consciousness, or disorganized thinking) were categorized as the SSD group.23

Outcomes:

The outcomes were assessed using the following validated tools collected at baseline (around hospital discharge) and 3, 6, and 12 months postdischarge.

Quality of Life:

The primary outcome of this secondary analysis was QoL. The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) was used to measure health-related QoL.28 The SF-36 covers 8 domains of health (physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health problems, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health). The responses are then combined into a Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS). These 2 scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores representing better QoL. For both PCS and MCS, a change in ≥ 2 points represents clinical significance.28

Mood:

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to screen for depressive symptoms and categorize their severity, whereas the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) was used to screen for anxiety and categorize its severity.29,30 The PHQ-9 consists of 9 questions (total score range, 0–27), with higher scores suggesting more severe depression. The GAD-7 has 7 questions (total score range, 0–21), with higher scores suggesting greater anxiety. For both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, a difference of 3 points is clinically relevant.29,30

Cognition:

We assessed cognition using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS).31 During the COVID-19 pandemic, RBANS assessments were modified to be delivered over the phone, thereby excluding visualization and drawing items in the RBANS. Therefore, a z score was calculated as the sum of 7 RBANS subscales completed over the phone (list learning, story memory, semantic fluency, digit span, list recall, list recognition, and story recall) and transformed by the sample mean and SD, without adjustment for age.

Other Data Collection

Clinical and demographic characteristics were collected including ICU severity of illness (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation [APACHE-II]) computed using variables from the first 24 hours of ICU admission, comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index), and pre-ICU cognitive and functional status as assessed by the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly, Katz Scale (activities of daily living), and Lawton Scale (instrumental activities of daily living) collected at baseline/hospital discharge.32–36

Statistical Analysis

For univariate comparisons among the 3 groups defined by delirium status (group), we used the analysis of variance for normally distributed data and the Kruskal-Wallis test for skewed data to compare continuous variables by delirium groups. The χ2 tests were used to test whether categorical variables differed by delirium group. Separate mixed effects models were used with longitudinal SF-36 PCS, SF-36 MCS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and RBANS z scores collected at baseline (index hospital discharge) and 3, 6, and 12 months postdischarge as the outcome measures. Because values for PHQ-9 (depression) and GAD-7 (anxiety) were highly skewed, we used log-transformed values in the models.

The first set of the mixed effects models included the delirium group, time, and an interaction term for delirium group × time as independent variables to compare the change in the outcome measures among the 3 groups. Covariates for the mixed effects models were selected to be clinical and demographic characteristics associated with PICS based on a systematic review of the literature6 that significantly differed by delirium groups in univariate analyses and randomization group and hospital site, which is the stratification variable for the parent trial. These variables were the following: severity of illness, ICU and hospital lengths of stay, mechanical ventilation type (invasive vs noninvasive), and discharge location. If the interaction was not significant, we reported results from a second set of models without the delirium × time interaction term. An unstructured variance-covariance matrix was used in the mixed effects models to account for correlated measurements within a patient. For models where the delirium main effect was significant, we constructed linear contrasts to estimate pairwise mean differences between delirium groups. The mixed effects models we used to examine these outcomes are robust to missing data under the missing at random assumption, and these models are the standard approach for modeling longitudinal outcomes with a modest amount of missing data.

To examine whether the observed differences on the outcomes of interest among the delirium groups were affected by severity of illness or intervention groups, we added interaction terms between delirium group and either APACHE-II or intervention status in additional mixed effects models. Because this is a secondary data analysis and among the first to examine the differences in longitudinal QoL measures among these 3 delirium groups, we used a 2-sided P value threshold of < .05 for all statistical tests and presented 95% CIs when presenting group differences, without adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Results

Of the 466 participants enrolled in the m-CCRP trial, 10 had outcomes assessments missing at all time points and were excluded from the current analysis. Among the remaining 456 participants, 13 did not provide outcomes assessments at baseline. In this cohort, 227 of 456 participants (49.8%) experienced ICU delirium, 110 (24.1%) had SSD, and 119 (25.97%) had no delirium. The demographics, comorbidities, pre-ICU cognitive and functional status, clinical characteristics, length of stay, discharge disposition, and randomization assignments of the cohort by delirium status are shown in Table 1.

SF-36 PCS Scores

In our mixed effects models (Table 2), after adjusting for hospital site, invasive mechanical ventilation, randomization to intervention arm, severity of illness, hospital and ICU lengths of stay, and discharge location, the groups did not differ by pattern of change in SF-36 PCS scores over time (group × time, P = .713). Across all 4 time points, mean SF-36 PCS scores did not differ significantly by delirium group (P = .074). In all 3 groups, SF-36 PCS scores significantly improved over time (P < .001). As shown in Figure 1 and Table 2, the largest magnitude of improvements in SF-36 PCS score occurred from baseline to 3 months, regardless of group, and were clinically significant (minimally important clinical change, ≥ 2 points).

SF-36 MCS Scores

The groups did not differ by pattern of change in SF-36 MCS scores over time (group × time, P = .965), as shown in Table 2. Across all 4 time points, there were significant differences in mean SF-36 MCS scores by delirium group (P = .011). Delirium survivors had significantly lower mean SF-36 MCS scores than SSD survivors (predicted mean difference, −2.81; 95% CI, −5.03 to −0.59; P = .013) and patients without delirium (predicted difference, −2.93; 95% CI, −5.17 to −0.69; P = .011). There were no significant differences between SSD survivors and patients without delirium (predicted difference, −0.12; 95% CI, −2.57 to 2.33; P = .924). In every group, SF-36 MCS scores significantly improved over time (P < .001). As shown in Figure 1 and Table 2, the largest magnitude of improvements in SF-36 MCS occurred from baseline to 3 months, regardless of delirium group, and were clinically significant (minimally important clinical change, ≥ 2 points).

Mood: Depression and Anxiety

The 3 groups did not differ by pattern of change in PHQ-9 scores over time (group × time, P = .667). There was no significant difference in mean PHQ-9 scores among the 3 delirium groups across all time points (P = .059). In all 3 groups (Fig 2, Table 2), PHQ-9 scores improved significantly over time (P < .001).

Similarly, all 3 groups did not differ by pattern of change in GAD-7 scores over time (group × time, P = .054). There was no significant difference in mean GAD-7 scores among the 3 delirium groups across all time points (P = .131). Regardless of the delirium group, GAD-7 scores improved significantly over time (P < .001). In Figure 2, we show mean GAD-7 scores over time by group.

Cognitive Performance

The groups did not differ by pattern of change in RBANS z scores over time (group × time, P = .645). Across all time points, there were significant differences in mean RBANS z scores by group (P = .022). Delirium survivors had significantly lower RBANS z scores than those with SSD (predicted difference, −0.19; 95% CI, −0.35 to −0.03; P = .023) or patients without delirium (predicted difference, −0.20; 95% CI, −0.36 to −0.03; P = .019). There were no significant differences in RBANS z scores between patients with SSD vs no delirium (predicted difference, −0.01; 95% CI, −0.19 to 0.17; P = .938). In all groups, RBANS z scores significantly improved over time (P < .001). Figure 3 shows mean RBANS z scores over time by delirium group.

Subgroup Analyses

The interaction terms for delirium group × APACHE-II were not significant for SF-36 PCS (P = .69), SF-36 MCS (P = .90), PHQ-9 (P = .44), GAD-7 (P = .66), or RBANS (P = .82), indicating the difference between delirium groups was similar across severity of illness. We show predicted mean differences (with 95% CI) between delirium groups by severity of illness (APACHE-II score ≥ 25 vs < 25) in e-Figure 1.

Similarly, the interaction terms for delirium group × intervention were not significant for SF-36 PCS (P = .78), SF-36 MCS (P = .78), PHQ-9 (P = .60), GAD-7 (P = .18), or RBANS (P = .86), indicating the difference between delirium groups was similar across intervention status. In e-Figure 2, we show predicted differences between delirium groups by intervention status.

Combining SSD With No Delirium

In e-Table 1, we compare outcomes for 2 groups: delirium vs no delirium/SSD. The 2 groups did not significantly differ by pattern of change in SF-36 PCS, SF-36 MCS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, or RBANS. Across all time points, patients with delirium differed on mean scores of SF-36 PCS (P = .023), SF-36 MCS (P = .003), PHQ-9 (P = .023), GAD-7 (P = .046), and RBANS (P = .006) from those without delirium. In both groups, scores significantly improved over time (P < .001).

Discussion

Our study provides 3 major insights in the recovery of ARF ICU survivors. First, although ARF ICU survivors in our study had severe QoL impairments from hospital discharge through 12 months, the pattern of change over time in QoL, depression, anxiety, and cognitive performance was similar between delirium groups. Second, in all 3 groups, there were significant improvements in scores across every outcome over the 12 months. Third, when comparing the delirium, SSD, and no delirium groups, patients with delirium had significantly lower SF-36 MCS and RBANS z scores than those with SSD or no delirium. In contrast, when SSD and no delirium were combined, delirium was significantly associated with lower QoL, worse depression and anxiety, and lower cognitive performance at every time point. Our results suggest patients with SSD experience a longitudinal recovery more similar to those without delirium, and that symptom recovery in PICS remains heterogenous. These findings highlight the clinical value of repeated longitudinal assessments postdischarge, and that ICU delirium survivors remain at risk for worse health outcomes during their first 12 months after critical illness.

Our study supplements the existing literature on longitudinal changes in QoL of ARF ICU survivors during the first 12 months after critical illness. We observed the largest magnitude of improvements in SF-36 PCS and SF-36 MCS scores from baseline to 3 months, regardless of delirium group. Based on these results, future clinical trials may need to focus their interventions during this time to augment recovery of ICU survivors. Consistent with systematic reviews and meta-analyses of studies in ICU delirium,6,7,37 we found that QoL improved within a year, but the absolute magnitude of the increase was small.7,37–39 Given the deleterious effects of ICU delirium on long-term QoL of ICU survivors as shown by our study, clinicians may consider delirium and mental health as modifiable risk factors while providing care for ICU patients and ICU survivors.

Although in prior studies ICU delirium survivors have had high rates of mood impairments,9,40–44 multiple systematic reviews have not shown delirium itself to be a risk factor.4,9 Our study adds nuance to these results. Delirium was significantly associated with lower SF-36 MCS scores, but not depression or anxiety, except in comparisons which combined SSD and no delirium groups. This may be due to the SF-36 MCS’s broader assessment of psychosocial well-being (including fatigue, social function, and stress) compared with the narrower focus of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. In studies that have shown strong correlation between SF-36 MCS and mood, depressive symptoms were more severe than in our study.45 Consistent with prior studies,8 we found delirium was significantly associated with lower RBANS 7-Item z scores, and the largest improvement in post-ICU cognition reflected by RBANS scores occurred over the first 6-month period.

Our use of validated assessments for PICS symptoms which were longitudinally collected over 12 months in a large ARF ICU survivor cohort is a strength of this study. Other strengths include our use of mixed effects models which account for within-patient correlations and are robust to missing or unbalanced data. To our knowledge, this analysis of change over time is among the first to compare delirium, SSD vs no delirium, and QoL in ARF ICU cohorts from the United States. Our results offer contrasts to some European cohorts where delirium was associated with worse mental health but not QoL.15,16,19 Furthermore, our study provides important new insights into how SSD survivors experience recovery more similar to those who never had delirium, rather than delirium survivors.

We acknowledge several limitations to our study. The use of a secondary analysis from an existing randomized controlled trial may have limited the generalizability of our findings. Our twice-daily CAM-ICU assessments may have missed transient episodes of delirium thereby underestimating true delirium incidence and potentially affecting group classification accuracy. We also observed this cohort’s depression and anxiety symptoms were mild, indicating that this cohort was not severely symptomatic. This suggests that more symptomatic patients may have been underrepresented. Furthermore, our study did not assess the relationship with delirium and posttraumatic stress disorder, which is also prevalent among ARF ICU survivors. The study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the stress of the pandemic may have negatively affected QoL. Our findings may not apply to the broader ICU survivor populations, particularly older adults, and those with more severe post-ICU symptoms who are seen at PICS recovery clinics. Finally, our study does not address whether delirium duration and delirium severity may also affect the outcomes of interest.

Interpretation

In this secondary analysis, we found delirium rather than SSD was associated with lower mental health QoL and lower cognitive performance up to 12 months postdischarge. Our findings reiterate the need to deliver interventions in the post-ICU setting to improve the long-term QoL outcomes among ARF ICU survivors. Further research is warranted to identify whether specific time points are critical for interventions aimed at improved outcomes.
