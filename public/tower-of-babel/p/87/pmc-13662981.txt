Pixel-registered multimodal synchrotron XRF and FTIR microscopies reveal salinity stress response mechanisms in pistachio
Authors: Jocelyn A Richardson, Georgia Drakakaki, Hannah McCurry, Shuxiao Zhang, Hoi-Ying N Holman, Samuel M Webb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalinity is a major abiotic stress that negatively affects nearly all plant species at all stages of growth. Drought and poor-quality irrigation cause high soil salinity and salt accumulation via evaporation, reducing crop productivity. Despite its critical importance, the spatial localization of salt ions and associated biochemical changes within plants experiencing high salinity remains largely unknown. In this study, we developed a multimodal imaging pipeline to understand the impact of salinity on the pistachio rootstock UCB-1 (Pistacia atlantica x Pistacia integerrima). We directly link biochemical fingerprints in stem tissue architecture with salt ion localization to provide insights into the strategies pistachio uses to tolerate salinity.ResultsWe observed that Pistacia spp. exposed to high salt conditions accumulated Ca, Si, Cl, Al and Mg as hotspots within the pith, compared to the control (of which only Ca and Al co-locate). In contrast, there was a decrease in K between the control and salinity treatment. Hotspots of amide I and II were present in the cortex and pith of the salinity treated sample. Additionally, the salinity treatment resulted in an increased abundance of pectin and carbohydrates within the pith compared to the control, and the abundance of esters/carboxylic acid was greater in the salinity treatment.ConclusionsWe determined that Cl and K, S and P, and biochemical components polysaccharide and pectin, esters and carboxylic acid, amide I and cellulose are the strongest drivers of salinity-treatment induced variability. In the cortex and phloem/xylem, a negative K-Ca correlation decreases in the salinity treatment. Several hotspots of elements and amide I (proteins) appear under salinity treatment, particularly in the cortex, suggesting an increase in the production of stress-related proteins (in response to high Cl) and/or structural proteins (i.e. Ca). Together, these results indicate that pistachio responds to salinity through ion compartmentalization coupled with a targeted biochemical adjustment, rather than a broadscale tissue-wide response. Overall, these novel, spatially resolved pixel-registered multimodal imaging data provide an enabling platform to understand the mechanisms of salinity tolerance in Pistacia spp and can be broadly applied to studying stress-related phenotype response in various plant tissues.Keywords: Pixel-registered multimodal imaging, Synchrotron XRF microscopy, FTIR spectromicroscopy, Pistacia spp., Salinity tolerance, Ion localization

Background

California’s central valley is a cornerstone of agricultural productivity in the United States. However, drought is a major threat to the region and has resulted in changes in soil composition and water salinity [1–3]. Due to their sessile lifestyle plants have developed multiple strategies to tolerate and adapt to environmental stresses. For example, pistachio (Pistacia spp.) is one of the most salt- and drought-tolerant tree crops grown in the Central Valley [4–6] and is an important part of the agro-economy [7]. The pistachio hybrid rootstock ‘UCB-1’ (P. atlantica x P. interegerrima) is considered the industry standard in the United States due to its heightened salinity and drought tolerance in comparison to other sensitive rootstocks [6, 8]. In contrast to annual model plants such as Arabidopsis, there is limited knowledge in salinity tolerance mechanisms in perennial woody plants. Additionally, pistachio is relatively tolerant compared to other perennial plants such as Poplar [9, 10]. Therefore, improving our understanding of pistachio salinity tolerance has broader implications for salinity research in perennial crops and trees. Given pistachio’s long lifespan, understanding the mechanisms of salinity tolerance in pistachio can be used to enhance the selection of drought and salinity-tolerant plant strains towards the establishment of long-term resilient agriculture.

Response to salinity stress is complex, involving both short- and long-term changes from a molecular to physiological level. It is largely comprised of responses to two stresses, (1) osmotic stress caused by salt ions reducing the water potential in the soil, and (2) ion toxicity caused by the salt ion’s disruption of metabolic processes and cell ion homeostasis [11–14]. Several phytohormones, including but not limited to Abscissic Acid (ABA), Salicylic Acid (SA) and ethylene, are known to respond to salinity stress [15–17]. For example, during salinity and osmotic stress, ABA stimulates cytosolic Ca2+ accumulation in guard cells, inhibiting proton pumps and K+ influx channels and promoting anion channels, which induces K+ efflux from guard cells, leading to decreased turgor pressure and stomatal closure [18, 19]. Further, in Arabidopsis roots exposed to high salinity, ABA has a slow osmotic stress-response which results in a decrease in Na-related gene expression despite high Na [20]. The salt overly sensitive (SOS) signaling pathway involves several Ca-dependent proteins, and salinity stress has also been shown to impact Na/K homeostasis in Arabidopsis [21]. Ultimately, osmoregulation in plants is tied to the sensitive nature of ion homeostasis, and external availability of common cations (Na+, Cl−, K+ and Ca2+) will likely have resulting impacts on the uptake, partitioning, and storage of these elements in plant tissues. It is essential to characterize the localization of salt ions in plant tissues exposed to high salinity conditions for several reasons: (1) Cl− can cause toxicity at high concentrations [22], and thus its site of localization can be linked to senescence; (2) K+ is a key regulator of plant response to osmotic stress, and plays a role in drought-stress tolerance in several plant species [23, 24], making it vital for tolerance to both drought and the initial phase of soil salinization; (3) Ca2+ is implicated in cell wall integrity such as higher pectin networks and lignin deposition, and thus may be involved in cell barrier differentiation, such as the deposition of lignin in the apoplastic barriers in response to salinity [25–27].

Recent research has demonstrated the importance of Na+ localization in salinity tolerance [28–30]. Confocal microscopy has shown that increased salinity tolerance in the pistachio rootstock species ‘UCB-1’ is associated with both vacuolar Na+ sequestration in the root cortex and increased suberin deposition at the apoplastic barriers, the exodermis and endodermis [31]. Although tissue specific localization of Na ions and vacuolar sequestration has previously been observed in other species such as Arabidopsis, tomatoes, and citrus, there has been no comprehensive spatial map of other salt ions such as Cl−, K+ and Ca2+ and their change in localization in response to salinity stress. There is limited available data of K+, Ca2+ and Cl− abundance (among other elements) in Pistacia spp [32], and these data come from bulk approaches on homogenized tissue, which ultimately lacks the tissue-specific spatial distribution required for understanding ion compartmentalization during salt stress.

Several imaging capabilities have been used to study nutrient and contaminant localization in plant tissues, including LA-ICP-MS [33, 34], SEM-EDX [35], PIXE [36] and synchrotron X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) [37–39]. Synchrotron XRF imaging is a technique capable of spatially resolved chemical mapping of elements [40], has precedence in plant biology [41], and has advantages over other elemental imaging techniques including increased sensitivity (down to ~ 1 ppm), ambient sample imaging environments, and minimal sample preparation requirements (i.e. no conductive coatings required that could cause interference). While XRF imaging is poised to address questions concerning the distribution of salt ions in pistachio tissues, changes in biomolecular components (such as cell wall composition) cannot be determined using XRF. Instead, Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectromicroscopy is a technique capable of mapping the distribution of complex biomolecular components in plants [42]. Ion uptake and management, as well as biomolecular changes are spatially organized at the tissue and cellular scales. Resolving how these chemical signatures are coordinated requires measuring both signals at the same scale, within the same tissue (and therefore in the same sample). Although serial sections could be measured with each respective technique (XRF and FTIR), the resulting ion and biochemical component maps can only be compared descriptively, and spatial offsets confound interpretations of how processes may be correlated at the cellular scale. Despite limited literature combining FTIR and XRF imaging on plant tissues, the combined approach has precedence in biological applications more broadly, particularly within animal and human tissues [43–45]. Here, our aim is to link biochemical changes of cellular structures (using synchrotron FTIR spectromicroscopy) with salt ion localization (using synchrotron XRF) on the same pistachio tissue cross-sections and spatially register the data at the pixel-level to provide insights into the salinity tolerance strategies in pistachio. Combined with a statistical comparative approach, this pixel-registered multimodal imaging enables us to identify specific response mechanisms of pistachio to salinity stress. This work presents a methodological framework to interrogate pixel-registered XRF-FTIR imaging in plants experiencing abiotic stress. The framework aims to establish both the imaging and analytical workflow, broadly applicable to plant tissue types and stress, while providing meaningful insights into pistachio salinity tolerance mechanisms that can be examined more thoroughly in future studies with increased biological replication.

Methods

Experiments and sample preparation

One year old seeded plants of pistachio rootstock ‘UCB-1’ were obtained from a commercial nursery and were grown under 25 C, 16:8 h light/dark conditions in a controlled environment facility until start of treatment. Humidity was not controlled or measured but was estimated to be 40% based on similar growth rooms of the facility. For the salinity treatment, 3 replicate plants of ‘UCB-1’ were watered once a week to saturation with 100mM NaCl made with deionized water for three weeks [31, 46–48]. Control plants were watered with deionized water at the same amount and frequency and for the same amount of time. Due to the difficulty in obtaining thin sections of live tissue from the heavily lignified tissue, we focused on the 5 cm stem region near the shoot apical meristem, which was embedded into 5% agarose (W/V in deionized water) and sectioned to 100 μm thickness using a vibratome (Leica VT1000 S Vibrating blade microtome, Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA). Because this study was focused mainly on method development, biological replicates measured in this study for control and treatment was 1, due to the time-intensive nature of image registration. The sections were placed onto glass microscope slides and dried. Two sections, one control and one from the salinity treated sample, were transferred from the glass slide onto a BaF window compatible with both XRF and FTIR for subsequent analyses. Although 100 μm sections are thicker than required for transmission FTIR (usually < 30 μm), raw FTIR spectra were inspected for band saturation prior to normalization; maximum absorbance remained < 0.8 for all bands in raw data and did not exhibit band flattening. This is likely due to the effective IR path length in plant stem cross-sections; the path length was reduced relative to the section thickness due to the presence of air-filled lumens surrounded by cell walls in our tissue sections.

Synchrotron fourier transform infra-red spectromicroscopy

Synchrotron FTIR spectromicroscopy measurements were performed at beamline 1.4.3 at the Advanced Lightsource (ALS), Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. The spot size of the IR beam is ~ 2–10 μm, based on the diffraction limit of light and the incident IR wavelength in the mid-IR range. The detector is an LN cooled MCT detector. Spectral resolution is approximately 8.0 cm−1. The BaF windows were mounted directly onto the beamline sample stage, and OMNIC 9.8 software (Thermo Fisher) was used to control the microscope. Maps were collected in transmission mode, with a 10 μm step size and ~ 2.28 s (4 scans per pixel). Spectra were collected over an IR range of 800-4,000 cm−1. All FTIR spectromicroscopy was completed prior to XRF imaging to ensure that signatures in FTIR were not altered by x-ray damage.

Synchrotron X-ray fluorescence microscopy

All XRF data were collected at beamline 14−3, a bending magnet beamline, at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource, SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, under standard SPEAR3 operating conditions (500 mA and 3GeV). Beamline 14−3 has a water-cooled double Si crystal monochromator which was set above the Ca K-edge at 4100 eV, in the phi = 0 orientation. A 7-element Hitachi Vortex Si drift detector, coupled to an Xspress3 and Zebra (Quantum Detectors) provide the detection hard- and software system at this beamline. This detection system is energy dispersive, allowing several elements to be imaged simultaneously across a sample (in this case Ca, K, Cl, S, P, Si, Al, Mg and Na). The beam is focused to a 5 μm spot size using SIGRAY axially symmetric optics. Samples were mounted onto reusable, custom 3D printed cassettes which clip into a larger multi-cassette mount and attach to a Newport VP-XL-25 stage via magnets. The stage is connected to a Newport XPS-D controller which permits fine-tuned motion at 10’s nm resolution. The sample stage, and detector, is within a He-purged chamber, to reduce x-ray attenuation in air, improving signal to noise. A high-performance optical microscope combined with a Lumencor light source and filter box together provide optical and fluorescence microscopy capabilities in situ at the beamline, which is a key aspect to our multimodal image registration workflows. Maps were collected at 4100 eV, with a 5 μm beam spot size, 10 μm step size and 50 ms dwell time.

Imaging data processing and alignment

Imaging data were processed in the MicroAnalysis Toolkit (v 3.0.11) [49]. Both XRF and FTIR imaging data were preprocessed and then aligned with the respective optical image from the modality prior to image registration. Briefly, XRF maps were fitted using PyMCA [50] to ensure each element map has no overlapping fluorescence signal from elements of similar energies. A filter was applied to all the elemental maps, which used a Gaussian distribution over an area of 5 × 5 pixels (with a standard deviation of 0.75). XRF maps are shown as heatmaps reflecting different counts, in which the intensity scales of Ca and K were normalized to the same total counts which is 10x Cl, S and P. Similarly, Cl, S and P were scaled 10x Al and Mg. This allows for direct elemental comparisons between the samples, while still being able to observe differences in distribution in each element. The optical images from the beamline were stitched using a vision tool that uses feature detection and merging to create a composite image, rather than stitching based on coordinates. The optical image was exported to the XRF file using a resize tool, which decreases the resolution of the optical image to that of the XRF, and uses coordinates in the file to align the images. Here, the loss of resolution of the optical image is acceptable, since the outline of the sample shape is the important marker for aligning XRF with FTIR images.

FTIR spectromicroscopy data were exported as ENVI format from Omnic 9.8 to be compatible with the MicroAnalysis Toolkit. This format allows the total FTIR signal map and corresponding individual FTIR spectra from every pixel to be opened and connected in the MicroAnalysis Toolkit using the multi-channel analysis tool. Spectra were corrected for atmospheric CO2 and water, and denoised using a Savitzky-Golay filter (window size of 9 and polynomial order of 3), chosen to reduce high-frequency noise while preserving the shape and position of bands in the fingerprinting region. A baseline rubberband method was applied, because the baseline across the acquired range is dominated by a smooth scattering offset well suited to a convex-hull correction, and was applied identically to every pixel and treatment so that any residual bias is systematic across the dataset. A mean normalization was applied at 1655 cm−1; while this can overlap with amide I, analyses were repeated with alternative normalization values (such as 2900 cm−1) which yielded the same results, confirming that observed spatial distributions are not an artefact of normalization. The corrected FTIR spectra were then used to bin wavenumbers corresponding to clear features in the spectra, as well as known wavenumbers that correspond to major plant tissue components (e.g. pectin, cellulose). The following wavenumbers were binned and designated as follows: 1030–1085 as polysaccharides and carbohydrate (C-O), 1090–1120 as cellulose and hemicellulose (C-O/C-O-C), 1140–1180 as polysaccharides and pectin (C-O-C), 1200–1260 as amide III and phospholipids (N-H, C-N, PO2−), 1300–1350 as cellulose and lignin (CH2/O-H), 1350–1390 as pectin and carbohydrates (CH/O-H), 1400–1425 as pectin (COO−), 1500–1560 as amide II (N-H and C-N), 1620–1680 as amide I (C = O), 1710–1760 as esters and carboxylic acids (C = O) and 2840–2950 lipids (aliphatic C-H) [51–54]. Once binned, the signal for each wavenumber at every pixel was mapped out based on intensity to create spatial distribution maps of chemical function groups. Intensity data across binned FTIR wavenumbers were normalized to the same scale to permit direct comparison of abundance of components across the samples. Subsequently, the brightfield image was aligned to the chemical maps using the same approach as the XRF image data. Given the overlapping nature of many FTIR bands in plant tissues, most assignments reflect the likely dominant contributors (such as polysaccharides, pectin and cellulose) and therefore components often appear more than once. We defined dominant contributors as those that display similar behavior between different maps and appear in multiple maps. Conversely, when the maps differ, this is attributed to the role of the secondary contributor.

Once pre-processed, the FTIR and XRF imaging data were pixel-registered using a manual alignment function. Manual alignment was chosen over automatic registration tools due to slight rotation in the sample between data collection from each mode, as well as differences in the data detection (at BL14-3, the detector is at 45° to the sample, while at BL1.4.3 the detector is 90° to the sample) which can create image distortions. In the manual image registration, up to 35 reference markers were placed on clear features of the optical image aligned with the XRF data, including the outline of each sample and internal features. Those same positions were marked on the brightfield image aligned with the FTIR maps. The FTIR chemical maps were then exported to the XRF data file, using the optical images from each as the master channel and markers for registrations. The step was performed several times using different numbers of markers and marker positions to obtain the optimal alignment with the least amount of shear. The RMSE was calculated to validate registration accuracy; the average RMSE for both alignments was 11 pixels. If removing clearly misaligned markers (due to shear) the RMSE decreases to 8 pixels. There are several methodological considerations that should be noted that can introduce uncertainty. First, while the step size was set to the same value for both XRF and FTIR imaging (10 μm), the size of the x-ray and IR beam differed (5 μm and 1–2 μm, respectively). Second, the image registration procedure carries a residual uncertainty reported above. Ultimately, this means that correlation of chemical imaging modalities on features smaller ~ 80 μm (8 pixel RMSE, each pixel ~ 10 μm), should be interpreted with caution (i.e. any features smaller than elemental hotspots Fig. 1). As a result, we focus on a descriptive interpretation of tissue-level architecture and chemical signals, rather than individual cells.

Imaging data analysis

Following multimodal spatial registration, relationships (covariance, inverse, or otherwise) between different datatypes, and across samples, can be analyzed in the MicroAnalysis Toolkit as well as by exporting the data into a column format for further analysis. In the MicroAnalysis Toolkit we performed a multifile weighted factor analysis (FA) to the control and salinity treated samples. The data channels from individual modalities were grouped together and auto-balanced by weighting the data channels by the overall sum of factor magnitudes within each data group. This process ensures that the inherently different intensity magnitudes of XRF versus FTIR data contribute comparably to the shared FA space. Using this method, the FA model was fit such that the two samples share a common loading (component) matrix while each sample has its own matrix score per loading. By applying a shared latent space to the samples, the FA matrix scores can be compared spatially between the control and treatment, enabling a visualization of how the treatment has impacted latent chemical or biological components. The FA generates as many components as data channels, in this case 20. Correlation plots between FA components, and between data channels were created in the MicroAnalysis Toolkit correlation plot function.

Several approaches were applied to determine the drivers of each component from the FA. The FA results and imaging data were exported from the MicroAnalysis Toolkit as column data to be statistically analyzed using Python 3.13.5 with NumPy (v2.3.2) and SciPy (v1.15.3) libraries, housed within a custom GUI developed using tkinter for interactive data exploration, with matplotlib (v3.10) for figure generation. Initially, variance ratios were calculated to identify the proportion of total variance explained by the control versus treatment in each FA component. The variance ratio is defined as the ratio of the individual component’s variance (i.e. variation of FA score across pixels) to the sum of variances across all components. Variance ratios > 1 indicate that the component had greater variability in the control compared to treatment; values < 1 therefore indicate that the component had less variability in the control compared to treatment. To determine the FA components that had the greatest effect on the control versus the salinity treated sample, Cohen’s d values were calculated for each component, which measures the difference in component scores between the samples. This was calculated using the mean component scores for a single component across datasets, normalized to the pooled standard deviation. Cohen’s d effect sizes are reported according to established nomenclature: |d| < 0.2 (negligible), 0.2 ≤ |d| < 0.5 (small), 0.5 ≤ |d| < 0.8 (medium), and |d| ≥ 0.8 (large). Positive d values indicate higher mean scores in the treated sample. Lastly, the relationships between channel (i.e. measured) data and the FA components were investigated using Pearson correlation coefficients (r) in NumPy. For each channel-component pair, the Pearson’s r was computed and represented as a heat map between − 1 and + 1, which indicates both the strength and directionality of the relationship. Through each of these tools, several important FA components were identified, and these were further analyzed using a vector biplot. Components were cross-plot and data channels (normalized to 1) were plotted as vectors within this x-y space. The directionality and strength of the data channel vectors correspond to relationships; for example, vectors of similar magnitude and direction are correlated with one another, whereas opposing vectors are negatively correlated. The magnitude and direction of vectors help to visualize the data that drives each FA component.

Elemental and FTIR abundance data were exported as column data from the MicroAnalysis Toolkit and imported into Excel. To visualize abundance data as violin plots, we used Python 3.13.5 with pandas, seaborn and matplotlib which imported the Excel format. Due to the wide range of XRF elemental abundance, XRF data were log-transformed. Violin plots also include internal box plots that show the median (white line), interquartile range (thick black line) and whiskers (thin black line). Since elemental abundances were collected simultaneously at the Ca K-edge, relative changes in abundance of lighter elements can be detected. However, the absolute abundance of elements from Mg to K is decreased compared to true abundance; this is because as the x-ray energy increases further from the specific excitation edge of an element, the probability of x-ray absorption decreases. Thus, the trending increase in elemental abundance from Mg to Ca reflects the energy of data collection, rather than true changes in elemental concentration. However, the spatial distribution of these lighter elements, and relative abundance between samples, can be compared. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed at the pixel-level on the XRF elemental abundance and FTIR wavenumber abundance data, to rank the magnitude of salinity-treatment variability, using the statsmodel library in python. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of 0.05, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test when results were significant. Effect sizes were calculated using eta-squared (η²) and interpreted according to Cohen’s conventions (small = 0.01, medium = 0.06, large = 0.14). These statistical results are reported as descriptive characterizations of the imaging data, rather than as population-level differences between biological replicates, given the proof-of-concept design of this study and the non-independence of pixels. These statistical analyses treat individual pixels as independent observations, which inflates the effective sample size relative to the biological sample number and is impacted by spatial autocorrelation between neighboring pixels. Therefore, the statistics are interpreted descriptively alongside visible observations in the imaging data to generate testable hypotheses for future studies of pistachio salinity tolerance mechanisms.

Results

Elemental abundance and spatial distribution

The spatial distribution of elemental abundance was higher in the cortex and phloem/xylem compared to the pith in both control and salinity treated samples (Fig. 1A and B). However, in the salinity-treated sample, there were isolated areas which we will refer to as “hotspots”, approximately 10–90 μm in diameter, of concentrated abundance of Mg, Al, Cl, Si and Ca in the pith which were absent in the control. Only hotspots of Mg and Ca were co-located, with some co-location with Al. In addition, there was an increase in Cl and P, and a decrease in K abundance, in the XRF maps of the treated sample when compared to the control.

Violin plots of the abundance data all indicate a bimodal distribution (Fig. 1C). The abundance was similar in Mg, Al, Si, P, S and Ca between the control and treatment, with only changes to the overall shape of the violin plots. One-way ANOVA results of Cl and K support the change in abundance between treatments, indicating an increase in Cl and a decrease in K with medium and small effects, respectively (p < 0.001, η² = 0.079 and η² = 0.059).

Biochemical component abundance and distribution

To understand the spatial distribution of elemental abundance and changes in the biochemical composition and structural architecture of plant tissues under salinity stress, we used FTIR spectroscopy. By measuring how molecular bonds absorb IR light, identification and quantification of macromolecules such as cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and lignin is possible [54]. The FTIR spectromicroscopy maps showed differences in abundance between the cortex and pith (Fig. 2A and B). The abundance of polysaccharides/carbohydrates (1030–1085 cm−1) and polysaccharides/pectin (1140–1180 cm−1) is greater in the control (Fig. 2A) than in the treated sample (Fig. 2B). The abundance of pectin (1400–1425 cm−1) increased in the salinity-treated sample compared to the control. Similarly, the abundance of pectin and carbohydrates (1350–1390 cm−1) was greater within the pith in the salinity-treated sample, and the abundance of esters/carboxylic acid (1710–1760 cm−1) was greater overall in the salinity-treated sample. Three different amide bands (I, II and III) were identified and mapped; the amide III (1200–1260 cm−1) spectrum is known to have contributions from phospholipids [54]. Amide III showed similar distributions in the two samples, but with an increase at the very edge of the cortex in the salt-treated sample. Amide I (1620–1680 cm−1) and II (1500–1560 cm−1) showed hotspots within the cortex and pith, which were not present in the control.

Several FTIR components showed statistical differences in abundance between the control and treatment including polysaccharides such as hemicellulose, cellulose, pectin, and non-polysaccharide cell wall components, such as lignin. Further, amide III and phospholipids (potentially skewed by high abundance on the sample exterior), and esters/carboxylic acid showed different abundances between samples. Amides I and II, as well as lipids have higher means in the treatment, but similar or longer tails in the control. Compared to XRF data, violin plots only displayed a bimodal distribution in the lipids in both samples.

Multimodal image analyses

Multimodal image overlays can reveal co-localizations between different imaging data types and have been widely used in previous studies [28–56]. In a comparison of Ca, Cl and amide I between the control and salinity treated sample (Fig. 3A and C), differences were observed within the pith corresponding to an increase in Cl. Although hotspots of amide I and II were present in the treatment only, much like hotspots in Ca, Cl, Si, Al and Mg, these hotspots did not co-locate in the image overlays. Ultimately, deriving clear relationships from image overlays of XRF and FTIR imaging data was challenging due to the different intensities of the imaging datatypes and because of the abundance of biomolecular components in plant tissues (Fig. 3). Ultimately, linking salt ion and biochemical signals at the pixel level requires moving beyond visual comparisons and toward statistical approaches that can delineate co-variation between imaging modalities.

To better understand the relationships between the elemental and biomolecular imaging data, we applied FA (Fig. 4), which decomposes the multimodal dataset into components representing patterns of pixel-wise co-variation. Unlike visual overlays, FA looks for underlying vectors that explain variance in the imaging data, permitting discovery of statistically distinct chemical and biological regions (Fig. 4B and C). The resulting FA components do not directly explain the measured data but instead identify regions of shared variance between data channels of the imaging modalities and reveal statistically distinct biochemical regions of a sample. The FA component maps for components 1 and 2 (Fig. 4D) were similar to the raw XRF signal of Ca, and the total FTIR signal, respectively. This was expected since FA first identifies the maximum variance in the initial components where the strongest common signal is present. Subsequent factor loading maps exhibited variability across different regions of the tissue architecture highlighting chemical variance in the cortex (component 4), and in some components appeared to be a combination of the data from FTIR and XRF (e.g. component 5; Fig. 4D).

We took several approaches to determine which data channels contributed to the FA component maps; first, a variance ratio comparing the treatment and control indicated that almost 50% of components (3, 4, 5, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 19, 20) had less variability in the control compared to the salinity treatment (Fig. 5A). Cohen’s d revealed that components 4 and 7 were moderately suppressed in the treatment, and only one component (component 8) was upregulated in the salinity-treated sample (Fig. 5B). This indicates that the treatment had targeted effects on pistachio, rather than a broadscale response (Fig. 5B). From these results, components 4, 7, and 8 were deemed important contributors to the differences observed in imaging data between the control and salinity treated sample (Fig. 5B). Component 9 was also retained for further analysis as it was the second greatest positive contributor in the salinity treated sample.

Having identified the components with the greatest effect on the samples, we then examined how the relationships within and between these components differ between the control and salinity treatment. Cross-plots of components 4 and 7, the two components with the greatest suppression in the salinity treatment, showed tighter clustering in the control, but separation in the salinity-treated sample (Fig. 5C). In components 8 and 9, cross-plots similarly showed clustering (Fig. 5D), with several outliers along both component axes, and in particular for component 8. While the treatment also showed outliers along both components 8 and 9, they were smaller in magnitude across both components. The cross-plots reveal how the pixel distributions of each component vary between the control and salinity treated sample, rather than pointing to a specific data channel that drives the variability in that component.

To determine the direction and magnitude of data channel correlations for each FA component we used Pearson correlations. Even though the two samples share factor space, the Pearson correlation between the data channels and the FA components may differ between the samples because (1) each sample has distinct variance and covariance structures in the imaging data and (2) channel–component relationships are calculated independently for each sample. As discussed above, the Pearson correlation showed a similarly strong correlation between the XRF data channels and component 1, and the FTIR data channels and component 2 across both samples (Fig. 6). In component 4, several XRF data channels had strong positive correlations within the control; in contrast these XRF channels correlated negatively with component 4 in the treatment, with emphasis on Ca which was more negatively correlated than all other elements. Polysaccharide and pectin, and lipids were the strongest positive correlation in component 7 within the control, yet these showed weak negative correlations in the salinity treatment. Potassium was positively correlated with component 8 in the control, which is also observed in the treatment. However, in the salinity-treated sample several other XRF channels have positive correlations with component 8 (such as Cl, S and P) which was not observed in the control. Lastly, in component 9 there were weak positive correlations from several XRF channels and weak negative correlations with the FTIR data channels in the control, the opposite was observed in the salinity treated sample.

Discussion

Compartmentalization as a response to salinity stress

Ion compartmentalization is a key mechanism of plant resilience and has been observed in several plant species in response to various stressors [31, 57–60]. Particularly, Na, Cl, K and Ca have been shown to compartmentalize within plant tissues under salinity stress using bulk techniques, where larger pieces of plant tissue are deconstructed/homogenized and measured using techniques such as extraction followed by photometry/spectrometry or ion chromatography [61, 62]. Visualizing salt ion compartmentalization within structural components of individual plant tissue types (in this case stem tissue) is novel, particularly in perennials, which may employ different tolerance strategies than short-lived annuals. A majority of salinity studies in plants explore NaCl as the salt stressor, for which the localization of salt ions can inform the mechanisms of plant response [63–66]. Localization of Na in plant tissues has been performed in the past using stains such as CoroNa Green (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), allowing for (sub)cellular resolution of detection using confocal microscopy [31, 67, 68]. Previous studies that use pistachio as a model of high salinity tolerance relied primarily on changes on biomass, leaf abscission, and ground whole tissue and sap ion content measurement using electrical conductivity of supernatants, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) mass spectrometry and ICP optical emission spectrometry [46–48]. To date however, there have not been any reports of spatial localization of Cl in plant tissues [69], nor has there been multimodal imaging to understand underpinning biochemical structures associated with the plants response. In addition, given that salinity stress tolerance requires both osmotic stress tolerance and ion toxicity tolerance [70, 71], the localization and accumulation of different ions at cellular level, such as vacuolar sequestration of excess sodium ions [31, 68, 72, 73] (Gonzalez et al., 2012, Guo et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2021, Shao et al., 2021) may play a key role in salinity tolerance. Furthermore, we propose the use of multimodal imaging across XRF and FTIR will allow correlational analysis of ion localization with oxidative stress parameters such as aldehydes and peroxides, and osmoprotectants such as proline glycine–betaine [74, 75]; this may yield novel insights regarding the cellular mechanism that can improve physiological response to salinity.

This study employing multimodal imaging followed by multivariate analysis identifies several components in pistachio stems that could be investigated as prominent in the spatial response to salinity. Component 4 is a key factor to explaining variability between the control and salinity treatments herein. Component 4 shows an antagonistic relationship between markers of osmoregulation and metabolism: K, amide III and phospholipids (positive), and markers of the cell wall: Ca, polysaccharides and carbohydrates (including both structural and non-structural carbohydrates; negative). This inverse relationship likely reflects the different physiological roles of K and Ca in plants; K is a mobile cation essential to osmoregulation, while Ca is generally less mobile in terms of transport across different plant tissues and it is important to cell wall structure [26, 76–78]. In the control sample, component 4 is abundant within the cortex, epidermis and phloem, reflecting the high K concentrations associated with water and nutrient transport. In contrast, component 4 abundance decreases in these tissues within the salinity treated sample (Fig. 4D), and instead hotspots of component 4 emerge within the pith. This means that the relationship between K, amide III and phospholipids and Ca, polysaccharides and carbohydrates is weaker in the salt-treated sample. The observed changes in these tissues is likely driven by the decrease in K abundance as shown by XRF, rather than Ca. The increase in hotspots within the pith is also correlated with K hotspots. This is consistent with salinity induced efflux of K, reported in many plant systems [79–81], though this is the first time a change in pith and cortical parenchyma localization under salinity treatment has been detected. However, the appearance of hotspots suggests that while there is an efflux of K, there is also a reallocation or spatial reorganization of K. Critically, our detailed multimodal analysis captures the tissue-specific response to salinity, demonstrating a shift from K homeostasis to conservation. The correlation of K with amide III and phospholipids is consistent with sequestration of K into structural compartments, which are actively being reinforced by the plants to create an osmotic reserve. Component 4 was significantly reduced in the salinity treated sample relative to the control (Cohen’s d = -0.7), consistent with the observed K depletion and shift from homeostasis to stress-related conservation.

In contrast to the K and Ca trade-off, component 8 is largely driven by Cl, followed by P, S and (hemi)cellulose. The salinity treatment resulted in an influx of Cl within the cortex and vascular bundles (Fig. 7B), which potentially indicates that the salt exclusion mechanism in the roots was saturated, leading to the entry of potentially toxic levels of Cl. This high abundance of Cl is correlated with biological components capable of managing Cl influx; for example, S for protein transporters, P for the energetic cost of ATPase that can help to pump Cl into different cells, and cellulose for structural support. However, further investigation into S and P chemistry within these samples is required to determine the specific source of these elements. Although possible with synchrotron XRF chemical speciation mapping, it is beyond the scope of the current study. As with K, these results suggest that the plant may be actively attempting to compartmentalize Cl and develop compensatory mechanisms. Component 8 was significantly higher in the salinity treated sample (Cohen’s d = -0.6), reflecting the increase in Cl abundance observed in XRF maps. Given the positive loadings of Cl and polysaccharides, we interpret component 8 to reflect Cl-associated cell-wall polysaccharide biochemical signatures. This may point to changes in cell-wall polysaccharide composition under salinity treatment. These changes point towards ion compartmentalization, in which salt tolerance may be aided by increasing ion binding in the cell wall, reducing ion movement and working with membrane transporters to sequester ions [82, 83]. Notably, component 4 and component 8 are driven in opposite directions in response to salinity; while component 4 decreases in the salinity treated sample, component 8 increases. This highlights a shift from K-associated protein and lipid signal in non-stressed conditions, to a Cl-S-P-associated polysaccharide signature of salinity stress.

While Ca and Si do not appear as strong positive contributors to any of the components with moderate effect sizes on the samples, the distribution in the XRF maps indicates their behavior is a part of the salinity stress response. This discrepancy highlights the complementarity of both statistical and visual analysis of data channels; FA is sensitive to high level variance, while XRF maps can reveal cellular level differences. A notable finding of the data is also that under salinity treatment, many hotspots of both Si and Ca were present within the pith. While both Si and Ca are known to play functional roles in structure within cells, such as cell walls [25, 26, 78, 84, 85], the hotspots are not always co-localized, indicating a differentiated strategy. For example, Si can protect plants from salt-stress [86, 87]. The latter may be what is occurring where Ca and Si are separated, and therefore performing individual roles, while at co-localized hotspots they form synergistic associations potentially for maximum structural reinforcement.

Biosynthesis of protective compounds

In the vascular bundle, the band near 1540 cm−1 (amide II; which includes N-H bending and C-N stretching) decreases in the control but increases under salinity treatment (Fig. 8) yet remains similar within the cortex and pith. Additionally, FTIR maps show hotspots of amide II within the salinity treatment. Previous work in brussel sprouts showed that the intensity of amide I and amide III was shown to increase with increasing external NaCl after 10 days of salinity treatment [88]. Amide II however was shown to decrease with increasing external NaCl in only 8 days of treatment, but by 10 days, the difference in amide II across different external NaCl concentrations was unchanging. This change in amide II was suggested to be related to the synthesis proline, energy and signaling related proteins [88]. Proline is an osmoprotectant, and helps with cell turgor during osmoregulation, in addition to being a signaling molecule, and is involved in metal chelation during plant stress [89]. Given that K decreases in all parts of the salinity treated tissues, and that K is important to osmoregulation, the production of compatible solutes, including proline among others, may offset this imbalance to maintain osmoregulation within the plant tissue. While our experiment only has one NaCl concentration and one time point, amide III was statistically significantly increased in the salinity treatment (Fig. 2C). Although amide I and II were not statistically different, they both had higher means in the salinity treatment, as well as changes in the distribution. This may indicate that increased salinity triggered a spatially heterogeneous protein response, with amide III changing more strongly than amide I and II at a single timepoint.

Using Raman Spectroscopy, an increase in anthocyanins and a decrease in carotenoids was observed 48 h after salinity stress in Coleus plant leaves [90]. Specific anthocyanins are known in pistachio, including cyanidin-3-galactoside and cyanidin-3-glucoside [91], and are typically found in the husk/kernel of the fruit and not in the woody tissue of vegetative organs. Certain carboxylic acids are known antioxidants [92] and are also found in pistachio shell/husk, such as caffeic acid [93]. While specific carboxylic acids cannot be identified in the FTIR spectra, the broad 1710–1760 cm−1 peak (Fig. 8) reflects esters and carboxylate groups that may include antioxidants. In the salinity treated sample, there is an increase in the abundance of carboxylic acids, particularly within the cortex (Figs. 2B and 8). Suberin groups can include carboxyl and ester groups [94], which was observed to increase in the salinity treated sample (Fig. 2B). Various other suberin groups observed include long aliphatic chains of suberin (2921, 2852 cm−1), carboxyl and ester groups (1737, 1242, and 1158 cm−1), phenolic compounds (1430 cm−1) and wax or suberin-like aliphatic components (1318 and 1372 cm−1) determined from the averaged tissue spectra (Fig. 8). Previously, suberization of Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) roots in response to heat and drought stress showed differences in suberization depending on root developmental stage [94]. Suberization of root cellular barriers in pistachio and almond rootstocks has been shown as a prominent response to abiotic stress [31, 68] and this response has also been seen in non-woody plant systems, such as barley and tomato [95–97]. Supported by these previous studies, we observe similar responses in stem tissue, where suberin groups were increased in the salinity treated sample within the cortex compared to the pith or vascular bundles, consistent with barrier formation.

Conclusions

Resolving how plant salt-ion management and biomolecular chemistry are related in response to environmental stress is essential to understanding tolerance (or otherwise) mechanisms across plant genotypes, however individual imaging modalities address only one aspect. Pixel-registered multimodal imaging brings both ionomics and biochemistry into the same co-ordinate frame which enables more mechanistic investigations which a single imaging mode cannot fulfil. The methodological advances in this study include pixel-level registration of synchrotron XRF and FTIR microscopies on the same plant tissue cross-sections, combined with statistical multivariate analysis pipeline. This approach allows us to determine how spatial ionomics and molecular composition can be analyzed together to provide a clearer, more mechanistic insight into how biochemical structural changes may be driven by certain salt ions during salinity stress. Critically, the results showed opposing shifts in ion and biochemical composition of pistachio under salinity treatment. The behavior showed that under control conditions, K-associated biochemical signatures dominated, which shifted to Cl-associated signatures under increased salinity, consistent with ion compartmentalization. Notably, this response was targeted and not broadscale throughout the tissue, suggesting that pistachio responds to salinity through specific cellular adjustments as opposed to tissue-wide changes. Additionally, there was an increased abundance of protective compounds specific to spatial regions of the plant stem tissue under salinity treatment. This could be indicative of one of the strategies pistachio uses to cope with salinity stress. The methodological and analytical framework is not limited to the system presented herein, and is broadly applicable to plant biology, microbiology and rhizosphere research where resolving coupled elemental, chemical and biomolecular response to stress is essential for the biodesign of resilient agriculture. For example, the combined FTIR-XRF approach could be applied to studies of other abiotic stressors such as drought and even heat stress in plant systems to gain similar insights into the ionomic and biochemical changes that control tolerance (or otherwise) across various genotypes. The analytical workflow presented is not limited to the imaging modes herein, and additional imaging modalities can be incorporated, such as fluorescence microscopy and even mass spectrometry imaging (where the most destructive imaging mode is the last step in the imaging methodological workflow).
