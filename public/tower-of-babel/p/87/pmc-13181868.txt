Risk factors for postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction in adult patients undergoing short laparoscopic surgery: a prospective exploratory observational study
Authors: Shufen Xie, Xiaomin Mo, Yuanfeng Yin, Runze Wang, Cunran Kong, Zhijing Zhang, Haihui Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAlthough postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD) is common after abdominal surgery, most evidence comes from major procedures, leaving its risk factors after short laparoscopic surgery poorly understood. This study aimed to explore the risk factors for POGD in this surgical context primarily and to examine modifiable strategies for enhancing gastrointestinal recovery secondarily.MethodsIn this prospective exploratory cohort study, 176 adults undergoing short laparoscopic surgery were included. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with POGD. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the relationships between perioperative variables and the time to first flatus.ResultsProlonged operating time (aOR = 1.294 per 10 min, 95% CI: 1.089–1.537, p = 0.003) and longer preoperative fasting for clear liquids (aOR = 1.192 per hour, 95% CI: 1.057–1.345, p = 0.004) were factors independently associated with POGD. Secondarily, in those patients undergoing short laparoscopic surgery, a higher step count on postoperative day 1 (aHR = 1.032 per 100 steps, 95% CI: 1.014–1.051, p = 0.001) and earlier postoperative feeding (aHR = 0.968 per hour, 95% CI: 0.939–0.999, p = 0.043) were significantly correlated with earlier time to flatus.ConclusionsIn patients undergoing short laparoscopic surgery, prolonged operating time and extended preoperative fluid fasting are modifiable risk factors for POGD. Earlier postoperative feeding and ambulation were associated with faster gastrointestinal recovery. These findings identify actionable perioperative targets for reducing POGD and enhancing recovery in this setting.Trial registrationChiCTR2400094411 (Registration Date: 2024-12-23)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03803-1.Keywords: Postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction, Laparoscopic surgery, Fasting, Operating time, Physical activity, Enhanced recovery after surgery

Introduction

Postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD) is a common and clinically significant complication following abdominal surgery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 10% to 30% across various surgical procedures [1]. This condition is characterized by a transient yet disruptive impairment of coordinated gastrointestinal motility, presenting as nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, tenderness, and delayed return of bowel function—specifically, the passage of flatus and stool [2]. While some degree of ileus is an expected physiological response to surgical stress, the persistence or severity of symptoms beyond the typical recovery period indicates a pathological state that can prolong hospital stay and delay recovery [3].

Risk factors for POGD include advanced age, open laparotomy, cardiac comorbidities, stoma formation, prior abdominal surgery, and male sex [4]. More recently, the gut microbiota has been implicated in the pathogenesis of POGD [5]. Conventional management has largely centered on symptomatic and supportive measures, such as acid-suppressive agents, prokinetics, and antiemetics. Although these conventional approaches may alleviate symptoms and promote recovery to some degree [6], their reactive nature is increasingly giving way to more proactive strategies. A clearer understanding of modifiable risk factors is crucial for enabling early identification and implementing targeted mitigation throughout the perioperative period.

Meanwhile, clinicians vigilantly monitor gastrointestinal recovery in patients at high risk for POGD (e.g., those undergoing major open surgery) [7–9], but limited attention is paid to individuals undergoing short laparoscopic surgery (≤ 2.5 h). Specifically, the occurrence of POGD in adult patients following short laparoscopic surgery remains unexamined in the literature. We therefore designed this prospective exploratory cohort study. The primary aim was to examine perioperative factors for their association with POGD following short laparoscopic surgery. Secondary aim was to investigate which perioperative modifiable factors, including preoperative fasting practices, intraoperative management, timing of postoperative feeding, and postoperative mobilization, were correlated with the speed of gastrointestinal recovery, namely the time to first flatus [10].

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, single-center, observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary-care university hospital in Dongguan, China, between January and May 2025. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) Accepted laparoscopic surgery with operation times ≤ 2.5 h; (2) Age: 40 to 65 years old; (3) American society of anesthesiologists (ASA) classification levels II to III; (4) Expected hospital stay of at least 2 days.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Those allergic to components or ingredients that may be used in the study, such as atropine, etomidate, sufentanil, cisatracurium, sevoflurane, remifentanil, and propofol; (2) Those with a history of gastrointestinal diseases or surgeries; (3) Individuals without a smartphone or wearable device to record step counts, or those unable to cooperate with the researchers; (4) Those with a history of mental illness and patients with cognitive dysfunction.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the occurrence of POGD within 48 h after surgery. The secondary outcome was time to first postoperative flatus, measured in hours from completion of surgery to patient-reported passage of flatus.

Baseline characteristics and outcome measures

The day before surgery, sociodemographic, laboratory test, and medical history data were collected. These included age, sex, ASA classification, body mass index (BMI), history of abdominal surgery, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, cerebral infarction, operating time, drainage tube, surgery type, bleeding, total remifentanil dose during surgery, the preoperative level of creatinine, albumin, potassium, and hemoglobin within 2 days before surgery.

Other recorded variables comprised preoperative variables (average step count in the 7 days before surgery and fasting duration for clear liquids and food) and postoperative variables (time to first feeding and flatus, step count on postoperative day 1). The average steps taken during the 7 days before surgery was obtained by reviewing data from the patient’s smartphone. The preoperative fasting durations for clear liquids and solid food were documented in detail by the anesthesiologist upon the patient’s arrival in the operating room. Step count on the first postoperative day was recorded using a fitness tracker wristband provided by the researcher, which the patient was required to wear continuously until collected during the follow-up visit. The time of the first postoperative flatus was ascertained by a designated anesthesiologist (S.X) through follow-up until the data were confirmed.

Evaluation of POGD

POGD was assessed from the time of emergence from anesthesia in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) until 48 h postoperatively. Patients were evaluated twice daily (at 8:00 AM and 5:00 PM) during this period. POGD was diagnosed if two or more of the following five criteria were present at any single assessment time point, without prior resolution, within the 48-hour window: (1) nausea or vomiting; (2) intolerance to oral diet within 24 h after the operation; (3) absence of flatus within 24 h after the operation; (4) abdominal distension; (5) radiological confirmation of ileus [11]. All assessments were performed by the same investigator (S.X.) to ensure consistency.

Sample size calculation

Based on the primary outcome, three covariates were entered into the logistic regression model. Following the events-per-variable rule (10 events per covariate) [12], 30 POGD events are required. With an expected POGD prevalence of 19% according to our pilot study data, this translates to 30 / 0.19 = 158 evaluable patients. Assuming 10% loss to follow-up, we aimed to enroll 176 participants (158 / 0.9 ≈ 176).

Anesthetic protocols

All patients received general anesthesia with transversus abdominis plane nerve block (0.25% ropivacaine 40 ml). Anesthesia was induced with intravenous dexamethasone (8 mg), penehyclidine hydrochloride (0.01 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg), etomidate (0.3 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg). Patients were connected to an anesthetic machine for mechanical ventilation, with settings adjusted to deliver a tidal volume within the range of 6–8 ml per kilogram of body weight and a breathing rate of 12 breaths per minute. The dosages of remifentanil (0.05–0.3 µg/kg/min), propofol (4–8 mg/kg/h), cisatracurium (1.5 µg/kg/min), and norepinephrine were adjusted intra-operatively based on the baseline blood pressure (± 20% of baseline) and Bispectral Index (BIS) values (40–60). Standard intraoperative monitoring included electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure measurement, and pulse oximetry. At the end of surgery, 50 mg of flurbiprofen and 4 mg of ondansetron were administered intravenously. Metoclopramide was administered as rescue therapy to patients experiencing severe postoperative vomiting.

Data completeness and missing data handling

The Cox regression analysis was based on the complete cases (n = 157) with no missing data for any covariates. Therefore, no imputation was required.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0, with a two‑tailed p‑value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) for continuous variables, and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Group comparisons were performed using unpaired Student’s t‑tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for proportions.

Variables included in the univariate analysis and in the Cox model were prespecified based on clinical relevance and their potential as modifiable perioperative factors. Those with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model, which confirmed no multicollinearity (VIF < 1.5). Linearity was formally tested using the Box-Tidwell procedure, with an FDR correction applied to the family of tests, and that the assumption was upheld for all variables. The Benjamini‑Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05 across all tested variables.

Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the association between perioperative factors and the time to first flatus. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox model was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violation was detected for any covariate. No automated stepwise selection was used in any multivariable model.

Participant characteristics

A total of 176 participants were initially recruited. After excluding 19 participants, 157 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Operating time and preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids are independently associated with POGD

Next, univariate logistic regression was conducted to identify the potential risk factors for perioperative management related to POGD (Fig. 2 and Suppl Table S1). Specifically, preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids (OR = 1.150 per hour, 95% CI: 1.023–1.284, p = 0.018), operating time (OR = 1.216 per 10 min, 95% CI: 1.041–1.42, p = 0.013) and BMI (OR = 0.842, 95% CI: 0.726–0.976, p = 0.022) were significantly different. No statistically significant difference was observed in the remaining variables.

Univariate logistic regression analysis of the factors related to POGD

Based on the above findings, operating time, BMI, and preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids were entered into the multivariable regression analysis (Fig. 3 and Suppl Table S1): We found that extended preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.192 per hour, 95% CI: 1.057–1.345, p = 0.004) and prolonged operating time (aOR = 1.294 per 10 min, 95% CI: 1.089–1.537, p = 0.003) independently associated with higher risk for POGD in patients undergoing short laparoscopic surgeries, while no significant association was found for BMI (aOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.761–1.017, p = 0.083).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of the factors related to POGD

Early ambulation and postoperative fasting are correlated with shorter time to first postoperative flatus in patients undergoing short laparoscopic surgery

Postoperative flatus is an important indicator of the recovery of gastrointestinal function [13]. We performed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis to examine the association between perioperative factors and the time to first flatus. The model included five modifiable covariates: preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids, preoperative fasting duration for food, operating time, postoperative time to first feeding, and step count on POD 1 (Fig. 4 and Suppl Table S1).

Multivariable Cox regression analysis of factors associated with time to first flatus

A higher step count on POD 1 was associated with a shorter time to flatus (aHR = 1.032 per 100 steps, 95% CI: 1.014–1.051, p = 0.001). Conversely, a longer delay to initiate postoperative feeding was independently associated with a longer time to flatus (aHR = 0.968 per hour, 95% CI: 0.939–0.999, p = 0.043). No significant association with time to first flatus was observed for preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids (aHR = 1.009 per hour, 95% CI: 0.957–1.063, p = 0.75), preoperative fasting duration for food (aHR = 0.992 per hour, 95% CI: 0.955–1.031, p = 0.679), or operating time (HR = 0.957 per 10 min, 95% CI: 0.893–1.027, p = 0.221).

Discussion

In this prospective, exploratory study, we primarily observed that prolonged operating time and extended preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids were independently associated with a higher incidence of POGD. Secondarily, regarding the pace of functional recovery across all patients, our data suggest that early postoperative mobilization and feeding are associated with a shorter time to the return of bowel function, even in short laparoscopic surgery. These findings highlight these factors as potentially important modifiable correlates of gastrointestinal recovery outcomes.

A comprehensive elucidation of the mechanisms responsible for the onset, duration, and severity of POGD is currently lacking. Nevertheless, several pathophysiological pathways have been proposed. These include spinal-intestinal sympathetic reflex arcs, increased sympathetic tone, inflammatory mediators, opioid effects, and electrolyte disturbances [5]. The impact of these mechanisms can be intensified by certain surgical and anesthetic practices [2]. In this study, a standardized anesthetic protocol that incorporated recommended multimodal analgesia [14] was used to control for confounders related to analgesia, opioid and anti-inflammatory drug use, allowing us to isolate the impact of modifiable peri-operative variables on POGD in patients undergoing short laparoscopic surgery.

While the detrimental effects of prolonged operating time and fasting are well-established in major abdominal surgery [15, 16], their significance in shorter, minimally invasive procedures remains less clear. Our study demonstrates that these temporal factors are, in fact, independently associated with POGD even in the context of short laparoscopic surgeries. Notably, in our multivariable model, preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids emerged as an independent correlate. The aOR was 1.192 per additional hour (95% CI: 1.057–1.345). These findings provide evidence supporting the ERAS protocol recommendation to shorten fasting periods. However, given the observed POGD incidence (19.1%) in our cohort, this aOR should be interpreted with caution, as it may overestimate the relative risk increase compared to a risk ratio. Similarly, the aOR for each additional 10 min of operative time was 1.294 (95% CI: 1.089–1.537) underscoring the importance of surgical efficiency and minimally invasive techniques even during brief procedures. These findings highlight prolonged preoperative fasting and operative time as important modifiable factors associated with POGD and support their consideration as targets to reduce the occurrence of POGD [17, 18].

Diagnostic criteria for POGD have only recently been established. Time to first flatus remains an important indicator of gastrointestinal recovery. Here, we observed that earlier postoperative feeding and higher levels of postoperative physical activity were associated with accelerated recovery of bowel function undergoing short laparoscopic surgery. Based on the Cox model estimates, an increase in POD 1 ambulation was associated with a reduced time to first flatus (aHR = 1.032 per 100 steps), although substantial individual variability remains unexplained by the model. Similarly, delay in initiating postoperative feeding was associated with delayed flatus (aHR = 0.968 per hour). However, unmeasured confounding arising from clinical decisions, such as surgeons withholding oral intake until flatus has passed, remains a possibility.

Despite being independent predictors of POGD, neither preoperative fasting duration nor operating time showed a significant association with the time to first flatus in the Cox model among all patients. This finding suggests that preoperative and intraoperative factors may be weakly associated with time to flatus following short laparoscopic surgery, but rather more closely related to other POGD symptoms such as abdominal distension, nausea, or vomiting. Active postoperative interventions may therefore be more effective in shortening the time to flatus.

Furthermore, time to first defecation was not included in the analysis due to a high proportion of patients being discharged before passing stool, resulting in substantial censoring, and because it is more prone to confounding by preoperative bowel preparation and dietary composition. We also did not identify independent associations between conventional risk factors, including preoperative BMI, albumin levels, intraoperative blood loss, or surgical site (upper vs. lower abdomen) [4, 8, 19]. This may be attributable to our study population, which comprised relatively healthy patients undergoing limited, minimally invasive procedures, wherein the influence of such factors is likely attenuated. Similarly, the lack of association with preoperative step count, a proxy for physical activity [20], may reflect both measurement limitations (smartphone-derived data may not fully capture activity quality or intensity) and a potentially reduced effect of preoperative conditioning on POGD risk in this already low-risk cohort undergoing short-duration surgery.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, while sufficient for initial exploration, remains moderate, and the number of POGD events was limited. This constrains the statistical power for subgroup analyses. Second, the severity grading and the exact duration of POGD episodes were not assessed using a validated multi-level scoring system, and data on direct complications or readmissions specifically attributable to POGD were incomplete. Third, there is potential for measurement bias. The assessment of POGD and gastrointestinal recovery, though based on defined criteria and collected prospectively, depended on patient reporting and a single assessor, introducing risks of recall and observer bias. Fourth, although we removed postoperative feeding time from the POGD risk factor analysis to avoid circularity, residual reverse causality remains in the Cox model examining time to flatus: clinicians may delay feeding in response to early signs of slow gastrointestinal recovery, making it difficult to disentangle feeding as a predictor versus a consequence of delayed GI function. Furthermore, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate associations but cannot establish causality. The relationships we observed between perioperative practices and outcomes may be influenced by unmeasured confounding factors. Additionally, our inclusion criteria focused on a younger and relatively healthier surgical population within 48 h after surgery. So, our findings may not be generalizable to older, frailer, higher-risk patients (e.g., ASA IV) or to those with POGD onset beyond 48 h. Future studies are needed to validate these observations in a broader, more heterogeneous surgical population.

Despite these limitations, this prospective cohort provides a focused examination of modifiable factors in a specific surgical context, and the identified associations offer actionable hypotheses for future, larger-scale studies designed to overcome these constraints.

Conclusions

In this exploratory analysis, prolonged operating time and preoperative fasting duration for clear liquids were identified as factors associated with the development of POGD after short laparoscopic surgery. Earlier postoperative feeding and ambulation were correlated with faster recovery of bowel function. These modifiable perioperative factors represent potential targets for future intervention studies aimed at risk reduction and recovery enhancement.
