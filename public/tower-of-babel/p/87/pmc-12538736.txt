Single-cell transcriptomics identifies PDIA4 as a marker of progression and therapeutic vulnerability in multiple myeloma
Authors: Wenjie Yu, Jiamin Zhan, Yanxia Wang, Xu Cao, Aoyang Yu, Yuexuan Rao, Can Huang, Xuan Zhang, Shunv Lu, Yanhua Liu, Chong Chen, Zhenyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic cancer marked by clonal expansion of plasma cells in the bone marrow. Although its genomic landscape has been extensively characterized, the transcriptional mechanisms that govern malignant progression and long-term tumor cell survival remain incompletely understood.MethodsWe integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from healthy donors (HD), monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM), and newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients, retrieved from GEO datasets GSE124310 and GSE271107, to construct a comprehensive transcriptional landscape of plasma cell differentiation. Pseudotime and enrichment analyses identified PDIA4 as a terminal-state–associated gene. The prognostic significance of PDIA4 was validated using the multiple myeloma research foundation (MMRF) CoMMpass cohort. Functional analyses were performed in vitro and in vivo to validate the role of PDIA4 in MM cell survival and therapeutic response.ResultsPseudotime trajectory analysis revealed progressive upregulation of genes involved in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), with PDIA4 identified as a top candidate in terminal-stage plasma cells. Survival analysis in the MMRF CoMMpass cohort further demonstrated that high PDIA4 expression correlated with poor overall survival. In RPMI-8226 cells, PDIA4 knockout activated the IRE1α/XBP1s branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR), impaired proliferation, and induced G1-phase arrest. PDIA4 depletion also sensitized cells to bortezomib. In vivo, sg-PDIA4 suppressed tumor growth in RPMI-8226 xenografts.ConclusionsPDIA4 is a key regulator of the unfolded protein response and MM cell survival. Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of abnormal plasma cells within the bone marrow [1–3]. Clinically, MM progresses through a well-defined spectrum, starting from monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), advancing to smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM), and ultimately developing into symptomatic newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) [4–6]. Although MGUS and SMM can remain asymptomatic for years, both have the potential to progress to overt MM [7, 8]. However, the molecular mechanisms driving this gradual malignant transition remain incompletely understood [9].

Recent advances in scRNA-seq have provided high-resolution insights into the molecular heterogeneity within the bone marrow microenvironment, enabling precise characterization of plasma cell differentiation [10–12]. In this study, we integrated publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets from the GEO, including samples from HD, MGUS, SMM, and NDMM patients, to reconstruct a comprehensive transcriptional landscape of plasma cell evolution. Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed a continuous progression from normal to malignant plasma cells, characterized by the gradual upregulation of pathways involved in antigen processing and presentation, and protein processing in the ER.

These findings are consistent with the established pathophysiology of MM, in which the high secretory burden from monoclonal immunoglobulin production places substantial stress on the ER, leading to the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins [13]. To alleviate this proteotoxic stress, malignant plasma cells activate the unfolded protein response, an adaptive signaling pathway that restores ER function and maintains proteostasis [14]. While initially protective, persistent UPR activation has been implicated in promoting MM cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to therapy, suggesting that ER stress–related pathways may represent therapeutic vulnerabilities [15, 16].

Given the critical reliance of MM cells on ER homeostasis, we sought to identify key regulators of this pathway involved in disease progression. Pseudotime analysis highlighted PDIA4, an ER-resident disulfide isomerase involved in protein folding and ER proteostasis, as a candidate driver of malignant transformation [17]. Subsequent experimental validation and survival analysis demonstrated that PDIA4 promotes MM cell survival, supports proliferative capacity, and modulates sensitivity to proteasome inhibition, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Data sources and preprocessing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from two publicly available datasets in the gene expression omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE124310 [18] and GSE271107. Together, these datasets included bone marrow samples from 14 HD, 11 from cases of MGUS, 15 from SMM, and 11 from NDMM. Bulk RNA-seq data with matched clinical and survival information were retrieved from the MMRF CoMMpass study via the MMRF Researcher Gateway (https://research.themmrf.org), encompassing 859 MM samples for external validation. A list of 171 genes involved in the “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” pathway was obtained from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database for pathway-based analysis. Public transcriptomic datasets and KEGG pathway gene sets used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Single-cell RNA-seq processing

Raw gene expression count matrices were imported and converted into Seurat objects using the CreateSeuratObject function (Seurat v4.4.0). Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes were excluded, and genes were retained only if detected in at least three cells. After merging the datasets, additional quality control steps were applied to exclude cells expressing more than 6000 genes or exhibiting over 20% mitochondrial gene content. All preprocessing and quality control steps were conducted in R (v4.4.3) using the Seurat package.

Gene expression data were normalized using the LogNormalize method, and 3000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified with the “vst” method. The dataset was then scaled using ScaleData, regressing out technical and biological confounders. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction, and the top 30 principal components were passed to the Harmony algorithm for batch effect correction. Harmony-corrected embeddings were used to construct a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph and perform clustering at a resolution of 0.7. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for visualization of cellular heterogeneity and integration across samples [19].

Cell type annotation was performed using a combination of automated and manual strategies. First, clusters were annotated using the SingleR package (v2.8.0) based on reference scRNA-seq datasets, followed by manual curation based on canonical marker gene expression [20, 21]. To identify cell type–specific marker genes, we applied the FindAllMarkers function (Seurat v4.4.0) using Wilcoxon rank-sum test across clusters. Genes were ranked by average log fold-change and adjusted p-value, and the top markers were selected for downstream visualization.

Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the find_diff_genes function from the Scillus package, which applies the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes detected in more than 10% of cells and exhibiting an average log2 fold change (log2FC) greater than 0.15 were included in the DEG analysis [22]. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple testing, and genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To characterize the potential biological functions and associated signaling pathways of DEGs across multiple myeloma stages, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.14.6) [23]. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was conducted with terms categorized into biological process (bp), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). KEGG pathway enrichment was also performed to identify significantly enriched pathways. Enrichment results with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), all genes were ranked by log2 fold change and evaluated against curated KEGG gene sets to capture broader transcriptional changes beyond individual DEGs.

Copy number variation (CNV) estimation

CNV estimation was performed using the “inferCNV” R package (v1.22.0) to discriminate malignant and non-malignant plasma cells based on scRNA-seq expression profiles and chromosomal location data. Plasma cells from HD were used as the reference group to calculate CNV scores for each cell in the observation group. Cells with CNV scores in the top 50% of the distribution within the observation group were designated as “top CNV” cells for further analysis. Malignancy status was determined based on two criteria: (1) CNV score exceeding 0.001, and (2) Pearson correlation coefficient with reference cells greater than 0.25. Cells meeting both criteria were classified as malignant [23].

Pseudotime trajectory analysis of plasma cells

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using the Monocle package (v2.30.1) to reconstruct the developmental paths of plasma cells based on their gene expression profiles. Dimensionality reduction and cell ordering were applied to arrange plasma cells along a continuous trajectory with distinct branching points, reflecting their differentiation states. Cells within the same branch were grouped into discrete states. To identify pseudotime-dependent genes, differential expression analysis was conducted using the differentialGeneTest function, with a significance threshold of q-value < 0.01 [24]. The top 100 most significant pseudotime-associated genes were selected for further analysis, and their expression patterns were visualized as heatmaps, revealing two major gene clusters representing early and late transcriptional programs.

Univariate cox regression analysis for prognostic gene identification

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was conducted to evaluate the prognostic relevance of terminally upregulated genes derived from pseudotime trajectory analysis. Clinical and survival data were obtained from the MMRF CoMMpass cohort. A total of 75 genes enriched in the terminal pseudotime branches were tested. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the survival R package (v3.7.0). Genes with significant associations with overall survival (p < 0.05, Wald test) were defined as prognostic and selected for further analysis.

Refinement of prognostic candidates by cox and KEGG pathway integration

To refine the list of candidate prognostic genes, the set of Cox-significant terminal-state genes was intersected with the KEGG-defined gene set for the “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” pathway. This intersection identified PDIA4 as a key prognostic marker. For validation, patients in the MMRF CoMMpass cohort were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to the median expression level of PDIA4. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to assess overall survival differences between groups, and statistical significance was evaluated using the log-rank test.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The relationship between PDIA4 expression and predicted drug sensitivity was evaluated using transcriptomic and drug response data from the MMRF CoMMpass cohort. Gene expression data were first stratified into high and low PDIA4 expression groups based on the median expression level. Drug sensitivity predictions were generated using the oncoPredict package (v1.2) [25, 26]. Pearson correlation coefficients (R) were calculated to assess the strength and direction of the relationship between PDIA4 expression and predicted drug sensitivity for each compound. Only compounds with |R|> 0.5 and p < 0.05 were considered to exhibit significant correlation.

Drug reagents

PACMA 31 (HY-100433) and bortezomib (HY-10227) were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE) and dissolved in DMSO to prepare 10 mM stock solutions (1 mL each). Stock solutions were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Working concentrations were freshly prepared by diluting the stock solutions in cell culture medium immediately before use.

Cell culture and genome editing

Multiple myeloma cell lines, including RPMI-8226, NCI-H929, and AMO-1, were obtained from Cobioer Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (https://www.cobioer.com). RPMI-8226 cells were cultured in IMDM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), while NCI-H929 and AMO-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS. All media and FBS were purchased from bio-channel. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

For PDIA4 knockout, two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting distinct exonic regions of the human PDIA4 gene were designed (Supplementary Table S3 for sequences). A non-targeting sgRNA (sg-NT) was used as a negative control. All sgRNAs were chemically synthesized and dissolved in DEPC-treated water prior to use.

Genome editing was performed using the GenCRISPR Cas9 v1.2 system (Z03702-1, GenScript). For each transfection, 16 μg of Cas9 protein was combined with 2.5 μg of each sgRNA in 50 μl of electroporation buffer. The sgRNA-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes were incubated on ice for 10 min prior to transfection. Cells were then collected and electroporated using the 4D-Nucleofector™ X Kit (SF Cell Line) with 100 μL cuvettes, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For transient knockdown of PDIA4, three independent siRNA sequences targeting distinct regions of the PDIA4 transcript were synthesized (Supplementary Table S3 for sequences). Each siRNA was tested individually to evaluate knockdown efficiency and cellular response. A non-targeting siRNA was used as a negative control. All siRNAs were dissolved in RNase-free water and electroporated into NCI-H929 and AMO-1 cells using the 4D-Nucleofector™ X Unit with the SF Cell Line Kit, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

RNA extraction and real-time and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 System (Roche) using SYBR Green I Master Mix. Primer sequences used for amplification are listed in Supplementary Table S4. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the comparative Ct (2^ − ΔΔCt) method.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured in 3 mL of complete growth medium. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were harvested at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-seeding, and absolute cell numbers were determined using a hemocytometer.

Flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured under standard conditions. At the designated time points, cells were harvested, washed twice with Annexin V Binding Buffer (BD Biosciences), and resuspended in 200 µL of fresh binding buffer. Annexin V-APC (AB_2868885, BD Biosciences) and 7-AAD (AB_2869266, BD Biosciences) were then added to each sample, followed by a 15-min incubation at room temperature in the dark. Stained cells were immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer to quantify apoptosis. Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1), with cells categorized as early apoptotic.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were harvested and fixed in 75% ethanol at 4 °C for 24 h to permeabilize the nuclear membrane. After fixation, cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min and washed twice with PBS to remove residual ethanol. Cells were then resuspended in 500 μL of PBS containing 50 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) and 100 μg/mL RNase A to stain nuclear DNA. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark to allow for complete DNA staining. Stained cells were immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer, and cell cycle distribution (G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases) was determined using MODFIT LT5.0 software.

Drug sensitivity and IC₅₀ determination

Suspension cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in 100 µL of complete growth medium. Cells were immediately treated with serial dilutions of the test compounds. Untreated wells served as negative controls, drug-treated wells as positive controls, and medium-only wells as blanks. Plates were incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere) for 48 h to ensure sufficient drug exposure.

Following incubation, 10 µL of DEEPBLUE cell viability reagent was added to each well, and the plates were returned to the incubator for an additional 2 h to allow complete reaction. Fluorescence was then measured using a microplate reader with 530 nm excitation and 620 nm emission filters.

IC₅₀ values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis with appropriate curve fitting.

Drug synergy analysis

The interaction between PACMA 31 and bortezomib was evaluated using the SynergyFinder 3.0 platform, which estimates drug combination effects based on the zero interaction potency (ZIP) reference model. The ZIP score was calculated to quantify the degree of drug interaction, where a score > 10 indicates synergy, a score < − 10 indicates antagonism, and a score between − 10 and 10 represents additive or non-synergistic effects. Following synergy analysis, functional validation was performed by treating multiple myeloma cell lines with optimized drug concentrations: RPMI-8226 cells were treated with bortezomib (20 nM) and PACMA 31 (20 μM), NCI-H929 cells with bortezomib (1 nM) and PACMA 31 (30 nM), and AMO-1 cells with bortezomib (1 nM) and PACMA 31 (90 nM). Cells were incubated for 48 h prior to flow cytometric analysis of cell viability and apoptosis.

Xenograft model

Six- to eight-week-old NSG mice (12 males and 12 females) were randomly assigned to two groups (sg-NT and sg-PDIA4), with equal sex distribution (6 males and 6 females per group). Each mouse was subcutaneously injected with 5 × 10⁶ RPMI-8226 sg-NT or sg-PDIA4 cells into the dorsal flank. Tumor volume was monitored every three days using the formula: (length × width2)/2. Mice were euthanized upon reaching ethical endpoints, and tumors were excised for volume and weight measurements. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with approved guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, while differences among more than two groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with statistical significance determined by the log-rank test. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software (v4.4.3) (https://www.R-project.org), RStudio (https://www.rstudio.com), and GraphPad Prism. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels were indicated as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant, p > 0.05.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic landscape across stages of multiple myeloma progression

To investigate the cellular heterogeneity and compositional changes associated with MM progression, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic datasets of BMMCs from HD, MGUS, SMM, and NDMM from GEO datasets GSE124310 and GSE271107. After quality control, a total of 148,293 high-quality single cells were retained, including 32,157 from HD, 40,581 from MGUS, 39,189 from SMM, and 36,366 from NDMM.

After log-normalization and identification of highly variable genes, cell cycle effects and technical covariates were regressed during data scaling. Principal component analysis was performed, and batch effects were corrected using Harmony (Fig. S1). Cells were clustered and visualized via UMAP and annotated into 13 major cell types based on canonical marker genes: T cells (CD3D, CD3E), B cells (CD79A, CD79B), NK cells (NKG7), plasma cells (MZB1), granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs, MPO), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs, CLEC4C), conventional dendritic cells (cDCs, CD1C), megakaryocytes (PPBP), pre;B cells (VPREB1), erythroid cells (HBB), hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs, CD34), CD16⁺ monocytes (FCGR3A), and CD14⁺ monocytes (CD14) (Fig. 1A, B). Expression levels of the top 7 marker genes across cell types are shown in the dot plot (Fig. 1C).

Comparative analysis of cell type composition and absolute cell numbers across disease stages revealed progressive expansion of plasma cells (Fig. 1D). These results demonstrate dynamic remodeling of the bone marrow microenvironment during MM progression and confirm the hallmark clonal expansion of plasma cells consistent with pathological diagnosis.

Characterization of single-cell expression profiles for plasma cells across different stages of multiple myeloma progression

Transcriptomic profiles of plasma cells from sequential stages of disease—MGUS, SMM, and NDMM—were compared pairwise (MGUS vs HD, SMM vs MGUS, and NDMM vs SMM) to identify stage-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Intersecting DEGs from the three comparisons were used to define a core gene set associated with progressive disease transitions (Fig. 2A, B). Functional enrichment using KEGG and GO databases identified pathways associated with oxidative phosphorylation, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, antigen processing and presentation, and mitochondrial function (Fig. 2C), suggesting progressive reprogramming of plasma cell metabolism and secretory function during malignant transformation.

Intratumoral heterogeneity and malignant state inference were assessed using inferCNV on plasma cells across all disease stages, with HD-derived plasma cells as reference. CNV profiles were generated and used to calculate malignancy scores. Plasma cells were classified as malignant or non-malignant based on CNV magnitude and correlation thresholds (Fig. S2). A progressive increase in the proportion of inferred malignant plasma cells was observed from MGUS to NDMM, in accordance with clinical stage severity (Fig. 2D, E).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using genes ranked by log₂ fold changes derived from the differential expression between malignant and non-malignant plasma cells. Pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, thermogenesis, and Huntington disease were significantly activated, reflecting increased metabolic and proteotoxic stress in malignant cells (Fig. 2F). Conversely, immune -related pathways—including IL-17 signaling, hematopoietic cell lineage, type I diabetes mellitus, and graft-versus-host disease—were significantly suppressed (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that malignant plasma cells undergo metabolic adaptation while suppressing immune regulatory programs during disease progression.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis identifies PDIA4 as a prognostic marker in multiple myeloma

While stage-wise comparisons and malignancy classification revealed progressive transcriptomic changes among plasma cells, these static analyses could not fully resolve the continuous trajectories of disease evolution at the single-cell level. To capture the dynamic transcriptional changes underlying plasma cell evolution, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed on integrated plasma cells from all disease stages using Monocle2 (Fig. 3A). This analysis revealed a branched developmental structure comprising five distinct transcriptional states (Fig. 3B), outlining a continuum from HD toward NDMM. Overlaying disease stages and inferred malignancy status onto the trajectory revealed a directional shift, with terminal branches primarily populated by malignant cells from later-stage disease (Fig. 3C, D).

Analysis of the molecular features associated with this directional shift identified the top 100 pseudotime-dependent genes, which were significantly enriched in KEGG pathways linked to distinct biological processes (Fig. 3E). Genes expressed in early states were predominantly involved in immune signaling, lymphocyte activation, and hematopoietic differentiation, reflecting the immunocompetent transcriptional features of early-stage plasma cells. In contrast, terminal-state genes exhibited marked enrichment in pathways related to proteasome function, antigen processing and presentation, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, aligning with the increased biosynthetic and metabolic demands of malignant plasma cells.

Given the potential clinical relevance of these terminal-state genes, a subset of significantly upregulated genes at the terminal branches was selected for univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis in the MMRF CoMMpass cohort. High expression of many of these genes was significantly associated with poor overall survival (p < 0.001), supporting their potential prognostic value (Fig. 3F). Given that the “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” pathway emerged as a significantly enriched category not only in our terminal-state gene analysis but also in multiple independent enrichment analyses, including those stratified by disease stage and malignancy status, we further refined our candidate list by intersecting these high-risk genes with the KEGG-defined gene set for this pathway. This approach identified four candidate genes: LMAN2, SEC61G, TMEM258, and PDIA4 (Fig. 3G). Among these, PDIA4—a protein disulfide isomerase involved in ER protein folding—was selected for further investigation. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis confirmed that elevated PDIA4 expression was strongly associated with reduced overall survival (p < 0.001) in the CoMMpass cohort (Fig. 3H), highlighting its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in MM.

Functional evaluation of PDIA4 in multiple myeloma models in vitro and in vivo

The baseline expression of PDIA4 was first assessed across three MM cell lines. Western blot analysis revealed higher endogenous PDIA4 levels in RPMI-8226 and AMO-1 compared to NCI-H929 (Fig. 4A). sg-PDIA4 and sg-NT constructs were electroporated into MM cell lines to evaluate the functional impact of PDIA4 depletion. Within 24 h post-electroporation, both NCI-H929 and AMO-1 sg-PDIA4 cells exhibited substantial cell death, thereby precluding downstream functional analyses (Fig. 4B). To independently validate the cytotoxic effect of PDIA4 suppression, three distinct PDIA4-targeting siRNAs were introduced into NCI-H929 and AMO-1 cells. Quantitative PCR at 12 h confirmed significant downregulation of PDIA4 mRNA across all siRNAs in both cell lines (Fig. 4C). By 24 h post-transfection, Annexin V/7-AAD staining revealed robust induction of cell death with each siRNA (Fig. 4D). Notably, qPCR analysis of the residual live cells revealed no significant reduction in PDIA4 mRNA levels, indicating that effective knockdown rapidly induces cytotoxicity and that even partial PDIA4 suppression is detrimental to cell viability in these lines (Fig. 4F).

In contrast to NCI-H929 and AMO-1, RPMI-8226 cells tolerated stable PDIA4 knockout without evident induction of basal cell death, as determined by Annexin V/7-AAD staining (Fig. 4F, Fig. S3). Western blot analysis confirmed efficient depletion of PDIA4 protein in RPMI-8226 sg-PDIA4 cells and revealed selective activation of the IRE1α–XBP1s branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR), as evidenced by elevated levels of spliced XBP1s and phosphorylated IRE1α, while p-eIF2α and ATF6 remained unchanged (Fig. 4G).

Despite this tolerance, longitudinal proliferation assays demonstrated a marked reduction in cell expansion over 96 h in RPMI-8226 sg-PDIA4 cells relative to sg-NT cells (Fig. 4H). Cell cycle profiling revealed a significant accumulation of RPMI-8226 sg-PDIA4 cells in the G1 phase and a concomitant decrease in S-phase population, with no appreciable change in G2/M-phase cells, suggesting a G1 phase arrest following PDIA4 depletion (Fig. 4I).

Following in vitro findings, RPMI-8226 sg-NT and sg-PDIA4 cells were subcutaneously injected into NSG mice to assess their tumorigenic potential. Tumor burden was significantly reduced in the sg-PDIA4 group at the experimental endpoint (Fig. S4). Tumors derived from sg-PDIA4 cells exhibited markedly decreased volume and weight in both male and female mice (Fig. 4J, L), demonstrating the in vivo relevance of PDIA4 in sustaining MM progression.

PDIA4 deficiency sensitizes multiple myeloma cells to bortezomib through enhanced activation of the UPR

The relationship between PDIA4 expression and predicted drug sensitivity was assessed using transcriptomic and oncoPredict modeling data from the MMRF CoMMpass cohort. High PDIA4 expression was broadly associated with decreased predicted sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic agents (Fig. 5A). Among these, two proteasome inhibitors—bortezomib and MG-132—showed strong inverse correlations with PDIA4 levels (Fig. 5B), implicating a potential role for PDIA4 in proteasome inhibitor resistance.

Functional validation was carried out by treating RPMI-8226 sg-NT and sg-PDIA4 cells with increasing concentrations of bortezomib. Flow cytometry revealed a dose-dependent increase in Annexin V/7-AAD–positive cells in the sg-PDIA4 group, with significantly increased apoptotic cell percentages observed at concentrations ≥ 1 nM, reaching a peak response at 10 nM (Fig. 5C, D). Consistent with these findings, sg-PDIA4 cells exhibited a substantially reduced IC₅₀ for bortezomib, confirming enhanced drug sensitivity (Fig. 5E).

Mechanistically, bortezomib treatment induced stronger activation of the IRE1α–XBP1s branch in sg-PDIA4 cells. Western blot analysis showed elevated levels of XBP1s, p-IRE1α, and CHOP following 3 nM bortezomib exposure in sg-PDIA4 cells relative to sg-NT controls, whereas ATF6 remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5F). These results suggest that PDIA4 deficiency amplifies bortezomib–induced UPR signaling and underscoring the functional role of PDIA4 in modulating therapeutic response via UPR regulation.

Pharmacological inhibition of PDIA4 potentiates proteasome inhibitor–induced cytotoxicity in myeloma cells

PACMA 31, a pan-PDI inhibitor, was tested in combination with bortezomib to assess whether pharmacological PDIA4 inhibition mimics the sensitization observed with genetic knockout. Three multiple myeloma cell lines—RPMI-8226, NCI-H929, and AMO-1—were treated with increasing concentrations of both agents. Drug interaction matrices and ZIP synergy scores consistently demonstrated significant positive synergy across all models, with ZIP scores of 12.94 (RPMI-8226), 10.65 (NCI-H929), and 10.85 (AMO-1), respectively (Fig. 6A–C). Notably, synergy was strongest and most sustained across a broader concentration range in RPMI-8226 cells.

Annexin V/7-AAD flow cytometry assays revealed that combination treatment markedly increased apoptosis compared to either agent alone across all cell lines at 48 h (Fig. 6D–F). Although both agents reduced cell viability as single agents, their combination consistently produced greater cytotoxic effects. These findings suggest that pharmacological inhibition of PDIA4 enhances the efficacy of proteasome inhibition and supports the therapeutic rationale for dual targeting of protein folding and degradation pathways in multiple myeloma.

Discussion

MM is a malignancy defined by the clonal expansion of abnormal plasma cells within the bone marrow. Clinically, MM progresses from MGUS and SMM to NDMM [27]. Although MGUS and SMM are largely asymptomatic, longitudinal studies estimate annual progression rates of approximately 1% and 10%, respectively [27, 28]. These observations underscore the need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving this transition, which may facilitate earlier risk stratification and development of precision therapies.

Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomic profiling have enabled unprecedented resolution in mapping intratumoral heterogeneity and cellular state transitions across disease stages [29]. Integration of scRNA-seq data from HD, MGUS, SMM, and NDMM samples enabled the reconstruction of a transcriptional continuum of plasma cell evolution during MM progression. Pseudotime and differential expression analyses revealed progressive transcriptional reprogramming associated with malignant transformation, including upregulation of pathways involved in antigen processing and presentation and protein processing in the ER. These alterations suggest that as disease advances, plasma cells become increasingly reliant on ER homeostasis and proteostasis mechanisms—a finding consistent with, and further supported by, prior reports of heightened ER stress responses in MM [30, 31].

Plasma cells are physiologically exposed to considerable ER burden due to their high-level immunoglobulin production [32]. In MM, this burden intensifies, frequently leading to activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR)—a conserved adaptive mechanism regulated by the IRE1α, PERK, and ATF6 signaling arms [33, 34]. While transient UPR activation facilitates survival by restoring ER homeostasis, sustained UPR signaling may allow malignant cells to adapt to chronic proteotoxic stress and evade therapeutic stressors [34–36]. Accordingly, the UPR network functions as both a critical survival axis and a potential point of therapeutic vulnerability in MM.

Terminal-state–enriched genes along the pseudotime trajectory were systematically analyzed to identify transcriptional regulators potentially involved in ER stress adaptation. Among these, PDIA4—a member of the protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) family localized to the endoplasmic reticulum—was markedly upregulated. PDIA4 catalyzes disulfide bond formation and protein folding, playing a critical role in maintaining ER function. Previous studies have implicated PDIA4 in tumor cell survival and chemoresistance across various solid tumors [37–40]. However, its functional contribution to MM pathophysiology has not been previously explored.

Survival analysis using the MMRF CoMMpass cohort confirmed that high PDIA4 expression is significantly associated with poorer overall survival, supporting its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker. Functional studies in RPMI-8226 cells revealed that sg-PDIA4 selectively activated the IRE1α–XBP1s branch of the UPR, with minimal impact on the PERK and ATF6 pathways. This pathway-specific response suggests that PDIA4 modulates the ER stress response primarily through the IRE1α–XBP1s signaling axis.

Phenotypically, PDIA4 ablation led to reduced cell proliferation and an increased proportion of cells in the G1 phase, indicating a blockade at the G1/S transition. While the precise mechanism remains unclear, this cell cycle arrest may reflect broader disruptions in ER proteostasis following PDIA4 loss.

Notably, PDIA4 depletion in NCI-H929 and AMO-1 cells induced rapid cell death, even under partial knockdown conditions. This dependency on PDIA4 suggests that MM cell lines exhibit heterogeneous thresholds of tolerance to ER proteotoxic stress, potentially reflecting differential reliance on proteostasis mechanisms. Previous studies have similarly reported inter-line variability in UPR activation and proteasome inhibitor sensitivity, which may underlie subtype-specific therapeutic responses [41, 42].

Consistent with the in vitro findings, in vivo xenograft experiments further confirmed the role of PDIA4 in sustaining MM cell growth. Subcutaneous injection of RPMI-8226 sg-PDIA4 cells into NSG mice led to markedly reduced tumor volumes compared to sg-NT controls. This observation underscores the in vivo dependency of MM cells on PDIA4 for maintaining proliferative capacity under physiological conditions. It also suggests that perturbation of ER proteostasis through PDIA4 depletion is sufficient to compromise tumor growth, thereby supporting the functional relevance of PDIA4 in MM progression.

Clinically, bortezomib—a proteasome inhibitor—induces cytotoxicity in MM cells by disrupting protein degradation and triggering lethal ER stress responses [43]. Its therapeutic efficacy has been associated with the ability of myeloma cells to buffer proteotoxic burden through adaptive proteostasis mechanisms [44]. In this context, our drug sensitivity prediction and experimental validation demonstrate that elevated PDIA4 loss correlates with resistance to bortezomib and MG132. Conversely, PDIA4 knockout substantially enhanced bortezomib efficacy in RPMI-8226 cells, and the addition of PACMA 31, a pan-PDI inhibitor, further amplified cytotoxic responses, as demonstrated by significant drug synergy. Together, our findings establish PDIA4 as a key regulator of ER stress adaptation and drug response in MM. Targeting PDIA4 may therefore represent a rational strategy to enhance the efficacy of proteasome inhibitors in multiple myeloma.

This study has certain limitations. Although our functional assays were conducted in both established MM cell lines and xenograft models, the differential response to PDIA4 depletion observed across models suggests subtype-specific differences in ER stress adaptation, which may help explain the heterogeneity in proteasome inhibitor sensitivity observed among patients. In addition, the translational potential of PDIA4-targeting strategies remains to be fully evaluated with respect to specificity, efficacy, and safety profiles.

In conclusion, this study delineates the transcriptional trajectory of plasma cell transformation at single-cell resolution and identifies PDIA4 as a critical modulator of ER stress signaling, cell cycle dynamics, and therapeutic response in MM. These findings enhance our understanding of how ER-associated proteostasis mechanisms contribute to disease progression and drug resistance, and highlight PDIA4 as a potential target to improve proteasome inhibitor efficacy in multiple myeloma.
