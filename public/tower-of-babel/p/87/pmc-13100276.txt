Longitudinal Evolution of Phenotypes in Severe Asthma: Clinical Implications for Personalized Medicine
Authors: Francisco Javier Álvarez-Gutiérrez, Marina Blanco Aparicio, José Valverde Molina
Published: 2026

Severe asthma continues to pose a significant challenge in clinical practice due to its marked clinical, inflammatory, and biological heterogeneity. Although advances in phenotypic research have enabled better patient stratification, most current conceptual frameworks remain fundamentally cross-sectional, limiting understanding of asthma's dynamic nature over time.

Various recent studies support abandoning a static view of asthma and considering it an evolutionary process, characterized by transitions and overlaps in phenotypes throughout the patient's life.1 In this regard, recent views consistently emphasize that phenotypes associated with type 2, non-type 2, and mixed inflammation are not mutually exclusive entities, but rather coexist and interrelate in response to environmental and infectious factors, comorbidities, and the plasticity of the immune system.

Early-onset allergic asthma usually features type 2 inflammation driven by allergen exposure, with variable eosinophilia and a mainly modulatory eosinophil role. Over the years, the disease may progress to complex, persistent forms where eosinophilic inflammation plays a more significant pathogenic role.

Asthma onset in adults is usually non-allergic, often eosinophilic, and frequently associated with chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps and recurrent exacerbations. Other phenotypical forms of the disease in adulthood with eosinophilic predominance may have been originally allergic in nature, but due to the effect of other factors (frequent viral infections, for example) or the effect of the exposome, they acquire predominantly eosinophilic characteristics, while maintaining minor allergic manifestations (such as allergic rhinitis), occasionally associated with seasonality and greater exposure to allergens.

This concept of phenotypical transition has recently been reinforced by Lommatzsch et al.,2 who emphasize that eosinophilia should not be considered an isolated treatable feature, but rather a complement to an accurate clinical characterization. In particular, accumulated evidence shows that eosinophils play a clearly distinct role depending on the predominant phenotype: secondary and variable in early-onset allergic asthma, but persistent and central in adult-onset “intrinsic” asthma. These differences have direct implications for the selection of and response to biological therapies.

In this context, note that the exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FeNO) can provide complementary information. FeNO is a non-invasive biomarker of type 2 airway inflammation, closely linked to IL-13 activity and epithelial changes such as mucosal hypersecretion and remodeling. Evidence suggests that persistently high FeNO predicts an increased risk of exacerbations and progressive loss of function in certain severe asthma subgroups, even if treated with inhaled corticosteroids. Clinically, persistent high FeNO may indicate poorly controlled type 2 inflammation and help explain suboptimal or lost efficacy with selected biologic therapies when the main inflammatory pathway is not addressed.

Furthermore, factors such as advanced age, immunosenescence, and “inflammaging” favor mixed or predominantly neutrophilic inflammatory profiles. These are often associated with corticosteroid resistance and additional comorbidities. Meanwhile, conditions such as obesity, smoking, recurrent respiratory infections, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, or bronchiectasis dynamically modulate the inflammatory axis, allowing oscillation between high- and low-type 2 patterns.

We propose that the evaluation of severe asthma systematically include a longitudinal perspective on the predominant phenotype. This approach should address key biomarkers, such as blood eosinophilia and FeNO, and track their changes over time. It should also consider the age at onset of the disease, the pattern of exacerbations, existing comorbidities, and new biomarkers linked to remodeling and inflammation (Fig. 1). In conclusion, longitudinal assessment of severe asthma clarifies the overlap and shifts in phenotypes over time. This step is essential for personalized medicine. Applying this perspective in practice and in future guidelines may help select targeted therapies and improve outcomes for patients with uncontrolled severe asthma.
