Long-term scratching analysis of mice using machine learning
Authors: Koji Kobayashi, Yusuke Miyazaki, Naoaki Sakamoto, Masahito Yamamoto, Nanae Nagata, Takahisa Murata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPruritus is a bothersome symptom that can occur at any time and is caused by a variety of physical and mental disorders. There is an urgent need to elucidate the detailed pathophysiology using animal models. Using image analysis and machine learning, we analyzed the scratching behavior of BALB/c mouse under both light and dark conditions and objectively quantified persistency, strength, and amount of pruritus. A 24-h analysis in naive mice revealed that mice scratched themselves more frequently and longer in the light period than in the dark period, and that scratching behavior in the light period was hard to extinguish. Administration of dinitrofluorobenzene (DNFB) induced persistent and biphasic scratching behavior while the vehicle only induced transient one. Of interest, DNFB-induced scratching behavior occurred even when the mice were resting. These features are revealed for the first time, highlighting the usefulness of automated long-term scratching behavior analysis.

Pruritus is a critical sensation caused by physical and psychological factors. The mechanisms underlying paroxysmal or persistent pruritus, which occurs with circadian rhythms and/or progresses alongside chronic diseases, remain unclear. We here developed an all-day scratching detection system using machine learning, enabling us to analyze mouse pruritus in home cages under physiological and pathophysiological conditions for 24 to 96 h. We found that, under resting conditions, scratching behavior is more intense and harder to extinguish during the light period compared with the dark period. Additionally, dinitrofluorobenzene-induced chronic dermatitis resulted in biphasic and persistent pruritus. This study presents a method to assess long-term mouse scratching behavior and quantify its intensity and persistence, underscoring the value of machine learning in animal behavior analysis.

Introduction

Pruritus, defined as “an unpleasant sensation that induces the desire to scratch,” is induced by various diseases, including hepatic disorders, renal disorders, and allergic diseases (1, 2). Pruritogens produced by inflammation activate peripheral sensory nerve whose signal transmits ascending and is finally perceived as “pruritus” in cerebral cortex. Mental stress also induces or exacerbates pruritus (3–5). Therefore, pruritus is under complicated control by inflammatory, neural, and phycological factors. Chronic pruritus significantly deteriorates the quality of life of the patients. Especially, nocturnal pruritus is one of the most serious problems since it deprives sleep of patients leading to reduced productivity and depression. Despite intensive studies, the mechanism of pruritus has not been fully elucidated, and its investigation and antipruritic medicine development are urgently required.

Scratching is the primary observable manifestation and indicator of pruritus in experimental animals. We do not know when, where, and how animals itch and scratch in their daily lives. In many studies, scratching observation is performed manually; besides producing subjective and unreproducible results, this is laborious and difficult in darkness. Since the long-term assessment of pruritus is rarely performed due to this restriction, the pathogenesis of paroxysmal or persistent pruritus, which occurs with circadian rhythms and/or chronic disease progression in humans, remains unelucidated. Indeed, most studies analyzing pruritus in mice observed scratching only for 30–60 min in the daytime (6, 7). Several groups developed automated methods to identify scratching behaviors by detecting electrical current induced by the rapid hind limb movement (8) or by processing mouse images captured by high-speed camera (9). However, an automated long-term scratch detection method has not been established which has water and feeding supply and is applicable for the analysis under the light/dark environment in home cage. The development of a more sophisticated scratching detection system will advance studies of pruritus and help to uncover overlooked insights.

In this study, we established an automated whole-day scratching analysis method for mice using machine learning by improving our previous method (10), and applied it to investigate qualitative and quantitative differences in physiological and pathological mouse scratching behavior.

Results

Establishment of a neural network model to detect whole-day mouse scratching

The mice spontaneously scratch without pathological stimulation. Analysis of such “physiological” scratching behavior not only helps to understand circadian patterns of pruritus but also provides basic information for the investigation of pruritus induced by mental stress or pathological stimuli. To measure it, we first established an automated scratching behavior classifier applicable to a whole-day analysis. A convolutional recurrent neural network (CRNN) was trained using a dataset of mouse behavior recorded under light and dark environments (see Document S1, Fig. S1, Tables S1 and S2 for details). Using this model, whole-day scratching behavior and locomotor activity were measured with high accuracy.

Whole-day measurement of physiological scratching behavior

The trained network model was used to investigate the 24-h scratching behavior of naive BALB/c mice. Figure 1A shows representative 1-min movements and scratching durations. Consistent with the nocturnal habit of mice, they actively moved throughout the dark period and repeated the active and resting phases during the light period. The total scratching time was longer during the light period than during the dark period. Most scratching during the light period clearly coincided with locomotor activity (Figs. 1A and S2A). A behavioral unit consisting of consecutive scratching frames was defined as a “scratching bout” (Fig. S2B). A scratching bout corresponds to a series of scratching movements, from lifting the foot from the floor to lowering it. Although the duration distribution of each bout was similar between both periods (Fig. 1B), the number and total time of scratching bouts in the light period were greater than that in the dark period (Fig. 1C and D).

Pruritus during the light period was persistent and hard to extinguish

As shown in Fig. 1E, the distribution of the interval between each scratching bout had two peaks at ∼1 s and after 10 s in both periods. Several scratching bouts occurred at short, subsecond intervals. Others occurred at longer intervals of 10 or more seconds, indicating that pruritus rarely reoccurred within ten seconds of subsiding. We defined a series of bouts whose intervals were <10 s as a “scratching episode” (Fig. S2B). An episode begins when pruritus appears and ends when it disappears, and the scratching bouts are repeated intermittently during an episode. Scratching episodes during the light period contained more bouts, with longer total time (Fig. 1F and G); pruritus in the light period required more scratching bouts to be extinguished. However, the number of scratching episodes was comparable in both periods (Fig. 1H). Scratch bouts are assumed to be events that extinguish the itching sensation with a certain transition probability p (Fig. S2C) (11). The transition probability during the light period was significantly lower (Fig. 1I). These observations demonstrate qualitative and quantitative differences in the physiological scratching behavior of mice. Although pruritus occurrence, indexed as the number of scratching episodes, is the same during both periods, it persists during light periods and is more difficult to extinguish. We confirmed these results in male mice and in a large sample size of female mice (Fig. S3).

Chemical pruritogen-induced scratching behavior is transient

We then analyzed scratching behavior induced by chemical pruritogens, which directly activate their GPCRs on itch sensory neurons, for 24 h. Vehicle (saline, 20 μL) or four pruritogens (histamine [200 μg], 5-hydroxytryptamine [5-HT, 10 µg], chloroquine [CQ, 100 µg], and lysophosphatidic acid [LPA, 200 nmole]) were injected into the nape of BALB/c mouse at 7:50 PM. Figure S4A showed the typical pattern of movement and scratching after the vehicle or pruritogen administration for 24 h. Treatment with these pruritogens did not influence mouse behavior during the total dark or light period (Fig. S4B–F). Contrarily, these pruritogens only elicited transient effects (Fig. S5A). Although histamine did not increase scratching behavior, it significantly reduced the movement after 15 min (Fig. S5B–D). Treatment with 5-HT and LPA induced transient (∼10 min) scratching without affecting movement. CQ induced a prolonged and robust scratching behavior that lasted for 30 min. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the number and duration of scratching bouts for 15 min were increased or tended to increase by the injection of these pruritogens and that CQ showing the most pronounced response (Fig. S5B–D). The treatment with 5-HT, CQ, and LPA increased both the number and duration of scratching episodes, similar to those observed with scratching bouts (Fig. S5E and F).

2,4-Dinitrofluorobenzene induced biphasic scratching behaviors

Next, we investigated pathological scratching behaviors in an atopic dermatitis mouse model. BALB/c mice were sensitized with 0.5% 2,4-dinitrofluorobenzene (DNFB) and stimulated six times with 0.2% DNFB or vehicle (4:1 acetone/olive oil, Fig. S6A). We applied vehicle or DNFB to the ears and started recording at 8:00 PM. Unexpectedly, the vehicle treatment significantly increased scratching behavior, which lasted for 6 h (Figs. S6B and 2A; third and sixth stimulations, day 0). Although the number and total time of scratching bouts after vehicle treatment significantly increased (Figs. 2B and S7A), the vehicle-induced scratching was transient and disappeared within 6 h (Fig. 2B and C). Scratching behaviors after stimulation by DNFB for three or six times peaked after 3–4 h (Figs. S7B and 2A) and increased the number and total time of scratching bouts (Figs. 2B and S7A). DNFB-induced scratching temporarily subsided and reappeared within 36 h. However, the second wave did not disappear after 96 h. We observed a significant increase in scratching behavior during the light period on day 3 (Figs. 2C and S7B).

DNFB-induced pruritus was persistent and disrupted rest

DNFB induced scratching even during the light-period resting phase (arrowheads in Figs. S6B and 2D), whereas scratching in vehicle-treated mice coincided with their locomotor activity like that in physiological mice. DNFB stimulation increased the number of bouts in an episode compared with the vehicle (Fig. 2E and F), thus indicating the increased persistence of DNFB-induced pruritus. The number and duration of scratching episodes exhibited trends similar to those of scratching bouts (Fig. S7C–F). Specifically, vehicle treatment led to a transient increase in scratching episodes only on day 0, whereas DNFB induced significant and prolonged increases in scratching episodes. Repeated stimulation with both vehicle and DNFB significantly decreased the total movement of mice during the dark and light periods (Fig. S8A and B); however, DNFB-treated mice exhibited less locomotor activity, probably because of dermatitis pathology. Transition probability also decreased with stimulation in both vehicle- and DNFB-treated mice, but DNFB stimulation further decreased transition probability compared with that in the vehicle treatment (Fig. S8C and D).

DNFB induced short and repetitive scratching

We finally assessed the duration distribution of each scratch bout. In the vehicle-treated mice, scratch bouts lasted for <0.5 s accounted for around 80% of the total bouts, whereas DNFB stimulation increased the proportion of short scratch bouts (Fig. S9). Tissue injury accompanied by dermatitis may induce pain, resulting in the early cessation of each bout. Collectively, we revealed that dermatitis-associated pathological pruritus induces long-lasting scratching; it occurs during the resting phase in mice in the absence of physiological pruritus and is persistent and difficult to extinguish. These features can mimic human pathology, especially “nocturnal pruritus” in human patients (12).

Discussion

We here established an automated scratching detection method for 24-h analysis. It was proved to be applicable to assess the scratching behavior of resting and pathological mouse and to be helpful to find novel features. Indeed, we found the qualitative and quantitative difference in the scratching pattern during dark/light periods. In addition, pathological pruritus persisted and disrupted mouse's rest mimicking human symptoms.

There are several automated scratching behavior detection methods that measure induction current, sounds, and movement (8, 9, 13, 14). Although these systems exhibited sufficient accuracy, they often use specially designed cages for measurement which require habituation and can affect the behavior of mice. More importantly, studies using these systems usually identify the scratching bouts lasting for at least 150 ms, while shorter bouts are ignored (11, 15–17). In this study, we could detect scratching bouts with >60 ms, and the prediction was highly correlated with the human observation. We also revealed that the duration of scratching bouts peaked at ∼300 ms and that scratching bouts with <150 ms accounted for a certain proportion (Fig. 1B). Although we cannot directly compare our results with those of other systems, our system is likely to detect shorter scratching bouts that may have been overlooked in previous studies.

There is little information for the circadian scratching pattern in experimental animals, probably due to the difficulty of long-term analysis. Several studies analyzed 24-h scratching behavior in BALB/c or NC/Nga mice using automated systems (15, 18). Although they did not focus on the circadian change nor show qualitative results about the scratching behavior in dark/light period, their results showed a trend that there were more scratching bouts in the dark period than in the light period. Recently, Honda et al. (11) qualitatively measured the 24-h scratching behavior in naive C57BL/6J mice and reported that mice scratched themselves more frequently in the dark period than the light period. Despite these previous studies, we here clearly showed that the number and duration of scratching bouts of naive BALB/c mice were significantly increased in the light period. Interestingly, the number of episodes was equivalent or smaller (Figs. 1H and S3G) in the light period, whereas a scratching episode in the light period contained much more scratching bouts than that in the dark period, indicating that the pruritus in the light period persists and is hard to extinguish. In addition, we also revealed the distinct scratching pattern of naive mice between dark and light periods; scratching in the dark period was sporadic and that in the light period coincided with the spontaneous activity. Since mice were nocturnal and inactive in the light period, these results are consistent with the human symptom where we suffer from itchiness at night. Although we could not provide mechanistic explanation for these phenomena at this moment, we assume that the circadian regulation of excitatory/inhibitory neurons involved in the itch sensation and transmission causes these differences, which were known in the pain regulation (19). In addition, we here interpreted spontaneous scratching behavior in naive mice as an indicator of physiological pruritus. However, the underlying mechanisms, including the stimuli and neural circuits involved, remain unclear, although we suggested the possibility that physiological scratching is independent of gastrin-releasing peptide receptor-expressing neurons, which are recognized as essential mediators of pathological pruritus (see Document S2 and Fig. S10). Further investigation is needed to determine whether this behavior genuinely reflects pruritic responses.

Atopic dermatitis is the major cause of chronic pruritus, and its animal model has been used for the pathogenesis investigation and drug development. DNFB-induced dermatitis is a popular atopic dermatitis model. They induce skin inflammation with pruritus, eschar, and skin thickening, which partially mimics human symptoms (20). Pruritus in dermatitis mice was commonly assessed by manual observation, where one counted scratching behavior for ∼30 min after stimulation (21, 22). In the present study, DNFB induced pruritus, which peaked at 4 h after stimulation and persisted for several days, whereas the vehicle induced transient but noticeable scratching behavior for 1 h. These results imply that pruritus immediately after reagent stimulation only reflects the response to the vehicle but not skin inflammation. We suggested that a scratching observation should be performed several hours after stimulation to correctly investigate dermatitis-related pruritus. Interestingly, although locomotor activity and scratching behavior in the light period were mostly synchronized in the resting and vehicle-treated mice, dermatitis induction disrupted this synchronization. Indeed, mice with dermatitis scratched themselves when they were inactive (Fig. 2D). A lot of human patients suffer from sleep deprivation due to the nocturnal pruritus. Our method of simultaneously measuring locomotor activity and scratching behavior will help to investigate the relationship between sleep and pruritus.

We further analyzed two additional chronic itch models: MC903-induced atopic dermatitis and imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis (Documents S3 and S4). Repeated treatment with MC903 or IMQ treatment elicited long-lasting and persistent scratching behavior, similar to DNFB treatment, and it occurred in the resting phase during the light period (Figs. S11 and S12). A single chemical pruritogen administration that directly activates itch sensory neurons induces only transient scratching without affecting its quality (Figs. S4 and S5). On the other hand, pruritus in chronic dermatitis induced by DNFB, MC903, and IMQ was quantitatively and qualitatively altered compared with that under physiological conditions. These results highlight the differences in extrapolability and pathology across animal models and the importance of choosing an appropriate model for research purposes.

Conclusion

In this study, we established a method for analyzing whole-day pruritus in mice, using which, we objectively defined terms (i.e. bouts, intervals, and episodes) and performed quantitative and qualitative analyses. Under physiological conditions, scratching behavior during the light period occurred when mice were active and was difficult to extinguish, whereas pruritus during the dark was sporadic and easily disappeared. Pathological pruritus was more persistent and severe, and it occurred even when the mice were at rest. To the best of our knowledge, these features are revealed for the first time, highlighting the usefulness of automated scratching behavior analysis.

Materials and methods

Animals

BALB/c mice (8–20 weeks old, male and female, n = 87) were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). All experiments were approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Tokyo (P19-079, P24-011, P24-127). Animal care and treatments were performed in accordance with the guidelines outlined within the Guide to Animal Use and Care of the University of Tokyo.

Video recording

For videos used in neural network training and evaluation, mice were placed into black cages (width: 30 cm, length: 20 cm, depth: 60 cm) and their behavior was recorded by video camera (HXR-NX80, Sony Corporation, Japan) set at a height of 110 cm above the cage for 60 min. Two mice, each in separate cages, were recorded simultaneously. Recorded data were saved in SD cards (SDSQXAV-256G, SanDisk, United States). We illuminated the cages with LED light (150 lux) during the light period and with low ambient light and infrared light (2 lux) during the dark period. We turned on the infrared filter of video cameras (normal mode) during the light period and turned it off (night shoot mode) during dark period (Fig. S1A). Other detailed recording conditions were as follows: frame rate: 60 Hz, resolution: 1,920 × 1,080 pixel. Nyquist frequency of recording was 30 Hz which was higher enough than the scratching stroke frequency (≤25 Hz) (23).

For 24-h recording, we put water gel (Sankyo Labo Service Corporation, Inc., Tokyo, Japan), diet pellets, and black paper bedding on the floor of cages. Mice behavior was recorded same as above. A 12-h light/dark cycle (light period: 8:00 AM to 8:00 PM, dark period: 8:00 PM to 8:00 AM) was used. We continuously recorded mouse behavior for 12 h (i.e. a full light or dark period). Each ∼160 GB video file was temporarily stored on an SD card with 256 GB capacity. At the time we switched the illumination, recording was stopped, and SD cards were replaced. Recorded video files were transferred to 8 TB-external hard disk drives (HD-LE8U3-BB, Buffalo Inc., Japan) and stored in them.

5-Hydroxytryptamine-induced scratching for neural network training and evaluation

In order to efficiently elicit scratching behavior, 5-HT (10 μg in 10 μL saline, Sigma-Aldrich, United States) was intradermally injected into the nape of BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old, male n = 5, and female n = 4), and their behavior was recorded. Original 60-min videos were split into 10-min small files, and we finally selected 59 (29 light period and 30 dark period) videos in which mice scratched themselves >10 times (Tables S3–S5).

Image preprocessing, integration, and dataset preparation

All videos were split into frame images and preprocessed, as previously described (10). Briefly, we calculated differential images between two continuous frames that were then gray scaled, binarized, denoized, and cropped around the geometric center of the mouse calculated according to our previous study. For a frame at time t, we grouped 21 preprocessed images from t − 10 to t + 10, which was named as the “segment” at time t.

We carefully observed 59 videos and annotated in which frame mice scratched themselves. Scratching frames were labeled as “1” and nonscratching frames were labeled as “0.” Fifty-nine video files were split into 30 training (dark: 15, light: 15), five validation (dark: three, light: two), and 24 test (dark: 12, light: 12) datasets (Tables S3–S5). We note that all datasets contained dark/light recording and male/female mice. The training dataset was used for neural network training, and the validation dataset was used for the performance evaluation of trained neural network. After we chose the best model, its performance for completely first-look data was evaluated using the test dataset.

Training and prediction of neural network

In this study, we used a neural network that was composed of a convolutional neural network (CNN), recurrent neural network (RNN), and fully connected (FC) block improving our previous study (Fig. S1B) (10). CNN block has six repeated convolutional layers, batch normalization layers, and max pooling layers. RNN block has two long short-term memory layers. FC block has three FC layers with dropout layers. The activation functions were ReLU except for the last layer, where sigmoid function was used. Detailed architecture of these models was shown in Table S6. We randomly selected 12,800 segments (batch size: 64, batches per epoch: 200) from the training dataset for one epoch. Data augmentation and training were performed as in our previous studies (10, 24). For prediction, each segment was input into the trained neural network without any data augmentation. The outputs of the neural network were interpreted as the scratching probability, and we identified a scratching segment when the prediction output was >0.5. In the analysis of 24 h video files, we transferred Python-written scripts for preprocessing and neural network calculation into C++-written ones using OpenCV and LibTorch library to shorten the processing time. It allowed us to process original video files at 3–4× speed (i.e. 4–3 h for a 12-h video file) with our desktop PC, which was fast enough for practical use.

Postprediction filter and quantification of scratching behavior

A series of frames that were predicted as “scratching” was defined as a “bout” and a series of frames between bouts was defined as an “interval” of bouts (Fig. S2B). The length of a bout was referred to as its “duration.” In order to exclude unlikely short scratching bouts and intervals in predicted results, we set postprediction filter as follows. First, we filled in too short (<70 ms) intervals and then omitted too short (<60 ms) bouts. We finally defined a series of bouts that were intermittently continued at <10 s intervals as an “episode” (Fig. S2B). The number, duration, and total time of scratch bouts/episodes were shown as results. In addition, we defined a minute where moving distance of mouse was <200 pixels as “resting phase” and calculated the ratio of scratching behavior occurred in resting phase.

The analysis of physiological scratching

We recorded the behavior of naive BALB/c mice (female: n = 4, 10 weeks old, male: n = 7, 9 weeks old) from 8:00 PM to next 8:00 PM for 24 h. Recorded videos were analyzed as described above. In addition, we analyzed the pretreatment data from DNFB- and MC903-induced dermatitis experiments, which are entirely untreated naive mice, as expanded female analysis (n = 43 or 44, Fig. S3, right panels).

Spontaneous locomotor activity measurement

The coordinates of mouse geometric center were recorded every 1 s. The Euclidean distance between two consecutive seconds was defined as the moved distance. This moved distance was summed up every 1 min or during total dark/light periods and shown as the “movement.”

The analysis of repetitive scratching

The analysis of repetitive scratching was performed according to the probabilistic model proposed by Honda et al. (11). In this model, a scratching bout is assumed as an event that can extinguish the itch sensation with a probability p, and mice continue scratching until their pruritus disappears (Fig. S2C). In other words, when a mouse scratches itself K times in one episode (i.e. K times bouts in one episode), it failed K − 1 times to extinguish pruritus and finally succeeded on the Kth try. Chemical reagent-induced acute scratching model

Histamine (His, 100 μg, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Japan), 5-HT (10 μg), CQ (200 μg, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), LPA (200 nmole, Avanti Polar Lipids, United States), or vehicle (saline, 20 μL) was intradermally injected into the nape of BALB/c mice (8–15 weeks old, female, n = 20) at 7:50 PM. After injection, their behavior was recorded from 8:00 PM to next 8:00 PM for 24 h as described above. One mouse was used for two experiments after an interval of two or more weeks.

DNFB-induced dermatitis model

For sensitization, DNFB (0.5%, 25 μL in 4:1 acetone/olive oil solution, Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) was applied to the shaved abdominal skin of BALB/c (8 weeks old, female) mouse on experimental day 0 (E0). For stimulation, vehicle (25 μL in 4:1 acetone/olive oil) or 0.2% DNFB (25 μL) was applied to both ears on E4, E7, E10, E13, E16, and E19 (Fig. S6A). All DNFB applications were performed at 7:50 PM We recorded the behavior of vehicle (sensitization + vehicle stimulation, n = 5–18), and DNFB (sensitization + DNFB stimulation, n = 6–18)-treated mice before sensitization (pre), 0–48 h after third stimulation (day 0–1), and 0–96 h after sixth stimulation (day 0–3, Fig. S6A).

Intrathecal administration of bombesin-saporin

Bombesin-saporin (SAP) or blank-SAP (400 ng in 5 μL phosphate-buffered saline) was intrathecally administrated to BALB/c mice (8 weeks old, female, n = 4 each) according to a previous study (25). Two weeks after, their behavior was recorded from 8:00 PM to next 8:00 PM for 24 h as described above. In addition, CQ (200 μg) was intradermally injected into the nape of these mice, and scratching behavior was analyzed for 30 min.

MC903-induced dermatitis model

MC903 (4 nmole in 20 μL ethanol, ChemScene, United States, n = 6) or vehicle (ethanol, 20 μL, n = 6) was applied to both ears of BALB/c mice (20 weeks old, female) every other day for 18 days. MC903 application was performed at 7:50 PM. We recorded the behavior of the mouse before the experiment (pre) and after the last stimulation (day 18) from 8:00 PM to next 8:00 PM for 24 h.

IMQ-induced dermatitis model

IMQ (Beselna cream 5%, 25 μg/ear, Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Japan) was applied to both ears of BALB/c mice (female, 21 weeks old, n = 8) every day for 5 days. IMQ application was performed at 7:50 PM. We recorded the mouse behavior before experiment (pre) and after the last stimulation (IMQ fifth) from 8:00 PM to next 8:00 PM for 24 h.

Statistical analysis

The results of experiments were represented individually or as mean ± SEM. Statistical evaluation of the data was performed by unpaired Student's t test for comparison between two groups. Comparison of multiple groups was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test. The value of P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Software and hardware

Trainings of neural networks were conducted on AI-Compliant Advanced Computer System at the information initiative center, Hokkaido University (Sapporo, Japan) equipped with NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU. Predictions and other processes were conducted on Desktop computers with NVIDIA GeForce RTX1080Ti, RTX3090, or RTX4090. Image preprocessing was conducted with OpenCV library in Python or C++. Neural network training and prediction were conducted using Pytorch library in Python or LibTorch library in C++.
