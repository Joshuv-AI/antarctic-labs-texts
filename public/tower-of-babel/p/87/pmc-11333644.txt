From Triple- to Penta-Exposed Multiple Myeloma: A Real-World Study in a Medicare Population
Authors: Thomas E Delea, Qiufei Ma, Glenn S Kroog, Wenzhen Ge, Aaron Moynahan, Natalia Sabater Anaya, Karen Rodriguez Lorenc, Xue Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTreatment of multiple myeloma (MM) has been transformed by novel therapies, including CD38 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), and proteasome inhibitors (PIs), resulting in increasing numbers of patients who are triple-class exposed (TCE; exposed to ≥ 1 drug in each class). Many patients are penta-exposed (PE; ≥ 2 IMiDs, ≥ 2 PIs, and a CD38 mAb), some are triple-class refractory (TCR), and some are PE and TCR (PE-TCR). Data on real-world outcomes in elderly patients with MM across this spectrum of exposure are limited.MethodsData from the Medicare Chronic Conditions Warehouse Database from November 2006–December 2020 were used to examine cohorts of TCE, TCR, PE, and PE-TCR patients. Outcomes were assessed from the start of the index line of therapy (LOT), defined as the first LOT after becoming TCE or PE.ResultsA total of 2830 TCE, 1371 TCR, 1121 PE, and 774 PE-TCR patients were identified. Pomalidomide was the most frequently used medication for the index LOT in all cohorts (32.6% [PE-TCR] to 43.3% [TCR]). The most frequently used regimens for the index LOT were pomalidomide plus daratumumab for TCE (17.2%) and PE (7.0%), pomalidomide plus carfilzomib for TCR (10.3%), and pomalidomide plus elotuzumab for PE-TCR (7.4%). Median time to discontinuation (TTD) ranged from 4.2 (PE-TCR) to 6.9 (TCE) months, and overall survival (OS) ranged from 13.0 (TCR) to 15.9 (PE) months. Healthcare resource utilization (HRU) was lowest for TCE and highest for PE-TCR patients. Mean monthly healthcare costs (HCC) ranged from $23,091 (TCE) to $24,412 (PE-TCR). MM medications represented 66.2% (PE-TCR) to 72.8% (TCE) of costs.ConclusionsIn this study across a spectrum of Medicare TCE patients, there was heterogeneity in treatment regimens, suggesting no standard of care. TTD and OS were short, and HRU and HCC were high. These results underscore the potential for new therapies in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-024-00291-6.Keywords: Healthcare costs, Healthcare resource utilization, Medicare, Multiple myeloma, Real-world evidence, Relapsed or refractory, Survival, Treatment patterns, Triple-class exposure

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Given the advances in the treatment of patients with multiple myeloma (MM), patients who are triple-class exposed (TCE) represent a growing subgroup of patients with relapsed or refractory MM (RRMM).Although several studies have examined treatment patterns, survival, healthcare resource utilization (HRU), and healthcare costs (HCC) for patients with TCE RRMM, these studies are largely limited to non-elderly commercially insured patients, which may not be reflective of most patients with RRMM in the USA, who are most likely to be elderly and Medicare-insured.This study examined treatment patterns, time to discontinuation (TTD), time to next treatment (TTNT), overall survival (OS), HRU, and HCC (2021 US dollars) in largely elderly Medicare-insured patients with RRMM who were TCE, triple-class refractory (TCR), penta-exposed (PE), or both PE and TCR (PE-TCR), using data through December 2020 from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Conditions Warehouse Database.What was learned from the study?Among 2830 TCE, 1371 TCR, 1121 PE, and 774 PE-TCR patients, the most frequently used regimens were pomalidomide plus daratumumab for TCE (17.2%) and PE (7.0%), pomalidomide plus carfilzomib for TCR (10.3%), and pomalidomide plus elotuzumab for PE-TCR (7.4%). Median Kaplan‒Meier TTD ranged from 4.2 (PE-TCR) to 6.9 (TCE) months, median TTNT ranged from 4.1 (PE-TCR, N = 427) to 5.2 (3.3, 9.2; TCE, N = 1346) months, median Kaplan‒Meier OS ranged from 13.0 (TCR) to 15.9 (PE) months, and mean monthly HCC ranged from $23,091 (TCE) to $24,412 (PE-TCR).In this real-world study of largely elderly Medicare fee-for-service TCE patients, there was a wide distribution of treatment regimens across these patients, suggesting no standard of care treatment. With the medications available at the time of the study, TTD, TTNT, and OS were relatively short, and HRU and costs were high, underscoring the potential for novel therapies with new mechanisms of action, such as bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies, that may improve disease control and increase progression-free and overall survival in this growing population.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a disease of plasma cells associated with a variety of complications, including, but not limited to, anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, bone loss and fractures, and kidney disease [1]. In the USA, approximately 36,000 new cases of MM are diagnosed, and approximately 13,000 people die from the disease each year [2]. The 5-year survival rate for patients diagnosed with MM is approximately 60% [2].

Through 2019, systemic treatments for MM include immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), such as lenalidomide, pomalidomide, and thalidomide; proteasome inhibitors (PIs), such as bortezomib, carfilzomib, and ixazomib; anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), such as daratumumab and isatuximab; anti-SLAMF7 mAbs, such as elotuzumab; selective inhibitor of nuclear export, such as selinexor; and chemotherapies [3, 4]. Thereafter, several novel treatments have been approved for use in patients with MM in the USA, including belantamab mafodotin, an antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) that was subsequently withdrawn from the market; the bispecific antibodies teclistamab and elranatamab, which target BCMA, and talquetamab, which targets G protein–coupled receptor class C group 5 member D (GPRC5D); and the BCMA-targeting chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies (CAR Ts) ciltacabtagene autoleucel and idecabtagene vicleucel [3, 4]. Other novel therapies are in development, including ADCs, cereblon E3 ligase modulator drugs (CELMoDs), immune fusion proteins (IFPs), and other bispecific antibodies [5].

For patients with symptomatic MM, initial treatment typically involves stem-cell therapy (SCT) for eligible patients, plus a combination of a PI, an IMiD, and a corticosteroid (typically, dexamethasone) [6]. Until the recent approval of BCMA- and GPRC5D-targeted immunotherapies, subsequent treatment after patients became refractory to or relapsed on their initial therapy often included different combinations of the aforementioned classes of therapies, and was determined by response to and tolerance of previous treatment, and eligibility for SCT [7]. Duration of response and overall survival (OS) from the start of each treatment decreases as patients progress and are exposed to an increasing number of medications and drug classes [8]. Life expectancy is particularly limited after patients become refractory to anti-CD38 mAbs [9–11].

Although advances in treatment have resulted in improved survival [12], they have also resulted in increasing numbers of triple-class exposed (TCE) patients, defined as patients who have received one or more IMiDs, one or more PIs, and one or more anti-CD38 mAbs [1]. Many TCE patients may be exposed to five or more drugs in these classes (penta-exposed [PE]), defined as having received two or more IMiDs, two or more PIs, and one or more anti-CD38 mAbs [13]. Some patients may be refractory to one or more drug(s) in each class (triple-class refractory [TCR]), and some are both PE and TCR (PE-TCR). Given the relatively poor outcomes for heavily pretreated patients with relapsed or refractory MM (RRMM) receiving conventional therapies, there is great potential for new treatments with different mechanisms of action, such as the BCMA- and GPRC5D-targeted immunotherapies, that may improve disease control and increase progression-free and overall survival in this growing patient population.

Evaluations of the potential value of novel treatments for RRMM require an understanding of the patterns of treatment, outcomes, and healthcare resource utilization (HRU), and costs for patients who are the potential recipients of such therapy. Published data on real-world outcomes for the spectrum of heavily pretreated, advanced patients with RRMM in the USA are largely limited to commercially insured patients, who tend to be non-elderly or young elderly, and therefore may not be representative of all patients with RRMM, the majority of whom are elderly and are therefore Medicare-insured [1, 14, 15]. Data for elderly Medicare fee-for-service (FFS)-insured patients are limited to TCE patients [13], and do not describe outcomes for those who are TCR, PE, or PE-TCR. As RRMM is largely a disease of the elderly [16], understanding outcomes for the spectrum of patients with advanced RRMM is particularly important to inform healthcare policy decision-making. This study sought to assess, for a spectrum of heavily pretreated patients with RRMM seen in real-world clinical practice, the following questions:

Methods

Data Source

Data for the study were obtained from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Chronic Conditions Warehouse (“the database”). The database includes demographic, enrollment, and claims information on all persons enrolled in Medicare FFS plans (i.e., Medicare Parts A, B, and D) in the USA. Information available for each person in the database includes age, sex, race, location, and dates of enrollment. Data available for each outpatient pharmacy claim (prescription filled) include the drug dispensed (in National Drug Code format), the dispensing date, and the quantity and number of therapy-days dispensed. Data available for each facility or professional service claim (“medical claim”) include dates of service and diagnosis, and procedure codes (in International Classification of Diseases, 9th Edition, Clinical Modification [ICD-9-CM] or 10th Edition, Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] format). Professional service claims also include procedure codes (typically in Current Procedural Terminology, Version 4 and Health Care Financing Common Procedural Coding System formats). All claims in the database include information on paid amounts (including copays, coinsurance, and deductibles). For each person in the database, claim-level data may be arrayed in chronological order to provide a detailed, longitudinal profile of all medical and pharmacy services received. A custom subset of the databases was provided to the investigators by CMS that consisted of all available data from the database for all patients who had one or more claims with a diagnosis of MM (ICD-9-CM code 203.0x or ICD-10-CM code C90.0x) from 2007 to 2020. Claims for these patients spanned the period from November 14, 2006, to December 31, 2020 (the “study period”), and therefore reflect treatment patterns prior to the use of BCMA- and GPRC5D-targeted immunotherapies.

Use of the data was governed by a Data Use Agreement (DUA) from CMS (DUA number: RSCH-2022-58036). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Advarra institutional review board (IRB Organization number [IORG]: 0000635. Protocol number: Pro00057753). This study is in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. This study has also been reviewed and approved by an ethics committee as part of the IRB review conducted by Advarra. Given this study used deidentified, retrospectively captured clinical data, participant consent was not required. The IRB granted a Waiver of Consent for this study. No identifiable information is included in the manuscript.

Study Design

An incident user cohort design was used (Fig. 1) [17]. A total of four study cohorts were examined: the TCE, TCR, PE, and PE-TCR cohorts. The PE and TCR cohorts were subsets of the TCE cohort, and the PE-TCR cohort was a subset of the PE and TCR cohorts. To identify the cohorts, patients with a diagnosis of MM during the study period were first selected from the database. The date of the first diagnosis of MM was designated the “MM diagnosis date.” The period from the MM diagnosis date to the end of the study period was partitioned into lines of therapy (LOTs) based on medical and pharmacy claims for MM medications, using an adaptation of a published algorithm that had undergone extensive review and refinement by clinical experts [18, 19]. For the TCE and PE cohorts, the start date of the first LOT that qualified the patient as TCE or PE was defined as the “TCE date” or the “PE date,” respectively. For both cohorts, the first LOT after becoming TCE or PE was defined as the “index LOT,” and the start date of the index LOT was defined as the “index date.”

For all patients, the “pre-index period” was defined as the 180-day period prior to the index date, and the “follow-up period” was defined as the period from the index date to the end of continuous enrollment, death, or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first. Demographic and enrollment characteristics were assessed at the index date, and comorbidities were assessed during the pre-index period. Outcomes were assessed during the follow-up period and included MM medications and treatment regimens received for the index LOT, time to discontinuation (TTD) and TTNT for the index LOT, HRU, and HCC from the index date to the end of follow-up, and OS from the index date. Diagnosis and procedure codes used in the study are listed in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S1–S25).

For both the TCE and PE cohorts, subgroups of patients who were also TCR at the TCE and PE date, respectively, were identified. The “TCR cohort” included all TCE patients who were TCR as of the TCE date. The “PE-TCR cohort” included all patients who were TCR as of the PE date. Patients who were TCE or PE and became TCR after the TCE or PE date, respectively, were not included in the TCR or PE-TCR cohorts. The index dates used for analyses of the TCR and PE-TCR cohorts were the same as those used for the patients in analyses of the TCE and PE cohorts, respectively. TCE and PE patients were considered TCR if they were refractory to one or more regimens including a PI, one or more regimens including an IMiD, and one or more regimens including an anti-CD38 mAb. Patients were considered refractory to any regimen if the period between the last claim or days supplied for any drug in the regimen and the start of the subsequent regimen was < 60 days, and none of the drugs in the regimen were present in the next regimen [20].

LOTs

LOTs were assessed by first identifying all claims for MM therapies on or after the MM diagnosis date and sorting the claims chronologically. MM therapies included IMiDs (thalidomide, lenalidomide, pomalidomide), PIs (bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib), anti-CD38 mAbs (daratumumab and isatuximab), other mAbs (elotuzumab), histone deacetylase inhibitors (panobinostat), other MM therapies, and SCTs. For simplicity, claims for corticosteroids (dexamethasone or prednisone) were not considered in the identification of LOTs. The first date with a claim for any MM therapy following the MM diagnosis date was defined as the initiation date for the first LOT (LOT1). The 60-day period beginning with the LOT1 initiation date was defined as the “treatment identification period” for LOT1. All medications received during the LOT1 treatment identification period were assumed to constitute the LOT1 regimen, except for the maintenance therapy described below. The start date for the second LOT was then identified based on the earliest (a) date of the first claim for a new MM therapy that was not among the MM medications constituting the LOT1 regimen (excluding lenalidomide maintenance therapy), or (b) date of the first claim for any MM therapy occurring > 180 days after the last claim or days supplied for any of the medications in the LOT1 regimen (i.e., a gap of 180 days in treatment was assumed to constitute the end of the LOT). Lenalidomide maintenance was identified when (a) the first claim for a new MM therapy after the LOT1 treatment identification period was lenalidomide, (b) the first such claim was ≤ 60 days after the most recent prior claim for a medication in the regimen, and (c) there were no claims for any other MM therapy in the 60-day period beginning with this lenalidomide claim. The start and end dates for subsequent LOTs were identified using the same approach as described above, except for maintenance therapy and any autologous SCT (ASCT) received after LOT1, which was considered part of the regimen.

Baseline Characteristics

For each patient, age (in years), sex, and race (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black, other, or unknown) were described. The year of index date was recorded. The prevalence of selected comorbidities (anemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic liver disease, chronic pain, chronic kidney disease [CKD], coronary heart disease, dementia, diabetes, and hypertension) were assessed based on diagnosis and procedure codes on medical claims during the 6-month pre-index period. The National Cancer Institute Modification of Charlson Comorbidity Index (NCI-CCI) was also calculated based on claims during the 6-month pre-index period [21]. Prior exposure to MM medications and ASCT, the number of prior LOTs for MM, and the number of months between the initial MM diagnosis and the index LOT were assessed for each patient.

Outcomes

For each patient, MM treatment regimens received for the index LOT were assessed. TTNT for the index LOT was defined as the number of days from the index date to the date of initiation of the next LOT. TTD for the index LOT was defined as the number of days from the index date to the last day of therapy for the LOT. The last day of therapy for the LOT was defined as (1) the day of the last claim or days supplied for a regimen medication occurring before the first day of the next LOT, or (2) the last claim or days supplied for a regimen medication preceding a gap of 180 days without a claim, or days supplied for a regimen medication. OS from the index date was assessed using date of death information in the database, based on information from the Medicare claims, the Social Security Administration, supplemented with online date of death edits submitted by family members, and other sources [22]. Over 99% of death dates are validated [22].

For each patient, measures of HRU were calculated over the follow-up period and included the number of prescriptions, the number of physicians’ office or hospital outpatient visits, the number of emergency department visits, the number of hospitalizations, and the number of inpatient days.

For each patient, MM medication costs, other MM-related costs, non-MM-related costs, and total all-cause costs were calculated over the follow-up period. Costs of administration were included in the costs of MM medications and were identified based on claims with procedure codes for chemotherapy administration for which there was a claim for an MM medication on the same date. Other MM-related costs included (a) costs of inpatient claims with a diagnosis of MM in any position or an MM-related adverse event (AE) or complication in the primary position and (b) costs of outpatient claims with an MM diagnosis in any position. MM-related AEs and complications were identified based on published studies derived from MM treatment guidelines (complications) and published prescribing information [19]. Costs were estimated based on reimbursed amounts and adjusted to 2021 US dollars [23].

Analysis

The number and percentage of patients meeting each of the inclusion and exclusion criteria were reported in an attrition table. Descriptive statistics on patient characteristics were reported for each cohort. Binary and categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. Means and standard deviations (SDs) were reported for continuous measures. For each cohort, treatment regimens were alternately classified based on specific medications received (e.g., pomalidomide, carfilzomib, daratumumab) and medication classes (e.g., IMiDs, PIs, anti-CD38 mAbs). TTD and OS were analyzed using Kaplan‒Meier (KM) methods. For TTD and OS, patients with missing values were censored at the end of the follow-up period for each patient. Descriptive statistics were calculated for TTNT for patients who received an LOT after the index LOT. TTNT was also analyzed using cumulative incidence methods, with death as a competing risk. Means and SDs were reported for all measures of HRU and HCC. HRU and HCC measures were reported on a per-patient-per-month (PPPM) basis to account for variable lengths of follow-up across patients. When calculating PPPM estimates, patients were weighted by the number of months of follow-up to ensure that the average PPPM estimates would equal the sum of the units of HRU or HCC over all patients divided by the corresponding sum of the months of follow-up. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for time to event outcomes. Consistent with the CMS DUA, numbers and descriptive statistics for cells with N < 11 were redacted and reported as “N < 11.”

Results

Study Population

A total of 137,707 adult patients were identified with a confirmed diagnosis of MM (Table 1). Of these, 16,720 were TCE patients, including 7494 who were continuously enrolled from 6 months prior to the MM diagnosis date to the TCE date. Among the 7494 patients, only 3124 (41.7%) had another LOT after the TCE date. After excluding patients with prior allogeneic SCT, and those with < 30 days of follow-up after the index date, the resulting TCE population comprised 2830 patients, of whom 1371 were TCR, 1121 were PE, and 774 were PE-TCR, respectively. The mean (SD) duration of follow-up ranged from 10.1 (9.3) months for PE-TCR patients to 12.4 (10.6) months for TCE patients.

Baseline Characteristics

The mean age was approximately 76 years in all cohorts; approximately 95% of patients in all cohorts were aged ≥ 65 years (Table 2). Approximately one-half of all patients in all cohorts were female. In all cohorts, approximately 85% of patients were non-Hispanic white, and 9–10% were non-Hispanic Black. The annual number of new TCE patients increased over time by almost eightfold, from 114 in 2016 to 890 in 2020. Similar increases over time were seen for the other cohorts. The most frequent comorbidity was hypertension, observed in approximately 70% of patients. It is possible that some occurrences of CKD, as well as other comorbid conditions such as bone pain and anemia, are manifestations of MM. The mean NCI-CCI varied from 1.57 for TCR patients to 1.71 for PE-TCR patients. The mean (median) time from MM diagnosis to the index date ranged from 44.3 (38.9) months for TCR patients to 55.0 (50.0) months for PE patients. The percentage of patients with a previous ASCT varied from 20.6% (TCR) to 29.3% (PE). Over 96% of patients in all cohorts had received prior treatment with lenalidomide, and a similar proportion had received prior treatment with bortezomib. A total of 42.9% of TCE patients and 53.0% of TCR patients had received pomalidomide. Over 95% of PE and PE-TCR patients had received previous treatment with pomalidomide. All patients had received prior treatment with daratumumab. Across the four cohorts, from 43.3% (TCE) to 57.3% (PE) of patients had received prior treatment with chemotherapies, most frequently, cyclophosphamide. The mean number of previous LOTs received ranged from 3.3 for TCE patients to 4.5 for PE and PE-TCR patients.

Discussion

In this large real-world study examining treatment patterns, HRU, HCC, and survival for Medicare patients with relatively advanced RRMM, including TCE, TCR, PE, and PE-TCR cohorts, the five most frequently used treatment regimens were used in less than half of all patients in each cohort. The most frequently received treatment regimen based on drug class was an IMiD plus an anti-CD38 mAb for TCE, an IMiD plus a PI for TCR, and a PI plus another class for PE and PE-TCR. For all four cohorts, the most frequently used medication was pomalidomide. Median TTD ranged from 4.2 to 6.9 months across the cohorts. Among patients who received a subsequent LOT, median TTNT ranged from 4.1 to 5.2 months. Median OS ranged from 13.0 to 15.9 months, and total HCC ranged from $23,091 to $24,412 PPPM. In all four cohorts, MM medications represented most HCC. These results suggest that despite increasing numbers of TCE, TCR, PE, and PE-TCR patients at the time of the study, there was no single standard of care treatment, and that there was a high degree of repeat usage of IMiDs, PIs, and anti-CD38 mAbs, even among patients who had received multiple courses of, or were refractory to, these classes of medications. With the medications available at the time of the study, TTD, TTNT, and OS were short, and HRU and HCC were high. These results underscore the great potential for emerging therapies with new mechanisms of action that may improve disease control and increase progression-free and overall survival, with a manageable safety profile and absence of cumulative toxicity in order to reduce patient disease and health system financial burden.

As the prevalence of patients with RRMM with exposure to multiple treatment regimens increases, data on treatment patterns, HRU, HCC, and survival for heavily pretreated patients with RRMM become increasingly important for healthcare decision-making. Although several retrospective studies have evaluated these outcomes for TCE patients in the USA, these studies have largely focused on non-elderly, commercially insured patients [1, 14, 15]. Currently, published data on these outcomes for elderly, Medicare FFS-insured patients are limited to a single study of TCE patients by Hlavacek et al., who used Medicare data through December 31, 2019 [13]. Although the methods of the two studies are generally similar, the results of this study add to the existing literature by providing a more detailed analysis of TCE patients and subpopulations of TCE including TCR, PE, and PE-TCR, and using more recent data through December 31, 2020, yielding a 50% increase in sample size, a 33% increase in potential follow-up, and a population that may be more representative of current treatment practices.

The results of this study validate and extend those of prior evaluations. This study confirms prior studies in commercially and Medicare-insured patients by demonstrating substantial heterogeneity in treatments received by TCE patients, with no clear standard of care treatment. As with other studies, pomalidomide was the most frequently used medication, and an IMiD plus an anti-CD38 mAb was the most frequently used treatment regimen. Median TTD for TCE patients in this study (6.9 months) was greater than that reported for other studies, including the study of Medicare patients by Hlavacek et al. (5.4 months) [13], and a study of largely commercially insured patients by Madduri et al. (4.2 months) [1]. The reasons for the higher TTD in this study may be related to differences in the specific methods employed, study populations, and/or improved outcomes for TCE patients over time. HCC in this study ($23,091 PPPM in 2021 US dollars) were similar but somewhat less than that reported for elderly Medicare TCE patients by Hlavacek et al. ($27,557 PPPM in 2020 US dollars) [13], and substantially less than those reported for largely non-elderly commercially insured TCE patients by Madduri et al. ($35,657 PPPM, base year not reported) [1], Jagannath et al. ($34,578 PPPM, base year not reported) [24], and Yang et al. ($29,761 PPPM in 2021 US dollars) [15]. The higher costs in studies of non-elderly commercially insured patients may be due to higher negotiated reimbursement rates for commercial plans versus Medicare [25]. The difference between the costs reported here and those reported by Hlavacek et al. for similar Medicare patients is unknown but may relate to differences in methodologies used for calculating PPPM costs (with patients weighted by duration of follow-up herein and methods not reported by Hlavacek et al.) or greater duration of follow-up for patients in this study.

With regard to survival, OS for TCE patients in this study (median 15.6 months) was similar to that for TCE patients in the study by Hlavacek et al. (16.6 months) [13] (OS was not reported for studies by Madduri et al. [1], Jagannath et al. [24], or Yang et al. [15]). Several prior studies have also reported poor OS. For example, the multinational, prospective, non-interventional LocoMMotion study reported a median OS of 12.4 months with real-life standard of care therapy in patients with TCE RRMM [26]. In the setting of TCR RRMM, the median OS was 11.1 months in LocoMMotion [26] and 11.6 months in a retrospective study using the US COTA healthcare real-world database [27]. Meanwhile, data from the US multicenter, prospective, observational Connect MM Registry indicated a median OS of 9.9 months in patients with TCR RRMM [28]. A similar median OS (9.2 months) was observed in the US multicenter, retrospective MAMMOTH study in patients with triple- or quad-refractory RRMM [11]. Potential contributors to the differences in OS between studies could include variations in setting, patient population (e.g., number and type of prior LOT), and study time period. Regardless of these variations, collectively our results and these prior findings highlight the poor prognosis associated with TCE RRMM and the need for advances in treatment to prolong survival.

Limitations of this research should be noted. First, this study is subject to inherent limitations with claims data involving coding accuracy and completeness [29]. The algorithm used to identify LOT and refractory status was based on information in health insurance claims. Although the algorithm was developed based on extensive clinical input, and is similar to that used in prior studies [1, 19], it has not been validated. Also, the algorithm requires identification of an initial diagnosis of MM. In this study, initial diagnosis was established based on a required “washout period” of 6 months of continuous enrollment prior to the first MM diagnosis during the study period under the hypothesis that patients with MM are not likely to have 6 months without a claim with an MM diagnosis. However, it is possible that some patients had a diagnosis of MM prior to the study period (e.g., prior to enrolling in Medicare), potentially resulting in misclassification of treatment history. The study period for this study ran through December 31, 2020, and several new treatments have since been approved by the FDA for patients with RRMM, including teclistamab, elranatamab, talquetamab, ciltacabtagene autoleucel, and idecabtagene vicleucel [3]. Although approved prior to the end of the study period, no patients in any cohort were identified who received belantamab mafodotin for the index LOT. The proportion of patients receiving selinexor (approved on July 3, 2019) for the index LOT ranged from only 1–6%, and the proportion of patients receiving isatuximab (approved on March 2, 2020) for the index LOT was < 1% for all cohorts [3]. New research using more recent data will be helpful to elucidate how treatment patterns for patients with MM may have evolved since the end of the study period.

Conclusions

In this large real-world retrospective study of elderly, Medicare FFS-insured TCE patients, there was a wide distribution of treatment regimens across the patients, suggesting no standard of care treatment. There was a high degree of repeat usage of IMiDs, PIs, and anti-CD38 mAbs, even among patients who had received multiple courses of, or were refractory to, these classes of medications. With the medications available at the time of the study, TTD, TTNT, and OS were short, and HRU and HCC were high. The heterogeneity of treatments was greater, and the outcomes were generally poorer for PE versus TCE patients, and for patients who were TCR versus not TCR. These results underscore the potential for novel therapies, including both the recently approved bispecific antibodies and CAR Ts, as well as other therapies in development, including ADCs, CELMoDs, and IFPs, which may overcome disease resistance, increase survival, and reduce patient burden in this growing population.
