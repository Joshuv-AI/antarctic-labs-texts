Efficacy of cluster care strategies and blood transfusion protocols in reducing nosocomial infections associated with triple catheter use in intensive care units
Authors: Hui Yang, Meiling He, Xue Xu, Zhuo-Juan Jiang, Wen-Fang Li, Yue Li, He-Hua Yu, Yan-Yun Tan, Fang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis investigation assessed the effectiveness of cluster care measures in conjunction with blood transfusion protocols in the prevention of nosocomial infections associated with the use of three catheters in intensive care units (ICUs).DesignCross sectional study.MethodsThe investigation included 179 critically ill patients who were admitted between January 2022 and December 2023. The control group consisted of 79 patients, received routine ICU care, whereas research group consisted of 100 patients, provided with cluster care measures and stringent blood transfusion protocols. Clinical data were analyzed in both groups, including infection control capacity of Nurses for Preventing Catheter-Associated Infections, catheter infection rates, catheter-associated urinary tract infections and central line-associated bloodstream infections.ResultsThe research group’s infection control capacity was substantially improved post-intervention, surpassing that of the control group, as evidenced by the results. It demonstrated significant decrease in the overall incidence of catheter infections, as well as superior clinical outcomes.ConclusionThe overall efficacy of the interventions was significantly influenced by the integration of blood transfusion protocols including the use of leukoreduced blood products and meticulous monitoring of transfusion thresholds. The integration of cluster care measures and blood transfusion protocols was determined to be significantly more effective than traditional ICU care in the management of three-catheter-associated nosocomial infections in ICUs.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is a critical environment in which patients with severe and complex conditions receive intensive medical care. Nevertheless, it is also a hotspot for healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs), which are the second most common cause of death worldwide and pose a substantial hazard to patient morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. HCAIs are developed by more than half of ICU patients, which not only extend hospital stays and increase medical expenses but also elevate the risk of secondary complications, thereby impeding the efficacy of treatment and recovery [3, 4]. The incidence of HCAIs in ICUs is influenced by a combination of pathogenic factors, such as virulence and antibiotic resistance, therapeutic factors, such as invasive devices and antibiotic pressure, and healthcare processes, including staff allocation and preventive measures [5, 6].

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI), and central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI) are three of the most common HCAIs in ICUs. These infections are directly correlated with the severity of patients’ conditions, compromised immunity, and multiple invasive catheterization procedures [7, 8]. The implementation of cluster care strategies and adherence to blood transfusion protocols are particularly important for ICU nurses, who are frontline participants in patient care, to prevent and control these infections [9].

Blood transfusion is a frequently performed procedure in intensive care units, particularly for patients who require complex surgeries or have severe anemia. Nevertheless, if not implemented with precision, transfusion practices may exacerbate the risk of HCAIs. The risk of infection is significantly reduced by transfusion protocols that prioritize the prudent utilization of blood products and the implementation of rigorous aseptic techniques during the transfusion process [10]. The incidence of nosocomial infections associated with invasive procedures, such as the use of a triple catheter, is significantly reduced by the integration of transfusion protocols into cluster care strategies, as demonstrated by research [11, 12]. For example, the application of leukoreduced blood products and the meticulous monitoring of transfusion thresholds have been linked to reduced rates of CLABSI and other transfusion-related complications [13–15].

This study aims to address the lacuna in the clinical efficacy of cluster care measures and transfusion protocols in controlling HCAIs associated with triple catheter use by analyzing and reporting on their combined impact in the ICU setting.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a non-randomized controlled comparative study conducted in a single ICU. Patients were assigned to either the control or research group according to admission sequence and clinical management requirements. Both groups were cared for within the same ICU environment under identical staffing levels, equipment availability, and institutional infection control policies to minimize environmental and staffing-related confounding. Neither participants nor caregivers were blinded to group allocation, but outcome assessors were independent of the clinical team and blinded during data analysis. Physicians and other healthcare providers were aware that the study was ongoing; however, standard clinical management protocols were followed uniformly for both groups and were not altered by study participation. The intervention (cluster care + transfusion protocols) was integrated into routine nursing practice for the research group throughout the ICU stay.

Baseline data

One hundred and seventy-nine critically ill patients admitted to our hospital were selected as the study subjects, and the enrollment period was from January 2022 to December 2023. The control group (n = 79) received routine ICU care, while the research group (n = 100) received cluster care. In addition to cluster care measures, the research group also adhered to specific blood transfusion protocols aimed at minimizing infection risks associated with transfusion. Clinical comparability was found between groups when analyzing their baseline data (P > 0.05).

Patient selection criteria

Inclusion criteria: Adult patients (≥ 18 years old) with a length of ICU stay > 2 days; estimated survival > 6 months; the need for ventilator and intubation treatment; intact medical records; normal communication and cognitive abilities; willingness to cooperate with the research.

Exclusion criteria

Infection before admission to ICU; serious cardio-cerebrovascular diseases and abnormal renal function; serious infectious diseases and coagulation dysfunction; myocardial infarction, ventricular premature beats, frequent premature atrial contractions; severe pulmonary hypertension; malignant tumor or severe organ dysfunction; recent use of anti-infection treatment.

Nursing methods

The control group received routine ICU care according to standard hospital infection prevention policy. Routine care included general hand hygiene compliance per WHO 5 moments, standard aseptic technique during catheter insertion and medication administration, daily ventilator assessments and routine ventilator circuit handling, basic three-catheter maintenance (regular visual inspection, dressing changes as per standard schedule, and catheter fixation), regular ward cleaning and disinfection, nutritional support, and routine skin, oral and limb care. No additional structured cluster-care reinforcement or targeted transfusion protocols were implemented in this group.

The research group was given the following cluster care measures: (1) Discussing problems: An anti-nosocomial infection cluster care group composed of head nurses and specialized nurses was set up. The team members discussed, analyzed, and summarized the problems existing in the nursing process of three-catheter nosocomial infections in ICUs in the past, mainly including catheter blockage, untimely catheter replacement, lax aseptic operation, and drainage tube detachment. (2) Collecting evidence-based evidence: The team members made full use of the Internet to query relevant literature through Wanfang, VIP, CNKI, and other databases, analyzed the intervention measures provided in the literature, and drew up nursing plans in combination with clinical practice and patient needs. (3) Nursing measures: ① Strengthen training. Training interventions for ICU nurses were strengthened, including strict hand hygiene, ventilator use, and aseptic procedures for catheterization. During disinfection, the comprehensiveness of disinfection was ensured, and the hygienic dead angles were minimized. ② Three-catheter maintenance. Antibacterial catheters were the main choice. The catheter interface was wiped with ethanol cotton pads, and the catheter was fixed with a 2% chlorhexidine paster. Catheter maintenance was carried out within 24 h on the day of the catheter puncture, and sterile dressings were used at the puncture site, which were replaced every 24 h. The ventilator pipeline was replaced once a week and was changed immediately if there was a suspected infection. When placing the catheter, the insertion length was reasonably controlled and the catheter was properly secured. During transportation, operation, and activities, attention was paid to protecting the catheter to prevent it from falling off. ③ Supervision and management. During the monitoring of patient vital signs, attention was paid to strengthening the three-catheter monitoring, including body temperature, blood routine, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, and other infection indicators. If there was an abnormality in body temperature, the occurrence of catheter infection was considered first, in which case, blood samples would be collected and the tip of the catheter would be kept for culture. If a three-catheter infection was confirmed, the condition would be immediately reported to the nosocomial infection department. A full-time supervisor was set up in the infection control cluster care team to supervise and manage the implementation of the rules and regulations of the team members. Both groups received nursing until discharge.

To minimize performance bias, both physicians and cleaning/disinfection staff were informed only of standard infection control procedures and not of group allocation or specific study objectives. All personnel followed uniform hospital infection prevention protocols, which were already well established prior to study initiation. No special instructions were given to either group, ensuring that routine clinical and cleaning practices remained consistent throughout the study period.

Transfusion protocols were standardized across all ICU patients in the research group. Packed red blood cells (PRBCs) were transfused when the hemoglobin concentration fell below 70 g/L in hemodynamically stable patients or below 80 g/L in patients with active bleeding, respiratory failure, or cardiovascular compromise. Leukoreduced blood products were used for all transfusions to minimize immunomodulatory effects, in accordance with institutional infection control policy. Platelet transfusions were initiated when platelet counts dropped below 20 × 10⁹/L in non-bleeding patients or below 50 × 10⁹/L in patients undergoing invasive procedures. All transfusions were administered using sterile techniques and monitored by ICU nurses at 15-minute intervals for the first hour and every 30 min thereafter. Monitoring included body temperature, blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, and signs of transfusion-related reactions. Any abnormalities were immediately reported, and infection markers (C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, complete blood count) were rechecked within 24 h post-transfusion.

In the research group, cluster care measures and transfusion protocols were applied concurrently as part of a comprehensive infection control bundle. All patients in this group received both interventions simultaneously throughout their ICU stay. The study was designed to evaluate the combined effect of the two interventions, not to isolate their independent contributions.

Statistical analysis

This study imported measurement and count data into the SPSS 20.0 software package for statistical analysis, with a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.05. The number of cases/percentage (n/%) was used to represent the count data and the Mean ± SEM to represent the measurement data. To identify differences, the χ2 test was used for count data, while the independent sample t-test (between-group comparison) and paired t-test (within-group comparison) were used for measurement data. In addition to χ² and t-tests, multivariable logistic regression models were applied to assess the independent effect of the intervention on the risk of catheter-associated infections. Variables included in the model were age, sex, comorbidities, baseline hemoglobin level, and length of ICU stay. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

After adjusting for age, sex, comorbidities, and ICU stay, the intervention remained independently associated with reduced infection risk (aOR 0.21, 95% CI 0.07–0.58, P = 0.003).

Multivariable analysis of infection outcomes

To control for potential confounding factors, a multivariable logistic regression model was performed including age, sex, comorbidities, ICU stay duration, and baseline hemoglobin levels.

The combined intervention (cluster care + transfusion protocol) was independently associated with a significant reduction in overall catheter-associated infections (adjusted OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07–0.58, P = 0.003).

When analyzed separately, the odds of CAUTI were reduced (adjusted OR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.06–0.88, P = 0.032), and the odds of CLABSI were reduced (adjusted OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.83, P = 0.022), while the odds of VAP were also lower but did not reach statistical significance likely due to low event counts.

Discussion

Healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) pose significant threats not only to the lives and health of patients and healthcare workers but also contribute to increased medical costs related to infection prevention within the healthcare system [16]. The risk of HCAIs can reach up to 15%, which severely impacts the quality of life for those affected and escalates their treatment costs [17]. In this study, 179 critically ill patients were included, and we demonstrated that cluster care measures, specifically targeted nurse training, strengthened hand hygiene and aseptic catheterization practices, standardized three-catheter maintenance procedures, continuous supervision, and active involvement of the nosocomial infection department, when combined with stringent blood transfusion protocols, produced significant preventive and control effects on nosocomial infections related to triple catheter use in ICUs. The improved outcomes therefore reflect not only the technical protocols but also the improvement in knowledge, practice execution, behavioral compliance, and supervisory reinforcement within the nursing team.

Our findings indicate that the infection control capacity was superior in the research group compared to the control group following the implementation of nursing interventions. This suggests that cluster care measures, particularly when complemented by transfusion protocols, significantly enhance infection prevention and control in critically ill patients. The tailored nursing strategies—such as strengthening training, specific three-catheter maintenance, and stringent supervision—are designed based on problem analysis and evidence-based research, leading to more effective targeted prevention and control [18, 19].

Moreover, the study revealed that the total incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI), and central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI) in the research group was 5.25%, statistically lower than the 20.25% observed in the control group. This outcome underscores the effectiveness of cluster care in reducing the risks of these infections in critically ill patients, consistent with previous studies [13–15]. The integration of cluster care measures, along with rigorous transfusion protocols such as the use of leukoreduced blood products and monitoring transfusion thresholds, plays a crucial role in minimizing infection risks. These transfusion-related practices reduce immunomodulatory effects and ensure aseptic administration, thereby lowering the likelihood of transfusion-related infections [14, 15].

Further analysis showed that patients in the research group had significantly shorter ICU stays, reduced overall hospital stays, and lower hospitalization costs compared to the control group. This suggests that the combination of cluster care and transfusion protocols not only prevents infections but also supports quicker recovery, ultimately reducing healthcare costs. The positive impact of these interventions on patient recovery is likely due to the effective prevention and control of VAP, CAUTI, and CLABSI, as well as the added benefit of minimizing transfusion-related complications, which contributes to better overall treatment outcomes [20]. This aligns with findings by Liu et al. [21] and Rice et al. [22], who reported that cluster care interventions could reduce ICU stay duration, hospitalization costs, and even readmission rates in critically ill patients.

Additionally, the research group showed statistically significant improvements in nursing quality—specifically in operation implementation, sterile awareness, and service attitude—compared to the control group. This demonstrates that cluster care, in conjunction with strict transfusion protocols, can significantly enhance the quality of nursing care provided to critically ill patients. The research group also reported higher nursing satisfaction rates (96.00% vs. 86.08%), indicating that these combined interventions positively influence patient satisfaction. Similar trends have been observed in other studies, where cluster care interventions have led to improved patient satisfaction, health-related quality of life, and family member satisfaction in ICU settings [23, 24].

Limitations

The study used a non-randomized allocation, which may introduce potential selection bias despite comparable baseline characteristics between groups. Although this limitation does not invalidate the findings, randomized controlled trials are warranted to further confirm the causal effects of the interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that implementing cluster care combined with standardized transfusion protocols significantly reduced catheter-associated infection rates, shortened ICU and hospital stays, and lowered hospitalization costs. In addition to the direct impact of the intervention, factors such as full-time supervision to ensure adherence to cluster care measures and increased staff awareness of infection control protocols may have indirectly contributed to these improvements. Moreover, patients could have more than one comorbidity, and the data reflected the frequency distribution of each condition rather than the total number of comorbidities per patient. These findings underscore the importance of integrated infection prevention strategies supported by effective supervision, while also highlighting contextual factors that should be considered when interpreting and replicating the results.
