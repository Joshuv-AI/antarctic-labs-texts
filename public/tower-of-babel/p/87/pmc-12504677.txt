A non-destructive method for measuring the surface fractal dimension of foam concrete
Authors: Qianqian Chen, Yongguang Feng, Guosheng Xiang, Yunze Bai, Zhe Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research focuses on the crucial parameter of foam concrete, its surface fractal dimension, which significantly influences mechanical and thermal characteristics. By correlating the fractal dimension D with thermal conductivity T, this study proves a robust relationship, confirmed through extensive analysis of measured thermal conductivity values from various literature.Moreover, by use of the physical foaming technique, foam concrete was produced from mostly cement and steel slag. The transient plane source technique was utilized to measure the thermal conductivity, from which the fractal dimension of the foam concrete was obtained.Consistent with that observed by the conventional SEM imaging technique, the fractal dimension dropped first and subsequently rose with the increasing steel slag content. Unlike conventional methods involving concrete slicing and sampling, our non-destructive approach relies on thermal conductivity measurement, offering simplicity, ease of operation, and preservation of concrete integrity. This innovative method presents a practical alternative for accurately determining the surface fractal dimension of foam concrete, enhancing efficiency, and minimizing structural damage.Keywords: Foam concrete, Fractal model, Nonlinear fitting, Sem, Thermal conductivitySubject terms: Civil engineering, Characterization and analytical techniques

Introduction

Low density, great thermal insulation, and simplicity of application make foamed concrete (FC) a lightweight building material ideal for some projects1–3. FC has strong plasticity, and its excellent mechanical and thermal properties depend on several factors, including the size and shape of foam bubbles, as well as the composition and density of the material. Many domestic and international scholars have studied the micro-structure of FC to understand the general correlation between its mechanical and thermal properties and micro-structure4,5. Parameters commonly considered in previous studies of microstructures include specific surface area, pore radius distribution, etc. Since the introduction of fractal geometry concepts by B.B. Mandelbrot6, an increasing number of researchers have come to see that the fractal dimension D offers a special way to characterize the intricacy of surface form. Pore structures in two- and three-dimensional regions can be characterized using the fractal dimension7–9. The higher the fractal dimension, the more irregular the pore distribution.

Fractal theory It is currently being widely used to study irregular problems, providing accurate quantification and characterization of irregular shapes. For example, Saouma et al.10 demonstrated that fractal dimension serves as a valuable metric for characterizing the roughness of concrete surfaces. Yin et al.11 suggested a damage identification computation technique based on crack fractal dimension, measuring fracture distribution with fractal dimension and following the whole course of concrete cracking. They developed a microscopic mechanical model of concrete and found the relationship among damage, strain, and fracture fractal dimension. Concurrently, studies have revealed that D is closely related to the macroscopic mechanical and thermal characteristics of complex materials12. Some researchers have explored the relationship between D and the compressive strength of concrete13–15, leading to simplifying the calculation of concrete compressive strength using fractal dimension. Fractal models are also used to study other properties of porous media16–19. Fractal theory has been widely validated in scientific research as an effective approach for analyzing the microstructure of FC. For instance, Jin et al.20 examined the microstructure of FC using fractal theory. Their experimental findings demonstrated clear fractal features in the microstructure of FC, with the fractal dimension rising as the amounts of fly ash and foaming agents increased. This established a relationship between the fractal dimension and the mechanical properties of FC. Chen et al.21 derived the theoretical relationship between compressive strength and porosity based on the fractal model of FC. Xiang et al.22 quantitatively analyzed the porosity of FC and investigated the correlation between the fractal dimension pertaining to FC and its thermal insulation and sound absorption properties. The results showed that with increased porosity, the fractal dimension also increased while thermal conductivity decreased. Xiang et al.23 examined the relationship between the fractal dimension of FC at different dry densities and its pore structure, using fractal theory as a basis. The experimental results showed that as dry density increased, the pore distribution became more uniform, leading to a decrease in the fractal dimension.

From the above studies, the fractal dimension is an important parameter to characterize the micro-structure of FC and is significant in predicting the performance of FC. Surface laser scanning24, scanning electron microscopy25, gas adsorption26, piezomercury porosimetry27 or structure measuring techniques are the primary methods being used to find the fractal dimension of FC. Guo et al.28 for example used X-ray computed tomography imaging to evaluate FC pore structure and found a declining pore fractal dimension with increasing FC density. Surface laser scanning24, scanning electron microscopy25, gas adsorption26 Huang et al.29 analyzed FC pore structure and micro-structure using scanning electron microscopy. However, these testing methods necessitate costly and sophisticated instruments in specialized laboratories, rendering them impractical for real-world engineering applications. Moreover, these testing methods typically involve cutting small pieces from concrete test blocks, posing risks of structural damage and providing potentially inaccurate representations of overall concrete properties. Therefore, this study proposes a novel approach to establish a fractal relationship between thermal conductivity and dry density of FC, validated using existing experimental data available in the literature. Subsequently, based on the measured data of thermal conductivity, the surface fractal dimension is computed and compared with the surface fractal dimension obtained from compressive strength experiments and SEM imaging to validate the reliability of the method further. The proposed surface fractal measurement method evaluates FC thermal conductivity from a macroscopic perspective, circumventing the need for destructive testing methods and ensuring more representative results.

Specifically, the primary objectives of this study are: To develop and validate a non-destructive method for determining the surface fractal dimension (D) of foam concrete (FC) based on the established theoretical relationship between thermal conductivity (κ) and D.The feasibility and reliability of this κ -based method are proved by experiments.To evaluate the practical advantages of this proposed method, particularly its non-destructive nature, operational simplicity, and preservation of concrete integrity, in contrast to existing destructive techniques (e.g., SEM, MIP, sample sectioning).The core hypothesis underpinning this work is that the theoretically derived κ-D relationship provides a robust and accurate basis for non-destructively determining the surface fractal dimension of FC, offering a viable and advantageous alternative to traditional microstructural analysis methods.

Verify

To assess the viability of the proposed relationship (Eq. (6)), this study juxtaposes the calculated thermal conductivity values with those experimentally determined in the existing literature. Novais et al.33 meticulously crafted fly ash FC with varying NaOH concentrations, water, and H₂O₂ contents, pinpointing the thermal conductivity within the range of 0.08–0.23 W·mK− 1. Wei et al.34 meticulously prepared five distinct densities of fly ash FC spanning dry densities from 300 to 1700 kg·m− 3, seeing an escalation in thermal conductivity with increasing dry density. Yang et al.35 leveraged Ca (OH)2, Mg (NO3)2, and Na2SiO3 as foaming agents for FC fabrication, noting a reduction of 0.04 W·mK− 1 in thermal conductivity for every 100 kg·m-3 decrease in dry density. To further confirm the plausibility of the proposed model, we used experimental data from Xiang et al.36, which examined the thermal conductivity of FC with identical mix ratios but varying ages. The comparison results between the experimental data and the thermal conductivity calculated using Eq. (6) are presented in Fig. 3 below. As depicted in Fig. 3, a discernible trend emerges: foam concrete’s heat conductivity decreases with age. This phenomenon could be attributed to the impact of foam concrete’s pore structure on its thermal conductivity. Specifically, varying ages entail the presence of unclosed and closed pores within the material, along with differences in thermal conductivity among the foam gases. Unclosed pores allow for the exchange of foam gases with atmospheric air, gradually transitioning to air over time, while the foaming gas contained within closed pores remains constant with age. Notably, the predicted thermal conductivity curves across different ages exhibit a commendable alignment with the experimental data gleaned from existing literature. This alignment underscores the broad applicability of the theoretical relationship to FC.

Preparation of foam concrete

(1) First, based on the calculated quantities of raw materials, the steel slag, cement, and water were weighed and mixed evenly. The foam mixture was prepared according to a water-to-foaming agent ratio of 30:1 and poured into the foam generator after preparation. (2) The foam generator was started to produce a suitable amount of uniform fine foam. This foam was mixed with the cementitious slurry. This mixing process was repeated 3 to 4 times. Then, 1 L of the mixed slurry was weighed using a beaker to measure its mass. If the density did not meet the design requirements, foam was added to the slurry again. This adjustment continued until the density met the requirements, after which the mixture was poured. 3)The mixed slurry was injected into molds pre-coated with lubricating oil and subjected to slight vibration simultaneously. After pouring, the surface was leveled, and the specimens were transferred to the curing room. After curing for 1 day, they were demolded. Following demolding, the specimens were returned to the curing room. Upon reaching the specified age, the specimens were removed, placed in a drying oven at (60 ± 5) °C, and dried. After drying for 4 h, specimens showing a mass difference of less than 1 g between consecutive weighings were removed, cooled to room temperature, and then subjected to relevant performance tests.

Measurement of thermal conductivity

In this study, the thermal conductivity of steel slag foam concrete was measured using the transient plane source (TPS) method, in accordance with the Chinese standard GB/T 32,064 − 201534. Figure 5 (a) shows the Hot Disk sensor used in the experiment. The specific test procedure was as follows: Prior to thermal conductivity measurement, the samples were dried in an oven at 60 ± 5 °C until constant mass was achieved. After drying, the samples were removed and allowed to cool to ambient temperature. The Hot Disk sensor is then positioned between two samples, creating a sandwich-like arrangement, as illustrated in Fig. 5 (b).

Thermal conductivity measurement device. (a) Hot Disk sensor; (b) specimen installation; (c) TC3000E Thermal Conductivity Meter.

Start the thermal conductivity measurement instrument, as shown in Fig. 5(c). The software was opened and the basic settings were configured. Select “block” as the sample type. A voltage of 1.0 V was selected. The data acquisition time was set to 10 s. The time interval between measurements was set to 3 min. Three measurements were conducted for each group. After confirming the sensor settings, a thermal equilibrium test was performed.Once the thermal equilibrium test was complete, the thermal conductivity measurement was initiated. The test was carried out at room temperature and pressure, conduct three samples for each group, testing each pair of samples once. Record three sets of data for each test. Consequently, nine sets of data were obtained for each group of specimens. Data exhibiting significant errors were discarded, and the average of the remaining data was calculated to obtain the final experimental result for each group.The mean thermal conductivity (κ) and standard deviation (SD) were calculated for each group. The coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean × 100%) was maintained below 5% to ensure data repeatability. Confidence intervals (95% CI) were determined using t-distribution.

SEM test

After completing the compressive strength test, we took samples from the specimens, which were natural cross-sections. We placed the specimens in anhydrous ethanol to stop the hydration reaction, dried them, and then subjected them to gold spraying treatment. We used the JEM-6510 electron scanning microscope (SEM) to analyze the micro-structure of the FC specimens with different steel slag contents.

Measurement of hole structure

Using the AutoPore IV 9510 instrument from Micromeritics Instrument Corporation, USA, the pore structure measurement can be reached using the MIP (Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry) technique. To ensure the results are representative, each sample must weigh at least 2 g. The mercury pressure is increased gradually from 3.7 kPa (0.54 psi) to 414 MPa (60,000 psi), with a 10-second equilibrium time at each pressure step. For calculating the Particle Size Distributions (PSDs) of the concrete samples, a contact angle of 130° for mercury on the substrate and a surface tension of 485 N/m for mercury are used.

Measurement of microscopic form

The field emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Quanta FEG650) is used to observe and analyze the microscopic morphology of the concrete samples. SEM samples with dimensions of about 1–2 cm are collected from the interior of compressed cubic specimens. During sample preparation, maximum care is taken to avoid any contamination and preserve the original fractured surfaces. During the testing, an acceleration voltage of 20 kV and a working distance ranging from 15 mm to 35 mm are used.

Results and discussion

As presented in Fig. 6, it can be noted that when the steel slag content remains constant, the thermal conductivity shows a tendency to increase as the dry bulk density goes up. Notably, the increase in thermal conductivity is more pronounced in steel slag foam concrete with a steel slag content of 20% or less compared to higher contents. For instance, in the case of 20% steel slag foam concrete, when the dry bulk density increases from 500 kg·m-3 to 600 kg·m-3, 700 kg·m-3, and 800 kg·m-3, the thermal conductivity of the material increases from 0.1075 W·(m·K)-1 to 0.1303 W·(m·K)-1, 0.1573 W·(m·K)-1, 0.1923 W·(m·K)-1, and 0.2384 W·(m·K)-1 respectively. This trend can be explained by the fact that as the dry density increases, resulting in higher solid content per unit volume, the internal pore structure of the steel slag foam concrete is reduced.The heat transfer in the concrete structure is smoother, thus reducing the loss of heat on the transmission path, ultimately leading to the increase of thermal conductivity. Under the same dry bulk density conditions, the thermal conductivity decreases with the increase of steel slag content. Taking a dry bulk density of 700 g·cm-3 as an example, at a 10% SSC, the thermal conductivity of steel slag foam concrete was 0.1696 W·(m·K)-1. Continue to increase the steel slag content to 20%, 30%, and 40%, and the thermal conductivity of steel slag foam concrete will continue to decline to 0.1540 W·(m·K)-1, 0.1351 W·(m·K)-1, and 0.1208 W·(m·K)-1. This is particularly due to the apparent density of steel slag used in this article being 3.4 g·cm-3, which is higher than the density of cement (3.0–3.15 g·cm-3). When preparing steel slag foam concrete with the same dry density, the more steel slag is added, the smaller the total volume of steel slag and cement required, and the solid volume in the final concrete is correspondingly reduced, resulting in the interior pore structure of steel slag foam concrete growing. At the same time, the steel slag itself also has many pores, which will increase more micro-pores in foam concrete. The increase in micro-pores and the overall porosity hinder the transfer of thermal energy, leading to a decrease in the thermal conductivity of concrete.For 20% SSC at 700 kg·m⁻³, thermal conductivity was 0.1573 W·(m·K)-1. ANOVA confirmed significant differences between groups (F(3,16) = 28.7, p < 0.001), with Tukey’s post-hoc test showing that 20% SSC differed significantly from 10%、30% and 40% SSC.

To further verify the accuracy of the thermal conductivity method in measuring fractal dimension, the commonly used SEM image method was employed for comparison. Figure 8 displays with varying steel slag contents the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of steel slag foam concrete with a dry density of 700 kg·m-3. It is evident that the microstructure of foam concrete (FC) undergoes significant changes as the proportion of steel slag increases. At 10% steel slag content, the concrete surface exhibits more voids, leading to an uneven texture. However, when the steel slag content rises to 20%, the number of voids decreases substantially, resulting in a smoother surface and a denser structure. However, as the amount of steel slag continues to increase, the phenomenon of structural fragmentation becomes more pronounced, and the surface illustrates a concave shape. Especially in concrete with steel slag content between 30% and 40%, large pores appear, and the pore structure becomes loose. Adding the right amount of steel slag helps to increase the fluidity of the slurry, which is the major reason that steel slag causes the pore structure of FC to be dense first and then loose during the production of FC; It makes it easier for the foam to bond with the slurry, the bursting of foam is reduced, so that a large number of holes with small aperture can be formed in the concrete. However, when the amount of steel slag is too large, more than 20%, the fluidity of the slurry will increase excessively, causing the steel slag to sink, the viscosity of the slurry to reduce, and the friction between the slurry and the foam surface to decrease accordingly. Therefore, the fluidity of the upper layer slurry is significantly enhanced, and the volume of foam expands continuously during the rising process, sometimes even breaking and thus forming connected pores in the concrete. When there are many connected pores, even large cracks are formulated.

To calculate the fractal dimension, MATLAB programming is typically employed to apply binary processing to SEM images. As shown in Fig. 9, the SEM image is converted into binary form, and then it is converted into black and white image to eliminate the brightness and unevenness of the image, so that the image is clear, and then the fractal dimension is determined by box counting method.

Binary diagram under different SSC: (a) 10%, (b) 20%, (c) 30% and (d) 40%.

In the given formula, the variable N(L) represents the number of box counts, while the variable L denotes the length of the box. From a theoretical perspective, as the box length l gradually approaches the value of 0, the corresponding box count will N(l) exhibit a tendency to increase without bound, ultimately approaching infinity. Therefore, the DC of FC with 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% steel slag is 1.802, 1.785, 1.844, and 1.865, respectively.

It should be noted that Dc is the fractal dimension obtained from a planar image, and the true fractal dimension D ≈ Dc + 1.

From here, it can be observed that the fractal dimension derived from SEM images coincides with the value gauged by thermal conductivity. Both sets of data or results indicate that the fractal dimension initially undergoes a decline and subsequently experiences an increase as the proportion of steel slag steadily rises. To quantify the agreement between the two methods, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was calculated for the fractal dimension values (D) obtained from thermal conductivity and SEM across the four steel slag contents (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%). The calculated correlation coefficient is r = 0.996, indicating a very strong positive linear relationship between the D values measured by the two independent methods. This high degree of correlation statistically confirms the consistency and reliability of the thermal conductivity method for determining the surface fractal dimension of foam concrete.

It is observed that as the steel slag content increases, both the average pore size µ and the standard deviation δ initially decrease and then increase, with the lowest values occurring at 20% steel slag content. This further reflects that adding 20% steel slag can make FC dense; at this time, the pores of FC are small and uniform. Statistical analysis (ANOVA, p < 0.05) verified that the minimum fractal dimension at 20% SSC (2.801 ± 0.012) was significantly lower than other compositions. Conclusions

1) The surface fractal dimension is a key parameter in describing the microstructure of foam concrete, offering valuable insights into how its mechanical properties relate to the microstructure. According to fractal theory, there is a theoretical correlation between thermal conductivity and foam concrete’s value of fractal dimension, which has been validated by experimental thermal conductivity data in previous studies. By applying this theoretical framework alongside the transient plane source method for thermal conductivity measurement, the fractal dimension of foam concrete can be determined with precision, without compromising the integrity of its structure.

2) FC is produced primarily using steel slag and cement as the base materials, employing a physical foaming technique, while its thermal conductivity is measured using the transient plane source method. It was observed that, at a constant dry density, the thermal conductivity decreases as the steel slag content increases. This trend is largely attributed to the increased porosity of foam concrete with higher steel slag proportions. Additionally, the fractal dimension of FC was calculated based on the thermal conductivity data, revealing a decrease followed by an increase in the fractal dimension with higher steel slag content, with the minimum fractal dimension occurring at a steel slag content of 20%. SEM analysis indicates that incorporating an optimal amount of steel slag results in a denser structure, leading to smaller and more uniformly distributed pores in the foam concrete, which in turn reduces the fractal dimension.After the steel slag exceeds 20%, the average pore size increases, and the span between the maximum and minimum pore sizes is larger, resulting in more obvious fragmentation and an increase in fractal dimension.Reflecting that this approach can precisely measure the heat conductivity without compromising the FC, the fractal dimension computed by the thermal conductivity method is consistent with that recorded by the box-counting method. Compared with other methods of calculating the fractal dimension of FC surface, the thermal conductivity method is simpler and easier to run without special instruments and software programs.Statistical analysis (ANOVA, p < 0.05) verified that the minimum fractal dimension at 20% SSC (2.801) was significantly lower than other compositions. The high consistency of measurements (CV < 3%) and methodological agreement (p > 0.2 for SEM vs. thermal method) confirm the robustness of this non-destructive approach.

3) Key Advantages: This method offers significant practical advantages over traditional destructive techniques (such as SEM, MIP, or sample sectioning):

Preserves Structural Integrity: Eliminates the need for sample extraction or preparation that damages the concrete structure.

Operational Simplicity: Requires only standard thermal conductivity measurement equipment (e.g., TPS device), avoiding the need for specialized, costly instruments (e.g., SEM) or complex software for image processing and fractal analysis.

Efficiency: Provides a direct and relatively fast pathway to obtain the fractal dimension parameter.
