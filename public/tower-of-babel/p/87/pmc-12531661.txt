Deep Learning-Based Segmentation in Musculoskeletal Imaging: A Review of Research Trends
Authors: Chunsu Park, Hyeyun Lee, MinWoo Kim, Chankue Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep learning-based segmentation has become a key tool for the precise and automated analysis of anatomical structures, such as bones, cartilage, and muscles, in musculoskeletal (MSK) imaging. This study examined the research trends by analyzing the number of related publications in PubMed since 2016 from both clinical and technical perspectives. Early studies primarily focused on the segmentation of major anatomical structures such as the spine and knee using large-scale datasets. However, recent studies have expanded to include the extremities and shoulders. In lesion segmentation, traditional topics such as body composition analysis, fractures, and tumors remain prominent, whereas deep learning-based detection and classification methods are increasingly integrated, leading to applications in newer areas. In addition, this study explored commonly used segmentation techniques and various applications of deep learning in MSK imaging. By systematically analyzing trends in deep learning-based segmentation research, we aim to provide insights into future directions for this rapidly evolving field.Keywords: Deep Learning, Segmentation, Musculoskeletal, Artificial Intelligence, Systemic Review

INTRODUCTION

Segmentation has advanced rapidly in medical image analysis and is widely used in various medical applications (1). In musculoskeletal (MSK) imaging, segmentation plays a crucial role in accurately delineating and quantifying anatomical structures such as bones, cartilage, and muscles, which are vital for diagnosis and treatment planning (2). Traditional manual segmentation, while effective, is time-intensive and prone to subjectivity. In contrast, deep learning-based segmentation can improve clinical workflows by enhancing accuracy and inter-reader agreement while reducing processing time through automated analysis (3,4).

Deep learning-based segmentation techniques have been actively utilized in the medical field with the introduction of U-Net in 2015. U-Net, a network architecture introduced by Ronneberger et al. (3), has demonstrated high segmentation accuracy in medical imaging and has since been widely adopted (1,3). However, challenges persist, including addressing data imbalance, ensuring generalizability across diverse patient populations, and enhancing model reliability (5).

The development of segmentation models for MSK imaging faces several challenges including similar relaxation times between tissues, imaging artifacts, and intensity inhomogeneities. These issues are more pronounced in joints affected by degenerative diseases (6). Despite these obstacles, advancements in deep learning technologies have demonstrated the potential to address these limitations, making automated segmentation a valuable tool for improving diagnostic and research outcomes.

This study aimed to analyze the trends in deep learning-based segmentation research in MSK imaging. Specifically, it investigates key subfields with active research, major research topics, and technological advancements, thereby providing insights into future research directions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

LITERATURE AND STUDY SELECTION

The identification of research trends was conducted through two approaches: a quantitative analysis, which involved counting the number of published papers by topic (excluding review articles), and a qualitative analysis, which included reviewing the titles of papers used in the quantitative analysis, as well as relevant review articles. Given that deep learning segmentation has been widely applied in the medical field since the introduction of U-Net in 2015, only studies published from 2016 onward were included in the analysis (3,7,8,9).

To approximate the number of relevant publications, a search query was conducted in PubMed using keywords such as “deep learning” and “segmentation,” and terms related to MSK anatomy and pathology, including “joint,” “cartilage,” “bone,” and “meniscus” (Supplement 1). Studies involving imaging modalities of all types, including CT, MRI, radiography, US, PET–CT, and bone densitometry were included. Studies that focused exclusively on animal research, videos, macroscopic images, microscopic pathology findings, or optical coherence tomography were excluded.

Independent searches were conducted for cases in which the classification of pathologies was clear, such as tumors and fractures, as well as for body composition. In other cases, studies were categorized according to anatomical regions, such as the knee, spine, shoulder, and hip, to identify general trends. Additionally, conditions traditionally considered different areas of deep learning rather than segmentation, such as inflammatory disorders and bone age assessment, were included in the analysis because they frequently appear alongside segmentation-related searches.

Studies related to muscles were excluded from the quantitative analysis because of the difficulty in defining a specific search query. Furthermore, cases focusing solely on muscle segmentation were excluded even when searching for the anatomical region. However, shoulder segmentation was an exception, as a substantial proportion of studies focused on rotator cuff muscles.

DATA ANALYSIS

All the studies were initially screened based on their titles, and ambiguous cases were further classified by reviewing their abstracts. “Extremity” was defined as studies excluding “Knee,” “Spine,” “Shoulder,” and “Hip,” with keywords such as “finger,” “foot,” “wrist,” “ankle,” and “toe” used for the search. Papers addressing segmentation in two or more body regions were categorized as “Multiple” for research trend analysis but were included under each respective category during specific analyses. For studies involving overlapping categories, classification criteria were established to assign the studies to the most relevant category. For example, vertebral fractures, which are often associated with osteoporosis, were classified under “bone mineral density” if the primary focus was on prediction. However, they were categorized as “Fractures” if the emphasis was on the presence of existing fractures. Finally, the number of studies in each category was identified to evaluate overall research trends.

Subsequently, the titles of the collected papers were reviewed to identify research themes based on pathological and anatomical categories. Categories with a limited number of studies, such as inflammatory disorders and bone age assessment, were categorized independently during the quantitative analysis, but were integrated into the description of relevant anatomical regions during qualitative analysis. Additionally, review articles were examined to comprehensively evaluate the overall research trends, clinical applications, and limitations.

RESULTS

CLINICAL PERSPECTIVE

RESEARCH TRENDS IN MSK DEEP-LEARNING-BASED SEGMENTATION

Research on deep learning-based segmentation has grown continuously, except for a slight slowdown in 2022. While most categories experienced an increase in the number of studies, research on knees showed a slight decline. In contrast, there has been a noticeable increase in studies focusing on previously underrepresented areas, such as the hand, wrist, foot, and ankle (Fig. 1).

Overall, the most actively studied areas were the spine (25%), knees (16%), and body composition (13%) (Fig. 1). The high level of research activity in these areas appears to be influenced not only by their clinical significance but also by data availability and anatomical characteristics. For example, knee and spine imaging is frequently performed in clinical settings to diagnose osteoarthritis (OA), intervertebral disc disease, and spinal stenosis, making it easier to access large-scale datasets. In particular, the knee benefits from publicly available datasets such as the Osteoarthritis Initiative and MICCAI’s SKI10 (10,11) provide a favorable environment for research. Additionally, the spine has distinct anatomical boundaries that allow for clearer differentiation from surrounding structures, making it well-suited for segmentation studies (9).

In 2020, 41 of the 58 studies (71%) included in this analysis focused on the spine, knee, and body composition (Fig. 2). However, by 2024, only 90 of the 198 studies (46%) would fall into these three categories, indicating a shift toward a broader range of anatomical structures and pathologies. In addition, segmentation research on pathological conditions, including tumor and fracture segmentation, has increased significantly (Fig. 2). For instance, the number of studies using fracture segmentation as a keyword increased from just 2 in 2020 to 22 in 2024 (Fig. 2).

Common Research Topics in Musculoskeletal Segmentation by Anatomy and Pathology

Deep learning-based segmentation techniques are actively employed in inflammatory diseases and bone age assessment because of their ability to enable the quantitative measurement of disease progression and growth indicators through precise anatomical boundary identification. For example, in ankylosing spondylitis, these methods allow objective quantification of lesion extent and severity , which can overcome the reliance on the physician’s expertise (12), whereas in bone age assessment, region of interest (ROI) segmentation for bone boundary detection is a foundational step for subsequent deep learning-based prediction and classification (13). In addition to reducing interpretation time and enhancing diagnostic accuracy (12,13), this technology can also contribute to personalized treatment planning, demonstrating substantial clinical value (14).

ANATOMY

Spine

The spine is the most extensively studied area in deep learning-based segmentation research. Studies have focused not only on the segmentation of vertebrae (15,16), intervertebral discs (9), paraspinal muscles (17), and the spinal cord (7,18), but also on clinical applications such as osteoporosis and fracture risk assessment (19), spinal stenosis diagnosis (20,21), surgery-related assessments (22), and radiation therapy planning (23).

Deep learning-based segmentation of intervertebral discs using MRI has achieved high accuracy. According to a meta-analysis by Wang et al. (9), the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) for intervertebral disc segmentation ranged from 0.81 to 0.98. The ability to automatically quantify disc height and protrusion distance makes intervertebral disc segmentation clinically significant.

Automated segmentation of the paraspinal muscles using deep learning plays a crucial role in MSK disease analysis. According to a systematic review by Kim et al. (17), MRI-based paraspinal muscle segmentation studies have utilized various algorithms, including U-Net and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), to achieve high DSC values ranging from 0.76 to 0.91 across healthy subjects, patients with low back pain, and patients with spinal deformities. However, improving model robustness concerning image quality, artifacts, and individual patient variations remains a challenge.

Deep learning-based segmentation techniques also enable spinal cord analysis and clinical applications for various diseases. The SCIseg model, utilizing T2-weighted MRI, has demonstrated high accuracy in automatic spinal cord and spinal cord lesion segmentation, achieving a mean DSC of 0.92 and 0.61 (18). Bueno et al. (7) developed a segmentation model in the cervical spinal cord for multiple sclerosis patients by, leveraging T1-weighted 3D MRI data, with a DSC of 0.90.

Knee

In deep learning-based knee segmentation research, MRI is the most utilized modality (60%), followed by CT (21%) and ultrasound (14%) (Fig. 3). Many studies on cartilage and meniscus segmentation have used MRI, which is well-suited for visualizing soft tissue structures. CT is the primary imaging modality employed in developing AI models for surgical planning, such as knee replacement arthroplasty (24).

Although the diagnosis and severity assessment of knee OA are traditionally categorized into detection and classification tasks, segmentation also plays a crucial role. Cartilage segmentation occurs on a continuum of anatomical segmentation, lesion detection, and grading. In this sense, segmenting the cartilage and measuring its thickness (25,26) as well as assessing joint space narrowing (27) are closely related to OA diagnosis and evaluation.

In this study, knee segmentation research was classified into four categories: Osteoarthritis, Anatomy, Injury, and Other Pathologies. Studies directly diagnosing or assessing OA severity were categorized as Osteoarthritis, whereas those focusing on structural analysis were classified as Anatomy. Studies on meniscus and cruciate ligament tears grouped under Injury. Among these, Osteoarthritis and Anatomy were the most actively studied topics; however, both showed a stable or declining trend in research activity, in contrast to the overall increase in deep learning-based segmentation research (Fig. 3).

Studies have tried to diagnose or predict early OA before visible changes in cartilage thickness occur. T2 relaxation time has been utilized to analyze cartilage composition and serves as an important biomarker for early OA assessment (28). Another study suggested that morphological measurements derived from deep learning-based cartilage segmentation have discriminative power comparable to manual method for identifying OA progression over 12–24 months (10).

Research on meniscus and cruciate ligament injuries has increased significantly since 2022 (Fig. 3). Meniscus and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are major risk factors for OA, and traumatic injuries, particularly ACL tears, are strongly linked to OA development (28). However, pathological menisci often exhibit low contrast and heterogeneous signal intensity in MRI, making segmentation challenging (29). ACL segmentation is difficult because of the anatomical complexity of the knee joint (30). Recent studies have integrated radiomics features with deep learning segmentation to enhance the diagnostic accuracy and severity staging (31). These advancements could contribute to the early diagnosis and improved treatment planning for ACL injuries.

Shoulder and Hip

Research on the shoulder, pelvis, and femur has been increasing in recent years. Studies on the shoulder have primarily focused on the segmentation of the rotator cuff and shoulder bones (32), with more recent efforts extending to the segmentation of the brachial plexus (33). In hip and pelvic segmentation, research has explored hip cartilage segmentation (34), sacroiliac joint segmentation for diagnosing inflammatory diseases (35), and hip dysplasia assessment (Table 1) (36).

Extremity

Studies on the extremities are relatively scarce compared to those on the knees and shoulders (Fig. 1). In the upper extremities, key topics in deep learning segmentation research include the anatomical structure of the median nerve and bone age assessment. Studies on median nerve segmentation and carpal tunnel syndrome have primarily focused on evaluating the shape and cross-sectional area of the median nerve, with ultrasound imaging (37) being the most used modality. However, MRI-based segmentation has also been explored for carpal tunnel syndrome assessments (38).

Traditionally, bone age assessment has been treated as a classification problem; however, recent studies have introduced deep learning segmentation to automatically extract ROI. Research has investigated bone age assessment using segmentation of hand bones, the radius, and the ulna (13,39).

Research on the lower extremities has focused on alignment and angle analyses. For example, landmark detection techniques have been used to measure the calcaneal angle, detect and segment fractures (40), as well as assess ankle alignment and torsional deformities (41). In studies targeting the entire lower limb, deep learning segmentation techniques have been applied to evaluate lower limb alignment and leg length discrepancy using lower extremity radiographs (42). Another study automatically assessed the foot arch morphology (FAM) and plantar soft tissue thickness (PSTT) using deep learning segmentation (43).

PATHOLOGIES

Body Composition

As body composition has been identified as a prognostic predictor in patients with chronic diseases (44) and it has been actively studied as a biomarker across various conditions. Considering that whole-body CT scans are frequently performed for cancer staging, AI-based body composition analysis has significant clinical potential.

In a meta-analysis, Winder et al. (44) reported that CT-based body composition analyses were four times more prevalent than MRI-based studies. Most studies have focused on subcutaneous fat tissue, visceral adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle, with the segmentation of subcutaneous fat, potentially owing to its relatively simple structure, achieving the highest accuracy. Abdominal body composition analysis has predominantly been conducted at the L3 vertebral level, as previous studies have demonstrated a high correlation between body composition measurements at L3 and those obtained across the entire body (44). However, some studies suggested that using multiple slices rather than a single image may improve data reliability (44).

For MRI-based body composition analysis, the DIXON technique (chemical shift-encoded water-fat imaging) is widely used for its high soft-tissue contrast and superior quantification of intramuscular fat infiltration. Ramedani et al. (45) developed a whole-body segmentation model utilizing all four image sequences from a 3D two-point DIXON sequence, achieving an average DSC of 0.86.

Tumor

Bone and soft tissue tumors exhibit high heterogeneity in terms of morphology, histology, and imaging characteristics, making an accurate diagnosis particularly challenging. AIbased radiomic analysis has gained attention as a promising technology for extracting quantitative features from medical images, enabling the differentiation of tumor characteristics, and improving diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Region-defining process such as segmentation can be used to define the specific anatomical area or lesion from which quantitative features are extracted in a radiomics analysis (46).

Gitto et al. (47) demonstrated that 3D segmentation is superior to 2D segmentation in terms of feature reproducibility for cartilaginous bone tumors using CT. They reported that up to 87% of the radiomics features were preserved with 3D segmentation compared to up to 75% with 2D segmentation. Peeken et al. (48) used a deep learning algorithm on MRI to automatically segment the gross tumor volume of extremity soft tissue sarcomas for radiomic analyses.

A systematic review by Paranavithana et al. (49) investigated segmentation approaches for bone metastases. The study reported that over half (51%) of the segmentation models were CT-based; however, the lack of a gold standard for tumor boundary definition was highlighted as a key limitation. Additionally, only 25% of the studies addressed the clinical applicability of segmentation techniques, indicating that further advancements are needed.

The incorporation of deep learning-based segmentation with MRI has been explored as a noninvasive and quantitative tool for bone marrow assessment in hematologic malignancies. Peng et al. (50) used U-Net and SegNet combined with a backbone to automatically segment femur bone MRI in patients with hematologic malignancies. In particular, on T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MRI images, the model achieved a high DSC of 0.91.

Fracture

Research on fracture segmentation has rapidly increased in recent years, accounting for approximately 11% of the studies conducted in 2024 (Fig. 2). Deep learning-based fracture segmentation can facilitate precise preoperative planning, 3D modeling, and surgical simulation, ultimately reducing surgical risk (8). Among the fracture segmentation studies that excluded head and neck fractures, vertebral fractures (51) accounted for 39% (17 of 44). Recently, research has expanded to include proximal femoral fractures (52), radial fractures (53), and multiple fractures (54). These studies aimed to develop automated and accurate segmentation models for a broader range of fracture types, potentially enhancing diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning.

TECHNICAL PERSPECTIVE

DEEP LEARNING METHODS AND APPLICATIONS

Early medical image segmentation relied primarily on contour-based algorithms (55) and traditional machine learning techniques (56) that leverage data patterns. However, with recent advancements, deep learning has emerged as the dominant methodology for medical image segmentation (57). Deep learning models, which are composed of layers of interconnected neurons, analyze complex patterns in input images, extract essential features, and perform segmentation with high accuracy. In this review, we explore various deep learning concepts and techniques applied to medical image segmentation (Table 2), including CNN (58), Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) (59), and Vision Transformer (ViT)-based architectures (60). We present a comprehensive overview of the evolution of deep learning-based segmentation, with particular emphasis on its applications in MSK imaging.

TECHNIQUES

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)

Deep learning–based medical image segmentation techniques rely predominantly on CNN. Among these, U-Net (3) stands out as a model specifically designed for medical image segmentation, comprising an encoder, decoder, and skip connections. The encoder comprises convolutional and downsampling layers that progressively extract salient features by expanding the receptive field. Subsequently, the decoder gradually upsamples the features to generate pixel-level semantic predictions. Skip connections were employed to integrate the features extracted by the encoder, thereby mitigating spatial information loss. Despite its straightforward design, the U-Net architecture demonstrated superior performance over conventional techniques and remains widely used in medical image segmentation (61). Building on this foundation, several extended versions such as Res-UNet (62), UNet++ (63), and ResUNet++ (64) have been introduced. Moreover, considering the three-dimensional nature of medical data, 3D U-Net (65) variants have recently been proposed.

Many MSK imaging applications rely on CNN-based techniques. Panfilov et al. (10) proposed a fully automatic segmentation method for articular cartilage in knee MRI, utilizing a pretrained encoder to extract crucial features efficiently. Marsilio et al. (66) introduced a novel CNN-based architecture for the 3D segmentation of knee bones affected by severe OA, thereby improving automatic femur and tibia segmentation for preoperative planning in total knee replacement arthroplasty.

Zhou et al. (20) developed a method for spinal canal segmentation in CT images to enhance the diagnosis of spinal stenosis. Liu et al. (67) developed Multi-Layer Perceptron-Res-Unet for the segmentation of IVD in multimodal MRI spine images. The multi-layer perceptron contributes to improved segmentation accuracy by effectively integrating complex features across different modalities.

Zhang et al. (68) introduced a U-Net-based model for the automatic segmentation of the acetabulum and proximal femur in pediatric hip MRI scans of children with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). Their method enables automatic measurement of key morphological parameters such as the center-edge angle, acetabular index, and femoral neck angle, which are critical for DDH diagnosis and treatment planning. Similarly, Zeng et al. (36) developed a deep learning-based method for the automatic segmentation of 3D hip joint models from MRI scans, specifically targeting conditions such as femoroacetabular impingement and DDH. Their approach facilitated radiation-free patient-specific preoperative planning for periacetabular osteotomies.

CNN-based segmentation techniques have also been extensively studied for upper- and lower-extremity imaging. Yang et al. (69) introduced a residual block-based model for the ulnar and radial segmentation of dual-energy X-ray images. Residual blocks contribute to the effective feature extraction and stable training in deep neural networks. Ning et al. (43) developed DeepLab V3+ segmentation models to automatically segment foot bones and soft tissues in Xray images. This method enables the assessment of FAM and PSTT and provides insights into foot biomechanics and potential pathologies.

For shoulder segmentation, Hess et al. (32) applied a deep learning-based nnU-Net model for automatic 3D segmentation of shoulder structures, including the humerus, scapula, and rotator cuff muscles, from MRI scans. nnU-Net contributes to high segmentation accuracy for various shoulder structures by automatically adapting its architecture and hyperparameters to the given data. In addition, their method integrates automatic error checking by analyzing prediction variances across subnetworks, demonstrating high segmentation accuracy across diverse datasets while reducing the need for manual verification.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)

Since their introduction in 2014, GANs have gained significant attention in image processing and generation. A GAN consists of two key components: generator and discriminator. The generator approximates the real data distribution and generates synthetic samples that resemble real data, whereas the discriminator distinguishes between real and generated (fake) data. In addition, the discriminator provides feedback to the generator based on its classification results, helping the generator iteratively refine its outputs to more closely resemble the real data. Ultimately, the goal is for both models to reach an equilibrium in which the generator produces images that are nearly indistinguishable from the real data.

GANs have been increasingly applied to complex image processing tasks, including semantic segmentation (70). In particular, adversarial training techniques have been explored for transforming images directly into the corresponding segmentation maps. Several studies have also explored the use of GAN-generated images to augment training datasets for segmentation models (71,72), thereby improving model generalization and performance. Furthermore, GANs have been applied to image-to-image translation tasks to generate transformed images (73), which are then used to enhance segmentation performance.

The following studies highlighted the application of GANs in MSK imaging, with a focus on segmentation. Gaj et al. (74) proposed a conditional GAN (cGAN) for the automatic segmentation of knee cartilage and meniscus in MRI images. The model was trained on 2D slices of 3D dual-echo steady-state (DESS) images and achieved an average Dice coefficient of 0.88. Yang et al. (75) introduced a transfer-learning-based cGAN approach for knee-cartilage segmentation in heterogeneous clinical MRI scans. A model pre-trained on a homogeneous research dataset (Osteoarthritis Initiative) was fine-tuned on a smaller heterogeneous clinical dataset to enhance generalizability. This study demonstrates that transfer learning improves segmentation accuracy, achieves results comparable to manual segmentation by radiologists, and exhibits robustness in cross-validation across different MRI vendors. Jeon et al. (76) proposed an adversarial learning-based segmentation network that incorporated an objectaware map for automatic meniscus segmentation in knee MRI images. Their approach utilized a 2D U-Net as the generator and discriminator to refine the segmentation results. The training process integrated adversarial and segmentation losses based on binary cross-entropy, effectively mitigating undersegmentation caused by inhomogeneous intensity levels.

Felfeliyan et al. (77) developed a CycleGAN-based framework for the domain translation of knee MRI images from turbo spin-echo (TSE) fat-suppressed sequences to DESS sequences for unsupervised segmentation. CycleGAN, an unsupervised deep learning method capable of generating synthetic images from unpaired datasets, uses TSE images as inputs to produce pseudo-DESS images, enabling a Mask-RCNN model trained on the DESS data to segment the pseudo-DESS images. This study demonstrated that the segmentation performance of pseudo-DESS images is significantly superior to that of the original TSE images. Lin et al. (78) introduced a GAN-based deep learning model (BMS-Gen) to generate realistic bone-metastasis scintigrams. Their method employed multi-receptive field learning and a two-stage training strategy to enhance the quality and diversity of the generated samples. The performance of the model demonstrates improved results in downstream tasks, such as image classification and segmentation. The primary objective of this study is to address the lack of large-scale datasets for training deep learning models for bone metastasis imaging.

Alsinan et al. (79) developed a novel GAN-based computational method for the real-time segmentation of bone shadow images from in vivo ultrasound scans. The segmented shadow images were further integrated into a multi-feature-guided CNN architecture for real-time and accurate bone surface segmentation. Quantitative and qualitative evaluations were performed on 1,235 scans collected from 27 subjects using two different ultrasound machines. A comparative analysis was conducted against state-of-the-art GAN architectures to assess the performance.

Vision Transformer (ViT)

With the growing success of transformers in Natural Language Processing, ViT has been proposed to adapt to transformer-based approaches for image processing. Although CNNs are inherently constrained in capturing long-range dependencies owing to the local nature of convolution operations, ViT overcomes this limitation by enabling global contextual learning through self-attention mechanisms. Specifically, ViT divides an input image into fixedsize patches and assigns positional embeddings to each patch, thereby enabling predictive modeling that captures spatial relationships across distant regions.

Based on these advantages, several studies have explored ViT-based approaches for various segmentation tasks in MSK imaging. Yildiz Potter et al. (80) proposed an automated method for bone tumor segmentation and classification using CT images to assist clinicians in determining the need for a biopsy. Their approach utilized the Mask2Former model for simultaneous segmentation and classification, where the two tasks share common features and operate in a mutually complementary manner, achieving a performance comparable to that of existing models using alternative imaging modalities. The model demonstrates a strong potential for the visual analysis of bone tumors. Gujarati et al. (37) introduced a transformerbased method called VisTR for automated segmentation of the median nerve in ultrasound videos. The transformer-based segmentation method effectively captures the global context, leading to an improved performance in complex medical images. Despite being trained on a limited dataset, the model exhibited superior performance compared with conventional methods. Payne et al. (21) explored deep learning-based detection of cervical spinal stenosis and spinal cord abnormalities, integrating attention mechanisms and novel grading systems to improve the diagnostic consistency and efficiency in spinal imaging. Their approach aimed to enhance automated detection accuracy and assist radiologists in clinical decision-making. Tao et al. (15) proposed spine transformers, a 3D transformer-based framework for automatic vertebra labeling and segmentation of spinal CT images with arbitrary fields of view. The model first predicts the center coordinates and anatomical labels of each vertebra using a transformer-based labeling network and then extracts local volumes around each predicted center to perform segmentation. Evaluations across multiple datasets demonstrated consistent and robust vertebral segmentation performance.

Combined (CNN + ViT)

The U-Net remains the cornerstone of medical image segmentation because of its effective feature extraction and spatial information preservation through skip connections. Recently, ViT has gained significant attention in medical imaging owing to its ability to complement CNN by capturing long-range dependencies, which is an essential feature for segmenting complex anatomical structures. To leverage the advantages of both architectures, hybrid models incorporating transformer-based self-attention into the encoder, decoder, or both components of the U-Net have been developed, leading to improved segmentation accuracy. Notable examples of such architectures include TransUNet (81), U-Net Transformer (UNETR) (82), and SwinUNet (83).

The following studies highlighted the application of transformer-based models in MSK imaging for segmentation and disease assessment. Kim et al. (84) introduced a UNETR model for precise segmentation of individual muscles in whole-thigh CT scans. UNETR leverages a transformer-based encoder to learn global structural information effectively, thereby contributing to the precise segmentation of complex muscle boundaries. This approach was designed to facilitate sarcopenia assessment by enabling accurate muscle quantification. Liu et al. (19) developed a radiomics-based screening model for osteoporosis using a hybrid transformer and CNN (HTCNN). The model automatically segmented the vertebral bodies and trabecular compartments from lumbar CT images, and used the extracted radiomics features to classify osteoporosis using LASSO regression and random forest algorithms. As a result, the vertebrae-based radiomics score achieved superior classification performance (area under the curve: 0.97) compared with traditional HU-based approaches. Chen et al. (85) proposed a Symbiotic Transformer-CNN (SymTC) network for precise instance segmentation of lumbar spine structures in MRI images. Their method employs a novel self-attention mechanism with relative position embedding. Zhang et al. (16) introduced a deep learning model for cancellous bone segmentation within the vertebral body in lumbar spine CT images. Their method leveraged a multiscale information extractor and attention mechanisms to achieve a high segmentation accuracy (DSC: 0.96). The model features a hybrid encoder that integrates a CNN with a swine transformer to enhance the feature representation.

LIMITATIONS

Even if the primary goal of a deep learning model was detection, studies where segmentation played a significant role during training and included the keyword “segmentation” in the title or abstract were not excluded from this analysis. However, some of the included studies may have utilized existing software for segmentation while applying deep learning only for classification or transformation, which may not be fully aligned with the scope of this study.

Muscle segmentation is an important aspect of MSK imaging. However, muscle-related studies were excluded from the quantitative analysis, except for rotator cuff muscle and body composition analyses. This means that only studies involving muscle segmentation and other anatomical structures were included, which is a limitation.

In conventional meta-analyses, where broader keywords are used, more than 95% of the retrieved studies are often excluded, whereas in this study, less than 50% of the retrieved studies were filtered out owing to the narrowly defined search criteria (Supplement 1, 2, Supplementary Tables 1, 2). Additionally, the number of studies analyzed in each category was limited to those indexed in PubMed, indicating that some studies may have been omitted. However, because the objective of this study was to analyze overall research trends and narrow search criteria were consistently applied across all categories, the representativeness of the findings is likely to have been preserved.

Hybrid models combining multiple segmentation techniques have demonstrated superior performance in MSK imaging. However, the problem of limited datasets remains a major challenge. Recently, the use of foundation models pretrained on large-scale imaging datasets has gained traction not only in general computer vision but also across various medical imaging domains. Although several models trained on extensive medical imaging datasets have been proposed, the development and application of foundation models for MSK imaging remain scarce. This gap presents a significant challenge for the researchers in this field.

CONCLUSION

This study aimed to analyze research trends in deep learning-based segmentation in MSK imaging, providing insights into current advancements and potential future directions. The intensity of the research activity varied depending on the anatomical region, availability of public datasets, joint location, and model development complexity.

From a clinical perspective, previous studies have primarily focused on the spine and knee; however, recent research has expanded to the extremities and shoulders. Additionally, deep learning-based segmentation remains highly active in traditional areas, such as body composition analysis, bone age assessment, tumor segmentation, and fracture segmentation, while also expanding into inflammatory diseases and nerve segmentation. Notably, there is a growing integration of segmentation with detection and classification techniques, suggesting that deep learning technology may generate synergistic effects across various applications.

From a technical perspective, recent models have relied predominantly on CNN-based architectures. However, the advent of transformers has led to the increased adoption of ViT-based attention mechanisms in computer vision, including MSK imaging applications. Notably, hybrid models that integrate the strengths of different architectures have demonstrated robust performances. Despite recent progress, generalizable models applicable across diverse imaging tasks and modalities remain limited. Future research that integrates multimodal imaging techniques is expected to play a significant role in advancing MSK imaging segmentation.

This study confirmed that deep learning-based segmentation in MSK imaging continues to evolve with expanding clinical applications. Further advancements in model refinement and dataset diversity are expected to enhance the accuracy and reliability of MSK imaging analysis.
