The associations between maternal disability and perinatal outcomes among Black and/or Hispanic women in PRAMS
Authors: Sarah E Harkins, Jianfang Liu, Maya Sabatello, Veronica Barcelona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen racialized as Black and/or Hispanic have high rates of adverse perinatal outcomes due to systemic racism. It is unknown if rates are higher among Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities, who may experience additional barriers to perinatal healthcare, including disability-based discrimination (i.e., ableism). Therefore, we examined the associations between maternal disability and preterm birth (< 37 weeks’ gestation), low birthweight (< 2500 g), small for gestational age (< 10th percentile), and attendance at a postpartum health visit among Black and/or Hispanic women.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) data from participants who identified as Black and/or Hispanic between 2018 and 2019 across 22 jurisdictions (N = 9,034). Maternal disability was the primary exposure measured using the Washington Group-Short Set of Questions on Functioning, which asks respondents to rate their level of difficulty seeing, hearing, walking or climbing steps, remembering or concentrating, self-care, and communicating. Response options include “no difficulty,” “some difficulty,” “a lot of difficulty,” and “I cannot do this at all.” We examined differences in perinatal outcomes by overall disability status, disability severity, and disability type. We adjusted for demographic and clinical factors using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThe prevalence of disability was 7.7%. There were no significant differences in preterm birth or low birthweight by maternal disability. Participants with any disability were significantly less likely to have a small for gestational age infant (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.56, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.37, 0.86) and to attend the postpartum visit compared to participants without disabilities (aOR = 0.38, 95% CI = 0.25, 0.57). We also found that as the level of self-reported difficulty increased, the odds of attending a postpartum health visit significantly decreased. Participants who reported blindness/low vision, cognition disability, communication disability, or multiple disabilities had significantly lower odds of attending the visit compared to participants without disabilities.ConclusionsMaternal disability was associated with lower odds of attending a postpartum health visit among Black and/or Hispanic participants in PRAMS, while small for gestational age differed by disability status and severity. Future research should identify barriers to postpartum healthcare for Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities to inform culturally and disability-specific interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09525-z.Keywords: Disability, Low birthweight, Perinatal health equity, Postpartum health, Preterm birth

Background

Racial and ethnic disparities in adverse perinatal outcomes are stark, persistent, and growing in the United States (US) [1]. Black women experience the highest rates of preterm birth (< 37 weeks’ gestation), low birthweight (< 2500 g), and small for gestational age infants (birthweight < 10th percentile for gestational age and sex), while Hispanic women have experienced the largest percent increase in preterm birth since 2014 [2]. Women racialized as Black and/or Hispanic also have increased rates of pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. However, Black and/or Hispanic women have low attendance at the postpartum health visit, which is crucial for identifying and treating pregnancy-related morbidities such as sepsis and depression [5]. These perinatal health inequities are the result of a legacy of discrimination [6, 7], as researchers have identified racism as a leading risk factor for adverse perinatal outcomes [8–10].

Women with disabilities, including those with long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments, which may limit full participation in society when interacting with attitudinal and environmental barriers [11], also have increased risks of adverse perinatal outcomes [12, 13]. There is heterogeneity in these risks based on disability severity and type. For example, the odds of preterm birth and low birthweight were significantly higher among women who reported experiencing greater difficulty across functional domains compared to women who reported no difficulties in a national study of pregnancy [14]. In addition, compared to women without disabilities, women with sensory disabilities had the highest risks of preterm birth, while women with intellectual disabilities had the highest risks of low birthweight and small for gestational age infants in a study of 19 hospitals across the US [12]. Little is known about the association between disability-based discrimination (i.e., ableism) and perinatal outcomes. However, women with disabilities have reported experiences of discrimination from clinicians who have questioned their parenting capabilities and encountered structural barriers preventing accessible perinatal care [15, 16].

The intersectionality framework posits that people with multiple marginalized identities experience compounded disadvantage [17]. Therefore, Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities may have an increased risk of adverse perinatal outcomes due to the synergistic influences of racism and ableism. For example, racism has been linked to adverse perinatal outcomes through biological and social pathways, including chronic stress, cellular aging, and weathering [18, 19], as well as structural barriers to high-quality pregnancy care and resources [20, 21], and interpersonal bias in clinical settings [22]. Ableism may further intensify this pathway by increasing barriers to accessible perinatal care and compounding psychosocial stress.

In a recent study of pregnancy, researchers found that 16.9% of Black women had a moderate disability and 21.5% had a severe disability, while corresponding estimates among Hispanic women were 13.7% and 13.8%, respectively [14]. Despite this prevalence, little is known about the perinatal health of Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities. One study found no significant differences in preterm birth or small for gestational age between Black and White women with intellectual or developmental disabilities [23]. Another study reported that Black women with intellectual or developmental disabilities had a 70% higher odds of preterm birth compared to White women with intellectual or developmental disabilities [24]. Though important, few researchers have studied the influence of disability, and the increased potential for discrimination and inaccessibility, on perinatal outcomes among Black and/or Hispanic women. In addition, studies have not examined the nuances in risk of perinatal outcomes by disability severity or type among Black and/or Hispanic women. Further, most studies have used White women as the comparison group, which upholds the notion of a “default” childbearing woman, and does not consider exposure to racism as a contributor to perinatal inequities [25, 26]. The rates of adverse perinatal outcomes among White women are also higher than the Healthy People 2030 childbirth objectives [27], making them an unsuitable reference group for goal-setting.

Therefore, the purpose of this cross-sectional study was to examine the associations between maternal disability and preterm birth, low birthweight, small for gestational age, and attendance at a postpartum health visit among Black and/or Hispanic women. We hypothesized that women with disabilities would be more likely to experience adverse birth outcomes and less likely to attend a postpartum health visit compared to women without disabilities.

Methods

Data source

We conducted a secondary analysis of Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) data from 2018 to 2019. PRAMS is an annual population-based surveillance project conducted across 46 US jurisdictions [28]. The PRAMS core survey is distributed to all jurisdictions and collects self-reported maternal behaviors and experiences. Each jurisdiction uses birth certificates to obtain a stratified random sample of approximately 100–250 postpartum women per month. Stratification variables are unique to each jurisdiction, though commonly stratified variables include maternal race, ethnicity, birthweight, and geographic location. PRAMS surveys are mailed to women two to four months after birth. If there is no response after three mailed attempts, women are interviewed by telephone to complete the surveys. PRAMS surveys are linked to birth certificate data and deidentified prior to distribution. Informed consent documents are provided to PRAMS participants, and consent is implied if the survey is completed. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Institutional Review Board at Columbia University approved this study (AAAV2497).

Exposure

Maternal disability was the primary exposure of interest. Disability was measured using the Washington Group-Short Set of Questions on Functioning (WG-SS), which has been validated in perinatal populations [29]. This self-report measure asks respondents to rate the level of difficulty they experience across six domains of functioning, including (1) seeing, (2) hearing, (3) walking or climbing steps, (4) remembering or concentrating, (5) self-care, and (6) communicating. Response options include “no difficulty,” “some difficulty,” “a lot of difficulty,” and “I cannot do this at all.”

We analyzed disability in three ways: (1) as a binary variable for overall disability, (2) as a categorical summary variable for disability severity, and (3) as a nominal categorical variable for disability type. The binary measure of overall disability was classified based on responses of “a lot of difficulty” or “I cannot do this at all” to any of the WG-SS items, which is consistent with recommendations by the Washington Group and prior PRAMS publications [30]. We followed other recommended thresholds to create a categorical summary variable using the highest level of self-reported difficulty as a proxy measure of disability severity [31]. ‘No difficulty’ was indicated by responses of “no difficulty” to all six WG-SS items; ‘Some difficulty’ was operationalized by responses of “some difficulty” to at least one WG-SS item, but no responses of “a lot of difficulty” or “I cannot do this at all;” ‘A lot of difficulty’ was measured based on responses of “a lot of difficulty” or “I cannot do this at all” to any WG-SS item.

We also created a nominal categorical variable to study differences by disability type. We used items 1–3 of the WG-SS to identify blind/low vision participants, deaf/hard of hearing participants, and participants with a physical disability, respectively. Items 4–6 of the WG-SS (i.e., cognition, self-care, communication) are not accurate measures to represent people with intellectual and developmental disabilities or those with mental disabilities (e.g., schizophrenia). As others have suggested [32], we examined all WG-SS items as individual disabilities based on responses of “a lot of difficulty” or “I cannot do this at all” to each functional domain (i.e., blind/low vision, deaf/hard of hearing, physical disability, cognition disability, self-care disability, communication disability, or more than one disability).

Outcomes

The four outcome variables were preterm birth, low birthweight, small for gestational age, and attendance at a postpartum health visit by the time of survey completion. The adverse birth outcomes were treated as binary variables in accordance with their clinical indicators, including preterm birth (< 37 weeks’ gestation), low birthweight (< 2500 g), and small for gestational age (birthweight < 10th percentile for gestational age and sex). Attendance at a postpartum health visit was measured as a binary variable based on responses to the PRAMS item, “Since your new baby was born, have you had a postpartum checkup for yourself? A postpartum checkup is the regular checkup a woman has about 4–6 weeks after she gives birth.”

Covariates

We adjusted for maternal age at birth (17 years, 18–34 years, 35 years), education (less than high school, high school diploma/GED, some college or greater), history of preterm birth (yes/no), parity (nullipara, primipara, multipara), pre-pregnancy body mass index (<18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, 30), and any smoking during pregnancy (yes/no) for models predicting adverse birth outcomes, and maternal education (less than high school, high school diploma/GED, some college or greater), insurance status (Medicaid, private, other), and income ($40,000, $40,001-$85,000, >$85,000) for analyses predicting attendance at a postpartum health visit. Covariates were selected a priori based on theory and prior research, and differ between analyses for birth outcomes and the postpartum visit to reflect the different confounding pathways for clinical outcomes versus health care utilization [5].

Study sample

PRAMS added the WG-SS in 2018 as an optional questionnaire [33]. The analytic sample included participants who reported their race as Black (regardless of ethnicity) or their ethnicity as Hispanic (regardless of racial identification) from the 22 PRAMS jurisdictions that administered the WG-SS in years 2018 or 2019 and had at least a 50% response rate (See Supplemental Table 1 for full list of included jurisdictions). As Hispanic is an ethnicity and not a race, the sample consisted of Non-Hispanic Black participants and Hispanic participants of any race. We only included years 2018 and 2019 to avoid confounding of the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, we limited the sample to singleton gestations for models predicting adverse birth outcomes.

Statistical analyses

We calculated descriptive statistics for all variables of interest and conducted bivariate analyses between disability and perinatal outcomes using Chi-square tests. We then employed multivariable logistic regression to estimate adjusted associations. We conducted subgroup analyses using mutually exclusive categories to examine differences between Non-Hispanic Black participants and Hispanic participants of any race, including Black. Participants who identified as Hispanic Black were included only in the Hispanic subgroup.

In addition, we conducted complete-case analyses based on all included variables. Alpha was set to < 0.05 for statistical significance, and we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings and control for multiple testing using Bonferroni adjustments. PRAMS data are weighted to account for the complex survey design, non-coverage, and non-response, and are required to estimate population prevalence. We conducted all analyses using weighted PRAMS data. Consistent with prior PRAMS publications [34], we report unweighted sample sizes and weighted percentages in the results and tables. We performed analyses using the survey packages in SAS 9.4 to adjust for survey weights and strata information. We adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies [35].

Results

The unweighted sample included 9,034 participants who identified as Black and/or Hispanic. We describe the demographics of the sample in Table 1. Most participants were non-Hispanic Black (56.5%), aged 18–34 years (82.5%), had an annual household income of <$40,000 (72.8%), and received Medicaid (63.3%). In the total sample, 7.7% of participants reported any disability, including 8.1% of non-Hispanic Black participants and 7.2% of Hispanic participants. The most frequently reported disability was cognition disability (2.8%), while self-care disability had the lowest prevalence (< 0.1%).

We present the two-way frequency distribution between overall disability and perinatal outcomes in Table 2. Participants with any disability were less likely to have a small for gestational age infant (5.9%) compared to participants without disabilities (10.0%). Fewer participants with any disability (72.2%) attended a postpartum health visit than participants without disabilities (88.1%). Rates of preterm birth were similar between participants with any disability (10.7%) and without disabilities (10.0%), as were rates of low birthweight (9.6%, 9.4%, respectively).

Non-Hispanic Black participants with any disability were significantly less likely to have a small for gestational age infant compared to non-Hispanic Black participants without disabilities (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI = 0.31, 0.81). Discussion

In this study of participants who identified as Black and/or Hispanic in PRAMS, we identified significant inequities in the receipt of a postpartum health visit by maternal disability. We also found no significant differences in preterm birth or low birthweight by overall disability, severity, or type, including multiple disabilities. However, we found that the odds of small for gestational age differed based on how disability was defined.

Our findings align with some previous research on disability and perinatal outcomes. A study of Rhode Island PRAMS data found that women who reported a ‘physical, mental, or emotional problem’ were significantly less likely to attend a postpartum health visit, though they did not stratify by race or ethnicity [13]. Another study among women with intellectual or developmental disabilities reported that Black women had significantly higher odds of preterm birth compared to White women [24]. In addition, a recent PRAMS study found no significant differences in small for gestational age by disability severity [14]. Our study addresses important knowledge gaps by identifying differences in the odds of perinatal outcomes by disability severity and type among Black and/or Hispanic women, which is critical to developing more tailored interventions to improve care.

We identified consistent inequities in attendance at a postpartum visit among Black and/or Hispanic participants by disability status, severity, and type. These disparities are particularly concerning, as rates of pregnancy-related death are highest during the postpartum period. For example, a review of 36 US states from 2017 to 2019 found that 23.3% of pregnancy-related deaths occurred between 7 and 42 days postpartum, and over 80% of all pregnancy-related deaths were considered preventable [36]. Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities also have increased risks of severe maternal morbidity and mortality [37, 38], underscoring the urgent need to remove individual and systems-level barriers to attending postpartum care.

Individual-level barriers to the postpartum visit may include fatigue, pain, and competing caregiving and recovery demands experienced after birth [39]. These factors may be exacerbated by structural barriers for Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities, such as inaccessible care environments, limited transportation, gaps in health insurance coverage, and prior discriminatory experiences in perinatal health settings [16, 40]. Expanded postpartum care models that extend beyond a single visit and incorporate several modalities, such as home-based care and telehealth may improve postpartum surveillance [41]. Clinician training and use of shared decision-making, a practice in which clinicians and women together discuss and decide on all perinatal care plans, may also enhance clinician-patient trust and postpartum engagement [42]. In addition, postpartum programs led by community midwives and doulas may further improve postpartum care utilization and health [43].

There are several potential explanations for the null results for preterm birth and low birthweight and inconsistent findings for small for gestational age. One explanation is that Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities received appropriate services and supports, and may have been followed closely by their care team, mitigating their risk of adverse birth outcomes. However, the wide breadth of published research in this area identifies significant inequities in preterm birth and low birthweight by disability status [12, 14, 44]. In qualitative studies, women with disabilities reported dissatisfaction with the current perinatal care system, often due to experiences of discrimination from providers and inaccessible care settings [16, 45, 46].

Another hypothesis to explain the null findings includes misclassification of the study exposure. Researchers have reported that recommended thresholds for defining disability using the WG-SS resulted in a lower disability prevalence compared to a binary impairment measure (e.g., ‘do you have a physical disability’) in an agreement study of 2,895 adult participants in New Zealand [47]. In addition, the WG-SS does not accurately identify people with mental, intellectual, or developmental disabilities [48]. Therefore, we are potentially missing populations of women with disabilities in the study sample. This misclassification may have affected our null findings, particularly given evidence that women with intellectual disabilities have among the highest risks of adverse birth outcomes compared to other disability groups [12].

Finally, selection bias may have contributed to the null findings. It is possible that women with disabilities were less likely to participate in a PRAMS survey or to disclose a disability if they did participate. Further, disability may have been underreported due to inaccessible survey formats or discomfort and fear of disclosing disability status in a government sponsored survey, leading to bias in the direction of the null. For example, women with intellectual and developmental disabilities have reported withholding their disability status from obstetric clinicians out of fear of being referred to child protective services (CPS) [16, 49]. These concerns are warranted, as parents with emotional or behavioral disabilities, learning disabilities, or intellectual and developmental disabilities are overrepresented in CPS investigations at a rate of two to six times higher than parents without disabilities [50]. This is important to consider among a sample of Black and/or Hispanic women, as women of color are also disproportionately overrepresented in CPS cases [51]. Therefore, the true proportion of Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities in our sample is likely higher than reported due to structural gendered racism and ableism.

Clinical Implications

More research is needed to better understand the risks of adverse birth outcomes and barriers to postpartum care among Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities. However, there are several evidence-based practice recommendations to implement now to improve perinatal care. First, addressing discrimination in pregnancy care is essential. Health professions’ education must include disability competency training and education on racism and ableism as primary drivers of perinatal inequities among racially and ethnically minoritized women with disabilities [52]. Including women with diverse types of disabilities on patient advisory boards and partnering with disability organizations is critical to advance health equity [53]. Further, providing patient-centered care that supports the autonomy of all women to birth and raise children is essential to promoting trust in the patient-provider relationship [54].

Second, perinatal care must be accessible. Clinicians and perinatal settings should implement strategies recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists in a recent committee statement to improve physical, procedural, and communication accessibility for women with disabilities [55]. To ensure physical access, examination tables should be height-adjustable with specialized equipment available. For procedural access, staff should ask about accommodation needs prior to the first visit, document them in the electronic health record, and ensure they are provided at all visits. Communication access can be improved by using plain language, providing documents in large font and screen-reader compatible formats, and offering in-person sign language interpreters.

Research implications

There is an urgent need for future research to understand and address barriers to postpartum healthcare for Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities. Future intervention studies may evaluate the acceptability of home-based postpartum care programs and emphasize co-design with women with disabilities [56]. Larger, nationally representative and prospective studies of pregnancy are needed to better understand associations between disability and adverse birth outcomes among Black and/or Hispanic women. In addition, researchers should include a “select all that apply” measure of self-identified disability to more accurately represent different disability groups in quantitative studies of perinatal health. Researchers should also employ the intersectionality framework to study the perinatal health of other minoritized women with disabilities due to their sexual identity, age, or socioeconomic status. Finally, researchers should examine the influence of racial and disability-based discrimination on perinatal outcomes to better understand the role of racism and ableism on perinatal inequities.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths, including being among the first to examine disability and perinatal outcomes among Black and/or Hispanic women. In addition, the 22 jurisdictions included in this analysis are representative of Black and/or Hispanic women from the Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, and Southwest regions of the US. This geographic diversity is important as Hispanic women differ in country of origin and acculturation by US region [57]. The use of a self-reported measure of disability is another strength as ICD codes have a low sensitivity [58].

This study also had limitations. Disability was measured using the WG-SS, which represents functional domains and not necessarily specific types of disabilities. Therefore, we could not examine perinatal outcomes for women with intellectual or developmental disabilities or mental disabilities in this study. Although we used recommended thresholds for defining disability with the WG-SS, it is possible that some participants were misclassified. The prevalence of disability in the study samples were low, precluding analyses by different levels of difficulty for each functional domain and by disability type within non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic groups. In addition, PRAMS data are not nationally representative and do not represent comprehensive surveillance of pregnancy health for the entire US. Therefore, findings may reflect state-level differences in policies such as Medicaid expansion for postpartum care and disability access requirements. PRAMS also does not include classifications for preterm birth by underlying cause, precluding analyses by etiology. Further, PRAMS data used in this study were collected prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. While perinatal health inequities have not improved in post-pandemic years, future research using more recent PRAMS data is needed to increase generalizability. Finally, the cross-sectional design of this study does not consider temporality to infer causation.

Conclusions

Maternal disability was associated with significantly lower odds of attending a postpartum health visit among Black and/or Hispanic participants in PRAMS. There were no significant differences in preterm birth or low birthweight by maternal disability, and the findings for small for gestational age differed by disability status and severity. Larger, prospective studies of pregnancy are needed to better understand the influence of disability and ableism on adverse pregnancy outcomes. Future research should also prioritize understanding the barriers to postpartum healthcare for Black and/or Hispanic women with disabilities to inform culturally and disability-specific interventions.
