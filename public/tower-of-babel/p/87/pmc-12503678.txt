Statin use in people with HIV
Authors: Raynell Lang, M John Gill, Jacqueline M McMillan
Published: 2025

People with HIV have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease

Despite advancements in treatment and HIV viral suppression, people with HIV experience a risk of myocardial infarction that is 1.73 times higher than in people without HIV.1 Believed to be in part a result of chronic low-grade inflammation, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) results in substantial morbidity and mortality.1

Statins can reduce this increased risk

In 2023, a randomized controlled trial (that included 7769 people living with HIV aged 40–75 years with low-to-moderate risk of ASCVD who were given either daily pitavastatin or placebo) was stopped early after 5 years, because the group using a statin had a 35% lower risk of major cardiovascular events than those given placebo.2 This reduction was larger than that expected for decreasing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol alone, suggesting other potential anti-inflammatory effects.2

Guidelines recommend statin use for people with HIV aged 40 years and over

People with HIV aged 40–75 years with 10-year ASCVD risk estimates of 5% to < 20% should receive at least moderate-intensity daily statin therapy (pitavastatin 4 mg, atorvastatin 20 mg, or rosuvastatin 10 mg).3,4 People with HIV with ASCVD risk of 0%–5% should have a shared decisionmaking discussion with their provider about the risks and benefits of statin initiation, as it has modest benefit.3,4 Given insufficient data among people with HIV younger than 40 years, as in the general population, statin therapy should be considered in select populations, such as people with diabetes.3,5

Statin therapy is typically well tolerated, but assessment for drug–drug interactions and monitoring of adverse events is recommended

Adverse events — including muscle-related symptoms (myopathy and rhabdomyolysis), liver function abnormalities, and incident diabetes — should be discussed with patients and monitored.4 Statin therapy should be discontinued if a person becomes pregnant or is breastfeeding. Those planning to conceive should not be initiated on a statin.3,4

Lifestyle modification should be recommended

In addition to statin use, clinicians should discuss lifestyle modifications, including dietary intake, exercise, smoking cessation, reducing alcohol consumption, and maintaining a healthy body weight with patients with HIV.4
