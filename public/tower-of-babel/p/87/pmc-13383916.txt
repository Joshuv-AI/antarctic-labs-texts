Mechanistic investigation of Liujing Toutong tablet in the treatment of cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury in rats by the integration of serum pharmacochemistry and metabolomics
Authors: Zihan Yu, Ke Pei, Xiulin Wang, Wenling Li, Hongchang Li, Wangyang Xie, Xianglong Meng, Tingting Zhao, Yan Ning, Haixin Liu, Wenbin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiujing Toutong tablet (LTT) is a traditional Chinese patent medicine. Previous studies have demonstrated that LTT exerts a protective effect in permanent cerebral ischemia and cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury (CIRI). However, the active compounds and underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This investigation aimed to integrate the analyses of serum pharmacochemistry, network pharmacology, and metabolomics to elucidate the therapeutic effects of LTT on CIRI.MethodsThe therapeutic effects of LTT were evaluated in the CIRI rat model, which was induced using the thread embolism method. The prototype active compounds of LTT absorbed into the serum (LPCs) were identified using ultra‐performance liquid chromatography‐quadrupole/orbitrap high‐resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC‐Q‐Exactive‐Orbitrap/MS). Potential targets of LPCs were determined using network pharmacological analysis. A combination of metabolomics and network pharmacology was employed to identify upstream key targets and downstream endogenous metabolites. The predicted mechanisms were then verified by molecular docking. Finally, key targets and signaling pathways were examined using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and Western blot.ResultsA total of 27 LPCs were identified as the active ingredients. Furthermore, glycerophospholipid and arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism were also identified, with PTGS2, ALOX5, and AchE as the upstream key targets, and phosphorylcholine, AA, phosphatidate (PA), hosphatidylcholines (PCs), and lysophosphatidylcholines (LysoPCs) as the core endogenous metabolites. Additionally, LPCs binding with these key targets might reduce the inflammatory response via nuclear factor Kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway.ConclusionLTT may effectively alleviate symptoms of CIRI by acting on PTGS2, ALOX5, and AchE, improving the glycerophospholipid and AA metabolism and reducing the inflammatory response by inhibiting NF‐κB signaling pathway‐related proteins.

INTRODUCTION

Stroke includes both ischemic and hemorrhagic types, with ischemic stroke accounting for the majority. It is associated with high mortality and disability globally. 1 During the processes of ischemia and reperfusion, severe oxidative stress and inflammatory responses are triggered, exacerbating brain tissue damage, a phenomenon known as cerebral ischemia and cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury (CIRI). 2 Currently, clinical treatment for ischemic stroke primarily involves intravenous thrombolysis combined with neuroprotection and intra‐arterial thrombectomy, with tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) being the only FDA‐approved treatment regimen. 3 However, the limitations of tPA can aggravate injury and diminish the benefits provided by reperfusion of the occluded artery. 4 Therefore, the identification of novel therapeutic agents for CIRI is urgently required.

LTT, listed in Volume 20 of the Drug Standard of the Ministry of Health, is a national second‐grade protected species of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). It consists of nine herbs, including Angelica dahurica (Hoffm.) Benth. & Hook.f. ex Franch. & Sav., Magnolia biondii Pamp., Ligusticum sinense Oliv., Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort., Pueraria lobata (Willd.) Ohwi, Asarum sieboldii Miq., Ligustrum lucidum W.T. Aiton, Leonurus Japonicus Houtt., and the oil of Schizonepeta tenuisfolia Briq., which have been long used for headaches, migraines, and nervous headaches. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Clinically, LTT is also used for stroke, 9 and several studies have shown that it could improve angiogenesis and reduce neuroinflammation by downregulation of calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) and nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity, upregulation of β‐endorphin (β‐EP), endothelin (ET), and dopamine (DA) expression, increasing cerebral blood flow and decreasing both whole blood and plasma viscosity. 10 , 11 Angiogenesis and antineuroinflammation are crucial to the modulation of brain function after stroke. 12 , 13 Our previous investigation found that LTT reduces the extent of brain damage in rats with permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO), with the mechanism potentially involving the reduction of neuroinflammatory damage through inhibition of the nuclear factor Kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway and regulation of the CKLF1/CCR5 axis. 14 However, the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of LTT on CIRI remain unclear.

TCM has been widely used to prevent and treat diseases in Asian countries for thousands of years. However, elucidating the therapeutic and underlying action mechanisms of TCM is still challenging due to the complexity of its chemical composition and the lack of defined therapeutic targets. Network pharmacology, which is based on systems biology theory, has been widely employed to explore interactions between components and upstream key targets. 15 Similarly, metabolomics, which focuses on changes in endogenous metabolic substances associated with disease progression, has been used extensively to elucidate the potential mechanisms of TCM. The altered endogenous components, regarded as the downstream effects of TCM, can also exert biological effects on the body. 16 In this study, we constructed the relationship networks of drug‐component‐target‐disease and compound‐reaction‐enzyme‐gene to investigate the correlations among active pharmaceutical ingredients, upstream key targets, and downstream core metabolites and uncover the mechanism of action of LTT in treating CIRI. Finally, the predicted mechanisms were validated using Western blot (WB) and ELISA analyses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and reagents

LTT was purchased from Shanxi Ren Yuan Tang Pharmaceutical Industry Co., Ltd. (Shanxi, China, lot number: 20210607). Nimodipine was obtained from the Central Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China, lot number: 2105012). Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC–MS) grade acetonitrile (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA, lot number: 223164) and methanol (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA, lot number: F25P24202) were purchased from Fisher Scientific. Ultrapure water was prepared using an UPR system (ULUPURE, Sichuan, China). The BCA protein concentration assay kit (lot number: AR0146) was obtained from Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Primary antibodies for NF‐κB p65 (lot number: ab76302), IκBα (lot number: ab32518), and p‐IκBα (lot number: ab133462) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom). The primary antibody for p‐IKKα (lot number: 2697) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA). Rat tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‐α, lot number: RA20035) and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β, lot number: RA20020) ELISA kits were purchased from Bioswamp (Wuhan, China). Rat arachidonate 5‐lipoxygenase (ALOX5, lot number: F21224‐A), prostaglandin G/H synthase 2 (PTGS2, lot number: F0016‐RA), and acetylcholinesterase (AchE, lot number: F3674‐A) ELISA kits were obtained from Shanghai Kexing Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Animals, construction of CIRI model, and drug administration

Healthy male Sprague–Dawley rats, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 180–220 g, were provided by Beijing SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China, license number: SCXK‐Jing 2019‐0010). The rats were housed in an air‐conditioned animal facility with a natural light–dark cycle for 1 week before experimentation. The room temperature was maintained at 22–26°C, with relative humidity set at 60% ± 10%. The study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi University of Chinese Medicine (AWE202209146).

The rats were randomly divided into six groups, with 14 rats per group: sham group (Sham), CIRI model group (Model), positive control group (Nimodipine, Nim), LTT high‐dosage group (LTT‐H), LTT medium‐dosage group (LTT‐M), and LTT low‐dosage group (LTT‐L). Nimodipine (200 mg/kg) or LTT (0.7, 1.4, 2.8 g/kg) was administered daily via intragastric gavage. The drug administration dose or period was determined based on the preliminary experimental results of the research groups and the characteristics of the CIRI model. The equivalent dose of LTT for an adult of 60 kg weight was 0.11 g/kg per day, which was converted to a low dose for rats (0.7 g/kg). Based on the low dose of LTT, the medium and high doses were set. The dosages of LTT were based on clinically recommended human doses. The model and sham groups were given a 0.4% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose solution, whereas the LTT and Nim groups received the respective treatments in a 0.4% carboxymethyl cellulose sodium suspension. Treatment continued for 7 days.

The CIRI model was established by inserting a nylon suture into the internal carotid artery (ICA). 17 Before modeling, rats were fasted for 12 h, anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and placed in a supine position. The left common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA), and ICA were isolated through an incision along the middle of the rat's neck. After the left ECA and CCA were ligated, a segment of the ICA was clamped with an arterial clip, and a small incision was made at the CCA using ophthalmic scissors. A nylon monofilament (diameter 0.25–0.28 mm) coated with a silicone tip was slowly inserted into the ICA to a depth of approximately 18 mm, blocking the middle cerebral artery's blood flow for 90 min. In the sham group, the same procedure was performed, except for the insertion of the nylon monofilament.

Pharmacodynamic evaluation of LTT for the treatment of CIRI

Zea Longa method

Neurological deficit evaluation was conducted on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 following CIRI surgery using the Zea Longa method. 18 The criteria for neurological deficit evaluation were as follows: 0, normal (no neurological deficit); 1, mild neurological deficit (the rat failed to fully extend the left forepaw); 2, moderate neurological deficit (the rat turned to the opposite side when the tail was lifted but walked normally); 3, severe neurological deficit (the rat spun or leaned to the opposite side while walking); 4, inability to walk spontaneously (the rat fell to the opposite side); 5, death. A higher score indicated a more severe neurological deficit. Rats scoring between 1 and 4 were included in subsequent experiments. The number of deaths and the body weight of surviving rats in each group were recorded daily.

Adhesive removal test

The adhesive removal test was used to assess sensorimotor impairments following CIRI surgery. 19 Rats were placed on a platform (45 × 35 cm2) for 1 min to familiarize themselves with the environment. A square adhesive label (0.7 × 0.7 cm2) was attached to the hairless forepaw on the contralateral side, and the corresponding area of the other forepaw was also pinched with the same force. The rats were allowed to move freely on the platform for 120 s while two timers were activated. The first timer was stopped when the rat contacted the adhesive label with its incisor or paw, or when the paw was shaken. This time was recorded as the label perception time, reflecting the sensory ability of the rat. The second timer was stopped when the rat completely removed the adhesive label, representing the motor ability of the rat. The label perception and removal times were recorded on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 after CIRI surgery. Each rat was tested thrice, with at least a 10‐min interval between tests. All rats underwent a 3‐day training period before the experiments.

Hot‐plate test

The hot‐plate test was conducted on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post‐CIRI surgery, following the method described previously. 20 Each rat was placed on a hot plate maintained at 55 ± 0.5°C to measure paw withdrawal thermal latency, defined as the time it took for the rat to lick its hind paws or jump off the surface. The baseline latency was assessed 1 day before surgery, and rats with a baseline latency of less than 5 s or greater than 25 s were excluded from the study.

Laser speckle contrast imaging test

Cerebral blood flow and microvascular distribution in rats were monitored using a laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) system (RFLSI, Shenzhen, China). Real‐time cerebral blood flow was recorded for 20 s on day 7 after CIRI surgery to assess the degree of brain damage.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to analyze histopathological injury in brain tissue after CIRI surgery. Brain tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution and then embedded in paraffin wax. Coronal brain slices were prepared from the paraffin‐embedded tissue and stained with H&E. Histopathological changes were observed using an optical microscope (× 400).

Serum pharmacochemistry and metabolomics analysis

Sample collection and preprocessing

Blood samples were collected via the abdominal aorta. A 100 μL aliquot of serum was mixed with 300 μL of methanol to denature proteins. The mixture was vortexed and then centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was transferred to a vial for UHPLC–MS/MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples, prepared by pooling 10 μL of each sample, were used to assess the stability of the experimental analysis system. One QC sample was injected after every 12 serum samples throughout the analysis.

UHPLC–MS/MS analysis

Serum metabolomics analysis was performed using an UPLC‐Q‐Exactive‐Orbitrap/MS system. Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Ultimate 3000 system (Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), consisting of a binary pump (HPG‐3400RS), an autosampler (WPS‐3000), a column compartment (TCC‐3000), and an online degasser. Separation was carried out using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and 0.05% (v/v) aqueous formic acid (B) and was used for the gradient elution as follows: 0–0.5 min: 5% A–5% A; 0.5–1.5 min: 5% A–15% A; 1.5–4.5 min: 15% A–30% A; 4.5–7 min: 30% A–60% A; 7–11 min: 60% A–70% A; 11–12 min: 70% A–100% A; 12–13 min: 100% A–100% A; 13–13.5 min: 100% A–5% A; 13.5–16 min: 5% A–5% A. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min.

Mass detection was performed using a quadrupole–orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface. The following mass spectrometer settings were optimized: sheath gas flow rate, 40 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 5 arb; sweep gas flow rate, 1 arb; spray voltage, 3.2 kV; capillary temperature, 320°C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350°C; S‐lens RF level, 50. Full MS/dd‐MS2 scanning was employed with a mass range of m/z 100–1000, fast switching between positive and negative acquisition modes, MS resolution of 70 000 FWHM, and MS2 resolution of 35 000 FWHM. The automatic gain control target and maximum injection time were set to 2 × 105 and 50 ms, respectively.

Data were processed using Xcalibur software version 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). LPCs were identified by comparing the detected and calculated mass, with an acceptable mass error of less than 10 ppm. MS2 data were matched against reference standards and literature data.

Metabolomics analysis

Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD 3.1) software was used to preprocess raw mass spectrometry data, including peak extraction, peak alignment, peak matching, and peak intensity correction. The raw data were imported into CD 3.1 software for processing, and the processed data were subsequently imported into SIMCA 14.1 software for multivariate statistical analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA) were employed for the detection of target metabolites. A 200‐permutation test was performed to evaluate the accuracy of the OPLS‐DA model. 21

Variable importance in projection (VIP, p > 1.0) from OPLS‐DA and t‐test (p < 0.05) were used to identify differentially expressed metabolites among the sham, model, and LTT administration groups. The selected metabolites were identified using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca/), LIPID MAPS Database (https://www.lipidmaps.org/), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/k), or relevant literature. The identified metabolites were then imported into MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) for metabolic pathway analysis. Finally, Cytoscape 3.7.1 software was used to construct a metabolic network diagram depicting the differential metabolites and their associated enzymes or genes.

Targets of LPC treatment for CIRI

The potential targets of LPCs were identified using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, https://tcmspw.com/) and the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). Gene names for the identified targets were verified using the UniProt database (https://www.UniProt.org/). Next, the keywords “cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury” and “cerebral ischemia–reperfusion” were used to retrieve CIRI‐related targets from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://omim.org/). 22 The intersections of LPCs‐related and CIRI‐related targets were identified using the Venny tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), which were considered potential pharmacodynamic targets of LPCs for treating CIRI. A drug‐compound‐target network diagram was constructed using Cytoscape 3.7.2 software.

Identification of upstream targets of differential metabolites

The upstream targets of the differential metabolites were identified using the Cytoscape 3.7.2 Metscape plugin. The differential metabolites identified in the “Metabolomics Analysis” section and the core proteins identified in the “Targets of LPCs Treatment for CIRI” section were entered into the compounds and genes columns, respectively. The “human” organism was selected, and a pathway‐based network was constructed. The upstream targets were visualized in a compound‐reaction‐enzyme‐gene diagram. In this analysis, metabolites with identifiable upstream targets were regarded as pivotal metabolites, and the upstream genes associated with these pivotal metabolites were considered core targets.

Molecular docking

The three‐dimensional (3D) structures of LPCs were generated using Chem3D software. The crystal structures of core targets, including PTGS1 (PDB ID: 6y3c), PTGS2 (PDB ID: 5f1a), ALOX5 (PDB ID: 6n2w), PLA2G2A (PDB ID: 1kqu), ACHE (PDB ID: 1vot), ALDH2 (PDB ID: 3inj), GSTM1 (PDB ID: 1xw6), AKR1B1 (PDB ID: 1ef3), and PLA2G7 (PDB ID: 5i9i), were obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Water molecules were removed from the structures of the core targets, and hydrogen atoms were added using PyMOL software. Molecular docking studies were conducted using the AutoDock program (version 1.5.6). The docking process was carried out using the Genetic Algorithm. Stable interactions between the core targets and their corresponding components were considered to occur when the binding energy was lower than −5 kcal/mol.

KEGG and GO analysis

Protein–protein interactions (PPI) for the core targets were analyzed using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/) and Cytoscape 3.7.1 software. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, and the creation of GO bar diagrams and KEGG bubble diagrams were performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) and R 4.1.2 software.

Validation of core targets and inflammatory factors using ELISA

Serum samples stored at −80°C were thawed at room temperature before analysis. Approximately 50 mg of brain tissue was weighed accurately and homogenized in an appropriate volume of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The mixture was homogenized for 90 s and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. Serum and brain tissue levels of PTGS2, ALOX5, and ACHE were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. To assess the effect of LTT on inflammation induced by CIRI, serum levels of downstream inflammatory factors of the NF‐κB signaling pathway, including TNF‐α and IL‐1β, were also measured using ELISA kits.

Western blot analysis

Rat brain tissue was thawed from −80°C and ground in ice‐cold RIPA lysis buffer followed by incubation for 15 min at 4°C. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12 000 rpm at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for Western blot analysis. The total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein samples were diluted in PBS, mixed with loading buffer (5 ×), and denatured by heating in a metal bath at 90°C for 5 min. Sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) was performed using a 10% gel, with 20 μg of total protein loaded per well. After separation by SDS‐PAGE, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, which was then blocked for 3 h with 5% skim milk prepared in TBST. Primary antibodies against NF‐κB p65, IκBα, P‐IκBα, and P‐IKKα were added and incubated overnight at 4°C. The horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:10 000 dilution) was then added and incubated for 4 h at room temperature. Target protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence, and quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9 software. All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and/or Student's t‐test were used to analyze differences among or between groups. Survival data were analyzed using the log‐rank (Mantel‐Cox) test and presented as survival curves. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Effects of LTT on CIRI rats

As shown in Figure 1A–C, CIRI surgery significantly reduced body weight, survival rate, and cerebral blood flow (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). Additionally, severe neurological impairment and motor dysfunction were observed in the model group rats (Figure 1D,E). However, LTT treatment resulted in weight gain (p < 0.05), while the survival rate increased, though not significantly (p > 0.05). Furthermore, cerebral blood flow in the ischemic hemisphere was restored following LTT treatment. CIRI surgery also lowered the pain threshold, which was elevated by LTT treatment (Figure 1F). Pseudo‐color map, speckle map, and reduction rate of cerebral blood flow perfusion volume result showed that the cerebral blood flow in the model group was significantly reduced, and this phenomenon could be alleviated by LTT treatment (p < 0.01) (Figure 1G,H). Histological analysis using H&E staining revealed disordered neuronal arrangement, pyknotic nuclei, widened intercellular spaces, and deepened staining in the model rats (Figure 1I). In contrast, LTT treatment ameliorated these pathological features (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). Therefore, LTT facilitated the recovery of neurological function and exhibited certain analgesic effects, contributing to the alleviation of brain injury. However, the active ingredients of LTT and their mechanisms of action remain unclear.

Identification of LPCs

A compound database for LTT, containing names, molecular formulas, accurate molecular weights, and chemical structures, was established through a comprehensive literature search. The peaks corresponding to these compounds were detectable in serum samples from the LTT group but not in the model group. Compounds were identified based on accurate mass measurements (mass error <10 ppm), primary and secondary mass spectra, fragmentation patterns, and reference to relevant literature. A total of 27 LPCs were detected in the serum samples, including 15 phenylpropanoids, 8 flavonoids, 3 butylphthalides, and 1 phenolic compound. Serum metabolomics analysis

Metabolic profile analysis

Representative TICs of serum samples from all groups under both positive and negative ion modes were obtained using UHPLC‐Q‐Orbitrap HRMS (Figure 3A,B). Multivariate statistical analysis was performed to characterize the metabolic disturbances induced by the CIRI model. PCA is an unsupervised multivariate technique frequently employed to examine clusters within metabolomics data. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of QC samples in both ion modes was calculated to assess the data quality and the reliability of LC–MS system. QC samples with RSD ≤30% constituted 77.55% and 78.70% of the total samples in the positive and negative ion modes, respectively, as shown in the corresponding bar charts. In PCA analysis, all QC samples clustered centrally, indicating that the data were reliable and the mass spectrometric system was accurate and stable (Figure S1). OPLS‐DA, a supervised method, was used to maximize the group separation. The OPLS‐DA results are shown in Figure 3C,D. Significant differences were observed between the sham and model groups. It could be clearly seen that LTT regulated the level of different metabolites in CIRI rats to (or near) normal level.

A heatmap was constructed to visualize the differences in metabolites across the five groups. Identification of upstream targets for differential metabolites

LTT consists of 9 medicinal herbs, from which 27 LPCs are derived. The Network Pharmacology Analysis approach was employed to predict the potential targets of these LPCs and their relevance to CIRI. A total of 577 targets for 27 LPCs and 1399 targets associated with the therapeutic effects of CIRI were identified. Using the Venny tool (Figure S3A), 158 common targets were obtained between LPCs and CIRI, which were considered as potential therapeutic targets for LPCs in the treatment of CIRI. The relationships among the 9 medicinal herbs, 27 LPCs, and 158 common targets were visualized in the drug‐compound‐target network (Figure S3B).

The differential metabolites identified in the metabolomics analysis and the common targets obtained through network pharmacology were imported into the Metscape plugin of Cytoscape 3.7.2 to construct compound‐reaction‐enzyme‐gene networks. The relationships between upstream proteins and downstream metabolites were then visualized. 21 The results are presented in Figure 6, where eight upstream targets, including PTGS1, PTGS2, ALOX5, PLA2G2A, ACHE, ALDH2, AKR1B1, and PLA2G7, were identified. Five corresponding downstream metabolites, including phosphorylcholine, arachidonic acid, PA, PCs, and LysoPCs, were identified as well, with involvement in two key metabolic pathways: “arachidonic acid metabolism” and “glycerophospholipid metabolism”.

Molecular docking was conducted to further analyze the interactions between upstream targets (PTGS1, PTGS2, ALOX5, PLA2G2A, AChE, ALDH2, AKR1B1, and PLA2G7) and the corresponding LPCs by calculating the binding energy (Table 3). The results were visualized using PyMOL software, with each target and its top binding compound shown in Figure 7A. Additional proteins exhibiting strong binding activity with active ingredients are presented in Figure S4. LPCs primarily bind to these core targets through hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and π–π stacking. The binding energy level reflects the binding strength of the compound to its target, with lower binding energies indicating stronger binding affinity. In this study, the binding energies of key targets and their corresponding LPCs were predominantly lower than −5.0 kcal/mol, suggesting strong binding activity of LPCs with the core targets. LTT inhibits NF‐κB signaling pathway

The KEGG results indicated that CIRI leads to the activation of NF‐κB, and inhibiting the NF‐κB cascade could offer significant therapeutic benefits in ischemia–reperfusion‐induced inflammation. NF‐κB is a central regulator of the neuroinflammatory response and plays a pivotal role in the polarization of microglia (M1 and M2). Upon external stimulation, phosphorylation of IκB is triggered, leading to the degradation of IκBα by the proteasome. Consequently, NF‐κB is released, activated, and translocated into the nucleus, where it induces the release of various pro‐inflammatory factors and chemokines. 37 Western blot analysis was performed to measure the expression of NF‐κB signaling pathway–related proteins, including NF‐κB p65, IκBα, and p‐IκBα in brain tissue. As shown in Figure 9A–C, the expression levels of NF‐κB p65 and the p‐IκBα/IκBα ratio were significantly elevated in the model group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). In contrast, the expression levels of NF‐κB p65 were significantly reduced in the LTT‐M and LTT‐H groups, whereas the p‐IκBα/IκBα ratio was significantly decreased in all treatment groups (p < 0.01).

DISCUSSION

TCM has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of ischemic stroke, primarily through its multicomponent and multitarget actions. However, due to the complexity of TCM ingredients, there is an urgent need for precise strategies to investigate the underlying mechanisms of action. 38 In the present study, we found that LTT exerted a therapeutic effect on CIRI rats, reducing mortality, improving neurological outcomes, alleviating pain, enhancing cerebral blood flow, and mitigating brain tissue damage. To elucidate the precise mechanism, we established an integrated approach combining serum pharmacochemistry, metabolomics, and network pharmacology analysis. Our findings suggest that the LPCs in LTT target key proteins, such as PTGS2, ALOX5, and AChE, regulate glycerophospholipid and AA metabolism and modulate the expression of proteins and inflammatory factors associated with the NF‐κB signaling pathway.

Network pharmacology has become an essential tool in analyzing the complex components and mechanisms of TCM in recent years, providing valuable insights for subsequent pharmacodynamics and mechanism studies. However, without experimental validation, network pharmacology analysis might yield false‐positive results. 39 Active ingredients of TCM typically need to be absorbed into the bloodstream to exert therapeutic effects. Therefore, identifying LPCs through serum pharmacochemistry, rather than directly utilizing active compounds from the TCM database, helps reduce prediction errors in network pharmacology, thereby improving the accuracy, reliability, and authenticity of the results.

Metabolomics enables the dynamic analysis of untargeted metabolites under external intervention or pathophysiological conditions, offering potential biomarkers and metabolic pathways. This approach facilitates the quantitative evaluation of drug efficacy and provides evidence for elucidating underlying mechanisms. 40 Our metabolomics analysis revealed that lipid metabolism is the primary regulatory pathway through which LTT affects CIRI. Lipid metabolites, including PCs, LysoPCs, PA, AA, and SM, play the significant roles in the endogenous regulation of nerve cell differentiation, proliferation, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. By combining metabolomics with serum pharmacochemistry and network pharmacology, eight core targets, including PTGS1, PTGS2, ALOX5, PLA2G2A, ACHE, ALDH2, AKR1B1, and PLA2G7, were identified, which regulate five downstream metabolites, such as phosphorylcholine, AA, PA, PCs, and LysoPCs. To further enhance the accuracy of our findings, molecular docking was employed, which confirmed the interactions between LPCs and core targets. Based on the number of relevant LPCs and their binding energies, PTGS2, ALOX5, and AChE were selected for further validation.

AA, a member of the omega‐6 fatty acid family, is a key component of phospholipids in cell membranes. Upon membrane stimulation, phospholipases regulate the release of free AA, which promotes inflammation. 41 The PTGS2 gene, which encodes cyclo‐oxygenase‐2 (COX‐2), is associated with cardiovascular diseases and ischemic stroke. COX‐2 functions as a prostaglandin H synthase and plays a central role in the cyclo‐oxygenase pathway, where it converts AA to prostaglandin H2 (PGH2). Unlike COX‐1, COX‐2 is inducible and is typically regulated by inflammatory stimuli. 42 ALOX5 catalyzes the peroxidation of AA, converting it into unstable hydroperoxy‐eicosatetraenoic acid (5‐HPETE) and the inflammatory mediator leukotriene A4 (LTA4). 43 Phosphatidylcholine is the main constituent of phospholipids in cell and organelle membranes, serving as a precursor to various signaling molecules and inflammatory mediators. It plays a crucial role in regulating lipid metabolism and maintaining lipid homeostasis. The expression of phosphatidylcholine is typically reduced in middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion models. 44 Lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC) is an intermediate product of glycerophospholipid metabolism, and its various forms have distinct functions. LysoPCs are bioactive pro‐inflammatory lipids generated during pathological processes. Some LysoPCs have neuroprotective properties, acting as inhibitors of lipoprotein‐associated phospholipase A2, thereby reducing neuroinflammation. 45 , 46 , 47 Abnormal LysoPC metabolism following stroke leads to LysoPC accumulation in the brain, which mediates phagocyte infiltration, potentially lowering LysoPC levels in plasma. 48 Choline, a crucial anabolic substrate for phospholipid synthesis, is phosphorylated by choline kinase to form phosphocholine, which serves as a precursor for phosphatidylcholine production. 49 Moreover, both phosphorylcholine and phosphatidylcholine could be hydrolyzed to free choline. 50 AChE, an enzyme responsible for the hydrolysis of acetylcholine, produces choline in synaptic clefts. AChE also plays a role in cell development and maturation, supporting neuronal development and regeneration. 51 The results of our investigation indicated that LPCs could regulate PTGS2, ALOX5, and AChE to modulate the levels of PCs, LysoPCs, and AA (Figure 10).

The schematic diagram of particularly significant key metabolites, core targets, and signal pathway–related proteins for intervention effect of Liujing Toutong tablet (LTT) on cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury (CIRI) rats.

Previous studies have shown that AA stimulates NF‐κB nuclear translocation in human promonocytic cells. 52 Pro‐inflammatory eicosanoids, such as PGE2 and LTA4, produced from AA, further activate NF‐κB signaling. 53 LysoPCs can also activate NF‐κB signaling by binding to TLR2 and TLR4 receptors. 54 In addition, KEGG analysis revealed that PTGS2, ALOX5, and AChE are significantly enriched in the NF‐κB signaling pathway. As shown in Figure 10, these findings suggest that LTT may reduce neuroinflammatory damage by modulating the disturbed lipid metabolites, PTGS2, ALOX5, and AChE, and subsequently regulating the NF‐κB signaling pathway.

CONCLUSION

The complex chemical composition of TCM presents a challenge in exploring its mechanisms of action. In this study, we propose a novel strategy for investigating the pharmacodynamic substance basis and mechanisms of TCM by combining serum pharmacochemistry, metabolomics, network pharmacology analysis, and molecular docking. The identified targets were further validated using ELISA kits and Western blot experiments. The results demonstrate that LTT targets the core proteins PTGS2, ALOX5, and AChE to modulate glycerophospholipid and AA metabolism. Additionally, LTT inhibits NF‐κB signaling pathway–related proteins and reduces inflammatory factors.
