Population-Level Changes in Folate, Iodine, and Vitamin D Status over More than 20 Years: Biomarker Data from the Cross-Sectional National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 1999–2000 through August 2021–August 2023)☆
Authors: Christine M Pfeiffer, Maya R Sternberg, Carissa D Powers, Cynthia L Ogden, Maria Elena D Jefferds, Krista S Crider, WenYen Juan, Jaime J Gahche, Michael E Rybak, Zia Fazili, Ming Zhang, Ekaterina M Mineva, Jeffery M Jarrett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) has monitored biomarkers for 4 key nutrients important for pregnancy outcomes, neurological development, and healthy growth—folate, vitamin D, iodine, and iron—during most survey cycles from 1999 to 2000 through August 2021–August 2023 as part of the continuous NHANES. These biomarker data offer a unique opportunity to assess long-term trends in the US population.ObjectivesThis study aimed to describe concentration trends for 7 biomarkers/indicators after adjusting for demographic changes over time.MethodsWe used the following NHANES data: serum folate (SFOL) and red blood cell folate (RBF) for persons aged ≥3 y and women of reproductive age (WRA, 12–49 y) for 1999–August 2023 (except 2017–2018), serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25OHD) for persons aged ≥3 y for 2001–August 2023, urine iodine (UI) for persons aged ≥3 y for 2001–2018, and serum ferritin [(FER) and FER adjusted for inflammation (FERadj)], serum soluble transferrin receptor, and body iron index (BII) for WRA and children aged 1–5 y for 2003–August 2023 (except 2011–2014). We assessed trends as overall concentration change using logistic regression (unadjusted and adjusted for age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin) and as a linear trend if the overall change was significant.ResultsAfter adjusting for demographic changes, mean concentrations of all 7 biomarkers/indicators showed significant overall temporal changes (except for FER in children and BII in WRA) and significant linear trends (except for FERadj in children). SFOL (∼25%–30%), RBF (∼10%), and UI (∼25%–30%) concentrations decreased, whereas 25OHD (∼20%) concentrations increased. FER concentrations decreased in WRA (∼15%) and remained unchanged in children.ConclusionsConsidering the long monitoring period, biomarker changes were moderate. Nonetheless, these changes highlight the need to address declining iodine, iron, and folate status in WRA and increasing vitamin D status in supplement users.Keywords: biomarker, trend, folate, vitamin D, iodine, iron indicator, vitamins

Introduction

The NHANES has provided nutritional biomarker data since the early 1970s and was initially limited to just a handful of biomarkers assessing the status of iron, a few trace elements, and vitamins (A, C, and folate) [1]. NHANES III, conducted from 1988 to 1994, featured a broader portfolio of 23 nutritional biomarkers, including urine iodine (UI) and 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25OHD) [2]. The first 6 y of the continuous NHANES, which started in 1999, provided data for ≤58 biomarkers for which summary statistics by demographic groups were featured in Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s first (NHANES 1999–2002) and second (NHANES 2003–2006) "National Report on Biochemical Indicators of Diet and Nutrition in the US Population" (CDC Nutrition Report) [3]. In 2026, CDC will release an updated CDC Nutrition Report covering all publicly available data for 131 nutritional biomarkers from the inception of the continuous NHANES in 1999 up to the most recent survey cycle conducted from August 2021 to August 2023 [4]. The report presents descriptive data from univariate analysis without adjusting for covariates. Despite the impressive number of nutritional biomarkers represented in the continuous NHANES, only a small number have been featured throughout most of the cycles during this 24-y period. They are biomarkers used to assess folate, vitamin D, iodine, and iron status.

Given the limitations of resources, past and present, both in terms of available specimen volume to analyze biochemical indicators and available funding to carry out NHANES, the portfolio of nutritional biomarkers is reassessed from one cycle to the next to meet public health needs to the extent possible. The introduction of folic acid fortification of enriched cereal-grain products 3 decades ago to reduce the risk of folate-responsive neural-tube defects necessitated continued monitoring of folate biomarkers in the US population [5,6]. Earlier reports of inadequate vitamin D status in subgroups of the US population, coupled with accumulating evidence from observational studies indicating that vitamin D status was inversely associated with nonskeletal health outcomes, elevated the importance of ongoing monitoring of 25OHD concentrations [[7], [8], [9]]. Awareness about the importance of iron and iodine status for pregnancy outcomes and cognitive development, coupled with notable rates of iron deficiency and borderline iodine status, particularly in women of reproductive age (WRA), justified the inclusion of iron status indicators and UI throughout much of the continuous NHANES [[10], [11], [12]]. The overarching goal of this paper is to present findings on long-term trends over >2 decades for concentrations of key biomarkers for folate, vitamin D, iodine, and iron status after adjusting for demographic changes over this extended period.

Methods

Participants and survey design

Through NHANES, the CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) collects cross-sectional data on the health and nutritional status of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population. In 1999, NHANES became a continuous survey with public data release in 2-y cycles [13]. The survey uses a stratified, multistage, probability sample to represent the US population. It includes health interviews and examinations, including use of detailed information on prescription medications and dietary supplements, as well as the collection of biological specimens such as blood and urine in a mobile examination center (MEC). To increase the precision of estimates for certain population subgroups, the survey design includes oversampling for some groups (for details, see footnotes in Supplementary Table 1). Interview and examination response rates for each survey period are publicly available [14]. Participants aged ≥18 y consented, parents provided consent for those aged <18 y, and youth aged 7–17 y assented. The NCHS Ethics Review Board approved NHANES [15].

Biomarker laboratory methods and data adjustment

The CDC laboratory at the Division of Laboratory Sciences, National Center for Environmental Health, analyzed biological specimens for serum folate (SFOL), red blood cell folate (RBF), 25OHD, UI, ferritin (FER), and serum soluble transferrin receptor (TFR) using validated laboratory methods described in detail on the NHANES website for each survey period [16] and summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Assay performance was monitored using Westgard-type quality control multirules [17]. Because of changes in laboratory methods over time, we followed the NHANES analytical guidelines and adjusted data to allow appropriate assessment of long-term trends in concentrations for this analysis (see footnotes in Supplementary Table 1).

Study variables

Data for the above-mentioned 7 biomarkers/indicators were collected in NHANES during most of the 24-y period covered in this analysis. Biomarkers for folate, vitamin D, and iodine status were measured in the US population, starting with children at different ages, depending on availability of data (Supplementary Table 1). For SFOL, RBF, and 25OHD, data were available for ∼7000 participants per cycle, whereas UI was typically measured on a 1/3 subsample of ∼2500 participants per cycle (Table 1). Although SFOL and RBF were measured during the 2017–2018 cycle, selected subpopulations were surveyed (full sample for females aged 12–49 y and 1/2 subsample for males aged ≥1 y, females aged 1–11 y, and females aged ≥50 y); we only used the SFOL and RBF data for WRA in this analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Although FER was measured in persons aged ≥1 y in 1999–2002, no data were collected for TFR during that time; to ensure that we use data from the same cycles for all 3 iron indicators, our analysis does not include the FER data for that period (Supplementary Table 1). Starting in 2003, serum FER and TFR were measured in children aged 1–5 y and in WRA, allowing the calculation of body iron index (BII), a de facto ratio of FER to TFR (Supplementary Table 1). We calculated BII as follows: BII (mg/kg) = –[log10 (TFR × 1000/FER) − 2.8229]/0.1207 [18], with the TFR concentration representing an adjusted concentration to make the Roche Tina-quant TFR concentrations equivalent to the original assay [19] used in the development of the BII model: original TFR = 1.5 × Roche TFR + 0.35 mg/L [20]. Data for ∼700 children (∼250 in August 2021–August 2023) and ∼2000 WRA were available per survey cycle (no iron indicator data were collected for 2011–2014) (Table 1).

Because data were not available for the same age groups across survey cycles, we assessed trends in concentrations by defining consistent age-specific analytic populations: persons aged ≥3 y for SFOL and RBF; persons aged ≥6 y for 25OHD and UI; and children aged 1–5 y and WRA (12–49 y) for FER, TFR, and BII. Our covariates were age, sex (male and female), race and Hispanic origin categories [Mexican American (MA), non-Hispanic Black (NHB), and non-Hispanic White (NHW) for NHANES 1999–August 2023 and additionally non-Hispanic Asian (NHA) for NHANES 2011–2023], and supplement use (dichotomized as yes/no based on self-reported consumption of any dietary supplements in the past 30 d). Before 2007, NHANES only collected a sufficient sample for MA persons but not for all Hispanic persons [21]. To retain comparable trends, estimates are shown for MA rather than all Hispanic persons. Other race and Hispanic origin groups were included in overall estimates but not presented separately per NCHS analytic guidelines [21].

Statistical analysis

We followed a data analysis plan approved by all authors before conducting the statistical analysis using SAS for Windows, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of NHANES by applying Taylor series linearization, which calculates appropriate variance estimates while accounting for the stratified, multistage cluster sampling design and incorporates survey weights. Data from all available 2-y survey cycles from 1999–2000 to August 2021–August 2023 were included, with the exception of the 2019–2020 cycle when field operations were suspended in March 2020 because of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the resulting data were not considered nationally representative [22]. The 2017–March 2020 dataset was not used to maintain 2-y cycle comparisons over time. NHANES resumed operations in August 2021, and the survey cycle concluded in August 2023. Although data for SFOL, RBF, 25OHD, and iron indicators were available from this most recent cycle for this analysis, data for UI were not yet publicly available. We did not exclude pregnant and/or lactating females from the analytical sample. In a sensitivity analysis, we determined that excluding females aged 20–44 y for whom pregnancy and lactation information was publicly available across all cycles made a minimal difference to the central tendency of biomarker concentrations (median percentage change in the geometric mean was 1%–2%).

Two-year MEC weights provided by NHANES were applied to account for unequal probabilities of selection and nonresponse. Exceptions included the use of subsample weights for SFOL and RBF in 2017–2018, and phlebotomy weights for the August 2021–August 2023 cycle because of notable nonresponse differences in the phlebotomy component of the MEC [23]. Biomarker concentrations were reported as weighted geometric means with 95% confidence intervals, unless otherwise specified. Exceptions include 25OHD and BII, where arithmetic means were reported because of the symmetric nature of their distributions. For UI, medians were reported for consistency with population reporting of iodine status [11]. In addition to checking that degrees of freedom were ≥8, the relative SE <30% was used to assess the reliability of the means.

Differences in geometric (or arithmetic) means across survey cycles were assessed using both unadjusted and adjusted Wald F-tests from linear regression models (with survey cycle modeled categorically). Adjusted models controlled for age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin to account for changes in population demographics over time. Vitamin D models were additionally adjusted for season to account for seasonal variation in serum 25OHD concentrations and the seasonal design of the NHANES data collection. For biomarkers summarized using geometric means (or medians), Wald F-tests were derived from linear regression models following log-transformed data. Adjusted linear Wald F-tests were also conducted by modeling survey cycle as an ordered continuous variable (with 2-y cycles coded sequentially through 2017–2018 and spacing adjusted to reflect the gap before the August 2021–August 2023 cycle, i.e., coded 1–10 and 12) in the linear regression models described above; results from these tests were not reported if the adjusted overall Wald F-test was not significant (P ≤ 0.05) or if <4 cycles were reported. Because FER is an acute-phase protein, we used R to calculate a specialized adjustment for FER concentrations using the Biomarkers Reflecting Inflammation and Nutritional Determinants of Anemia (BRINDA) approach, which considered each participant’s C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration to adjust for inflammation [[24], [25], [26]].

Results

Long-term trends in key nutritional biomarker concentrations

We observed significant overall changes in concentrations over time for SFOL, RBF, 25OHD, and UI before and after adjusting for demographic changes, both for the US population and for WRA, as well as significant linear trends (Table 2, trend column). The concentrations shown in Table 2 represent unadjusted concentrations. Mean SFOL concentrations declined ∼25% between 1999–2000 and August 2021–August 2023, whereas those for RBF decreased ∼10% during the same time. Mean UI concentrations declined ∼25%–30% between 2001–2002 and 2017–2018. Conversely, mean 25OHD concentrations increased ∼20% between 2001–2002 and August 2021–August 2023.

We observed a mixed picture for long-term trends of the 3 iron indicators (Table 3). WRA showed a decrease in iron status based on mean FER and ferritin adjusted for inflammation using the BRINDA equation (FERadj) concentrations declining (∼15%) significantly between 2003–2004 and August 2021–August 2023. However, mean TFR concentrations decreased (∼6%) significantly during the same time (instead of increasing), and BII remained unchanged. On the other hand, children aged 1–5 y showed an improvement in iron status based on a significant decrease in mean TFR concentrations (∼15%) and a significant increase in BII (18%). However, mean FER and FERadj concentrations remained unchanged. After adjusting for inflammation, mean FERadj concentrations were ∼5% and 3% lower in WRA and children, respectively, compared with unadjusted FER concentrations.

Long-term trends by population subgroups

For each of the 7 biomarkers/indicators, we generated graphs to illustrate changes in unadjusted concentrations over time by demographic subgroup and supplement use and prepared corresponding data tables. We also generated heat maps for the P values from the 3 trend tests to allow for a visual representation of the significance level by biomarker and population subgroup (Supplementary Figures 1–3). Both folate biomarkers, SFOL (Figure 1 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3) and RBF (Figure 2 and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5), showed significantly declining mean concentrations over time for both supplement users and nonusers among ages ≥3 y and in WRA. The significant linear trend was applied similarly to supplement users and nonusers and to different age groups, as well as race and Hispanic origin groups. Mean 25OHD concentrations among ages ≥6 y (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 6) increased significantly over time in all subgroups, except for children aged 6–11 y and adolescents aged 12–19 y. Mean UI concentrations among ages ≥6 y (Figure 4 and Supplementary Tables 7 and 8) decreased significantly over time in all subgroups, except for persons aged ≥60 y and NHA persons. UI concentrations were similar for supplement users and nonusers and are, therefore, only shown in the data tables for this subgroup.

Discussion

Biomarker concentrations for 4 nutrients—folate, vitamin D, iodine, and iron—were available for most NHANES cycles covering a 24-y period from 1999–2000 to August 2021–August 2023 and thus allowed us to assess long-term trends in concentrations after adjusting for demographic changes. Folate status, related to the risk of neural-tube defects, and iodine status, important for thyroid function and central nervous system development, decreased over time. Vitamin D status, critical for bone health, increased. Iron status, important for pregnancy outcomes and neurological development, decreased in WRA based on FER and improved in children based on TFR and BII.

To allow for an accurate interpretation of trends in concentrations of key nutritional biomarkers over an extended period of time, our analysis adjusted for demographic changes over time. Both folate biomarkers, SFOL and RBF, indicative of short- and long-term status, respectively, showed decreasing concentration trends from 1999–2000 to August 2021–August 2023, with a modest 10% decline in RBF and a larger ∼25% decline in SFOL. Following increases in blood folate concentrations from 1988–1994 to 1999–2000 (more than doubling of SFOL and ∼50% increase in RBF) in response to the mandatory folic acid fortification of cereal-grain products in 1996, a slight decrease in folate status was reported during the first 3 cycles of the continuous NHANES (1999–2004) [27], likely attributable to the preference for low-carbohydrate diets in the early 2000s [28]. Between 2003–2004 and 2015–2016, folate status showed only minor fluctuations [29]. The recent data for persons aged ≥3 y for August 2021–August 2023 and for WRA for 2017–2018 and August 2021–August 2023 showed a second decreasing trend starting in 2013–2014, both for supplement users and nonusers as well as for all race and Hispanic origin groups. Despite this decline in folate status, blood folate concentrations (nmol/L) for persons aged ≥3 y were still higher in August 2021–August 2023 (geometric mean SFOL of 33.3 and RBF of 1060) compared with the prefortification period (median SFOL of 18.2 and RBF of 721). On the basis of NHANES data from "What We Eat in America" for persons aged ≥2 y, the mean daily intake of folic acid from supplements fluctuated ∼100 μg between 2013 and March 2020, but the mean daily intake of folic acid from foods/beverages and of food folate decreased between 2013–2014 [186 μg and 524 dietary folate equivalents (DFE), respectively] and August 2021–August 2023 (136 μg and 432 DFE, respectively), which may explain the observed decreases in blood folate concentrations [30]. Given that low folate status is associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes from birth defects to cancers [31], continued monitoring of folate status through biomarkers is important as dietary patterns continue to change.

UI is used to assess recent dietary iodine intake and showed a decreasing trend from 2001–2002 to 2017–2018, with median UI concentrations declining by ∼25%–30% both in persons aged ≥6 y and in WRA. The decline we observed for children and adolescents may be partially explained by the decline in the contribution of dairy products [32]. This is the largest iodine intake food category and has been shown to be associated with total iodine intake in US girls and females in NHANES between 2011–2012 (49.3%) and 2017–2020 (43.6%) [32]. On the basis of WHO epidemiological criteria [11], all age groups in the overall population aged ≥6 y achieved adequate iodine nutrition (median UI between 100 and 199 ng/mL) across NHANES cycles, whereas children aged 6–11 y had iodine intakes above requirements (median UI between 200 and 299 ng/mL) during the earlier cycles up to 2009–2010. WRA achieved adequate iodine nutrition in the earlier NHANES cycles, but their status declined to borderline mild iodine deficiency (median UI between 50 and 99 ng/mL) in some of the later cycles (particularly females aged 40–49 y in 2017–2018). Pregnant females and WRA are key groups for ensuring adequate iodine nutrition because of the critical role of iodine in fetal growth and development. However, because of the small number of pregnant females surveyed in NHANES (e.g., 223 from 2007 to 2014), this subpopulation was not part of our trend analysis. Others have raised concerns that the iodine status of US pregnant females remains slightly below the WHO cutoff for adequacy [33].

25OHD integrates both sources of vitamin D, namely, sunlight exposure and diet. Vitamin D status increased over time, with ∼20% higher 25OHD concentrations in August 2021–August 2023 compared with 2001–2002. We found pronounced increases in mean 25OHD concentrations in supplement users, as well as in persons aged ≥60 y, females, and NHW persons—groups known to have higher intakes of dietary supplements [34,35]. This is in line with a recent analysis of NHANES data from 2007–2008 to August 2021–August 2023, which found a sharp increase in 25OHD concentrations >125 nmol/L (“may be of concern”) and a sharp decrease in 25OHD concentrations 50–125 nmol/L (“sufficient”) in persons using multiple vitamin D-containing supplements [36]. Previously reported large differences in 25OHD concentrations between NHB and NHW persons [37] were still apparent during August 2021–August 2023 (60.3 compared with 84.8 nmol/L for NHB compared with NHW persons). A case has been made that 25OHD concentrations may be lower in a state of inflammation [38]. This may contribute in part to the incongruencies between better bone health despite lower vitamin D status in Black compared with White persons, as Black persons have higher rates of low-grade inflammation [39].

The interpretation of long-term trends for iron status is complicated because of the gap in biomarker data during 2011–2014, as well as the data adjustments made to account for small differences in assays used over time. On the other hand, the availability of BII data adds value as it allows the full range of iron status to be assessed, from positive balance (BII ≥0 mg/kg) when there is residual storage iron to negative balance (BII <0 mg/kg) representing functional iron deficiency. We observed a decrease in iron status over time in WRA and an increase in children, both in the order of 10%–15%, based on different iron status indicators. Because of the moderately small changes over time and gaps in biomarker data, it is more difficult to recognize patterns across demographic subgroups or across the 3 biomarkers/indicators, and caution should be used against overinterpreting these changes. It has been noted previously that more complete data on iron status indicators, as well as an updated foundational guidance on iron deficiency and anemia, are needed to improve assessment and diagnosis and to strengthen surveillance [40]. We found that adjusting for inflammation using the BRINDA approach changed FER concentrations only minimally (≤5%), likely because of lower rates of inflammation found in US children and WRA compared with other higher infection-burden populations [41].

Our current trend analysis has multiple strengths. The data we used are nationally representative and thus provide unique insights into the nutritional status of the US population over time. The key biomarkers we selected have been measured in all or most NHANES cycles over the last 24 y and thus allowed the analysis of trends over an extended period previously not available. High-quality laboratory methods were used to generate the biomarker data, and we performed data adjustments as recommended by the NHANES documentation to account for method changes, enabling data comparisons over time. However, these data adjustments most likely do not account for all potential differences. We adjusted for demographic changes that occurred over this extended period to ensure accurate interpretation of trends. We approached our statistical analysis without a priori assumptions regarding the types of differences we might encounter, conducting an omnibus test for overall change first and then evaluating whether the trend was linear. This approach reduced our false-positive rate but may also be more conservative as the omnibus test is less sensitive to specific patterns, such as a linear trend.

The main limitation of this analysis is that we only addressed 4 key nutrients, as other nutritional biomarkers were measured in fewer survey cycles and did not allow an assessment of longer-term trends. Data on NHA females were limited because oversampling of this group only started in 2011; it was apparent, though, NHA females had low UI status. Although we adjusted for 2 analyte-specific factors that may confound the interpretation of findings (inflammation for FER and season for 25OHD), we did not adjust for other general factors that may have changed over time, as conducting further exploratory analyses on the reasons for biomarker changes was beyond the scope of this paper. For example, obesity prevalence (BMI >30 kg/m2) increased in US adults from 30% in 1999–2000 to ∼40% during the last 3 cycles covered in this analysis (2015–August 2023) [42,43]. Conversely, inflammation prevalence (CRP >5 mg/dL) remained stable at around 20%, though CRP data were missing for some cycles (1999–2002 and 2011–2014) (data not shown). Lastly, although NHANES response rates have declined over time, nonresponse bias assessments performed by NCHS in 2017–2018 and August 2021–August 2023 found no major sources of bias that were not accounted for by weighting adjustments [23,44].

The NHANES remains the reference standard of nationally representative population surveys used to inform public health, research, and the clinical sector, and serves as a blueprint for countries seeking to develop comparable surveys to generate reliable, high-quality data to support public health policymaking. In this analysis on long-term trends focused on key biomarkers available in most NHANES survey cycles, we noted decreases in folate and iodine status and an increase in vitamin D status. The interpretation of changes in iron status depended on the biomarker used. Although most biomarker changes were moderate, they highlight the need for continued monitoring to address these changes.
