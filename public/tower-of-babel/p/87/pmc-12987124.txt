Mulberry Drought Diagnosis: Integrating Proximal Sensing and Metabolomics for Remote Monitoring
Authors: Liang Yang, Cheng Li, Huaqi Gao, Zhiqi Hong, Yong He, Lingxia Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought is the most severe natural hazard threatening agricultural production. Mulberry (Morus alba L.) is an important crop for the sericulture industry, and its drought tolerance has been extensively studied. In this study, the phenotypic and physiological responses of two different mulberry tree genotypes (711 and NS8) to drought stress were investigated, with the aim of screening potential nondestructive traits and understand interrelationships. The significant reductions of digital biomass (DB), leaf area (LA), and projected leaf area (PLA) in morphological traits indicated that drought led to a decrease in mulberry yield. The change of color traits RFarRed and RNIR were associated with pigments and leaf morphology. Vegetation indexes were also significantly affected by drought stress. Due to their had high correlation coefficients and good linear relationships with yield, DB and LA can be used as yield proxy traits for this measure. Drought-sensitive traits were identified using PCA and correlation analysis, and the results showed that greenness (GR) was a proxy predictor of drought stress. For antioxidant defenses, CAT activity and phenolic compound content were significantly decreased. Metabolomics analysis revealed that genotype 711 exhibited 1691 differential metabolites under drought stress; these mainly comprised amino acids, lipids, and phenolic acids, which were mainly enriched in secondary metabolism and flavonoid biosynthesis. Drought also reprogrammed carbohydrate, secondary compounds, and amino acid metabolism. The results revealed that the phenotypic response of two mulberry trees to drought, as well as the integration of phenotypic traits with metabolic traits, could help us to understand drought tolerance mechanisms and benefit efficient selection and breeding of fitter genotypes.

1. Introduction

Drought is a severe abiotic stressor that decreases agricultural production and threatens food security, the social economy, human lives, and political stability [1,2]. It results from abnormal continuously dry weather influenced by changes in global weather patterns such as the effects of El Niño and La Niña, as well as human activities [3]. Droughts are among the most complex and devastating natural hazards globally [4]. China experiences frequent severe droughts due to its vast territory and diverse climate [5]. Annually, drought impacts an average of 200,000 km2 of farmland in China; in some years, the affected area extends to >400,000 km2. As a result, annual yield from agricultural yield has decreased in some years by ≥30 Mt, causing direct annual economic losses of 44 billion yuan.

Mulberry (Morus alba L.) is the only food source for domestic silkworms; this fruit is also a source of livestock feed, paper, herbs, and more [6]. Mulberry is not only the primary fodder for silkworms but also valued for its fruit, which possesses high nutritional and antioxidant properties [7]. It demonstrates moderate tolerance to environmental stressors, including heavy metals [8], drought [9], waterlogging [10], and salinity [11], enabling its use in phytoremediation, water retention, windbreaks, sand fixation, and saline soil reclamation [12]. As sericulture expands into water-limited areas [13], drought impairs mulberry quality and yield. It significantly reduces morphological traits, such as plant height, base diameter, and root surface area [14], while physiological indicators such as malondialdehyde, proline content, and antioxidant enzyme activity increase significantly [13]. Drought stress also has a significant effect on photosynthesis in mulberry trees, leading to decreases in CO2 fixation rate, transpiration rate, stomatal conductance, and intercellular CO2 concentration [15]. Several genes that influence drought tolerance have been identified in mulberry, including MnEIL3 [16], MaRACK1 [17], MaGβ, MaGγ1, and MaGγ2 [18]. While these physiological and molecular responses provide deep insights into drought mechanisms, there is an increasing need for non-destructive technologies that can diagnose these changes in real-time at both the individual level and in fields.

Plant phenomics investigates the development of measurable phenotypic traits during organism growth [19]. High-throughput phenotyping, enhanced by advanced sensors and information technology, addresses bottlenecks in phenotypic data acquisition for breeding and genomics, serving as an effective tool for detecting plant drought stress responses [20,21,22,23]. In the broader context of agricultural monitoring, these technologies are increasingly used for proximal sensing, which operates in close proximity to the target [24]. Unlike large-scale remote sensing (RS) which utilizes satellites or unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to monitor plant health across vast landscapes, proximal sensing offers significantly higher spatial and spectral resolution [25]. These two approaches are complementary: high-resolution data from proximal sensors provide the essential “ground truth” and foundational spectral signatures required to calibrate and validate remote sensing models and assess plant health [26,27]. Key sensor technologies include RGB imaging for morphology and biomass [28]; near-infrared for water content; and hyperspectral imaging for physiological status via vegetation indices [29]. These indices, such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI), are widely used in remote sensing to assess canopy greenness and photosynthetic efficiency, respectively [30]. Integrating these proximal measurements with metabolic data allows for a more robust diagnosis of stress than imagery alone, such as fluorescence for photosynthesis and thermal imaging for plant temperature [31]. Integrated phenotyping platforms with control systems and multi-sensors show excellent performance. PHENOPSIS pioneered environmental control with RGB imaging [32], Phenoscope enables high-throughput photosynthesis and growth monitoring [33]; and scanalyzer3D combines multiple imaging sensors to evaluate salt tolerance and nutrient/water deficits [34]. By utilizing these proximal sensing platforms to establish precise correlations between imagery-derived traits and internal metabolic reprogramming, we can identify early-stage “proxy” indicators of drought. Such insights are vital for scaling localized diagnostic results to broader remote sensing applications, thereby improving the accuracy of satellite-based plant health assessments in complex agricultural environments [24,35]. These platforms have analyzed phenotypic changes under heat/drought stress in oilseed [36], screened stress-associated traits, assessed biostimulant effects on drought mitigation, and studied drought impacts across diverse species including wheat [37], barley [38], cotton [39], tomato [40], and Arabidopsis [41]. Plant phenomics thus offers a high-throughput, nondestructive method for extensive plant drought tolerance testing. Phenomics combined with metabolomics can analyze plant drought response mechanisms at the metabolite and molecular levels [42,43,44]. Metabolites are small molecules produced as end products of metabolic processes and physiological pathways [45], and they define key physiological and agronomic features in plants [46]. Studies have characterized drought tolerance-related diversity in the chickpea metabolome [47], which identified the first population-level metabolomic variations underlying crop drought adaptation [45]. Recent studies in model species have further elucidated the sophisticated role of metabolites in drought adaptation. For instance, in tomato, phenylamines have been identified as key regulators that manage the trade-off between plant growth and drought resistance [48], demonstrating that metabolic shifts are central to surviving environmental stress while maintaining physiological balance. However, there is currently a lack of multi-omics studies that integrate high-throughput phenomics and metabolomics to investigate mulberries under drought stress.

2. Results

2.1. Yield and Physiological Biomarkers of Drought Stress

The soil moisture content is shown in Figure 1. Soil water content in WW was maintained at all times >25%, and soil water content in WD gradually decreased to <10%. The yields of mulberry trees with two genotypes were significantly affected by drought stress, as evidenced by clear decreases in AFBM and LFBM (Table 1). The decrease in yield was greater for 711 than NS8: AFBM decreased by 41.4%, and LFBM decreased by 65.5% for treatment WD compared to treatment WW. NS8 also underwent a 14.8% decrease in AFBM and a 57.0% decrease in LFBM.

A lack of water significantly affected both biomarkers, resulting in a significant increase in biomarker concentrations (Table 2). For treatment WD, TBARS concentration in genotype 711 increased by 142.66 nmol/g DW compared to WWM; however, in NS8, the concentration increased by only 36.01 nmol/g DW. Pro concentrations changed similarly, with a greater increase (3.95 mg/g) in 711 and a smaller increase (0.87 mg/g) in NS8.

2.2. High-Throughput Digital and Multispectral Phenotyping Analysis

Lack of water greatly affected the phenotypic traits of mulberry trees, and we observed significant changes in morphological traits (DB, PH, LA, PLA, LANG, and LINC), color traits (RRed, RGreen, RBlue, Hue, SAT, and VAL), and multispectral traits (RFarRed, RNIR, GR, NDVI, NPCI, PSRI, ChlIdx, and AriIdx).

2.3. Changes in Morphological Traits

Morphological traits are shown in Figure 2. DB was greater in WW than in WD, and the values of DB were significantly different at the end of the experiment (DAT30, 35). Plants with genotype 711 had a greater growth rate in WW than in WD, with a significant difference from DAT10 onward. Tree growth in WW had a clear trend of slow followed by rapid growth; DB increased by 387% for 711 plants and 395% for NS8 plants in WW. In contrast, plant growth was significantly reduced in WD; DB increased by 47% for 711 plants and 288% for NS8 plants. DB showed a significant main effect of G and T, but there were few significant G × T interactions at most timepoints (Table S1).

In plants with genotype 711, PH initially increased in both WW and WD. However, starting at DAT25, plants began to show a significant stress effect as PH decreased in WD, and was 31.6 mm less than WW at DAT35. In NS8 plants, lack of water had less effect on PH, and the levels remained similar in WW and WD with no significant differences. At DAT35, PH for NS8 in WW was only 6.5 mm greater than in WD. Similarly to DB, the PH also had a significant main effect of G (Table S1).

In plants treated with WW, LA gradually increased before rising more rapidly after DAT20. In contrast, for the WD treatment, LA was inhibited from DAT10 in 711 plants and from DAT30 in NS8 plants. Values of LA at DAT35 were 65.2% (711) and 48.3% (NS8) less in WD than in WW. LA had a significant main effect of T and few significant main effects of G and G × T interactions (Table S1). The increase in PLA trended similarly to the increase in LA, and PLA was significantly greater in WW than in WD at the later stages of the experiment for both genotypes. PLA exhibited a significant main effect of G and T (Table S1).

In plants of genotype 711, LANG in both WW and WD had an overall increasing trend, and there were no significant differences between treatments except for a significant difference at DAT10. For NS8 plants, LANG changed slightly throughout the experiment during treatment in WW; LANG initially decreased and then increased in WD and was significantly different from WW only at DAT5 and DAT10. LINC initially increased in mulberry trees of both genotypes in both WW and WD and then gradually decreased after peaking at DAT10; significant differences were found between treatments at most timepoints. Both LANG and LINC showed few significant main effects and interactions (Table S1).

2.4. Changes in Color Traits

The color traits of mulberry trees at DAT35 were found to be significantly affected by drought stress (Figure 3). In 711 plants, RRed and RGreen significantly decreased in WD, whereas RBlue significantly increased. NS8 plants behaved differently. There were significant increases in RRed and RBlue but no significant change in RGreen in WD. RRed had significant main effects of G, T and G × T interactions, and RGreen and RBlue had a significant main effect of T and interaction of G × T interactions (Table S1).

Hue, SAT, and VAL were calculated from the reflectance of red, green, and blue. All three indicator values were lower in WD treatment than in WW; this difference was significant in 711 plants but not in NS8 plants. Hue, SAT, and VAL all had a significant main effect of G, but there were a few significant main effects for T and G × T interactions (Table S1).

2.5. Changes in Multispectral Traits

Drought stress led to decreases in RFarRed and RNIR (Table S2). For 711 plants, RFarRed was significantly lower for WD than for WW from DAT10 onward but was not significantly less for NS8. There was little change in RNIR in either WW or WD throughout the growth period. WD, compared to WW, decreased RNIR; this difference was significant for 711 plants starting from DAT20 but not significant for NS8. Both RFarRed and RNIR had a significant main effect of T, and a few significant main effects for G and interactions of G × T (Table S2).

Four vegetation indices (GR, NDVI, NPCI, and PSRI) were calculated based on reflectance at different wavelengths (Figure 4). GR initially decreased, before increasing and decreasing again. The maximum values of GR in WW were at DAT0 and DAT15, and in WD were identified at DAT0, followed by a gradual decrease. From DAT15, the GR of both phenotype plants was significantly lower in WD than in WW. GR exhibited significant main effects of G, T, and interactions of G × T in the later stage of the experiment (Table S1). NDVI varied only slightly during this period (Figure 4). NDVI for 711 plants was consistently less in WD than in WW, but this difference was significant only at DAT20 and DAT35. There was a significant difference in NDVI between treatments for NS8 plants at DAT 35 only. NDVI did not have significant main effects or interactions for G or T (Table S1). NPCI decreased from 0.2 to approximately 0 (Figure 4). NPCI decreased rapidly in 711 plants in both WW and WD and was significantly less in WD than in WW at DAT35. NPCI for NS8 initially increased, reached a maximum at DAT15, and then decreased. NPCI was significantly lower in WD than in WW except at DAT0. NPCI displayed significant main effects for G and T and G × T interactions (Table S1). In general, PSRI gradually decreased during the experiment; PSRI values for the two genotypes in treatment WD were greater than those for WW, with significant differences at DAT35. PSRI displayed a significant main effect for G (Table S1).

ChlIdx increased steadily for the 711 plants in both WW and WD (Figure 3), with no significant difference until DAT25; from DAT30 onward, ChlIdx was significantly lower in WD than in WW due to drought stress. ChlIdx for the NS8 plants increased slowly to a plateau in the early period but increased steadily after DAT25 with no significant difference between WW and WD. AriIdx increased gradually for all plants (Figure 4). WD had no great effect on 711 plants in the early stage of the experiment; AriIdx was significantly lower in WD than in WW, starting at DAT25, and decreased by 0.356 compared to WW at DAT35. For NS8 plants, AriIdx was significantly lower in WD than in WW, starting at DAT5, and was 0.233 less at DAT35. Both ChlIdx and AriIdx showed significant main effects for G and T at the later stage of the experiment (Table S3).

2.6. Relationships Between Traits

2.6.1. Correlation Analysis for Phenotypic Traits

Correlation analysis was performed for 20 phenotypic traits measured using a high throughput phenotyping system, two yield indicators (AFBM and LFBM), and two drought physiological biomarkers (TBARS and Pro) obtained after harvest. The results are presented as heatmaps (Figure 5). In WW, most phenotypic traits were well correlated (Figure 5A, Table S4). Significant positive correlations were found between morphological traits DB, PH, LA, and PLA and multispectral and color traits GR, NPCI, RRed, RGreen, RBlue, ChlIdx, and AriIdx, and strong negative correlations were found with PSRI. Strong negative correlations were also found between LINC, PSRI, and other traits. Correlations between Hue, SAT, VAL, and most other phenotypic traits were weak; only VAL was significantly correlated with NDVI and RFarRed.

WD treatment resulted in significant changes to the correlations between phenotypic traits (Figure 5B, Table S4). The correlations between morphological traits such as DB, LA, and PLA and other traits significantly decreased, and the correlations between PH and GR, NPCI, RRed, RGreen, and RBlue became significantly negative. Similar results were observed for ChlIdx and AriIdx, where drought stress resulted in a decrease in the correlation between these two indicators and morphological traits; ChlIdx and AriIdx were strongly negatively correlated with NPCI, RFarRed, RNIR, RRed, RGreen, RBlue, Hue, SAT, and VAL. In contrast, in WD, the color and multispectral traits maintained positive correlations, but drought stress increased positive correlations.

2.6.2. Yield Proxy Traits

We noted that in WW, most phenotypic traits had high positive correlations with AFBM and LFBM, but this correlation decreased in WD for most phenotypic traits, and only DB and LA had high correlation coefficients (Figure 5B, Table S4). Regression analysis of AFBM, LFBM, DB, and LA showed significant linear relationships for LFBM with both DB and LA, with R2 for DB = 0.8724 and R2 for LA = 0.9602 (Figure 6B,D). There was also a significant linear relationship between AFBM and DB (R2 = 0.9121) and a slightly weaker linear relationship between AFBM and LA (R2 = 0.7925) (Figure 6A,C).

2.6.3. Proxy Traits for Drought Stress Response

In WW, there was no significant correlation between TBARS and any phenotypic traits. The correlation between Pro and phenotypic traits was also weak, with significant negative correlations found for DB, PH, RGreen, and AriIdx only (Figure 5A, Table S4). In WD, TBARS, and Pro were significantly correlated with most phenotypic traits. Both biomarkers were significantly positively correlated with PH, ChlIdx, and AriIdx and significantly negatively correlated with GR, NPCI, RNIR, RRed, RGreen, and RBlue (Figure 6B, Table S4). These nine phenotypic traits can, therefore, be used as drought stress response proxy traits (Table S5).

PCA results are shown in Figure 7. For morphological traits, the first two principal components with eigenvalues > 1 (PC1 and PC2) explained 66.1% and 20.9% of the variance, and the effects of treatments WD and WW were well distinguished by PC1 (Figure 7A). Traits sensitive to drought stress can be selected according to the loadings of phenotypic traits on the principal components. DB, LA, and PLA were the greatest sources of variation in PC1, and there was a significant negative correlation between LINC and PC1 (Figure 7B).

For color traits, the eigenvalues of PC1 and PC2 were 3.52 and 1.49, respectively, and the first two principal components together explained 83.5% of the variation according to which the different treatments and different genotypes of mulberry trees could be distinguished (Figure 7C). The largest contributors to variations in PC1 were RGreen, SAT, VAL, and Hue (Figure 7D). For multispectral traits, the first two principal components were >1 and together they explained 78.1% of the variance. They were well differentiated for drought stress in mulberry (Figure 7E). AriIdx and GR caused the greatest source of variation in PC1, and PSRI had the greatest negative correlation with the PC1 (Figure 7F).

2.7. Antioxidant Characteristics

POD, CAT activities, antioxidant capacity (ABTS), and antioxidant metabolites (TPC and TFC) showed significant main effects of G; CAT, SOD activities, antioxidant capacities (DPPH and ABTS), TPC, and TFC showed significant main effects of T and POD activities; and DPPH and TPC revealed significant G × T interactions.

In antioxidant enzyme activity, POD and SOD activity were not significantly affected by drought stress for either mulberry genotype, and only minor differences were observed between WW and WD (Figure 8A,C). In contrast, CAT activity showed significant differences. In both genotypes, WD treatment resulted in a significant decrease in CAT activity (Figure 8B). WD produced a significant decrease in TPC for 711 trees but had an insignificant effect on TPC for NS8. However, the TFC of both genotypes was significantly reduced in WD (Figure 8D,E). Antioxidant capacities (DPPH and ABTS) were significantly greater in WD than in WW because of drought stress. DPPH increased by 1.36 mg Trolox/g DW in 711 and 2.19 mg Trolox/g DW in NS8 in WD. ABTS showed a greater increase than DPPH, up to 13.66 mg Trolox/g DW in 711 and 7.83 mg Trolox/g DW in NS8 (Figure 8F,G).

2.8. Metabolite Changes Under Drought Stress

To clarify metabolite variations across genotypes under drought stress, primary and secondary metabolites were identified using the UPLC-MS wide-target metabolomics platform. PCA revealed that all samples clustered into two distinct groups (Figure 9A), aligning with physiological assessments that identified NS8 as being more drought-tolerant than 711. To further elucidate response mechanisms, the metabolic profile of 711 was scrutinized under varying degrees of stress. We identified 1691 metabolites categorized into 13 classes, dominated by amino acids and derivatives (16.09%), lipids (14.13%), phenolic acids (10.70%), and flavonoids (10.53%). Pairwise comparisons after 30 days of drought showed that up-regulated metabolites significantly outnumbered down-regulated metabolites (Figure 9C). Differential metabolite (DEM) analysis highlighted 271 amino acids and derivatives, 210 lipids, and 131 phenolic acids (Figure 9D). Specifically, key amino acids such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) showed substantial up-regulation under drought (Figure 9D). These acids are known to function as primary osmolytes and signaling molecules in mulberry to maintain cell turgor and mitigate oxidative damage. In the flavonoid class, key antioxidants such as rutin and quercetin-3-O-glucoside were significantly enriched, which directly correlates with the increased DPPH/ABTS scavenging capacity observed in our physiological assays. Notably, the accumulation of specific lipids such as lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC 18:2) and lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LPE), as highlighted in Figure 9D, suggests that mulberry trees undergo significant remodeling of membrane lipids to maintain membrane fluidity and integrity during prolonged periods of water deficit. It is shown in Figure 9E that the number of up-regulated flavonoids, lipids, phenylpropanoids, amino acids and derivatives, alkaloids, organic acids, and terpenoids increased with drought stress intensity. This indicates that as drought stress persists, more key physiological and metabolic activities in mulberry might be activated.

2.9. Metabolic Pathway Enrichment and Key Regulatory Pathways

To elucidate the metabolic regulatory mechanisms underlying the response of mulberry to drought stress, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the identified differential metabolites (Figure 10). In CK-711 vs. WD30-711, differential metabolites, which could relate to drought stress response, were mainly annotated and enriched in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, flavonoid biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and so on (Figure 10A). Under drought conditions, plant carbohydrate metabolism undergoes marked reprogramming. Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are both active, facilitating the interconversion of glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate. Sucrose accumulates to maintain cell turgor, while trehalose stabilizes proteins and membranes to enhance osmotic adjustment. The pentose phosphate pathway is also activated, which results in NADPH for scavenging drought-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the supply of ribose for nucleic acid synthesis. Both these processes support stress-responsive gene expression. In secondary metabolism, the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway is notably up-regulated. Flavonoids (e.g., quercetin, rutin, and kaempferol) and anthocyanins (e.g., cyanidin and delphinidin), derived from phenylalanine, function as potent antioxidants and may also provide photoprotection under drought-associated stress. In amino acid metabolism, tryptophan serves as a precursor of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), modulating root development and stomatal movement to enhance drought tolerance. Within the TCA cycle, changes in metabolites such as 2-oxoglutarate (a precursor of glutamate) and GABA contribute to stomatal regulation and stress signaling. Altered cysteine metabolism is linked to glutathione synthesis, further strengthening the antioxidant defense system. Collectively, these metabolic alterations illustrate the comprehensive reconfiguration of plant metabolism in response to drought (Figure 10B).

3. Discussion

Drought is an extremely natural hazard that occurs across the globe. This study employed 3D imaging and multispectral imaging with physiological and metabolomics analyses to elucidate the dynamic adaptation mechanisms in mulberry genotypes 711 and NS8.

Unlike labor-intensive destructive sampling, digital biomass (DB), leaf area (LA), and greenness (GR) enable longitudinal monitoring of individual plants. The strong correlations between DB/LA and yield validate these parameters as reliable predictive proxies. Notably, GR enables detection of incipient senescence prior to visible wilting, facilitating rapid, large-scale genotype screening essential for modern breeding under fluctuating climates. Furthermore, the phenotypic and metabolic shifts observed in this study are closely linked to the expression of known drought-resistant genes in mulberry. Accumulation of flavonoids and amino acids reflects metabolic outputs from transcriptional activation of biosynthetic genes, including MaMYB [49]. Such extensive transcriptome data can confirm up-regulation under conditions of water deficit. Morphological adjustments, specifically LA reduction and DB decline, constitute downstream consequences of ABA-mediated stress signaling. Transcription factors such as MaWRKY [50] coordinate these phenotypic modifications to optimize the trade-off between growth and survival. These findings position metabolic reprogramming as a functional intermediary linking genetic regulatory networks to observable phenotypic adaptation.

Water deficit significantly decreased DB, plant height (PH), LA, and projected leaf area (PLA), with PLA undergoing a reduction by day 5 after treatment initiation (Figure 2). The decrease in leaf area was considered to the most important and direct response of mulberry trees to drought stress, which is associated with the reduction in biomass [51,52]. The reduction of leaf area is a drought tolerance strategy to avoid dehydration in tissues and reduce metabolite consumption [53,54]. A lower specific leaf area correlates with better drought tolerance [55]. Leaf angle is an important canopy parameter because it affects light acquisition capacity and photosynthesis [56]. Here, the increase in LANG and the decrease in LINC indicated a reduction in the compactness of mulberry trees and canopy closure. However, no significant differences were observed between LANG and LINC for WW and WD treatment (Figure 2). This suggests that plant compactness is less affected by drought and may be primarily influenced by natural growth. Duan et al. [57] obtained a similar result in a study of plant phenotypes.

Variation in color traits in plants undergoing drought stress implied changes in water content and pigments. RRed and RGreen decreased for 711 but increased for NS8, while RBlue increased for both genotypes under drought (Figure 2). Mertens et al. reported that red reflectance increased and blue reflectance decreased due to drought stress [58]. Reflectance in plants depends primarily on pigment content; red and blue are strongly associated with chlorophyll content. Drought can cause the termination or reduction in chlorophyll synthesis, leading to decreased absorption of red and blue wavelengths, which, in turn, leads to an increase in the observed red and blue reflectance [59]. We observed that RRed showed greater variation in both genotypes, which may be caused by leaf water content, as reflectance in the red spectrum is more sensitive to water pressure [60]. Drought stress caused significant decreases in Hue, SAT, and VAL for 711 plants, but a nonsignificant decrease for NS8 plants. Tolley et al. found that in wheat, drought stress resulted in a significant decrease in SAT, but a significant increase in Hue [61]. Another study of plant phenotypes found that drought stress significantly reduced Hue, SAT, and VAL, which suggests that a change in pigment content occurs in the plant [62].

Drought stress in WD decreased RNIR, RFarRed, GR, NDVI, and NPCI, and increased PSRI compared to WW; ChlIdx and AriIdx gradually increased as mulberry trees grew, but drought led to lower values of these indicators in the WD trees. Lower NIR reflectance has been reported previously for drought [63], and it was mostly caused by changed leaf morphology and reduced water content. It has already been identified that the reflectance of NIR depends more on physical properties, such as leaf morphology, thickness, water content, and optical properties, including light scattering, than on pigment [64]. GR and NPCI indicated the intensity of green color and are associated with leaf chlorophyll concentration [65]. NDVI is the most commonly used vegetation index and can be regarded as a valid indicator to identify and quantify drought stress [66]. In the middle of the drought (DAT15), there was an interval of increased GR, NPCI, and NDVI. This is because the osmotic pressure caused by drought leads cell water to penetrate outward, and then plasmolysis occurs in the plant cell. In addition, pigments such as chlorophyll increase slightly in the early stages of stress [13]. This leads to enhanced green coloration in plant leaves under mild drought stress. At DAT35, the decrease in GR, NDVI, and NPCI indicated a reduction or termination of chlorophyll synthesis. PSRI is an indicator of plant senescence that is associated with leaf water deficiency [67]; the increase in PSRI indicated accelerated mulberry leaf senescence caused by drought stress. In support of this, Shaar-Moshe et al. found that abiotic stress led to a higher PSRI value, and there was a strong correlation between PSRI and genes associated with senescence [68].

Notably, despite reductions in total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC), overall antioxidant capacity (DPPH/ABTS) increased substantially under drought stress. This metabolic shift suggests the reallocation of resources from carbon-intensive secondary metabolism toward alternative antioxidant compounds. Proline and soluble sugars emerged as primary contributors—proline functions as both an osmolyte and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger, while soluble sugars protect membrane integrity. Genotype 711 exhibited pronounced amino acid accumulation, driving non-phenolic antioxidant defense.

Yield is critical for agriculture/breeding, and mulberry leaf yield is key for sericulture. Drought significantly reduced AFBM and LFBM, confirming prior studies [51,69]. Measurement of conventional yield is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and destructive. For rapid nondestructive yield estimation, DB and LA were screened as potential proxies: both correlated highly with AFBM/LFBM under WW/WD (Figure 4A,B). The results of the regression analysis showed there was a significant linear relationship between AFBM and DB (R2 = 0.9121) and LFBM and LA (R2 = 0.9602) (Figure 6A,D). DB is, therefore, a potential proxy trait for AFBM, LA, and LFBM. DB was calculated from the volume of plants; Neumann et al. reported that the correlation between plant fresh weight and digital biomass was high (R2 > 0.9), and this correlation was higher in the same genotype (R2 > 0.96) [70]. Chen et al. found that whole-plant volume acted as an excellent surrogate for the above-ground fresh weight of Brassica after regression analysis (R2 = 0.939) [36]. Leaf size and area are likewise good indicators of leaf biomass and plant growth [71]. Cho et al. found high correlations between fresh weight and leaf length and between fresh weight and leaf width [72]. Stem volume accurately predicted shoot weight, and total leaf area was highly correlated with shoot biomass [73].

TBARS and Pro are classical physiological biomarkers for drought stress detection. The concentrations of TBARS and Pro in 711 and NS8 trees significantly increased in WD, which indicates electrolyte leakage and membrane damage caused by drought [13,74]. The correlation matrix showed that nine phenotypic traits (PH, GR, NPCI, RNIR, RRed, RGreen, RBlue, ChlIdx, and AriIdx) were significantly correlated with the two biomarkers TBARS and Pro (Figure 4B, Table S5). PH and AriIdx had the most significant positive correlation coefficients, and GR and NPCI had the highest negative correlations (Table S5). NPCI was an indicator of plant senescence, and greenness was found to correlate highly with unifoliate senescence and wilting (R2 > 0.7183) [75]. Furthermore, PCA was used to find the phenotypic traits most sensitive to drought stress. The loadings of each phenotypic trait indicated that morphological traits, DB and LA, color traits, RGreen and SAT, and multispectral traits, AriIdx and GR, contributed most to variation and were the traits most sensitive to drought. A previous study reported that RRed, RGreen, and VAL are sensitive to drought stress treatments [76]. Both the correlation analysis and PCA suggest that GR has great potential to serve as a proxy trait of drought stress response. Such proxy traits (GR) made it possible to achieve rapid and nondestructive drought stress detection in mulberry trees. The use of GR as a drought indicator has been documented for other plants: Rolando et al. [77] found that leaf greenness was a drought-tolerance-related trait of potatoes; Mukeshimana et al. [78] thought that stem greenness of beans may be an effective trait for screening drought-tolerant genotypes; and Lazarević et al. [62] found that greenness was one of the traits most responsive to drought stress.

Two mulberry genotypes (711 and NS8) differed in drought tolerance. Under drought (WD), 711 exhibited larger reductions in DB (68.7%), PH (10.1%), LA (65.2%), and PLA (53.1%) than NS8; NS8’s DB, LA, and PLA decreased by 49.6%, 48.3%, 48.0% vs. the control, while PH remained unchanged (Figure 2). This suggests that NS8 is more morphologically and genetically drought-tolerant. Trees with the 711 genotype have higher yield (Table 1), and plant defense under stress often conflicts with yield [79]. Van der Weijde et al. [80] also showed that genotypes with less biomass are less likely to be affected by drought. All color traits of 711 changed significantly, but NS8’s values for RGreen, Hue, SAT, and VAL did not, indicating that NS8 maintains better pigments. Antioxidant profiling revealed differential physiological responses. We noted that CAT activity significantly decreased under stress (unaffected by POD/SOD) (Figure 7A–C), confirming CAT’s critical role in enzymatic H2O2 scavenging. In this study, total phenolic and flavonoid content decreased in both genotypes, yet antioxidant capacities (DPPH/ABTS) increased (Figure 8D–G), suggesting that non-phenolic antioxidants contribute to ROS mitigation, which is consistent with reports on other species [81,82]. This increased antioxidant capacity can be attributed to the up-regulation of specific metabolites. The identification of specific phenolic compounds in our study aligns with recent metabolic profiling of different mulberry germplasms, which highlights the diverse antioxidant capacity of free and bound phenolics within the species [83]. In this study, NS8 had a lower decrease in metabolites and increased antioxidant capacities compared to 711, which suggests its better physiological tolerance to drought, consistent with the results of phenotypic analysis.

Mulberry’s drought response involves 1691 differential metabolites. They show intensified up-regulation with stress duration (Figure 8E and Figure 9C,D) and reflect progressive metabolic activation [84]. Amino acids regulate plant stress tolerance by modulating osmotic pressure, ion transport, stomatal opening, and chemical homeostasis [85]. Specifically, the sharp increase in proline serves as a critical osmolyte, protein stabilizer, and ROS scavenger in Morus alba leaves. Phenylalanine-driven flavonoid synthesis via the phenylpropanoid pathway further scavenges reactive oxygen species; for instance, rutin up-regulation neutralizes hydroxyl radicals within vacuoles and mitigates oxidative damage [86,87,88,89]. Furthermore, enrichment of LPCs and LPEs in genotype 711 highlights robust membrane lipid remodeling, which is essential for maintaining integrity and preventing cellular desiccation. The down-regulation of ribose-5-phosphate and ribulose-5-phosphate revealed suppressed carbon assimilation and Calvin cycle activity, likely due to CO2 limitations and reduced RuBP regeneration [90]. Crucially, drought significantly up-regulated indole-3-acetonitrile (IAN), which is a direct precursor of IAA [91]. Accumulated IAN likely facilitates rapid auxin signaling for adaptations like stomatal closure and root remodeling and redirects metabolic flux toward defensive indole alkaloids. This dual role of IAN coordinates an integrated hormonal and defensive strategy against water deficits. While this study utilized broadly targeted metabolomics, future application of widely targeted metabolite modificomics is necessary [92]. This approach would allow for the discovery of diverse modified metabolites, particularly functional secondary metabolites, thus providing a more comprehensive understanding of the metabolic mechanisms underlying mulberry’s drought tolerance.

Furthermore, leveraging deep learning to analyze correlations between metabolomic and phenomic data facilitates nondestructive, in situ detection of plant stress responses. This integration of multi-omics data with advanced algorithms can bridge the gap between molecular-level changes and visible phenotypic traits, and, in turn, provide a more rapid and precise approach to monitor and assess mulberry’s adaptation to drought.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Plant Materials and Management

In this experiment, mulberry trees were selected for two genotypes, Yu 711 (711, Yu 54 × Yu 2) and Nongsang 8 (NS8, Ichinose × Lunjiao 109). Both are widely grown and important varieties in mulberry production, mainly in the Yangtze River basin and the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River. Genotype 711 is favored for its high leaf yield, while NS8 is frequently used due to its strong adaptability to abiotic stresses. Investigating these two specific genotypes provides results that are directly applicable to local agricultural practices and can help breeders understand how the most economically significant varieties respond to increasing drought frequency. One-year-old seedlings from Tongxiang were maintained under natural conditions at Zhejiang University (20 °C avg., 80% humidity) with daily watering (500 mL). After one month, six uniform seedlings per genotype were selected: three were subjected to drought stress via water deficit (WD), and three were used as well-watered controls (WW) for 35 days. Soil water content was monitored daily using a handheld meter (RS-*-SC-1, Shandong Renke Control Technology Co., Ltd., Jinan, China).

4.2. High-Throughput Phenotyping for Data Acquisition

4.2.1. Multispectral 3D Imaging

A PlantEye F500 multispectral 3D scanner (Phenospex, Heerlen, Netherlands) captured nondestructive plant data. It measured reflectance in red (620–645 nm), green (530–540 nm), blue (460–485 nm), and near-infrared (820–850 nm), and used a 940 nm 3D laser. Phena software (Version 1.2; Phenospex, Heerlen, Netherlands) generated 3D plant point clouds, and HortControl software (Version 3.3; Phenospex, Heerlen, Netherlands) calculated morphological traits and vegetation indices from these models. Scanning occurred every 5 days post-drought treatment (DAT0 to DAT35), totaling 8 timepoints. To ensure high data reproducibility and eliminate the influence of ambient light fluctuations, the scanning process was performed in a closed, light-controlled environment, ensuring that the 3D laser and multispectral sensors operated without interference from external light sources. Scanner settings included the following conditions: 120 cm Z-range, 0.7 mm X-resolution, 1 mm Y-resolution, 0.2 mm Z-resolution, and 50 Hz XZ-profile scanning rates. Measured morphological traits comprised digital biomass (DB), plant height (PH), leaf area (LA), projected leaf area (PLA), leaf angle (LANG), and leaf inclination (LINC). Calculated vegetation indices included greenness (GR), NDVI [93], the normalized pigment chlorophyll ratio index (NPCI), and plant senescence reflectance index (PSRI) [67], which were calculated according to the following equations:(1)GR= 2 ×RGreen−RRed−RBlueRGreen+RRed+RBlue(2)NDVI=RNIR−RRedRNIR+RRed(3)NPCI=RRed−RBlueRRed+RBlue(4)PSRI=RRed−RGreenRNIR

4.2.2. Multispectral Imaging

A PlantExplorer Field system (PhenoVation B.V., Wageningen, Netherlands) generated multispectral plant images. This contained cabinet system was designed to completely shield the plants from external environmental light, integrating a CCD camera with multispectral/visible imaging capabilities and high-intensity LEDs. The use of a closed cabinet ensured that measurements were independent of fluctuations in natural light. The LEDs emitted photons at controlled spectral bands (400–1000 nm) with adjustable intensity (0–3000 μmol/m2/s) during data acquisition, providing a constant and uniform light environment for all samples. Plant images were acquired using a lens (10 Mp, 200 LP/mm resolution, 400–1000 nm spectral range) and CCD camera (1.3 Mp, 1296 × 966 pixels). Data analysis software (Version 5.6.9) processed images to produce color and multispectral outputs. Measurements used 300 μmol/m2/s intensity, capturing reflectances at RRed (640 nm), RGreen (550 nm), RBlue (475 nm), RNIR (769 nm), and RFarRed (710 nm). Red, green, and blue reflectances were normalized (0–1) to calculate Hue (0–360°), Saturation (SAT; 0–1), and Value (VAL; 0–1) using standard transformations based on maximum/minimum RGB values. Hue was calculated according to the following rules for each case: if max = RRed, Hue = 60 × [0 + (RGreen − RBlue)/(max − min)]; if max = RGreen, Hue = 60 × [2 + (RBlue − RRed)/(max − min)]; if max = RBlue, Hue = 60 × [0 + (RRed − RGreen)/(max − min)]; and if Hue is <0, then add 360. VAL was calculated as follows: VAL = (max + min)/2; SAT was calculated as follows: if VAL > 0.5, SAT = (max − min)/(max + min); if VAL < 0.5, SAT = (max − min)/(2 − max − min). Here, the max and min values are derived from RRed, RGreen, and RBlue. The chlorophyll index (ChlIdx) [94] and anthocyanin index (AriIdx) [95] were used to nondestructively assess the chlorophyll and anthocyanin content of plant leaves in real time. The calculations were as follows: ChlIdx = 1/R 700 − 1/R 769, and AriIdx = 1/R 550 − 1/R 700.

4.3. Manual Reference Measurements

4.3.1. Harvest and Yield

An agronomic trait, yield, was measured. At the end of the experiment (DAT 35), the mulberry tree in each pot was cut down at the soil level and aboveground fresh biomass (AFBM) was determined. The leaves were then separated from the main stem and the total leaf fresh biomass (LFBM) was determined.

4.3.2. Physiological Biomarkers of Drought Stress

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARSs) and proline (Pro) are established physiological biomarkers for plant drought stress responses [96,97,98,99]. Mulberry leaves were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, rinsed with deionized water, and lyophilized at −50 °C and 6 Pa for 48 h using a Scientz-18N freeze dryer (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China). The lyophilized mulberry leaves were then ground and sieved through a 60-mesh sieve for subsequent analysis.

To determine TBARSs, 0.05 g of leaf powder was homogenized in 10% trichloroacetic acid, centrifuged at 12,000× g for 10 min, and 2 mL of the supernatant was reacted with 0.6% thiobarbituric acid in boiling water for 30 min. After cooling in an ice bath and centrifugation at 10,000× g for 10 min, supernatant absorbance was measured at 450, 532, and 600 nm for TBARS quantification [100]. For Pro analysis, 0.1 g of powder was extracted with 3% sulfosalicylic acid at 100 °C for 15 min, centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min, and 1 mL of the supernatant was reacted with glacial acetic acid and the ninhydrin reagent at 100 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 520 nm after cooling, and quantification was based on a proline standard curve [65].

4.4. Physiological Traits

4.4.1. Activity of Antioxidant Enzymes

Peroxidase activity (POD, EC 1.11.1.7) was measured using an assay kit (Grace Biotechnology, Suzhou, China). H2O2 oxidation of guaiacol catalyzed by POD produced a reddish-brown product that had maximum absorption at 470 nm. The activity (1U) was defined as an increase of 0.5 in absorbance at 470 nm. The activity of catalase (CAT, EC 1.11.1.6) was measured using the corresponding assay kit (Biosharp, Beijing, China), and the activity (1U) was expressed as the catalytic decomposition of 1 mol of H2O2. The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD, EC 1.15.1.1) was determined using WST-8 according to the assay kit instructions (Grace Biotechnology, Suzhou, China). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and the activity (1U) was defined as 50% inhibition of the reaction system.

4.4.2. Quantification of Antioxidant Metabolites and Capacity

Antioxidant metabolites were extracted following the method of Yu et al. [101] with minor modifications. Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined via the Folin–Ciocalteu method [102]. 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity was assayed according to Lou et al. [103]. For 2,2-azino-bis-(3-ethylenebenzothiozoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical cation scavenging activity, the method of He et al. [104] was used with minor modifications: 10 μL of extracted solution was mixed with 170 μL of fresh ABTS solution, incubated in the dark for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Results were expressed as μg Trolox/g DW.

4.5. Correlation Analysis of Yield and Physiological Biomarkers with Phenotypic Traits

Pearson correlation coefficients between yield, physiological biomarkers, and all phenotypic traits were calculated and presented as heatmaps. Linear regressions were performed on the yield and selected phenotypic traits to model the relationships between them. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to analyze morphological traits, color traits, and multispectral traits at DAT35, and a biplot was created for the first two principal components.

4.6. Data Processing and Statistical Analysis of Metabolic Data

Based on two different genotypes (711 and NS8) of mulberry trees, 10 leaves were collected for each of the following points of analysis: water deficit day 0 (control, CK), and water deficit days 15 and 30 (WD15-711, WD30-711, WD15-NS8, and WD30-NS8). Metabolomic sampling was conducted at DAT30 to capture the peak molecular response, and phenotypic traits were recorded at DAT35 to allow for the full expression of morphological changes under sustained drought stress. Leaves were vacuum freeze-dried (Scientz-100F) and ground (30 Hz, 1.5 min; MM 400, Retsch) to powder. A total of 50 mg of powder was weighed (MS105DΜ), mixed with 1200 μL of a pre-cooled (−20 °C) 70% methanolic internal standard extract, and vortexed (30 s/30 min, 6 times). After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 3 min), the supernatant was filtered (0.22 μm) for UPLC-MS/MS (ExionLC™ AD, QTRAP-MS). UPLC was conducted with an Agilent SB-C18 column, mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid–water)/B (0.1% formic acid–acetonitrile), gradient program, flow 0.35 mL/min, 40 °C column oven, and 2 μL injection. ESI parameters were 550 °C, ±4500/5500 V, GSI/GSII/CUR 50/60/25 psi, and MRM scans.

Metabolite data were log2-transformed for statistical analysis and normalization. Metabolites from 30 samples were used for hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), principal component analysis (PCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). RStudio (Version 2024.12.1+563) was used to study metabolite accession-specific accumulation, and p- and fold-change values were set to 0.05 and 2.0, respectively.

4.7. Data Processing and Statistical Analysis of Phenotypic Traits

Phenotypic trait data were acquired using HortControl and Data Analysis software (Version 3.3; Phenospex, Heerlen, Netherlands). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Student’s t-test compared phenotypic traits between treatments, with significant differences identified at p < 0.05. One-way ANOVA determined differences in yield and physiological biomarkers. Two-way ANOVA assessed the effects of genotype and drought treatment. All graphical representations were created using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

5. Conclusions

High-throughput phenotyping techniques characterize the phenotypic response of mulberry trees to drought rapidly, nondestructively, and in real time. Drought significantly reduced morphology, color, and spectral traits across genotypes. NS8 exhibited superior tolerance with less phenotypic change and better yield/physiology (TBARS, Pro). DB and LA correlated strongly with yield, while GR emerged as a key indicator of drought stress via PCA and correlation analyses. Antioxidant data highlighted CAT’s role and non-phenolic contributions to antioxidant capacity. Metabolomics indicated drought-induced metabolic shifts: enhanced secondary metabolites for stress resistance, regulated amino acid pathways, and adjusted carbohydrate metabolism, synergistically enabling adaptation. In conclusion, integrating advanced high-throughput phenotypic and metabolomic analyses with conventional physiological trait assessment offers a more comprehensive understanding of the response of mulberry trees to drought stress. This not only establishes a solid foundation for developing rapid, nondestructive approaches to evaluate plant stress adaptation, but also provides valuable insights for identifying drought-responsive biomarkers and enhancing drought resistance in plants.
